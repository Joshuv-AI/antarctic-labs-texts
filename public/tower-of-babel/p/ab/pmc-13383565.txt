Widely targeted metabolomics reveals organ-specific metabolite accumulation and functional differentiation of leaves, pulp and seeds in wild jujube (Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa)
Authors: Jidong Ju, Jing Xuan, Xiaochen Song, Hui Fang, Yongqing Zhang, LongFei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundZiziphus jujuba var. spinosa, commonly known as ‘wild jujube,’ is a significant medicinal and edible plant. While the value of its seeds and pulps is well established, the potential of its leaves has been largely neglected.MethodsIn this study, we determined the contents of basic chemical components—including total flavonoids, total phenolics, and total saponins—in the leaves, pulp, and seeds of Z. jujuba var. spinosa, and we evaluated their antioxidant activities. In addition, widely targeted metabolomics combined with high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-based targeted validation was employed to systematically compare the metabolite profiles.ResultsThe three organs exhibited distinct metabolic functional differentiation. Leaves displayed the highest contents of total flavonoids, total phenolics, and total saponins, along with the strongest in vitro antioxidant activity. The characteristic accumulation of flavonoid glycosides, such as rutin and isoquercitrin, and of triterpene saponins, such as pedunculagin, may be associated with defense functions. Pulp accumulated significant amounts of soluble sugars and organic acids, such as citric acid, and contained moderate levels of flavonoids. Seeds functioned as a protein reservoir and accumulated C-glycosylated flavonoids and dammarane-type triterpene saponins. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis are the core pathways driving this organ-specific metabolic differentiation. This study systematically suggests, for the first time from a metabolomics perspective, the “defense–dispersal–reserve” functional differentiation pattern among the leaves, pulp, and seeds of Z. jujuba var. spinosa. These findings provide a scientific basis for developing the leaf, a nontraditional organ, as a high-value antioxidant resource and lay a theoretical foundation for comprehensive whole-plant utilization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09088-3.Keywords: Ziziphus jujuba var. spinose, Metabolomics, Organ specificity, Flavonoids, Saponins, Resource utilization

Introduction

Ziziphus jujuba Mill. var. spinosa (Bunge) Hu ex H. F. Chou is an important plant resource with both edible and medicinal value, rich in nutrients such as nucleotides, amino acids, polysaccharides, and fatty oils [1]. Its dried mature seeds, known as “Suanzaoren” in traditional Chinese medicine, have exhibited sedative–hypnotic and neuroprotective activities and were widely used to treat insomnia, anxiety, and palpitations [2, 3]. By contrast, the pulp and leaves, which constitute the majority of the plant’s biomass, have long been undervalued. In production practice, pulp and leaves are often treated as byproducts or even waste during processing of Z. jujuba var. spinosa seeds, a practice that not only wastes resources but also constrains the efficiency and sustainable development of the related industry. Although previous studies have reported pulp applications in processed foods and beverages and identified flavonoids such as rutin in leaves, systematic chemical characterization and functional validation have remained lacking [4, 5].

The metabolite composition of plant organs closely reflects their physiological and ecological roles. Leaves, as the primary sites of photosynthesis and the first line of defense against environmental stress, commonly accumulate abundant secondary metabolites—such as flavonoids and phenolic acids—to mitigate ultraviolet radiation and deter herbivores [6, 7]. Pulp, by contrast, tend to concentrate flavor compounds, including sugars and organic acids, which function as chemical signals that attract animal dispersers and promote seed dispersal [8, 9]. Seeds are more likely to store nutrient reserves—proteins and oils—while producing stable defensive compounds that secure the survival of the next generation [10, 11]. A recent study of Pimpinella brachycarpa documented the organ-specific distribution of 66 metabolites across flowers, leaves, stems, and roots, and found that flower and leaf extracts exhibited the strongest antioxidant activities [12]. These studies indicate that the organ-specific distribution of metabolites is not random but instead reflects long-term evolutionary adaptation. However, most existing reports have concentrated on individual components of Z. jujuba var. spinosa seeds, leaving systematic chemical characterization of leaves and pulp scarce. The few investigations that include leaves or pulp have typically been restricted to detecting known constituents and thus have not revealed the full metabolic landscape. Critically, there is no systematic evidence showing whether metabolic differences among Z. jujuba var. spinosa organs correspond to their distinct ecological roles in plant survival. This gap in knowledge limits our understanding of organ-specific physiological functions in Z. jujuba var. spinosa and constrains efforts to develop and utilize the plant’s whole-plant resources for higher-value applications.

This study conducted a integrated analysis to characterize metabolic differences among the leaves, pulp, and seeds of Z. jujuba var. spinosa, to examine how differential metabolite accumulation relates to the physiological and ecological functional differentiation of these organs, and to clarify each organ’s functional roles and potential for high-value utilization. First, we measured basic nutritional components (crude protein, soluble sugar) and core bioactive constituents (total flavonoids, total phenolics, total saponins) in leaves, pulp, and seeds, and we evaluated their in vitro antioxidant activities. Second, we applied widely targeted metabolomics to systematically profile the metabolites of the three organs, identifying the distribution of differential metabolites and the principal enriched pathways that underpin organ-specific accumulation. Finally, we performed targeted quantitative validation of characteristic metabolic markers in each organ using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the metabolomics results. This study systematically maps, for the first time, the metabolite profiles of the leaves, pulp, and seeds of Z. jujuba var. spinosa, revealing from a metabolomics perspective the metabolic basis underlying the functional differentiation of these organs and thereby filling a research gap in the systematic chemical characterization of the species’ non-medicinal parts. The findings contribute to advancing fundamental research on organ-specific metabolism in Z. jujuba var. spinosa and provide systematic scientific data to support the industry’s transition from single-part medicinal use to high-value, whole-plant resource exploitation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

Ziziphus jujuba Mill. var. spinosa plants were collected in August 2024 from a standardized planting base in the Yellow River Delta Agricultural High-tech Industrial Demonstration Zone, Shandong Province, China (37.6°N, 118.5°E), managed by Shandong Zhongchangyuan Investment Group Co., Ltd. The material represented a typical germplasm of this variety, and no specific cultivar was identified. Fully mature pulps and healthy leaves from the third to fifth nodes of the apical shoots were harvested at 100 days after flowering. Nine plants with consistent age and growth status were chosen and randomly assigned to three biological replicates, with three plants per replicate. Images of the plants and samples are provided in Fig. S1. Following collection, samples were placed in ice boxes and transported to the laboratory. Under a clean bench at 4 °C, seeds were manually separated from the pulp. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, freeze-dried at − 50 °C and 0.1 mbar for 48 h, and then ground into powder. Powdered samples were passed through a 60-mesh sieve, aliquoted into cryovials, and stored at − 80 °C for metabolomic analysis. Leaves were rinsed with distilled water and air-dried, whereas seeds, pulp, and additional leaf material were dried at 50 °C to constant weight. The dried materials were then ground to a fine powder with an electric grinder, sieved again through a 60-mesh screen, and stored in a desiccator for basic component determination and targeted high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis. All specimens were authenticated as Ziziphus jujuba (Rhamnaceae) by Professor Yongqing Zhang at Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. These authenticated samples are currently preserved in the Key Laboratory of Traditional Chinese Medicine Resources and Quality Control, School of Pharmacy, Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (specimen numbers: ZZS-2024–091, ZZS-2024–092, ZZS-2024–093).

Determination of basic nutrients and active components

Crude protein and total soluble sugars

We determined the total protein and soluble sugar content in wild jujube seeds, pulps, and leaves using commercial kits. For protein content, we employed the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method, while soluble sugar content was assessed using a kit from Suzhou Grees Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Measurements followed the manufacturer's instructions, using wavelengths of 595 nm for protein and 620 nm for sugar. Results were expressed in milligrams per gram of dry weight (mg/g DW).

Total flavonoids, total phenols, and total saponins

Weigh 50 mg of powder accurately. Add 3 mL of 70% (v/v) methanol solution and perform ultrasonic extraction at 60 °C and 40 kHz for 90 min. Centrifuge the extract at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min, then transfer the supernatant for further analysis. Determine the total flavonoid content using the aluminum nitrate-sodium nitrite colorimetric method with rutin as the standard at a wavelength of 510 nm. Measure the total phenolic content using the Folin-Ciocalteu method with gallic acid as the standard at 765 nm. Assess the total saponin content using the vanillin-perchloric acid color development method with jujuboside A as the standard at 590 nm. Express the results as milligrams of rutin equivalent, gallic acid equivalent, and jujuboside A equivalent per gram of dry weight, respectively.

Determination of in vitro antioxidant activity

To assess the free radical scavenging ability and total antioxidant activity of wild jujube seeds, pulps, and leaves, we used the 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging rate commercial detection kit and the Total Antioxidant Capacity Assay kit (Ferric Reducing Ability of Plasma, FRAP) from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer's instructions. Ground samples were extracted, and the supernatant was collected post-centrifugation for testing.

In the DPPH assay, vitamin C served as the standard. We prepared a series of working solutions at various concentrations and measured absorbance at 517 nm to calculate the IC50 value of the free radical scavenging rate. For the FRAP assay, Fe2⁺ was the standard. We prepared gradient concentration solutions and conducted detection at 593 nm, calculating the total antioxidant capacity based on the standard curve.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis

Weigh 100 mg of the sample powder accurately. Add 1 mL of a 70% methanol solution containing 0.1% formic acid. Perform ultrasonic extraction at 40 kHz for 90 min at 60 °C. Centrifuge the mixture at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, then filter the supernatant through a 0.22-μm nylon membrane. In seeds, six components were identified: cryptochlorogenic acid, caffeic acid, spinosin, 6‴-feruloylspinosin, jujuboside A, and jujuboside B. In pulp, seven components were identified: citric acid, gallic acid, catechin, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, rutin, and quercetin-3-O-glucoside. In leaves, thirteen components were identified: citric acid, neochlorogenic acid, catechin, caffeic acid, rutin, quercetin-3-O-glucoside, kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside, isochlorogenic acid B, astragalin, quercetin, jujuboside A, jujuboside B, and kaempferol.

The experiment utilized an Agilent 1260 Infinity Ⅱ binary liquid chromatography system, featuring a diode array detector (DAD) and an evaporative light scattering detector (ELSD) (Agilent Technologies, USA). A ZORBAX SB-C18 chromatographic column (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) was employed. The flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 30 °C. The binary solvent system comprised 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution (A) and 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile solution (B). Gradient elution programs were optimized for different organs to achieve optimal separation: for seeds, 0–5 min (15–25% B), 5–8 min (25–36% B), 8–9 min (36–39% B), 9–17 min (39–42% B), 17–18 min (42–43% B); for pulp, 0–15 min (5–13% B), 15–30 min (13–15% B), 30–45 min (15–20% B); for leaves, 0–11 min (5–25% B), 11–12 min (25–29% B), 12–20 min (29–50% B), 20–24 min (50–73% B). Reference substances, such as spinosin and jujuboside A, were sourced from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. We used the external standard method for quantification. A series of standard solutions at different concentrations was prepared, and standard curves were plotted. Each sample was injected three times (see Table S1 in the appendix). Representative chromatograms of the standard and the sample are shown in Fig S2.

Wide-targeted metabolomics analysis

Wuhan Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. conducted the extraction of powder samples from various organs and performed metabolomics detection. The extracts were analyzed using an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-electrospray ionization-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-ESI–MS/MS) system. This system included an ultra-high-performance liquid chromatograph (ExionLC AD; Sciex Division, Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) and a mass spectrometer (Sciex 4500 QTRAP, Applied Biosystems). Metabolite identification relied on the self-built MWDB metabolite database (Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) and was achieved through secondary mass spectrometry matching. Following quality control, the raw data were normalized for statistical analysis. Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) values and fold change (FC) from orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), with significance thresholds set at FC ≥ 2 or FC ≤ 0.5 and VIP ≥ 1 [13]. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (http://www.kegg.jp/kegg/compound/) and the self-built Metmap pathway database (Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) were utilized for functional annotation and pathway enrichment analysis of the differential metabolites.

Statistical analysis

The results for basic component analysis, antioxidant activity, and targeted high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) quantification are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three biological replicates. We identified significant differences between groups using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test, conducted with SPSS 25.0 software (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). A p-value of less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Figures and tables were created using Origin 2017 SR2 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). For metabolomics data, we conducted multivariate statistical analyses, including PCA and OPLS-DA, and performed correlation network analysis for metabolites and antioxidant indicators using R packages, as detailed in each section.

Results

Unique chemical and antioxidant properties of different tissues of Z. jujuba var. spinosa

To thoroughly characterize the three organs of Z. jujuba var. spinosa, we performed quantitative analyses of their basic chemical components and in vitro antioxidant capacities. Significant differences emerged among the leaf, pulp, and seed tissues (Fig. 1A). The leaf exhibited the highest total flavonoid content (TFC) at 35.76 mg rutin equivalent per gram of dry weight, total phenolic content (TPC) at 41.73 mg gallic acid equivalent per gram of dry weight, and total saponin content (TSC) at 67.97 mg jujuboside A equivalent per gram of dry weight. The pulp followed, with the seed showing the lowest values. Conversely, the seed had the highest crude protein content at 36.14 mg per gram of dry weight, while the pulp was richest in soluble sugar content at 88.85 mg per gram of dry weight.

The antioxidant activity assessment revealed a clear pattern. The leaf demonstrated the highest ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP, 54.65 μmol Fe2⁺/g dry weight) and the most effective DPPH free radical scavenging, indicated by its lowest half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀, 0.11 mg/mL) (Fig. 1A, B). In contrast, the pulp exhibited moderate antioxidant activity, while the seed showed the weakest activity. A radar chart, incorporating seven parameters (crude protein, soluble sugar, TFC, TPC, TSC, FRAP, and 1/DPPH IC₅₀), illustrated the distinct chemical profiles of each tissue (Fig. 1C). The leaf was notable for high antioxidant indices, the pulp for high sugar content, and the seed for high protein content.

To clarify the material basis of the antioxidant activity, we performed Pearson correlation analyses between total flavonoid content (TFC), total phenol content (TPC), and the antioxidant indices FRAP and DPPH. TFC correlated strongly and positively with FRAP (r = 0.975, p < 0.01) and with DPPH scavenging capacity (r = 0.995, p < 0.01). TPC showed analogous correlations with both antioxidant indicators (Table S2). Together, these results indicate that flavonoids and phenolic compounds likely underlie the pronounced antioxidant activity observed in sour date leaves.

Global metabolomics analysis

To elucidate the metabolic basis of phenotypic differences, we performed widely targeted metabolomics analysis. The overlapping total ion chromatograms of quality control (QC) samples (Figure S3) and the strong intra-group correlations among biological replicates (Fig. 2A) demonstrated effective sample preparation. We identified 2836 metabolites in the analysis (Table S3). Flavonoids (17.17%) and terpenoids (20.84%) were the most abundant categories, followed by alkaloids (11.95%), phenolic acids (9.84%), and amino acids and their derivatives (9.45%) (Fig. 2B).

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation of leaf, pulp, and seed samples along the first two principal components, with PC1 accounting for 52.44% and PC2 for 30.4% of the variance (Fig. 2C). Peak areas of all metabolites were log-transformed (log2) and mean-centered prior to OPLS-DA analysis. To assess potential model overfitting, 200 permutation tests were performed for each comparison group. The raw R2Y and Q2 values for each model fell within an acceptable range (Leaf vs Pulp: R2Y = 0.999, Q2 = 0.997; Leaf vs Seed: R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.998; Seed vs Pulp: R2Y = 1, Q2 = 0.996), indicating strong discriminatory and predictive performance without evidence of overfitting. The OPLS-DA score plot showed clear separation of samples for each comparison group (Figure S4). Additionally, unsupervised hierarchical clustering of all detected metabolites confirmed significant differences in metabolite accumulation patterns across the different tissues (Fig. 2D).

Identification and classification of differential metabolites

Volcano plots depicted the differential metabolites (|log2FC|> 1, VIP ≥ 1) between tissues (Fig. 3A–C). The leaf–seed comparison showed the greatest number of differentially accumulated metabolites, totaling 2116 species. Comparisons of leaves versus pulps and pulps versus seeds identified 1879 and 1832 species, respectively, underscoring pronounced metabolic differentiation among plant organs (Table S4). Differentially accumulated metabolites across the three tissues were categorized into 10 subclasses using K-means clustering based on their accumulation patterns (Fig. 4A). Subclasses 3, 4, and 5 showed peak abundance in leaves. Subclasses 6 and 10 were dominated by metabolites highly accumulated in seeds, while subclasses 1, 2, 7, and 8 were prominent in pulps. Figure 4B provides details on the categories and compositions of differentially accumulated metabolites in each pairwise comparison. Flavonoids and terpenoids constituted the major classes of differentially accumulated metabolites, with most significantly upregulated in leaves.

Tissue-specific accumulation of key bioactive components

To identify representative key metabolites in each tissue, we selected 20 compounds with notable fold differences and known bioactivity to create a heatmap (Fig. 5A). The analysis showed that leaves tended to accumulate flavonol glycosides, such as Rutin and Isoquercitrin, and phenolic acids like Neochlorogenic acid and Chlorogenate. They also contained unique terpenoids, including Pedunculoside and triptotriterpenic acid A. Citric acid was notably abundant in the pulp, which also had moderate flavonoid levels. Seeds specifically accumulated C-glycosylflavones characteristic of the Ziziphus genus, such as Spinosin and its 6‴-p-coumaroyl derivatives, along with Jujuboside A/B. These findings were further supported by targeted HPLC quantitative analysis (Figs. 5B–D). Rutin, a primary antioxidant flavonoid, was most concentrated in leaves (9.66 ± 0.11 mg/g dry weight), less so in the pulp, and absent in seeds (Fig. 5B). Jujuboside A, known for its sedative properties, was predominantly found in seeds (0.66 ± 0.01 mg/g dry weight), with significantly lower levels in leaves (0.27 ± 0.01 mg/g dry weight) and undetected in the pulp (Fig. 5C). Citric acid, the main sour component, was highly concentrated in the pulp (13.75 ± 0.04 mg/g dry weight) (Fig. 5D). Comprehensive data for all 15 quantified compounds are provided in Table S5.

Enrichment analysis of differential metabolic pathways

To clarify the biological processes that underlie metabolic differences among organs, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on differential metabolites from three pairwise comparisons (Fig. 6). In the leaf versus seed comparison, metabolites showed a pronounced organ-specific accumulation pattern within the flavone and flavonol biosynthesis pathway (Fig. S5A). Specifically, flavonoid O-glycosides—rutin, isoquercitrin, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside, and kaempferol-3-O-rutinoside—were enriched in leaves, whereas flavonoid C-glycosides—vitexin, isovitexin, vitexin 2′′-O-β-D-glucoside, and isovitexin 2′′-O-β-D-glucoside—were significantly enriched in seeds.

In the leaf versus pulp comparison, biosynthesis of protocatechuic acid derivatives emerged as the most distinctive pathway driving metabolic divergence between these tissues (Fig. S5B). Phenolic acids—including vanillate, 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate, 2,3,4-trihydroxybenzoic acid, and protocatechuic acid glucosyl xyloside—accumulated to high levels in leaves but were present only at trace amounts or low expression levels in pulp, making this class the primary contributor to the metabolic differentiation between the two organs.

In the comparison of seed and pulp, apigenin C-glycoside biosynthesis and the biosynthesis of quercetin aglycones I emerged as the defining pathways that capture the core metabolic distinctions between the two organs, with metabolites in these pathways showing completely opposite accumulation patterns (Fig. S5C, Fig. S5D). C-glycosylated flavonoids within the apigenin C-glycoside pathway—for example, apigenin-6-C-glucoside-7-O-sophoroside, isovitexin glycoside derivatives, and vitexin glycoside derivatives—were highly and specifically expressed in seeds and were nearly undetectable in pulp, constituting a seed-specific metabolic signature. By contrast, flavonols in the quercetin aglycones I pathway, including quercetin glycoside derivatives and quercetin rutinoside derivatives, accumulated at much higher levels in pulp than in seed, producing a clear pulp-specific enrichment. Furthermore, metabolites that differed between leaf and pulp were significantly enriched in tryptophan metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation pathways, while those that differed between seed and pulp were enriched in arginine and proline metabolism and linoleic acid metabolism pathways.

Differential metabolites between leaf and seed were also enriched in triterpenoid biosynthesis pathways, underscoring organ-specific metabolic specialization. Together, these results reflect distinct metabolic regulation and physiological functions among the organs of Z. jujuba var. spinosa.

KEGG enrichment analysis revealed clear functional differentiation of metabolic pathways among leaves, seeds, and pulp. Leaf metabolism was associated primarily with photosynthesis-linked secondary metabolism and the synthesis of photoprotective compounds. Seed metabolism was enriched in pathways for storage compound synthesis, amino acid accumulation, and stress-resistance mechanisms. Pulp metabolism was enriched in pathways related to fruit development, ripening regulation, and flavor compound production.

Overall, these findings provide a concise framework for further investigation of the metabolic roles of different plant tissues in growth, development, and environmental adaptation.

Discussion

Z. jujuba var. spinosa is a traditional medicinal and edible plant in China whose seed-derived sedative and calming properties and whose edible pulp are widely recognized. Yet the material basis and utilization potential of non-medicinal organs—primarily leaves and pulp, which comprise most of the plant’s biomass—have not been systematically elucidated, nor have the patterns of metabolic functional differentiation among organs been clearly defined [14, 15]. In this study, we quantitatively analyzed basic chemical components, assessed in vitro antioxidant activity, conducted widely targeted metabolomics, and performed HPLC-based targeted validation to characterize systematically the metabolic profiles of three major organs—leaves, pulp, and seeds—of Z. jujuba var. spinosa. We analyzed metabolomic evidence for a “defense–dispersal–reserve” pattern of functional differentiation among organs and provided new scientific insights for high-value utilization of the whole plant.

Leaves, as the primary site of photosynthesis and the vegetative organ most exposed to the external environment, face concurrent stresses from intense light and ultraviolet radiation as well as from herbivorous insects and pathogenic fungi. In this study, total flavonoid, total phenolic, and total saponin contents in Z. jujuba var. spinosa leaves were the highest among the three organs examined, and their in vitro antioxidant activity was significantly greater than that of pulp and seeds. This pattern aligns closely with metabolic traits reported for woody plants such as sea buckthorn, whose leaves also concentrate flavonol glycosides at levels markedly higher than those in fruits and seeds [16, 17]. Pearson correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between total flavonoid and total phenolic contents and the in vitro antioxidant activity of Z. jujuba var. spinosa. We therefore hypothesize that leaf-enriched flavonol glycosides (for example, rutin and isoquercitrin) and phenolic acids (for example, neochlorogenic acid) constitute the primary material basis for the leaves’ strong antioxidant activity [18–20]. Phenolic hydroxyl groups in these compounds effectively scavenge reactive oxygen species produced during photosynthesis, thereby preserving the stability and performance of the photosynthetic apparatus; this represents a core metabolic strategy by which leaves cope with photooxidative stress [21, 22]. At the same time, leaves accumulate triterpene saponins such as pedunculagin, which are characteristically bitter and possess membrane-disrupting activity [23, 24]; these properties reduce leaf palatability and deter herbivorous insects. Their broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity also protects leaves from pathogen invasion [25, 26]. This integration of photoprotective antioxidant defenses with biotic-stress resistance has been observed in other woody species, for example Sapindus mukorossi [27] and Hippophae rhamnoides [16, 28], and likely reflects a synergistic adaptive strategy in Z. jujuba var. spinosa leaves shaped by the dual selective pressures of maintaining physiological function and providing ecological defense.

The pulp’s metabolic profile differs markedly from that of the leaves, reflecting its primary ecological role of attracting animal consumers through flavor and nutrition to promote seed dispersal. In this study, we confirmed that soluble sugar and citric acid concentrations in Z. jujuba var. spinosa pulp were significantly higher than those in leaves and seeds. These two compounds determine the pulp’s sweet–sour taste and serve as key signals that attract natural seed dispersers such as birds and mammals [29–31]. We also detected moderate levels of flavonoids in the pulp. We hypothesize that these flavonoids have a dual ecological function: some act as pigments that contribute to fruit coloration and thereby provide visual cues of ripeness, while others serve antioxidant roles that mitigate oxidative damage during ripening and add nutritional value for consumers [32–34]. Together, these traits increase the fruit’s appeal to dispersers and provide a biochemical basis for developing Z. jujuba var. spinosa pulp as a healthy food ingredient.

Seeds, as reproductive organs, display a metabolic profile that differs markedly from those of leaves and pulp. Their principal functions are to provide chemical defenses during dormancy and to store the nutrients and energy required for subsequent germination. In this study, we validated the high protein content of Z. jujuba var. spinosa seeds, which is consistent with their role as nutrient storage organs. More importantly, the seeds specifically accumulated two classes of core secondary metabolites: C-glycosylated flavonoids (for example, spinosin and 6‴-feruloylspinosin) and dammarane-type triterpene saponins (for example, jujuboside A and jujuboside B). Unlike the more common O-glycosidic bonds found in plants, C-glycosidic bonds are chemically more stable and resistant to hydrolysis [35], a property that preserves these compounds’ structural integrity and function throughout prolonged seed dormancy. From an ecological-adaptive perspective, modern pharmacology has confirmed that spinosin and jujubosides constitute the principal material basis for the sedative, hypnotic, and anxiolytic effects of Z. jujuba var. spinosa seeds [36–38]. They act as central nervous system inhibitors by modulating the GABAergic and serotonergic systems. Because these compounds produce pronounced neuroactivity in mammals, they could plausibly induce lethargy and slowed reactions in animals, thereby deterring herbivory. The metabolic profile of Z. jujuba var. spinosa seeds reflects the integration of two evolutionary pressures—nutrient storage and chemical defense—and humans have co-opted the latter to constitute the seeds’ principal medicinal value.

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis showed that differential metabolites among the three organs were significantly enriched in three core pathways: flavonoid biosynthesis, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, and terpenoid backbone biosynthesis. The elevated levels of flavonoids and phenolic acids in leaves likely arise from upregulation of the upstream phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway [39]. Differences in flavonoid and terpenoid structures and abundances between leaves and seeds are probably determined by organ-specific modification steps after backbone assembly, such as oxidation and glycosylation [40, 41]. These findings offer a focused starting point for molecular investigations into organ-specific accumulation mechanisms of bioactive compounds in Z. jujuba var. spinosa and for breeding programs aimed at developing high-quality germplasm.

Drawing on the organ-specific metabolic profiles and functional differentiation patterns described above, we propose a targeted strategy to increase the high-value utilization of the whole Z. jujuba var. spinosa plant. Leaves, which have typically been discarded as byproducts of seed processing, are rich in flavonoids, phenolic acids, and saponins and show strong in vitro antioxidant activity; therefore, they can be valorized as functional tea substitutes, natural antioxidant additives, or dietary supplements [13, 42]. During industrial development, establishing quality-control standards that focus on total flavonoids and rutin will help maximize retention of these active constituents. The pulp remains well suited to the health-food market: processing methods should be optimized according to its sugar–acid profile, and combining pulp with leaf-derived flavonoid extracts can yield composite products that pair desirable flavor with enhanced functionality, thereby increasing product value [43, 44]. For the plant’s traditional medicinal component—the seeds—efforts should continue to standardize cultivation and processing and to develop high-precision medicinal and functional formulations using spinosin and jujuboside A as core quality markers. Implementing this organ-specific, metabolism-driven development model can substantially reduce resource waste in the Z. jujuba var. spinosa industry and markedly improve whole-plant utilization efficiency and overall industrial returns.

This study used samples dried at a low temperature of 50 °C for quantification of basic active components, in vitro antioxidant assays, and HPLC targeted validation to reflect industrial processing conditions. However, ex vivo processing and drying can affect some thermosensitive metabolites and therefore do not fully represent the in vivo metabolic state. The LC–MS–based, widely targeted metabolomics platform employed here did not capture volatile metabolites or the complete primary metabolome; future studies should integrate multiple analytical platforms to achieve a more comprehensive characterization of the full metabolic profile of Z. jujuba var. spinosa. Finally, this work described organ-specific metabolite accumulation patterns and enriched pathways but did not examine the underlying molecular regulatory mechanisms. Subsequent research could combine multi-omics approaches to identify regulatory genes and thereby provide more complete theoretical support for breeding high-quality Z. jujuba var. spinosa germplasm.

Conclusions

This study systematically characterized the metabolic profiles of three major organs—leaves, pulp, and seeds—of Z. jujuba var. spinosa by combining basic chemical component assays, in vitro antioxidant tests, widely targeted metabolomics, and HPLC-based targeted validation. The analyses revealed pronounced metabolic differentiation among the organs. Leaves were enriched in flavonoids, phenolic acids, and triterpene saponins and exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity, consistent with their role in defending against photooxidative and biotic stress. Pulp contained significantly higher levels of soluble sugars and organic acids, which likely form the flavor basis that attracts seed dispersers. Seeds accumulated C-glycosylated flavonoids (for example, spinosin) and dammarane-type triterpene saponins (for example, jujuboside A and B), reflecting an integrated strategy of nutrient storage coupled with chemical defense. Together, these results revealed the distinct material values of the different Z. jujuba var. spinosa organs and provided a scientific foundation for moving from single-part use toward whole-plant resource exploitation. It should be noted that this study focused on characterizing metabolite accumulation patterns; the molecular regulatory mechanisms underlying the associated metabolic pathways remain to be investigated.
