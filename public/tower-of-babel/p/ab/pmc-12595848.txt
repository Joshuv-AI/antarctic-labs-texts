Daytime napping and nighttime sleep and motoric cognitive risk syndrome among Chinese older adults: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Jingxian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSleep is necessary for survival, and daytime napping is a common habit for health. However, associations between nighttime sleeping and daytime napping with motoric cognitive risk syndrome (MCR) have not been thoroughly explored, especially in less developed countries. This study aims to examine cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between nighttime sleep duration and daytime napping with the risk of MCR among older Chinese people.MethodsA cohort of 4332 individuals who were 60 or older was recruited from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and followed up from 2011 to 2015. Self-reported questionnaires were used to evaluate nighttime sleep and daytime napping variables. MCR was subjective cognitive complaints and objective slow gait speed without mobility disability or dementia. The Cox proportional hazard model was used to estimate the longitudinal associations between sleep-related variables and incident MCR. The multivariable logistic regression model was used to examine the cross-sectional associations.ResultsA total of 695 individuals developed MCR during follow-up years. Compared to moderate sleep (6–8 h/night) duration, short sleep (< 6 h/night) duration and long sleep (>8 h/night) durations increased the risk of incident MCR (< 6 h HR = 1.40, 95% CI = 1.19–1.64; >8 h HR = 1.39, 95% CI = 1.10–1.75). Moreover, in the adjusted logistic models, a cross-sectional correlation was observed between daytime napping and MCR risk (30–89 min OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.57–0.85; >=90 min OR = 0.74, 95% CI = 0.58–0.95), but there was no significant longitudinal association.ConclusionsShort or long nighttime sleep duration(< 6 h/night or ＞ 8 h/night) increased the risk of incident MCR in older people in China. Introduction

There are currently over 55 million people in the world who have dementia, and about 10 million new cases every year [1]. Dementia now ranks as the seventh main cause of death globally [1]. Motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome is subjective cognitive complaints and objective slow gait without dementia or any mobility disability [2]. Evidence shows that MCR is a predementia syndrome, and the diagnosis of MCR is suggested as an indicator to screen for dementia [3, 4]. Appropriate intervention measures, such as cognitive training exercises, help delay or prevent the progression of MCR [5]. Dementia lacks effective treatment methods [6]. Therefore, identifying the risk factors of MCR syndrome and taking early intervention is important. This helps to lower the occurrence of dementia and lessen the rising burden of dementia on both an individual and a social level.

Sleep is essential for survival, and it can maintain homeostasis and physiological function [7]. The National Sleep Foundation in the USA suggests that people over 65 are supposed to sleep seven to eight hours per night [8]. However, a study observed that 49.7% of older people in China over 65 only had a short sleep duration (< 7 h/night) [9]. Compared with young people, older people slept less at night and had more sleep disorders [10]. It is necessary to focus on the sleep of old people. Many studies found that nighttime sleep duration was related to cognitive function or dementia [11–15]. For example, a national research study found that sleeping more than 10 h per night decreased cognitive function in older people aged over 60 years [15], and another longitudinal research study found an increased risk of late-onset dementia among people who slept less than 6 h at age 50 and 60 [11]. However, studies on the association between MCR and nighttime sleep duration are limited in developing countries, especially in China, and there is even less evidence of longitudinal associations. Therefore, related epidemiological studies are required.

Daytime napping can be a common and healthy habit [16]. Research showed that the number of people who had napping habits in Southeast Asia, South America, and the Middle East ranged from 30% to 70% [17]. Another study reported that the proportion of daytime napping increased with age in China, with 46.8% in females aged 60 or older and 61.7% in males aged 60 or older [18]. Although daytime napping is beneficial in alleviating fatigue, there are still gaps in the research on the impacts of daytime napping on human health. Previous research reported the relationships between cognitive function and daytime napping among older people [19–22], but the findings were not consistent. Some studies revealed that napping lasting more than one hour was beneficial for cognitive function [21, 22]. On the contrary, others showed that daytime napping for over 60 min had a negative correlation with cognitive function [19, 20]. A cross-sectional study further investigated the relationship between naps and MCR in China [23]. However, there is no longitudinal study on the relationship between daytime napping and incident MCR.

Therefore, this study primarily aims to evaluate the longitudinal and cross-sectional relationship between nighttime sleep duration and the risk of MCR in older adults in China, based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). In addition, we also explore the cross-sectional and longitudinal relationship between daytime napping and the risk of MCR.

Methods

Study population

CHARLS was a national study, and it was conducted in 150 counties and 450 communities throughout 28 provinces in China every 2 or 3 years [24]. Based on the multi-stage, stratified, probability proportional to size (PPS) random sampling method, the survey collected detailed microdata of individuals at least 45 years old [25]. In the Wave 1 (2011) survey, 17,708 individuals in 10,257 households participated. Participation rates in Wave 1 (2011), Wave 2 (2013) and Wave 3 (2015) were 80.51%, 87.15% and 82.63%, respectively. We utilized the CHARLS Wave 1 (2011), Wave 2 (2013) and Wave 3 (2015) surveys in this study [26]. The Ethics Review Committee of Peking University approved CHARLS (IRB00001052–11015). The official website provided detailed information (https://charls.pku.edu.cn/en/).

We excluded 10,036 individuals aged < 60 years, 1796 participants without data on important covariates, 669 participants missing data on MCR, nighttime sleeping duration or daytime napping, and 284 participants with memory-related diseases (i.e., Alzheimer’s disease, brain atrophy, and Parkinson’s disease) or physical disability, and 4,923 participants remained in the baseline survey. After further excluding 1,488 participants with missing MCR data during the follow-up and 668 participants with MCR at the baseline survey, and including new eligible participants in Wave 2, and 4,332 individuals were eventually included in the longitudinal research. In our study, the participation rates at baseline and final were 86.43% and 74.95%, respectively. Figure 1 shows the flow chart.

Main variables

Assessment of MCR

MCR was described as subjective cognitive complaints and slow gait speed without mobility disability or dementia among older people [2]. The question “How do you assess your memory right now?” was used to evaluate subjective cognitive complaints. The answer was based on a five-point scale (poor, fair, good, very good, and excellent). Participants who answered ‘poor’ or ‘fair’ were categorized as having subjective cognitive complaints [27, 28]. Gait speed was calculated by measuring the average time for participants to walk twice on a 2.5-meter-long straight path. Slow gait was defined as walking speed lower than one standard deviation below the means of the speed stratified by age and sex [29]. The exclusion of dementia was based on the question “Have you been diagnosed with the following chronic disease by a doctor?” and the answer “memory-related disease (i.e., Alzheimer’s disease, brain atrophy, and Parkinson’s disease)”. The exclusion of mobility disability was based on the question “Do you have one of the following disabilities?” and the answer “physical disabilities”. Supplementary Table 1 shows the cutoff scores of slow gait speed.

Assessment of sleep variables

The question “How many hours did you actually sleep at night in the past month?” was used to assess the nighttime sleep duration variable. According to the previous studies [30, 31], the nighttime sleep duration variable was classified into three groups: < 6 h, 6–8 h and >8 h. Daytime napping data was obtained from the question “How long (minutes) did you take a nap after lunch in the past month?” and respondents were classified into three groups: <30 min, 30–89 min and ≥ 90 min [32, 33].

Assessment of covariates

In this study, sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral factors, and health status were included as covariates. Sociodemographic variables included gender, age, educational level, marital status and residential region. Behavioral factors included alcohol consumption status and smoking status. Health status included self-reported chronic disease and body mass index (BMI). The self-reported chronic diseases included dyslipidemia, cancer, liver disease, diabetes, chronic lung disease, hypertension, heart attack or other heart problems, kidney disease, stroke, psychiatric problems, stomach disease, asthma, and arthritis or rheumatism. The participants were considered to have chronic disease if they suffered from any one of the above diseases.

Statistical analysis

The characteristics of participants included continuous and categorical variables. Mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to describe continuous variables and frequency with percentages was used to describe categorical variables. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the cross-sectional associations between nighttime sleep duration and daytime napping with MCR risk. The results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Cox proportional hazard models were used to assess the longitudinal associations between nighttime sleep duration and daytime napping with incident MCR risk during the follow-up. The results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). <30 min nap was as daytime napping duration reference, and 6–8 h/night was as nighttime sleep duration reference. We also performed the linear trend tests by entering dummy variables of daytime napping and nighttime sleep duration in the Cox models and each dummy variable as a single continuous variable in the models [34].

Additionally, we employed the Restricted Cubic Spline (RCS) with four knots to evaluate the dose-response associations of sleep variables with incident MCR on a continuous scale [35], and sensitivity analysis. We also performed subgroup analyses by characteristics, health status factors and behavioral factors. All sub-analyses were adjusted for covariates. BMI was divided into two groups (< 24.0 kg/m2 or ≥ 24.0 kg/m2) in subgroup analysis based on the cutoff of the overweight standard (24.0 kg/m2) for adults in China [36]. In this study, we applied SPSS 23.0 and R version 4.4.1 for analysis. P value < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

The average follow-up duration for all participants in this study was 2.26 years. Among them, individuals without incident MCR had an average follow-up of 2.17 years, while those with incident MCR had an average follow-up of 3.38 years. Among the 4,923 individuals at baseline, the mean age was 67.73 ± 6.45 years, and 2,463 (50.03%) were male. In addition, 4,332 individuals were included in longitudinal analysis, of whom the average age was 69.39 ± 5.83 years, and 2,239 (51.69%) were male. A total of 695 individuals had incident MCR, in which 339 (48.78%) participants napped less than 30 min, 323(46.47%) participants slept 6–8 h every day, more than three-quarters (84.46%) of the participants had chronic disease, 295 (42.45%) participants were alcohol drinkers, and almost a half (46.62%) were smokers. Cross-sectional association between sleep and MCR

Table 2 shows the cross-sectional associations of daytime napping and nighttime sleep duration with the risk of MCR. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that short nighttime sleep duration (< 6 h/night) was linked to MCR risk (OR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.01,1.43), compared with moderate sleep (6–8 h/night). The results also showed that the risks of MCR for those who napped for 30–89 min and more than 90 min per day decreased, with ORs (95% CIs) of 0.70 (0.57, 0.85) and 0.74 (0.58, 0.95), respectively, when compared to those napping for less than 30 min.

Longitudinal association between sleep and incident MCR

Table 3 shows the longitudinal associations of daytime napping and nighttime sleep duration with the risk of incident MCR. The multi-adjusted models revealed higher risks of incident MCR in older people who slept less than 6 h or more than 8 h every night compared to the reference group of 6–8 h every night. The HRs (95% CIs) were 1.40(1.19,1.64) and 1.39(1.10,1.75), respectively. However, longitudinal associations between daytime napping and incident MCR across 3 Cox regression models were not significant. In addition, we performed the trend analysis on associations of nighttime sleep duration and daytime napping with incident MCR, indicating that the linear trends were not significant. We also performed the sensitivity analysis on longitudinal associations between nighttime sleep duration and incident MCR with sleep as a four-category variable, and the results support our findings. Subgroup analyses

Table 4 presents a comparison of the subgroup-specific relationships between nighttime sleep duration and incident MCR, divided by sociodemographic characteristics, health status factors and behavioral factors. We found that incident MCR risk was higher when nighttime sleep duration was less than 6 h and more than 8 h per night among females (< 6 h HR = 1.35, 95% CI = 1.09–1.69; >8 h HR = 1.56, 95% CI = 1.14–2.14), older people with no formal education or primary school education (< 6 h HR = 1.44, 95% CI = 1.21–1.71; >8 h HR = 1.52, 95% CI = 1.20–1.93), rural people (< 6 h HR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.22–1.73; >8 h HR = 1.51, 95% CI = 1.18–1.93), married people (< 6 h HR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.17–1.70; >8 h HR = 1.43, 95% CI = 1.09–1.88), older people without alcohol consumption(< 6 h HR = 1.41, 95% CI = 1.14–1.75; >8 h HR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.14–2.06), nappers for less than 30 min (< 6 h HR = 1.53, 95% CI = 1.22–1.92; >8 h HR = 1.45, 95% CI = 1.03–2.04), older people whose BMI less than 24 kg/m2 (< 6 h HR = 1.55, 95% CI = 1.27–1.89; >8 h HR = 1.33, 95% CI = 0.99–1.80), and older people without chronic disease (< 6 h HR = 2.09, 95% CI = 1.37–3.19; >8 h HR = 2.46, 95% CI = 1.45–4.20). In addition, we did not find an interaction role of daytime napping and nighttime sleep duration on incident MCR (P = 0.883).

Discussion

This study examined the longitudinal and cross-sectional associations between nighttime sleep duration and daytime napping with MCR among the Chinese older people. We found that older people who slept less than 6 h or more than 8 h had an increased risk of incident MCR, and a U-shaped relationship was observed between nighttime sleep duration and incident MCR risk. In addition, although daytime napping > 30 min showed a cross-sectional association with decreased MCR risk in older adults, no longitudinal association was observed.

In this large cohort research, we reported that sleeping less than 6 h and more than 8 h had an association with increased incident MCR risk compared with 6–8 h. Additionally, we also observed that the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and incident MCR risk exhibited a U-shaped curve. This conclusion was similar to a cross-sectional study [37], which discovered the risk of MCR was high for people who had long (≥ 10 h) or short (< 6 h) sleep duration, and the relationship between MCR and nighttime sleep duration represented a U-shaped curve. Studies on nighttime sleep duration and MCR are limited, and more studies focus on the associations of nighttime sleep duration and dementia or cognitive function. Some epidemiological studies in the Americas and Asia are in concordance with our results. A cohort study of Puerto Rican older people investigated that the curve on the relationship between sleep duration and cognitive decline was U-shaped, indicating that cognitive decline is related to long or short sleep duration [38]. Another cohort study in Japan also observed that the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and dementia incidence was a U-shaped curve among older people [39]. These studies have analyzed the relationship between nighttime sleep duration and cognitive function or dementia, which supports the findings of our study.

From a pathological perspective, both inadequate sleep and excessive sleep duration may promote inflammation process, increase circulating markers of inflammation, such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), thereby damage brain neurons, and could contribute to promoting neurodegeneration, which may adversely affect cognitive and motor functions [40, 41], potentially increasing the risk of MCR.

We found that the risk of MCR among older people who took a nap for more than 30 min a day was lower than older people who took a nap for less than 30 min in the cross-sectional analysis. A cross-sectional study reported that people who took a nap for 30–89 min each day had a lower risk of MCR, compared with non-nappers, which was similar to our study [23]. This association may be explained by the fact that people with daytime napping more than 30 min are more likely to enter slow-wave sleep (SWS), and SWS facilitates the formation of long-term memory through the synergistic mechanisms of slow oscillations and spindle activity, which may potentially delay cognitive decline [42]. This mechanism might help to reduce the risk of MCR. However, although we have analyzed the potential physiological mechanisms underlying the association between longer naps and lower MCR risk based on previous studies, our epidemiological analysis did not find a longitudinal causal association between daytime napping with incident MCR. And the results of longitudinal studies on the relationship between napping and MCR-related outcomes, such as cognitive function, were different. A 10-year-long longitudinal research in Britain reported that the risk of cognitive impairment was lower no matter how long napping duration among older people [21]. Another longitudinal study followed up for 5 years in Japan revealed that people who took a nap for less than 30 min had a lower risk of cognitive decline among older adults [43]. A study investigated older males in the community enrolled at six clinical centres in the USA, and it found that men with longer napping duration increased the risk of cognitive impairment. Specifically, compared to men who took a nap of less than 30 min, men with a nap duration of over 120 min were 66% more likely to develop cognitive impairment [44]. These different conclusions may be because of the variations in participants, data collection of daytime napping (timing or duration of napping), evaluations of dementia or cognitive function, and selection of covariates [45].

The discrepancy between the longitudinal and cross-sectional findings regarding the relationship between napping and MCR may be attributed to the following reason: in the cross-sectional study, daytime napping relied on self-reported data and was measured only once, which may overestimate the true association. Additionally, participants’ napping habits might change over time. Thus, even if the cross-sectional results showed the association between napping and MCR, the longitudinal study, being more sensitive to measurement errors and changes, did not show similar results.

The research has several potential limitations. Firstly, data on sleep variables came from the self-reported questionnaire, but such a method inevitably led to recall bias. The objective evaluation of sleep variables by polysomnography and actigraphy is suggested to be used in future research [46, 47]. Secondly, the measurement of cognitive complaints in MCR relied on a single question in our study, which may cause some bias. Future studies should adopt more comprehensive methods such as multi-item assessments [48]. Thirdly, 1,448 participants were lost to follow-up due to the missing MCR data, which led to a shorter follow-up duration in the non-MCR group and the overall cohort, and potentially caused bias and affected the findings to some extent. To evaluate the potential influence of bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses, such as reanalysis with sleep as a four-category variable and further analysis with restricted cubic spline, to validate the robustness of our findings. However, one should be cautious when the findings of this study are extended to other people. Fourthly, our cohort study showed that nighttime sleep durations (<6 h or >8) were associated with an increased risk of incident MCR, and sensitivity analyses supported the association. Nevertheless, these findings may still be influenced by reverse causation—that is, as predementia syndrome, MCR-related early neurodegenerative changes may lead to sleep disturbances, rather than sleep problems directly causing MCR. Future studies should employ methods such as Mendelian randomization and randomized controlled trials to further validate the causal relationship between nighttime sleep duration and incident MCR.

However, the research has several strengths. Firstly, this study used CHARLS, which is a national survey of older Chinese people in the community, and has a good representation of the research population. In addition, the study performed a longitudinal analysis, which made it possible to examine potential temporal associations of sleep variables on the risk of incident MCR among older people in China.

Conclusions

This cohort research reported that short (< 6 h/day) and long (>8 h/day) durations of nighttime increased the risk of incident MCR among older Chinese people. Additionally, we also found that long napping (> 30 min/day) duration had a cross-sectional association with MCR risk. The research may provide some meaningful suggestions for guiding the sleep behavior of older people in China. Interventions on optimal nighttime sleep duration and napping are suggested to reduce the risk of incident MCR among older people.
