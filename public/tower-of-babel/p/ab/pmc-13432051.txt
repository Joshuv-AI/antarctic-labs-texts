46,X,+mar(Y) as a Rare Etiology of Non-obstructive Male Infertility
Authors: Kumari Pritti, Rohina Aggarwal, Devanshi Dalal, Hetvi Patel, Dipak Dhoriya, Ankita Suthar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMarker chromosomes derived from the Y chromosome are rare and can present with either disorders of sex development (DSD) or infertility. We assessed a unique case of a 38-year-old Indian male who presented with primary infertility and azoospermia. Cytogenetic analysis demonstrated a karyotype of 46,X,+mar, and further fluorescence in situ hybridisation studies confirmed the marker to be of Y chromosomal origin. Microarray revealed an interstitial deletion of 15.7 Mb spanning Yq11.1–q11.23 with preservation of the Yp arm. Multiplex polymerase chain reaction showed complete loss of the azoospermia factor (AZF) a, AZFb and AZFc regions. Based on integrated cytogenetic and molecular findings, the final karyotype was revised to 46,X,der(Y)del(Y)(q11.1q11.23). Interestingly, the marker chromosome retained the sex-determining region Y (SRY) gene, explaining the development of a male phenotype, but the absence of the AZF loci accounted for the failure of spermatogenesis. This case highlights the importance of integrated cytogenetic and molecular approaches in evaluating unexplained male infertility and represents a rare example of SRY-positive testicular DSD associated with a Y-derived marker chromosome lacking critical spermatogenic regions.

INTRODUCTION

Infertility affects approximately 15% of couples worldwide, with male factors accounting for nearly half of the cases. Among these, chromosomal anomalies – particularly those involving the Y chromosome – are well-established contributors to impaired spermatogenesis. Sex chromosome abnormalities, including Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY), represent well-established genetic causes of male infertility, while structural variants of the Y chromosome, such as marker chromosomes, are increasingly recognised as rare contributors.[12]

A marker chromosome is a small, structurally abnormal chromosomal fragment that appears during karyotyping but cannot be clearly identified using standard cytogenetic techniques. The associated phenotypic impact depends on inheritance, mode of ascertainment, chromosomal origin and the marker’s morphology, content and structure. When derived from the Y chromosome, these markers can vary widely in size and composition – potentially including or excluding key functional regions such as the sex-determining region Y (SRY) on Yp11.3 and the azoospermia factor (AZF) regions on Yq11. The AZFa, AZFb and AZFc regions harbor several genes essential for spermatogenesis.[34]

Importantly, not all Y-derived marker chromosomes confer the same phenotypic outcome. The presence of SRY may result in male external genitalia, but the absence of AZF regions often leads to testicular dysfunction and non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA). The phenotype can further vary depending on marker size and mosaicism level. Thus, combining cytogenetic and molecular biology techniques provides comprehensive genomic analysis, essential for effective genetic counseling.[5]

Here, we report a rare case of a 38-year-old phenotypic male with an initial karyotype of 46,X,+mar, which was subsequently characterised as a Y-derived derivative chromosome. Molecular analyses demonstrated retention of the SRY gene with complete loss of the Yq euchromatic region, including all AZF loci, resulting in spermatogenic failure.

CASE REPORT

A 38-year-old Indian male presented with a 2-year history of primary infertility. There was no contributory personal or family history of reproductive or genetic disorders. He had a normal male phenotype with well-developed external genitalia, appropriate secondary sexual characteristics and no gynecomastia. His anthropometric evaluation revealed short stature, with a height of 152 cm and a weight of 48 kg.

Scrotal ultrasonography showed bilateral testes of near-normal size (right: 45 mm × 20 mm; left: 43 mm × 20 mm). Semen analysis, repeated twice, demonstrated azoospermia. Hormonal evaluation revealed elevated serum FSH (36.26 mIU/mL), normal luteinising hormone (LH) (5.95 mIU/mL) and testosterone in the lower normal range (17.7 nmol/L), consistent with hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. DISCUSSION

The AZF regions on the Y chromosome play a crucial role in normal spermatogenesis, and their deletion is a well-established cause of male infertility. In our patient, there was a complete loss of the Y chromosome’s long arm (Yq), which encompasses the AZFa, AZFb and AZFc regions. These regions are known to contain key genes involved in germ cell development and maturation. Notably, genes such as DAZ, RBMY and USP9Y, located within the Yq11 region, are essential for spermatogenesis, and their deletion is associated with severely impaired or absent sperm production.[6] The complete deletion of these regions, as confirmed by microarray and multiplex polymerase chain reaction analysis in our case, accounts for the patient’s NOA despite the presence of normal male secondary sexual characteristics.

The presence of a male phenotype is explained by the detection of the SRY gene, which is located on the short arm of the Y chromosome (Yp11.3). According to Colaco and Modi, the Yp arm contains genes vital for male sex differentiation, including SRY, which triggers testis development and male phenotypic features, while the Yq arm is predominantly involved in spermatogenesis.[7] Our patient’s intact Yp region resulted in normal external genitalia and secondary sexual characteristics, while deletion of the Yq arm led to infertility – emphasising the functional compartmentalisation of the Y chromosome.

Moreover, Colaco and Modi also highlighted that SRY gene mutations are seen in approximately 15% of 46,XY females (Swyer syndrome) and that SRY translocation to the X chromosome results in some 46,XX males with a male phenotype but infertility. Although it has been proposed that ZFY, a gene located on the Yp arm, may contribute to spermatogenesis, particularly in meiotic division and sperm formation, this appears inconsistent with our findings.[7] These findings further support the concept that SRY is sufficient for testis determination but not for spermatogenesis, which depends on intact AZF regions on Yq.

Supporting evidence from Vogt et al. and Hopps et al. shows that Y chromosome abnormalities with deletion of Yq and AZF regions consistently result in azoospermia, even when SRY is retained.[68] In contrast, partial deletions, especially of AZFc, have been reported in some patients with residual spermatogenesis and potential for sperm retrieval,[9] a feature not seen in our patient with complete AZFa/b/c deletions.

Endocrine evaluation revealed elevated FSH with normal LH and testosterone, indicating primary testicular failure, such as Sertoli cell-only syndrome or testicular maturation arrest, both of which are commonly associated with AZF deletions.[1011] Normal testosterone levels suggest preserved Leydig cell function, consistent with a functioning SRY pathway but absent germinal epithelium.

Overall, this case highlights the functional compartmentalisation of the Y chromosome, where the Yp segment is essential for male phenotypic development, whereas the Yq segment is indispensable for spermatogenesis and male fertility.

CONCLUSION

This case illustrates the diagnostic value of combining cytogenetic and molecular tools to evaluate male infertility associated with a marker chromosome. Identification of the SRY gene alongside complete AZF deletions on a Y-derived marker chromosome explained the azoospermia in this otherwise phenotypically normal male. This case represents a rare presentation of a Y-derived derivative chromosome with complete AZF deletion associated with NOA. Genetic counseling is essential in such cases, particularly regarding the prospects of biological paternity and reproductive options.
