Investigation of the Incidence, Risk Factors and Characteristics of Respiratory Device‐Related Pressure Injuries
Authors: Sevda Sönmez, Buğse Böleç, Zilan Baran, Dilek Özden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study aims to investigate the incidence of respiratory device‐related pressure injuries (RDRPI) in patients hospitalised in chest disease clinics, as well as the associated risk factors and wound characteristics. This prospective, descriptive and cross‐sectional study was conducted at a chest disease hospital in a province in western Turkey between February and July 2024. A total of 225 patients who were hospitalised for 24 h or more and used respiratory devices in the chest diseases clinic of the hospital were included in the study. It was found that 32.9% of the patients developed RDRPIs. The study identified that prolonged hospitalisation, low albumin and haemoglobin levels and inappropriate sizing of the respiratory device were associated with an increased risk of developing these injuries. The study found a high incidence of RDRPIs. It is recommended that high‐quality evidence‐based studies be conducted on the prevention and reduction of these injuries in hospitalised patients, and that institutional strategies be developed to address this issue by targeting modifiable risk factors such as device fit and nutritional status.Keywords: medical device safety, nursing care, pressure injury, respiratory device

Introduction

Pressure injuries (PIs) have been defined as a long‐standing issue in healthcare [1]. The prevalence and incidence of PIs are indicators of the quality of care [2].

The National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel (NPIAP) defined medical device‐related pressure injuries (MDRPIs) in 2016 as PIs resulting from the use of medical devices for diagnostic or therapeutic purposes, where the shape of the injury is consistent with the medical device [3]. Unlike general PIs, which develop as a result of friction, pressure or shear due to body weight and immobility, the cause of pressure in MDRPIs is attributed to the use of medical devices [4].

Medical devices are widely used in the treatment of various diseases and in the care and monitoring of patients, leading to an increasing prevalence of MDRPIs [5]. These injuries differ from traditional PIs in that they affect both the skin and mucous membranes and are associated with advancements in therapeutic device use [6]. Medical devices made from synthetic materials such as plastic create a localised environment with increased heat and moisture, altering the microclimate of the area and reducing tissue tolerance [7]. Additionally, the application of pressure on areas with low adipose tissue and the use of fixation devices increases the likelihood of elevated pressure in oedematous areas that are in contact with a medical device, further raising the risk of MDRPI development [3, 8].

The prevalence and incidence of MDRPIs vary according to patient population characteristics, hospital settings and the types of medical devices used. A meta‐analysis conducted by Zhang et al. [9] reported an incidence of 19.3%, while Crunden et al. [10] highlighted significant inter‐institutional differences influenced by patient population profiles. In a study conducted by Coyer et al. [11] with intensive care unit (ICU) patients, at least one hospital‐acquired MDRPI developed, with a prevalence of 11.3% [11]. The highest prevalence, at 25%, was associated with PIs caused by continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or bilevel positive airway pressure (BPAP) masks [12]. In a study by Rashvand et al. [13], among the nine medical devices that caused PIs, the most common were nasal oxygen, oxygen face masks and endotracheal tubes [13]. In studies examining MDRPIs in adult patients in ICUs, non‐invasive ventilation (NIV) masks were identified as the devices most frequently associated with PIs [13, 14, 15].

Although studies on MDRPIs have increased internationally in recent years, a review of the literature reveals that most of these studies focus on ICU patients ([13, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22]). Studies specifically addressing PIs related to respiratory devices are limited [7, 23, 24, 25, 26]. Upon reviewing these studies, no research was found specifically involving respiratory devices used in the respiratory system or conducted with patients hospitalised in chest diseases clinics. Therefore, there is a need for studies aimed at determining the incidence of respiratory device‐related pressure injuries (RDRPIs) in patients admitted to chest disease clinics, where NIV masks are frequently used, especially in clinical settings outside of ICUs.

Aim

This study aims to investigate the incidence, risk factors and characteristics of PIs associated with the use of respiratory devices in hospitalised patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

The study is of a prospective, descriptive and cross‐sectional design.

Sample and Setting

The study population consisted of patients hospitalised between February and July 2024 at a chest diseases hospital located in the western region of Turkey. The hospital has a total of 211 beds, including ICUs, chest diseases and chest surgery clinics. The study was conducted in a chest diseases clinic with 18 beds. As this is an incidence study, all patients who met the inclusion criteria were included, without random sampling. A total of 225 patients who were hospitalised for 24 h or more and used respiratory devices in the chest diseases clinic were included in the study. Patients who did not consent to participate, were hospitalised for less than 24 h, did not use respiratory devices, had pre‐existing PIs related to respiratory devices upon admission, or whose follow‐up was interrupted due to emergency interventions (e.g., cardiac arrest, ICU transfer) were excluded from the study.

Data Collection Tools

The data collection tools used were the ‘Patient Information Form’, the ‘Respiratory Device‐Related Pressure Injury Observation Form’ and the ‘Braden Pressure Injury Risk Assessment Scale’.

Patient Information Form

This form was developed by the researchers based on a review of the literature and consists of 20 questions regarding the patients' age, gender, body mass index (BMI), length of hospitalisation, admission diagnosis, smoking status, comorbidities, nutritional status, antibiotic use, oedema status, laboratory values (albumin, haemoglobin, haematocrit, CRP levels) and vital signs (temperature, heart rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation). After the form was prepared, it was evaluated by consulting five experts, including two wound care nurses and three faculty members. The patient information form was completed using laboratory findings retrieved from the medical records.

Respiratory Device‐Related Pressure Injury Observation Form

The form, developed by the researchers based on a review of the literature, consists of two sections.

The first section is the ‘Daily Monitoring Form’ for all patients using respiratory devices, which includes questions about the monitoring day, the type of respiratory device used, frequency of use, whether rotation is performed with the device, and nursing interventions applied to prevent injuries.

The second section is the ‘Medical Device‐Related Pressure Injury Record Form’, which includes the record of the PIs caused by the respiratory device, the day the injury occurred, all characteristics of the injury, the facial area where the injury developed and the nursing interventions applied [17, 27, 28, 29]. After the form was prepared, it was evaluated by consulting five experts, including two wound care nurses and three faculty members.

Braden Pressure Injury Risk Assessment Scale

Developed by Braden and Bergstrom [30], this scale was validated and tested for reliability in Turkish by Oğuz and Olgun [31].

The Braden Pressure Injury Risk Assessment Scale evaluates risk across six subscales: sensory perception, activity, mobility, moisture, nutrition and friction/shear. The subscale scores range from 1 to 3 for friction/shear, while the remaining five subscales are scored between 1 and 4, with the total score ranging from 6 to 23. As the score on the scale decreases, the risk of PI development increases. Risk categories were classified as follows: no risk (19–23), mild risk (15–18), moderate risk (13–14), high risk (10–12) and very high risk (≤ 9) [30]. The scale was administered daily by the researcher to all patients during hospitalisation.

Implementation of the Study

The data were collected by the second researcher, a doctoral student who worked as the charge nurse in the chest diseases clinic where the study was conducted and held a wound care course certificate. The researcher observed all patients who met the inclusion criteria daily between 08:00 and 12:00 during their hospitalisation and respiratory device use.

The researcher filled out the data collection forms while monitoring the skin under and around the respiratory device for signs of injury. All data have been recorded in the forms. The data were transferred from paper‐based forms to Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software by the researcher.

To classify the PI stages in patients with MDRPIs, the researcher used the NPIAP classification criteria valid at the time of the study (2019 version). Mucosal membrane pressure injuries (MMPIs) were evaluated separately, as they are not staged according to NPIAP guidelines.

Statistical Analysis

The collected data were analysed using the SPSS version 24.0. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation were used. The normality of the data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test.

For comparing variables that followed a normal distribution, the independent samples T‐test was used; for variables that did not follow a normal distribution:

Categorical variables were analysed using the Chi‐square test.

Categorical variables were analysed using the Chi‐square test.

Continuous variables were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Continuous variables were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Binary logistic regression was performed using PI occurrence as the dependent variable. Independent variables included age, BMI, Braden score, length of hospital stay and type of respiratory device.

The incidence of PIs was calculated as the number of new PI cases divided by the total number of patients at risk during the study period, multiplied by 100.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical approval for conducting the study was obtained from the Non‐Interventional Ethics Committee of X University, and the necessary written permissions were obtained from the institution where the study was conducted. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

It was found that 46.2% of these patients were followed up with a diagnosis of lung cancer, and 66.7% had comorbidities. When examining the average laboratory values of the patients, the albumin level was 34.2 ± 10.6 (min: 11), haematocrit level 36.2 ± 6.3 (min: 16), haemoglobin level 11.9 ± 2.2 (min: 8), CRP level 66.4 ± 60.3 (min: 14) and the Braden Pressure Injury Risk Score was 19.2 ± 2.1 (min: 9) (Table 1).

It was determined that 54.7% of the patients used nasal cannulas and nebuliser masks, 62.2% of them did not use the correct size device, 16.4% had dry skin, 41.8% applied rotation to the respiratory device they used, and 79.1% did not receive protective interventions to prevent injuries. It was found that 32.9% of the patients followed up with respiratory devices developed PIs (Table 1).

Characteristics of Respiratory Device‐Related Pressure Injuries and Nursing Care

It was determined that the PIs in patients using respiratory devices (n: 74) occurred on average on the 16.3 ± 8.3 days of hospitalisation. The injuries occurred in the under‐eye region in 12.9% of the patients; 23.1% were Stage 1, the average wound size was 2.6 ± 0.6 cm in width and 3.4 ± 1.2 cm in length. In 22.2% of the cases, saline solution was used for wound cleaning; 10.7% of the injuries were treated with barrier cream; and in 24.9% of the cases, no intervention was applied to the wound area. Rotation was applied to 12% of the respiratory devices, and 32.4% of the injuries were not documented in the patients' medical records but were identified by the research team during data collection (Table 2).

Regression Analysis of Clinical Characteristics and Respiratory Device‐Related Pressure Injury Formation

The demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with and without RDRPIs are shown in Table 1. A significant relationship was found between the length of hospital stay and the formation of PIs (p < 0.05). Gender, age, BMI, smoking status, admission diagnosis and number of comorbidities were not significantly associated with PIs (p ≥ 0.05).

According to the findings, a significant difference was found between the patients' nutritional status, albumin, haemoglobin, haematocrit levels, type of respiratory device used, whether the device was of proper size, application of rotation and the use of preventive nursing interventions, and the occurrence of RDRPIs (p < 0.05). There was no significant difference between antibiotic use, CRP level, vital signs, Braden score, oedema status and the development of RDRPIs (p ≥ 0.05).

The regression analysis revealed that longer hospital stays, low albumin and haemoglobin levels, and the inappropriate size of the respiratory device increased the risk of RDRPIs (Table 3).

Regression Model Details

In the logistic regression analysis, the variables found to be statistically significant in the univariate analysis (length of hospital stay, albumin level, haemoglobin level and inappropriate device size) were included in the model.

Prior to performing the regression, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. All variables had VIF values below 2.0, indicating no multicollinearity problem.

The model's goodness‐of‐fit was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and the results indicated that the model was a good fit for the data (p > 0.05).

The effects of variables on the occurrence of PIs were interpreted using odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI).

According to the regression results, longer hospital stay, lower albumin and haemoglobin levels, and use of an inappropriate respiratory device size significantly increased the development of RDRPIs (Table 3).

Discussion

In order to prevent RDRPIs, it is essential to have a clear understanding of the incidence and risk factors of these injuries. Although studies specifically examining respiratory devices are limited, most research has been conducted in ICU settings [7, 23, 24, 25, 26]. In this context, the identification of the incidence of PIs related to respiratory device use, the characteristics of the wounds and the risk factors in patients hospitalised in the chest diseases clinic is considered significant in this study.

When the study findings are examined, it was determined that 32.9% (n = 74) of the 225 patients developed PIs. This rate is somewhat consistent with current studies in the literature, although differences may arise due to variations in patient populations, clinical settings and types of respiratory devices used. In a study conducted by Yalçın and Güneş [20] on ICU patients, it was found that 32.5% of the patients developed RDRPIs, with the most common injuries arising from endotracheal tubes (18.5%) and CPAP masks (15.4%) [20]. When reviewing the literature, it was found that approximately 30% of skin injuries were directly caused by medical devices, with respiratory devices being the main cause of these skin injuries [15, 32, 33]. According to the meta‐analysis by Wei et al. [7], the incidence of PIs on the face in adults using NIV mask devices was 25% [7]. Similarly, in the study by Wu et al. [25], it was reported that in patients receiving non‐invasive positive pressure ventilation (NIPPV), 10.07% of ICU patients developed PIs on their faces [25]. Although the sample in our study consisted of non‐ICU patients, the incidence of PIs in respiratory device users was still high, indicating that all patients using medical devices may be at risk for RDRPIs regardless of care setting.

According to the findings of our study, the most common respiratory devices causing injuries in patients were nasal cannulas and nebuliser masks, accounting for 44% of the cases. Similar to our findings, Karacabay et al. [18] reported that nasal cannulas were the most common cause of PIs in their study [18]. In the study by Temiz et al. [22], it was noted that the devices most frequently causing PIs were endotracheal tubes (61.4%) and oxygen masks (52.6%) [22]. In the study by Bueno et al. [21], it was found that more PIs occurred with ventilation device fixation devices and nasal cannulas [21]. While some studies have focused on invasive respiratory devices, the findings in our study—limited to non‐invasive devices—are in alignment with the general trend of nasal cannulas and oxygen masks contributing significantly to RDRPIs.

Upon examining the study findings, it was determined that the most common areas for PI formation due to respiratory devices were under the eyes (12.9%), on the cheeks (8%) and on the nose (4.4%). The anatomical regions affected by PIs resulting from medical devices vary depending on the type of support devices used [34]. In a study investigating RDRPIs in hospitalised adults, the ventilation support devices causing PIs and the most affected areas were reported as follows: nasal catheters and ears; masks and the nasal bridge; endotracheal tubes and lip commissures and tracheostomy cannulas and the neck [34]. Barakat‐Johnson et al.'s [35] study found that MDRPIs were more frequently observed around the ears when using oxygen cannulas [35]. In Kayser et al.'s [36] study, it was determined that PIs were more commonly seen around the ears and nose [36]. Wei et al. [7] found that the most common location for PIs related to NIV equipment in adults was the nasal bridge (29%) [7]. Another study also found that the most frequent locations for MDRPI were around the nose and the nasal area [37]. Similarly, Wu et al. [25] reported that patients using NIV were more likely to experience PIs around the nose [25]. While our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies, the higher incidence of PIs under the eyes and on the cheeks may be attributed to the combined use of multiple devices (e.g., BPAP, nasal cannulas and nebuliser masks), which may increase pressure and friction in these facial regions.

According to our study findings, when examining the stages of PIs that occurred in the patients, it was found that the majority (70.3%) were classified as Stage I, which aligns with the findings of other studies where Stage I was reported as the most common form of MDRPI [7, 12, 13, 19, 36, 38]. In Dallı et al.'s [15] study, it was reported that all patients using endotracheal tubes experienced mucosal membrane PIs, while other medical devices most frequently caused Stage I PIs on the skin surface. Our study supports these findings, indicating that early‐stage PIs are the most frequent presentation of RDRPIs.

According to the regression analysis conducted in the study, it was determined that an increase in patients' length of hospital stay increased the risk of developing a PI associated with respiratory devices by 1.9 times. Other studies have also reported that prolonged hospital stays and extended use of devices increase the risk of injuries [13, 14, 15, 37, 39]. These findings highlight the need for early and continuous skin assessment for patients with longer hospitalisations to mitigate the risk of RDRPI development.

In our study, it was determined that low serum albumin and haemoglobin levels increased the risk of developing PIs associated with respiratory devices. Similarly, studies have found that the higher the serum albumin level, the lower the risk of PI [40, 41, 42]. Low albumin levels can lead to changes in colloid osmotic pressure and the formation of oedema, which impairs tissue nutrition and increases tissue deformation [43]. On the other hand, high haemoglobin levels improve the oxygen‐carrying capacity of tissues, ensuring adequate tissue nutrition [44]. Thus, ensuring adequate nutritional and haematological status is critical in PI prevention protocols for hospitalised patients.

In our study, when examining the nutritional status, a significant difference was found in the incidence of PIs between patients who were fed orally and those who were parenterally fed. It was observed that the incidence of PIs was much higher in patients with oral intake compared to those fed parenterally. This unexpected result may be related to the possibility that patients fed orally were using respiratory devices more continuously, without regular breaks, whereas parenterally fed patients might have required more clinical interventions, inadvertently leading to more frequent adjustments or removal of the device. Further studies are needed to explore this association in more depth.

In our study, when examining nutritional status, a significant difference was found in the incidence of PIs between patients who were fed orally and those who were fed parenterally. Interestingly, the incidence of PIs was higher in patients with oral intake. This unexpected result warrants further investigation, as oral intake generally requires interruptions in device use, which should theoretically reduce pressure injury risk. Alternative explanations could involve device fit, movement during oral intake or other clinical factors not captured in this study [11] (Gefen et al., NPIAP). Further studies are needed to explore this association in more depth.

In the regression analysis conducted in this study, it was found that improper fitting of respiratory devices increased the incidence of PIs by 2.6 times. It is recommended that respiratory devices be used in sizes and dimensions appropriate for the patient [45]. Therefore, whether the devices are too tight or too loose can be considered a risk factor for the formation of PIs. Additionally, NIV masks are often made from hard materials like plastic or rubber, which can cause friction or pressure on the facial tissue, contributing to a higher incidence of PIs [7]. Thus, device selection and proper fitting should be prioritised as a preventive measure, and alternating between different types of respiratory support, when clinically feasible, may also help reduce pressure and friction injuries.

Unlike many previous studies, our research also evaluated the nursing interventions applied for the prevention and management of RDRPIs. The findings revealed that the rates of preventive measures—such as rotating devices, applying prophylactic dressings or documenting injury development—were remarkably low. This finding provides an explanation for the high incidence of RDRPIs in our study. Guidelines recommend the application of prophylactic dressings on high‐risk sites and frequent repositioning or offloading of the device [45]. Strengthening nursing practices in these areas is vital to effectively reduce the occurrence and severity of RDRPIs.

Conclusion

This study found that 32.9% of patients using respiratory devices developed PIs. The regression analysis revealed that longer hospital stays, low albumin and haemoglobin levels, and the use of improperly sized respiratory devices significantly increased the risk of developing RDRPIs.

These findings highlight the importance of early identification of risk factors and implementation of targeted preventive strategies, especially by nurses who play a key role in the daily care of patients using respiratory devices.

It is recommended that hospitalised patients at risk be monitored more frequently for signs of PIs and that appropriate device selection, regular skin assessments and nutritional optimisation be integrated into routine clinical practice.

Additionally, institutional protocols and training programmes should be developed to standardise the prevention and management of RDRPIs across all units, not limited to intensive care settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single‐centre study conducted in the chest diseases clinic of a specific hospital, the results may not be generalisable to other clinical settings or patient populations.

Second, although respiratory devices were monitored during the day, one of the main limitations was the inability to accurately calculate and examine the duration and positioning of alternating respiratory devices during night shifts.

Third, due to the observational nature of the study, the presence of confounding factors (e.g., staff‐to‐patient ratios, individual nursing practices or other institutional protocols) could not be entirely controlled.

Finally, the study did not include a follow‐up period after hospital discharge, so the long‐term effects or healing processes of the PIs could not be evaluated.
