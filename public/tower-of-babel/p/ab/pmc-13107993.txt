Rod Pathway Dysfunction in Early-Stage Diabetic Retinopathy Assessed by ERG and Pupillometry
Authors: Jason C. Park, Andrew Cross, J. Jason McAnany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo provide insight into rod pathway dysfunction in early-stage diabetic retinopathy (DR) by measuring dark-adapted ERGs and pupillary light reflexes (PLRs) across a broad range of stimulus luminance.MethodsSeventeen diabetics with no clinically apparent DR (NDR), 17 with mild nonproliferative DR (MDR), and 15 nondiabetic controls participated. Dark-adapted, full-field ERGs and PLRs were obtained. Achromatic (−4 to 1 log cd-s-m−2) and long-wavelength (−4.0 to 2.6 log cd/m2) flashes were used for ERG and pupillometry, respectively. The b-wave amplitudes and pupil diameters were fit with Naka-Rushton functions to obtain (1) maximum b-wave amplitude (Vmax), (2) maximum PLR (Pmax), (3) b-wave sensitivity (kb), and (4) PLR sensitivity (kp).ResultsERG a-wave amplitude was reduced (0.13 log µV averaged across stimulus luminance) in DR compared with the controls, but this was not statistically significant (F = 1.41; P = 0.25). ERG Vmax did not significantly differ among groups (F = 2.20, P = 0.12), whereas kb was elevated (reduced sensitivity) for both groups (both t > 2.40; P < 0.02). Pupil Pmax was reduced in MDR (t = 2.83, P = 0.01), but not NDR (t = 0.99, P = 0.33). Pupil kb was significantly elevated in NDR and MDR (both t > 2.1; P < 0.04).ConclusionsReduced b-wave amplitude may largely be accounted for by the reduced a-wave amplitude. By contrast, pupil sensitivity loss greatly exceeded the b-wave sensitivity loss, suggesting sites of abnormality beyond the bipolar cells contribute to pupil response deficits in diabetics.

Although diabetic retinopathy (DR) has historically been considered a disease of the retinal microvasculature, there is emerging consensus that retinal neurodegeneration can accompany, or precede, the clinically apparent vascular changes.1–4 Electrophysiological studies in DR have provided evidence for abnormalities of photoreceptor and postreceptor function.5,6 Neural abnormalities assessed by conventional ERG have been reported in later disease stages, whereas ERG abnormalities are less consistently observed early in the disease time course.5,7,8 There are, however, two exceptions: the oscillatory potentials (OPs) of the flash ERG and the high frequency flicker ERG (greater than approximately 50 Hz) can be abnormal in diabetics who have no clinically apparent retinopathy.6–8 Furthermore, OP abnormalities have been reported to become more severe as the disease progresses (Tzekov and Arden6 provide a review).

Accumulating evidence suggests a fundamental role of the photoreceptors in the pathophysiology of DR, which may account for the ERG abnormalities.9–13 Abnormal photoreceptor function is expected to affect downstream function, reflected by the ERG b-wave, flicker responses, and OPs. Consistent with this expectation, Holopigian et al.12 measured and modeled the leading edge of the a-wave (generated primarily by photoreceptors) in a heterogeneous group of DR subjects and showed that photoreceptor sensitivity loss was sufficient to account for b-wave sensitivity loss. Similarly, we showed that a-wave model parameters predict high frequency flicker ERG amplitude losses (primarily generated by bipolar cells) in diabetics who have mild or no retinopathy.14

In addition to the ERG, pupillometry has been used to examine retinal function in individuals with diabetes.15–20 Recent advances in high-speed, infrared videography allow pupil responses elicited by light stimuli to be recorded under scotopic and photopic conditions, an approach that shares commonalities with scotopic and photopic ERG recordings. The response of the pupil to a flash of light, the pupillary light reflex (PLR), is largely mediated by intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells that contain the photopigment melanopsin.21–24 Importantly, intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells also receive inputs from rod and cone photoreceptors.23–26 For low to moderate luminance flashes (below the melanopsin activation threshold), signals from the rods and cones are sent to intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, which in turn innervate the olivary pretectal nucleus. Stimulation with low to moderate luminance flashes elicits a transient response that is characterized by a rapid constriction followed by a rapid redilation to baseline. For high luminance flashes (above the melanopsin activation threshold), the PLR is characterized by a transient constriction, as well as a sustained component that is driven by melanopsin activation. Given that the PLR allows assessment of outer and inner retinal neurons, as well as afferent and efferent postretina pathways, pupillometry has become of interest in the study of diabetic eye disease.

The goal of the present study was to gain a more complete understanding of the nature and extent of rod photoreceptor and postreceptor abnormalities in diabetics who have mild nonproliferative DR (MDR) or no clinically apparent DR (NDR). Dark-adapted ERGs were measured across a broad range of stimulus luminance to generate stimulus–response functions. This classic technique, which has been performed in diabetics previously,12,27,28 allows b-wave sensitivity and the maximum b-wave amplitude to be quantified. Abnormalities in b-wave sensitivity and/or maximum saturated amplitude can have important implications for understanding the underlying pathophysiology of DR and potential for recovery. An analogous technique was used to measure dark-adapted pupil sensitivity and maximum pupil response for the first time in DR; correlations between the ERG and pupil parameters were determined.

Methods

Subjects

This research project followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by an institutional review board at the University of Illinois Chicago. All subjects provided written informed consent before participating. Thirty-four individuals diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus were recruited from the Department of Ophthalmology and Visual Sciences at the University of Illinois Chicago. Comprehensive histories were obtained from their medical records and each subject received a comprehensive eye examination. Based on clinical examination by a retina specialist, each subject was clinically classified as NDR (n = 17) or MDR (n = 17) according to the Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study scale.29 Subjects classified as MDR had retinal vascular abnormalities, including microaneurysms, hard exudates, cotton wool spots, and/or mild retinal hemorrhage (equivalent to Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study level 35 or less29). Other than diabetes, no subject had disease known to affect retinal function. Fifteen visually normal, nondiabetic, control subjects also participated. All control subjects had best-corrected visual acuity of 0.10 log MAR (equivalent to 20/25 Snellen acuity) or better. The mean age of the control subjects (51.7 ± 6.8 years) did not differ significantly from that of the diabetic subjects (55.7 ± 6.0 years; F = 2.22; P = 0.12). Of note, six of the control subjects and nine of the diabetic subjects participated in a previous study of ERG abnormalities in individuals with mild or no DR,30 and this study was used to establish the sample size used in this report.

Pupil Apparatus, Stimuli, and Procedure

Pupil responses were obtained monocularly with the fellow eye patched. Typically, the right eye was tested, but in the infrequent case of disease asymmetry, the better eye (less advanced disease) was tested. All stimuli were generated by and presented in a ColorDome ganzfeld (Diagnosys, Lowell, MA, USA). Pupil responses were recorded using an infra-red videography system (Arrington Research, Scottsdale, AZ, USA). A minimum of two responses for each stimulus luminance were obtained and averaged for analysis. Full-field, dark-adapted pupillometry was performed using methods that have been described in detail elsewhere.19 In brief, subjects were dark adapted for 10 minutes, then 1 second, long wavelength (642 nm) flashes of light were presented. Stimulus luminance ranged from −4.0 to 2.6 log cd/m2 in eight steps spaced approximately 1 log unit apart.

Pupil Analysis

Pupil diameter was normalized by the mean baseline pupil diameter during the 1 second preceding the flash, which helps to minimize the effects of intersubject differences in baseline pupil size. The normalization was performed for each subject's mean pupil response obtained for each stimulus luminance. The transient PLR amplitude was defined as the difference between the normalized baseline diameter and the maximum pupil constriction after stimulus onset. The transient PLR amplitude was plotted as a function of log stimulus luminance and the data were fit with a Naka–Rushton function31 to derive the maximum saturated PLR (Pmax) and kp (the PLR semisaturation constant; 1/kp is a measure of pupil response sensitivity): (1)PLR=PmaxLn/Ln+kpn,where L is the stimulus luminance and n is the slope of the function. The values of n, Pmax, and kp were determined by minimizing the mean squared error between the data and the fit. The value of n is often set as a constant, but there is minimal existing literature to establish a constant for PLR measurements, so n was permitted to vary.

ERG Apparatus, Stimuli, and Procedure

After obtaining the pupil measurements, the pupil of the tested eye was dilated with 2.5% phenylephrine hydrochloride and 1% tropicamide drops. A DTL electrode was placed above the lower eyelid in contact with the cornea (active electrode). An ear clip electrode was used as the reference, and a gold cup electrode was positioned on the forehead for the ground. Flash stimuli were generated by and presented in the same ColorDome ganzfeld system used for pupillometry. After 20 minutes of dark adaptation, ERGs were obtained from the test eye, with the fellow eye patched. Stimuli consisted of brief (4 ms) achromatic flashes that ranged in luminance from −4.0 to 1.0 log cd-s-m−2 in 21 steps that were spaced 0.25 log units apart. The flashes were presented in the dark. The responses were bandpass filtered (0.31–300.00 Hz; sampling frequency was 2 kHz), per convention32 and a minimum of three responses for each stimulus luminance were obtained and averaged for analysis.

ERG Analysis

The a- and b-wave amplitudes were defined according to convention.32 Specifically, the a-wave amplitude was calculated as the difference between the prestimulus baseline (0 µV) and the trough of the first negative response. The time of the trough was defined as the a-wave implicit time (IT). The b-wave amplitude was calculated as the difference between the a-wave amplitude and the peak amplitude of the positive potential after the flash; b-wave IT was defined as the peak time of the positive waveform component. For low luminance flashes where an a-wave was not apparent, the b-wave amplitude was calculated as the difference between the baseline (0 µV) and the peak of the positive potential after the flash. OPs, which are high frequency oscillations apparent along the rising limb of the b-wave, were calculated according to a previous approach:33,34 First, the waveform was bandpass filtered (70–300 Hz) to isolate the OPs. Then, the total power in this frequency band was calculated by Fourier transform.

The b-wave amplitudes were fit with a Naka–Rushton equation, equivalent to that described above for the pupil data to derive the maximum saturated b-wave amplitude (Vmax) and the b-wave semisaturation constant (kb); 1/kb is a measure of b-wave sensitivity. However, as described in detail elsewhere,35,36 the b-wave stimulus–response function is characterized by two limbs, unlike the PLR stimulus–response function. The first limb is thought to be primarily generated by rod ON bipolar cells. The asymptote of the rod ON bipolar cell response is followed by a hyperbolic limb that likely represents an interaction between the processes responsible for the a- and b-waves.36 The second limb can appear at different stimulus luminances among individuals, and this limb can confound application of the Naka–Rushton function. To avoid this confound, a previously described heuristic method was used to identify and exclude the second limb as follows.35 First, a least-squares fit of a two-sigmoid model was obtained for the entire stimulus-response function (both limbs). Second, the stimulus luminance at which the second limb contributed 10 µV to the response was determined. Third, all responses above this luminance were removed. Finally, the remaining data points were fit with a single Naka–Rushton function that had a slope (n) equal to 1.0, consistent with convention.31

Results

Pmax for the control (0.49 ± 0.01), NDR (0.47 ± 0.02), and MDR (0.44 ± 0.02) groups were compared by one-way ANOVA, which indicated a significant difference among the groups (F = 4.19; P = 0.02). The log b-wave amplitude elicited by the DA01 did not differ significantly among the subject groups (F = 3.00; P = 0.06). Discussion

The purpose of this study was to gain a more complete understanding of the nature and extent of rod photoreceptor and postreceptor abnormalities in diabetics who have mild or no clinically apparent retinopathy. In this sample of subjects, the primary findings were that (1) the ERG a-wave was modestly reduced (0.13 log µV averaged across stimulus luminance); (2) the maximum saturated b-wave was modestly reduced (0.07 log µV); (3) b-wave sensitivity was significantly reduced (0.20 log units); (4) the maximum saturated PLR was not meaningfully affected (3% reduction); and (5) PLR sensitivity was significantly reduced (0.47 log units).

The pattern of ERG a-wave data described herein is consistent with prior reports in individuals with early stage DR, in that there was a trend for a-wave amplitude losses that did not achieve statistical significance.14,30,31 However, prior reports have indicated significant dark-adapted a-wave sensitivity losses estimated from Naka–Rushton functions in early stage DR14 and in a mixed cohort of DR.12 Of note, the present study did not employ stimulus luminances that were sufficiently high to saturate the a-wave, which prohibits modeling the a-wave amplitude maximum and sensitivity. This limitation impacts the ability to fully determine how photoreceptor dysfunction impacts downstream (e.g., bipolar cell) function.

The pattern of ERG b-wave data obtained from our sample of DR subjects is consistent with prior reports in individuals who had a broad range of DR severity.12,27,28 Specifically, we observed a small (nonsignificant) loss of maximum b-wave amplitude and a larger (statistically significant) loss of b-wave sensitivity. Our results provide confirmation of three reports published more than 25 years ago,12,27,28 which described scotopic b-wave stimulus–response functions in heterogeneous samples of diabetic subjects (types 1 and 2 with broad ranges of disease severity). Roecker et al.28 found greater losses of b-wave sensitivity compared with maximum b-wave amplitude, and that b-wave abnormalities were most apparent at moderate stimulus luminances. In their large sample of diabetic subjects (n = 152) who had a broad range of disease severity, b-wave sensitivity correlated with disease stage. This report was followed by a study from Chung et al.,27 who also measured scotopic b-wave stimulus–response functions in diabetics who had a broad range of disease severity (n = 65). Diabetic groups with at least mild nonproliferative DR (including background DR, preproliferative DR, and proliferative DR) had significant b-wave sensitivity losses, whereas diabetics with no clinically apparent retinopathy had normal b-wave sensitivity. Results for the maximum b-wave amplitude were more complex: diabetics with no clinically apparent retinopathy had supernormal maximum b-wave amplitudes, diabetics with mild nonproliferative DR (background DR) had normal maximum b-wave amplitudes, and diabetics with preproliferative and proliferative DR had reduced maximum b-wave amplitudes. No explanation was provided for the supernormal b-waves of the NDR group, but it may be related to the relatively few number of stimulus luminances that were tested and to possible inclusion of the second b-wave limb in some subjects. They also reported that the b-waves were delayed for some stimulus luminances in diabetics with no DR and mild nonproliferative DR; b-wave delays were reported at nearly all stimulus luminances in diabetics with preproliferative and proliferative DR. Delays of the b-wave were also apparent in the present dataset, but these differences did not attain statistical significance (Fig. 4). The most recent report of b-wave stimulus–response functions in diabetics (Holopigian et al., 199212) also found normal b-wave maximum amplitudes with significantly reduced b-wave sensitivity. They observed that the loss of a-wave sensitivity was sufficient to account for the loss of b-wave sensitivity, implicating a role for rod photoreceptor abnormality. This finding is consistent with our observation that, when averaged across stimulus luminance and subject group, the a- and b-wave amplitude losses were both 0.13 log µV. This conclusion is also supported by the significant correlations between a-wave amplitude and b-wave Vmax and kb. However, as noted elsewhere in this article, the range of stimulus luminance used did not saturate the a-wave, and it is possible that a-wave losses may not fully account for b-wave losses for high flash luminances.

The PLR has also been studied in diabetic subjects who have a range of disease severities,15–17,19,20,37 but we are not aware of previous reports of PLR stimulus–response functions in diabetics. Prior reports of the PLR in diabetic subjects have used methodologies that are more heterogeneous than those of the ERG studies reviewed in this article (e.g., PLR studies differed in stimulus luminance, duration, wavelength, and size). Nevertheless, prior studies are in general agreement that the PLR amplitude can be reduced in diabetics with clinically apparent retinopathy.15–17,19,20,37 By contrast, there is somewhat less agreement regarding the extent of PLR abnormalities in diabetics without clinically apparent retinopathy, with some studies reporting abnormalities15,16 and others not.19,20,37 Some of the discrepancy among previous reports may be explained by the use of different stimulus luminances in different studies. To address this potential issue, the present study assessed the PLR across a broad range of stimulus luminances and modeled the responses using methodology akin to that used for the b-wave analyses. The data of the present study showed relatively small maximum PLR losses, but considerable losses of PLR sensitivity. Thus, these data highlight the value of measuring the PLR at multiple stimulus luminances for a more comprehensive assessment of PLR abnormality in diabetes.

Although both ERG and PLR abnormalities were observed in the present study, ERG amplitude and sensitivity were generally poor predictors of PLR abnormalities. For example, the maximum PLR was not correlated with a- or b-wave amplitudes. There was a borderline significant correlation between pupil sensitivity and a-wave amplitude. There was no correlation between pupil sensitivity and b-wave sensitivity. Overall, the loss of PLR sensitivity (0.47 log units) exceeded the loss of ERG b-wave sensitivity (0.20 log units), suggesting that sites beyond the retinal bipolar cells may play a role in the PLR sensitivity loss. Specifically, any additional abnormality at the level of the retinal ganglion cells would have a far greater impact on the PLR compared with the ERG. The PLR is also dependent on postretinal pathways that would not affect the ERG. However, the experimental design used in the present study does not allow afferent and efferent pupil abnormalities to be separated unequivocally. Future work could expand upon these findings by using chromatic pupillometry, or other techniques, to better differentiate efferent vs. afferent defects. Nevertheless, the present results show (Fig. 2D) that the extent of PLR reduction is dependent on the flash luminance (greatest effects for moderate luminance flashes), which may not be expected to result from an efferent defect. Although speculative, an efferent defect (e.g., iris denervation) may be expected to make the pupil less responsive across all luminances.

A key finding of the present study is that diabetic subjects can have losses of ERG b-wave and PLR sensitivity assessed by stimulus–response functions. Sensitivity losses in these individuals appear to be a consistent finding in the literature,12,27,28 and can occur before retinopathy is clinically apparent. Distinguishing between a loss of maximum amplitude and a loss of sensitivity may have clinical implications. For example, a reduced maximum response (Vmax) has been interpreted to indicate a reduced response capacity owing to fewer functioning rods, a disruption of the rod dark current, and/or response compression.31 A loss of sensitivity (kb) has been interpreted to reflect a reduced efficiency of quantal capture and/or abnormalities within the visual cycle.31 Sensitivity losses indicate that the capacity to respond is present, but a stronger stimulus is required to elicit a response of comparable amplitude to that of an individual with normal sensitivity. This discrepancy is often conceptualized in terms of a dark glasses model, where the effect of wearing dark glasses can be overcome by increasing the stimulus luminance. Computational modeling38 and ERG simulation studies39 indicate that a patchy/nonuniform loss of photoreceptors would have (1) a small effect on Vmax, (2) a larger effect on kb, and (3) no effect on n. The elevation in kb is expected to be approximately proportional to the reduction in photoreceptor input into the bipolar cells. This predicted pattern nicely matches the data from our diabetic subjects (Fig. 3). This finding contrasts with an alternative pattern in which large areas of the retina uniformly lose photoreceptors (as in retinitis pigmentosa, for example), which would reduce Vmax in proportion to the number of receptors lost. We note, however, that heterogeneous photoreceptor damage can have effects on kb and Vmax that are more complex than this simplified interpretation.38 For example, kb is also be expected to increase if all photoreceptors have reduced sensitivity (i.e., uniform, nonpatchy loss). Furthermore, kb could increase as a region of the retina loses sensitivity and then decrease, becoming more normal, as this region becomes nonfunctional.38 Although speculative, restoring rod sensitivity (improving kb) in DR may be a more achievable task than regenerating missing rods (improving Vmax). Additional work is needed to determine the cause of the sensitivity loss in diabetes and the extent to which it may be improved.

The relatively small sample size of diabetics in the present study (17 NDR and 17 MDR) is a limitation. Given this sample size, the statistical power was suboptimal (<80%) for some of the statistical analyses (Vmax power = 0.24; Pmax power = 0.57). As such, results of the ANOVAs should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, abnormalities in kb (0.20 log units) and kp (0.46 log units) are likely clinically meaningful, whereas Vmax (0.07 log µV) and Pmax (3%) are not. The small sample size may also have contributed to the finding that ERG amplitudes and PLRs tended to be more affected in the NDR subjects than in the MDR subjects; the study was not powered to detect small subgroup differences. We note, however, that there can be minimal vascular differences between the clinical stages of these groups: a single microaneurysm can distinguish MDR from NDR and the subject characteristics (Table) were similar between groups. Disease severity in our sample of subjects may be better thought of as a continuum of early DR, rather than two clearly distinct and nonoverlapping populations. Microaneurysms and mild retinal hemorrhages that are characteristic of MDR are expected to have minimal direct effect on the full-field ERG and PLR. Likewise, a history of diabetic macular edema is not likely to be detectable by the full-field ERG and PLR. Three of the 17 MDR subjects had a history of diabetic macular edema and anti-VEGF therapy. Of these three subjects, two had ERG and PLR responses that fell in the middle of the MDR group; the third subject had considerable ERG and PLR abnormalities. Anti-VEGF injections in these three individuals did not appear to have a clear or consistent effect on the experimental measures, but the study is underpowered and not designed to evaluate the effects of treatment on ERG and PLR responses. A more likely explanation for variation within the diabetic groups is that vascular and neural abnormalities may appear and progress at different rates in different individuals. In this case, the severity of retinal vascular disease in diabetics would not be an ideal predictor of the severity of retinal neural abnormality.

Conclusions

Modest a- and b-wave amplitude losses were observed in this sample of diabetic subjects. The small losses of b-wave amplitude could largely be accounted for by losses of a-wave amplitude, suggesting a photoreceptor origin of the abnormality. By contrast, the subjects had substantial losses of b-wave sensitivity assessed by stimulus–response/Naka–Rushton analyses. The b-wave sensitivity losses were smaller than the PLR sensitivity losses, suggesting that sites beyond the bipolar cells contribute to PLR abnormalities in diabetics. Although DR is clinically defined by the nature and extent of retinal vasculature disease, our findings align with evidence that diabetes also impacts retinal neural function, including the photoreceptors. These findings underscore the need for additional research into the role of neural dysfunction in DR and suggest that stimulus–response functions may be valuable for such investigations.
