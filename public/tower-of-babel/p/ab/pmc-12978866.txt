Lack of Population Structure of an Extreme Migratory Shorebird: Evidence of Gene Flow Between Geographically Disparate Populations
Authors: Camila Gherardi‐Fuentes, Jorge Ruiz, Nathan R. Senner, James A. Johnson, José A. Masero, Josefina Gutiérrez, Claudio Verdugo, Juan G. Navedo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGene flow affects the distribution of genetic variation of a species over time and thus can be crucial for a population's persistence and adaptive capacity. Given the importance of gene flow, it is key to understand the connectivity and genetic differentiation between populations of species with small and segregated breeding populations that are facing population declines, such as many long‐distance migratory birds. In this study, we explored population structure in Hudsonian Godwits ( Limosa haemastica ) from two geographically distinct breeding areas in the North American sub‐Arctic and two nonbreeding areas in South America using nuclear microsatellites. Despite being spatially and temporally segregated during most of the annual cycle, our results indicate no evidence of population differentiation between breeding populations, nor clustering between individuals from breeding and nonbreeding populations connected by migration. Considering the phenology of the species, godwits from both breeding populations could co‐occur during southward migration and/or throughout the oversummering period, likely in the Las Pampas ecoregion of Argentina. As with many other long‐distance migratory shorebirds, immature godwits stay in their nonbreeding areas until sexual maturity is reached, during which time they can explore, interact, and follow flocks of adults to different nonbreeding areas, thus increasing the chances of mixing between populations. This highlights the importance of recognizing the key role of early life period within the full life cycle of migratory birds for understanding their demography and evolutionary potential.

Introduction

Gene flow affects the distribution of genetic variation within a species over time (Lowe et al. 2017). Site fidelity and philopatry (i.e., the return of individuals to their natal sites to breed) can play an important role in limiting gene flow by restricting reproduction between individuals from different breeding areas (Wenink et al. 1993; Arnold and Wilkinson 2015; Jahner et al. 2016). If population sizes are small, reduced gene flow can negatively affect population persistence by decreasing local genetic diversity and adaptive capacity (Slatkin 1987; Tigano and Friesen 2016), while high gene flow can improve population stability through increases in genetic variation (Reed and Frankham 2003). Given the importance of gene flow, it is therefore relevant to understand the connectivity and genetic differentiation between populations of species with small and segregated breeding populations that are facing population declines, such as those of many long‐distance migratory birds (Rosenberg et al. 2019; Smith et al. 2023).

Migratory shorebirds (order Charadriiformes) carry out long‐distance annual movements between breeding and nonbreeding areas, with many species breeding in the sub‐Arctic and spending the nonbreeding season in Sub‐Antarctic areas in the Southern Hemisphere. Selection for optimal timing of breeding in the sub‐Arctic, among other environmental and evolutionary forces within their annual cycle (e.g., wind patterns and nonbreeding habitat distribution; Alerstam and Lindström 1990; Conklin and Battley 2011; Gill et al. 2014; Helm et al. 2017), has shaped the migratory routes of species, both in space and time. Within each migratory route, many shorebird populations are highly faithful to staging sites (i.e., areas used en route at which they recover their fuel reserves; Warnock 2010), as well as to the areas used during their nonbreeding period (Reneerkens et al. 2019). In addition, some species show a very high consistency in the scheduling of northbound migration to reach their breeding grounds (Conklin et al. 2013). These behaviors can cause spatial and temporal segregation, affecting the gene flow between populations (e.g., Bauer et al. 2016). Past studies have identified a high number of subspecies and/or high levels of genetic structure among shorebird populations (Wenink et al. 1993; Ottvall et al. 2005; Höglund et al. 2009; LeBlanc et al. 2016; Conklin et al. 2022). However, site fidelity can vary at different scales (Lourenço et al. 2016; Oudman et al. 2018; van Leeuwen and Jamieson 2018), especially in large shorebird species due to delayed sexual maturity of up to 3–4 years (e.g., Battley et al. 2020; Navedo and Ruiz 2020; Rogers et al. 2025). Without the pressure to return to the sub‐Arctic to breed, immature birds can show more dispersive and exploratory behaviors during their early years (Clobert et al. 2001; Battley et al. 2020), time during which they can accumulate information on the habitat quality of different sites before settling (Metcalfe and Furness 1985; Baccetti et al. 1999). Once an individual settles for its first breeding attempt and joins a given breeding population, it almost always remains faithful throughout its lifetime, particularly in monogamous species (Kwon et al. 2022). In large migratory shorebirds with disjunct breeding areas, natal dispersal therefore represents a potential key source of gene flow between populations.

The Hudsonian Godwit ( Limosa haemastica ; hereafter, “godwit”) is a monogamous species that breeds in three geographically distinct areas of North America: Alaska (USA) with 30,000 individuals, Hudson Bay (Canada) with 56,000 individuals, and a much smaller (< 1000 individuals) breeding population in the Mackenzie River Delta in northwest Canada (Figure 1; Hayman et al. 1986; Andres et al. 2012; Walker et al. 2020). Godwits migrate c. 15,000 km south to spend the nonbreeding season at high latitudes within South America, mainly in two separate coastal areas, one in the Pacific and the other in the Atlantic (Senner 2012). On the one hand birds from Alaska tend to migrate to the Chiloé Archipelago and the surrounding continental areas in Chile, while birds from Hudson Bay most frequently migrate to the Atlantic coast of Tierra del Fuego (Argentina and Chile; Walker et al. 2020; Figure 1). Therefore, these two populations are generally segregated on both the breeding and nonbreeding grounds. Notably, there are exceptions, with individuals that show some degree of mixing at nonbreeding sites (Andres et al. 2012; Senner 2012; Senner et al. 2014; Linscott et al. 2022; authors unpubl. data). Recent data show that godwits exhibit high site fidelity and spatial segregation within the Chiloé Archipelago across years (Basso et al. 2023, 2024), but information other than annual abundance estimates at the other main nonbreeding area is virtually absent (Walker et al. 2020).

In this light, recent estimates have shown concerning trends: godwit counts during the southward migration on the Atlantic coast of North America have plummeted in the last 40 years (−90% abundance, Smith et al. 2023), resulting in godwits being classified as “Vulnerable” on the IUCN Red List (BirdLife International 2024). Although godwits from all breeding populations should fly through that region, the abundance of nonbreeding birds on the Chiloé Archipelago (i.e., Alaska breeders) has been stable throughout the last decade (Valenzuela et al. 2023). Therefore, these population declines might correspond predominantly to the Hudson Bay breeding population. This fluctuation in population size can have consequences for the viability of the species, as it can affect its genetic diversity, allele frequencies, and levels of inbreeding (Allendorf et al. 2022).

To our knowledge, the only previous phylogenetic study of godwits was that of Haig et al. (1997). In their study using random amplified polymorphic DNA (RAPD), they included samples from Hudson Bay (n = 10), Alaska (n = 10), and the Mackenzie River Delta (n = 5) breeding areas, as well as at a staging site in Saskatchewan (n = 10), and found genetic differences between the three breeding areas. By using species‐specific microsatellite markers in samples obtained from breeding and nonbreeding areas, we aimed to decipher current godwit population structure, adding to our understanding of the population genetics of this remarkable, and rapidly declining, migratory species. Considering the results of Haig et al. (1997) and the spatial segregation of the populations, we expected to find clear population structure between godwits from Alaska‐Chiloé and Hudson Bay‐Tierra del Fuego. Our findings could also reveal important aspects of the population ecology of long‐distance migratory shorebirds, useful in future assessments and decision‐making for species of conservation concern.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

We obtained blood samples from either the caudal‐tibial vein of godwit chicks or the brachial vein of adults captured during the breeding period (May–July) at Beluga (Alaska breeding area, USA; n = 14) and Churchill (Hudson Bay breeding area, Canada; n = 14; Figure 1). During the nonbreeding period (October–March), samples were obtained from the jugular or brachial vein of adult godwits at two sites in the Chiloé Archipelago (bahías Caulín and Pullao; n = 7 and 8, respectively) and at Bahía Lomas (Tierra del Fuego nonbreeding area, n = 13), both in Chile (Figure 1; see Supporting Information for additional capture details). Samples were either stored in 95% ethanol at 4°C until processing or on FTA cards (Whatman, United Kingdom). These four sites represent the two known breeding‐nonbreeding population pairs (Beluga‐Chiloé and Churchill‐Bahía Lomas). DNA was extracted from all blood samples using a commercial kit (E.Z.N.A. Tissue kit DNA extraction, Omega Bio‐tek, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions.

Captures and sampling at Chiloé were made under bioethics approval #260/2016 from Universidad Austral de Chile and permission #5932/2016 from SAG/Gobierno de Chile. Alaskan captures and sampling were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at Cornell University (Application #2001‐0051) and carried out in accordance with their animal care guidelines. Handling of birds was carried out under Migratory Bird Banding Permit #20022, Federal Collecting Permit #MB‐150573, Federal Fish and Wildlife Permit #MB789758‐9, and Alaska State Permit #11‐092.

Microsatellite Identification and PCR Optimization

A sequence dataset was previously obtained by shotgun sequencing 12 individuals (see Gherardi‐Fuentes et al. 2020) and used to search for microsatellites using msatcommander v.1.0.8 (Faircloth 2008). We found 19,366 microsatellites from which 30 loci were randomly selected and primers designed in the flanking regions. To facilitate allele call, we selected 15 tri‐ and 15 tetra‐ nucleotide repeats because these are easier to differentiate in chromatograms. After amplification and polymorphism tests using 8 samples, 14 loci that did not amplify or were monomorphic were discarded. We designed new forward primers for the remaining 16 polymorphic microsatellites, including the M13 sequence (TGTAAAACGACGGCCAGT) at the 5′‐end for fluorescent attachment following Schuelke (2000) and optimized through PCR. Three additional loci showed excessive stuttering bands at peak calling and were discarded. Thus, we used a final set of 13 polymorphic species‐specific microsatellites for genotyping (Table 1).

PCR Protocols and Genotyping

The protocol consisted of two PCRs, one for amplification of the microsatellite and the other for fluorophore addition (Schuelke 2000). Filter tips and different pipettes were used in each processing step for all lab procedures. The first PCR had a PCR mix of 15 μL per sample (5.25 μL of dnase‐free water, 7.5 μL of AbmMastermix, 0.375 μL of each primer—forward and reverse—and 1.5 μL of template DNA) in a protocol with an initial denaturation at 94°C for 1 min, 30 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 1 min, annealing at 55°C–63°C for 20 s (Table 1, “T°”), and an extension at 72°C for 20 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 2 min. The second PCR had a PCR mix of 14.5 μL per sample (Dnase‐free water 8.5 μL, AbmMastermix 4.1 μL, forward primer 0.1 μL, forward primer with fluorophore (PET, VIC, NED, 6‐FAM; Table 1, “Fluorescent”), 0.4 μL, reverse primer 0.4 μL, first PCR product 1 μL) in a protocol with an initial denaturation at 94°C for 4 min, 30 cycles with a denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, an annealing at 58°C for 45 s, and an extension at 72°C for 45 s, followed by 8 cycles with a denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 53°C for 45 s and extension at 72°C for 45 s, ending with a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. Microsatellites were divided into two groups for fragment analysis, with each group having two microsatellites labeled with the same fluorescence but of different sizes that were easily recognizable during peak calling (Table 1, “Group”). Using these 13 loci, we genotyped 56 samples in an ABI3500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems). Allele calling was performed automatically using the microsatellites plugin in Geneious Prime v.2022.1.1 (Biomatters Company, New Zealand) and manually verified for accuracy as recommended (Guichoux et al. 2011). The peak calling for each sample was performed by the same person. Samples with low amplification (i.e., missing data in five or more alleles) were not included in further analyses (one individual from Churchill, and two from Bahía Lomas). The data were checked for scoring errors, large allele dropouts, and null alleles using MICROCHECKER v.2.2.3 (van Oosterhout et al. 2004). Checks for departure from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) and linkage disequilibrium (LD) were performed using Arlequin v.3.5.2.2 (Excoffier and Lischer 2010), followed by a sequential Bonferroni correction (Rice 1989).

Overall Genetic Diversity

The number of alleles (N a), observed (H o) and expected (H e) heterozygosity by loci and population, and the inbreeding coefficient (F IS) were calculated using Arlequin v.3.5.2.2, while the number of private alleles (P a) was obtained in GenAlEx v.6.502 (Peakall and Smouse 2006, 2012). The polymorphic information content (PIC) and gene diversity (GD) were estimated using PowerMarker v3.25 (Liu and Muse 2005). The PIC value shows the capacity of a marker to detect polymorphisms within a population from highly informative (PIC > 0.5) to reasonably (0.5 > PIC > 0.25) and slightly informative (PIC < 0.25; Botstein et al. 1980), while GD indicates the probability that two randomly chosen alleles are different. Loci with PIC < 0.25 were excluded from further analysis. Null allele frequencies were estimated in MICROCHECKER v.2.2.3. GenAlEx was also used to convert the data to the format required for each program.

Bottleneck Analysis

Given the potential effect of the glacial history of their breeding grounds on their population genetics (i.e., due to distributional shifts and habitat loss; Arcones et al. 2021), we used BOTTLENECK v.1.2.02 (Luikart and Cornuet 1999) to search for bottleneck effects. We used a two‐phase model (TPM; Di Rienzo et al. 1994), which is a combination of the stepwise mutation (SMM) and infinite allele models (IAM), and is considered more appropriate than the other two models by themselves for microsatellites (Chen et al. 2006). We ran a set of analyses for 10,000 iterations with the SMM/IAM ratio varying between 80%–95%. This ratio was selected because our microsatellites have tri‐ and tetra‐ nucleotide repeats, and thus SMM is the most probable mutational model over IAM (Shriver et al. 1993; Cornuet and Luikart 1996). Evidence of bottlenecks was evaluated using a Wilcoxon test of excess heterozygosity, which is indicated as robust for data containing < 20 polymorphic loci (Cornuet and Luikart 1996).

Population Genetic Analyses

We estimated population pairwise F ST (10,000 permutations) to evaluate the differences in allele frequencies between populations, F IS (10,000 permutations) to evaluate the genetic diversity within populations, and quantified the amount of variation between and within populations from an Analysis of Molecular Variance (AMOVA) using Arlequin v.3.5.2.2. Additionally, we evaluated population differentiation based on genetic distance using Nei's Genetic Distance method (Nei 1972, 1978) in GenAlEx v.6.502. We evaluated the population structure through a discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC; Jombart et al. 2010) using the package adegenet v.2.1.8 (Jombart 2008; Jombart and Ahmed 2011) in R (v.4.4.0; R Core Team 2024). The number of PC (principal components) to be retained was determined following Thia (2022), who proposed the use of the k − 1 criterion (number of axes = k infer − 1, in our case three since otherwise it would be one and infeasible). Only the samples from the two breeding sites (29 samples) were included in the main DAPC analysis. An additional analysis included the samples from the nonbreeding sites with the objective of visualizing if these individuals clustered in any form with those of the breeding sites.

Results

The resulting set of 13 microsatellites were polymorphic overall for all populations and included 65 alleles (Table 1). The Chiloé samples were monomorphic for HUGO10 and HUGO21, whereas the Bahía Lomas samples were monomorphic for HUGO10. We found no evidence of scoring errors or large allele dropout using Microchecker. When all populations were analyzed together, null alleles were observed in one microsatellite (HUGO26, Table 1); when analyzed per population, this feature was only observed in individuals sampled in Beluga. The N a per microsatellite varied between 3 and 8, with a mean (±SD) of 4.31 ± 1.89 for all markers. The mean H o and H e values were 0.48 ± 0.25 and 0.47 ± 0.21, respectively (Table 1).

Three loci (HUGO02, HUGO11, and HUGO26) departed from HWE after Bonferroni correction when all populations were tested together. However, when each population was tested separately, only HUGO02 departed from HWE in the Beluga population. A linkage disequilibrium analysis after Bonferroni correction showed some non‐random relationships between two loci (HUGO21 and HUGO10) when considering all populations, but these were not observed in individual population analyses. Most of the loci were informative, except for HUGO10 (PIC < 0.1, Table 1), probably due to it being monomorphic for two sampling sites and its low number of alleles (n = 3, Table 1). HUGO10 was thus discarded from further analyses.

N a was higher in Beluga and Bahía Lomas than in Churchill and Chiloé, which represent opposite breeding‐nonbreeding population pairs (Table 2). H o was lower for the nonbreeding sites and F IS was not significantly different from zero in any of the sampled populations (Table 2). The number of private alleles was low in all sites (range: 1–3) and were found at both breeding and nonbreeding sites (Table 2). We found no evidence of bottlenecks in any of the breeding populations nor in the complete dataset, with a lack of significance in all Wilcoxon tests of excess heterozygosity (Table S1).

Population Structure

Nei's genetic distance (0.020–0.038) and F ST calculations (−0.009–0.019) were minimal between all godwit populations (Table 3). AMOVA results showed < 1% variance between sampled populations, similar to the pairwise F ST analysis, where no significant differences were found after Bonferroni corrections (Table 3). This pattern was also observed in the DAPC, where all samples clustered in a unique group, both in the analysis only including samples from the breeding sites (Figure 2), as well as the analysis including all samples (Figure S1).

Discussion

Contrary to the findings of previous studies (Haig et al. 1997), we found no evidence of population structure in Hudsonian Godwits, despite overall segregation in their breeding and nonbreeding areas, as well as temporal segregation during the northward migration of the two breeding populations (Senner 2012). This lack of genetic structure across the godwit distribution suggests a high degree of connectivity between areas that may be maintained by ecological processes occurring throughout their annual cycle. Adult godwits have shown a high site fidelity during the austral summer; however, resightings of banded individuals and locations of satellite‐tracked godwits demonstrate a small but measurable degree of movement of individuals between nonbreeding areas (Andres et al. 2012; Senner 2012; Walker et al. 2020; Linscott et al. 2022; Basso et al. 2023; J.G. Navedo unpubl. data), providing evidence of population mixing.

There are two periods during which birds from all breeding areas could co‐occur. First, birds could co‐occur at common staging sites in the Las Pampas ecoregion in Argentina (Figure 1) during southward migration before they split their migratory route towards their specific nonbreeding areas further south. Secondly, “oversummering” godwits (i.e., birds that do not migrate north to breeding areas; McNeil 1970) gather in Las Pampas during the austral winter, with some flocks composed of more than 1000 individuals (Navedo and Ruiz 2020; Martínez‐Curci et al. 2025). This behavior, although observed in small numbers of adult godwits from Chiloé that have aborted their northward migration and moved to these inland areas (Basso et al. 2024), is employed by nearly all juveniles and most immature godwits, since individuals delay their first breeding attempt until reaching sexual maturity during their 3rd or 4th years (Navedo and Ruiz 2020). In both periods, individual juveniles might thus follow flocks of adult birds flying to either of the two nonbreeding areas in September‐October because they do not yet have a fixed destination (Loonstra et al. 2023), increasing the chances of mixing between populations. As observed in Verhoeven et al. (2022), variation in the migratory behavior of Black‐tailed Godwits can arise due to differences in the timing and migration route between adults and juveniles during their first migration southwards and can change later in life via phenotypic plasticity.

During their early years of life, similar to their congeners (Battley et al. 2020), immature Hudsonian Godwits likely spend an important fraction of their annual cycles exploring and interacting with conspecifics in flocks composed of both adult and immature godwits, most probably from a range of breeding sites (Navedo and Ruiz 2020). Hence, natal dispersal might simply occur as a consequence of immature godwits following adults to different nonbreeding areas during their first years before reaching sexual maturity, and then migrating north for the first time to an area different from the one in which they were born for their first breeding attempt (e.g., Christie et al. 2023). This could help explain the low differentiation between geographically distant breeding populations due to regular gene flow, which could be sufficient to maintain similar levels of allele frequencies between populations (Lowe and Allendorf 2010), thus decreasing levels of genetic differentiation.

Other Limosa species have shown varying degrees of population structure. For example, Zhu et al. (2021) recently described a new subspecies of Black‐tailed Godwit ( L. limosa bohai) from Bohai Bay, China, where it co‐occurs with another previously described subspecies (L.l. melanurides), but differs in its morphology and mtDNA. In contrast, and using the same type of molecular marker, no genetic differentiation was found within a subspecies of Bar‐tailed Godwit (L. lapponica taymyrensis), even though two groups segregate on both their breeding and nonbreeding grounds, differ in their migration timing and routes, and even differ in their morphology (Bom et al. 2022). This exemplifies how some bird populations, although spatially segregated and with clear phenotypic differences, do not necessarily show genetic differences (Zink 2004).

A lack of population structure has been described in other migratory bird species as well (e.g., Veit et al. 2005; Küpper et al. 2012; Pellegrino et al. 2015; Haase et al. 2019; Bom et al. 2022). This pattern could be due to recent population divergence or secondary contact (i.e., gene flow) between isolated populations after the last glaciation (Bensch et al. 1999). However, traits might evolve rapidly (e.g., Berthold et al. 1992; van Gils et al. 2016) with insufficient time to observe changes in mtDNA and microsatellite allele frequencies (Bensch et al. 1999). This highlights the importance of molecular marker selection, where—depending on each species' evolutionary history—one type of marker might be better suited for the detection of population differentiation over other options. For example, some studies have shown similar results between microsatellites and Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) in the detection of population structure (Langin et al. 2018; Zimmerman et al. 2020; Hauser et al. 2021). However, in the case of L. haemastica , further studies using SNPs—which provide a far more exhaustive and fine‐scale assessment of genetic variation—could reveal subtle or recent patterns of differentiation and demographic change that remains undetectable with microsatellites (e.g., Osborn et al. 2022).

Alternatively, spatial and temporal overlap at staging sites (through migratory synchrony) has been hypothesized as a source of “homogenization” within migratory species (Bom et al. 2023). In comparison with its congeners ( L. limosa and L. lapponica , whose breeding distribution spans northern Europe, Asia and N. America, and have nonbreeding areas in Europe, Africa, Asia, and Oceania), Hudsonian Godwit populations have more restricted space use in the Southern Hemisphere because of the region's geography (mountainous and narrow), limiting the potential for different migratory routes to develop. This could make long‐distance migrants such as godwits born at separate breeding sites prone to mixing during specific periods of the annual cycle and, specially, during their early years of life.

Notably, our results differ from the findings of Haig et al. (1997), who found genetic differences among the three godwit breeding populations using RAPD. These contrasting results could be related to the type of marker, number of loci, and sample size of each study. For example, Miller et al. (2013) repeated the analysis of Haig et al. for Semipalmated Sandpipers ( Calidris pusilla ) using samples from the same populations used in the original study with similar results to our study: with RAPD there was population differentiation but not with microsatellites. In our study, the low and non‐significant F ST (< 0.02), low genetic distance (< 0.04), low percentage of variation (0.1%), and clustering among breeding populations suggest an absence of population structure within the two main godwit populations (which include 98% of known individuals). It is important to note, however, that we did not include samples from the Mackenzie River Delta breeding population (under 1000 individuals, < 2% of the total population; COSEWIC 2019). Although they appear to overlap in Argentina with individuals from the other breeding populations during the nonbreeding period (Smith and Watts 2013), we cannot be certain if this population is panmictic too.

Besides the contemporary ecological factors discussed above that could influence rates of gene flow among populations, Hudsonian godwits could have formed a single population during the last glacial maximum (20,000 years ago) in the few refugia available in the Nearctic (Shafer et al. 2010; Arcones et al. 2021), expanding to the current breeding areas afterwards and leaving insufficient time to observe genetic differentiation, as seen in Red Knots (e.g., Calidris canutus rufa and islandica; Conklin et al. 2022). Further studies using different molecular markers, such as SNPs or whole‐genome sequencing, and including samples from breeding godwits from Mackenzie River Delta, could allow for the reconstruction of the recent evolutionary history of the species and evaluate the effect of past events on present population genetics (Schnelle et al. 2026), as well as detect subtle population structure (Henn et al. 2010), finally disentangling the population genetics of this “Vulnerable” species.

In this light, godwits rely on coastal mudflats and inland wetlands during the nonbreeding season and at their staging sites, respectively, habitats that are limited and threatened. As with other sub‐Arctic breeders, climate change is a threat that might have stochastic consequences for their fitness, as their life histories and migration timing are tightly dependent on environmental factors such as Pacific winds (Linscott et al. 2022), prey availability and quality for chicks (Wilde et al. 2022), and the synchronicity between their breeding phenology and ecosystem productivity (Senner et al. 2017). Hudsonian Godwits are considered a species of high conservation concern because of their small population size, limited and fragmented breeding distribution, highlighting the risk of catastrophic events during the nonbreeding season (Senner 2010), when they form flocks of thousands of individuals concentrated in few bays (Navedo et al. 2019; Morrison and Ross 1989).

These characteristics increase the risk of population decline and potential bottlenecks, especially when their population size is currently thought to be decreasing at an alarming rate (Smith et al. 2023; but see Valenzuela et al. 2023). The connection between populations, as evidenced by the high gene flow we found in our study, could allow godwits to cope with threats in a genetic sense (i.e., adaptive potential), but actions are needed to ensure the conservation of their crucial breeding, staging, and nonbreeding sites. The protection of their habitats throughout the annual cycle, especially in areas with a high concentration of individuals where there are currently human activities threatening biodiversity (e.g., salmon aquaculture; Navedo et al. 2021; Navedo and Vargas‐Chacoff 2021), is crucial for the maintenance of this high connectivity, which could become particularly relevant in the face of global change (Piersma et al. 2022). Our results also highlight the potential importance of the Las Pampas ecoregion in Argentina (Navedo and Ruiz 2020; Martínez‐Curci et al. 2025), where godwits from both breeding populations may merge at different periods within the annual cycle and over the course of their lifetimes. Further studies should endeavor to disentangle the relevance of the oversummering stage for the maintenance of gene flow between godwit populations and among populations of other shorebird species.
