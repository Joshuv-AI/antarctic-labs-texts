Coaxially nested component with asymmetric fiber resonant cavity and separation membrane for gaseous and dissolved gases detection
Authors: Fu Wan, Weiping Kong, Hongcheng Sun, Tongqin Ran, Weigen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dynamically, simultaneously and sensitively embedded or external detection of multi-component gaseous or dissolved phase gases in millilitre volumes is critical for many frontier applications. Raman spectroscopy based on a single-wavelength laser can achieve simultaneous detection of various gases (except noble gases), but it lacks sensitivity. Therefore, this paper firstly develops a theoretical framework for Raman signal generation within the fiber resonant cavity (FRC), and based on which, an asymmetric FRC is proposed to balance the laser power enhancement and the intracavity loss caused by a high-reflectivity input cavity mirror, enhancing the Raman signal intensity by 170 times when compared to only hollow-core fiber and 36 times to only geometry resonant cavity, achieving sensitive gas detection with a limit of detection (LOD) as low as 0.01 ppm*bar. Meanwhile, a coaxially nested component with FRC and separation membrane is proposed, which can enable approximate synchronization of liquid-gas separation and detection. This work provides an effective solution of measurement instruments to realize d high-precision detection of multi component gases in various states with gas consumption ranging from 0.3 mL to 2 mL, enabling further refinement of the associated generation and decomposition pathways of different gases, and the coaxially nested component also can be used in other scattering-based spectroscopic techniques (e.g., Brillouin spectroscopy) and absorption-based spectroscopic techniques.

Introduction

Disease diagnosis based on breath analysis, status assessment of battery and large-scale equipment such as power equipment and space rocket, etc. urgently require dynamic, simultaneous and highly sensitive (0.1 ppm level) detection of multi-component gaseous gases1–3 or dissolved gases4–7 in low-volume (a few ml) whether it is embedded detection by diffusion or external detection by pump. Conventional absorption spectroscopy8–10, photoacoustic spectroscopy, and photothermal spectroscopy11–13 based on absorption effect can typically employ wavelength-specific lasers preconfigured for known gases to perform time-sharing detection of multi-component gases. Notably, Ye et al.14 proposed a modulated ringdown comb interferometry method for simultaneous measurement of multiple gases. By sweeping the comb lines across the cavity resonance with sufficient modulation depth, all comb lines can be coupled through the cavity in sequence, and then the complex cavity ring-down mode containing gas information in the cavity can be extracted with the help of Michelson interferometer and non-uniform Fourier transform. However, this method faces challenges in detecting homonuclear diatomic molecules (e.g., H₂ and O₂) and suffers from relatively complex and expensive system. Raman spectroscopy using a single-wavelength laser can simultaneously and cost-effectively detect highly complex gas mixtures even with undefined composition (excluding noble gases) while performing real-time calibration during detection15–17. However, its sensitivity remains limited. At present, multi-pass cavity18,19, resonant cavity20,21, and hollow-core fiber22–25 are often used to improve the interaction distance, laser power build-up within the cavity, or the Raman signal collection efficiency, improving the gas detection sensitivity. However, the Raman signal enhancement in multi-pass cavity is limited by the finite number of light reflections, with detection limit typically at 10 ppm*bar levels. High-finesse resonant cavity, by establishing intracavity laser resonance, can achieve a detection limit of 0.015 ppm*bar21. However, this approach requires gas sample volumes exceeding 1 L. The hollow-core fiber effectively enhances Raman signal collection efficiency (spontaneous Raman scattering is an 4Pi process) by confining both the excitation laser and the gas within the hollow-core area, enabling Raman spectroscopy to achieve ppm-level detection limits26–30, with a gas sample volume of a few mL, but it is incapable of detecting dissolved gases.

In this paper, we propose a coaxially nested component with asymmetric fiber resonant cavity and separation membrane for gaseous and dissolved gases detection by Raman spectroscopy. At first, synergistic enhancement model for Raman signal is built-up. With that, an asymmetric-reflectivity configuration is proposed to reduce the transmission losses of both the laser and Raman signals within the cavity, with the gain effect of the asymmetric reflectivity FRC is 2.5 to 22 times higher than that of the symmetric reflectivity case at different wavenumbers, achieving a Raman detection sensitivity as low as 0.01 ppm*bar. In addition, a coaxially nested component with asymmetric FRC and separation membrane for integration of liquid-gas separation and detection is proposed, which enables simultaneous liquid–gas separation and detection. To verify the effectiveness of this technology, Raman spectroscopy detection experiments of human breath gas, dissolved gases in battery electrolytes, dissolved gases in mineral insulating oil, SF₆ and C₅F₁₀O as well as their respective decomposition products by partial discharge fault are carried out with gas sample consumption ranging from 0.3 mL to 2 mL.

However, the theoretical enhancement shown in Fig. 2b is limited by the intracavity round-trip losses of both the laser and Raman signal, as well as by the laser linewidth. Unlike the single-pass transmission loss measured with a broadband source, the round-trip loss accumulates over multiple resonance cycles. Minor alignment imperfections can lead to a small fraction of light coupling into higher-order modes, resulting in a slightly increased effective round-trip loss compared to the single-pass loss of the fundamental mode. In addition, the laser linewidth should be much narrower than cavity resonance width to maintain stable resonance coupling and achieve enhancement close to theoretical value.

Experimental setup

The schematic diagram of the coaxially nested component for integration of liquid-gas separation and detection is shown in Fig. 3a, where the FRC is formed by the tight coupling of large mode area fibers (LMAFs) with mirrored ends and a drilled hollow-core anti-resonant fiber33,34. The composition of the liquid-gas separation membrane is divided into two cases. For gaseous or water-dissolved gas detection, a hydrophobic Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) membrane with an internal porous structure (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3a–b) is used as the separation membrane, which can not only filter impurities in the sample gas but also achieve water-gas separation. For oil-dissolved gas detection, a Teflon AF2400 membrane is deposited on the surface of PTFE membrane via dip-coating to achieve oil-gas separation (as shown in Supplementary Fig. 3c–d).Fig. 3Gaseous and dissolved characteristic gases detection system based on FRCERS.a The schematic diagram of the coaxially nested component. b The mechanical structure of the coaxially nested component. c The schematic diagram of the gaseous and dissolved characteristic gases detection system.

Figure 3b shows the mechanical structure of the coaxially nested component (more details can be found in Supplementary Note 4), and its fabrication process and characteristics can be found in Methods section. Among them, both gas and liquid samples can enter through port 1. For gas detection, port 2 should be closed, and the sample gas is filtered through the separation membrane into the FRC for detection. For dissolved gas detection, a peristaltic pump circulates the sample through ports 1 and 2, and then the separation membrane separates the characteristic gas dissolved in the sample into the hollow-core area of the drilled HC-ARF for detection.

Figure 3c is the schematic diagram of the gaseous and dissolved characteristic gases detection system based on FRCERS. The laser (Precilasers, 532 nm) is modulated at a high frequency (20 MHz) by an electro-optic modulator (EOM) and then aligned collinearly by adjusting the tilt and rotation of a mirror and a dichroic mirror. The aligned laser is coupled into the FRC using an adjustable lens and a five-axis nano-positioning stage. Under optimal coupling, the Pound-Drever-Hall (PDH) error signal exhibits a monotonic linear region with a single zero-crossing, enabling stable frequency locking to the FRC resonance. However, if fiber bending or alignment errors occur, the error signal becomes non-monotonic and introduces multiple spurious zero-crossings, which severely degrade the locking stability. To address this issue, a loose PDH locking technique has been proposed to achieve stable locking (can be maintained over many tens of hours, further details are provided in Supplementary Note 6). The enhancement performance of asymmetric-reflectivity FRC

To evaluate the influence of input mirror reflectivity on Raman signal enhancement performance, FRCs consisting of a 32 cm HC-ARF (transmission loss: 0.06 dB/m) and mirrors with different reflectivity are constructed. Under a laser power of 500 mW and a measurement time of 200 s, the Raman spectra of a standard-concentration gas mixture were measured using symmetric- and asymmetric-reflectivity FRCs, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 16. Compared to Supplementary Fig. 16a and b, it is evident that for the symmetric-reflectivity FRC, the Raman signal is significantly suppressed at wavenumbers below 2500 cm⁻¹, and this suppression trend is consistent with the simulation results (red line in Supplementary Fig. 16a). Supplementary Fig. 16c presents a comparison between theoretical and measured enhancement factors, showing a similar trend for both. The measured enhancement factors agree with the simulated values at wavenumbers above 2000 cm⁻¹, whereas below 2000 cm⁻¹, the measured enhancement deviates more noticeably from the theoretical predictions. To evaluate the performance of the coaxially nested component for gaseous and dissolved characteristic gases in real samples, embedded and external detection experiments were conducted at a laser power of 500 mW on various samples including human breath gases, dissolved gases in battery electrolyte, and dissolved gases in mineral insulating oil.

Human breath gas detection

The detection of CO2 isotopes, CH4, and H2 in breath gases can be used for diagnosing gastrointestinal diseases. Before gas detection, the coaxially nested component is first evacuated. The sample gas from a 28-year-old volunteer then flows into the component under negative pressure, with a response time of 20 s, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 20. To compare the changes in gas composition during human respiration, the coaxially nested component is used to analyse ambient air and breath samples, as shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Comparison of detection results between ambient air and human breath gas.Ambient air is drawn into the coaxially nested assembly by a vacuum pump for detection, human breath samples are collected using aluminium foil sampling bags and then introduced into the coaxially nested component for detection.

Due to the high CO2 concentration in breath gas, its rotational-vibrational peak can be clearly obtained within 0.2 s of spectral acquisition. Using the CO2 peak at 1388 cm⁻¹ for quantitative analysis, the CO2 concentration in breath is determined to be 3.9%. Moreover, a distinct characteristic peak at 1370 cm⁻¹, attributed to 13CO2 is observed, which can be utilized for diagnosing gastric and duodenal diseases. When the measurement times are 60 s and 200 s, the CH4 (v₂ vibrational mode, 2915 cm⁻¹) and H2 (Q1(1) at 4157 cm⁻¹), which can reflect the state of gut microbiota, can be observed in breath gas. Detection of dissolved gases in battery electrolytes

When a lithium-ion battery (LIB) experiences internal faults such as local internal short circuits, overcharging, thermal runaway, or mechanical damage, the electrolyte and electrode materials of the LIB decompose and generate gases, and then dissolve into the electrolyte. Detecting dissolved gases (CH4, C2H4, H2, CO2, CO and HF, etc.) in the electrolyte can help determine the type of fault and the decomposition pathways of LIB materials35,36. In this experiment, a LiNi1Co1Mn1O2 (NCM111)-graphite LIB (the maximum charging voltage is 4.2 V) is used as a sample and the coaxially nested component is implanted into the battery, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 23. The implantation procedure is shown in the Methods section. Before overcharging (black line), slight decomposition of the electrolyte occurs, leading to the appearance of CO2 Raman peaks at 1285 cm⁻¹ and 1388 cm−1, C2H4 Raman peaks at 1346 cm−1, 1627 cm−1, and 3018 cm−1, as shown by the black line in Fig. 5. Peaks 1388 cm−1 and 3018 cm−1 are used for quantitative analysis of CO2 and C2H4, and their concentrations are 40.3 ppm and 4.5 ppm, respectively (the calibration curve is shown in Supplementary Fig. 24). After overcharging (red line), characteristic gas peaks of H2, CO2, CH4, C2H4 and CO can be clearly observed. The lithium dendrite growth caused by overcharging can penetrate the solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) layer, leading to further decomposition of the electrolyte and generating large amounts of CO2 and C2H4. The S0(1), S0(2) and S0(3) vibration peaks of H2 can be observed at 582 cm−1, 812 cm−1 and 1034 cm−1, which are generated by the reaction between the graphite electrode and the binder. The v0 vibration mode produced by CO can be found at 2146 cm−1, which is generated by the decomposition of the battery electrolyte and the SEI layer. The v1 vibration mode of CH4 can be found at 2915 cm−1, which also resulting from SEI layer decomposition. In addition, by comparing the spectra before and after overcharging, a weak Raman peak near 3958 cm⁻¹ is observed, which corresponds to the v₀ vibrational mode of hydrofluoric acid. Using the Raman peaks at 1388 cm−1, 3018 cm−1, 582 cm−1, 2145 cm−1 and 2915 cm−1 for quantitative analysis, the concentrations of dissolved CO2, C2H4, H2, CO and CH4 in the electrolyte are determined to be 2135.9 ppm, 1673.4 ppm, 3657.3 ppm, 146.8 ppm and 133.6ppm, respectively. Benefiting from the capability of simultaneously and dynamically detecting multiple gaseous species, the proposed technique enables further refinement of the battery material decomposition pathways during LIB failure (the reaction pathways provided in the Supplementary Note 14).

Detection of dissolved gases in mineral insulating oil

When internal faults (such as overheating of oil and paper, partial discharge, spark discharge, arc discharge, etc.) occur oil-immersed power equipment, the internal insulating materials (insulating oil (mineral insulating oil, vegetable insulating oil, synthetic ester insulating oil, etc.) and insulating paper of the equipment will decompose and generate gases, and then dissolve in the insulating oil. Detecting the gases dissolved in the insulating oil can be used to determine the fault type of equipment and to better understand the decomposition pathways of the insulating materials. To detect dissolved gases in insulating oil, the gases are generally separated from the oil and then pumped into the gas chamber for detection. The whole process takes up to 3 h, resulting in delayed fault detection. To evaluate the application performance of coaxially nested component in this field, a needle-plate electrode is used to perform breakdown discharge in a liquid pool of mineral insulating oil (the oil temperature was stabilized at 50 °C using a temperature control system, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 25) for 10 min. Then, mineral insulating oil containing dissolved gases was circulated into coaxially nested component at flow rate of 50 mL/min for gas detection (measurement time of 60 s). Discussion

This study demonstrates that the coaxially nested component consisting of an asymmetric-reflectivity FRC and separation membrane can achieve a LOD of gas Raman spectroscopy as low as 0.01 ppm*bar, with a gas consumption ranging from 0.3 mL to 2 mL.

Firstly, we proposed the Raman signal intensity model of the fiber resonant cavity to optimize the cavity structural parameters. By analysing the influence of the FRC construction parameters on Raman signal intensity, it is found that the high transmission loss of HC-ARF in the visible band (about 0.06 dB/m, 30 times higher than in the infrared band33,37) leads to increased Raman signal attenuation when higher-reflectivity mirrors are used. Based on the Raman signal intensity model of FRC, it is found that an asymmetric-reflectivity FRC (with the input mirror reflectivity slightly lower than that of the output mirror) can reduce the transmission loss of the laser and Raman signal in FRC, the gain effect for N2 at 2328 cm−1 of the asymmetric reflectivity FRC is 6 times higher than that of the symmetric reflectivity case. We should emphasize here the reflectivity of the input cavity mirror is not fixed; its optimal reflectivity decreases as the intracavity loss increases.

At a laser power of 500 mW and a measurement time of 60 s, the LOD of CH4 can reach 0.014 ppm*bar. Finally, a coaxially nested component consisting of side-drilled FRC and separation membrane is proposed, which can enable approximate synchronization of liquid-gas separation and detection. Based on which, high-sensitivity and dynamic Raman detection of gaseous and dissolved characteristic gases in the fields of medical diagnostics, energy storage, and energy power systems are achieved. For gaseous gas detection, the response time is less than 20 s; for oil-dissolved gas detection, when combining with the proposed dynamic estimation algorithm, the response time is less than 10 min. Additionally, we demonstrate the applicability of this technique for dynamic, highly sensitive and simultaneous detection of SF₆ and C₅F₁₀O as well as their respective decomposition products. Based on which, the corresponding refined decomposition pathways of SF₆ and C₅F₁₀O are provided in Supplementary Note 21.

In the future, the sensitivity of this component can be improved by further reducing the transmission loss of HC-ARF in the visible band. If the loss can be further reduced to 0.001 dB/m, the sensitivity of Raman spectroscopy in this study can reach ppb level or even ppt level. Sensitivity may also be enhanced by increasing the cavity finesse through higher output mirror reflectivity (one should know that higher finesse makes frequency stabilization more difficult and may limit applicability in harsh environments) or by combining the coaxially nested architecture with absorption based methods such as absorption, photothermal, or photoacoustic spectroscopy, etc.

Methods

Fabrication and characterization of coaxially nested component

The coaxially nested component is composed of an asymmetric hollow-core FRC and a separation membrane, in which the FRC is composed of a 32 cm long HC-ARF and LMAFs with high-reflection mirrored ends. The physical picture is shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, and the fabrication process is as follows: (1) the LMAFs are first fixed on the ceramic ferrule with epoxy resin, and then high-reflectivity TiO2/SiO2 dielectric coatings with reflectivity of 99.2% and 99.9% (corresponding to the 532 nm band) are deposited onto the end faces of the two LMAFs via ion beam deposition; (2) the 27.5 cm long separation membrane is passed through the drilled HC-ARF and fixed to the end of the ceramic ferrule by epoxy resin; (3) the end faces of the HC-ARF are tightly attached to the mirrored end faces of the LMAFs and fixed with a ceramic sleeve to ensure that the resonant light does not leak from the cavity (the coupling efficiency from large-mode-area fiber to HC-ARF reaches 90%).

At the same time, a gas cell composed of two tees and a Teflon tube is designed to enable the coaxially nested component to realize the detection of gaseous and dissolved characteristic gases, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 4c. Meanwhile, the ceramic ferrule of the HC-ARF is sealed and fixed in the tee with epoxy resin, ensuring the airtightness of the FRC.

Subject definition and test conditions in breath analysis

A 28-year-old healthy male volunteer who had fasted overnight participated in the morning measurement. Human breath samples were collected in aluminum foil sampling bags and subsequently introduced into the coaxially nested component by evacuating the component to introduce the gas sample.

The process of coaxially nested component implanted into NCM111-graphite LIB

The coaxially nested component is inserted through the interior of the NCM111-graphite LIB, allowing the dissolved gases in the LIB electrolyte to pass through the separation membrane of the component and enter the drilled HC-ARF for the detection of dissolved gases, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 23. The implantation procedure is as follows: (1) inside an argon-filled glovebox, 1.5 mm-diameter holes are drilled at both ends of the aluminium-plastic film of the NCM111-graphite LIB using a bench drill. Then, the drilled HC-ARF equipped with a separation membrane is inserted through the LIB and sealed in place with epoxy resin; (2) LMAFs with high-reflection mirrored ends are connected to the HC-ARF using a ceramic sleeve to form a coaxially nested component; (3) the interfaces between the ceramic sleeve and the ceramic ferrule are sealed with epoxy resin to prevent oxygen from entering the NCM111-graphite LIB.

Data acquisition and processing

In our experiments, Raman spectra were recorded using an HRS-300-S spectrometer (Princeton Instruments) equipped with a PIXIS™ 400B CCD detector and a 1200 g/mm grating. Baseline correction was performed using the asymmetric least squares smoothing method with a smoothing factor of 2, a threshold of 0.001, and 10 iterations.
