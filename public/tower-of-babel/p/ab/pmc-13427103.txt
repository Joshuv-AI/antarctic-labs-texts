Open Conversion at Robotic Adrenalectomy: Rates and Predictors

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRates and predictors of open conversion at robotic adrenalectomy (rRA) are unknown.MethodsWe identified patients treated with rRA (National Inpatient Sample 2009‐2019). First, temporal trends in open conversion were assessed using Estimated Annual Percentage Changes (EAPC). Second, multivariable logistic regression models were used to identify predictors of open conversion during rRA.ResultsOf 2,292 rRA patients, 286 (12.0%) underwent open conversion. The rates of open conversion decreased over time from 39.2% to 10.2% over the study span (EAPC −15.9%; p = 0.0003). Open conversion patients during rRA were more frequently male (59.0 vs. 53.0%; p = 0.04) and had more comorbidities (Charlson Comorbidity Index [CCI] ≥ 4: 28.0 vs. 17.0%; p < 0.001). In multivariable logistic regression models predicting open conversion, CCI ≥ 4 versus 0‐3 (OR: 2.0; p < 0.001) and low versus medium‐high surgical volume hospital (OR: 2.0; p < 0.001) were independent predictors.A dose‐response effect was observed when CCI and hospital volume were combined. Specifically, the rates of open conversion were respectively 13.9 versus 25.9% in patient with CCI 0‐3 versus CCI ≥ 4 at low surgical volume hospitals (p < 0.001). Conversely, the rates of open conversion were respectively 7.7 versus 13.4% in patient with CCI 0‐3 versus CCI ≥ 4 at medium‐high surgical volume hospitals (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe rate of open conversion at rRA significantly decreased over time from 39.2% to 10.2%. Individuals at particularly elevated risk consisted of patients with CCI ≥ 4 in whom rRA is contemplated at low‐volume hospitals (25.9%). Relegation of such individuals to medium‐high surgical volume hospitals may reduce the conversion rate by half (13.4%).

Introduction

Robotic radical adrenalectomy (rRA) is an emerging surgical technique that offers a minimally invasive approach to adrenal gland surgery [1, 2, 3]. When rRA is performed, an open conversion may be required due to intraoperative complications, failure to progress, anatomical difficulties, or cancer control considerations [4, 5]. Previous studies have reported variable open conversion rates, typically ranging between 0% and 10%, depending on laparoscopic surgical technique, tumor size, pathology, and surgeon experience [6, 7, 8]. Additionally, Pirzada et al. described open conversion rates in a systematic review comparing minimally invasive to open adrenalectomy [9]. However, evidence regarding how these rates have evolved over time in parallel with the widespread adoption of robotic platforms and growing surgical expertise remains limited. Similarly, patients and/or institution characteristics that might be associated with open conversion are equally unknown. Using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database (2009–2019), we aim to assess temporal trends and predictors of conversion from robotic to open adrenalectomy. We hypothesized that such variables may be identified with the intent of avoiding the combination of unfavourable patient characteristics along with institution characteristics that might predispose to high conversion rates.

Methods

Data Source

To test for rates of open conversion during robotic RA, we relied on discharge data from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS 2009‐2019). The NIS is a collection of longitudinal hospital inpatient databases that are part of the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP), developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) [10]. All diagnoses and procedures were coded using the International Classification of Disease (ICD) 9th revision Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM), ICD 10th revision Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM), as well as ICD 10th revision Procedure Coding System (ICD‐10‐PCS). Due to the deidentified nature of the NIS, International Review Board approval was not required for this study.

Study Population

First, we selected patients who underwent RA (ICD‐9‐CM codes: 07.2, 07.21, 07.29; ICD‐10‐CM codes: 0G524ZZ, 0G533ZZ, 0G534ZZ, 0G543ZZ, 0G544ZZ, 0GB23ZZ, 0GB24ZZ, 0GB33ZZ, 0GB34ZZ, 0G523ZZ) either for benign conditions or for malignancies. Only patients treated with robotic approach were included (ICD‐9‐CM code: 17.4x, ICD‐10‐CM codes: 8E0W0CZ, 8E0W3CZ, 8E0W4CZ, 8E0W7CZ, 8E0W8CZ, 8E0WXCZ). Subsequently, patients were stratified according to open conversion (ICD‐9‐CM codes V64.4 and V64.41; ICD‐10‐CM Z53.31, Z53.3, Z53.39) according to previously established methodology [4, 5].

Definition of Variables for Analyses

Demographic characteristics consisted of age at admission, sex (male vs. female), race (Caucasian, African‐American and Others), Charlson Comorbidity Index (0–3 vs. ≥ 4), diagnosis (benign condition vs. malignancy), as well as hospital characteristics including teaching hospital status (teaching vs. non‐teaching), hospital bedsize (large [≥ 400 beds] vs. medium [200‐399 beds] vs. small [< 200 beds]) and hospital surgical volume. The hospital volume variable was constructed by first calculating, for each year, the total number of procedures performed in each hospital. Hospitals were then divided into tertiles (low, medium, high volume) based on their annual case volume. Each patient inherited the volume category of the hospital where they were treated. Additional binary variables were later created to group hospitals as low v. high‐medium.

Statistical Analyses

First, patient and hospital characteristics were tabulated. Medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were calculated for continuously coded variables. Frequencies and proportions were computed for categorical variables. Wilcoxon rank sum test and Pearson chi‐square test were applied. Second, estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) were used to assess temporal trends in open conversion rates over time. Specifically, the EAPC was calculated using a generalized linear model under the assumption of linearity on the log scale, which corresponds to assuming a constant rate of change. Third, open conversion rates at robotic RA were tested in multivariable logistic regression (MLR) models. Variables selected for MLR were based on clinical relevance and prior literature, rather than solely on univariate p‐values [4, 5]. These variable consisted of age at admission, sex, race/ethnicity, diagnosis, teaching hospital status, hospital bedsize. All multivariable models relied on generalized estimating equations to further adjust for clustering [4, 5]. All analyses and their reporting followed the NIS reporting guideline. Specifically, exact counts and associated proportions were not reported for samples with membership < 11 [10]. All tests were two sided, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. R software environment was used for statistical computing and graphics (R version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Descriptive Characteristics of the Study Population

Within the NIS (2009‐2019), we identified 2,292 patients treated with rRA. Of those, 286 (12.0%) underwent open conversion. Patients who underwent open conversion were more frequently male (59.0 vs. 53.0%; p = 0.04) and exhibited a higher number of comorbidities (CCI ≥ 4: 28.0 vs. 17.0%; p < 0.001). Additionally, open conversion more frequently occurred in low volume hospitals than medium‐high volume hospitals (63 vs. 37%; p < 0.001). Conversely, no statistically significant differences (all p‐values > 0.2) were recorded in age at admission, race/ethnicity, diagnosis, teaching hospital status and hospital bedsize (Table 1).

Rates of Open Conversion at rRA over the Study Period (2009–2019)

Rates of open conversion at rRA were plotted over the study period (2009‐2019). The proportion of open conversion during rRA (Figure 1) decreased from 39.2% to 10.2% (EAPC: −15.9%, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −21.3, −11.1, p = 0.0003).

Crude Rates of Open Conversion at rRA According to Patient and Hospital Characteristics

The rates of open conversion were virtually the same according to CCI stratification 0 versus 1 versus 2 versus 3 (12.8 vs. 11.8 vs. 10.7 vs 14.0%; p = 0.9). Conversely a significantly higher open conversion rate (p < 0.001) was recorded in patients with CCI ≥ 4 (19.4%) versus CCI 0–3 (11.0%). Open conversion rates were virtually the same for high (7.4%) and medium (10.5%) volume hospitals (p = 0.9). Conversely, higher rate of open conversion (p < 0.001) was recorded at low (15.4%) versus medium‐high (9.3%) surgical volume hospitals. No other patient or hospital characteristics were significantly associated with open conversion at rRA (Figure 2).

Multivariable Analyses Predicting Open Conversion at rRA

In multivariable logistic regression models (Table 2), CCI ≥ 4 independently predicted twofold higher rate of open conversion (Odds ratio [OR]: 2.0; p < 0.001). Similarly, low versus medium‐high surgical volume hospital also independently predicted twofold higher rate of open conversion (OR: 2.0; p < 0.001). No other independent predictors (age at admission, sex, race/ethnicity, diagnosis, teaching hospital status, hospital bedsize) were identified (Table 2).

Multivariable Analyses Predicting Open Conversion at rRA: Testing Dose‐Response Effects

Separate multivariable analyses tested for potential dose‐response effects that may result from combining of CCI (≥ 4 vs. 0–3) and hospital volume (low vs. medium‐high) in models addressing open conversion rate. In univariable analyses, the combination of low hospital surgical volume characteristic with either CCI ≥ 4 or alternatively

CCI 0‐3 yielded open conversion rates of 25.9% and 13.9%. Conversely, the combination of medium‐high hospital surgical volume characteristic with either CCI 0‐3 or CCI ≥ 4 yielded open conversion rates of 7.7% and 13.4% (Figure 2). In multivariabe logistic regression models adjusted for all other variables (age at admission, sex, race/ethnicity, diagnosis, teaching hospital status, hospital bedsize) where the reference consisted on the combination of CCI 0‐3 and of medium‐high surgical volume hospitals, the combination of CCI ≥ 4 and medium‐high surgical volume hospitals independently predicted 1.9‐fold higher rate of open conversion rate. In the same models, the combination of CCI 0‐3 and medium‐high surgical volume hospitals independently predicted 1.9‐fold higher open conversion rate. Finally, in the same models, the combination of CCI ≥ 4 and low surgical volume hospitals independently predicted 4.1‐fold higher open conversion rate (Table 2).

Discussion

Contemporary rates and predictors of open conversion during robotic radical adrenalectomy are unknown. We addressed this knowledge gap. Additionally, we attempted to identify patient and/or institution predictors of higher open conversion in this patient population. We made several noteworthy observations.

First, open conversion occurred in 12.0% (n = 286) of rRA patients (n = 2,292). To the best of our knowledge, the current report represents the first population‐based analysis of open conversion during rRA. Previous single‐center series have reported substantially lower conversion rates, ranging from 0% to 2.5% [Kahramangil et al., (2018); Brandao et al. (2014); Dickson et al. (2013)], likely reflecting the experience of high‐volume centers and carefully selected patient populations. In contrast, our higher nationwide rate likely results from the inclusion of low‐volume hospitals and unselected cases, consistent with our finding that hospital volume was an independent predictor of conversion. Additionally, comparisons with conversion rates at similar surgeries may be attempted. For example, prior studies reported open conversion rates of 0.6% for minimally invasive partial nephrectomy (MIPN) and 3.8% for radical prostatectomy (RARP) [4, 5]. These open conversion rates are substantially lower than the 12% open conversion rate at robotic RA in the current study [4, 5]. However, this discrepancy is clearly attributable to substantially higher expertise with MIPN than with rRA. Virtually the same explanation may be proposed for RARP versus rRA open conversion rates.

Second, the proportion of open conversion during rRA decreased in both clinically meaningful and highly statistically significant fashion over the course of the study: from 39.2% to 10.2% (EAPC: −15.9%, p = 0003) (Figure 1). It is noteworthy that the recorded number of rRA patients sharply increased from 51 in the initial study year to 537 in the final study year. In consequence, the observed average open conversion rate at rRA (12%) predominantly originates from the large number of contemporary rRAs and it is relatively little affected by the small number of historic rRAs. The observed decrease in open conversion rates from nearly 39.2% to 10.2% is in agreement with the practice makes perfect hypothesis, where robotic surgeons gradually acquired better expertise that ultimately resulted in lower open conversion rates. This phenomenon was also observed in MIPN and RARP data, where equally pronounced decrease in open conversion rates also occurred over time [4, 5].

Third, open conversion rates varied across patient and hospital characteristics. Although sex, CCI, and hospital surgical volume were significant in univariable analyses, only CCI ≥ 4 (OR 2.0; p < 0.001) and low hospital volume (OR 2.0; p < 0.001) remained independent predictors in multivariable models. Conversion rates were stable across CCI values 0–3 and increased only at CCI ≥ 4, justifying this cutoff in subsequent analyses. Similarly, medium‐ and high‐volume centers showed comparable rates, whereas low‐volume hospitals demonstrated a marked increase. These patterns confirm the central role of CCI ≥ 4 and low hospital volume as key determinants of open conversion during rRA.

Fourth, further testing of CCI ≥ 4 versus CCI 0‐3 and of low versus medium‐high hospital surgical volume examined potential interaction effect. Specifically, CCI strata (≥ 4 vs. 0‐3), as well as hospital volume strata (low vs. medium‐high) were combined to test for clinically meaningful differences that are supported by statistically significant values or ideally independent predictor status. Indeed, the combination of two different CCI levels and two different surgical hospital volumes levels that yielded four combined categories, with both highly clinically meaningful differences and highly statistically significant values, supporting the pertinence of this differences. Specifically, conversion rates ranged from 7.7% for CCI 0‐3 and medium‐high hospital volume, through 13.4% for CCI ≥ 4 and medium‐high surgical hospital volume, and 13.9% CCI 0‐3 and low‐hospital volume, to as high as 25.9% for CCI ≥ 4 and low surgical hospital volume (p < 0.001). In multivariable models that included these four levels of risk, independent predictor status of the proposed dose response scheme was confirmed.

Specifically, CCI ≥ 4 patients treated at low surgical volume hospitals (observed open conversion rates: 25.9%) were 4.1‐times more likely to undergo open conversion then their counterparts (CCI 0‐3 and medium‐high surgical hospital volume). Intermediate OR values of 1.9 and 1.9 were respectively recorded for CCI ≥ 4 patients treated at medium‐high hospitals and CCI 0‐3 patients treated at low volume hospitals. These observations indicate that CCI ≥ 4 and low surgical hospital volume not only represent key determinants of open conversion at rRA but additionally their combination or individual consideration resulted in absolute and relative values that are perfectly compatible with what would be expected from a dose‐response effect. Neither the independent predictor status of CCI and hospital surgical volume, nor the combined effect of various CCI and hospital surgical volume categories have been previously tested. In consequence, direct comparison cannot be made.

Despite the novelty of our observations, the present study is not devoid of limitations.

First, selection and reporting biases may have remained due to the retrospective nature of the NIS. This limitation applies to the current study as well as to all previous analyses relying on the NIS [11, 12] or other large‐scale retrospective databases, such as the Surveillance Epidemiology and End Results database. Second, data included in the current report may be relatively old, and the observed conversion rates may overestimate current figures given advances in robotic surgery and surgeon experience. Third, the amount of details included in the current analysis was limited due to the nature of the NIS. For example, the exact nature of the lesion treated with rRA, its dimension, as well as surgical time and surgeon experience were not available. Fourth, the NIS database does not provide information on patient performance status, ASA score, CCI lookback period, or laboratory values. Fifth, the NIS database does not capture specific reasons for open conversion at rRA.

Nevertheless, the present study provides the most detailed and methodologically structured analysis on rates and predictors of open conversion during rRA.

Conclusion

The rate of open conversion at rRA significantly decreased over time from 39.2% to 10.2%. Individuals at particularly elevated risk consisted of patients with CCI ≥ 4 in whom rRA is contemplated at low‐volume hospitals. Relegation of such individuals to medium‐high surgical volume hospitals may cut that rate in half (13.4%).

SYNOPSIS

Open conversion during robotic radical adrenalectomy occurred in 12% of cases and declined significantly from 2009 to 2019. High comorbidity (CCI ≥ 4) and low hospital surgical volume were the strongest independent predictors of conversion, with their combination conferring the highest risk and supporting referral of complex cases to higher‐volume centers.
