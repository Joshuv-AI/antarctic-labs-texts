Type D personality and quality of life among polish university students: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Iga Florczyk, Maciej Stokłosa, Gniewko Więckiewicz, Paweł Dębski, Piotr Gorczyca
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Type D personality has been linked to poorer psychosocial functioning and selected somatic conditions, yet evidence in student populations remains limited. This study examined associations between Type D personality traits and quality of life among Polish university students, and explored whether negative affectivity and social inhibition show domain-specific patterns. We conducted a cross-sectional, anonymous online survey among students in Poland. Type D personality was assessed using the DS-14, and quality of life using the WHOQOL-BREF. Participants reported clinician-diagnosed anxiety or depressive disorders and provided sociodemographic data. A total of 364 questionnaires were analyzed (60.2% women; age range: 19–41 years). Type D criteria were met by 50.5% of participants in this sample. Students classified as Type D reported lower quality of life across all WHOQOL-BREF domains (physical, psychological, social relationships, and environment). Self-reported anxiety and depressive disorders were more frequent in this group. In multivariable analyses, Type D personality remained associated with lower quality of life across all domains, while anxiety and/or depression showed domain-specific associations. In this student sample, Type D personality traits were common and consistently associated with lower multidimensional quality of life. These findings highlight the potential relevance of Type D traits for identifying students with poorer perceived well-being in academic settings.

Background

Type D (distressed) personality is a personality construct defined by the co-occurrence of negative affectivity (NA)—a tendency to experience anxiety, anger, and low mood—and social inhibition (SI)—a tendency to withdraw and to suppress emotional expression due to fear of negative evaluation1. A substantial body of research shows that this profile is associated with poorer psychosocial and health-related functioning. Reported correlates include higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms, lower quality of life, and less favorable health behaviors2. The most widely used measure is the DS-14 scale, and systematic reviews suggest that both components—NA and SI—contribute additively to poorer psychosocial and health outcomes3.

In the general population, Type D prevalence is typically estimated at 15–25%, depending on the sample and methodology, as reported in normative analyses and population-based reviews4. Higher rates have been observed in samples with cardiovascular conditions, including individuals with coronary artery disease or a history of myocardial infarction, where prevalence may reach 30–40%5,6. In Poland, high rates have also been reported among young adults in student samples, and Type D traits have been linked to poorer health self-care7. However, outside the context of chronic illness, the relationship between Type D personality and quality of life remains underexplored.

Beyond psychosocial outcomes, Type D personality has been linked to the development and poorer prognosis of somatic conditions, particularly cardiovascular disease. In a meta-analysis of 49 clinical studies, Lodder et al. showed that patients with coronary artery disease and heart failure more often displayed Type D traits, which were associated with worse clinical outcomes and a higher risk of cardiac complications8. Similarly, a systematic review by Sánchez-Díaz et al. suggested that Type D personality may serve as a marker of poorer quality of life and greater vulnerability to comorbid mood disorders in people with chronic conditions, including cancer and dermatological diseases9. Together, these findings indicate that the combination of NA and SI may contribute to maladaptive coping and poorer adjustment to chronic illness, potentially amplifying pathophysiological processes and symptom burden.

Although Type D is often conceptualized as relatively stable, targeted psychosocial interventions may modify its expression and improve well-being. A randomized study showed that an 8-week mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) program reduced both negative affectivity and social inhibition10. In cardiac populations, a 12-week randomized educational intervention reported that rehabilitation programs and structured health education improved psychological well-being, health-promoting behaviors, and selected domains of quality of life among individuals with Type D traits11. From a practical perspective, multimodal approaches combining psychoeducation, mindfulness or relaxation training, and cognitive-behavioral elements appear promising.

Existing evidence on Type D personality largely comes from clinical samples, in which quality of life is closely tied to symptom burden and ongoing treatment. Evidence in young adults—especially university students—remains limited. As a result, it is still unclear whether Type D is associated with reduced quality of life outside chronic illness, and which WHOQOL-BREF domains (physical, psychological, social relationships, and environment) are most strongly related to its components. This question is particularly relevant during university years, a period marked by high adaptive demands and stress, but also a key opportunity for prevention and mental health promotion. Accordingly, we aimed to examine the association between Type D personality and quality of life among Polish university students using standardized measures (DS-14 and the validated WHOQOL-BREF), to assess the relative contribution of negative affectivity and social inhibition, and to estimate the prevalence of Type D traits in this group. The findings may be relevant to future public health strategies aimed at supporting mental well-being in academic settings.

Materials and methods

Participants

Participants were students from 20 universities in Poland, representing a range of study programs. A non-probability convenience sampling strategy was used because the study was conducted as an anonymous online survey distributed through existing student Facebook groups across multiple universities in Poland. This approach allowed us to reach students from different academic disciplines and locations in a feasible manner while preserving anonymity; however, it also limits representativeness and may increase the risk of self-selection bias. Participation was fully voluntary and anonymous. Only complete questionnaires were included in the final analysis. Eligible participants were adults (≥ 18 years) who, during the study period, reported current student status at a higher education institution in Poland, provided informed consent electronically, and completed the survey sufficiently to compute questionnaire scores. We excluded respondents who did not meet the inclusion criteria (e.g., non-students, age < 18 years, or no consent), had missing data that prevented calculation of scale scores, or provided responses indicating low data quality (e.g., substantial internal inconsistencies).

Measures

DS-14 scale

Type D personality was assessed using the DS-14 (Type D Scale-14) developed by Denollet, in the Polish adaptation by Ogińska-Bulik1,12. The instrument includes 14 items: seven assess Negative Affectivity (NA) and seven assess Social Inhibition (SI). Responses are recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“false”) to 4 (“true”). A score of ≥ 10 on both subscales classifies an individual as having Type D personality. The DS-14 shows high reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.88 for NA and 0.86 for SI) and good construct validity.

WHOQOL-BREF questionnaire

Quality of life was measured with the World Health Organization Quality of Life questionnaire (WHOQOL-BREF), a shortened version of the WHOQOL-100, using the Polish version developed by Jaracz et al.13,14. The WHOQOL-BREF consists of 26 items. Two items assess overall quality of life and general health satisfaction, while the remaining 24 items form four domains: Physical health—including energy, sleep, and capacity for work; Psychological—covering emotions, self-image, and concentration; Social relationships—assessing interpersonal relations and social support; Environment—including safety, financial resources, and access to services. Domain scores were calculated according to WHO guidelines, with higher scores indicating better quality of life.

Study procedure

The study was conducted as an online survey using Google Forms. This ensured full anonymity (no email addresses or IP addresses enabling participant identification were collected) and facilitated distribution within student communities. Administrators of student Facebook groups (gathering students from specific cohorts and study programs) were asked for permission to post the survey link in their groups. Before participation, respondents were informed about the study aims, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any time. Data were collected between March and November 2024.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Due to the anonymous and non-interventional nature of the survey, approval from the Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia in Katowice was not required (case number: PCN/CBN/0052/KB/214/23). To protect participants, the study was limited to adults (≥ 18 years), participation was fully voluntary and anonymous, and no directly identifying information, including email addresses or IP addresses, was collected. Before entering the survey, participants received information on the study purpose, the anonymous and voluntary nature of participation, and the option to discontinue participation at any time before submission. Electronic informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to questionnaire completion. The survey was distributed through student groups only after obtaining administrators’ permission, and no institutional incentives or coercive recruitment procedures were used.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using STATISTICA 13.3 (StatSoft, Kraków, Poland). Categorical variables were compared using the χ² (chi-square) test. Normality of continuous variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. For non-normally distributed continuous data, the Mann–Whitney U test was used for two-group comparisons, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for comparisons across multiple groups. Associations between variables were evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation. For regression analyses, a two-step approach was applied. First, univariate analyses were performed to examine associations between potential explanatory variables (including sociodemographic and clinical variables such as age and sex) and each WHOQOL-BREF domain. Variables that showed significant associations with the dependent variables were then entered into multivariable linear regression models, followed by backward elimination. To ensure the robustness of the multivariable linear regression models, diagnostic tests were performed. Multicollinearity was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) and tolerance values. Furthermore, the stability of the models and the potential influence of outliers were evaluated using Cook’s distance and leverage diagnostics. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 376 responses were collected. Twelve questionnaires were excluded due to incomplete data, leaving 364 cases for analysis. The sample comprised 60.2% women and 39.8% men, with an age range of 19–41 years.

Sample characteristics

Based on the DS-14, participants meeting the criteria for Type D personality constituted 50.5% of the sample. Table 1 presents participant characteristics stratified by Type D status (Type D group vs. non–Type D group). Most respondents were women, and the proportion of biological sex did not differ between groups.

The mean age was significantly lower in the Type D group than in the non–Type D group (Mann–Whitney U test; Z = 3.0; p = 0.002). Overall, 10% of respondents had at least one child. Having children was less common in the Type D group compared with the control group (χ² test; χ² = 7.9; p = 0.005).

Most participants lived away from their family home. No between-group differences were found for place of origin or current place of residence.

Medical history

Self-reported anxiety disorders were significantly more frequent in the Type D group than in the remainder of the sample (χ² test; χ² = 5.67; p = 0.017). Similarly, a higher proportion of respondents reporting a diagnosis of depression was observed in the Type D group (χ² test; χ² = 4.56; p = 0.032) as shown in Table 2.

Nearly 35% of respondents had attended at least one psychiatric consultation in their lifetime, and 6% reported a sexology consultation. Psychiatric medication use was reported by 19% of the sample. Only 1% reported sexual dysfunction. None of these variables differed significantly between groups.

No between-group differences were found in self-reported chronic conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, hypothyroidism, or polycystic ovary syndrome.

Regression analysis of variables associated with quality of life (WHOQOL-BREF)

Univariate and multivariate linear regression analysis with backward elimination of the variables was performed to identify factors associated with quality of life (WHOQOL-BREF) among the study population, as presented in Table 6. Diagnostic analyses for the regression models showed no issues with multicollinearity, with all VIF values ranging between 1.013 and 1.044 (tolerance > 0.95). Additionally, Cook’s distance values were well below the threshold of 1.0, indicating that no influential outliers or individual cases disproportionately affected the model parameters.

Discussion

In our sample, Type D personality was observed in 50.5% of respondents, which is relatively high compared with several reports in young adult populations. The prevalence of Type D personality observed in our study should not be interpreted as a population estimate for Polish university students. Rather, it reflects the proportion identified in an anonymous, self-selected convenience sample. This relatively high value may be related to the standard dichotomous DS-14 classification, the online self-report format, and recruitment through voluntary participation channels that may have preferentially attracted students experiencing higher distress or greater interest in mental health topics. In medical students, for instance, prevalence was 44% and was discussed in relation to cognitive load and coping styles15. By contrast, a prospective cohort of Japanese students reported a lower prevalence of 39.2%, underscoring how sample characteristics and study context may shape observed rates16. We found no between-group differences in place of origin or current residence, suggesting that the degree of urbanization was not a key factor associated with Type D status in this population. Instead, the observed prevalence may reflect individual and academic environment factors (e.g., grading pressure and chronic stress), as well as methodological considerations, including the dichotomous DS-14 classification and potential self-selection bias. Notably, a similar lack of variation in Type D prevalence across residential categories (rural areas vs. cities of different sizes) has also been reported elsewhere17.

The lower age observed in the Type D group may indicate that, in this sample, Type D traits were more frequently observed among younger students. This period is often associated with higher adaptive demands and sustained stress (e.g., a new environment, academic requirements, and grading pressure). However, studies in healthy student samples do not consistently show age differences between individuals with Type D personality and controls. This indicates that, within a narrow age range, effects may be subtle and dependent on sample structure and context (e.g., field of study or year of study)18. Data from clinical populations point to a different cross-sectional pattern. In some patient cohorts, Type D prevalence is higher in older age groups, suggesting that the association between age and Type D may vary across populations. This likely reflects sampling effects, cumulative disease burden, and long-term exposure to stress19. Therefore, our finding should be interpreted with caution. In additional analyses, it may be useful to adjust for potential confounders such as year of study or academic seniority, which could influence the observed age effect.

The significantly higher frequency of self-reported diagnoses of anxiety disorders and depression in the Type D group is consistent with the overlap between Type D traits and internalizing psychological burden. A similar pattern has been reported in young, physically healthy individuals. For example, among medical students, participants with Type D personality showed significantly higher anxiety and depression scores on the HADS, and Type D prevalence exceeded 50%. This pattern may be consistent with the role of academic context, including pressure, chronic stress, and competition; however, this interpretation cannot be tested directly in the present study20. Importantly, evidence also comes from large population-based studies in which Type D was associated with a higher risk of depression independent of somatic indicators. This is consistent with the view of Type D as a relatively stable psychological vulnerability, rather than merely a short-term correlate of current health status21. Although Type D personality was independently associated with lower quality of life in the multivariable models, its overlap with internalizing psychopathology should be interpreted cautiously. The DS-14 assesses a personality profile, whereas anxiety and depressive disorders in the present study were assessed through self-reported prior diagnoses. Because no standardized symptom severity scales were used, the independent contribution of these constructs cannot be fully disentangled. While Type D personality traits often overlap with internalizing symptoms like anxiety and depression, our multivariable analysis suggests that they represent a distinct psychological construct associated with quality of life. The very low VIF values observed in our study confirm that Type D traits and self-reported clinical diagnoses contributed independently to the models without significant multicollinearity. Moreover, the stability of these associations across a heterogeneous student sample was supported by the absence of influential outliers in the diagnostic checks (Cook’s distance analysis).

The breadth of the observed differences suggests that Type D personality was associated with lower subjectively perceived quality of life across multiple domains in this sample. This pattern is consistent with the conceptual characteristics of the Type D profile, including higher distress and social inhibition, which may reflect a more negative appraisal of everyday functioning. However, the mechanisms underlying these associations cannot be determined in a cross-sectional study. In young adults, a similar pattern has been reported among Korean university students. In a sample of 380 participants, Type D personality was significantly associated with lower quality of life, including indirect pathways involving health status and health-promoting behaviors. This suggests that the association between Type D and quality of life may also be observed outside clinical populations22. By contrast, a study using the DS-14 and WHOQOL-BREF in individuals with type 2 diabetes showed that Type D components were related to lower domain-specific quality of life, which is consistent with a broad and multidimensional association between this profile and well-being. It should be noted, however, that this was assessed in the context of chronic illness, which may strengthen the observed associations23. At the same time, relatively few studies have examined quality-of-life differences by Type D status in healthy, young student samples. This highlights an important research gap and underscores the value of the present findings.

Despite more frequent self-reported diagnoses of depression and anxiety in the Type D group, responses did not differ on the WHOQOL-BREF item assessing the perceived need for medical treatment to function in daily life. In young adults, this item may be interpreted mainly in somatic terms, while anxiety and depressive difficulties may be normalized or viewed as part of student stress, reducing its sensitivity to between-group differences. This is also consistent with evidence that social inhibition does not necessarily imply lower healthcare use. In some studies, Type D has been linked to greater help-seeking, possibly in relation to higher distress and negative affectivity, and these associations appear to depend on clinical and situational context24. Likewise, in urgent health decisions (e.g., time to seeking care in acute coronary syndrome), findings have been mixed and moderated by factors such as sex and negative affectivity, suggesting a conditional rather than uniform association of Type D with health behavior25.

Although Type D was consistently associated with lower quality of life across all WHOQOL-BREF domains, the DS-14 components showed different patterns. Negative affectivity was most strongly related to the physical, psychological, and environmental domains, whereas social inhibition was most strongly linked to social relationships. This is consistent with the interpretation that negative affectivity is more closely related to distress and negative self-appraisal, whereas social inhibition is more closely related to interpersonal withdrawal and perceived limitations in social functioning26. Prior work using WHOQOL-BREF suggests that the association between Type D and quality of life may be driven largely by dichotomized DS-14 classification, particularly negative affectivity, with domain-specific contributions of social inhibition27. Importantly, our multivariable models showed that Type D remained independently associated with all WHOQOL-BREF domains, whereas self-reported anxiety related mainly to the physical domain and depression to the psychological, social, and environmental domains. Taken together, these findings align with earlier reports indicating that Type D may show an association with quality-of-life outcomes even after accounting for co-occurring psychological burden28.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design does not allow conclusions regarding the direction or causality of the observed associations between Type D personality, self-reported psychological difficulties, and quality of life. In addition, we did not apply exclusion criteria related to the presence or severity of psychopathology. Second, the study was based on online recruitment and voluntary participation, which may have introduced self-selection bias and limited representativeness. In particular, students experiencing higher levels of distress or greater interest in mental health–related topics may have been more likely to participate. The predominance of women further limits the generalizability of the findings, especially because both Type D prevalence and quality of life may differ by sex and other sociodemographic factors.

Another limitation is the use of self-report measures. Although the DS-14 and WHOQOL-BREF are standardized instruments, responses may have been influenced by recall bias, current emotional state, and the online mode of administration. In addition, anxiety and depressive disorders were assessed through self-reported prior diagnoses and were not verified using clinical interviews, medical records, or standardized symptom severity scales. As a result, the independent contribution of Type D personality relative to co-occurring internalizing psychopathology cannot be fully disentangled.

The prevalence of Type D personality observed in this study should also be interpreted cautiously. Because we used the commonly applied dichotomous DS-14 classification, some information on the dimensional severity of negative affectivity and social inhibition may have been lost. Moreover, the high prevalence observed in our sample may partly reflect the characteristics of the recruited sample and the recruitment procedure, rather than the prevalence of Type D personality in the wider student population.

Finally, the sample was heterogeneous, including students from different academic majors and with varying mental health backgrounds, and we did not assess several potentially important correlates of quality of life, such as academic stress, social support, sleep quality, or year of study. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the regression models should be interpreted as limited and exploratory rather than fully explanatory. Future studies would benefit from more representative sampling, broader assessment of potential confounders, and the use of longitudinal designs. We also acknowledge that direct comparisons with previous studies should be made cautiously, as differences in prevalence estimates and effect sizes may partly reflect variation in recruitment procedures and modes of questionnaire administration (e.g., online self-report vs. paper-and-pencil assessment), in addition to sample characteristics.
