The nature of cultural competency and its constructs in health sciences: Insights from qualitative research in Iran
Authors: Samaneh Sargazi, Nikoo Yamani, Arash Najimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Cultural differences are an important issue in the healthcare system because they may lead to minority cultural groups receiving less appropriate healthcare services in comparison to the majority group. Healthcare providers need to recognize, diagnose, and address these challenges effectively by acquiring “cultural competency”. The aim of this study is to methodically define, extract, and articulate the constituent elements of cultural competency within the specific contextual parameters of Iran.MATERIAL AND METHOD:This research represents a qualitative study conducted at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in 2023 in 3 steps: 1. A literature review was undertaken to examine existing definitions of cultural competency in medical sciences 2. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with medical professors, cultural competency experts, and healthcare providers from different cities 3. Data extracted from literature review and transcribed interviews were imported into MaxQDa software and the content analysis was conducted.RESULTS:During the literature review 38 definitions were extracted. In addition, 14 interviews were conducted with 8 medical professors, 3 cultural competency experts, and 3 healthcare providers. From the analysis of the results of the literature review and qualitative study, 173 primary codes and two main themes (the essence of cultural competency and constructs of cultural competency were obtained.CONCLUSION:In this research, cultural competency is seen as a dynamic and evolving process. Various perspectives contributed to the definitions of cultural competency, demonstrating the inherently multifaceted nature of cultural competency, strongly tied to context, culture, and individuals. Moreover, various components were identified in four domains: cognitive, affective, psychomotor, and environmental for cultural competency.

Introduction

Cultural diversity is generally considered a fundamental characteristic of today’s world[1] and its importance cannot be understated in the realm of health.[2] The American Psychological Association declared that culture influences how illness experiences are described and understood and also affects symptomatology, treatment expectations, disease coping, and treatment response.[3]

Attention to the cultural aspect of patients is crucial in holistic care.[14] People prefer to experience health and illness from their cultural perspective, and they express their expectations of health services based on these experiences.[5] Evidence has shown that even individuals with similar illnesses may have different interpretations of their illness, its causes, treatment methods, and other related factors due to their diverse beliefs and cultures.[6] The influence of socio-cultural factors on individuals’ health beliefs is to the extent that, in some cases, individuals may prefer traditional healing methods provided by traditional healers over seeking healthcare from the formal health system.[7]

Alosaimi and Ahmed found that cultural, linguistic, and religious differences between nurses and patients hinder the proper execution of nursing duties.[8] Additionally, cultural differences may lead to minority cultural groups receiving less appropriate healthcare services in comparison to the majority group.[9]

Healthcare providers need to recognize, diagnose, and address these challenges effectively to ensure the delivery of high-quality care to all patients and one appropriate solution entails healthcare providers acquiring “cultural competency”.[10] In 1989 for the first time, Bazron and colleagues defined cultural competency as “a set of congruent behaviors, attitudes, and policies that come together in a system, agency, or among professionals to enable that system, agency, or professionals to work effectively in cross-cultural situations”.[1112] It enables individuals to provide effective care considering factors such as gender, age, religion, cultural beliefs, norms, and values of the counterpart.[13] A study indicated that health sector inequalities related to culture and ethnicity can be reduced through training students in this area.[14]

Despite the importance of this issue, cultural competency training has not been integrated into the official educational curricula of medical sciences in Iran, and even in continuing education courses for healthcare personnel, it is not adequately addressed. Heydari and colleagues concluded in their research that despite the daily interaction of nurses with patients from different cultures, the absence of cultural education and cultural care in the curriculum of nursing in Iran is evident.[1] In another study in Iran, nurses acknowledged their deficiencies in effectively communicating with patients from diverse cultural backgrounds.[15] Consequently, the authors of the present study identified a compelling imperative to methodically define, extract, and articulate the constituent elements of cultural competency and its definitions within the specific contextual parameters of Iran because it is necessary for any further attempts in the field of cultural competency.

Material and Methods

Study design and setting

This research represents a qualitative study employing content analysis, conducted at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences in 2023. Initially, a literature review was undertaken to examine existing definitions of cultural competency in the field. Definitions of “cultural competency” in the medical sciences, from the perspectives of experts, were investigated from the emergence of this term until 2020. A search was conducted on Web of Science, Scopus, Medline, Google Scholar, using keywords such as “Cultural competency,” “Cultural competencies,” “Cross-cultural competency,” “Cultural skill,” “Cultural knowledge,” and “Cultural awareness.” The obtained articles were imported into EndNote, and after reviewing titles and abstracts, a substantial number were excluded. Finally, through a comprehensive reading of the full-text articles, definitions of cultural competency and its components in medical sciences were compiled for data extraction form. Subsequently, semi-structured interviews were conducted (online and face-to-face) with medical professors, cultural competency experts, and healthcare providers.

Study participants and sampling

Participants were purposefully selected from cities such as Mashhad, Iranshahr, Zahedan, Isfahan, Tabriz, Sanandaj, Gorgan, Rasht, Khorram abad, and Ahvaz, representing Sistani, Balouch, Turk, Lor, Kurd, Gilak, Turkmen, Fars, and Arab ethnicities. The purposeful selection of participants was based on having more than 5 years of clinical experience or having information about cultural competency in the healthcare system.

Data collection tools and technique

Each interview lasted between 30 and 40 min. Field notes were also taken during the interviews. Participants were asked questions such as: What is cultural competency? What concepts does it entail? What constructs and features does it encompass? Interviews continued until saturation in data (no new codes and categories were identified), resulting in 14 interviews. All interviews were audio recorded and transcribed verbatim immediately after the end of each session. Data analysis was conducted continuously and concurrently with data. The data extraction form obtained from the literature review was also analyzed using MaxQDA software. The content analysis was conducted, employing the Graneheim and Lundman method. The analysis involved several steps: multiple readings of interviews to gain a comprehensive understanding, extraction of meaning units, categorization into condensed meaning units, summarization and categorization of condensed units, and selection of appropriate labels (codes). Subsequently, sub-categories were organized based on similarities and differences, leading to the creation of categories. Finally, suitable titles covering the resulting themes were selected.[16]

Ethical consideration

Participants signed the written informed consent for recording interviews. Also the approval from the ethics committee was obtained on 5/12/2021. Ethical code: IR.ARI.MUI.1400.014

Study rigour

To ensure the validity and reliability of the data, criteria from Guba and Lincoln, including confirmability, credibility, dependability, and transferability, were applied.[17] The entire transcripts of the interviews, inclusive of coding and initial categorization, underwent scrutiny by a proficient academic for confirmation. Credibility was fortified by conducting interviews within 6 months to mitigate potential biases associated with prolonged data collection, and participants were directed toward a specific topic to prevent unwarranted extension of data gathering. For dependability, the coding method underwent verification by multiple researchers. In terms of transferability, a comprehensive elucidation of the contextual setting, participant selection process, data collection methodology, and analytical procedures was provided, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings in diverse situations.

Upon completion of the analysis, definitions, and components of cultural competency were derived based on the integration of results from literature review and expert opinions within the Iranian context.

Results

Results are provided in 3 parts: literature review results, Qualitative Study Results, and Integration of results from literature review and qualitative study.

Literature review results

In the literature review phase, a total of 1533 articles were initially retrieved and imported into the EndNote software. After reviewing titles, 347 articles remained, and their abstracts were examined by one of the authors. In addition, 118 Articles were selected based on the study’s objectives for a full-text review. Ultimately, 45 articles related to definitions or components of cultural competency in medical sciences were thoroughly examined, and 38 definitions were extracted from them.

The findings revealed that the first person to coin the concept of culturally appropriate treatment was Dr. Madeleine Leininger, who, in the field of nursing, introduced the term “culturally congruent care.” This term served as the origin for the terminology of cultural competency in the medical sciences.[18] Subsequently, in the year 2002, Dr. Leininger defined cultural competency as encompassing various dimensions such as values, health beliefs, religion, and philosophy.[19] Social services were among the first fields to emphasize “cultural awareness” as a crucial factor in service provision.[20] However, as mentioned in the introduction, the term cultural competency was initially introduced in medical sciences by Bazron, Cross, and colleagues in 1989, outlining levels from cultural destructiveness to cultural proficiency.[21] Subsequently, scholars such as Bacote and Purnell provided definitions and presented models for cultural competency, which were expanded and finalized over time. Bacote defined cultural competency as “a process to acquire competencies such as cultural awareness, cultural knowledge, cultural skills, cultural encounter, and cultural desire,” emphasizing the acquisition of competence.[22] Purnell defined cultural competency as “a set of components, including cultural self-awareness, knowledge and understanding of the client’s culture, respect for cultural differences, openness to cultural encounters, and care based on cultural congruence”.[23] Multiple definitions of cultural competency were proposed by various scientific organizations, such as the Occupational Therapy Association, the Department of Health and Human Services, the Health Education Department, the Mental Health Center, and the American Medical Association. However, the most significant definition among them is that of the National Center for Cultural Competency in the United States, which requires organizations to have defined values and principles and exhibit behaviors, attitudes, policies, and structures that enable them to work effectively across cultures.[24] The American Psychological Association also emphasizes that cultural competency involves cultural humility, considering it a lifelong process that involves reflection and commitment.[25] In summary, cultural competency is a developmental process evolving over a long period, with both individuals and organizations exhibiting awareness, knowledge, and skills throughout the continuum of cultural competency.[26]

Theme one: Essence of cultural competency

Definitions obtained from the literature review and those provided by participants presented from different perspectives, categorized into 3 categories:

Category one: Competency acquisition in the cultural domain

Some definitions emphasize competency acquisition, considering cultural competency as obtaining knowledge, attitudes, and skills for working with patients from diverse cultures. For example, the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) defines cultural competency as “a collection of compatible behaviours, attitudes, and policies that converge in a system, agency, or among professionals and enable them to work effectively in cross-cultural conditions”.[27] A participant expressed: “When we talk about cultural competency, it means the person has the knowledge, has the necessary skills, and also has the right attitude towards their work, which affects the community”(P. 11). Another participant highlighted the need for healthcare providers to “acquire knowledge and skills based on the values and beliefs of that culture”(P. 8).

Category two: Culturally competent patient care

Definitions in this category emphasized treatment alignment with the patient’s culture as a prerequisite for successful care. Green emphasizes the ability of trained staff to adapt professional duties and work styles to the values and cultural preferences of clients.[20] Participant number 14 believed that a healthcare provider should “have the competence to play different roles well in different situations”(P. 14). Participant number 8 defined cultural competency as “providing care based on the values, beliefs, and convictions of the people and culture being cared for”(P. 8). In other words, “the service provided should be compatible with the culture of the recipient community”(P. 3).

Category three: Health Justice

This category included definitions that consider cultural competency as a necessity for achieving health justice. Emphasis is placed on providing high-quality services to all patients, regardless of their cultural backgrounds. Betancourt defines cultural competency as “the ultimate goal of a healthcare and workforce system is to provide the highest quality care to every patient, regardless of race, ethnicity, cultural background, or language proficiency”.[28] Shiu-Thornton stated that cultural competency is “a term that deals more with providing direct services to underserved populations, linguistic or culturally minority, or individuals who have difficulty accessing services”.[29] Cultural competency in medical education and professionalism, in essence, involves health justice, where physicians are obligated to offer services to all patients without considering cultural factors (P. 6).

Theme two: Constructs of cultural competency

In the content analysis of literature review and interviews, 8 sub-categories (components) emerged, categorized into 4 categories based on their nature.

Category one:

Affective perspective

This category included components related to affective and attitudinal aspects, referring to values, beliefs, and internal characteristics of individuals. In this category, components of cultural attitude, cultural sensitivity, and cultural desire are placed.

Cultural attitude

This component refers to having an appropriate attitude toward culture and cultural differences in the healthcare provider. Interviewee number 5 believed that the healthcare provider should “preserve human values and respect them”(P. 5). Another participant expressed, “The individual can be to some extent trained, and a proper attitude can be instilled in the student” (P. 8). Diaconu et al.[30] also stated that a culturally competent healthcare provider “values individuals, families, and communities and safeguards their dignity”.

Cultural sensitivity

Cultural sensitivity involves understanding, respecting, and feeling comfortable when dealing with patients of cultural diversity. Frusti considered individuals’ ability to respect the uniqueness of each person as an essential characteristic of a healthcare provider derived from having cultural sensitivity.[31] Giger believed that “real sensitivity can be demonstrated by individuals, healthcare systems, and communities only when self-awareness is combined with insight into others”.[32]

Cultural desire

Bacote defined cultural desire as the “motivation of healthcare providers to desire participation in the cultural competency process”.[22] Participant number 14 emphasized the importance of healthcare providers “enhancing their knowledge of different cultural details, terminologies, and immersing themselves in that culture”(P. 14).

Category two:

Cognitive perspective

In this category, components contributing to the cognitive enhancement of cultural competency in healthcare providers were gathered, including two components: cultural knowledge, and cultural awareness.

Cultural knowledge

Cultural knowledge refers to continuously acquiring information about different cultures. Participant number 13 believed that “one of the features of a culturally competent nurse is to be familiar with the language and concepts of the region where they work. Religious customs are also important to be familiar with”(P. 13). Additionally, participant number 12 stated, “You can study in this area or talk to people from different cultures and gain information”(P. 12). Suh defined cultural competency as a process that requires individuals and the healthcare system to increase their capabilities to be informed about cultural diversity.[33]

Cultural awareness

Cultural awareness means understanding the impact of a culture, helping individuals recognize their biases and prejudices, and establishing criteria to examine the values and perspectives of others. Giger emphasized that “awareness of cultural differences is essential for creating sensitivity and competence”.[32] Sue also stated that healthcare providers should be aware of “cultural values, biases, and their impact on relationships with clients”.[34] Participant number 3 also emphasized that “service providers must know their cultural differences and those of the people, including linguistic differences”(P. 3). Paige and colleagues expressed that cultural competency can serve as an understanding for healthcare providers about how their culture and the patient’s culture can affect the effectiveness of the patient-physician relationship concerning the patient’s health status, behaviors, and decisions made.[35] Participant number 9 believed that “someone with this cultural competency is, in my opinion, a student who has a good understanding of the cultural issues in the health field (P. 9).

Category three:

Practical perspective

In this subcategory, components leading to the enhancement of cultural competency in the psychomotor domain are compiled, incorporating the components of cultural skill and cultural communication.

Cultural skill

Cultural skill refers to having practical skills to work with diverse patients. Bacote defined cultural skill as the “ability to collect relevant cultural data regarding the patient’s health history, present problems, and perform accurate cultural-specific physical assessments”.[22] Sue also considered one element of cultural counseling to be the “ability to develop and implement appropriate cultural interventions”.[34] Participant number 8 stated that the healthcare provider must have the “skill to behave differently with patients of different cultures”(P. 8).

Cultural communication

This component refers to the healthcare provider’s ability to establish appropriate communication with diverse patients. Participant number 10 believed that “cultural competency is communication-based”(P. 10). According to participant number 11, “the first characteristic of a culturally competent nurse is communication; being able to have a good relationship with the patient”(P. 11). A cultural healthcare provider should be able to “facilitate effective and appropriate interaction with an individual who is linguistically and culturally different,” as well as “have appropriate and effective interaction with various groups of people”.[36] In other words, they should have the “ability to establish effective and appropriate communication in cross-cultural situations”.[37] Participant number 14 emphasized that “the healthcare provider, in addition to verbal communication, should also effectively use non-verbal communication and body language to convey eagerness to connect with the other person”(P. 14).

Category four:

Environmental perspective

The environmental perspective refers to a factor that, when present in the environment, leads to the creation of conditions for acquiring or enhancing cultural competency. This component is cultural encounter.

Cultural encounter

Cultural encounter refers to the interaction or engagement between two individuals with different cultures. Healthcare professionals need to have encounters with patients from diverse cultures to enhance their cultural competency. One of the participants expressed “The experience of dealing with Turkmen patients made me develop cultural sensitivity, and if I did not have this encounter, I might not have attached importance to these differences”(P. 12). Bacote also emphasized that healthcare providers are encouraged to directly participate in cross-cultural interactions with patients from diverse cultural backgrounds to enhance their cultural skills.[22]

Discussion

This research, conducted in three steps to find definitions of cultural competency and its components in medical sciences, ultimately yielded two main themes titled “The Essence of Cultural competency” and “Constructs of Cultural competency.”

A common point among most of the reviewed definitions is that cultural competency is seen as a dynamic and evolving process. Bacote contends that gaining cultural competency in the field of health services is a dynamic process, where the service provider continuously strives to acquire the ability to effectively work within the cultural context of the care recipient (individual, family, community).[22] Cross and colleagues introduce cultural competency as a growth process, where individuals move step by step toward cultural proficiency.[21] In interviews, participants also acknowledged the dynamism of cultural competency, considering it as one of its characteristics. Researchers like Brottman and their colleagues found, in examining texts related to cultural competency education, that cultural competency training never ends and is a lifelong process. Individuals acquire part of this competence during their encounters with various patients.[38] Nurok emphasizes that the concept of cultural competency in healthcare should be seen as a dynamic process.[39] All these findings indicate that cultural competency has no endpoint, and individuals always have opportunities for the enhancement and improvement of this skill.

In this research, various perspectives contributed to the definitions of cultural competency, demonstrating the inherently multifaceted nature of cultural competency, strongly tied to context, culture, and individuals. Brown and colleagues, analyzing the conceptual framework they had for cultural competency in treatment in 2017, described the term as complex and multifaceted, often indirectly expressed in various texts, leading to a lack of consensus on its meaning.[40] The complexity and multifaceted nature of cultural competency may stem from the nature of culture itself, which is inherently multifaceted and has diverse definitions.

Examining cultural competency through the lens of acquiring proficiency reveals that gaining proficiency in cultural knowledge, cultural attitudes, and cultural skills is an undeniable necessity in the field of health. Despite its significance, there are significant deficiencies in the knowledge of students and healthcare professionals.[41] From this perspective, cultural competency becomes a crucial skill in today’s world, signifying the acquisition of capabilities in understanding, communicating, and interacting with patients from diverse cultures.[42] The enhancement and acquisition of individuals’ capabilities in cultural competency can occur through various methods, including education, experience, guidance, and self-assessment.

Another aspect of cultural competency outlined in this research involves the sufficient understanding of the patient’s culture by the healthcare provider and the formulation of treatment and care plans based on the patient’s cultural characteristics. Patient culture encompasses beliefs, perspectives, values, behaviors, and customs influenced by factors such as race, ethnicity, region, gender, age, religion, and socioeconomic status. These elements can impact how problems are presented, treatment expectations, treatment goals, the patient-provider relationship, and the patient’s communicative style. Obtaining information about the culture and traditions of people, on the one hand, facilitates the improvement of healthcare services and reduces misunderstandings between service providers, patients, and their families. However, it enhances the quality of services provided to ethnic and cultural minorities, promoting health equity.[43] A culturally sensitive healthcare provider should be able to understand and respect the patient’s culture, possessing the necessary knowledge and skills to choose appropriate treatment methods based on the patient’s culture and establish effective communication with the patient and their family.

The third perspective on cultural competency is from the standpoint of health justice, meaning that the cultural competency of healthcare providers is a vital characteristic for reducing health inequalities among patients. This perspective emphasizes the attention given to the needs of cultural and linguistic minority groups, increasing their health outcomes. Recognizing the importance of this concept, cultural competency is considered a crucial factor in reducing systemic health inequalities and is defined accordingly.[44]

In reviewing various definitions of cultural competency in the examined articles, it can be generally stated that most definitions include or emphasize at least one of three components: cognitive, affective, and practical. However, some definitions place greater emphasis on the affective or attitudinal aspect,[212832344546] while others highlight the cognitive and functional aspects.[33474849] Betancourt and colleagues focused on addressing the cultural and social needs of patients, considering it the capability of the service provider and an organizational attribute.[28] Burchum emphasized the emotional domain and focused on understanding, sensitivity, and interaction, which were less emphasized in other definitions.[49] Purnell, in his definition, referred to knowledge, self-awareness, understanding, and respect for culture, as well as openness, as features of a culturally competent service provider, mostly related to the affective and cognitive domains.[23] In the current study, all these components were considered, and various components were identified in four domains: cognitive, affective, psychomotor, and environmental for cultural competency.

In the first place, a successful healthcare provider must be sensitive to cultural differences among patients, pay attention to them, and respect these differences. Additionally, they should have sufficient knowledge and understanding of the cultural context of people. This includes awareness of their beliefs, values, religious convictions, and customs. However, they should be able to apply this knowledge in practice so that the medical and care services they provide are culturally appropriate for the patient. This leads to better patient acceptance and, consequently, more effective treatment. All of these aspects come together effectively when the healthcare provider or service provider establishes a respectful and trustworthy connection with the patient, fostering initial trust between them.

Limitation and recommendation

One of the limitations was the impossibility of face-to-face interviews with all participants. Also, there was no access to the full text of some articles. The authors suggest interviewing policymakers of the health system in future research.

Conclusion

This research delved into the multifaceted nature of cultural competency within the realm of medical sciences and underscored the dynamic and evolving nature of this competency.

The definitions and components of cultural competency identified in this research underscored its cognitive, affective, and practical dimensions. The cognitive aspect involves acquiring knowledge about different cultures, the affective aspect relates to developing cultural sensitivity, and the practical aspect entails applying this knowledge and sensitivity in interactions with patients. These components were found to intersect and influence each other in a nuanced manner, contributing to the overall cultural competency of healthcare providers.

In essence, the findings of this research contribute to the broader understanding of cultural competency, emphasizing its dynamic nature, multifaceted components, and the pivotal role it plays in providing equitable and patient-centered healthcare. This study calls for ongoing efforts in educational, experiential, and self-directed learning to enhance cultural competency among healthcare professionals, ultimately fostering a more inclusive healthcare system.
