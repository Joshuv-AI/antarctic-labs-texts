An In Vitro Comparative Evaluation of Microleakage and Microhardness of Omnichroma and Silver Nanoparticles- incorporated Omnichroma
Authors: Mrithyunjay Satish Mendon, Mansi Jain, Suma Sogi, Gulbar Shah, Gagandeep Bhagat, Simran Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionComposite resins are one of the favored choices of dental restoration due to their esthetic appearance, adhesion, and tooth structure preservation. However, they have certain drawbacks, such as lower wear resistance, polymerization shrinkage, and microcracks at the margins. Silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) release cationic silver with oxidative potential, contributing to improved mechanical and enhanced antibacterial properties of dental materials.AimTo determine whether AgNPs-incorporated in Omnichroma decrease the microleakage and increase the microhardness.Materials and methodsA class V cavity was prepared on 40 premolar extracted teeth, which were randomly segregated into four groups: Group I—Omnichroma composite resin for microleakage evaluation; group II—AgNPs-incorporated Omnichroma composite resin were assessed for dye penetration by Michal Staininec and Mark Holtz scoring using a stereomicroscope; group III—Omnichroma composite resin for microhardness evaluation; group IV—AgNPs-incorporated Omnichroma composite resin were evaluated for microhardness values using a Vickers microhardness tester.ResultsAll the data were statistically analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21. Iitroduction

A bright smile with healthy teeth can significantly boost a person's appearance and confidence. Oral health plays a vital role in maintaining an individual's overall health and wellness.1 Dental caries is one of the most common oral health issues, impacting a large portion of the population. Therefore, caries, especially early childhood caries, are of significant concern for pediatric dentists.2

Composite resins are a favored choice due to their considerable characteristics: Esthetical appearance and tooth structure preservation. However, they have certain drawbacks, such as lower wear resistance, polymerization shrinkage, microcracks at the margins, and discoloration.3 Omnichroma composite resin contains filler particles that alter the light transmitted along the red-yellow spectrum, thus permitting an excellent color match (smart chromatic technology) and higher polishability.4

Recurrent or secondary caries can occur due to fracture of the material itself, influencing its marginal seal and leading to restoration failure. Microleakage permits the infiltration of bacteria between the cavity wall and the composite resin, potentially leading to tooth sensitivity and pulpal pathology.5 According to Bajabaa, Omnichroma composite resin displayed high microleakage values at the cavity floor.6 Apart from microleakage, microhardness is another essential property determining a material's ability to resist indentation, penetration, and abrasion. Improving the marginal seal of restorations helps to prevent secondary caries, and increasing microhardness can increase the longevity of the restoration.7 Nanotechnology offers a way to enhance restorative materials by increasing their microhardness and reducing microleakage, thereby improving their overall mechanical performance.

Nanotechnology involves the manipulation of materials at the nanoscale, typically ranging from 10 to 100 nm in size, making them highly desirable in material sciences.8 In recent years, silver has been incorporated into dental materials as nanoparticles with silver atoms ranging from 15,000 to 20,000, with a diameter of 100 nm, greatly enhancing its properties to release cationic silver with oxidative potential.9

However, limited research has been conducted on the impact of incorporating silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) into dental resins on their mechanical properties.

Hence, the present study was conducted to determine whether AgNPs-incorporated in Omnichroma decrease its microleakage and increase microhardness.

Materials and Methods

This in vitro experimental study, approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC No. 2284), was conducted on 40 healthy premolars extracted for orthodontic or periodontal purposes. Teeth exhibiting root resorption, fractures, malformations, or cracks were excluded. The selected specimens were randomly assigned to four groups:

Incorporation of AgNPs in Composite Resin

The entire content of Omnichroma resin (4 gm) was extracted on a glass slab in a dark room.

0.3 wt% of AgNPs was weighed per 1 gm of composite resin.

The weighed AgNPs were hand-spatulated with Omnichroma resin on the glass slab using a Teflon-coated spatula in a dark room.

The modified Omnichroma composite resin was stored in a black container.

Preparation of Specimens

All specimens were cleaned using an ultrasonic scaler to eliminate debris and calculus. Each tooth was then polished with a pumice slurry applied with a prophy cup and subsequently stored in distilled water. A class V cavity was created on the cervical third of the buccal surface of extracted premolars, which were then restored using Omnichroma composite resin (groups I and III) and with AgNPs-incorporated Omnichroma (groups II and IV). The specimens underwent thermocycling between 5°C and 55°C in a water bath, with a dwell time of 30 seconds and a transfer time of 10 seconds, for around 250 cycles.

Microleakage Evaluation

Specimens of groups I and II were coated with double coats of nail varnish, leaving margins of 1 mm in the restored class V cavity. The apices of the teeth were sealed with sticky wax, then the specimens were immersed in 1% methylene blue dye for 24 hours. Each tooth was sectioned buccolingually through the center of the restoration using a low-speed diamond disc mounted on a straight handpiece. The specimens were subsequently examined for dye penetration under 40× magnification using a stereomicroscope.

The microleakage assessment was based on a scoring system evaluating dye penetration depth and its extent along the restoration wall, as described by Michal Staininec and Mark Holtz (1988). Scoring was assigned as follows:

Score 0: No dye penetration.

Score 0: No dye penetration.

Score 1: Dye penetration up to half of the occlusal or gingival wall.

Score 1: Dye penetration up to half of the occlusal or gingival wall.

Score 2: Dye penetration beyond half of the occlusal or gingival wall.

Score 2: Dye penetration beyond half of the occlusal or gingival wall.

Score 3: Dye penetration extending to the axial wall (Fig. 1).

Microhardness Evaluation

Specimens of groups III and IV were sectioned mesiodistally with a low-speed diamond disc mounted on a straight handpiece and were subjected to Vickers microhardness testing (Vaiseshika Electron Devices) with the help of a diamond indenter applying a load of 980.7 mN for 10 seconds at three separate locations (Fig. 2).

The obtained data were distributed and presented as individual tables and graphs. Descriptive data were reported for each variable. Student's t-test was applied for the variables, and all data were statistically analyzed using SPSS version 21, with a significance level set at p < 0.05 (Fig. 3).

Discussion

Dental caries can affect your diamond and influence its clarity. It is primarily caused by cariogenic bacteria that adhere to the tooth surface and metabolize sugars to produce acids, which gradually demineralize the tooth structure. Despite being largely preventable, this condition remains a significant public health issue, with its prevalence showing little decline over the past 30 years.10

Composite resins, known for their tooth-colored appearance, are the preferred restorative material because of their advantages in esthetics, adhesion, and preservation of tooth structure.3 Microleakage typically occurs at the cavosurface margin of restorations, allowing bacterial fluids, molecules, and ions to pass between the cavity wall and restorative material, which often goes undetected clinically.5 This phenomenon can lead to recurrent caries, postoperative sensitivity, and pulpal disease. As microleakage is a major factor influencing the durability of dental restorations,11 A composite's capacity to reduce microleakage at the restoration interface is essential for ensuring the long-term success of the treatment.12

Omnichroma is a new universal composite resin introduced by Tokuyama Dental America in 2019. It has a smart Chromatic Technology, which uses uniformly sized spherical filler particles. These fillers alter the light that is transmitted along the red-to-yellow area of the color spectrum, thus allowing an exact color match of the patient's adjacent teeth. While traditional composite resins often contain bisphenol A-glycidyl methacrylate (Bis-GMA), triethylene glycol dimethacrylate (TEGDMA), and ethoxylated bisphenol A dimethacrylate (Bis-EMA), Omnichroma incorporates 260 nm silica and zirconia fillers along with urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA) and TEGDMA monomers, which are high molecular weight particles that significantly reduce polymerization shrinkage.4

The idea of nanotechnology was introduced by American physicist Richard Feynman in his lecture titled “There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom” (Feynman, 1992). The core principle of nanotechnology is that limitless possibilities exist at the atomic and molecular scale.13 In dentistry, the integration of nanoparticles into dental materials—referred to as “nanodentistry”—enhances their physicochemical properties.14 Various physical and chemical methods are employed to fabricate materials and structures sized between 0.1 and 100 nm.15

Silver nanoparticles are well known for their antibacterial property. When incorporated into a composite resin, these nanoparticles furthermore enhance its mechanical properties, such as fracture toughness, flexural strength, and microhardness.14 Addition of AgNPs into resin at concentrations ranging from 0.1% to 1.0% by weight can impart antibacterial effects without compromising mechanical properties or bond strength.16 Additionally, resin modified with 0.3% AgNPs demonstrated enhanced compressive strength.17

To prepare a modified resin with a 0.3 wt% concentration, 24 mg of nanoparticles was measured and mixed with 4 gm of Omnichroma resin. The two components were manually blended on a glass plate using a Teflon spatula for one minute inside a darkened room to prevent premature polymerization. The resulting homogeneous mixture was then stored safely until needed.18

For the present study, class V cavities were prepared on the buccal surface of the teeth due to their low configuration factor and ease of preparation and restoration, which reduces technique sensitivity. These cavities were restored using Omnichroma resin, both with and without incorporated AgNPs. Subsequently, the restorations were evaluated for microleakage and microhardness.

To simulate the aging of specimens, thermocycling was intended by introducing the restored teeth to thermal stress. Various thermocycling protocols have been used for testing dental restorations, typically within a temperature range of 4°C to 60°C.19 In the present study, specimens were subjected to thermocycling in a digitally controlled unit between water baths maintained at 5°C and 55°C, with temperatures continuously monitored using thermometers.

Microleakage Evaluation

To ensure dye penetration occurred only at the restoration margins, each tooth was coated with two layers of clear nail polish following thermocycling, leaving a 1 mm area around the restoration margin uncoated.20

Various techniques have been used to evaluate microleakage, including air pressure, bacterial infiltration, dye penetration, radioactive isotope tracing, neutron activation analysis, and microcomputed tomography. Among these, the dye penetration method is the most commonly utilized. This technique uses contrasting dyes as an immersion medium to highlight areas of microleakage, after which the tooth–restoration interface is inspected for signs of dye penetration. The most commonly used solutions are 0.5% basic fuchsin, 1% methylene blue, and 50% silver nitrate.21

Specimens were immersed for 24 hours in methylene blue dye, which was preferred as it has several advantages: Smaller molecular size leading to greater penetration, and no radiation or reactive chemicals. Dye penetration using methylene blue may lead to an overestimation of the microleakage due to the dye's low molecular weight, which is inferior to that of bacteria; thus, these dye tests can also detect leakage that bacteria cannot penetrate.22

The microleakage was assessed under a video stereomicroscope at 45× magnification. This instrument is a type of optical microscope specifically designed for low-magnification observation. Unlike traditional light microscopes that rely on transmitted light, it uses reflected light to examine the surface of specimens. It operates with two separate optical paths, each equipped with its own objective and eyepiece, providing slightly different angles to each eye. This creates a three-dimensional visual of the sample. It is particularly useful for analyzing solid specimens with complex surface textures, where 3D visualization is critical for detailed examination. It is even employed for macro photography to record and examine various specimens.22

Data analysis reveals that group I comprises 80% specimens exhibiting a microleakage score of 3 with a mean value of 0.4138, 10% specimens each displayed microleakage scores of 1 (mean value 0.131) and 2 (mean value 0.238), whereas none of the samples showed 0 microleakage scores. Meanwhile, within group II, 60% specimens presented a microleakage score of 0, with a mean value being 0.04930, 10% cases each displaying a microleakage score of 1 (mean value 0.113) and 2 (mean value 0.288), with only 20% samples demonstrating a microleakage score of 3, having a mean value of 0.3235.

Data obtained from the study for groups I and II revealed statistically significant differences in mean microleakage values, 0.368 (+0.1073) and 0.1344 (+0.1254), respectively, with student's t-test analysis. The obtained p value of 0.0057 is significantly lower than the conventional threshold of 0.05, indicating strong evidence to reject the null hypothesis.

Silver nanoparticles, when added to polymers, it improves the thermal stability of the resulting nanocomposite, which may help reduce marginal defects at the interface between the material and tooth structure, particularly under thermocycling conditions.23

Microhardness evaluation

Enhancement of the longevity of restorations can be accomplished by enhancing the microhardness and reducing the microleakage of composite resins.24 These properties are influenced by factors such as the composition of the resin matrix, the type, shape, size distribution, and proportion of fillers, all of which play a crucial role in determining the mechanical and physical performance of the reinforced resin.25

Microhardness of composite resin refers to the resistance against depression, abrasion, indentation, and permanent deformation against the force of exertion. To evaluate such changes, the microhardness test is widely utilized. It is determined by a variety of methods, such as abrasion, scratch, and indentation techniques.26

The Vickers hardness tester, also known as the 136-degree diamond pyramid hardness test indenter, was introduced by Smith and Sandland in 1921. This versatile technique is applicable for both macro and microhardness testing across a broad spectrum of materials.27 It creates a square-shaped indentation, the diagonals of which are measured to determine hardness. The Vickers hardness number (VHN) is directly obtained from the testing machine. Microhardness measurements are useful since even the slightest change in degree of polymerization may yield vivid variations in microhardness.28

The Vickers Hardness test is simple, user-friendly, and uses a small indenter; this feature minimizes the risk of damaging the test material. Moreover, the indenter is suitable for testing materials of any hardness level.29 The hardness calculations are independent of the indenter size, and the method offers a broader measurement range compared to other hardness tests. Additionally, only a single test force is required. The microhardness value can be determined by the depth of indentation using loads of 490.3 to 1961.3 mN. Typically, smaller indentations indicate greater hardness.30

To calculate the hardness of a test surface, the lengths of both diagonals of the indentation on the surface are measured and averaged. The hardness value is then calculated using the ratio of the applied force (F), measured in kilogram-force (kgf), to the surface area (A) of the indentation in square millimeters.31 This hardness value is expressed as either Vickers hardness (HV) or diamond pyramid hardness (DPH), typically presented as a numerical figure without units.27

In the present study, the specimens were indented with a diamond indenter of a Vickers microhardness tester with a load of 980.7 mN for a fixed period of 10 seconds.

The obtained microhardness values were in the range of 57.76–60.91 with a mean being 59.434 (+1.02233067) for group III, whereas for group IV, 58.48–62.91 was the range with a mean being 60.829 (+1.29034405). The observed p value of 0.0801 exceeds the significance threshold of 0.05, indicating insufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis. Although the difference in mean microhardness values between material groups III and IV is not statistically significant, group IV showed an improvement in microhardness.

This could be attributed to the smaller size of AgNP. This facilitates tight cross-linking of resin particles, resulting in a uniform interface between the composite resin and nanoparticles, thereby preventing degradation.32 Nanoparticles act as fillers. Decreasing the size of fillers, increasing their volume and dispersion, leads to an increase in microhardness of the composite resin. These AgNPs also help in relocating the load from the resin matrix to the NPs, consequently improving the mechanical properties of nanocomposites.33

Contrary to our study, Moradian concluded that adding 1 wt% AgNPs to the composite resin reduced its microhardness but did not negatively impact surface roughness.18 This phenomenon can be explained by the reduction of Ag+ ions and the formation of atomic and nanoparticle clusters during curing, which interferes with the free radical polymerization process.33

Another major observation from the present study was the discoloration of Omnichroma resin after incorporation of AgNPs. It has adversely affected its esthetic properties, thus limiting its application in anterior restorative dentistry. Sánchez-Tito et al. stated that when AgNPs were incorporated in different concentrations in composite resins, they released reduced free silver ions (Ag+), which aggregate, precipitate, and penetrate the surface, leading to the appearance of dark stains, which raise esthetic concerns.34

Hence, an alternative strategy has to be considered, such as incorporating AgNPs into the bonding agent.

Wang et al. investigated the effect of AgNPs on the dentin bond strength of adhesives modified at 0.05% and 0.1% by weight was evaluated. The study concluded that adhesives containing AgNPs demonstrated potent antibacterial activity without compromising bond strength. Furthermore, the esthetic qualities of the composites remained unaffected.35

Observation of the present study concluded that AgNPs incorporation into the Omnichroma composite resin statistically decreased the microleakage. Microhardness was also improved, though not statistically significant. AgNPs possess unique properties that can improve dental materials; however, their incorporation has been associated with discoloration of composite resins, negatively impacting the esthetic qualities of Omnichroma resin and restricting its application in esthetic dentistry.

Conclusion

Silver nanoparticle incorporation into the Omnichroma composite resin statistically decreased the microleakage. Microhardness was also improved, though not statistically significant. AgNPs have distinct characteristics and, therefore, while AgNPs can enhance the properties of dental materials, their use has been associated with discoloration of composite resin, which negatively impacts the esthetic qualities of Omnichroma resin and limits its suitability for esthetic dentistry.
