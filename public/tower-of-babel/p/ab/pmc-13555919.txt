Neighborhood factors and the geography of type 2 diabetes in Malaysia: an ecological based geospatial modelling study
Authors: Kurubaran Ganasegeran, Mohd Rizal Abdul Manaf, Nazarudin Safian, Lance A. Waller, Feisul Idzwan Mustapha, Khairul Nizam Abdul Maulud, Muhammad Faid Mohd Rizal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 2 diabetes (T2D) often exhibits long-standing disparities across populations. Spatial regression models can identify associations between local rates and observed neighborhood factors to inform timely, localized public health interventions. We identified area-level distributions of T2D rates across Malaysia and estimate associations between different neighborhood covariates and local T2D burden.MethodsWe obtained aggregated counts of national level T2D cases data by administrative-districts between 2016 and 2020 and computed district-wise crude rates to correlate with district-level neighborhood demographic, socio-economic, safety, physical activity and resources access, and urbanization process indicators from various national sources and census data. We applied a simultaneous spatial autoregressive (SAR) modeling strategy to account for spatial autocorrelation and estimate risk factors for district-level logged T2D crude rates in Malaysia.ResultsDistrict-level logged T2D crude rates were associated with the proportion of households living below 50% of the median income (β = 0.009, p = 0.003) and below the national poverty line (β = ˗0.011, p = 0.001), income inequalities (β = ˗1.778, p = 0.015), CCTV coverage per 1000 population (β = 0.060, p = 0.049), and perceived access to recreational parks (β = 0.007, p = 0.001) and to bowling centers (β = ˗0.003, p = 0.009).ConclusionArea-level district-wise logged T2D crude rate estimates were associated with neighborhood socio-economic vulnerabilities, neighborhood safety, and neighborhood perceived access to physical activity facilities, after accounting for residual spatial autocorrelation via a SAR model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28250-y.

Introduction

Neighborhoods are places with social and cultural meaning to local residents, defining where they typically reside, work, play, or have a sense of belonging [1, 2]. Neighborhoods are based on people’s living needs, expectations, and circumstances, alongside urbanization processes offering basic physical and landscape features (e.g., parks, sidewalks, or bicycle lanes), resulting from local township planning, maintenance, or restructuring [2] to achieve sustainable local outcomes for livability and viability [3]. More generally, neighborhoods reflect geographical entities nested within larger units like cities, states, or regions [2].

With respect to neighborhood operations and type 2 diabetes (T2D), observed local rates are often driven by local upstream social, political, or commercial influences on local community health throughout the life course [4] – e.g., as evidenced by the rise of T2D or obesity burdens with evidence of longstanding disparities between sociodemographic sub-populations [5, 6]. Such associations postulate neighborhoods at increased T2D risk owing to limited access to healthy food and/or physical activity facilities – and when coupled with poverty, local crime rates, social delinquency, local colonialism, or globalization processes can increase potential psychosocial stress to local residents, prompting a decline of lifestyle behaviors and resulting greater susceptibility to T2D risks or other chronic conditions [6, 7].

Socio-economic vulnerabilities such as poverty, income inequalities, and unemployment rates have been positively linked to various lifestyles and chronic health conditions [7–11]. These established linkages are often interpreted at the individual level across the literature without considering the specific geographical scales capable of providing a snapshot to local population risks for public health interventions. Area-level economic markers of income inequalities (e.g., the Gini coefficient) are useful for understanding local landscapes of human behavior and living circumstances across communities - where the rich are often clustered in areas of decent living, while the poor typically struggle to survive within the social and commercial environmental hazards – often predisposing them to poorer overall health [3, 12, 13].

A neighborhood’s demography (i.e., proportions of ethnic/racial minorities; women; middle-to-older aged people) and socio-economic vulnerabilities often reveal significant correlations with high neighborhood-level obesity and T2D rates [8, 14]. These vulnerable groups are often segregated to a subset of neighborhoods, accelerating local safety concerns due to higher incidence of social delinquency, local crime, or drug addiction – again, tending towards increasing psychological stress and T2D risk [15, 16].

Local safety concerns may decrease physical activity - a cornerstone for T2D risk management - for residents dwelling within impacted neighborhoods, e.g., due to reduced walking, cycling, or perceived access to nearby exercise or recreational areas (e.g., neighborhood parks, sports, or physical activity facilities) [14, 16]. Physical inactivity is often connected to people’s demographic, socio-economic characteristics, and health outcomes, where ethnic/racial minorities or women from lower income groups in urban areas exhibit greater risks for poorer health [17, 18]. However, some neighborhood characteristics were linked to lower rates of physical activity among rural residents [19], raising risk for chronic diseases [16, 20].

Regional atlases estimate Malaysia topped T2D prevalence among countries in the Western Pacific Region as of 2021 [21], and the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2019 Report claimed that approximately 1 in 5 adults in Malaysia was afflicted with T2D, totalling to approximately 3.9 million people above the age of 18 years [22]. Countrywide point prevalence of T2D increased from 11.2% in 2011 to 18.3% in 2019 [22].

The burden of T2D escalated alongside demographic, societal, and cultural changes within Malaysian communities. Common individual-level risk factors for T2D generally include physical inactivity, poor dietary behaviours, and demographic profiles such as age, ethnicity, socio-economic status, or type of occupations [23–26]. But aggregate summaries of these attributes also associate with T2D rates for neighborhood communities [27], and overall poor health amongst different residential areas [28]. That is, local T2D prevalence rates and associated risk factors can both be influenced by “place.”

Motivation of the current work

Following on how neighborhood landscapes operate and connect to human livelihoods as described above, our work aims to provide local population level insight into local social and environmental correlates with local rates of T2D to focus policy developments and follow ups. As we believe that individual- and local area-level goals should align to improving the health of individuals, our analysis aims to provide regional insight into associations with policy-relevant administrative variables (e.g., encouraging local physical activity), rather than specific inference on individual-level risk factors (e.g., physical activity and behavior). We specifically focus on our analytic goals that facilitate the selection of variables based on general categories of local variables relating to T2D rates (e.g., sociodemographic covariates) and aggregate values related to potentially policy-modifiable factors related to T2D risk (e.g., perceived local physical activity facilities accessibility and public safety). While there are many variables within these categories, we seek to find those within these categories exhibiting the strongest associations at the aggregate level as potential consideration for follow-up and policy development for healthy urbanism and neighborhood developments. We clarify our motivation and goals in the conceptual model outlined in Fig. 1.

Conceptual framework of neighborhoods T2D risks

The conceptual framework developed for this study builds upon a history of population health models examining relationships between aggregated level risk factors and ill-health. The Population Health Metrics Model emphasizes that the population’s health is largely afflicted by non-communicable diseases, and their causes are multi-level, extending from distal determinants throughout the life-course to proximal determinants of individual’s lifestyle behaviours causing ill-health [29, 30]. We adopt and modify Mujahid et al.’s [6] Health Equity Model for our local setting, emphasizing the historical context and structural and social drivers as key determinants of neighborhood environments ultimately shaping local rates of T2D (Fig. 1). Our conceptual framework highlights how urban transformations like greater land use (i.e. the expansion and intensification of built environments) arising from urbanization and township development, would encompass increased residential density, commercial activity, and infrastructure. This process catalyzes neighborhood upstream social determinants of health, which subsequently shape behavioral and metabolic risk factors for T2D across the human life course.

Methods

Study design, setting, and population

For our ecological study, we assembled and linked district-level population data (n = 144), countrywide, for Malaysia (see geographical location in relation to surrounding countries in Fig. 2), using multiple data sources including the National Diabetes Register (NDR) [31], administrative shapefiles (level 1 for states; level 2 for administrative districts) [32], and population wellbeing surveys and social indicators integrated within the Malaysian Population Census [33]. Data on 271,553 active T2D adults aged ≥ 20 years from 2016 to 2020 by administrative districts were retrieved from the National Diabetes Register (NDR) of Malaysia [31]. T2D case counts include those diagnosed based on local clinical practice guidelines [34] with at least one follow-up visit to the registered primary health clinic in a year were captured [35]. National universal healthcare coverage defines catchment areas for primary health services in Malaysia, approximately within those neighborhoods within five km of service providers, nested within local administrative districts.

Operationalization of neighborhoods

Neighborhoods were operationalized as district-level administrative units [36], rather than socially perceived residential areas (e.g., city blocks or suburbs). This approach adopts existing administrative boundaries of varying geographical size as proxies for neighborhoods that aggregates individuals into broad swaths of geography defined for administrative purposes [36]. Districts were selected because they are the smallest policy-relevant units with consistent, population-level administrative and public health data available in Malaysia, allowing spatial overlay of administrative data through adjacency matrix, whereby districts sharing a common boundary were considered neighbors. For reference, the land area of administrative districts in Malaysia ranges from approximately 40 sq.km (i.e. smallest district) to 19,050 sq.km (i.e. the largest district in the country) [33].

They are structured based on the fundamentals of “sports place theory” that correlates the provision of sports or recreational facilities with the level of urbanization processes within neighborhoods (i.e., greater urbanization catalyzes greater development of sports or physical activity infrastructures), alongside with neighborhood demographics and socioeconomic statuses [52–56]. Such physical activity resources, like mini stadiums or recreational parks, are not built within immediate surroundings. The 5 km perceived accessibility threshold was selected based on established urban planning framework that conceptualizes access to physical activity infrastructures or recreational facilities beyond immediate walkable distances. This threshold reflects typical travel behavior for leisure-time physical activity, considering residents motorized and public transport use, aligning with municipal service planning standards for community-level recreational, sports, or physical activity infrastructure access. Evidence suggests that larger buffer sizes of perceived accessibility would more accurately capture exposure to physical activity environments and their associations with health outcomes [57–59]. Our data for perceived access to sports, physical, or recreational facilities were sourced from the Population Wellbeing Fitness Survey [33].

Evidence suggests that larger buffer sizes of perceived accessibility would more accurately capture exposure to physical activity environments and their associations with health outcomes [57–59]. Our data for perceived access to sports, physical, or recreational facilities were sourced from the Population Wellbeing Fitness Survey [33].

Data analysis

We log-transformed the district-level T2D crude rates to allow better approximation via the Gaussian distribution. As crude rates were subjected to variance instability that leads to spurious outliers, we also performed rate smoothing using Empirical Bayesian methods [52] via GeoDa version 1.18 software (Center for Spatial Data Science University of Chicago, IL, USA) to better visualize the distribution of local rates. As the logged Empirical Bayes smoothed rates did not vary much, we opted for the use of logged transformed T2D crude rates throughout our regression modelling. Next, we estimated between district-level covariates via ordinary least squares regression as a baseline model then extended the analysis to allow for spatial correlation in model errors.

Geographic data processing and cartographic visualizations

Quantum GIS (QGIS, version 3.22) spatially joined attribute data from multiple agencies and allowed visualization via a quantile map of T2D rates by administrative districts in Malaysia.

Moran’s I index of spatial correlation roughly falls between the values − 1 to 1 [63], but the precise bounds are defined by the specific adjacency matrix [64]. Inference for Moran’s I is based on comparison to the null hypothesis of spatial independence of outcomes across small areas. We subsequently yield the Local Indicators of Spatial Autocorrelation (LISA), i.e., the local Moran’s index [65].

Spatial simultaneous autoregressive modelling

As a baseline, we first fit an ordinary least square (OLS) regression model to the log-T2D crude rates. We next fit a spatial error model (SEM) allowing spatial correlation of model residuals based on the Queen’s adjacency matrix. The SEM model attributes potential spatial correlation in outcomes due to spatial correlation in missing covariates absorbed into residual error terms, while the spatial econometric SLM model attributes such correlation to spillover effects [66] linking T2D prevalence between neighboring regions. In our setting, and given that T2D is a multifactorial problem, the SEM model is conceptually more attractive than the SLM model, given the absence of clear data indicators to measure and directly assess such potential spillover effects driving prevalence in adjacent regions.

Covariate selection

We consider neighborhood covariates within the broad classes of local factors described above (i.e., physical activity facilities, demographics, socioeconomics, etc.). Our focus is to identify area-level associations that can be monitored in administrative data to motivate and evaluate policy decisions. Again, while of interest, we are not aiming for perfect assessment of individual-level risk factors. We prioritized our conceptual framework, and we complement variable selection via plausibility supported by our conceptual framework. Covariates with biological plausibility (i.e. even if the covariate was not statistically significant in the univariate OLS level but importantly being a risk factor for T2D as evidenced by the national country report, or the literature theoretically, or are biologically influencing predictors like gender, age, or ethnicity) were included in the full multivariate models. We modelled age using categorical groups to capture non-linear risk patterns to allow clearer interpretation of elevated risk among older populations. Established non-modifiable risk factors such as gender and ethnicity were retained in multivariable models to minimize residual confounding or biased effect estimates in spatial regression.

All analyses were conducted using GeoDa version 1.18.

Interpretations of statistical significance and model performance

We interpreted statistical significance in our SEM as follows [67]; reporting p-value is less than 0.001, between 0.001 and 0.01, between 0.01 and 0.05, between 0.05 and 0.10, or greater than 0.10 to consider the evidence linking main outcome and neighborhood attributes (i.e., against the null hypotheses) to be very strong, strong, moderate, weak, or none, respectively. We compare model fit via the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), where lower AIC values indicate better model fit.

Spatial autocorrelation of type 2 diabetes rates

Discussion

Areas with higher logged T2D rates were predominantly concentrated along the West of Peninsular Malaysia, across districts in the Southern, Central, and Northern regions, and parts of the area districts in the North, South, and West of Sarawak state. We observed significant spatial autocorrelations in logged T2D rates and significant associations with district-level neighborhood indicators, suggesting that these indicators may provide helpful information in planning district-level public health responses for T2D control in Malaysia.

Our final SEM revealed associations between neighborhood socio-economic vulnerabilities, neighborhood safety features, the influence of local residential perceived access to neighborhood resources for physical activities, and logged T2D rates (n.b. we provide descriptive cartographic distributions of these associations for comparisons via bivariate maps for visualizations as available in Supplementary Figs. 4–10). These associations were consistent with previous geospatial investigations from the USA [14–16, 68], Saudi Arabia [69], Australia [70], Bangladesh [71], and Brazil [72]. We note that these studies originate from both developed and developing nations with different geo-political, social, and cultural norms; alongside differences of spatial attributes within the rural-urban matrix suggesting some generality in the use of such district-level variables for developing and monitoring public health policies relating to T2D. However, we also note that different applications of place-based correlations and regression modelling at different spatial scales, either at the regional, state, or county levels yielded different directions of relationships when linking with local neighborhood risks, a condition known as the modifiable areal unit problem (MAUP) and broader examination of spatial scale and direction of association may provide important insights into the best spatial scale for defining T2D policies.

Income and poverty are strong population health indicators with higher income typically indicative for better health. Studies have found positive correlations between these indicators and T2D [73, 74], but our study found otherwise. A unit rise in the proportion of households living below the national poverty line was related to a decrease in logged T2D rates, but a unit rise in the proportion of households living below 50% of the median household income increased the risk of T2D across neighborhoods. We note that the metrics of spatial economic covariates through spatially adjusted econometric approaches with ecological linkages to T2D rates are based on different income threshold definitions used to operationalize poverty and low-income status, and that income distributions in Malaysia have local associations with the urban/rural geography of the country. Specifically, the proportion of households living below the national poverty line (i.e., extreme poor with monthly income threshold of USD 533.10) were mostly distributed across the rural districts in the state of Sabah, and parts of the rural districts within the East Coast region of the country – these areas had low T2D rates (see joint distributions in Supplementary Figs. 4–10). In contrast, the proportion of households living below 50% of median household income (i.e., households with income threshold of USD 709.54) had positive relationships with T2D rates. Although not within the extremes of poverty, this income group exists within the upper end of the bottom 40% (B40) income classification in Malaysia and is distributed in the most rural districts, with fewer clusters observed across the urban districts in the country. The distribution of T2D rates within this income group was most dense across the districts in East Malaysia and Southern regions, with some clusters around the Northern and Central metropolitans, and townships within the East Coast regions (see joint distributions in Supplementary Figs. 4–10), consistent with the effect direction of our regression models. Inconsistencies of spatial distribution between different economic covariates linking to T2D or obesity rates appear in related research [8, 75–80], suggesting that local interactions of income with other risk factors may moderate observed associations, a promising direction for future research.

While poverty accelerates disease risk and poor health outcomes, the associations between income inequality measured by the Gini coefficient exhibit inconsistencies in the spatial epidemiology literature [8, 81, 82]. We found that a unit rise in income inequality decreased district-level logged T2D rates, when principally it was anticipated to be high, a condition most likely attributable to the “Swiss paradox” wherein increased income inequality that supposedly cause negative health impacts would inversely show better population health outcomes when measured on a finer geographical scale of aggregation [81]. These areas mostly comprised of districts within the rural regions of similar population distributions residing below the national poverty line as described earlier. As income inequality is specific to geographical scales influenced by local social or commercial drivers, inconsistencies between the association of income inequality and chronic conditions can be influenced by the different spatial measurement units (e.g., state, district, or county levels) or the scales used (i.e., a single measure through Gini coefficient or composite measures of neighborhood deprivation scores) [6, 8]. Inequalities and poverty collectively pressure local communities within deprived neighborhoods, often resulting in poor health outcomes. Different social gradients may pose different levels of stress within neighborhood living circumstances, with poor communities paying less attention to health needs, practicing less healthy lifestyles, or having less accessibility to health or physical activity facilities as compared to richer ones [83–85]. Our findings showed that apart from poverty attributes, neighborhood safety features likely influence local residents’ intentions to access physical activity facilities (i.e., bowling centers) and parks, potentially depriving local communities from participating in physical activity, a circumstance potentially increasing incidence of obesity and T2D rates in local neighborhoods, consistent with previous literature [6, 8, 15, 16].

Strengths and limitations

Study strengths

Our district-level results provide evidence for defining local neighborhood policy interventions to control the T2D burden at the district level in Malaysia. We note area disparities of different neighborhood attributes and clusters of T2D, thereby suggesting local behavioral health interventions, health promotions, or local townships restructure or sustainability efforts based on specific targets and areas rather than the practice of implementing generic interventions nationwide. For example, in highly urbanized districts of the Central region like districts within the state of Selangor or Kuala Lumpur city, interventions could prioritize improvements in neighborhood walkability, access to safe recreational or physical activity spaces, and workplace-based lifestyle modification programs. In Northern and Southern Peninsular districts, where peri-urban and semi-rural characteristics are more prominent, strengthening primary care led diabetes screening and integration with existing community health volunteer programs may be effective. Across affected districts in Sarawak, particularly those with greater rurality and limited healthcare access, policy responses could focus on enhancing outreach services, mobile diabetes screening, and culturally appropriate health promotion tailored to indigenous and remote communities. These interventions illustrate how spatially clustered T2D burden and neighborhood characteristics can inform region-specific strategies for more equitable and cost-effective T2D control in Malaysia. Since policies are implemented at an aggregate level rather than being individualized, the ecological regression approach suggests areas of high, low, or epidemiologically transitioning areas of local diabetes burden with their associated place-based characteristics, guiding planning, implementation, and ongoing evaluation of local public health responses.

Rather than to seek the best-fitting combination of variables, we report the associations above to allow policymakers to consider (a) the relative strengths of association and (b) the ease of adjusting these regional variables in order to assess potential adjustments to the local physical environment. For instance, proposing additional local recreational parks may be far more economical than proposing a new football stadium. Taken together, the result suggests multiple directions for potential improvements to local resources that may reduce local prevalences of T2D.

The data used in this study are reliable, accurate, and generalizable, as countrywide disease registries are official health data captured through proper diagnostics by health personnels using validated clinical practice guidelines, thereby tackling many limitations from more commonly used cross-sectional surveys that are subject to non-generalizability, social-desirability bias, compromised accuracies due to measurements used, and potential residual spatial correlation. Our use of spatial modelling approaches accounts for spatial autocorrelations and spillover effects [86–89], thus strengthening evidence and interpretation of effect associations.

Study limitations

While the data were aggregated to the district level in the NDR database, we note that T2D cases were captured based on the primary care clinic’s location in that district’s established catchment area (i.e., principally within five km of each clinic in accordance with Universal Health Coverage to local communities in Malaysia). Therefore, we were unable to establish spatial-analytical connections to the smallest spatial unit (i.e. sub-district or mukim levels) for more robust estimates as they may share local borders or corners that may contaminate the exact location of analysis within the anonymized residence address of the NDR data. We note that administrative boundaries may not actually represent more local perceptions of “neighborhood” by residents, a concept known as spatial misclassification. We also note that administrative boundaries are relatively static in nature, whereas human mobility within an area is dynamic, giving rise to assumptions regarding one’s experience to neighbourhood characteristics exposure (i.e. in reality, residents don’t expose themselves to all areas within an administrative boundary) [36]. Due to the nature of our estimates from analysis of ecologic (aggregate) data, we clarify that directions of associations cannot directly compare with studies determining associations between T2D and individual-level risk factors but instead optimize interpretations of association related to area level T2D risks. We note that public health outcomes are often spatially clustered, observing regional differences of disease distributions [90]. We also note the lack of explicit land-use mix measures (e.g., residential, commercial, or industrial) that may influence local community activity patterns. Our analysis relied primarily on vector-based administrative datasets, and high-resolution raster-derived land-use data were not available for harmonized integration without introducing spatial scale or alignment consistencies. While we measured urbanization indicators through proxy indicators such as population density and accessibility to physical activity facilities, we recommend future work to incorporate raster-based land-use mix or entropy indices derived from satellite imagery to better characterize mixed-use environments for strengthening outcomes from spatial regression analyses.

Conclusion

Our study established that district-level T2D rates in Malaysia were associated with neighborhood-level socio-economic vulnerability, safety, and perceived access to physical activity facilities. Our maps reveal variations of T2D rates across administrative districts in Malaysia, and we observe these variations across areas in the Northern, Central and Southern regions of Malaysia, with some disparities across the North, South, and West of Sarawak state, influenced by different gradients of urbanization processes, alongside with different levels of neighborhood socioeconomic, demography, safety, and physical activity environment attributes being associated closely. The results of the spatial regression analysis suggest that T2D can be influenced by various policy-adaptable factors, allowing for localized planning of public health interventions to reduce risk burden of T2D.
