Abdominal Obesity and Kidney Function Among Middle-Aged and Older Adults With Normal Body Mass Index
Authors: Ying Jia, Fan Zhang
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

Obesity is a risk factor for chronic kidney disease,1 but fat distribution may better predict kidney dysfunction than body mass index (BMI).2 Individuals with normal BMI score but elevated waist circumference (“metabolically obese normal weight”) have higher cardiometabolic risk.3

However, whether abdominal obesity is associated with reduced kidney function among individuals with normal BMI score across different populations remains unclear. Using nationally representative data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and the US Health and Retirement Study (HRS) cohorts, we assessed the association between abdominal obesity, defined by waist circumference within BMI score categories, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Data were drawn from the CHARLS cohort 2015 and HRS cohort 2016, 2 nationally representative surveys of middle-aged and older adults. Participants with implausible BMI scores (< 12 or > 50 kg/m2), waist circumference (< 50 or > 180 cm), or underweight individuals (BMI score < 18.5 kg/m2) were excluded. Final samples included 12,071 (CHARLS cohort) and 4,272 (HRS cohort) (Figure S1).

BMI score categories (normal 18.5-23.9, overweight 24.0-27.9, and obesity ≥ 28 kg/m2) were applied to both cohorts.4 Abdominal obesity was defined using the World health organization waist circumference cutoff values. Participants were classified into 6 BMI score-waist circumference groups.3 The eGFR was calculated using the 2021 Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine-cystatin C equation. Models adjusted for age, sex, education, smoking, drinking, hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Missing data (< 15%) were imputed (Table S1).

Multivariable linear regression estimated the association between each BMI-waist circumference category and eGFR rate. Sensitivity analyses included (1) logistic regression using eGFR rates < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2; (2) eGFR rates estimated from creatinine or cystatin C levels alone; (3) Asian-specific waist circumference thresholds in the CHARLS cohort; and (4) models excluding hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia. Results are reported as β with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). For more detailed methods, please refer to Item S1.

CHARLS cohort participants were younger and had lower BMI scores and waist circumference than the HRS cohort, with lower prevalence of obesity and metabolic conditions (Table S1). Within the CHARLS cohort, 36.2% had normal BMI scores without abdominal obesity, 14.2% had normal BMI scores with abdominal obesity, and 25.9% were overweight with abdominal obesity. In the HRS cohort, 52.7% were obese with abdominal obesity, and only 7.3% had normal BMI scores without abdominal obesity (Fig 1A).

(B) Adjusted associations of BMI score-waist circumference categories with estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR). In the CHARLS cohort, participants with normal BMI scores and abdominal obesity had significantly lower eGFR rates (β = −1.02; 95% CI, −1.93 to −0.11; P = 0.03) compared with the reference group (Fig 1B). eGFR rates further declined among those who were overweight with nonabdominal (β = −1.22; 95% CI, −2.18 to −0.27; P = 0.01) or abdominal obesity (β = −2.16; 95% CI, −2.90 to −1.41; P < 0.001), with the greatest reductions in obese participants with abdominal obesity (β = −3.24; 95% CI, −4.12 to −2.35; P < 0.001). In the HRS cohort, patterns were directionally similar but less precise: eGFR rate was lower among those who were overweight with abdominal obesity (β = −2.38; 95% CI, −4.52 to −0.24; P = 0.03) and obese with abdominal obesity (β = −4.71; 95% CI, −6.78 to −2.65; P < 0.001). All sensitivity analyses yielded similar findings (Tables S2-S5).

Across 2 cohorts, abdominal obesity was associated with a lower eGFR rate, even among adults with normal BMI scores. Visceral fat is known to promote glomerular hyperfiltration, insulin resistance, and chronic inflammation, potentially accelerating renal injury independent of BMI score.5 Our findings are consistent with prior studies using imaging-based measures of visceral adiposity, which have also demonstrated associations with kidney function decline.6,7

The magnitude of the observed associations was modest, particularly among individuals with normal BMI scores, and did not reach statistical significance in the HRS cohort. These findings may reflect early or subclinical variation in kidney function rather than clinically overt impairment. Importantly, the consistency lies in the overall graded pattern across adiposity categories rather than identical effect sizes across cohorts.8 Differences in age distribution, adiposity profiles, and comorbidity burden between the CHARLS and HRS cohorts may further contribute to heterogeneity in estimates, and direct comparisons should be interpreted with caution. These findings highlight BMI’s limitation as a sole marker of obesity-related renal risk and support incorporating waist circumference into clinical risk stratification—even for individuals within the normal BMI range. Although the observed differences in eGFR rates were modest in magnitude (approximately 1-3 mL/min/1.73 m2), such differences may reflect early renal vulnerability at the population level. In middle-aged and older adults, even small shifts in eGFR rate distribution may translate into a higher long-term risk of chronic kidney disease and related adverse outcomes.

Because of the cross-sectional nature of the study, causal direction cannot be established, and reverse causation cannot be excluded, and kidney function was assessed using a single eGFR rate measurement, which may introduce random variability. Despite adjustment for major metabolic and lifestyle factors, residual confounding from diet, physical activity, or medication use cannot be excluded. To ensure cross-country comparability, a uniform World Health Organization-based definition of abdominal obesity was applied rather than population-specific cutoffs, which may underestimate associations in Asians but enhance consistency between cohorts. Finally, potential differences in laboratory assays and biomarker calibration between the CHARLS and HRS cohorts could contribute to measurement variation despite use of the same eGFR equation.9

In conclusion, abdominal obesity was associated with reduced kidney function independent of BMI. Incorporating waist circumference into population screening may improve early identification of individuals at risk for chronic kidney disease. Longitudinal analyses are warranted to clarify causal pathways.
