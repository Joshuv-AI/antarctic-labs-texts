What is the prevalence and temporal trends of high perceived social support among children and adolescents across 45 countries? Findings from the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study
Authors: Camila Miño, Masoud Rahmati, Rodrigo Yañéz-Sepúlveda, Héctor Gutiérrez-Espinoza, Jorge Olivares-Arancibia, Juan Hurtado-Almonacid, Dong Keon Yon, Brendon Stubbs, Lee Smith, José Francisco López‒Gil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerceived social support is a key determinant of pediatric health and well-being, yet little is known about how its sources vary and evolve across populations. This study evaluated the prevalence, time trends, and correlates of perceived social support from family, friends, classmates, and teachers in a large, multinational sample.MethodsData were drawn from 455,031 participants (mean age = 13.5 years; 51% female) in 45 countries participating in the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children study (2013–2018). Perceived social support was assessed separately for family, friends, classmates, and teachers. Linear and generalized logistic mixed models with country-level random intercepts were applied to account for clustering of participants within countries and to predict social support scores and high social support status for each domain adjusted by age, sex, socioeconomic status (SES), body mass index, and survey wave. Temporal comparisons between survey waves reflect population-level differences across repeated cross-sectional samples rather than within-individual developmental change.ResultsFamily was the strongest source of support, with 71% of participants reporting high family support, followed by friends (60%), while support from teachers (5.7%) and classmates (4.0%) was less common. Older adolescents had lower odds of high family support (odds ratio [OR]= 0.65; 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.63 to 0.66 for ages 12.5 to 14.5; OR = 0.49; 95% CI 0.48 to 0.50 for ages 14.5 to 17; both p < 0.001), reflecting age-related differences at the population level, and friends’ support (OR = 0.95; 95% CI 0.94 to 0.97; p < 0.001), indicating a small but statistically significant difference. Girls reported higher odds of support from friends (OR = 1.63; 95% CI 1.60 to 1.65; p < 0.001) and classmates (OR = 1.33; 95% CI 1.29 to 1.38; p < 0.001) but slightly lower odds of support from family (OR = 0.89; 95% CI 0.88 to 0.91; p < 0.001). Higher SES was positively associated with high family (OR = 1.48; 95% CI 1.44 to 1.52; p < 0.001) and friends’ support (OR = 1.44; 95% CI 1.41 to 1.47; p < 0.001) but inversely associated with classmates’ support (OR = 0.83; 95% CI 0.78 to 0.88; p < 0.001) with effects reflecting average population-level differences.ConclusionPerceived social support among youth varies by source and individual characteristics. Family and friends remain the main providers, although their role gradually decreases with age, whereas classmates and teachers offer limited but modestly increasing support. These temporal differences represent aggregate changes across survey cycles rather than longitudinal change within individuals. Our findings underscore the potential value of targeted efforts such as strengthening family communication during adolescence, fostering inclusive peer climates, and enhancing teacher–student relationships through school-based social and emotional learning and inclusion-focused interventions, particularly for vulnerable groups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01877-4.Keywords: Adolescence, Childhood, Family support, Friends support, Classmate support, Teachers support, Perceived social support, Social health, Health inequalities, School health

Background

Social support during childhood and adolescence is a critical determinant of psychological and psychosocial well-being [1]. However, in the United States, only 59% of adolescents report always or usually receiving the social and emotional support they need [2]. Indeed, high perceived social support from family, friends, classmates, and teachers plays a multifaceted and critical role in adolescent development, influencing psychological well-being, academic functioning, stress regulation, and life satisfaction [3, 4]. In contrast, loneliness (i.e., the uncomfortable emotion stemming from a deficiency in the quality or quantity of social connections) is reported in approximately 17% of adolescents worldwide and is strongly linked to poorer subjective health, lower life satisfaction, and increased psychosomatic complaints [5]. Therefore, these findings collectively underscore the importance of supportive family, school, and community environments in promoting youths’ health.

Family support, particularly from parents, is closely linked to global well-being, self-worth, and life satisfaction and contributes to emotion regulation and resilience [6, 7]. Additionally, peers’ or friends’ support is important for psychological well-being, especially as children and adolescents age, with best friends and significant others gaining importance in later adolescence [7, 8]. Similarly, support from teachers and classmates is positively related to self-worth, physical well-being, and school engagement and could (at least partially) buffer the negative effects of low support from other sources [9]. Furthermore, students who experienced substantial support from their teachers indicated enhanced physical well-being and a greater sense of self-worth [7].

Previous analyses of the Health Behaviour in School-aged Children (HBSC) study have examined selected dimensions of perceived social support and their associations with adolescent health outcomes across countries. For example, multinational HBSC studies have explored patterns of family, peer, and school-based support in relation to sociodemographic characteristics, life satisfaction and sexual health [10, 11]. However, to our knowledge, no previous study has simultaneously examined the prevalence, temporal trends, and individual-level correlates of multiple domains of perceived social support among children and adolescents across 45 countries from the HBSC from 2013 to 2018. Given that childhood and adolescence are marked by rapid transformations in social networks and that sociocultural contexts could influence the perception of social support, it is essential to understand how support sources change over time in younger populations [10]. Therefore, the present study aimed to: (1) estimate the prevalence of high perceived social support from family, friends, classmates, and teachers across participating countries; (2) examine population-level temporal differences between the 2013/2014 and 2017/2018 survey waves; and (3) investigate associations between age, sex, socioeconomic status, excess weight status, and perceived social support across domains.

Methods

Study design and population

The present research used data from the HBSC cross-sectional study of 45 countries and regions across two waves (2013/2014 and 2017/2018). The HBSC study is a collaborative international survey conducted by the World Health Organization and is recognized as one of the most extensive studies on children and adolescents aged 10–17 years [12, 13]. Each HBSC survey wave adheres to a standardized international protocol where the participants are chosen via a multistage stratified random cluster sampling technique. Consequently, school classes or entire schools function as the sampling units. Individuals filled out a standardized questionnaire anonymously in their native language and were allowed to leave questions unanswered. Given that the current research entailed a secondary analysis of anonymized data, it was not necessary to obtain approval from an ethics committee. However, the HBSC team from each geographic region received ethical approval to conduct the survey. Additionally, the research was carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from both the participants and their parents. The Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guideline checklist was used during the reporting of this study.

The analytical sample size consisted of 455,031 children and adolescents who participated in the 2013/2014 and 2017/2018 HBSC surveys with a mean age of 13.5 years [SD (standard deviation) = 1.6 years] and 51% were female.

Measures

Countries were eligible for inclusion if they assessed at least one domain of perceived social support (family, friends, classmates, or teachers) in at least one of the 2013/2014 or 2017/2018 survey waves. Because not all countries measured all domains in both waves, the analytic sample varied by support source and by wave. Consequently, domain-specific analyses were conducted using all available data for that particular support measure, and the effective sample size and contributing countries differ across domains and survey cycles. Family and friends support was evaluated via subscales from the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support instrument in both HBSC survey waves [14]. Family and friends support sources were evaluated with four questions (e.g., family: “My family is willing to help me make decisions”; friends: “I can count on my friends when things go wrong”). The participants rated their level of disagreement or agreement on a scale from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). The average of each subscale was calculated (the individual scores were added and divided by four), and then, both final scores were dichotomized into low (< 5.5) or high (≥ 5.5) support. Scores dichotomization was based on established HBSC international reporting practices, particularly the HBSC International Report from the 2013/2014 survey [15], where distribution-based criteria were applied to identify participants reporting consistently high levels of perceived support across all items of the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support. Additional psychometric validation of this instrument in adolescent populations supports its reliability and construct validity [16, 17].

The social support received from teachers and classmates was assessed via the corresponding subscales from the Teacher and Classmate Support Scale, which has been utilized in the HBSC study since 2013–2014 [18]. Each source of social support was evaluated with three questions (e.g., classmates: “Other students accept me as I am”; teachers: “I feel a lot of trust in my teachers”). The participants rated their level of disagreement or agreement on a scale from 1 (strongly agree) to 5 (strongly disagree) [original coding, before reversal]. The original codes were reversed (strongly disagree = 1 and strongly agree = 5), and the average of each subscale was calculated (the individual scores were added and divided by three). Both final scores were then dichotomized into low (< 2.5) or high (≥ 2.5) support. Scores were dichotomized following prior HBSC applications of the Teacher and Classmate Support Scale [17] and its original validation study [18]. Cut-points were done according to previous evidence based on the reliability and external validity of the Teacher and Classmate Support scale to represent distribution-based criteria that aim to identify participants who consistently report elevated levels of perceived support across the scale items [17–19]. Dichotomization of scores has been used to facilitate interpretability and comparability of prevalence estimates across countries and survey waves.

Throughout both HBSC waves, participants submitted self-reported information regarding their height and body weight, which was used to calculate body mass index (BMI) in kg/m2. Importantly, z-scores for BMI-for-age and height-for-age were used to assess “thinness” (low BMI-for-age), “overweight” and “obesity” (high BMI-for-age) in participants according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards [20]. For additional analysis, the presence of excess weight (i.e., overweight or obesity) was defined as follows: 0 = no excess weight (i.e., thinness or normal weight); 1 = excess weight (i.e., overweight or obesity). Weight status was operationalized as a binary variable (excess weight vs. non-excess weight) to align with established HBSC reporting practices and to facilitate interpretability in cross-national public health analyses. This approach also reduces potential misclassification arising from small errors in self-reported height and weight that may disproportionately affect finer BMI categories.

The children and adolescents provided their own age and gender in both HBSC study waves. For descriptive and analytical purposes, age was categorized into three developmental groups: 10.0–12.49 years, 12.50–14.49 years, and 14.50–17.00 years. Although the HBSC study traditionally samples students aged approximately 11, 13, and 15 years [12], the present categorization reflects the observed age distribution in the pooled dataset and allows inclusion of all participants rather than restricting analyses to exact target ages. Age categories were used in regression models to facilitate interpretation of developmental differences in perceived social support. Moreover, socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed in both survey waves with the Family Affluence Scale-III (FAS-III), a composite indicator of material affluence developed specifically for the HBSC study [12, 13]. The FAS-III assesses the material living conditions of households through six specific indicators: ownership of a car, ownership of a dishwasher, the presence of a personal bedroom, the frequency of international holidays, and the quantity of computers and bathrooms within the residence. The FAS-III instrument has demonstrated good reliability, construct validity, and cross-national comparability across diverse cultural and economic contexts, including multinational HBSC samples spanning Europe and North America [21]. A low SES was characterized by a score ranging from 0 to 7 points, whereas a medium SES was indicated by a score between 8 and 11 points, and a high SES was represented by a score of 12 to 13 points. To improve cross-national comparability and account for differences in absolute wealth distributions across countries, raw FAS-III scores were transformed into country-specific proportional ranks. This approach expresses each participant’s relative position within their national affluence distribution (range: 0–1). For descriptive purposes, these ranks were categorized into three groups representing low (bottom 20%), medium (middle 60%), and high (top 20%) relative affluence within each country, consistent with HBSC international reporting practices. The country-relative SES rank variable was used in all regression models, ensuring that associations reflect within-country relative socioeconomic differences rather than absolute cross-national wealth differences. Weighted prevalence estimates were calculated using HBSC survey weights to account for the complex sampling design and to ensure national representativeness.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as counts and percentages (%) for categorical variables, means ± SDs for continuous variables that are normally distributed, and medians along with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables that do not follow a normal distribution. The HBSC study employs a multistage clustered sampling design, with students nested within classes and schools. Sampling weights provided by the HBSC network were applied to prevalence estimates and incorporated into mixed-effects models to account for differential selection probabilities and nonresponse. In the multilevel models, country was included as a random intercept to account for between-country heterogeneity. Due to the lack of harmonized school- and classroom-level identifiers across all countries and waves, lower-level clustering could not be explicitly modeled. Therefore, while country-level clustering was accounted for, residual clustering at the school/class level may remain. Besides, weighted prevalences were calculated for each social support domain across countries considering the latest available wave. Analyses for each social support domain were conducted using all available countries with data for that specific domain and wave. Survey years vary by country within each HBSC wave, and cycle labels represent data collection windows rather than exact calendar years.

We fitted linear mixed models (LMMs) for continuous social support scores and generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) for high social support status. All models included a country-level random intercept (1 | country) to account for clustering of individuals within countries. Age group, sex, SES, excess weight, and survey wave were modeled as fixed effects. We examined more complex random-effects structures (e.g., age-by-sex and country-by-wave) but retained the random-intercept model because added complexity did not improve model fit and/or produced singular fits. LMMs results were presented as unstandardized beta coefficients (B) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and p-value. Moreover, adjusted generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) were applied to examine which correlates (age, sex, SES, excess weight status, and survey wave) predicted a “high social support” in each domain. GLMMs results were presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs and p-value. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better model performance. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were reported to quantify the proportion of variance attributable to between-country differences. Given the multistage cluster sampling design of the HBSC study, students were nested within classes and schools. In the present analysis, models incorporated a country-level random intercept to account for cross-national clustering. However, harmonized identifiers for schools and classrooms were not consistently available across all participating countries and survey waves, precluding the inclusion of additional lower-level random effects (e.g., school- or classroom-level clustering). Consequently, residual within-country clustering at the school or classroom level could not be explicitly modeled and may have resulted in modest underestimation of standard errors.

Cross-national time trends were assessed using HBSC survey wave as the temporal indicator, contrasting data from the 2013/2014 and 2017/2018 cycles. Time was therefore modeled as a categorical variable, allowing the estimation of overall differences between survey waves rather than assuming linear annual change. This approach was chosen to better reflect the discrete structure of the HBSC study design and the irregular timing and availability of data across countries. Countries and regions were included as random effects to account for clustering. Interaction terms between age group and sex were explored during model development but were not retained in final models due to limited and inconsistent contributions to model fit.

All analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach; participants with missing data on the outcome or any covariate included in a given model were excluded from that specific analysis. Consequently, sample sizes varied across models and are reported in the corresponding tables. Although this approach ensures comparability across model terms, it may introduce bias if data are not missing completely at random. Therefore, findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies using longitudinal designs and multiple imputation strategies are warranted to assess the robustness of these associations. In addition, BMI was missing for 97,636 participants (21.5%). Therefore, to assess the robustness of findings to BMI missingness and potential selection bias, three additional sensitivity analyses were conducted: (1) models excluding BMI as a covariate; (2) models using multiple imputation for BMI under a missing-at-random assumption; and (3) models including a missing-indicator category for BMI. Estimates from these approaches were compared with complete-case results to evaluate the stability of effect sizes and inference.

Statistical analyses were conducted via R statistical software (version 4.4.1; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio (version 2024.09.0 + 375; Posit, Boston, MA, USA). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 presents the descriptive information of the study participants. Overall, more participants were between 12.5 and 14.5 years of age (35%) and the majority were girls (51%). Furthermore, more children and adolescents reported a medium SES (59%) and were classified as not having excess weight (80%). In addition, for both survey waves the median score of social support from friends was 6.0 (IQR 4.5 to 6.8), whereas the median score of social support from family was 6.5 (IQR 5.0 to 7.0). The median scores of social supports from classmates and teachers were 2.0 (IQR 1.5 to 2.7) and 2.0 (IQR 1.3 to 2.7), respectively. In terms of perceived high social support status, support from family and friends were more prevalent (71% and 60%, respectively) in comparison to support perceived from teachers (5.7%) and classmates (4.0%).

Figure 1 illustrates the geographic distribution of high perceived social support prevalence from family, friends, classmates, and teachers among children and adolescents considering the latest available HBSC survey wave. Overall, family support had the highest prevalence, with most countries in Northern and Eastern Europe reporting levels above 85%. High perceived family support was more prevalent in participants from North Macedonia (89.3%), Albania (87.8%), and Serbia (84.5%). In contrast, England (54.2%), Canada (52.3%), and Bulgaria (40.2%) presented the lowest prevalence of high perceived family support. Friends’ support was also relatively high, with a prevalence ranging from 30% to over 75%, particularly in Central and Eastern Europe. For example, individuals from Spain (78.3%), Denmark (77.0%), and Hungary (77.0%) reported the highest levels of perceived friend support, whereas Ukraine (31.0%), Poland (33.1%), and Bulgaria (38.3%) had the lowest prevalences of high perceived friend support. In contrast, support from classmates was consistently low across countries, with most estimates between 1% and 8%, and only a few regions approached the upper range. For example, Turkey (8.5%), Ukraine (6.8%), and Bulgaria (6.6%) presented the highest prevalences of high perceived support from classmates. Conversely, Azerbaijan (1.1%), Germany (1.6%), and Armenia (1.7%) reported poor perceived high support from classmates. Similarly, teacher support was limited, with a prevalence generally below 10%, although slightly higher values were observed in northern and eastern European countries. Indeed, high perceived support from teachers was more common in individuals from Serbia (10.6%), Spain (9.4%), and Croatia (9.3%). Similarly, Azerbaijan (1.5%), Albania (2.9%), and Georgia (2.9%) reported the lowest prevalences of high perceived support from teachers. These country-level differences are presented descriptively; given the absence of uncertainty measures in the figure, apparent differences in prevalence should be interpreted as indicative patterns rather than statistically significant contrasts. Reported cross-national prevalence estimates and temporal comparisons are domain-specific and reflect the countries contributing data for that particular support measure in each survey wave. Figure 2 illustrates descriptive country-level changes between waves (2013/2014 vs. 2017/2018) in the perceived social support score from family, friends, classmates, and teachers among participants from the HBSC study. In terms of family support, Luxembourg (+ 14.7%), Belgium-Flemish (+ 12.6%), and Greenland (+ 12.2%) increased their prevalence of high perceived family support between the study waves. In comparison, Bulgaria (-18.8%), Czechia (-18.4%), and Slovenia (-10.0%) decreased their prevalence of high perceived family support. Furthermore, an increase in high levels of perceived friend support were observed in Belgium-Flemish (+ 13.6%), Malta (+ 9.4%), and Spain (+ 9.3%). In contrast, decreases in high perceived friends support were reported in Poland (-19.5%), Czechia (-15.8%), and Bulgaria (-14.1%). In addition, Ukraine (+ 2.8%), Greenland (+ 2.7%), and Wales (+ 2.7%) had increased their high perceived support from classmates, whereas decreased prevalences were observed in Armenia (-1.2%), Russia (-1.0%), and Estonia (-0.8%). Finally, Ukraine (+ 4.3%), Croatia (+ 2.6%), and Austria (+ 2.3%) increased their prevalence of high perceived support from teachers. Conversely, Scotland (-3.4%), Israel (-2.2%), and Ireland (-1.3%) decreased their prevalence of high perceived support from teachers between study waves (2013/2014 vs. 2017/2018). Although some countries showed noticeable percentage changes between waves, these shifts represent aggregate changes in the prevalence of dichotomized support and should be interpreted as modest population-level trends rather than substantial changes in individual perceptions of social support. Table 2 presents associations between children and adolescents’ characteristics and social support score by source. With respect to family support scores, older adolescents, girls, and participants with excess weight perceived less family support (p < 0.001 for all). In addition, medium and high SES were associated with higher family support scores (p < 0.001 for both). Participants who were girls and had a medium and high SES reported higher support scores from friends (p < 0.001 for all). Conversely, participants between 12.5 and 14.5 years (p = 0.007) and those with excess weight presented lower support scores from friends (p < 0.001). With respect to classmates’ support, older age, female sex, and excess weight were associated with higher perceived support scores (p < 0.001 for all). In contrast, medium and high SES were associated with lower support scores from classmates (p < 0.001 for both). Finally, teachers’ support scores were higher among participants who were older, female, had high SES, and presented excess weight (p < 0.001 for all). When contrasting data from the 2013/2014 and 2017/2018 survey waves, statistically significant associations were observed for survey wave across all support sources (p < 0.001 for all). However, the corresponding coefficients were small in magnitude, indicating gradual population-level changes in support scores rather than substantively large shifts at the individual level.

Table 3 describes the associations between participant characteristics and high social support status by source. For family support, older adolescents, girls, and participants with excess weight were less likely to report high perceived family support (all p < 0.001), whereas participants with medium and high SES were more likely to have greater family support (p < 0.001 for both). With respect to friends’ support, older individuals and participants with excess weight had lower odds of presenting high perceived support from friends (all p < 0.001). In contrast, girls and participants with high and medium SES had increased odds of high perceived support from friends (all p < 0.001). Similarly, older adolescents, girls, and those with excess weight were more likely to have high perceived support from classmates (all p < 0.001). However, medium and high SES were inversely associated with high support from classmates (p < 0.001 for both). Notably, the odds of reporting high classmate support were similarly elevated among adolescents aged 12.5–14.5 years and 14.5–17 years compared with younger children. Furthermore, older participants, those with high SES, and individuals with excess weight were more likely to report a high perceived support from teachers (all p < 0.001). Nevertheless, girls and individuals with medium SES presented lower odds of high perceived support from teachers (p < 0.001 for both). Finally, although survey wave was statistically associated with the likelihood of high perceived support across sources (p < 0.001 for all), the observed odds ratios were close to unity, indicating modest changes in prevalence over time rather than large shifts in the probability of reporting high support. Although many associations reached statistical significance, likely due to the very large sample size, most effect estimates were small in magnitude. These findings therefore reflect modest population-level differences, and their practical or clinical relevance should be interpreted cautiously rather than inferred solely from statistical significance.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses addressing BMI missingness yielded substantively consistent results across all modeling strategies (complete-case, exclusion of BMI, multiple imputation, and missing-indicator approach). Effect estimates for age, sex, SES, and survey wave were highly stable in magnitude and direction across models. Associations between excess weight and perceived social support also remained consistent, with only minor attenuation under multiple imputation. These findings suggest that BMI missingness did not materially influence the primary conclusions (Supplementary Tables S3–S4).

Discussion

The present study provides novel insights into the patterns and correlates of perceived social support among children and adolescents across multiple countries from the HBSC study between 2013/2014 and 2017/2018. In general, we identified that family emerged as the strongest and most stable source of support, with approximately 7 out of 10 participants reporting high levels of family support, although this finding declined with age and was less frequently reported by girls and individuals with excess weight. Friends were reported as the second most important source, yet their support showed a small declining trend when contrasting data from the 2013/2014 and 2017/2018 survey waves, reflecting limited population-level shifts rather than substantial changes in individual perceptions. In contrast, support from classmates and teachers was consistently low, although statistically detectable increases of small magnitude were observed, particularly among older adolescents. Notably, older participants (12.5–14.5 years and 14.5–17 years) showed similarly elevated odds of high classmate support compared with younger children, suggesting that increases in perceived classmate support may occur primarily in early adolescence and then stabilize across later ages. Socioeconomic gradients were evident, with higher SES associated with greater support from family and friends, whereas lower SES was linked to greater perceived support from classmates; however, these associations were generally small in magnitude and reflect average differences at the population level. Given the large sample size, even very small effects achieved statistical significance; therefore, findings (especially those contrasting 2013/2014 vs. 2017/2018 HBSC waves) should be interpreted in terms of their practical magnitude, which reflects gradual, incremental changes at the population level rather than pronounced shifts in perceived social support. Accordingly, the public health relevance of these findings lies less in the magnitude of individual-level effects and more in the identification of consistent directional patterns across large populations, which may signal structurally patterned inequalities in social support. Taken together, our findings highlight the central role of family and peers in childhood and adolescent development while also highlighting important social and contextual disparities in perceived support.

Our results are in line with those of previous studies [5, 8, 10, 11, 22, 23]. For example, large-scale cross-sectional and longitudinal studies have shown that both the frequency of consultation with family members and satisfaction with family support tend to be lower at older ages, whereas support from peers and romantic pairs becomes more prominent and satisfying with increasing age [8, 11]. Boys reported higher levels of family support than girls in a previous study from the HBSC survey [11]. In addition, consistent with our results, Bi et al. [10] reported that the perceived support of classmates and teachers was lower than that of family and friends. In addition, community-based longitudinal studies suggest that teachers may be consulted more frequently as adolescents grow older, even if satisfaction with teacher support remains relatively stable [8]. In terms of excess weight status, previous research has shown that family functioning and support are generally protective for weight-related behaviors and psychosocial outcomes in adolescents but does not suggest that adolescents with excess weight receive more family support than their peers do [22]. Additionally, previous research has shown that lower SES in childhood and adolescence is consistently linked to poorer perceived social support, both within the family and in broader social contexts [23]. Although the specific mechanisms explaining how perceived social support from family, friends, classmates, and teachers change over time and differ across subgroups are not fully understood, several factors are likely involved. These include pubertal developmental processes, gender roles and expectations, communication patterns, emotional expressiveness, relational needs, digital communication, parenting practices, resource availability, social networks, stigma, and inclusion dynamics, as well as broader policy changes and evolving cultural norms.

Previous longitudinal and neurodevelopmental studies suggest that pubertal processes may contribute to shifts in social orientation during adolescence [24, 25], which could partially align with the age-related patterns observed in our data. The hormonal and physical changes of puberty drive significant brain maturation, particularly in regions involved in social cognition and emotional processing, which recalibrates adolescents’ social motivations and perceptions [24]. This neurodevelopmental shift increases the salience of peer relationships and decreases reliance on caregivers, as adolescents become more attuned to social cues from age-matched peers and seek greater autonomy from family structures [24, 25]. In addition, pubertal development also retunes perceptual and cognitive systems, such as facial recognition, away from caregivers and toward peers, facilitating the formation of new social bonds and the prioritization of peer acceptance and group belonging [25]. Longitudinal data confirm that as adolescents progress through puberty, there is a decline in perceived support from parents and grandparents, whereas support from best friends and romantic partners becomes more prominent and satisfying [8]. The evolving social environment, shaped by both intrinsic developmental processes and extrinsic social contexts, underlies the observed changes in social support during this critical period. This neurodevelopmental and psychosocial shift during puberty aligns with our findings showing a gradual decline in perceived family support with increasing age, alongside stable or modestly increasing levels of teacher and classmate support. However, given the repeated cross-sectional design, these interpretations remain hypothesis-generating. The present findings capture population-level differences across age groups rather than within-individual developmental trajectories. Longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether these patterns reflect true developmental change.

Changes in gender roles during childhood and adolescence have a significant effect on the nature and availability of social support. Observational and longitudinal data indicate that girls tend to engage more in emotionally supportive problem talk and receive more positive, engaged responses from peers, which fosters emotional closeness in friendships [26]. Boys, in contrast, were less likely to participate in problem talk and more likely to use humor as a pathway to closeness, suggesting that traditional masculine norms may limit the expression and reception of emotional support among boys, but alternative forms of support (e.g., humor) can be functionally significant for them [26]. As gender roles and norms evolve, there is evidence that endorsement of masculine-type behaviors decreases during middle adolescence and that fluctuations in these behaviors are closely tied to peer experiences [27]. Specifically, negative peer experiences can reinforce masculine-type behaviors, whereas friend support is associated with reduced adherence to these norms, highlighting the dynamic interplay between gender role socialization and peer-driven support processes [27]. Additionally, gender expectations further shape the type of support received. For example, males tend to receive more instrumental support, whereas females are more likely to receive emotional support in online contexts; however, recent trends show that emotional support for females may be declining, whereas males experience consistent increases across all support dimensions [28]. These gendered patterns of social interaction align with our results, which showed small but consistent differences whereby girls reported higher perceived support from friends and classmates but lower perceived support from family compared with boys. Sex differences in perceived social support may be influenced by gender socialization processes, communication norms, and culturally embedded expectations regarding emotional expression and relational engagement. Nevertheless, these interpretations should be considered speculative. Because the current analyses are observational and cross-sectional at each survey wave, causal mechanisms cannot be established. Future longitudinal and cross-cultural research is required to clarify the extent to which gender norms or socialization processes explain these population-level differences.

Parenting practices influence changes in social support among children and adolescents through several interrelated mechanisms grounded in social learning, emotional development, and the modeling of interpersonal behaviors [29–32]. Longitudinal evidence demonstrates that supportive parent‒adolescent relationships predict the development of supportive peer and romantic relationships, suggesting that the quality of early familial interactions is internalized and carried forward into broader social contexts [29]. Furthermore, daily life studies have shown that children and adolescents who perceive greater parental care and autonomy support report more positive social experiences in their everyday interactions, not only with family but also with peers, indicating that parenting styles shape the adolescent’s broader social world [30]. Moreover, parental warmth and autonomy support are associated with the development of adaptive emotion regulation strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal), which facilitate prosocial behavior and the ability to seek and maintain social support [31]. Additionally, the intergenerational transmission of social capital occurs indirectly: children and adolescents’ perceptions of their parents’ sociability influence their own social trust, network formation, and tendencies to both give and receive help. This process is shaped by social learning, where children and adolescents model the social behaviors and attitudes observed in their parents [32]. In line with this framework, our findings indicate that perceived family support gradually declines with age but remains socially patterned by SES, with adolescents from higher-affluence families reporting consistently greater family support. Differences in family support across socioeconomic groups may reflect variation in parenting practices, time availability, or family stressors. However, this explanation cannot be directly tested within the current dataset. These proposed mechanisms are hypothesis-generating and should be examined in future research using longitudinal designs or intervention studies that can more directly assess family processes and their temporal relationship with adolescents’ perceived support.

Weight stigma, social exclusion, and altered interpersonal dynamics could influence the level and quality of social support among children and adolescents with excess weight [33, 34]. Indeed, individuals with overweight or obesity experience higher rates of teasing, bullying, and discrimination from peers, family, and even healthcare providers, which directly undermines their access to supportive social relationships and increases psychological distress [33]. Social network analyses reveal that adolescents with overweight or obesity occupy more peripheral positions in peer networks, with fewer and less intense social connections, particularly among females [35]. This marginalization is associated with lower social prestige and reduced likelihood of being nominated as friends, romantic interests, or admired individuals, contributing to social isolation and diminished social support [33, 35]. Moreover, the experience of weight-related stigma and self-stigmatization further erodes trust in close others and willingness to disclose personal concerns, reinforcing social withdrawal and loneliness [33, 36]. Importantly, the negative impact of weight stigma on psychosocial well-being appears to be more significant than the direct effect of obesity itself; poor social support is strongly associated with increased depression, anxiety, and loneliness, and can mediate the relationship between weight status and psychological distress [36]. One study found that teacher intervention (specifically, when students perceived teachers as helpful in preventing future weight-based victimization) attenuated the negative impact of peer mistreatment on academic performance among adolescents with high body weight [37]. Furthermore, robust teacher support was linked to more accurate weight perceptions among adolescents, which may indirectly reduce social exclusion by decreasing internalized stigma and misperceptions [38]. Although the models accounted for between-country variability through random intercepts, we did not formally examine cross-national heterogeneity in the association between excess weight and perceived teacher or classmate support. It is plausible that this relationship varies across sociocultural contexts, particularly in settings with differing norms regarding body image, stigma, and inclusive school practices. Future research should explore country-specific patterns and potential contextual moderators to better understand whether supportive school environments differentially buffer weight-based social exclusion across settings.

Despite this literature, our study identified a more complex pattern. Adolescents with excess weight reported, on average, lower perceived support from family and friends but slightly higher perceived support from classmates and teachers. This finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect differences between informal personal relationships and structured school environments. The differential patterns of perceived support may reflect a higher frequency of weight-based criticism and teasing from family and friends, contrasted with the potential for structured support and protective interventions from teachers and classmates within the school setting [38, 39]. For instance, previous literature demonstrates that parents and siblings often make critical or restrictive remarks about weight, shape, or eating behaviors, which is associated with diminished social support and self-esteem in affected youth [40]. Likewise, friends are also common perpetrators of weight-based teasing, contributing to negative emotions and avoidance behaviors, further eroding perceived support from these relationships [39, 40]. In contrast, some adolescents may perceive higher support from classmates and teachers, particularly in structured school environments where anti-bullying policies and teacher interventions may mitigate overt victimization [41]. Furthermore, teacher support (informational and emotional) is also associated with more accurate weight perceptions and may buffer against the negative effects of weight stigma [38, 41]. Importantly, these associations may vary by sex, region, school climate, or national inclusion policies, none of which were explicitly examined in the present analyses. Future research should therefore prioritize stratified analyses by sex and geographic region to clarify whether these patterns reflect true contextual differences or residual confounding. Finally, it is important to emphasize that the observed associations reflect average population-level differences of generally small magnitude across repeated cross-sectional survey waves and should not be interpreted as evidence of causal relationships or within-individual developmental change.

This research has certain limitations that should be considered when our results are analyzed. First, although we examined changes across survey waves, the HBSC data are based on repeated cross-sectional samples rather than longitudinal follow-up of the same individuals. Consequently, the observed trends reflect population-level changes over time and cannot be interpreted as within-individual developmental trajectories or causal effects. Differences between survey years may partially reflect cohort effects, changes in sample composition, or unmeasured contextual factors rather than true changes in perceived social support at the individual level. Second, reliance on self-reported measures for both outcomes and covariates, including SES and BMI, may introduce recall bias, social desirability bias, and potential misclassification. Third, while the instruments employed provided valuable insights into perceived support, they may not fully capture the complexity of supportive behaviors or the quality of relationships across different contexts. In fact, unmeasured confounders that were not directly assessed could influence perceptions of support in children and adolescents. Fourth, although country was included as a random effect to account for clustering, the analyses did not formally examine effect modification by country-level or cultural factors, and thus regional patterns should be interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of systematic cross-national differences. We encourage future multilevel models with country-level indicators, such as economic inequality, education systems, or social norms, to potentially provide better contextualization of cross-national differences. Fifth, a notable proportion of the responses were missing, which could introduce bias if not missing completely at random. BMI had substantial missingness (21.5%); however, sensitivity analyses using multiple imputation, missing-indicator modeling, and BMI exclusion demonstrated highly consistent findings across analytic strategies. This reduces concern that selection bias due to BMI missingness materially affected the observed associations. In addition, complete-case analysis may introduce bias if data are not missing completely at random, so future studies using longitudinal designs and harmonized data collection procedures should further address this issue using appropriate imputation strategies. Sixth, although temporal trends were explored, each survey wave (2013/2014 vs. 2017/2018) was treated as a categorical variable, rather than modeling year as a continuous predictor. This approach reflects the discrete structure of the HBSC design, avoids assuming linearity over time, and aligns with the pooled nature of the data across countries with different survey years. Seventh, not all countries measured all domains of social support in both survey waves. Specifically, family support was not available for Denmark (2013/2014 and 2017/2018) and Canada (2013/2014); friends’ support was not available for Denmark and Canada in 2017/2018; teacher and classmate support were not available for Poland and Latvia in 2017/2018. Moreover, seven counties reported data only for a single study wave (2013/2014 = Finland; 2017/2018 = Azerbaijan, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Lithuania, Serbia, Turkey). Consequently, trend analyses for each domain were based on slightly different subsets of countries, and temporal changes therefore reflect within-subset trends rather than changes across all 45 participating countries. This may limit comparability of trends across domains and could introduce bias if excluded countries differ systematically in social or educational contexts. However, given that only two countries per domain and wave were excluded, and that models accounted for country-level clustering, we consider it unlikely that these exclusions fully explain the observed overall patterns, although effect sizes should be interpreted cautiously. Eighth, perceived social support scores were dichotomized into “high” versus “low” categories using cut-points established in previous HBSC reports to facilitate interpretability and comparability across countries. However, dichotomization of continuous measures inevitably results in loss of information, reduced statistical power, and potential misclassification, particularly for individuals with scores near the threshold. In addition, alternative cut-points or continuous modeling may yield different prevalence estimates.

Despite these limitations, the study also has important strengths. It analyzes a very large, multinational sample of children and adolescents, which enhances the statistical power and generalizability of the findings across diverse social and cultural contexts. Furthermore, the standardized instruments and protocols employed across countries allowed for valid cross-national comparisons and robust trend analyses. Moreover, the study considered multiple sources of social support; therefore, it provides a comprehensive overview of support networks among children and adolescents rather than focusing on a single domain. Finally, the use of advanced statistical models that account for clustering and potential confounders strengthens the validity of the observed associations. Nevertheless, future longitudinal cohort studies with objective measures of social support that incorporate further confounding variables could enhance the comprehension of the causal mechanisms of changes in the perceived social support experienced by youths. These temporal patterns should not be interpreted as within-individual longitudinal change. In addition, although age–sex interactions were explored, their limited and inconsistent contribution suggests that more nuanced effect modification may be better examined in stratified or domain-specific analyses in future research.

Conclusion

The present study highlights meaningful heterogeneity in perceived social support prevalence, trends, and correlates among children and adolescents, although most observed temporal changes were modest in magnitude. Families were the most consistent and robust source of support, whereas classmates and teachers provided limited support, although both showed modest increases between the study waves. The modest decline in friends support between waves and the consistently lower levels of support perceived by girls, older adolescents, and those with excess weight underscore the potential value of targeted interventions. Public health and educational policies should therefore prioritize strengthening supportive environments within families and schools, with a particular emphasis on fostering inclusive peer relationships and enhancing teacher‒student connections. Promoting equitable access to supportive networks for vulnerable groups, such as children and adolescents from lower socioeconomic backgrounds or those experiencing weight-related stigma, may help reduce social inequalities and contribute to healthier developmental trajectories during adolescence.
