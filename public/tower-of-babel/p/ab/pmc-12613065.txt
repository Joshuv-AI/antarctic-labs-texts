Phage therapy: Innovative approaches for refractory pulmonary infections
Authors: Yan Qian, Zhichen Zhu, Jie Zhu, Liang Chen, Hong Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Drug resistance is reducing the effectiveness of antibiotics for lung infections.•Phage offers a promising therapeutic strategy for pulmonary infection.•Emerging phage therapies pose high efficiency and minimal side effects.

Introduction

Among the most fatal infectious diseases, lower respiratory infections rank as the fourth most common cause of mortality worldwide. Antibiotics are still the only recognized treatment for lung infections. However, the World Health Organization has identified antibiotic-resistant bacteria as one of the top three public health dangers of the twenty-first century due to the overuse and abuse of antibiotics (Williams et al., 2023). Antibiotic-resistant illnesses caused 4.95 million indirect deaths and 1.27 million direct deaths in 2019. Pneumonia and other lower respiratory tract infections were the main cause of death among them, accounting for 1.9 million deaths (Global burden of bacterial antimicrobial, 2022). By 2050, diseases caused by bacteria resistant to antibiotics might kill 10 million people annually if current trends continue (Trotter et al., 2019).

Phage therapy is gaining attention as an alternative treatment for pulmonary infections due to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria and a decline in new antibiotic development. Bacteriophages or phages are viruses that infect and multiply inside bacterial cells. They are abundant and ubiquitous in the natural environment, making them the most common organisms on Earth (Clokie et al., 2011). Phages can have either double-stranded or single-stranded DNA or RNA. Morphologically, they can be caudate, polyhedral, filamentous, or pleomorphic (Ackermann, 2009). Based on their lytic ability, phages are classified into lytic (virulent) phages and lysogenic (temperate) phages. Lytic phages replicate within the host bacterium and eventually lyse it, which is why phage therapy typically utilizes lytic phage (Xu et al., 2022).

Lysogenic phages integrate their DNA (called prophage) into the host bacterium's nucleic acid, allowing the phage nucleic acid to replicate along with the host cell without causing lysis. This process, known as lysogenic transformation, enables the phage to proliferate with the host cell through successive generations (Xu et al., 2022). The lysogenic phage affords the host bacterium with “superinfection immunity.” The prophage incorporated into the host chromosome commonly keeps to explicit a repressor protein. This protein now no longer handiest inhibits the expression of the prophage`s personal lytic genes, however additionally mediates immunity in opposition to next infections with the aid of using different phages that encode the equal or exceedingly comparable repressors (Bondy-Denomy et al., 2016). However, recent advancements in synthetic biology and genetic engineering are exploring the use of mild phages for therapeutic purposes (CHEN and TONG, 2023).

Bacteriophages are host-specific and do not disrupt normal flora. Compared with antibiotics, it has the following advantages: (1) the bacteriophage has strong reproductive ability and can effectively kill bacteria; (2) Safe proteins and nucleic acids make up the majority of bacteriophages; (3) There is no cross-resistance with antibiotics (Ling et al., 2022). Phage therapy is categorized into traditional and emerging approaches. Traditional phage therapy involves using phages solely as therapeutic agents to treat bacterial infections and can be further divided into single-phage and multi-phage therapies. Emerging phage therapy encompasses synergistic treatments that combine phages with antibiotics, the use of phage derivatives, the integration of phages with bioengineering, and the development of phage vaccines.

Most studies have focused on natural phage treatments for pulmonary infections, including both animal models and human cases, typically administered through nebulization inhalation or intravenous infusion. The safety of these methods has been confirmed. However, there are currently no reviews summarizing the emerging phage therapeutic approaches for pulmonary infections. This review aims to highlight recent advances in these emerging phage therapies for treating pulmonary infections.

Combination therapy with antibiotics

Phage-antibiotic synergy (PAS) was initially discovered by Comeau et al., who observed that bacteria can significantly boost their production of cytophagosomes when exposed to sublethal concentrations of antibiotics (Comeau et al., 2007). The application of phages in combination with antibiotics is currently one of the most promising approaches for addressing antibiotic resistance, and represents a transitional step from conventional antibiotic therapy to phage therapy. The relationship between phage therapy and conventional antibiotics is complex and poorly understood. In vitro studies on the combination of phages and antibiotics have shown a range of effects, including additive, synergistic, antagonistic, or no effect (Gu Liu et al., 2020). Numerous studies have demonstrated that combining phages and antibiotics can be effective, particularly in promoting the destruction of biofilms, which often obstructs antibiotic therapy (Chan et al., 2016). The use of phage-antibiotic pairings has been shown to be more efficient at eliminating germs in biofilms than antibiotics alone. Interestingly, certain antibiotics might be more effective at lower dosages when employed in conjunction with the phages. Additionally, the effectiveness of antibiotics in the treatment of biofilm infections is greatly enhanced when phages are applied before antibiotics (Chaudhry et al., 2017). In addition, different dosing sequences can affect the results (Akturk et al., 2019; Berryhill et al., 2021).

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a common causative agent of refractory lower respiratory tract infections, often leading to conditions such as cystic fibrosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and bronchiectasis (Chang et al., 2022). This bacterium is notorious for its drug resistance and biofilm formation, particularly with the emergence of carbapenem-resistant strains in the past decade, making antibiotic treatment increasingly ineffective. As a result, there has been growing interest in using phages against this pathogen. Lin et al.( 2021) established a pulmonary infection model of P. aeruginosa and verified the synergistic activity of a phage PEV20-ciprofloxacin combined powder preparation. They discovered that the endotracheal delivery of the PEV20-ciprofloxacin combination notably decreased the lung bacterial load in mice by 5.9 log10 (p < 0.005), whereas single treatments failed to achieve this reduction. In another study, Martin et al. assessed the efficacy of intratracheal and intraperitoneal (IP) administration of phage therapy (both by itself and in conjunction with meropenem) in a mouse model of acute P. aeruginosa pneumonia that is immunocompromised. Their multiple experiments revealed that the survival rate with the combined treatment was nearly 100 %. Additionally, the bacterial load in the lungs and spleen of all treatment groups was significantly reduced, and the pulmonary pathological score was improved (Duplessis et al., 2021).

Now, the conventional notion of PAS can be significantly broadened, as phages and antibiotics are used in the bulk of phage therapy case reports that have been published. Aslam et al. (2019) documented the application of phages in treating respiratory tract infections in three lung transplant patients. During phage therapy, all patients continued antibiotic treatment. Although the pathogen was not completely eradicated following combination therapy, the patients exhibited significant symptomatic improvement, and no adverse effects from phage therapy were observed. However, one patient succumbed to secondary antimicrobial poisoning. Dedrick et al. (2023) used phage therapy compassionately in 20 patients with drug-resistant mycobacteria, 90 % of whom had pulmonary infections. During phage therapy, most patients received at least one or two partially active antibiotics alongside phages. All but two patients received intravenous phage therapy, followed by nebulized phage therapy after the intravenous regimen. The results showed improvement or regression of culture conversion, smear, or clinical and imaging mycobacteria in more than half of the 20 patients treated. Importantly, the study reported no adverse effects attributable to the treatment, regardless of the pathogen, phage administered, or route of administration. However, recent studies have shown that in certain cases, phages and antibiotics can exhibit antagonistic interactions. The underlying mechanisms remain unclear, but may involve antibiotic-mediated interference with phage replication (Łusiak-Szelachowska et al., 2022). Additionally, the optimal route of phage administration and the adequacy of tissue penetration remain unknown, and the dose and regimen of administration need to be optimized. Therefore, PAS must be based on precise experimental diagnostics. Overall, these research findings uphold the idea that phages can boost the effectiveness of antibiotics in treating such infections.

Phage derivatives

Phage derivatives refer to the proteins encoded by phage genes, such as virion-associated peptidoglycan hydrolase (VAPGH) and polysaccharide depolymerases (Chandran et al., 2022; Danis-Wlodarczyk et al., 2021). VAPGH is a structural component of the virus, typically located on the phage substrate, that locally degrades peptidoglycan during infection, leading to changes in the permeability of the bacterial cell wall. As stated by Pires et al. (2016) , The majority of depolymerases are encoded within or adjacent to structural genes found in phage genomes, such as base plates and tail fibers. Their primary function is to degrade the polysaccharide components of the bacterial cell envelope, such as the extracellular matrix of biofilms, lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and the bacterial capsule. The breakdown of these structures reveals the phage receptor, which is necessary for the phage to successfully infect the host.

Depolymerases can be classified into hydrolases and lyases based on their mechanisms of action. Both types degrade peptidoglycan, capsular polysaccharide (CPS), or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) of bacteria into oligosaccharides. Hydrolytic enzymes include sialase, acetylase, xylosidase, glucanase, and rhamnosaminase, which catalyze the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds. Lyases, such as hyaluronase, alginate lyase, and pectin lyase, cleave the β−1,4-glycosidic bond through a β-elimination mechanism (Knecht et al., 2019). Through the degradation of polysaccharides, phage-derived depolymerases can prevent the establishment and maintenance of biofilms, thereby exposing bacteria to antibiotics and the host immune system in the external environment.

Phage-derived enzymes bear greater resemblance to traditional antibiotics and depolymerases do not lyse bacteria or release endotoxins. Therefore, in certain instances, enzymes might be favored over whole phages. SAL200, originating from a phage endolysin, is a new candidate drug for treating Staphylococcus aureus infection. Research by Bae et al. shows that intranasal administration of SAL200 can improve survival rates, reduce bacterial loads in the lung and blood, and promote histopathological improvement of lung tissue without significantly increasing cytokine release in a mouse model of Staphylococcus aureus pneumonia (Bae et al., 2019).

Bacteriophage endolysins Cpl-1 and ClyJ-3 have demonstrated promising antibacterial activity against Streptococcus pneumoniae. Raz et al. used a systematic phylogenetic approach to explore the sequenced genome of P. aeruginosa and selected phage lysins PlyPa03 and PlyPa91 from 16 lysins. Both PlyPa03 and PlyPa91 hemolysins were not impaired in tests with concentrations up to 25 % in a combination of bovine lung surfactant and artificial surfactant. In a mouse model of pulmonary infection, intranasal and intratracheal administration of PlyPa91 lysin resulted in 70 % of the mice surviving on the 10th day, while all untreated mice died within 48 h after infection (Raz et al., 2019). Some scholars have discussed the potential role of polymerase in secondary bacterial infections in COVID-19 patients (Nazir et al., 2023). The use of enzymes produced by phages can significantly slow the development of resistance. Phage-derived lytic enzymes, including endolysins and depolymerases, often exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity. The catalytic domains of these enzymes exhibit substrate promiscuity, while C-terminal cell wall-binding domains adapt to diverse surface structures, allowing recognition of distinct bacterial targets (Abdelrahman et al., 2021). Particularly depolymerases, demonstrate non-specific substrate affinity, meaning they can act on similar polysaccharide structures across different bacterial species. While this enhances their potential as broad-spectrum antibiofilm agents, it also raises concerns about possible off-target effects on beneficial microbiota or non-pathogenic bacteria (Letarov and Letarova, 2023).

Phage-derived enzymes can also work in combination with antibiotics. Due to biofilm formation, bacteria have additional resistance to antimicrobials such as fungicides and antibiotics. Phage-encoded depolymerases can induce susceptibility to antimicrobials, penetrate biofilms, and destroy their structures, which is usually not possible with antibiotics alone. Chen and colleagues discovered, cloned, and produced polymerase Dpo71 from a phage unique to Acinetobacter baumannii. In addition to acting as an adjuvant to improve the effectiveness of antibiotics like colistin, Dpo71 makes MDR A. baumannii more vulnerable to host immunological attacks. >90 % of the bacterial cells buried in the biofilm were destroyed by colistin with the aid of Dpo71 (Chen et al., 2022). Depo32 (Cai et al., 2023), a depolymerase produced by the gene gp32, was discovered by Cai et al. to preferentially break down K2 Klebsiella pneumoniae capsule polysaccharide (CPS). This enzyme stimulates the phagocytosis of macrophages and increases the bactericidal action of serum. When combined with gentamicin, all mice survived, and the histology of the lung tissue and the bacterial clearance were noticeably better than when either medication was taken alone. In addition, the effectiveness of the combination of phage-derived enzymes and antibiotics has been widely proved, especially in the destruction of biofilm (Azeredo et al., 2021; Eghbalpoor et al., 2024; Ferry et al., 2021; J Kim et al., 2025; Sanmukh et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2025). The depolymerase's therapeutic efficacy is time-dependent, though.

Despite these encouraging outcomes, several limitations hinder clinical translation. The isolation, cloning, expression, purification, and functional evaluation of phage-derived enzymes are technically demanding and costly. Moreover, obtaining highly pure enzyme preparations is more labor-intensive and expensive than using intact phages. Combined with the potential risks of non-specific enzymatic activity, these factors currently limit their clinical applicability. Thus, while phage-derived enzymes (used alone or in combination with antibiotics) represent a promising strategy for combating resistant and biofilm-associated infections, further technological and regulatory advancements are required for their widespread clinical implementation.

Phage genome engineering

Phages are widely found in the environment, but owing to their diversity and complex relationship with bacteria, only some natural phages are used for treatment due to safety concerns. Natural phage isolates have to satisfy specific criteria: All contaminated bacteria should be lysed; they should not spread bacterial DNA or harbor genes that make them virulent or resistant to antibiotics. They must also resist the host's immune response (Fernández et al., 2019). Despite recognize that hundreds of phages that target bacterial infections have been identified, none have met all these criteria. The application of phage therapy with natural phages is limited by issues such as narrow host range, phage resistance, or unstable phage particles. Consequently, there is increasing interest in genetically engineering phages for safer and more suitable therapeutic applications.

A genetically modified phage is obtained in two stages: (1) replacing the complete phage genome or a portion of it with a modified one in vivo or in vitro, and (2) recovering the transformed phage (Łobocka et al., 2021). For phage genome engineering, the most prevalent method is homologous recombination (HR) between host - mediated phage DNA and donor DNA cloned within a plasmid (Court et al., 2002). This approach is frequently utilized to swap and link phage genes associated with receptor recognition, thereby bringing about alterations in the host range or to investigate the interactions between phages and newly identified bacterial defense systems (Loose et al., 2021). For instance, Mahichi et al. added the long-tail fiber gene of phage IP008 into phage T2 of Escherichia coli through homologous recombination to expand the host range (Court et al., 2002). The resultant chimeric phage demonstrated the wider host range of phage IP008 while retaining the potent lytic activity of phage T2 (Mahichi et al., 2009). One limitation of this method is the low recombination efficiency and the need to screen a large number of plaques due to the inability to use selectable markers during phage lytic growth. Recombinants can also be detected by plaque hybridization.

CRISPR-Cas systems, which consist of CRISPR-associated proteins and clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats, are used to counter-select unmodified phages and enrich mutant offspring (Loose et al., 2021; Wetzel et al., 2021). The CRISPR-Cas locus consists of short identical repeats interspersed with spacer sequences flanked by Cas protein genes. Repeat length varies from 21 bp to 47 bp depending on the species, and the spacer bars are similarly sized at 20–72 bp (Karginov and Hannon, 2010). The protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) serves to recognize target DNA. The protospacer, a downstream DNA fragment, is replicated from the foreign DNA and converted into CRISPR RNA (crRNA) after PAM detection. When crRNA and trans-activated crRNA direct the Cas9 endonuclease to the cleavage site of the invasive DNA, foreign DNA or RNA is degraded (Jinek et al., 2012).

CRISPR - Cas programmable targeting has effectively increased the prevalence of phage recombinants by eliminating wild - type (WT) phages from the population. Additionally, it has been combined with the addition of anti-CRISPR genes that function as selective indicators. Guan et al. genetically modified the jumbo phages ΦKZ and OMKO1 and the small podophage PaMx41 using the RNA-targeting CRISPR-Cas13a system and homologous recombination. These phages all target CRISPR-Cas systems and are resistant to DNA (Guan et al., 2022). This method can produce synthetic phages with desired characteristics accurately and efficiently, not only for double-stranded DNA phages but also for designing single-stranded DNA phages. Some researchers surmise that within a few years, a set of CRISPR - based tools will make it easier to carry out smaller edits or engineer entire phage genomes. This would enable them to meet any design requirements for a wide variety of therapeutic uses (Strathdee et al., 2023).

By adding Type I anti-CRISPR (AcrIF1, AcrIF2, and AcrIF3) genes into the P. aeruginosa bacteriophage DMS3/DMS3m, Qin et al. investigated engineered anti-CRISPR (Acr) gene-containing phages (EATPs, eat Pseudomonas). The impacts of these on MDR pathogenic P. aeruginosa infections were examined via an infection model of the respiratory tract in mice. The results showed that the EATPs effectively reduced the infection of clinically isolated MDR P. aeruginosa strains, including PA-154,197 and PA-139,357, both in vitro and in vivo, at an absolute inhibitory dose of 1 × 108 PFU/mL with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.2 (Qin et al., 2022). Notably, DMS3acrIF1 could inhibit the expansion of MDR strain PA14, with an effective inhibition rate of around 40 %, which antibiotics alone couldn't achieve. This demonstrates that EATPs, when combined with antibiotics, significantly enhance the suppression of antibiotic resistance. Additionally, by creating synthetic phages with genomes that are smaller in size and contain fewer genes that code for hypothetical proteins, researchers have developed a yeast-based phage genome reconstruction technique that enables the large-scale dissemination of modified phages (Pires et al., 2021). Even though the aforementioned methods have achieved success, it's crucial to recognize that numerous approaches for phage genome modification have been devised.

Fehér et al. constructed a streamlined E. coli strain named MDS42 by deleting insertion sequences (IS elements) and redundant genes, resulting in a significantly reduced genome and an exceptionally low mutation rate (Fehér et al., 2012). On this basis, Yuan et al. introduced a genome reduction approach to iteratively remove non-essential regions from multiple tailed phages, achieving 8–23 % genome compaction while retaining infectivity, and in some cases enhancing it compared to parental strains (Yuan et al., 2022). This method provides a minimalist phage chassis, improving safety, increasing space for payload integration, and potentially enhancing therapeutic performance, which is a valuable complement to targeted genome editing. All of these techniques have its own set of drawbacks and isn't applicable across the board to all phages, as Łobocka et al. elaborate in their review (Łobocka et al., 2021).

Phage engineering is already being used sympathetically in people infected with drug-resistant bacteria. Dedrick et al. reported the first instance of human phage therapy using genetically modified (GE) phages (Dedrick et al., 2019). A combination of three bacteriophages (Muddy, BPs, and ZoeJ) was used to treat a 15-year-old patient with cystic fibrosis who had disseminated M. abscessus following a bilateral lung transplant that did not react to immunosuppressive medications and several intravenous antibiotics. Of the hundreds of Mycobacterium phages examined, Muddy was the only one that successfully eradicated the infected strain. BPs and ZoeJ were modified to enhance their lytic activity: BPs and its lysate product (BPsΔ33HTH) were poorly infectious to Mycobacterium abscessus GD01, but genetically engineered isolates (HRM10 and HRM01) acquired high lytic activity against M. abscessus. ZoeJ was engineered via Bacteriophage Recombineering of Electroporated DNA (BRED) to accurately excise its repressor gene, allowing it to efficiently infect and eliminate GD01. After at least 32 weeks of intravenous administration of a three-phage cocktail every 12 h, the patient's lung function improved significantly, and sternal wounds and skin lesions healed with local administration. Phage vaccine

During a viral respiratory infection, viruses can interact with bacteria, inducing pathogenic bacteria to migrate from the upper respiratory tract to the lungs, where they produce more toxins. These bacteria, shielded by the virus, evade immune cells and multiply in the lungs, leading to secondary bacterial pneumonia (Bakaletz, 2017). With the global COVID-19 pandemic and the rise in secondary pneumonia cases, developing an effective vaccine has become critical (Kim et al., 2021). Vaccines, as biological products, can effectively prevent pathogens from harming the human body, and increasingly, researchers are looking at phages for this purpose (Mohammad Hasani et al., 2023).

Phage vaccines mainly include phage DNA vaccines and phage display vaccines, with the latter having broader application prospects. The significance of phage display platforms was underscored by the 2018 Nobel Prize awarded “for the phage display of peptides and antibodies” (Jaroszewicz et al., 2022). This technology is already used in developing vaccines against various microbial pathogens. Phage display technology enables the high-level presentation of immunogenic peptides or proteins on the phage surface, effectively stimulating humoral and cellular immune responses (Bao et al., 2019). Among bacteriophages, filamentous (M13-like) and tail (T4-, T7-, and λ-based) structures are the most frequently utilized (Grabowski et al., 2023).

Using the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2 as a model, researchers developed a high-throughput universal vaccine platform combining CRISPR technology with phage T4 engineering. They integrated the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid protein (NP), epitopes, and spike trimers into the T4 capsid. The CRISPR project facilitated the construction of recombinant phages with various vaccine-targeting combinations. Tests in mouse models showed that the phage-delivered SARS-CoV-2 genes did not induce an immune response on their own. However, without adjuvants, T4 nanoparticles stimulated high antibody titers and strong immunogenicity and protective efficacy against the proteins and peptides delivered by the phage. This approach minimized the emergence of escape mutants and highly transmissible strains. In rabbit models, the vaccine also improved disease outcomes and survival (Zhu et al., 2021). Zhu et al. used the phage T4-CoV-2 vaccine for intranasal inoculation and injection in mice. Both methods induced strong mucosal, humoral, and cellular immunity, stimulating serum Th1 and Th2 responses. Surprisingly, the intranasal vaccine elicited a much stronger response than the injected one, possibly due to mucosal sIgA stimulation unique to intranasal inoculation. These findings suggest that the phage T4-CoV-2 vaccine can effectively eliminate the virus and may prevent SARS-CoV-2 transmission. Notably, no adverse effects were observed in animal models (Zhu et al., 2022). Lin et al. (2022) discovered that phages contain K1-ORF34 and K2-ORF16 capsule polysaccharide (CPS) depolymerases that successfully preserve immunogenicity by cleaving K1 and K2-CPS into whole structural units of oligosaccharides with intact modifications. To create CPS-conjugate vaccines, the resultant K1 and K2 oligosaccharides were subsequently coupled to the carrier protein CRM197. The CPS combination vaccines increased the bactericidal activity of anti-CPS antibodies for K1 and K2 by 128 and 64 times, respectively, in comparison to mice that were not vaccinated, according to mouse vaccination trials. The study of attack showed that animals were protected from Klebsiella pneumoniae infection by immunization with K1 or K2 CPS and bivalent vaccine (the combination of K1 and K2- CPS vaccine), which proved its clinical potential in preventing bacterial infection and contributed to the development of vaccines.

Currently, there are few clinical studies on the application of phage vaccines for pulmonary infections. However, developing phages as vaccines is a promising field. Future efforts should focus on understanding the interaction between phages and host immunity, which will be crucial for developing highly effective phage-based vaccines.

Phage administration for pulmonary infection

Phage therapy has largely been empirical, with various dosing regimens, including single phage or phage cocktails, different administration routes, dosages, frequencies, durations, and concurrent or sequential antibiotic use (Mitropoulou et al., 2022). There is limited clinical data and experience regarding the optimal mode of phage administration for pulmonary infections. Phages can be administered orally, by injection, or directly (topically) to infected sites. Injections deliver phages effectively and quickly into the bloodstream, bypassing significant body defenses. Intraperitoneal injections work faster than intravenous or subcutaneous injections, achieving higher titers and effectively protecting animals from fatal septicemia. However, most case reports indicate that phage preparations have been administered intravenously (Dedrick et al., 2019; Mitropoulou et al., 2022; Tamma et al., 2022). In contrast, oral and topical administration can reduce the number of active phages introduced into the body, only about 20 % effective, with 33 % of cases showing no penetration (Dąbrowska and Abedon, 2019). Topical delivery of phages to the lungs via inhalation has been successfully shown to control respiratory infections. Local delivery typically targets the skin, mucous membranes, or wounds, often focusing on biofilms (Dąbrowska and Abedon, 2019). In animal models of pulmonary infection, intranasal administration is generally preferred, ensuring high concentrations of phages reach lung bacteria for faster therapeutic effect. Inhaled phages have also been used in certain clinical trials. However, the intranasal/inhalation route presents certain theoretical challenges. One study suggested that high mucin and DNA levels increase the viscosity of cystic fibrosis dysfunctional mucus, thereby limiting the diffusion of antibiotics. Phage delivery may also be affected under these conditions (Cocorullo et al., 2024).

Current challenges and prospects

In phage therapy, choosing the right phage is crucial and needs to take into account specificity, effectiveness, and preventing negative immune reactions (Principi et al., 2019). Given that several strains of bacteria may show differing degrees of phage susceptibility, another major issue with phage therapy is phage resistance. As a result, altering the phage formulations' host range can have a big impact on treatment outcomes (Dunne et al., 2021). Determining the therapeutic efficacy of phages requires an understanding of their pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) (Palma and Qi, 2024). Studying the body's phages' absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion is known as PK in phage therapy (MK Kim et al., 2025). Furthermore, the therapeutic results of phage treatment may potentially be impacted by different immunological states (Tang et al., 2024). Since almost every phage-bacterium-patient combination may exhibit distinct PK/PD traits, creating standardized models appropriate for various clinical situations.

Only in cases of acute multidrug-resistant or chronically resistant illnesses is phage therapy used to treat pulmonary infections after antibiotic treatment has failed. The use of phage therapy as a first-line treatment is still up for debate because there is a dearth of standardized clinical trial evidence. Conclusions

Phage therapy provides new approaches for managing drug-resistant pulmonary infections through four main strategies: synergy with antibiotics, enzyme derivatives, engineered modifications, and vaccine development. In recent years, there has been a significant upsurge in interest regarding phages as an alternative or supplementary option to antibiotics. Up until this point, the majority of data related to the treatment of pulmonary infections using phages has stemmed from animal models and case reports. Numerous fundamental queries about the utilization of phages in human patients still remain unresolved. In the future, we need to build a large-scale global phage library to improve the efficiency of matching and treatment. Further preclinical studies are essential to enhance our understanding of how phages affect antibiotic resistance, coordinate with antibiotics, and interact with the immune system. Additionally, we must ensure the safety and stability of phage therapy by improving the preparation, approval, clinical application, and supervision processes. Despite the many challenges, phage therapy holds great potential for treating pulmonary infections.
