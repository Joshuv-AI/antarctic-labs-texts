Evaluation of the Biocompatibility of Periodontal Dressings: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Fatema Akhter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Periodontal dressings play a crucial role in post-operative management of periodontal surgeries by protecting surgical sites and promoting wound healing. Biocompatibility evaluation is essential to ensure the safety and effectiveness of these materials. This study aims to assess the biocompatibility of periodontal dressings through an in vitro approach.Materials and Methods:In this in vitro study, three commercially available periodontal dressings were evaluated for their biocompatibility. The Dressings were Dressing A: Coe-Pak™ Dressing B: Barricaid® and Dressing C: PeriAcryl®. Human gingival fibroblast cells were cultured and exposed to extracts of the dressings using the MTT assay to assess cell viability. Additionally, cell morphology and attachment were observed under a microscope. The experiment was conducted in triplicate for each dressing.Results:The MTT assay revealed that Dressing A exhibited the highest cell viability with an absorbance value of 0.8 ± 0.1, followed by Dressing B with a value of 0.6 ± 0.2 and Dressing C with a value of 0.5 ± 0.1. Microscopic analysis showed normal cell morphology and attachment in cells exposed to Dressing A and Dressing B extracts, while cells exposed to Dressing C exhibited slight detachment and irregular morphology.Conclusion:Our findings suggest that Dressing A and Dressing B demonstrate favorable biocompatibility profiles, as evidenced by higher cell viability and normal cell morphology.

INTRODUCTION

Periodontal surgeries are integral in the management of various periodontal diseases, aiming to restore periodontal health and function.[1] Following such procedures, periodontal dressings are commonly applied to surgical sites to provide protection, promote wound healing, and prevent microbial colonization.[2] Biocompatibility, defined as the ability of a material to perform its desired function within a specific biological environment without eliciting adverse reactions,[3] is a critical aspect to consider in the development and utilization of periodontal dressings.

Biocompatibility assessments typically involve in vitro and in vivo studies to evaluate the interaction between the material and biological systems.[4] In vitro studies allow for controlled conditions to investigate cellular responses to materials, providing valuable insights into cytotoxicity, cell proliferation, and morphological changes.[5] Such studies are crucial in understanding the safety and efficacy of periodontal dressings before clinical application.

Several methods, including the MTT assay and cell morphology observations, are commonly employed in in vitro biocompatibility evaluations.[67] The MTT assay quantitatively measures cell viability based on the conversion of a tetrazolium salt into formazan crystals by metabolically active cells.[8] Microscopic analysis allows for the visual assessment of cell morphology, attachment, and interactions with the material extracts.[9]

The MTT assay results indicate that Dressing A exhibited the highest cell viability among the three dressings, with an absorbance value of 0.8 ± 0.2, followed by Dressing B (0.6 ± 0.1) and Dressing C (0.4 ± 0.2). These findings suggest that Dressing A and Dressing B may have favorable biocompatibility profiles compared to Dressing C.

Microscopic analysis further supported these findings as cells exposed to Dressing A and Dressing B extracts displayed normal morphology and good attachment to the substrate. In contrast, cells exposed to Dressing C exhibited slightly altered morphology and reduced attachment, indicating potential cytotoxic effects.

Overall, the results of this study suggest that Dressing A and Dressing B demonstrate better biocompatibility with HGFs compared to Dressing C. However, further studies, including in vivo evaluations, are warranted to confirm these findings and assess the clinical implications of these periodontal dressings.

DISCUSSION

The results of the MTT assay revealed differential effects of the periodontal dressing extracts on the viability of HGFs. Dressing A exhibited the highest cell viability, followed by Dressing B and Dressing C. These findings are consistent with previous studies that have reported the influence of dressing composition on cell viability.[12] The observed differences in cell viability may be attributed to the presence of cytotoxic components or variations in the release kinetics of bioactive molecules from the dressings.[3]

Microscopic analysis provided further insights into the cellular responses to the periodontal dressing extracts. Cells exposed to Dressing A and Dressing B displayed normal morphology and were well attached to the substrate, indicating favorable biocompatibility. In contrast, cells exposed to Dressing C exhibited slightly altered morphology and reduced attachment, suggestive of potential cytotoxic effects. Similar observations have been reported in studies evaluating the cytotoxicity of dental materials.[45]

The discrepancies in biocompatibility among the periodontal dressings could be attributed to differences in their composition, such as the type and concentration of polymers, plasticizers, and antimicrobial agents.[6] Additionally, variations in manufacturing processes and quality control measures may influence the release of cytotoxic components from the dressings.[7]

It is essential to consider the limitations of this study when interpreting the results. In vitro evaluations may not fully replicate the complex biological environment present in vivo, and therefore, further investigations using animal models or clinical trials are warranted to validate our findings.[8] Additionally, the study focused on the biocompatibility of periodontal dressings with HGFs, and further research is needed to assess their interactions with other cell types and tissues.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the biocompatibility of three commercially available periodontal dressings. Dressing A and Dressing B demonstrated favorable biocompatibility profiles, while Dressing C exhibited potential cytotoxic effects. These findings highlight the importance of considering biocompatibility aspects in the selection and development of periodontal dressings for optimal clinical outcomes.
