Dominating Role of 2‑Mercaptobenzothiazole as a Capping Ligand in Silver Nanocluster Synthesis
Authors: Bedanta Borah, Avishek Sarkar, Sukesh Shill, Abdul Wahab, Apurba Kr. Barman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This work highlights the dominating role of the capping ligand 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (2-MBT) in the synthesis of a silver nanocluster (AgNC). AgNC was synthesized through ligand exchange (LE) in a two-phase reaction system by reacting a preformed water-soluble glutathione (GSH)-stabilized silver nanocluster (Ag-SGNC) with 2-MBT dissolved in dichloromethane. We observed the formation of a fluorescent AgNC in the organic phase from the nonfluorescent Ag-SGNC. The composition of the nanocluster was determined with the help of UV–vis spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, powder X-ray diffraction, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy, MALDI mass spectrometry, and thermogravimetric analysis. We recently synthesized Ag16(2-MBT)12 clusters by the direct reduction (DR) of AgNO3 using sodium borohydride in the presence of 2-MBT in a one-phase reaction. Interestingly, the composition of the nanocluster synthesized through the LE reaction was determined to be exactly the same as that of Ag16(2-MBT)12, which was synthesized by the DR method. To our knowledge, there is no such report on the synthesis of luminescent AgNCs where the same nanocluster is obtained from two drastically different synthetic methods. In general, syntheses of metal nanoclusters are highly susceptible to changes in reaction parameters and often result in nanoclusters of variable compositions. Moreover, we justified the LE reaction and tried to find a possible mechanism through time-dependent studies. We proposed that Ag-SGNCs were etched into smaller fragments on reacting with 2-MBT. Some of these fragments where glutathione molecules were replaced by 2-MBT transferred into the organic phase from the aqueous phase. These fragments then possibly combined together to yield a thermodynamically stable Ag16(2-MBT)12 cluster.

Introduction

Metal nanoclusters with atomic precision, especially of silver and gold, continue to be a thriving research field in nanochemistry during recent decades. These nanoclusters (NCs) occupy the gap between discrete atoms and plasmonic nanomaterials and thus have unique characteristics in comparison to the conventional molecules and nanoparticles. − Among the many useful properties, one is photoluminescence (PL) with high photostability. Further, the low toxicity and superior biocompatibility of these nanoclusters make them excellent candidates for exploring chemical/bio-sensing, bioimaging, cell labeling, phototherapy, and drug delivery. , However, the synthetic processes are difficult to control and repeat, and this often leads to unpredictable outcomes. Thus, there are only a handful of well-characterized luminescent AgNCs. , These hinder the applicability of AgNCs despite their immense probability. Fundamental questions such as what governs the final outcome of the nanocluster composition/structure during a synthesis are still quite unclear. Does it depend on the reaction conditions (solvent, temperature, pH, solvent, etc.), types of reducing agents, starting materials, or any other factor? − For a viable production of nanoclusters on a large scale that is necessary for realistic application in future, these simple but fundamental questions need to be addressed. There are studies that indicate that quite often; on varying the reaction parameters and keeping the stabilizing ligand constant, different types of AgNCs are formed. For example, the synthesis of several Agn(SG)m clusters, namely, Ag11(SG)7, Ag16(SG)9, Ag32(SG)19, Ag75(SG)40, etc., using the same precursors has been reported, i.e., using Ag­(I) salts and glutathione (GSH) as the ligand. − Here, the final cluster formed is controlled by the reaction conditions. In such cases, it is very difficult to obtain the same cluster on repeating a synthetic procedure. Replacement of the capping ligand post synthetically, i.e., the exchange of an already bound ligand by a new incoming ligand, has also been explored in some cases for AgNCs. Most of these ligand exchanges (LEs) with AgNCs resulted in an alteration of the composition and structure of the nanoclusters. Exceptionally, upon employing LE reactions with 4-fluorothiophenol (most probably the only such ligand reported yet), the same nonfluorescent Ag44(SPhF)30 cluster formed even when different starting clusters Ag25(SPhMe2)18, Ag59(SPhCl2)32, and Ag35(SG)18 were used as precursors. − This is quite interesting in the sense that the ligand plays a dominating role in the formation of the nanoclusters. Such ligands may be very helpful for repeated synthesis of the same nanoclusters. Further, on varying the electronic properties of such ligands, we may be able to tune the properties of the AgNCs in a predictable way, which is still lacking in this area. In our recent work, we reported the synthesis and characterization of a luminescent Ag16(2-MBT)12 nanocluster. In this case, we synthesized it by directly reducing AgNO3 by sodium borohydride in the presence of 2-mercaptobenzothiazole (2-MBT). Also, we and Jin et al. explored how a single atom variation in the capping ligand drastically changes the luminescent properties of the AgNCs. , Our work is focused on developing good repeatable synthetic methods and exploring the fundamentals of the luminescence properties for AgNCs. We wanted to understand the rationale behind the formation of Ag16(2-MBT)12 by exploring different types of synthetic strategies. Herein, we report the exploration of a two-phase synthesis using 2-MBT. We found that the ligand played a very striking role, which resulted in the formation of the same Ag16(2-MBT)12 irrespective of the method of synthesis. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first such report in the context of fluorescent AgNCs, where two contrasting synthetic methods, direct reduction (DR) and LE, result in the formation of the same AgNC. We strongly believe that this study will encourage researchers working on AgNCs to further explore the role of the ligands, which may enable the tuning of electronic properties and the generation of novel fluorescent nanoclusters.

Results and Discussion

Water-soluble silver-glutathione nanoclusters (Ag-SGNCs) were synthesized using a previously reported method. This Ag-SGNC was obtained as a dark-brown-colored solid. The LE reaction was carried out by stirring an aqueous solution of Ag-SGNC with 2-MBT in dichloromethane (DCM) (Scheme ). The DCM layer was separated after 8 h of reaction and then evaporated. The light-yellow nanocluster obtained (Ag-MBTNC-LE) was washed several times with ethanol and methanol to remove any unreactive 2-MBT. In our previous report, the DR method was employed where the ligand 2-MBT and AgNO3 were mixed and reduced directly by NaBH4 (Scheme ). The cluster obtained by the DR method was characterized as Ag16(2-MBT)12.

Synthetic Schemes of AgNCs in One-Phase and Two-Phase Syntheses

We performed UV–vis spectroscopy, fluorescence spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, MALDI mass spectrometry, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) to determine the structure and possible mechanism of formation of Ag-MBTNC-LE.

The UV–vis spectra of Ag-SGNC show three peaks. One very strong peak at 210 nm corresponds to the glutathione ligand. The others at 305 nm and a broad hump at ∼490 nm are characteristic of Ag-SGNC (previously referred to as band 6). Moreover, the continuum of absorption in the visible region is also a characteristic of AgNCs (Figure a). − , The characteristics of the UV–vis spectrum of Ag-MBTNC-LE are as follows: (a) peaks at 235 and 325 nm of 2-MBT origin; (b) a continuum of absorption; (c) no peak of glutathione origin; and (d) no surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak; these characteristics indicate the formation of a glutathione-less nanocluster stabilized by 2-MBT only (Figure b). ,

UV–visible spectra of (a) l-glutathione reduced and Ag-SGNC and (b) AgMBTNC-LE. (c) Fluorescence emission spectra of Ag16(MBT)12 synthesized using the DR method and AgMBTNC-LE synthesized using the LE method when excited at 420 nm. Images of (d) Ag16(MBT)12 and (e) AgMBTNC-LE.

The red-emitting Ag-MBTNC-LE has an emission maximum at 660 nm, which is identical to that of Ag16(2-MBT)12 obtained by the DR method (Figure c–e). The quantum yield (QY) and the excited state lifetime were found to be 15.69% and 6.64 μs, respectively (Figures S2 and S3, Supporting Information). This longer fluorescence lifetime is usual in the case of AgNCs. ,

The bulk nature of Ag-MBTNC-LE was examined using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). The absence of the characteristic sharp PXRD peaks for Ag nanoparticles at 38.19°, 44.37°, 64.65°, and 77.47° corresponding to the planes with Miller indices of (111), (200), (220), and (311), respectively, indicated them to be nanoclusters rather than nanoparticles (Figure S4, Supporting Information). ,

The FTIR spectra of Ag-SGNC and Ag-MBTNC-LE indicate the binding of Ag with l-glutathione reduced and 2-MBT through the S atom, which is evident from the disappearance of the -SH stretching frequency of the ligands around 2500 cm–1 (Figure S5, Supporting Information). ,

XPS was performed to determine the elemental compositions and charge states. XPS spectra of Ag-MBTNC-LE obtained from the organic phase after 8 h of reaction showed peaks corresponding to C, N, S, O, and Ag (Figure a). The Ag 3d5/2 and Ag 3d3/2 peak positions at 368.0 and 374.0, and 1.53 eV fwhm of the Ag 3d5/2 peak, indicated the formation of nanoclusters with the presence of Ag in both 0 and +1 oxidation states (Figure b). −

(a) Full-scan XPS survey spectrum of AgMBTNC-LE. High-resolution XPS spectra of (b) Ag 3d, (c) S 2p, (d) N 1s, (e) O 1s, and (f) C 1s.

The S 2p spectrum could be deconvoluted into three peaks at 162.2, 163.5, and 164.6 eV, respectively. There are two types of S atoms in 2-MBT: one exocyclic and one endocyclic. The peak at 162.2 eV corresponds to the exocyclic S 2p3/2 peak, while the peak at 164.6 eV is that of endocyclic S 2p1/2. The intense middle peak at 163.5 eV is a result of the combination of exocyclic S 2p1/2 and endocyclic S 2p3/2 peaks (Figure c). The exocyclic peak position shifts toward a higher BE compared to that of free 2-MBT, which indicates the strong binding of exocyclic S with Ag. The endocyclic peak also moved slightly to a higher BE, indicating that the S atom of the thiazolyl aromatic ring may also have some kind of interaction with Ag. , The major peak position for N 1s at 398.5 eV indicated that the N atom of the 2-MBT ligand bonded with Ag in Ag-MBTNC-LE (Figure d). In the case of nonbonded pyrrole type N, as in free 2-MBT, the BE is observed at about 400 eV. , The deconvoluted 1s spectra showed a single peak at 532.0 eV (Figure e). Oxide formation is ruled out since the O 1s peak for silver oxide was earlier reported in the 528.0–530.0 eV region. Thus, this oxygen peak most probably arises from trapped solvent molecules such as water or alcohol, which is common in nanoclusters. ,,

The deconvoluted C 1s spectrum showed two peaks at 284.3 and 285.7 eV (Figure f). These values fall in the BE ranges of C–C, C–S, C–N, and C–O bonds. The C 1s XPS peak of glutathione has components at about 287–289 eV, which arise due to the carbonyl (peptidic) and carboxylic groups. , The absence of these peaks indicates that Ag-MBTNC-LE does not contain any glutathione molecule (Figure f). This confirms the LE reaction, wherein all glutathione ligands are replaced by 2-MBT during the formation of the nanocluster. Also, the atomic percentage ratios of C/S and N/S obtained from XPS perfectly match with that of 2-MBT only (Figures S6 and Table S1).

(a) TGA curve of AgMBTNC-LE. (b) Positive-ion-mode MALDI-TOF mass spectra of AgMBTNC-LE. Inset: Zoomed-in experimental (blue) and simulated (red) isotopic distributions.

The UV–vis spectrum, fluorescence emission, QY, lifetime, and XPS peaks of Ag-MBTNC-LE are almost identical to those for our earlier reported Ag16(2-MBT)12 cluster. The same identity was further confirmed by TGA and mass spectrometry.

TGA was carried out to determine the amount of metal, organic matter, and solvent molecules present. The TGA graph of Ag-MBTNC-LE showed a gradual loss of about 3.6% of mass up to 200 °C, which is due to the loss of solvent molecules. , A sharp loss of 52.2% of mass up to 500 °C was seen. This can be assigned to the loss of organic molecules. Thereafter, no loss of mass was seen up to 800 °C. Thus, the remaining 44.2% can be assigned as the mass percentage of Ag in Ag-MBTNC-LE (Figure a).

The MALDI-TOF mass spectra were recorded to analyze the mass and composition of Ag-MBTNC-LE. Mostly, only one strong peak was observed at 3863.32 Da (Figure b). 44.2% mass residue observed in TGA equals 16 Ag atoms (the theoretical value is 44.7%; Table S2, Supporting Information). 52.2% mass is due to the loss of organic ligands, which can be assigned to 12 2-MBT molecules (the theoretical value is 51.6%). 3.6% mass for solvents can be allocated to one ethanol and three methanol molecules. Ethanol and methanol were used as solvents during the purification and recording of spectra. Overall, the mass spectrum peak at 3863.32 Da for Ag-MBTNC-LE can be assigned to [M + H]+, where M = [Ag16(2-MBT-H)12 + C2H5OH + 3CH3OH], which matches well with the theoretical value of 3863.35 Da. The isotopic distribution also corroborates this assignment.

Thus, we observed the formation of the same Ag16 nanocluster irrespective of the method of preparation when 2-MBT was used as the capping ligand. This is very interesting since in most cases of AgNC syntheses, varying reaction conditions result in the formation of different nanoclusters. Even repetitions of the same procedure become difficult often. Thus, certain ligands such as 2-MBT may play a very dominating and the most crucial role in the outcome of the AgNC synthesis. We believe to the best of our knowledge that this observation is reported here for the first time in the case of a fluorescent AgNC.

We conducted a time-dependent study to reveal the probable pathway leading to the Ag16 nanocluster and to determine that this cluster was indeed formed through the LE reaction. The UV–vis spectra (Figure ) and fluorescence spectra (Figure ) were recorded at various time intervals for both the aqueous and organic layers. Initially, both Ag-SGNC (aqueous layer) and 2-MBT (organic layer) were nonfluorescent. Gradually, fluorescence started to appear in the organic layer approximately after 1.5 h, increased with time, and reached its maximum intensity at about 8 h, indicating the completion of the reaction (Figures and ). For determining the precise time of evolution of fluorescence, we recorded the spectra at 15 min intervals. Initially, at 15 min, there was a very-low-intensity emission peak at 383 nm, which shifted to 410 nm at 30 min upon excitation at 350 nm. This peak disappeared with the evolution of multiple peaks at 376, 398, and 422 nm up to 1.5 h. After this, the strong emission peak at 660 nm appeared along with the weak peaks, and the intensity increased with time (Figure S6, Supporting Information). This showed a dynamic transformation of clusters during the LE reaction. The UV–vis spectra of the aqueous phase showed clear transformation of Ag-SGNC. The characteristic peaks at 305 and 490 nm disappeared completely after about 3 h, while other features appeared at about 454 nm, which may be due to a change in the composition of the Ag-SGNC (Figure a). The UV–vis spectra of the organic phase also showed a gradual and clear transformation (Figure b).

We also recorded the MALDI mass spectra of the intermediates isolated from both the aqueous and organic layers after 1, 2, and 3 h of reaction (Figures and ). Peaks corresponding to small clusters such as Ag2(SG)1S, Ag4(SG)1(2-MBT)­1, Ag4(2-MBT)1S, and Ag5(2-MBT)2S2 could be identified from the mass spectra of intermediates isolated at 1 h from the aqueous layer (Figure a,d). Similarly, peaks for Ag3(SG)1S, Ag3(SG)2, Ag5(SG)2S, Ag3(SG)2(2-MBT)1, Ag4(SG)1(2-MBT)1, Ag4(SG)2(2-MBT)2, Ag5(SG)2(2-MBT)1S, Ag3(2-MBT)2, and other clusters were observed for the intermediates isolated from the aqueous layer at 2 h (Figure b,e). At the end of 3 h, the peaks observed were for Ag4(2-MBT)1S2, Ag5(2-MBT)2, Ag6(2-MBT)2S, and Ag7(SG)1(2-MBT)1S (Figure c,f). The details of these peaks and isotopic distributions are given in the Supporting Information (Tables S3–S5 and Figures S7–S9). A few of these peaks are separated by a mass unit of 248, which corresponds to the Ag2S fragment that is often seen in nanoclusters obtained by the cleavage of the C–S bond of the ligand. In all of these spectra, cluster sizes are mostly in the range of Ag3 to Ag7. Some of these clusters are capped by either glutathione or 2-MBT alone, while others are capped by both glutathione and 2-MBT. These observations indicate (i) the degradation of the parent Ag-SGNC to smaller fragments and (ii) the displacement of glutathione by 2-MBT.

The mass spectrum of the intermediate isolated at 1 h (Figure a,d) from the organic layer showed peaks corresponding to Ag2(SG)1S, Ag4(2-MBT)1S2, Ag5(2-MBT)2S2, Ag5(2-MBT)3, Ag6(2-MBT)3S2, Ag8(2-MBT)3S3, and Ag11(2-MBT)4S4. The peaks are separated by Ag2S or AgL (L = 2-MBT) fragments, which are common fragmentations in AgNCs. However, one noticeable observation is the presence of Ag2 and Ag4 peaks corresponding to m/z values of 554 and 662 in both aqueous and organic layer intermediates at the same time, which indicates the transfer of these smaller clusters from the aqueous to the organic layer. In the case of the 2 h intermediate, similar peaks were observed for the lower mass region, comprising Ag5, Ag6, Ag8, and Ag9 clusters, but, interestingly, a peak corresponding to Ag15(2-MBT)13 (m/z = 3446) was observed in the higher end (Figure b,d). For the intermediate isolated at 3 h, peaks for smaller clusters similar to those of 1 and 2 h intermediates were observed, together with peaks for Ag16 (m/z = 3586), Ag18 (m/z = 3833), and Ag20 clusters (m/z = 4082) stabilized by 2-MBT. The details of these peaks and isotopic distributions are shown in the Supporting Information (Tables S6–S8 and Figures S10–S12). Thus, a gradual evolution of larger clusters with an increase in the reaction time shows the formation of these clusters starting from smaller ones. Notably, most of these fragments do not have glutathione, which is understandable as GSH is highly polar in nature. This can also be confirmed from the XPS spectra of the intermediates at 2 h, which showed the absence of the C 1s peak arising from carbonyl groups (Figure S13, Supporting Information). We also recorded the TGA for the 2 h intermediate (Figure S14, Supporting Information). The mass percentages of Ag and organic ligands for the intermediate are 48.8% and 47.6%, respectively, which indicates a Ag/ligand ratio of greater than 1. This also corroborates with the assignments of the mass spectrum peaks of the corresponding intermediate.

Thus, it was envisaged that during the LE reaction, Ag-SGNC in the aqueous layer breaks down into smaller unstable fragments due to the displacement of glutathione by 2-MBT. Some of these fragments were transferred to the DCM layer and then combined to form larger and thermodynamically stable clusters, ultimately leading to Ag16(2-MBT)12 (Figure ). The presence of common small clusters in the 1, 2, and 3 h intermediates and the observation of the Ag16 cluster in the 3 h intermediate are highly supportive of this mechanism. This probable mechanism fits the earlier reported “destruction–reorganization” pathway. This type of time-dependent growth from smaller to larger metal nanoclusters has been previously demonstrated.

Conclusions

In conclusion, all of the characterization and optical properties of the AgNCs synthesized using LE (Ag-MBTNC-LE) match with those of our previously reported Ag16(2-MBT)12 cluster, synthesized using the DR method. Thus, 2-MBT plays such a commendable role that the Ag16 cluster only forms upon using it as a capping ligand, irrespective of the precursors/method of synthesis. We propose a possible pathway for the LE transformation with the help of mass spectrometry and XPS. The robustness of the combination of 2-MBT and Ag indicates that cleverly substituted 2-MBT molecules may also lead to AgNCs with similar structures but with different optical properties. This may lead to a deeper understanding of the role of ligands in AgNC synthesis and also to the origin and fundamentals of AgNC fluorescence, which are yet to be understood. We are exploring such modifications and hope to publish our findings in the near future.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Silver nitrate (AgNO3, Loba Chemie, 99.8%), l-glutathione reduced (GSH, HiMedia, ≥98%), 2-MBT (Loba Chemie, 99.8%), sodium borohydride (NaBH4, Loba Chemie, 99.8%), deionized water, and other organic solvents (DCM, ethanol, and methanol) (Merck Milipore, reagent grade) were purchased and used without further purification.

Synthesis

Synthesis of the Ag-SGNC Nanocluster

A mixture of AgNO3 (50 mg, 0.294 mmol) and GSH (134 mg, 0.436 mmol) was dissolved in NaOH solution (83 mg of NaOH in 8 mL of water) at room temperature, and then, the solution was vigorously stirred (∼1000 rpm) with a magnetic stir bar for 10 minutes. A colorless solution was obtained, indicating the formation of Ag­(I)-SG complexes. After that, 8 mL of an ice-cold aqueous solution of NaBH4 (33 mg) was added to the solution of complexes. The solution gradually changed from colorless to orange and then to reddish black over a period of ∼5 min, indicating the formation of the Ag-SG nanocluster. The reaction is allowed to proceed for 2 h and left for drying (yield, 110 mg).

Synthesis of AgMBTNC-LE

Two equivalents of 2-MBT (98 mg, 0.588 mmol) in 8 mL of DCM was added to the purified aqueous solution of Ag-SGNC. After some time, silver cluster core etching and secondary growth occurred in the phase-transfer process. The reaction was allowed to proceed for 8 h at room temperature with vigorous stirring. After that, the organic phase was separated by a separating funnel and dried using an evaporator. The yellowish nanocluster AgMBTNC-LE was washed several times with ethanol and methanol to remove unreacted ligands (yield, 54 mg).

During the reaction, the two-phase intermediates were monitored by UV–vis and fluorescence spectroscopies, mass spectrometry, XPS, and TGA. The intermediates were isolated by the separation of the two phases, centrifuged, and then washed with the desired solvent (water/ethanol/methanol) to remove unreacted organic ligands.

Characterization

UV–visible spectra were measured with a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer in the range of 200–1400 cm–1. The emission spectra were recorded using a Horiba FluoroMax-4 instrument. FTIR spectra were recorded in a Bruker APEXII spectrophotometer in the frequency range of 4000–400 cm–1. PXRD measurements were performed on a Rigaku Ultima IV powder X-ray diffractometer. XPS spectra were obtained by an ESCALAB Xi+ (Thermo Fisher Scientific Pvt. Ltd., UK). The mass spectra were obtained by using a MALDI-TOF (Bruker, Model: Autoflex speed) mass spectrometer in a positive mode using sinapic acid as a matrix and ethanol as a solvent. TG measurements were conducted on a Mettler Toledo instrument in the temperature range of 25–1000 °C and a heating rate of 20 °C min–1. Additional details of characterizations can be found in the Supporting Information.
