‘I Didn't Know That in the Background They Write Reports About Me’: Understanding the Health Literacy Implications of Allied Health Reports through the Experiences of People With Lifelong Communication Disability
Authors: Harmony Turnbull, Leigha Dark, Phillippa Carnemolla, Ian Skinner, Bronwyn Hemsley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPeople with lifelong communication disability receive services, including written reports, from allied health professionals. Allied health reports can contribute to health literacy barriers or facilitators in relation to people with communication disability accessing, understanding, appraising, and using the information in the reports. Research into allied health reports has not adequately included the experiences of people with lifelong communication disability and this group are also underrepresented in the health literacy research. The aim of this study was to understand the experiences and views of people with lifelong communication disability about accessing, understanding, appraising, and using information in their allied heath reports. This was done to inform clinical implications for report writers and reduce health literacy barriers for people with lifelong communication disability.MethodsThis qualitative study applied constructivist and pragmatist methodology underpinned by a well‐established health literacy theoretical model. Australian adults with lifelong communication disability were recruited through purposive and snowball sampling methods. Participants able to give their own informed consent and who had received services from at least one allied health professional were eligible to participate. In‐depth interviews were analysed using reflexive thematic analysis to derive main themes and component subthemes.ResultsTwo main themes were identified in the data: ‘This is personal’ and ‘These are our documents’, each with three subthemes. Although all participants wanted to receive, read, and understand their allied health reports, only six of the nine had received and read their allied health reports. For allied health reports to fulfil the intended purposes, participants wanted allied health professionals to build trusting relationships and use feedback to write reports that accurately represent them and their support needs.ConclusionsPeople with lifelong communication disability have a right to access their health information in formats that they can understand and use to achieve their goals. However, they may not be informed that reports are being written about them or given their reports. Reading allied health reports is a highly personal and emotional experience for people with lifelong communication disability who emphasise the importance of building and maintaining trusting relationships with allied health professionals. To reduce health literacy barriers, allied health professionals should provide people with lifelong communication disability access to the information in their own reports in ways that can be understood and used.WHAT THIS PAPER ADDS What is already known on the subject Health literacy involves the ability to access, understand, appraise, and apply health information in the context of individual and environmental barriers and facilitators. People with lifelong communication disability have not adequately been included in health literacy research to date and are underrepresented in research into allied health reports. What this paper adds to existing knowledge People with lifelong communication disability do not always receive a copy of their allied health reports. When they do, reading these reports can be a personal and emotional experience. People with lifelong communication disability want to access and understand their reports. People with lifelong communication disability also want trusting relationships with allied health professionals where they can give feedback on reports for more accurate representations of themselves. What are the potential or actual clinical implications of this work? Allied health professionals should support the health literacy of people with lifelong communication disability. They need to build and maintain trusting relationships with their clients, provide access to reports, and listen to feedback on the reports so that people with lifelong communication disability can understand and use their own report to achieve their goals.

Introduction

Allied health professionals provide services that include written reports which can be an environmental barrier or facilitator for the health literacy of people with lifelong communication disability. A recent review of the literature into allied health reports has shown that prior research has related to a limited number of contexts (e.g., reports within school or hospital settings) and a limited number of allied health professions (e.g., psychology, occupational therapy, speech pathology); and has not yet adequately considered the views or needs of people with lifelong communication disability (Turnbull et al. 2020). Health literacy is a multidimensional concept that involves accessing, understanding, appraising, and applying health information moderated by individual factors, and situational or environmental facilitators and barriers (Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care 2014; Sørensen et al. 2012). In allied health contexts, relational practices (e.g., building trust, engaging in dialogue, and inviting feedback) are central to enabling health literacy for people with lifelong communication disability (Spencer et al. 2025). People with lifelong communication disability can encounter various barriers to health literacy (Turnbull et al. 2023). Individual factors forming barriers and facilitators to heath literacy include communication abilities, and attention or memory skills; while situational and environmental factors forming barriers or facilitators can include internet access, health systems design, and health professional interactions. For people with lifelong communication disability, health literacy reflects an important interaction of personal skills, the quality of relationships with allied health professionals, and systemic factors including funding models and organisational practices.

Health literacy barriers for people with lifelong communication disability can also arise due to the model of health and disability informing the design and delivery of health and allied health services (Areheart 2008). Health and disability have been conceptualised with various models and disability definitions (World Health Organisation and The World Bank 2011). A medical model of disability (Engel 1977) conceptualises disability as a health condition with an emphasis on treatments to improve function. Although the medical model leads important advances in healthcare, the role of social and environmental factors may be overlooked. In contrast, the social model of disability (Hogan 2019) emphasises factors in environments as having a disabling impact on individuals with a focus on removing barriers to inclusion. A bio‐psycho‐social model, described within the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (World Health Organization (WHO) 2001) conceptualises disability as arising from the interaction of many factors in the individual, their activities, and their environments. Aligning allied health services with the medical model of disability, more than the social or biopsychosocial models, is likely to focus more on the individual's impairments and deficits as targets for interventions, rather than on strengths or environmental supports to improve access and participation (World Health Organisation and The World Bank 2011). Consideration of both individual and environmental factors is necessary for more accessible allied health services, including written reports for people with lifelong communication disability (Blake and McLeod 2018).

Difficulty communicating by understanding and using language, including written language, may also form a health literacy barrier. A person's lifelong communication disability relates to speech, language, or cognitive communication impairments which interact with environmental barriers to impact on interactions and activities (Communication Disability Centre 2015). However, written information can be presented in ways that are easier to understand. Strategies to improve the information accessibility of written information and support understanding are described in the literature. These strategies include Easy Read or aphasia friendly materials, narrated interactive videos and photo stories, and decision aids. Easy Read information utilises features such as large font, simplified sentences, and images (Chinn and Homeyard 2017; Hurtado et al. 2014). However, Easy Read materials alone may not meet the communication support needs of people with lifelong communication disability (Waight and Oldreive 2021) and be better understood when combined with verbal description (Buell et al. 2019). Aphasia friendly materials designed for people who have acquired communication disability following a stroke (Rose et al. 2011) have been shown to help people with aphasia to understand written information (Wilson and Read 2016; Worrall et al. 2007). For older people, narrated, interactive videos and photo stories have been beneficial as a communication tool during health consultations (van‘t Jagt et al. 2016). Decision aids designed by health professionals support education and informed decision‐making for a range of populations (Holmes‐Rovner et al. 2005; Stacey et al. 2017). There is emergent evidence that artificial intelligence could be used to improve readability and increase reader understanding; with limitations and risks relating to any unchecked inaccuracy in information and rapid dissemination of misinformation on health topics (Nutbeam 2023).

A common strategy to evaluate and improve the likelihood of readers understanding written information is the use of readability measures to guide writing for audiences with varying literacy levels. Such measures were developed by the US Military during the early 1900's as a way of quantifying features of a document informing a writer's estimation of whether their intended audience will be able to understand the information (DuBay 2004). However, readability measures are inadequate for the assessment of health information (Kandula and Zeng‐Treitler 2008) and, by their objective nature, cannot consider the unique backgrounds, attitudes, skills, and knowledge of readers in understanding a piece of written information, especially people with lifelong communication disability. For this, the process of understanding written information (i.e., reading comprehension) can be considered using the science of reading (Gough and Tunmer 1986). Scarborough's Reading Rope (Scarborough 2001) is a metaphor of the strands of a rope representing the word recognition and language skills required for reading comprehension. Language skills include vocabulary knowledge, syntactic knowledge, background knowledge, reasoning, and literacy knowledge (Scarborough 2001). Allied health reports contain unique vocabulary, complex syntactic structures, and require a level of background knowledge, reasoning skills and literacy knowledge that are potentially above the skills of many people with lifelong communication disability (Nilsson et al. 2025).

There is lack of research into reading and understanding allied health reports, and the health literacy implications for people with lifelong communication disability; therefore, the aim of this study is to understand the experiences and views of people with lifelong communication disability on receiving, understanding, and using their allied heath reports. The research question for this study was: What are the health literacy implications of allied health reports written for or about people with lifelong communication disability? Answering this research question will inform clinical recommendations to reduce health literacy barriers and guide report writing practices that mean people with lifelong communication disability can access, understand, and use their own allied health reports.

Methods

Design

This research involved in‐depth interviews with people with lifelong communication disability to align with the underpinning methodology, aiming to understand their experiences of receiving and reading allied health reports. This study was part of the doctoral research of the first author under the supervision of the co‐authors and received human research ethics approval from the University of Technology Sydney. A qualitative paradigm was used as best suited to understanding the ‘what’ and ‘how’ of the research topic (Lyons and McAllister 2019, 3) and aligns with the study aims of understanding participants’ experiences of a phenomenon that are likely to be rich, varied, and contextual (Braun and Clarke 2013). The methodologies applied in this research study acknowledge the importance of contextualisation and embrace the pluralism of individuals’ perspectives based on unique contexts; while facilitating actionable knowledge (Colliver 1999). Social constructivism (Creswell and Plano Clark 2018) recognises that each person has a unique experience and an individualised journey which contributes to the construction of their opinions and shapes their understanding of the world. The pragmatist world view (Glogowska 2011) is well suited to defining and exploring problems underpinning research questions based on practical experiences. The integration of these world views ensures interpretive depth with practical relevance; and provides insights that are theoretically meaningful and also actionable in practice (Pretorius 2024). The Sørensen et al. (2012) model of health literacy (Sørensen et al. 2012) informed the design and development of the research. This model provides a multidimensional health literacy definition developed from the synthesis of multiple published health literacy definitions and aligns well with the aims of this study. The model emphasises the interaction of multiple factors relating to accessing, understanding, appraising, and using health information in the context of individual and environmental barriers and facilitators. Additionally, health literacy is positioned as encompassing relational dynamics of trust and collaboration; alongside systemic influences that incorporate policy and reporting requirements. This positioning helps to explain how health literacy factors potentially shape information accessibility in allied health reports. Methods and results are reported using the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (O'Brien et al. 2014).

Participants

To be eligible for inclusion, participants were required to (a) be over the age of 18 years, (b) have a lifelong communication disability such as childhood onset disability (i.e., disabilities present before, during, or after birth or up to the age of 18), (c) be able to provide informed consent (e.g., with access to visual, auditory and written information about the study in accessible formats), (d) have adequate communication skills in English language (i.e., to understand and respond to the interviewer) to participate in interviews, and (e) have received services from allied health professionals. The focus of this research was on the experiences of people with lifelong communication disability. As such, people with communication disability associated with a primary diagnosis of adult onset or acquired disability (e.g., traumatic brain injury, motor neuron disease, stroke), stuttering, or mental health were excluded as their experiences are likely to be substantially different to people with lifelong conditions considering the cause and trajectory of communication disability through health and rehabilitation services.

Recruitment

Purposive and snowball sampling methods (Given 2008) were used to recruit participants that aligned with the aims and objectives of this qualitative research. Recruitment materials were text‐based posters and a video in plain language to support comprehension. Participant information materials were also in plain language with supporting visuals where appropriate. Materials were distributed through the networks of the researchers, social media (specifically Twitter, LinkedIn, and Facebook), emailed to organisations that provide services to adults with lifelong disability, and relevant associations in Australia. Multiple rounds of distributing recruitment materials continued until no new participants could be recruited over a period of 2 years. A priori sample size is not considered to be appropriate within reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2022; Wutich et al. 2024); however, the research team identified ten participants as a goal consistent with the theoretical underpinnings and practicalities of resources and availability of participants meeting inclusion criteria.

Data Collection

An interview guide was developed by the research team based on the prior literature on allied health reports (Turnbull et al. 2020). The guide was also informed by considering a multidimensional conceptual model of health literacy (Sørensen et al. 2012) developed from the synthesis of multiple published health literacy definitions, and aligning well with the aims of this study. A copy of the interview guide is available on request from the first author.

Informed consent was supported by verbal explanation of the plain language participant information and discussion about anything that was unclear to the participants. Consent was recorded in writing, or verbally if signing the consent form was prohibitive for the participant (i.e., unable to sign electronically or print and scan consent form). Participants were invited to have a support person accompany them in interviews such as a family member, friend, or support worker if they wished. This person's role was to support communication of the information about the study during interviews, but not to speak on behalf of the participant. The researcher, with the support person's assistance as required, explained the participant information and used ‘teach‐back’ strategies as an evidence‐based method to ensure comprehension of verbal or written information (Anderson et al. 2020). The first author conducted in‐depth interviews in‐person or online using Microsoft Teams videoconferencing and were between 40 and 90 min duration. In the interviews, the researcher monitored participants for any signals of discomfort or distress, none of which were evident in the discussions covered. In‐person interviews were audio recorded and online interviews were audio and video recorded using the Microsoft Teams recording feature. Demographic data were collected with each interview and participants were asked questions in line with their communication support needs. Questions and follow‐up questions according to the topic guide were used where relevant to the conversational‐style in‐depth interview, in addition to topics raised by participants to allow deeper exploration of the topic where possible (Given 2008).

The first author transcribed and de‐identified interviews into a Microsoft Word document and then used a Microsoft Excel Workbook for analysis of the data. The names of any people, organisations, communication devices, or other potentially identifying content was replaced with a descriptive label. The workbook was accessible only to members of the research team using a secure Microsoft Sharepoint folder. Participants were emailed an interpretive summary written by the first author. The summary was based on repeated listening to audio recording and reading of each transcription. The summary included the main points from the interview for participants to verify the researcher's interpretation without adding unduly to the effort required of participants. Participants were invited to check, verify, add, or change any information in the summary, and to attend a follow‐up interview if desired, to discuss the summary. Participant verification and confirmation is an important element of rigour in qualitative research (Lyons and Roulstone 2018). These methods provided an efficient and flexible way for participants to engage in the verification process according to their own preferences. Seven participants responded via email to verify the interview summary and three of those had a second interview to verify and expand on the first interview. No participants requested changes to the written summary of main points from each interview and all who had a second interview verified the summary through discussion and confirmed that it reflected their views.

Analysis

A process of constant comparative analysis was used to analyse data as it was collected (Glaser 1965; Hewitt‐Taylor 2001). Inductive data analysis using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun and Clarke 2021) was used to enable in‐depth exploration of the individual contexts, experiences, and opinions of the participants. The Sørensen et al. model of health literacy (Sørensen et al. 2012) was the analytic lens applied throughout the analysis, informing the coding of the interview data through appreciation of the access, understanding, and use of health information. However, while informing the analysis, and being the lens adopted by the researchers, the model did not restrict the coding of the data or the development of themes as inductive coding was the primary method.

All authors contributed to the interpretive analysis through group discussions of the data in an iterative process as the first author coded the data within the reflexive approach to thematic analysis. The first author grouped codes that aligned with various topics followed by an iterative process of developing themes from patterns identified in the data and discussed with co‐authors. Input from co‐authors provided diverse perspectives which generated deeper discussion and exploration of the data within an iterative process of coding and identifying themes. The regular multidisciplinary group meetings of the researchers facilitated acknowledgement of researcher assumptions throughout the analysis process. Final code and theme generation was verified in discussion with all authors.

Reflexive thematic analysis is an interpretive process that aims to derive meaning from patterns in qualitative data (Lyons et al. 2021). Reflexivity is an important component and can involve reflection on the tools and processes as well as what the researcher brings to the study (Braun and Clarke 2013, 37, 2021, 7). The motivation for this research arose both from the literature and the experiences of the first author; a speech pathologist with extensive experience in working with people with lifelong communication disability. All authors held personal and professional experience in working with populations with communication disability across multiple disciplines. This history and connection of the authors with the topic is embraced as a strength contributing to the rigour in this reflexive, qualitative study. This connection to the topic is viewed as facilitating a deeper understanding and interpretation of patterns of meaning derived from the data (Braun and Clarke 2021).

Results

Participants

In‐depth interviews were conducted with nine participants, all living in Australia and aged between 25 and 54 years. Three of these nine participants also took part in an individual follow‐up interview, resulting in a total of 12 interviews. Participants reported primary diagnoses that included intellectual disability (n = 3), cerebral palsy (n = 2), autism (n = 1), physical disability (n = 1), and a childhood neurological condition (n = 1). Two participants use augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) in the form of a speech generating device as their main communication method, one used oral speech and AAC in the form of a speech generating device, and seven used oral speech as their main communication method. Participants reportedly attended mainstream school (n = 7), support class within mainstream school (n = 4), or support school (n = 2), with some participants attending multiple school contexts throughout schooling. The highest level of Australian equivalent education was Masters qualification (n = 3), Bachelor qualification (n = 1), Year 12 or Higher School Certificate (n = 4), and Year 9 schooling (n = 1). Thematic Analysis

Two main themes were derived from the data, each with three subthemes, ‘This is personal’, with subthemes: ‘We need to access and to understand’, ‘This is emotional’, and ‘Build a trusting relationship with us’; and ‘These are our documents’, with subthemes: ‘Your content to achieve our purposes’, ‘Our reports need to be an accurate representation’, and ‘We have feedback for you’. These themes and subthemes are represented in Figure 1.

Visual representation of two main themes each with three sub‐themes.

The two main themes reflect key messages from people with lifelong communication disability about their experiences of receiving, understanding, and using their allied heath reports. The theme “This is personal” highlights the relational and emotional experiences of receiving and reading allied health reports; however, people with lifelong communication disability may not even know reports are written about themselves. The theme “These are our documents” recognises that allied health reports can be valuable in achieving important outcomes through accurate representation of the person with lifelong communication disability, as well as emphasising the importance of feedback for improvements in report writing.

Major Theme One: This Is Personal

The process of allied health assessments and receiving the written reports is a personal experience for people with lifelong communication disability. Participants described their experiences and opinions of allied health reports in various ways related to their personal situations. They talked about the topic as being very personal in nature and wanted to be the key audience of allied health reports. This theme included three sub‐themes: ‘We need to access and to understand’, ‘This is emotional’, and ‘You need to build a trusting relationship’.

Subtheme One: We Need to Access and to Understand

“That's the easy to read one. It's still very hard to read and understand.” (P4)

“That's the easy to read one. It's still very hard to read and understand.” (P4)

Most participants expressed their desire for allied health professionals to write reports that achieve the key purpose while making them physically accessible and information accessible to them as a person with lifelong communication disability. This subtheme begins with the perspectives of participants who did not have access to their allied health reports followed by those that did have access to reports written about them.

Three participants (P5, P6, P7) reported that they had not been given their own allied health reports. As P6 explained: “I didn't know that in the background they write reports about me… and I think in a way that… I think I do know, but… I didn't think about it.” These three participants indicated they would like more access to their reports and that they were interested in knowing what information was included in allied health reports written about themselves. When asked by the interviewer: “Would you like to see [reports about yourself] more often?” P7 said: “Yes, I would… it'd be good to know.” These participants also expressed a desire to understand more about why they did not receive their allied health reports. P5 said: “If it's about me I'm allowed to ask, but I won't ask what they're writing about other people.” P7 described an understanding of their rights in relation to accessing information about themselves: “I can always go back under the Freedom of Information Act.” P7 also observed that gaining physical access to their reports would not be very helpful if the information in the reports was too difficult to understand. When the interviewer commented: “I mean, you'd have it, at least?” P7 responded: “Yeah, but would I understand that though?” P4 had accessed some of their allied health reports and had similar thoughts about difficulty understanding. Even though P4 had access to some of their allied health reports, they also indicated low motivation to read them because of difficulty understanding them. P4 said: “Some of them are hard to read and hard to understand… because they use too much [sic] political words… some language not from this era as well.”

P1, P2, P4, and P9, had received and read their allied health reports but also recognised that when they were younger, they had less opportunity or motivation to read allied health reports about themselves than as adults. As P2 said: “Grow[ing] up, I had lots of reports which I didn't get.” P4 explained that other people assumed the responsibility of using information from reports to make decisions on their behalf: “[Allied health professional] did, without me knowing, make a [written] suggestion with the principal that I… start being in the disability unit [at school] … but I didn't get to have a say in that.”

Participants who had received their allied health reports expressed that the reports are often difficult to read and understand and made suggestions for ways that allied health reports could be written or presented to meet their needs as a reader. P2 commented: “The language was too difficult.” indicating that language used in reports is a barrier to their understanding.

Most participants, regardless of whether they had received reports, viewed that allied health professionals should give people access to the information in their reports in ways that help them to understand; and that this might involve using plain language along with a discussion about the content of the allied health reports. P6 recommended Easy Read as a useful strategy: “There's a thing called easy read. I'm not sure if you've heard, but that's really useful.” These participants identified that allied health reports could be easier to understand through various strategies that meet the needs of people with lifelong communication disability as the reader such as formatting and language considerations. For example, P2 said: “I like them in plain English so I can understand that.” Formatting suggestions included bold text, highlight text, bullet points, and larger font sizes. Some participants also commented that images with subtitles to match written content could be useful (e.g., assistive technology equipment or physiotherapy exercises). P6 said: “[It is] a whole lot easier to have things that are bold, things that are sticking out, things that are highlighted, things that are bullet points.”

P2, P3, and P8 suggested that allied health professionals should ask people with lifelong communication disability what their needs as a reader are and consider them as a customer, paying for their own reports. As P8 explained: “I say the products I'm looking for… because it costs so much to get an assessment for assistive technology.” These participants indicated an understanding of the contextual barriers that allied health professionals might experience when writing reports that are accessible for people with lifelong communication disability. P8 related this to service‐level demands and funding models for disability in Australia, which is the National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS; Department of Social Services 2018): “I also 100% understand the limitations of the NDIS [wanting reports written a certain way].” In contrast, P7 asserted that allied health professionals should not find it difficult to write their reports in more accessible formats. In response to an interviewer comment: “[Some allied health professionals] say it's really hard to do Easy English.”, P7 responded: “That's rubbish… [advocacy organisation] put stuff in Easy Read all the time.”.

Subtheme Two: This is Emotional

“It can be traumatic… and it is for me.” (P9)

“It can be traumatic… and it is for me.” (P9)

Participants described experiences of both allied health services and the written reports, across the lifespan, that were associated with a variety of emotions. This subtheme situates the emotions and subsequent coping strategies of participants within their individual and personal experiences. To the participants, allied health reports were seen as personal and the experience of reading them held emotional risk. As P9 stated: “I think that's a huge issue. The way that reports are written. It can be traumatic… and it has been for me.” P9 expanded with their view of their own disability, providing additional insight to the emotional impact of deficit‐based allied health reports: “I don't think of myself as sick or broken. I'm a very healthy person who has aspects of my experience that are not typical, and so I need reports to access technology and support.”

Despite negative experiences and emotions relating to allied health reports, some participants focussed on and described their positive experiences. P9 emphasised this by saying: “I've had some good experiences and some not so good experiences. And I'll tell you about some good experiences first, because I think they're important to know about too.” Participants described positive or empowering emotions such as courage, confidence, gratefulness, and amusement. These emotions were often linked to experiences where the emotional burden weighed less on participants and interpersonal relationships and supports were stronger. P6 said: “All of the people that we've written down have been amazing for how they have helped me through 25 years… So I'm just grateful that they actually help me.” P9 described an experience resulting in a successful funding outcome where factors related to the allied health professional, the steps leading to the report, as well as the content of the report contributed to a positive experience. P9 said: “I had a great [allied health professional] who wrote from a human rights perspective… And she also gave me a copy of that before she sent it anywhere… so that was brilliant.”.

Negative emotional experiences for participants involved written reports associated with fear, frustration, and discomfort. Related to specific events, participants described the burden that they felt when reading and understanding their own reports as well as the need to be resilient. A pattern of experiences emerged where participants reconciled something that was negative, to move forward or achieve positive outcomes. P1 described being compelled to accept reports that might be upsetting so that they could gain supports and funding: “[Allied health professionals] have to focus on my weaknesses… I understand that (shrugging gesture).” P9 also described powerful negative emotions arising from the way disability is viewed or described by allied health professionals. P9 said: “It's very upsetting to read when it's about yourself especially when they use words like complex or complex history… Those words to other people might not sound like a big deal but when it's about you, it's really upsetting.”

There was a pattern across the data of recognition that deficit‐focussed reports were written to funding bodies (e.g., National Disability Insurance Scheme, NDIS) for the purpose of obtaining individualised funds for support needs (e.g., therapy, supported independent living). Such deficit‐focussed reports generated negative emotions for participants. P1 described an emotional experience with a report that had incorrect and deficit‐focussed content: “[The report] said I have a lot of cognitive deficits… I actually showed it to a psychologist who had known me for years… she said ‘you and I both know this is wrong’, and put it to one side.” P9 identified that allied health professionals can shift their focus to the supports that facilitate access and participation as a better alternative to deficit‐focussed reports, saying: “It's very frightening thing to be like, constantly focussing on… what people can't do… that might change. But if you're looking at the support people need… it's not necessarily going to change”.

Many participants described some of their coping strategies to manage or avoid impact of negative emotions. Participants described coping strategies related to building resilience, focussing on positive experiences, and having a support network or links to advocacy organisations. P7 hadn't received any of their allied health reports, but still identified the potential role of advocacy organisations: “I am a member of [advocacy organisation] so… I'll get them involved if I need to.” P1 carefully selected allied health professionals according to their own specific needs as a strategy when there was variable understanding of specific impacts of their disability on their activities and participation. P1 said: “I feel my disability is not understood by everyone.” P8 described collaboration on preparation of the reports with allied health professionals as a strategy for coping with some of the emotional burden of reading about their deficits and impairments. This meant that the information in their allied health reports was already familiar. P8 said: “Like, I respect their professional opinion, and I'm interested in what they have to say, but I don't want it to be a surprise.”

Subtheme Three: Build a Trusting Relationship With Us

“People are people.” (P9)

“People are people.” (P9)

Many participants valued the time they spent in‐person with their allied health professionals, building relationships over time, facilitating more trust, and a feeling of being better understood as a person. Those who had experienced good relationships with their allied health professionals observed that this facilitated better direct services and reports. P9 explained: “I think knowing that people develop trust… if you don't hide stuff, people develop trust that you are wholeheartedly on their side.” P1 described needing to work with an allied health professional they did not know, such as during a hospitalisation: “I had never met her before… it was all a bit rushed.” These times were rare, and P1 indicated they preferred their well‐known allied health professionals: “I have it sorted but I can imagine if I suddenly had to go into hospital…” Some participants described experiences where relationships had ended abruptly, unsatisfactorily, or were absent. P4 said: “They wanna know everything about you, they wanna know how they can help you… and then they're like, I can't help you anymore.” Experiences where relationships were not ideal were used by some as opportunities to learn about their own needs and the importance of building meaningful and productive relationships with their allied health professionals. For P9, individualised funding facilitated an ability to select allied health professionals who understood them and their disability: “The NDIS has sort of been good in that you… get more control and choice and who writes your reports… not just anyone.” P1 managed this by purposefully selecting their allied health professionals to fit with them and their ways of living: “I have my handpicked team.” Opportunities for participants to select their allied health professionals meant that there was a stronger relationship and more trust, resulting in more satisfactory services and reports. P9 commented: “[It] didn't feel like [allied health professional] was trying to assert power over me or anything like that… so that was brilliant.”

Most participants viewed that allied health professionals should not spend a lot of time writing reports or other documents at the expense of direct services (e.g., assessment or therapy). Although allied health reports have an important function in relation to funding and supports, participants often saw direct services as a higher priority, as P2 said: “I have to [have a] good relationship with [the allied health professional] first… Rather than reports.” P1, P2, P8, and P9 referred to pressures for frequent written allied heath reports required by funding bodies, meaning that allied health professionals could not spend as much time with them. P1 commented: “I think the NDIS has made it difficult…”

For P1, P3, P8, and P9, trust was impeded by issues in upholding confidentiality or similar ethical issues. P3 described concerns about security of sharing information and whether their personal information will be safe when shared. P3 said: “I get really worried about when I hear information needs to be spread or exchanged, I want to make sure it's being done properly not where it can be exposed to hackers.” P1 described the relaxed approach to confidentiality that some allied health professionals had when working with them. P1 said: “We want the expertise but not this small town gossip… I have stopped choosing [provider].”

Major Theme Two: These Are Our Documents

This theme encapsulates participant opinions about the content, purpose, value, and importance of allied health reports in achieving their own purposes throughout life. Allied health reports held varying levels of importance for participants depending on factors such as access to their own reports, involvement in developing the content, the overall services from allied health professionals (e.g., time spent interacting or working toward therapy goals), and the purpose or expected outcome of the report (e.g., accessing funding). Allied health reports can be valuable in achieving goals and many participants emphasised importance of accurate representation in the reports and the value of feedback for ongoing improvements in report writing. This theme included three sub‐themes: ‘Your content to achieve our purposes’, ‘Our reports need to be an accurate representation’, and ‘We have feedback for you’.

Subtheme One: Your Content to Achieve Our Purposes

“I think these report[s] are good to get funding. However, it's so hard to read.” (P2)

“I think these report[s] are good to get funding. However, it's so hard to read.” (P2)

Comments about the content of allied health reports related to language (e.g., words and sentences), language adjustments (e.g., Easy Read, plain language), information about the person (e.g., skills, deficits, and support needs), and the framing of the information (e.g., person‐centred, outcomes‐focussed). P8 identified the importance of having a strengths‐based lens in allied health reports by contrasting the patronising feel of deficit‐based content. P8 said: “When [allied health professionals] don't write in a strengths‐based way, unless we've agreed that that's OK, it just feels patronising… I'm not delusional, like, I know what my strengths are and what I need to work on.”

Some participants indicated that it was the role of their allied health professionals to generate the content of reports; however, people with lifelong communication disability need that content, and that report, to achieve their individual purposes. P4 identified that a report can contain good information but also use confusing language: “Basically it was a good report, but it was just written out in the wrong English… or just trying to say things that were just the wrong way round.” Most participants identified that the key purpose of allied health reports was often funding for supports or equipment and that the content of allied health reports is closely linked to that purpose and getting their support needs met. These comments from participants indicated a complex relationship between the content, purpose, and importance such that an allied health report may not be personally valuable to a participant but could facilitate important supports. P8 said: “They are important in terms of ticking the box for things I need… Are they helpful to me personally? No, not really.”

Participants who had read their allied health reports, built skills over time to be able to judge whether a report was good enough to be used for their individual purposes. These judgements related to a range of factors and experiences which were unique to the participant, their context and the allied health professional involved. Participants who had read their allied health reports described appraising those reports; however, P1 noted that a report deemed ‘good’ may not be successful in achieving funding or supports recommended by the allied health professional. P1 described an experience where funding was only provided after numerous rounds of rewriting. When the interviewer asked: “Is there a time when a report is written and you think this is a really good one, and then it doesn't help you to get the funding?” P1 responded: “(Emphatic vocalisation and nodding) Like our [AAC device] saga!”

Subtheme Two: Our Reports Need to be an Accurate Representation

“I want it to be reflective of what's actually happening.” (P8)

“I want it to be reflective of what's actually happening.” (P8)

Most participants viewed that allied health reports should accurately represent the person and their support needs because they are an enduring record about a person. The content in their allied health reports may be reproduced into future documents and reports, with ramifications for the supports available to the participant. P9 described the difficulty in having inaccuracies in their allied health reports corrected and the challenges of speaking up about incorrect or inaccurate information (e.g., name, diagnosis, existing supports), compounded by their communication disability. P9 said: “[Inaccuracies] often get copied and written on the next report, you know, someone will read, especially when you're not easily able to communicate, to say it's not correct, it'll just get copied from one to the next.”

Some participants described times when allied health reports were not a full representation of them (e.g., only describing deficits). A pattern of experiences was seen across the data where there was reluctance of allied health professionals to include many strengths in reports. P1 linked this issue with the perceived requirement to emphasise support or equipment needs: “It is written to make the person look the most disabled.” P1 also described this as only painting part of a picture and expressed a desire for allied health professionals to be able to write reports that describe the whole person. P1 said: “I think therapists should be free to paint the real picture.”

The importance of allied health reports presenting a realistic understanding of their disability was identified by some participants, and how these experiences shaped their support needs. For P1, experiences of misunderstood allied health reports resulted in a misrepresentation that impacted funding for equipment. P1 said: “[Allied health professional] and I had a horrible time getting the [device] because they thought I was too able… the only reason I am competent is because I have the [device]!”

Some participants pondered whether incorrect information in an allied health report was worth the effort to have corrected. P9 said: “So there's been times when I've just let things go through when they're wrong because, they're wrong, but… what difference is it going to make to whether I get my [assistive technology] funded?” For these participants it appears that there was a threshold of incorrect information that they could tolerate because they determined that the report would still achieve the purpose. Participants made these decisions on a case‐by‐case basis regarding each report that contains incorrect or inaccurate information. P4 described a scenario where an allied health report needed multiple corrections; however, this did not impact the use of the report or their relationships. P4 said: “[Allied health professional] wrote this report that mum had to go over and correct everything… but [allied health professional is] nice.”

Subtheme Three: We Have Feedback for You

“Ask people for feedback on your reports and listen to the answers.” (P8)

“Ask people for feedback on your reports and listen to the answers.” (P8)

Participants who read their own reports described times when they wanted, needed, or tried to give feedback on their allied health services and reports. Participants raised the importance of feedback in allied health services generally as well as allied health reports. P6 described a positive experience of an allied health professional responding to general feedback: “I think I posed the idea and… now I think she's changed her mind about emailing me [session notes]… which is really useful.” Where participants had the opportunity to authentically provide feedback on or contribute to the content of their allied health reports, these reports increased in importance and value for the participants. Participants indicated that routinely available feedback opportunities in allied health practices would support improvements in their reports. P8 emphasised that their involvement would improve how they are represented in their allied health reports: “It's about me and I want to make sure that I've come across in a way that works for me.” For P7, the use of accessible language to support understanding was most important to them: “Put everything in plain English and easy to understand.”

P2, P8, and P9 described situations where allied health professionals were reluctant to receive feedback or adjust writing based on feedback. P2 identified that allied health professionals may feel reluctant to hear feedback that means a report needs to be worked on again: “[Allied health professionals] mostly think that I'm just being difficult.” P8 and P9 made a link to the level of experience of the allied health professional and acceptance of feedback, with the implication that allied health professionals with less experience seemed less open to receiving and responding to feedback on their reports. P8 emphasised the value of feedback in improving skills in report writing: “I think unless you get feedback about what you do, which some people are OK with and some people aren't, then you can't change.” These participants described some positive experiences where allied health professionals embraced feedback and used these opportunities to collaborate. P9 said: “Experienced people are always open to [a] bit of critical reflection and, ‘oh I'd not thought of that’… and I find that great. I love that.”.

Discussion

This research provides an insight into the experiences of people with lifelong communication disability in relation to allied health reports and the associated relationships, and services from allied health professionals. This discussion links the findings to existing research and the key areas of the Sørensen et al. (2012) health literacy model (Sørensen et al. 2012) of accessing, understanding, appraising, and applying information in allied health reports; in the context of individual and environmental barriers and facilitators for people with lifelong communication disability.

A foundational component of health literacy (Sørensen et al. 2012) is to gain access to health information. The findings of this study show that people with lifelong communication disability may not be given access to reports or even be aware that their allied health professionals write reports about them. Without access to health information, individuals do not have the opportunity to understand that health information and make informed decisions to achieve their health and therapeutic goals. Findings indicate that people with lifelong communication disability want access to their allied health reports. However, there is also a risk that deficit‐focused reports can cause long‐term emotional trauma and reduce motivation to read their own reports or engage with allied health professionals. It remains unclear why allied health professionals may not inform this group that they write reports or provide copies of all reports. For allied health reports to be a health literacy facilitator for people with lifelong communication disability, allied health professionals need to provide access and use strategies to support understanding of report content.

Results indicate that for those who do receive a copy of their allied health reports, poor information accessibility can be a health literacy barrier. Even reports that are deemed ‘easy to read’ by the authors may still be too difficult for some readers. One strategy for determining whether a document is ‘easy’ or ‘plain’ is through application of readability measures (Kandula and Zeng‐Treitler 2008). However, Scarborough's Reading Rope (Scarborough 2001) may provide insight into supporting reader comprehension of allied health reports. Results of this study indicate that people with lifelong communication disability may have variable skills required for reading comprehension (e.g., background information, vocabulary knowledge). Additionally, recent advice that readability and information accessibility are best determined by the audience (The British Standards Institution 2023), increases the imperative for collaboration and feedback opportunities to improve readability and information accessibility of reports. People with lifelong communication disability desire collaboration with allied health professionals to contribute to a report that they understand and that achieves the purpose (e.g., access to funding for equipment).

Despite a lack of research into the ‘appraise’ dimension of health literacy for people with lifelong disability (Turnbull et al. 2023), this study found that those who read their own allied health reports were appraising their reports. Readers are evaluating whether each report is understandable and will achieve the intended purpose (e.g., accessing supports or equipment); however, opportunities to share feedback with their allied health professionals are limited. People with lifelong communication disability should be at the centre of allied health report‐writing, especially in relation to seeking and using feedback to improve reports. Feedback opportunities align with principles of person‐centred care, where services respond to individuals’ perspectives, engage in shared decision‐making, and where health information is co‐constructed. Results of this study suggest that routine feedback processes in allied health practices might not only improve reports but also respect autonomy and foster collaboration.

The Sørensen model of health literacy (Sørensen et al. 2012) identifies that health information is received, understood, and appraised, so that it can be used to achieve the intended outcomes. This study has highlighted that information in allied health reports can be used even if there has been no opportunity for the person to access, understand, or appraise those reports. For people with lifelong communication disability, it appears that the information in their allied health reports is used by others without always accessing for themselves. Additionally, allied health reports are used by funding bodies to allocate support funds and serve systemic purposes (e.g., resource allocation) without input or awareness of the person that the report is about; and there are times when the person is not aware that their personal information is being shared. It appears that there may be barriers to the involvement of people with lifelong communication disability in the use of their allied health reports for funding decisions.

Clinical Implications

Allied health professionals have an opportunity to reduce health literacy barriers for people with lifelong communication disability and enhance trusting relationships by acknowledging the personal and emotional nature of written reports, and clients’ ownership of their own reports. When possible, people with lifelong communication disability should be told that allied health professionals write reports about them and be given copies of their reports, so that they can evaluate their own reports and provide their feedback. Allied health professionals should ensure that reports are not only physically accessible but also linguistically accessible, including for people with cognitive disabilities. Such multi‐directional accessibility includes the use of plain language, easy‐to‐read formats, visual supports, and formatting strategies (e.g., bold text, bullet points, larger fonts) aligning with the preferences and needs of readers. These accessibility features should be embedded into routine clinical documentation practices and supported in professional standards and training.

Emotional experiences for people with lifelong communication disability reading their reports seems to be linked to the representation of their skills in allied health reports. It appears that the system‐driven purposes of allied health reports contradict the relational, person‐centred practices of many allied health professionals. Allied health professionals should consider moving away from reports grounded in a medical model of disability to reduce the deficit focus of information in written reports. This shift needs to be supported through changes in funding body requirements for reports, so that people with lifelong communication disability can be represented in ways that are affirming, are accurate, and achieve their purposes. This research highlights the need to advocate for funding bodies to encourage and accept allied health reports that are both clinically rigorous and accessible, reducing the impetus for clinicians to prioritise bureaucratic language over person‐centred narratives. Additionally, trauma‐informed and strengths‐based principles should be embedded into allied health reporting‐writing guidelines and allied health training standards.

Allied health professionals need report‐writing time, supported by workplace policies and funding bodies, as well as clinical guidelines for collaboration with people with lifelong communication disability. Reports should be accessible for people with lifelong communication disability while supporting person‐centred, ethical practice and relationship‐building through reduced focus on deficits.

There are numerous factors influencing reader comprehension of allied health reports, with factors relating to language skills potentially creating barriers to understanding written information (e.g., background knowledge, reasoning skills; Scarborough 2001). There may be a role for allied health professionals to support background knowledge or vocabulary to support comprehension. Professional associations and employers should develop and support the implementation of guidelines for clinicians to produce accessible written reports tailored to the needs of readers with lifelong communication disabilities. Additionally, government and relevant agencies should revise reporting criteria to prioritise accessibility, accuracy, and client involvement, and consult with people with communication disability to develop allied health report‐writing guidelines.

Limitations and Directions for Future Research

The findings of this research should be interpreted with caution. Although rigorous in the application of qualitative research methods, verification of findings with colleagues who had access to the raw data, and verification with participants, this was a small study with only nine participants. Heterogeneity in the participant group is considered to be a strength in reflexive, qualitative research and strategies to improve the information power and reduce the limitation of low participant numbers were applied. Sufficiency of the sample size was considered by evaluating information power (Malterud et al. 2016) through the use of clear study aims, robust application of established theory, high quality interview dialogue, and the extensive in‐depth data analysis strategy. Although the sample size was small, the quality of the data and depth of analysis means that this study provides a powerful exploration of the lived experiences and views of adults with lifelong communication disability; a population often excluded from health literacy research. Additionally, three of the participants did not reply to verify the summary of researcher interpretations. A further limitation is that some participants had little experience with reading reports about themselves, and therefore their experiences and opinions related to this rather than the information accessibility of allied health reports.

There is considerable need for additional co‐designed research to inform ways that allied health professionals can support the health literacy of people with lifelong communication disability through report‐writing. Future research should include larger numbers of people with lifelong communication disability and other readers of allied health reports about people with lifelong communication disability such as family members, support workers, and employees of disability organisations and funding bodies. Research on the experiences of the allied health professionals who write reports about people with lifelong communication disability would also add considerably to this body of knowledge.

Conclusion

People with lifelong communication disability have a right to access information about themselves in formats that they can understand and use to achieve their goals; however, they may not be given their reports or informed that reports are being written about them. Where possible, people with lifelong communication disability should be supported to have active involvement in the writing of allied health reports that are an accurate representation of themselves and achieve intended purposes. People with lifelong disability value their allied health professionals and need their reports to have accessible content to achieve important purposes.

For people with lifelong communication disability, reading their allied health reports can be a highly personal and emotional experience with the need to develop coping strategies and resilience. Although allied health reports facilitate access to important funding for supports and equipment, people with lifelong communication disability emphasise the importance of trusting relationships with allied health professionals.

To reduce health literacy barriers for people with lifelong communication disability, allied health professionals should provide access to reports that are written in ways that the individual can understand and use. Allied health professionals can discuss appropriate strategies to improve the information accessibility of their reports through partnership with readers. Additionally, allied health professionals can build a culture of continuous improvement by routinely seeking and responding to feedback on reports from people with lifelong communication disability.
