Fundamental safety-capability trade-offs in fine-tuning large language models
Authors: Pin-Yu Chen, Han Shen, Payel Das, Tianyi Chen, Derek Abbott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFine-tuning large language models (LLMs) on some task-specific datasets has been a primary use of LLMs. However, it has been empirically observed that this approach to enhancing capability inevitably compromises safety, a phenomenon also known as the safety-capability trade-off in LLM fine-tuning. This article presents a theoretical framework for understanding the interplay between safety and capability in two primary safety-aware LLM fine-tuning strategies, providing new insights into the effects of data similarity, context overlap, and alignment loss landscape. Our theoretical results characterize the fundamental limits of the safety-capability trade-off in LLM fine-tuning, which are also validated by numerical experiments.Keywords: large language model, generative AI, safety-capability trade-off

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are artificial neural networks that are pretrained on large textual datasets using next-token prediction loss and further refined to follow instructions and achieve compliance (1). The latter process is also known as alignment, which updates the pretrained model weights to align the LLM’s response with the desired output. In particular, safety alignment focuses on preventing LLMs from generating harmful responses. Beyond the alignment stage, an aligned LLM is often fine-tuned on a domain-specific dataset to improve its capabilities in downstream tasks, such as coding, reasoning, and mathematical problem-solving. This fine-tuning practice can be viewed as a form of domain adaptation (2, 3). However, recent studies have found that the increase in capability comes at a hidden cost of breaking the innate safety guardrail, even when the task-specific fine-tuning dataset does not contain any malicious data samples (4). For example, after being fine-tuned on insecure coding examples, leading frontier LLMs such as GPT-4o exhibit misaligned behaviors when answering generic free-form questions and instructions (5). These unsafe requests would have been rejected by the original LLM. The degradation of safety following fine-tuning poses significant usability challenges for LLMs (6). More broadly, this phenomenon can be viewed as a form of catastrophic forgetting in the continual learning process of LLMs’ safety alignment.

In this article, we establish a theoretical framework to study the problem of safety-capability trade-off in fine-tuning LLMs. While recent work has provided empirical evidence of safety degradation after LLM fine-tuning and proposed various mitigation methods (7), a comprehensive theoretical understanding of the interplay between safety and capability in LLM fine-tuning remains elusive. To fill this critical gap, we provide theoretical interpretations and novel insights to characterize the safety-capability trade-offs in LLM fine-tuning. Our framework considers two practical safety-aware fine-tuning strategies: (i) Alignment loss constraint—where a proxy safety instruction-tuning dataset is used together with the downstream capability dataset during fine-tuning to constrain the safety loss, such as (8 , 9); and (ii) Alignment parameter constraint—where the model parameter updates are constrained to be in a local neighborhood of the aligned model to maintain a similar level of safety after fine-tuning, such as (10, 11). Our results reveal the primary factors that influence safety and capability in LLM fine-tuning and provide a fundamental understanding of the associated trade-offs, including similarity between the original and proxy safety data, context overlap between the safety and capability data, and alignment landscape in the model parameter space of the original LLM.

Overview

We first provide the mathematical notations, formalize the theoretical analysis of safety-aware LLM fine-tuning and then present numerical results to validate our findings. All proofs are given in Supplementary material.

Notations

Let x and y denote the input and output token sequences, respectively. Safety alignment or task-specific fine-tuning refers to instruction tuning on a set of inputs and their preferred outputs, {x,y}, which are drawn from some statistical distributions over all possible input and output sequences. Define Ds(x) and μs(y|x), respectively as the original safety alignment input and target output distribution, which might be inaccessible during fine-tuning. In practice, one may use a proxy safety instruction-tuning dataset sampled from the alternative distributions D^ and μ^(⋅|x) for fine-tuning. We also denote Df(x) and μf(y|x) as the input and output distributions of the downstream task data. The goal is to fine-tune the model on Df(x) and μf(y|x) for capability improvement while preserving the model’s safety alignment on Ds and μs. For brevity, given x, we write μs(⋅|x) as μs(x) and similarly for other output distributions. We use O for big O notation, ‖⋅‖ for canonical norm, ‖⋅‖TV for total variation, DKL(⋅|⋅) for Kullback–Leibler divergence, supp(⋅) for the support of a probability distribution, ∇ for gradient, and ⟨⋅,⋅⟩ for inner product.

In summary, our theoretical analysis reveals the primary factors underlying the safety-capability trade-offs in LLM fine-tuning, including data similarity, context overlap, and alignment loss landscape. These fundamental results provide practical insights into LLM fine-tuning. For practitioners, our findings suggest that the best practice for mitigating safety-capability trade-offs, given the task data at hand, is to use high-similarity safety data and LLMs with low context overlap and benign alignment loss landscapes. For researchers, our analysis specifies the theoretical limitations of LLM fine-tuning methods. Next, we present experiments on fine-tuning LLMs that demonstrate these findings.

Experiment setup

We design numerical experiments using Llama-2-7B base model. The instruction-tuning datasets used are: (i) Orca (12). The inputs are various task instructions. The target outputs are generated by GPT-4; (ii) Alpaca/Alpaca-GPT-4 (13). The inputs are similar to Orca. The target outputs are generated by OpenAI’s text-davinci-003 or GPT-4; (iii) Commitpackft (14). The inputs are GitHub commits formatted into code modification requests, and the target outputs are modified codes after the commits; (iv) Open-platypus (15). The inputs are reasoning problems, and the target outputs are generated by GPT-4 or by humans.

Before fine-tuning, we first align the Llama-2-7B base model on Orca, which is treated as the alignment dataset generated by Ds and μs. To validate our theoretical analysis, we measure the safety alignment loss gap Gs(Pθ) in (3) and the capability loss gap Gf(Pθ) in (4) by the empirical loss of the associated data samples. Additionally, the minimum value terms Ex∼Ds,y∼μs(x)[−logμs(y|x)] or Ex∼Df,y∼μf(x)[−logμf(y|x)] are approximated with the Llama-2-7B based model trained on the alignment dataset or the fine-tuning dataset without any constraints.

Conclusion

This article establishes a theoretical framework to understand the fundamental trade-offs between safety and capability when fine-tuning LLMs. Our results reveal how data similarity and context overlap impact safety and capability. These insights can inform future AI safety research in LLMs.
