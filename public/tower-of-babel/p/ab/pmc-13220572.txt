Identification of biomarkers associated with mitochondria and macrophage polarization in acute myocardial infarction: a bioinformatics analysis and validation study
Authors: Nan Qu, Fawen Bai, Jin Lan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies have shown that mitochondrial dysfunction in macrophages worsens inflammation and impedes repair after acute myocardial infarction (AMI). This study aimed to identify and validate biomarkers of AMI associated with mitochondria-related genes (MRGs) and macrophage polarization-related genes (MPRGs), offering new targets and strategies for therapeutic intervention of AMI.MethodsIn this study, the GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets were employed. Initially, candidate genes were identified by overlapping the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from differential expression analysis, key module genes from weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), and MRGs. Then, biomarkers were identified by machine learing, receiver operating characteristic (ROC), and gene expression analyses. Finally, functional enrichment, immune infiltration, drug prediction, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analyses were performed to explore the roles of these biomarkers.ResultsThe study identified APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1 as biomarkers associated with AMI, all of which exhibited reduced expression in AMI samples. RT-qPCR results further validated these findings. Notably, all 4 biomarkers were predominantly co-enriched in the “ribosome” pathway, highlighting its significance in AMI. Additionally, 11 differential immune cells were identified. Correlation analysis revealed that these biomarkers showed the strongest positive correlations with activated CD8 T cells and the most negative correlations with neutrophils. Drug prediction indicated that valproic acid, which targeted all 4 biomarkers, could be a promising therapeutic option for AMI.ConclusionsIn this study, APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1 were identified as biomarkers for AMI, with their expression levels validated in clinical samples. These findings offered a potential theoretical foundation for developing targeted treatments for AMI.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-03972-7.

Background

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is a common and serious cardiovascular disease that poses a significant threat to human health. The pathogenesis involves the rupture of coronary atherosclerotic plaques, which results in thrombus formation. It occurs when a thrombotic event partially or completely occludes an atherosclerotic coronary artery [1]. If treatment is not administered promptly, it can lead to serious complications such as arrhythmia, heart failure, and sudden death [2]. Currently, early reperfusion therapy, including percutaneous coronary intervention or thrombolysis, is widely used to significantly improve myocardial perfusion and patient prognosis, and has been recommended by the 2023 ESC ACS guidelines [3]. Despite the effectiveness of current treatments, the mortality remains high. A meta-analysis of 38 studies showed that the in-hospital mortality of ST-elevation myocardial infarction is 9.9%, and that of Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction is 6.3% [4]. Biomarkers such as CK-MB and cardiac troponins (cTnI, cTnT) are vital for detecting AMI. While CK-MB and cTnI offer valuable diagnostic information, they are not without drawbacks. CK-MB can produce false positives due to skeletal muscle injury [5], and cTnI levels may rise in non-ischemic conditions such as chronic kidney disease [6], leading to diagnostic uncertainties. Therefore, finding biomarkers for AMI and developing new prediction models can help understand pathogenesis and early diagnosis as well as explore potential drugs, we can improve the diagnosis, prognosis, and quality of life of patients.

Mitochondria are complex organelles covered by a two-layer membrane and are present in most eukaryotic cells [7, 8].They are the energy-producing structures in cells and the primary sites of aerobic respiration [9]. Mitochondria play crucial roles in energy conversion, ATP production, and numerous cellular signaling events [10]. After myocardial ischemia, mitochondria are damaged due to energy metabolism changes, ion imbalance, oxidative stress, and Mitochondrial Permeability Transition Pore (MPTP) opening. Mitochondrial calcium overload, oxidative stress, ATP depletion, and rapid pH correction converge on the MPTP, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiomyocyte death. Mitochondria can counteract myocardial injury by targeting the MPTP, regulating mitochondrial fusion and fission proteins, and participating in endogenous ischemic conditioning strategies, which prevent mitochondrial dysfunction and reduce cardiomyocyte death [11].

Macrophage polarization is the process by which macrophages differentiate into different functional states in response to various microenvironmental signals. It is typically divided into M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory) types [12]. In AMI, M1 macrophages promote inflammation, engulf debris, and release pro-inflammatory cytokines, causing damage to myocardial cells. Conversely, M2 macrophages have anti-inflammatory effects, stimulate tissue repair and angiogenesis, and secrete factors that promote the recovery of myocardial cells, playing a crucial role in the healing process [12]. Moreover, mitochondria are crucial for regulating the activation, differentiation, and survival of macrophages and other immune cells [12]. The functional status of mitochondria, such as energy metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and mitochondrial dynamics, can influence the polarization direction of macrophages [13, 14]. For example, mitochondrial dysfunction can lead to increased ROS production, promoting M1 macrophage polarization [15]. Changes in mitochondrial metabolism can affect the energy acquisition of macrophages and regulate their polarization state [16, 17]. Alterations in mitochondrial dynamics are also related to macrophage polarization [18, 19]. Importantly, mitochondrial dysfunction in macrophages promotes inflammation and inhibits repair after AMI [20]. In conclusion, the occurrence of AMI is closely related to mitochondria and macrophage polarization. Studying the association among AMI, macrophage polarization, and mitochondria can help better understand the pathogenesis of AMI.

This study utilizes data from the GEO database to identify biomarkers related to mitochondria and macrophage polarization in AMI. Through comprehensive analyses, including differential expression, WGCNA, LASSO, ROC, and gene expression analyses, the biomarkers were identified.

The expression of these biomarkers in clinical samples was verified by RT-qPCR analysis. Furthermore, based on these biomarkers, immune infiltration analysis, construction of regulatory networks, and drug prediction analysis were conducted to further investigate their roles and potential mechanisms. This provides new insights for the diagnosis and treatment of AMI.

Methods

Data collection

Transcriptomic datasets of acute myocardial infarction (AMI) were mined from the GEO database. Specifically, GSE61144 (GPL6106 platform) included 7 AMI and 10 healthy control blood samples, while GSE60993 (GPL6884 platform) comprised 7 AMI and 7 healthy control blood samples. The GSE48060 (GPL570 platform) dataset, comprising 30 AMI samples and 21 controls, was used for model validation. Log2 transformation was performed if the 99th percentile > 100. Probe IDs were mapped to gene symbols (highest average expression for multiple probes per gene), with sample matching/cleaning to exclude mismatched/missing data. PCA of GSE61144 (training) and GSE60993 (validation) showed clear disease-control separation within each dataset (Supplementary Fig. S2), indicating biological variation dominance; no batch correction (e.g., ComBat) was needed. Additionally, 1,136 mitochondria-related genes (MRGs) (Supplementary Table S1) were retrieved from MitoCarta 3.0 database ((https://www.broadinstitute.org/mitocarta)) and GSEA database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp), and 35 macrophage polarization-related genes (MPRGs) were obtained from the MsigDB database (Supplementary Table S2).

Differential expression analysis

The differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between disease and control samples in the GSE61144 dataset were identified using the “limma” package (version 3.19) [21], with thresholds set at |log2 FC| > 0.5 and P < 0.05. The DEGs were then visualized using the volcano plot and heat map (only displayed the top 10 genes ranked by |log2FC|), generated through the “ggplot2” (version 3.4.4) [22] and the “ComplexHeatmap” packages (version 2.18.0) [23], respectively.

WGCNA

To explore the correlation between macrophage polarization and gene co-expression networks in AMI, we utilized the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) scores of MPRGs as phenotypes and employed WGCNA to identify gene modules significantly associated with macrophage polarization. In the GSE61144 dataset, the MPRGs score for each sample was computed using the single-sample GSEA (ssGSEA) algorithm, with 35 MPRGs serving as the background gene set. The differences in MPRGs scores between disease and control samples were analyzed and presented, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05, refers to the inter-group difference (AMI vs. Control) rather than individual sample enrichment. This criterion ensures the exclusion of non-significant variations, focusing the analysis on robust alterations in MPRG activity associated with AMI. Furthermore, WGCNA was conducted using the “WGCNA” package (version 1.7.1) [24]. Hierarchical clustering based on euclidean distances of the sample expression profiles was performed to identify and exclude outliers. The optimal soft threshold (power) was determined to achieve a scale-free R2 value greater than 0.85 and a mean connectivity close to zero. A co-expression matrix was then constructed, with a minimum module size of 300 genes and a module merge threshold set at 0.25. Gene modules of different colors were identified, and the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated between these modules and the MPRGs scores to identify the modules most correlated with MPRGs (|cor| > 0.3 and P < 0.05). The key modules exhibiting the highest correlation with the MPRGs scores were selected, and the genes within these modules were considered as key module genes. Key module genes were identified by setting thresholds of Gene Significance (GS) > 0.6 and |Module Membership (MM) > 0.6.

Identification and analysis of candidate genes

Candidate genes were identified by intersecting DEGs, key module genes, and MRGs using the “ggVenndiagram” package (version 0.1.10) [25]. GO and KEGG analyses were then performed to investigate the functions and pathways associated with these candidate genes using the “clusterProfiler” package (version 4.10.0) [26], with a significance threshold of P < 0.05. Additionally, to explore protein interactions among the candidate genes, a PPI network was constructed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) with a confidence score > 0.4. After removing isolated genes, the PPI network was visualized with Cytoscape (version 3.10.2) [27]. Additionally, Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationships among the candidate genes, using |cor| > 0.3 and P < 0.05 as criteria.

Search for the biomarkers

Based on the candidate genes, the LASSO method was applied to identify feature genes. This analysis was performed using the “glmnet” package (version 4.1.4) [28], with 10-fold cross-validation employed to determine the optimal lambda value. Following LASSO, ROC curves for the feature genes were generated using the “pROC” package (version 1.18.0) [29] to assess their diagnostic value for AMI in the GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets. Genes with an AUC greater than 0.7 were selected as diagnostic genes. Subsequently, gene expression analyses of these diagnostic genes were conducted in both datasets (disease vs. control) using the Wilcoxon test. Genes showing significant expression differences (P < 0.05) and consistent trends across both datasets were identified as biomarkers for further investigation.

Function analysis of biomarkers

To assess the function similarity among the biomarkers, “GOSemSim” (version 3.19) [30] was utilized, and the functional similarity scores were calculated. Moreover, functional enrichment analysis was conducted to explore the function pathways of biomarkers. Firstly, in the GSE61144 dataset, differential expression analysis was performed between disease and control samples, the DEGs were ranked by log2FC (from high to low). Meanwhile, the ‘c2.cp.kegg.v2023.1.Hs.symbols’ was downloaded from the MSigDB as the background set. Subsequently, GSEA was performed using the “clusterProfiler” package to enrich the ranked genes within this background gene set, applying a significance threshold of P < 0.05.

Immune infiltration analysis

To investigate immune cell infiltration in both disease and control samples from the GSE61144 dataset, an immune infiltration analysis was conducted. First, the ssGSEA was used to calculate the infiltration scores for 28 immune cell types [31], and samples with P > 0.05 were excluded from further analysis. The Wilcoxon test was then applied to compare the differences in infiltration scores between disease and control samples. Immune cells with significant differences (P < 0.05) were identified as differential immune cells for further investigation. Subsequently, Spearman correlation analysis was performed using the “psych” package (version 2.4.1) [32] to explore the relationships between differential immune cells, as well as between biomarkers and differential immune cells, considering correlations with |cor| > 0.3 and P < 0.05.

Regulatory network analysis

In this study, to explore the molecular regulatory mechanisms of biomarkers, the microRNA database (miRDB, http://mirdb.org) and miRWalk database (http://mirwalk.umm.uni-heidelberg.de/) were used to predict miRNAs targeting these biomarkers. Then, the miRNAs predicted by the two databases were intersected. Based on the overlapping miRNAs, a miRNA-biomarker network was constructed and visualized using Cytoscape. Additionally, the disease signatures database (DSigDB, https://www.dsigdb.org/) was employed to identify drugs that targeted the identified biomarkers. A biomarker-drug network was then created and visualized. Meanwhile, the structures of the predicted drugs were searched from PubChem database.

RT-qPCR

A total of 10 blood samples (5 normal and 5 AMI) were acquired from the clinic in the Ruikang Hospital Affiliated to Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine. All participants provided informed consent, and blood samples were collected immediately upon admission. The study had the approval of the Ruikang Hospital Affiliated to Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine ethics committee (approval number: KY2024-203). Total RNA from the 10 samples were extracted with the TRIzol reagent (Ambion, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Then the RNA concentration was tasted using NanoPhotometer N50. The cDNA was synthetised by reverse-transcribed using the SureScript-First-strand-cDNA-synthesis-kit, and the reverse-transcribed was performed with S1000TM Thermal Cycler (Bio-Rad, USA). The sequences of all primers can be found in Supplementary Table S3. The qPCR assay was performed with CFX Connect Real-time Quantitative Fluorescence PCR Instrument (Bio-Rad, USA) (pre-denaturation at 95℃ for 1 min, denaturation at 95℃ for 20 s, annealing at 55℃ for 20 s, extension at 72℃ for 30 s, a total of 40 cycles). The relative quantification of mRNAs was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. The results from the RT-qPCR were exported to Excel, and then imported into GraphPad Prism 5 for statistical analysis and visualization (P < 0.05).

Statistical analysis

The R (v 4.2.2) was utilized to conduct statistical analysis. Differences analysis between cohorts was executed via the Wilcoxon test (P < 0.05).

Results

Identification of DEGs and key module genes

Through differential expression analysis, a total of 1,759 DEGs were identified. Of these, 966 genes were up-regulated and 793 genes were down-regulated in disease samples (Fig. 1a-b, Supplementary Table S4). Additionally, control samples exhibited a significantly higher MPRGs score compared to control samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1c). In the WGCNA analysis of the GSE61144 dataset, no outlier samples were detected (Fig. 1d). The optimal power value was determined to be 4, as it exceeded the red line indicating an R2 value of 0.85 and the mean connectivity was close to 0 (Fig. 1e). Similar modules were merged from the co-expression matrix, resulting in the identification of 6 gene modules (excluding gray modules for unclassified genes), each represented by a different color (Fig. 1f). The ME turquoise module (cor = 0.92, P = 2 × 10 − 7) showed the strongest correlation with the MPRGs score (Fig. 1g, Supplementary Table S5). Consequently, the ME turquoise module was identified as the key module, from which a total of 8,324 key module genes were screened out. These findings facilitated the subsequent genes screening.

Function exploration of candidate genes

By intersecting the 1,759 DEGs, 8,324 key module genes, and 1,136 MRGs, 26 candidate genes were identified (Fig. 2a). Subsequent functional enrichment analysis revealed that these 26 candidate genes were significantly associated with 337 GO terms [including 237 biological processes (BPs), 36 cellular components (CCs), and 64 molecular functions (MFs)] and 88 KEGG pathways. Notable GO terms included “cellular respiration” (BP), “mitochondrial matrix” (CC), and “active transmembrane transporter activity” (MF), etc. (Fig. 2b). In KEGG pathways, the candidate genes were significantly enriched in pathways such as “Huntington disease,” “non-alcoholic fatty liver disease,” and “prion disease” (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, a PPI network was constructed using these 26 candidate genes, comprising 24 nodes and 46 edges after excluding 2 outlier genes (Fig. 2d). For instance, CYC1 was found to interact closely with MDH2 and MRPL23 within this network. Additionally, correlation analysis indicated that FTH1, SOD2, ACSL1, MCL1, and CASP8 had a significantly positive correlation with each other, while they showed a notably negative correlation with the remaining candidate genes (|cor| > 0.3 and P < 0.05) (Fig. 2e). These findings highlighted candidate genes of AMI and their functional roles, providing insights into the underlying mechanisms of the AMI.

Determination of biomarkers

Based on the 26 candidate genes, 5 feature genes (NDUFS6, APEX1, ECHDC2, PUS1, and NME3) were identified using the LASSO method (lambda min = 0.0043) (Fig. 3a). ROC analysis showed that these 5 genes had AUC values exceeding 0.7 in the GSE61144 and GSE60993 datasets, highlighting their strong diagnostic potential for AMI (Fig. 3b-c). Expression analysis revealed significantly lower levels of APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1 in disease samples compared to controls (P < 0.05) across both datasets (Fig. 3d-e). Consequently, APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1 were identified as biomarkers for AMI. Additionally, RT-qPCR of biomarkers confirmed the expression of APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1 were obviously lower in AMI samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 3f). These findings underscored the potential of these biomarkers in both diagnosing and treating AMI, warranting further investigation.

Disclosure of the biomarker function

Function similarity analysis revealed a strong functional resemblance among the 4 biomarkers, with each scoring above 0.5. Notably, APEX1 exhibited the highest functional similarity score (Fig. 4a). Further enrichment analysis indicated that APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1 were significantly associated with 129, 129, 128, and 127 pathways, respectively. Remarkably, among the top 5 most significant pathways enriched for each biomarker, all 4 biomarkers were co-enriched in “ribosome”, “olfactory transduction”, “Huntington’s disease”, and “lysosome” (Fig. 4b-e). This suggested a shared involvement of these biomarkers in crucial biological processes and pathways.

Exploration the correlation of biomarkers with differential immune cells

The Fig. 5a displays infiltration proportion of 28 immune cell types in disease and control samples. Through Wilcoxon test, 11 immune cell types were found to significantly differ between disease and control samples (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5b). Among them, activated B cells, activated CD8 T cells, effector memory CD4 T cell, effector memory CD8 T cell, T follicular helper cells, and T helper type 17 cells had obviously higher infiltration scores in control samples compare to disease samples (P < 0.05). Furthermore, correlation analysis revealed that the activated CD8 T cell had the highest positive correlation with effector memeory CD8 T cell (cor = 0.8, P < 0.05), and the highest negative correlation with neutrophil (cor = −0.79, P < 0.05). The 4 biomarkers had the most significant positive correlations with activated CD8 T cells and the most negative correlations with neutrophils (|cor| > 0.7, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5c). These findings revealed distinct immune cell infiltration patterns in disease and control samples and biomarker correlations that could offer insights into the underlying mechanisms of the AMI.

Construction regulation networks and prediction drugs

After searching the miRNAs targeting biomarkers from the miRDB and miRWalk database, a total of 36 intersecting miRNAs were predicted, with 7, 18, 5, and 6 miRNAs were predicted to target APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1, respectively. A miRNA-biomarker network was then constructed, consisting of 91 nodes and 87 edges (Fig. 6a). For example, ‘hsa-miR-513b-5p’ targeting ECHDC2, ‘hsa-miR-3160-3p’ targeting PUS1, ‘hsa-miR-6773-5p’ targeting NME3, and ‘hsa-miR-27a-5p’ targeting APEX1. Additionally, 30 drugs targeting these biomarkers were identified from DSigDB, with 13 targeting APEX1, 9 targeting ECHDC2, 11 targeting NME3, and 13 targeting PUS1. A drug-biomarker network was then constructed, comprising 34 nodes and 46 edges (Fig. 6b). Notably, valproic, co-targeting all four biomarkers, emerged as a potential key player in AMI treatment. The structures of these 30 drugs were also examined and displayed (Supplementary Fig. S1). Overall, these analyses provided valuable insights into the miRNA interactions and drug targets related to biomarkers in AMI, highlighting potential therapeutic avenues and informing future research directions in the treatment of AMI.

Discussion

AMI is a life-threatening event with significant morbidity and mortality, often leading to heart failure and arrhythmias. Mitochondria are essential organelles in cells responsible for energy production, metabolic regulation, and the control of apoptosis, playing a crucial role in maintaining cellular function and health [33]. Macrophage polarization refers to the differentiation of macrophages into different functional states, such as M1 (pro-inflammatory) and M2 (anti-inflammatory), in response to various microenvironmental signals [34]. The functional status of mitochondria, including energy metabolism and ROS production, can influence the polarization direction of macrophages, thereby playing a regulatory role in AMI [20].

This study identified four biomarkers, APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3, and PUS1, through bioinformatics analysis and verified by RT-qPCR. They were all highly expressed in the control group but low expressed in the AMI group.

APEX1 is a multifunctional protein-coding gene, whose primary activities include participating in DNA repair, specifically as the major AP endonuclease in human cells. As a crucial DNA repair enzyme, APEX1 helps maintain cellular genomic integrity by repairing oxidatively damaged DNA, thereby protecting cardiomyocyte survival during ischemia-reperfusion injury [35]. Research has shown that APEX1 can localize to mitochondria and repair oxidative damage within mitochondrial DNA, preserving the integrity and function of the mitochondrial genome [36]. Furthermore, inhibition of APEX1 promotes macrophage polarization towards the M2 phenotype and improves blood-brain barrier integrity, suggesting its pivotal role in regulating the immune microenvironment [37]. Additional studies indicate that APEX1 may regulate macrophage polarization through its influence on the NF-κB signaling pathway [38]. Consequently, APEX1 not only exerts protective cytoprotective and DNA repair functions but also facilitates the post-myocardial infarction recovery process by modulating immune responses. This dual regulatory property positions APEX1 as a highly promising therapeutic target for AMI. However, current research remains preliminary. Future investigations are needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms of APEX1 in AMI and explore strategies for optimizing AMI therapeutic approaches through the regulation of its activity.

ECHDC2 (Enoyl-CoA Hydratase Domain-Containing Protein 2), a mitochondrial protein critical for regulating cell death and myocardial injury, maintains cellular bioenergetics and metabolic homeostasis through its essential roles in lipid metabolism and energy production. Proteomic analysis revealed that for AMI, Xueshuantong injection can alleviate myocardial ischemic injury by upregulating the expression of ECHDC2, improving fatty acid metabolic disorders in myocardial cells during the early stage of AMI. This suggests that ECHDC2 may serve as a key target for AMI [39]. ECHDC2, a mitochondrial protein, may regulate cell death and myocardial injury by enhancing branched-chain amino acid (leucine and valine) metabolism, and its high expression increases the susceptibility to myocardial ischemia/reperfusion injury [40]. Multiple studies have linked ECHDC2 to fatty acid metabolism [41–44]. Fatty acids play a critical role in regulating macrophage polarization: saturated fatty acids (e.g., palmitic acid) promote M1 polarization [45, 46], while polyunsaturated fatty acids (e.g., DHA) and n-3 fatty acids (e.g., EPA/DHA) induce M2 polarization [45–47]. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that ECHDC2 may regulate macrophage polarization through its role in fatty acid metabolism, thereby providing a potential link between mitochondrial function and inflammatory regulation in acute myocardial infarction.

The protein encoded by the NME3 gene exhibits nucleoside diphosphate kinase activity, localizes predominantly in the cytoplasm, and participates in cellular processes including apoptosis and nucleotide metabolism. Research demonstrates its critical role in maintaining mitochondrial function: under oxidative stress, NME3 safeguards mitophagy by preserving mitochondrial thiol redox homeostasis. NME3 dysfunction drives the accumulation of defective mitochondria, heightened reactive oxygen species, and mitochondrial DNA damage, underscoring its potential relevance to oxidative stress-mediated disorders [48]. During hypoxia, NME3 governs DRP1-dependent mitophagy by binding phosphatidic acid and preventing DRP1 ubiquitination, a mechanism essential for protection against ischemic injury [49]. Furthermore, under nutrient deprivation, NME3 promotes mitochondrial fusion by mediating PLD6-induced mitochondrial tethering, a process vital for mitochondrial quality control; dysregulation of this pathway is closely associated with metabolic diseases and cellular energy metabolism disorders [50]. Notably, mitochondrial fusion typically correlates with anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization [51, 52], while excessive fission promotes pro-inflammatory M1 polarization [53]. In the present study, NME3 is downregulated in AMI samples. Its functional deficiency directly impairs mitochondrial thiol redox balance and mitophagy, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria that fail to be cleared in a timely manner. This further triggers excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and insufficient ATP synthesis. Mitochondrial ROS are well-recognized as key signaling molecules that activate the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB pathway, inducing macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype [54]. This exacerbates the inflammatory response in the myocardial infarction area, simultaneously inhibiting the tissue repair mediated by M2 macrophages, ultimately aggravating AMI pathological damage and hindering myocardial repair processes [55]. Given the fundamental importance of mitochondrial function in maintaining normal cardiac performance and myocardial protection [56], NME3 may play an important role in the occurrence and development of acute myocardial infarction by regulating mitochondrial dynamic balance (fusion/fission) and macrophage polarization mechanisms.

Pseudouridine synthase 1 (PUS1) plays a pivotal role in both nuclear and mitochondrial compartments by catalyzing the conversion of uridine to pseudouridine in tRNA, which enhances the efficiency of protein synthesis. Mutations in PUS1 are associated with the rare disorder myopathy, lactic acidosis, and sideroblastic anemia (MLASA). A study on two Italian brothers identified a nonsense mutation in PUS1, resulting in combined defects in mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes and reduced mtDNA translation products [57]. Additionally, research on a mouse model of MLASA showed that mutations in PUS1 led to reduced exercise capacity, lower mitochondrial density, and smaller mitochondria, indicating significant alterations in muscle metabolism and oxidative capacity [58]. Overall, these findings indicate that PUS1 plays a crucial role in maintaining the normal function of mitochondria. PUS1 directly influences the synthesis efficiency of key metabolic proteins such as mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes by regulating mitochondrial tRNA modification [59]. PUS1 exhibits a close functional relationship with tRNA modification and regulation [60, 61]. The modification activity of PUS1 directly influences tRNA stability and decoding capacity [62]. Structural biology studies further reveal that PUS1 targets specific uridine residues to its active site by binding to double-stranded RNA regions. While this binding mode shows similarity between tRNA and mRNA substrates, tRNA modification demonstrates greater dependence on tertiary structural features [63, 64]. The tRNA-modifying enzyme Elp3 regulates U34 wobble uridine modification, suppressing the translation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in M1 macrophages while promoting the synthesis of mitochondrial ribosomal proteins and metabolic reprogramming required for M2 polarization [65]. These findings suggest that PUS1, likely through its role in tRNA modification, may regulate macrophage polarization and thereby play a significant role in acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Although these results provide important clues for further investigation, the underlying specific mechanisms still require in-depth exploration and validation. Such efforts would provide new perspectives for future AMI treatment strategies targeting mitochondrial function and inflammatory phenotypes. These findings indicate that PUS1 regulates macrophage polarization through its role in tRNA modification, thereby playing a significant role in AMI pathogenesis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms require further investigation and validation, which will offer new perspectives for developing AMI therapies targeting mitochondrial function and inflammatory phenotypes.

In this study, GSEA analysis identified the pathways related to ribosomes, olfactory transduction, Huntington’s Disease, and lysosomes.

Ribosome plays a vital role in the heart and myocardium. Ribosome biogenesis is involved in the pathological process of various cardiovascular diseases. For instance, during the pathological progression of cardiac hypertrophy, ribosomes provide a material basis for the adaptive growth of cardiomyocytes by upregulating rRNA synthesis rate and enhancing protein translation efficiency. This process is precisely regulated by signaling molecules like UBF (upstream binding factor) and mTOR: as a key regulator of RNA polymerase I transcriptional initiation, UBF dynamically interacts with S6K1 (ribosomal protein S6 kinase 1) to integrate nutritional signals into ribosome biogenesis, sustaining the demand for cell growth and proliferation in this pathological progression [66, 67]. Additionally, the normal assembly and function of mitochondrial ribosomes are crucial for cardiac energy metabolism. However, abnormal RNA processing can disrupt mitochondrial ribosome assembly, impairing the synthesis of respiratory chain proteins. This metabolic derangement and oxidative stress activation ultimately drive cardiomyopathy and heart failure [68]. In myocardial infarction (MI), downregulation of ribosomal proteins L9 (RPL9), rRNA and ribosomal proteins L26(RPL26) is associated with ribosome dysfunction, which can contribute to further damage to the heart [69]. In atherosclerosis, the over-activation of ribosome biogenesis results in abnormal proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, accelerating the progression of the disease. Ribosomal proteins L13 (RPL13) inhibits macrophage-induced inflammation and atherosclerosis by inhibiting the translation of inflammatory genes [70]. Moreover, ribosomal dysfunction impairs the synthesis of anti-apoptotic proteins and mitochondrial respiratory chain proteins in cardiomyocytes, exacerbating post-ischemic cardiomyocyte apoptosis [71], while abnormal ribosomal pathways in macrophages alter the translation efficiency of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines to regulate the inflammatory microenvironment in infarcted areas [72], and altered expression of ribosome-related genes is closely correlated with AMI-related prognostic indicators such as infarct size and cardiac function recovery [73]. Overall, ribosome biogenesis is closely linked to the normal functioning of the heart, and any disturbances in this process can have significant implications for cardiovascular health.

The enrichment of the Huntington’s Disease (HD) pathway observed in our analysis likely reflects shared molecular pathologies rather than a direct comorbidity [74]. The reference gene sets for neurodegenerative diseases like HD are heavily populated by genes encoding mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) complexes and energy metabolism regulators [75]. Given that mitochondrial dysfunction is a hallmark of both neurodegeneration and ischemic cardiomyocyte injury, the significance of this pathway highlights the severe bioenergetic collapse and oxidative stress occurring in AMI. This aligns with our identification of mitochondrial-related biomarkers (ECHDC2, NME3), reinforcing the conclusion that cellular energy failure is a core pathological mechanism in the infarcted myocardium.

Lysosomes play a crucial role in MI. They are essential for autophagy, a pathway that delivers cytoplasmic contents to lysosomes for degradation. Autophagy includes conventional macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy. Macroautophagy involves the sequestration of cellular components by double-membrane vesicles known as autophagosomes, which subsequently deliver to lysosomes for degradation. In contrast, microautophagy entails the direct engulfment of cellular components by lysosomes. Chaperone-mediated autophagy relies on the chaperone protein Hsc70 to transport target proteins to lysosomes for degradation [76]. It was reported that autophagy can mitigate acute MI caused by coronary artery occlusion [77]. However, Doxorubicin inhibits lysosomal acidification and lysosomal function, thereby blocking autophagic flux in cardiomyocytes. The accumulation of autolysosomes leads to increased ROS production and subsequent cardiac damage [78]. Therefore, lysosomal dysfunction may result in the accumulation of autolysosomes due to the blockade of autolysosome degradation in AMI. Mitophagy is a selective form of autophagy that specifically targets mitochondria for degradation and clearance. Upon damage from external stimuli, mitochondria lose their outer membrane potential due to depolarization. An autophagosome subsequently engulfs the damaged mitochondria, creating a mitochondrial autophagosome. Lysosomes fuse with the mitochondrial autophagosome, facilitating the breakdown of mitochondrial material [79]. Mitochondria occupy a crucial position in the bioenergetics of the cardiovascular system, so mitophagy is particularly important for maintaining the homeostasis of the cardiovascular system in both healthy and diseased states [80, 81]. Mitochondria and lysosomes have a close relationship. There are dynamic contact mechanisms between mitochondria and lysosomes. Mitochondria-lysosome contact regulates mitochondrial fission through RAB7 GTP hydrolysis [82]. After MI, abnormal mitochondrial-lysosomal contact occurs. This prolongs excessive metabolic exchange between the two organelles, increases lysosomal osmotic pressure, and leads to lysosomal enlargement, poor acidification, and dysfunction. Ultimately, it blocks the lysosome-dependent degradation of damaged mitochondria during ischemia or prolonged hypoxia. The accumulation of damaged mitochondria results in the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential, excessive production of ROS, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and cardiac dysfunction [83]. Further research is needed to fully understand the complex relationship between lysosome and MI and to develop effective therapeutic strategies based on this knowledge.

Studies have reported that immune cells play a crucial role in the processes of myocardial repair and remodeling after AMI [84, 85]. In this study, we found that 11 types of immune cells showed differences through immune infiltration analysis. We discovered that APEX1, ECHDC2, NME3 and PUS1 were all significantly positively correlated with activated CD8 T cells, and all were significantly negatively correlated with neutrophil. Activated CD8 T cells play a dual role in CVD. They can protect against atherosclerosis by recognizing and killing cells modified by oxLDL, but excessive activation can lead to increased inflammation and plaque instability. For example, in mice models of atherosclerosis, depletion of CD8 T cells reduce atherosclerotic lesions [86]. Neutrophils are also involved in atherosclerosis, accumulating at the lesion site, releasing inflammatory mediators and reactive oxygen species, promoting plaque formation and instability. They can also cause plaque rupture and thrombosis by releasing proteases and interacting with platelets. In CVD, the rapid migration and activation of neutrophils trigger inflammation, further damaging blood vessels [87]. Following MI, neutrophils play dual roles in the heart. They cause damage by releasing ROS, granular components, and pro-inflammatory mediators. They can form neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) and release extracellular vesicles (EVs) to aggravate inflammation and cardiac injury. For example, NETs contribute to thrombosis and activate macrophage NLRP3 inflammasome, while EVs can increase endothelial permeability and stimulate the production of inflammatory mediators. However, neutrophils also have beneficial effects. They phagocytose tissue and cellular debris, promote inflammation resolution through apoptotic neutrophils, induce a pro-reparative macrophage phenotype, and are involved in angiogenesis and scar formation. Additionally, neutrophils can produce specialized pro-resolving mediators to facilitate the resolution of inflammation. They regulate fibroblast functions by upregulating TGF-β1 expression. Overall, neutrophils have both detrimental and beneficial roles in post-MI cardiac remodeling [88].

Alternatively, the identified biomarkers may negatively regulate neutrophil recruitment and activation in the infarcted myocardium.Reduced expression of these biomarkers may weaken their inhibitory effects on neutrophil recruitment and activation, thereby facilitating excessive neutrophil infiltration and inflammatory injury in acute myocardial infarction. Nevertheless, this inference is based on correlative analyses, and the causal relationship warrants further experimental validation.

Drug prediction analysis identified 30 candidate drugs targeting the biomarkers, among which valproic acid (VPA) targets all four biomarkers identified in this study. Bioinformatics prediction suggests that VPA has the potential for drug repurposing in AMI treatment by targeting these key biomarkers, but this hypothesis requires further experimental validation. VPA is a drug with anticonvulsant and mood-stabilizing effects, mainly used to treat epilepsy, bipolar disorder, and other diseases [89]. It can increase the level of gamma-aminobutyric acid in the brain, inhibit neuronal repetitive firing, and also has histone deacetylase inhibitory activity. In terms of the heart or cardiovascular system, VPA has a protective effect on the heart after MI. Firstly, VPA treatment in animal studies has shown a marked reduction in infarct size by approximately 50% and helped maintain cardiac contractile function. This is achieved through the inhibition of histone deacetylation, which in turn reduces oxidative stress, promotes cell survival, and mitigates inflammation and cell death [90]. Secondly, VPA has been associated with a decreased risk of developing heart failure post-MI. Specifically, high doses of VPA (≥ 1000 mg/day) have been linked to a 30% reduction in heart failure risk following a MI event, whereas lower doses did not demonstrate a significant impact [91]. These findings suggest that VPA could have therapeutic potential in managing cardiac outcomes after MI. It is suggested that the protective effect of VPA may exhibit a dose-dependent effect. In animal models of myocardial infarction, VPA also shows protective effects: Intraperitoneal administration of VPA in AMI rats activates the Foxm1 pathway, suppresses oxidative stress and inflammation, and concurrently reduces infarct size while enhancing LVEF [90]; In rabbit AMI models, VPA reverses endothelial dysfunction through antioxidant effects and increases nitric oxide bioavailability [92]; Additionally, direct evidence of interaction between VPA and these four biomarkers has not been found so far. However, the roles of these molecules in key pathological processes such as mitochondrial function and cellular metabolism suggest that they might serve as important targets of VPA.

While this study has yielded significant findings, several limitations must be acknowledged and addressed in future research. First, although we verified the correlation of core genes with AMI using clinical samples, their specific regulatory mechanisms and causal roles remain unconfirmed. The current study relied solely on mRNA expression, lacking corroboration at the protein level or functional validation through gene knockout and overexpression models. Second, the use of peripheral blood samples with low macrophage content, may not fully reflect changes in the local cardiac immune microenvironment. Third, the RT-qPCR validation were constrained by the small sample size, which chould limit our ability to perform detailed stratification by clinical subtypes. Additionally, we excluded a preliminary diagnostic nomogram from this study due to overfitting risks associated with the limited sample size, deferring its rigorous validation to future large-scale, multi-center cohorts. Finally, the optimal diagnostic thresholds for these biomarkers remain undetermined, and the proposed repurposing of valproic acid is currently a theoretical hypothesis based on target prediction, lacking pharmacological verification in cellular or animal models. To address these limitations and enhance study reliability, future plans include: First, to validate protein-level translation, we will perform ELISA and Western Blotting on expanded clinical cohorts, analyzing correlations between biomarker concentrations and clinical severity (e.g., infarct size). Second, to elucidate cellular mechanisms, we will employ gene knockdown/overexpression in macrophage models (e.g., THP-1) to assess effects on polarization, mitochondrial function (ATP, ROS, membrane potential), and inflammatory secretion, while also verifying the pharmacological rescue effects of Valproic acid in vitro. Third, to ensure target specificity and physiological relevance, we will utilize magnetic bead sorting to enrich CD14 + monocytes from clinical samples and establish AMI animal models with targeted gene editing to evaluate impacts on cardiac function and tissue remodeling. Finally, we plan to conduct large-scale, prospective, multi-center clinical studies to determine optimal diagnostic thresholds and validate the prognostic utility of these biomarkers across diverse patient populations.

Conclusion

This study primarily highlighted the identification of four biomarkers associated with mitochondrial function and macrophage polarization, which were validated for their expression in clinical samples through RT-qPCR analysis. Furthermore, functional enrichment analysis, immune infiltration studies, regulatory network construction, and drug prediction analyses were conducted, offering new insights into the diagnosis and treatment of AMI.
