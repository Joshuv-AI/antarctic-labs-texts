Experimental investigation on deformation behavior and failure modes of limestone under coupled effects of water content and end friction
Authors: Peijie Lou, Lichen Sun, Cheng Pan, Bolun Zhang, Yangdong Li, Wenbing Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding the deformation and failure behavior of rock under complex boundary and environmental conditions is critical for the design and stability assessment of geotechnical structures. In particular, the influence of water content and end friction during laboratory testing significantly affects the mechanical properties of rocks. In this study, uniaxial compression tests were conducted on dry and water-saturated limestone specimens under two end conditions: with and without a coupling agent, to investigate the coupled effects of water saturation and end friction on rock deformation and failure behavior. The results indicate that the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS) in the dry state is 84.2% higher than that in the water-saturated state, and the elastic modulus (E) shows an 84.7% increase in the dry state compared to the water-saturated state. The use of a coupling agent was found to enhance overall strain and reduce the extent of end friction effects. Radial shrinkage occurred in the early loading phase near the specimen ends and gradually weakened toward the center. Failure modes varied with test conditions: specimens without vaseline primarily exhibited X-shaped conjugate shear fractures due to strong end restraint, whereas vaseline-coated specimens showed cleavage failure characterized by more pronounced end damage. Water-saturated specimens exhibited more ductile behavior, while dry specimens showed brittle splitting. Fracture observations indicated that early crack formation and stress rebound influenced radial deformation and crack evolution.

Introduction

Limestone, as one of the most widely distributed sedimentary rocks, plays a fundamental role in various engineering fields, including underground excavations, slope stabilization, and reservoir engineering1–3. Understanding its mechanical behavior is crucial for ensuring structural safety and long-term stability in geotechnical applications4. Among the many factors influencing the deformation and failure behavior of limestone, water content and end friction effects are two critical aspects that impact both strength parameters and deformation characteristics5–7. However, traditional studies have often treated these factors in isolation, which may lead to a limited understanding of in-situ rock behavior. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically investigate the coupled effects of water content and end friction to better simulate field conditions and improve predictive models for rock performance.

Numerous experimental and theoretical studies have been carried out to investigate the static mechanical behavior of limestone under different levels of water saturation. For instance, Cherblanc et al.8 reported a significant reduction in compressive and tensile strength with increasing water saturation, attributing this to the weakening of intergranular bonds. Similarly, Da Silva et al.9 focus on studying the poroelastic behavior of a water-saturated limestone. They conducted an experimental investigation to determine a set of poroelastic parameters, such as the Biot coefficient, drained and undrained bulk moduli, and Skempton coefficient, for the Sorcy limestone. On the other hand, dry limestone tends to exhibit more brittle failure characteristics, as discussed by Cheng et al.10. Moreover, Xu et al.11 compared dry and saturated samples under uniaxial compression, showing that water saturation alters both the failure mode and mechanical properties. In addition, recent work by Fan et al.12 highlighted the importance of water-rock interactions at the micro-scale, particularly in pore pressure effects and capillary forces. Overall, these studies provide valuable insights into the individual effects of water content on limestone but often overlook the influence of boundary testing conditions such as end friction.

End friction, introduced by the contact between the rock specimen and loading platens during mechanical testing, can significantly alter the stress distribution within the sample13–15. This frictional constraint may induce lateral confinement, leading to an overestimation of strength and modulus values. While methods such as lubrication and the use of friction-reducing materials have been proposed, the residual frictional effects are still non-negligible, especially under varying moisture conditions. Studies have shown that the interaction between water content and end friction may amplify or mitigate deformation localization and failure modes16. For instance, in saturated specimens, reduced friction may facilitate slip failure, while in dry specimens, higher end restraint may promote axial splitting. Despite these findings, systematic studies on the coupled influence of water content and end friction on limestone behavior remain limited.

Given the complexity of the deformation mechanism of natural rocks, this study deeply explored the influence of the coupling effect of end friction and water content on the mechanical behavior of limestone. It systematically investigated the interdependent effects of water content state and end friction on the static deformation characteristics and failure modes of limestone. The structure of the rest of this article is as follows: Sect. “Experiment set-up” describes the materials and experimental methods. Section “Test results and analyses” presents the results and analysis of deformation behavior under different test conditions. Section “Macro damage characterization” discusses macroscopic damage characterization. Finally, Sect. “Conclusions” summarizes the main findings.

Experiment set-up

Sample Preparation

To achieve homogeneous samples and reliable mechanical property measurements, the limestone specimens underwent systematic preparation with the following key processes:

Specimen preparation

The limestone rock was initially obtained as large blocks, from which cylindrical specimens measuring Φ50 mm × 100 mm were core-drilled, cut, and polished to meet standard testing dimensions. To ensure consistency in physical properties, non-metallic ultrasonic velocity measurements were performed on each specimen to determine the longitudinal wave velocity. Specimens with similar wave velocities were selected to minimize variability in their physical and mechanical behavior. Three parallel specimens were prepared for each group to reduce experimental errors. This procedure is illustrated in Fig. 1(a).

Drying and weighing

The selected limestone specimens were then dried to a constant weight by placing them in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h. The drying process continued until the mass change was less than 0.1%17. Afterward, the specimens were carefully weighed using a high-precision electronic balance to record their initial dry mass. The drying process and the specimens are shown in Fig. 1(b).

Strain gauge installation: strain gauges were mounted on the outer surface of each specimen at three specific axial locations: 1 cm, 2.5 cm, and 5 cm from the upper end face. The placement of the strain gauges was done in a circular pattern around the specimen’s circumference to monitor axial and radial strain distribution. To ensure accurate measurements, the strain gauges were labeled with odd numbers for axial strain and even numbers for radial strain. To protect the strain gauges from potential short-circuiting during the water-saturation process, a layer of soft, transparent silicone rubber was applied over the strain gauges (without affecting their deformation behavior). A waterproof adhesive was then applied over the silicone rubber to seal the strain gauges and their terminals. The final setup of the strain gauges and protective layers is illustrated in Fig. 1(c).

Water saturation treatment

After the strain gauges were securely protected, the specimens underwent water saturation. The waterproof adhesive-cured specimens were placed in a vacuum water-saturation device. The specimens were positioned with their upper end face facing the device’s top, and their strain gauge wires were insulated and taped to the device’s inner wall to prevent water exposure to the wire ends. Distilled water was added to the device, ensuring the specimens were submerged by approximately 4–5 cm. The device was then sealed and subjected to a negative pressure of -0.1 MPa for 2 h to remove any air bubbles. Following this, the specimens were soaked in distilled water for over 48 h to achieve full saturation. The vacuum saturation setup and the immersed specimens are shown in Fig. 1(d).

Experimental procedure

In this test, a uniaxial compression test was conducted on limestone specimens using an RMT-150B testing system. An ST-3 C dynamic and static resistive strain gauge was used to measure strain at designated locations on the specimen. The strain gauges were connected to the data acquisition system via wires, and strain data were recorded at a frequency of 5 Hz. The 1/4 bridge method was employed to ensure measurement accuracy. The specimen was centrally positioned on the machine’s loading platform and subjected to preloading to eliminate initial mechanical slack. The testing system was initiated after completing the preloading phase. The loading rate was set to 0.002 mm/s, as recommended in prior studies18. Simultaneously, the strain measurement system was activated to record deformation data. The layout of the strain gauges is illustrated in Fig. 1(e), and the specimen loading process is illustrated in Fig. 1(f).

Uniaxial compression test data.

As shown in Table 1, the uniaxial compressive strength (UCS), elastic modulus, and deformation modulus (Ed) of the specimens tested under dry conditions were significantly higher than those under water-saturated conditions. The average UCS of the dry group was 143.85 MPa, representing an 84.2% increase over the water-saturated group (78.09 MPa). Under the same moisture conditions, vaseline lubrication caused a noticeable decline in mechanical performance; for example, the UCS of the dry Vaseline group (126.16 MPa) was 21.7% lower than that of the dry non-vaseline group (161.54 MPa). This deterioration can be attributed to two primary mechanisms19,20. First, water–rock interactions may lead to the dissolution of cementing agents within the rock matrix, thereby weakening its cohesive strength. Second, vaseline reduces the friction coefficient at the specimen ends, thereby weakening radial confinement. This alters the boundary conditions and brings the stress state closer to ideal uniaxial compression, ultimately resulting in a reduction in the apparent strength.

Characterization of stress-strain curves

The stress-strain curves of four groups of specimens obtained from uniaxial compression tests are shown in Fig. 2. Compared with the dry group, the water-saturated specimens exhibit a tendency of increased peak strain and total strain. This behavior is primarily attributed to the presence of water, which reduces the interparticle cohesion within the material, making it more susceptible to relative sliding and deformation under loading. Thus, larger strain can be accumulated before reaching the peak strength. In contrast, under dry conditions (Fig. 2(a)), pores and microcracks are filled with air, which promotes stress concentration during loading and accelerates crack propagation. This leads to material failure at relatively lower strain levels. For the water-saturated state (Fig. 2(b)), water fills these voids, alleviating energy release at the crack tips and enabling the specimens to sustain greater overall deformation before failure. Furthermore, under the same moisture condition, vaseline-treated specimens show more pronounced strain increases, indicating that the lubrication effect further weakens end constraints and facilitates axial deformation.

Deformation characterization

Axial and radial microstrain variation curve

To investigate the compressive deformation characteristics of rocks under different water saturation states and end friction conditions, axial and radial microstrain responses of typical specimens A1, B2, C2, and D3 were analyzed. It should be noted that the vertical axis of radial microstrain was defined such that compression is positive and tension is negative. Figure 3 presents the time-dependent evolution of axial and radial microstrain recorded at 1 cm, 2.5 cm, and 5 cm below the top end of each specimen, along with the corresponding axial stress curves. The analysis reveals evident strain jumps in all specimens, characterized by abrupt shifts, referred to as “jump points”, in the strain curves at specific time intervals, as marked by ellipses in the figure. Simultaneously, fluctuations in the stress–time curves suggest the occurrence of local structural failure within the specimen. These phenomena are likely due to local stresses exceeding the rock’s strength threshold, resulting in rapid failure of structural units, accompanied by stress release and energy redistribution. During this process, part of the mechanical energy supplied by the testing machine is dissipated as frictional heat and energy for crack propagation, leading to instantaneous strain surges in localized areas21–23. Variations in the magnitude and duration of strain jumps across different specimens and measuring points may result from differences in crack size, spatial distribution, and the degree of stress concentration. In particular, under water-saturated conditions, the mechanical stability of the rock structure is further reduced, making stress-induced rupture more likely. Consequently, strain jumps are more frequent and pronounced in Figs. 3(c) and 3(d).

To further analyze the influence of end effects on microstrain, a comparison of the strain curves at various measurement points in Fig. 3 was conducted. Under dry conditions, the radial microstrain at the 1 cm location of specimen A1 in Fig. 3(a) was significantly lower than that of specimen B2 at the same location in Fig. 3(b), indicating that A1 experienced a stronger end-confining effect. This constraint limited plastic deformation near the specimen’s end, thereby hindering radial strain release. Although A1 exhibited abnormal radial strain fluctuations at the 2.5 cm position, the overall trend suggests that the specimen remained affected by end friction constraints throughout the loading process. Comparing Figs. 3(c) and 3(d), the radial strain at the 1 cm position of specimen D3 under saturated conditions was greater than that of C2. In the pre-peak stage, the differences in radial strain among the three measurement points in D3 were minimal, suggesting lower end friction and more uniform deformation transfer. This aligns with the characteristics of a weakened end effect, where radial stress distribution becomes more uniform. In the post-peak stage, specimen D3 exhibited a pronounced secondary rebound behavior: axial strain at 5 cm rapidly transitioned from compression to tension, strain at 2.5 cm dropped sharply, while strain at 1 cm continued to increase. This complex response may result from a combination of factors. First, elastic modulus heterogeneity across different locations in the rock may contribute. Second, the increased ductility of saturated limestone facilitates stress release and structural rebound after peak stress. Overall, the deformation magnitude of the four groups generally followed the trend 5 cm > 2.5 cm > 1 cm, indicating that the inhibitory effect of the specimen ends on deformation intensifies with proximity to the loading end. This reflects a clear spatial gradient in the end effect. Although vaseline was applied to reduce end friction, the lubrication treatment could only partially mitigate the end effect and was insufficient to eliminate it entirely.

To more intuitively analyze the influence of water saturation and end effects on radial microstrain at different positions across variable groups, the radial microstrain–time curves at identical measurement positions for specimens A1, B2, C2, and D3 were selected for comparative analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The curves indicate that all specimens exhibit an evolution trend of initial compression followed by tension, suggesting that during the early loading stage, a compaction process of pores and microcracks likely occurred. This compaction led to the closure of primary voids and fractures, resulting in an apparent volumetric reduction and a radial displacement trend of contraction rather than expansion. This phenomenon was particularly pronounced at the 1 cm position, where specimen B2 showed the most significant contraction, followed by C2, with D3 exhibiting a relatively smaller degree. In contrast, radial contraction at the 2.5 cm position was generally weaker across all specimens, while at 5 cm, slight radial expansion rather than contraction was more commonly observed. This variation trend is likely attributable to the end effect. During the initial loading phase, the ends of the specimen were constrained by the loading platens, generating a local circumferential stress field that enhanced the closure of pores and microcracks. As the measurement position moved farther from the specimen end, this effect gradually diminished, and the radial strain increasingly reflected the intrinsic material response.

In Fig. 4(a), the radial microstrain curve at the 1 cm position exhibits a generally downward-opening semi-parabolic shape. A sudden and rapid expansion was observed in the later stage for specimen A1, which is likely associated with the peak stress point occurring at 329.5 s. Given the pronounced brittle behavior of this specimen, the sharp post-peak failure led to an abrupt increase in radial expansion. Therefore, data from this stage were excluded from subsequent comparative analysis. Further comparison of the radial strain curves in Fig. 4(a) between A1 and B2, as well as C2 and D3, reveals that at the location near the specimen end (1 cm), the radial microstrain of the vaseline-treated specimens was significantly greater than that of the untreated ones. This indicates that the application of Vaseline effectively weakens end constraints, thereby enhancing the sensitivity of local deformation responses. In addition, as shown in Figs. 4(b) and 4(c), at the 2.5 cm and 5 cm positions, the radial strain generally increased with the distance from the specimen end. This trend further supports the consistent attenuation of end effects as the measurement location moves away from the loading interface.

Transient radial-to-axial ratio

To reflect the relative variation trends of axial and radial deformation per second at different positions during uniaxial compression, this study defines the ratio of radial to axial microstrain per second as the transient radial-to-axial ratio. Curve fitting was performed based on this parameter, and the results are presented in Fig. 5.

Fig. 5Transient diameter-axis ratio (a) Dry without vaseline A1. (b) Dry with vaseline B2. (c) Water-saturated without vaseline C2. (d) Water-saturated with vaseline D3.

Transient diameter-axis ratio (a) Dry without vaseline A1. (b) Dry with vaseline B2. (c) Water-saturated without vaseline C2. (d) Water-saturated with vaseline D3.

At the initial loading stage, the external load was relatively low, and the internal structural evolution of the rock was slow. Minor dislocation and rotation occurred among particles, along with the closure of pores and microcracks, resulting in predominantly negative radial strain values. This trend was particularly evident at measurement points near the specimen ends, exhibiting significant radial contraction. However, since the transient radial-to-axial ratios during this stage often deviated from the typical strain evolution pattern, the corresponding data were excluded from the statistical analysis.

In the dry group, the transient radial-to-axial ratio at the 2.5 cm position of specimen A1 exhibited significant fluctuations between 100 and 150 s, with a rapid rise followed by a sharp drop. The ratio peaked at 0.74 at 120 s before suddenly decreasing to 0.18. A second sharp increase was observed after 300 s. According to the fracture characteristics analyzed in Sect. 4.3, this behavior may be attributed to localized crack damage occurring at the strain gauge position during this stage, which caused a sudden increase in radial strain. However, the crack was not fully developed in the early phase. As loading continued, axial deformation intensified, and the ratio temporarily returned to a relatively stable state. During this process, the formation of a plastic zone at the crack tip led to local stress concentration, accelerating crack propagation and eventual penetration, which significantly enhanced lateral deformation and caused another abrupt shift in the transient radial-to-axial ratio. In contrast, specimen B2, with vaseline applied as a coupling agent, showed reduced constraint from end friction, resulting in a more consistent trend of transient radial-to-axial ratios across all measurement points. The values remained generally stable at approximately 0.3, as shown in Fig. 5(b), further indicating that the coupling agent can improve load uniformity and reduce the interference of end effects on deformation distribution. In the water-saturated group, the transient radial-to-axial ratio at the 1 cm position of specimen C2 was relatively stable and showed limited variation, as shown in Fig. 5(c). This was likely influenced by both end friction and the initial radial contraction. For specimen D3, the transient ratio curve between 100 and 150 s showed signs of deformation slowing, which may indicate that a degree of axial failure had already occurred internally, resulting in a sudden increase in axial displacement. Notably, after 200 s, the transient ratio at the 1 cm position exceeded 0.5, indicating a reduction in axial compression rate and an increase in radial deformation. In contrast, the ratios at 2.5 cm and 5 cm gradually stabilized around 0.2, suggesting that regions further from the ends experienced more moderate deformation, as shown in Fig. 5(d).

Macro damage characterization

To investigate the influence of water–rock interaction and end friction effects on the failure morphology of limestone under uniaxial compression, a comparative analysis of the macroscopic failure characteristics of specimens from each variable group was conducted. The analysis focused on three aspects: lateral surface failure, top-end surface failure, and fracture features in regions instrumented with strain gauges.

Lateral surface damage characterization

The post-failure lateral surface morphology of specimens in the dry group is shown in Fig. 6. In the dry, no-vaseline group, specimens typically exhibited pronounced inward deformation in the midsection, indicating that lateral deformation was constrained by end friction, which inhibited the propagation of fractures in the vertical loading direction (Fig. 6a). A characteristic feature observed was the presence of X-shaped conjugate shear fractures, suggesting that shear failure was dominant. Due to the influence of end effects, the radial stress distribution in this group was uneven, leading to stress concentration in the middle section and a high likelihood of localized deformation and failure. In contrast, in the dry vaseline group, where end friction was significantly reduced, the failure morphology appeared more uniform, with little visible inward deformation. The specimens exhibited large-scale spalling and through-going splitting cracks, indicative of tensile failure mechanisms and a more pronounced brittle behavior (Fig. 6b).

The failure morphology of the water-saturated group is presented in Fig. 7. Due to the application of waterproof adhesive and the attachment of strain gauge wires on the specimen surfaces, a connective layer was formed, which helped to partially prevent surface spalling. However, as the adhesive is relatively soft, it had no significant influence on crack propagation or internal failure. Figure 7(a) shows the failure characteristics of the saturated specimens without vaseline. After the removal of major loose rock fragments, a distinct concave deformation appeared in the central region, along with the formation of X-shaped conjugate shear bands. In the side view, a through-going crack extended vertically through the midsection, forming a semicircular bulge along its path. The crack tips deviated outward, suggesting that the crack propagation path was governed by a radial stress gradient-likely caused by the uneven stress distribution resulting from end friction. In contrast, the saturated specimen with vaseline, shown in Fig. 7(b), exhibited a more uniform failure morphology after surface debris was removed, with comparable damage observed in both the central and end regions. The reduced coefficient of friction between the rock and the loading platen due to vaseline lubrication mitigated the end constraint effect, leading to a more homogeneous radial stress distribution. In the side view, distinct splitting-shear mixed-mode fractures were observed, indicating that local tensile failure played a dominant role in the failure mechanism.

Characterization of damage to the upper face

To further explore the effects of water saturation and end friction on the failure characteristics of specimen end faces, a comparative analysis was conducted on the top surface morphology of selected representative specimens, as shown in Fig. 8. The results indicate that the specimens coated with vaseline (B3 and D2) exhibited more pronounced damage on the upper end face than those without vaseline (A1 and C2). This observation may be attributed to the reduction in end friction, which led to a more significant concentration of local stress near the loading surface and subsequently triggered more severe failure in this region. End friction plays a crucial role in regulating the accumulation and release of strain energy within the rock. Under high-friction conditions, strain energy tends to be more uniformly distributed along the axial direction of the specimen. When friction is reduced, strain localization becomes more pronounced, particularly near the loading end, causing failure energy to be concentrated and released in the upper end face region, thereby exacerbating local damage. Under high friction, enhanced shear constraint facilitates the propagation of cracks in inclined or wing-shaped patterns toward the specimen center, leading to a mixed tensile–shear failure mode. In contrast, the presence of vaseline reduces shear confinement, allowing cracks to propagate more readily in the vertical direction along the maximum principal stress, resulting in tensile splitting that typically originates at the top surface. Compared with the dry vaseline group, the saturated vaseline specimens exhibited relatively milder end-face failure. This is likely due to the weakening effect of water–rock interaction, which reduces the overall strength and brittleness of the limestone. As a result, the specimen is more susceptible to uniform deformation and distributed crack propagation during loading, thereby diminishing the influence of localized stress concentration at the end face on the overall failure pattern.

Fracture characteristics in strain gauge regions

Based on the transient radial-to-axial strain ratio discussed in Sect. 3.3.1, the failure path in the strain gauge bonding region of representative specimens was analyzed to investigate the macroscopic causes of localized strain anomalies.

Figure 9 presents the fracture morphology near the 2.5 cm position of specimen A1. In the left image, a major fracture clearly cuts through the area where the axial strain gauge was installed. Upon closer examination, the thickness of the rock mass on the left side of the fracture increases progressively from top to bottom, suggesting a non-uniform radial stress distribution in this region. After removing the fragmented rock in the right image, distinct traces of radial splitting failure are visible. Further observation reveals that the fracture path consists of shear cracks with increasing inclination from the specimen end toward the center, indicating that stress concentration in this area induced early crack nucleation. During the pre-peak phase, microcracks coalesced and propagated radially, leading to a rapid increase in radial strain over a short period. This behavior corresponds well with the deformation characteristics observed in specimen A1 during 100–150 s, as shown in Fig. 4(b). According to rock fracture mechanics, stress concentration is highest at the crack tip, driving further crack growth until coalescence. This explains the sharp rise in radial microstrain observed in specimen A1 after 300 s. The inferred crack evolution path is illustrated in Fig. 10.

Figure 11 illustrates the fracture morphologies corresponding to the strain gauge locations at three positions on specimen D3. In the magnified views from the end toward the middle, no apparent surface cracks are observed around the strain gauge closest to the loading end. A minor axial crack appears beneath the gauge at the 2.5 cm position, while two prominent macrocracks intersect the radial strain gauge at the mid-height location. During the test, this specimen was not subjected to complete crushing in the post-peak stage, allowing it to enter a stress rebound phase during which the rock retained a certain level of structural integrity. As shown in Fig. 4(d), the specimen continued to absorb external energy after the peak load. Influenced by this post-peak rebound, some elastic deformation persisted, leading to a continued but slow increase in axial and radial displacement near the 1 cm position. Due to the abrupt transition from compression to tension in the specimen’s central zone, the internal stress distribution may have rapidly changed. The corresponding elastic energy was likely released in the form of surface energy needed for microcrack formation. At the mid-height region, where the localized stress concentration exceeded the critical strength, numerous microcracks nucleated and eventually coalesced into macrocracks propagating outward toward both ends. However, since the specimen had not undergone complete failure at that time, the number of developed cracks remained limited, and the overall damage was relatively mild.

Conclusions

(1) Under the coupled influence of water saturation and end effects, the uniaxial compressive strength, elastic modulus, and deformation modulus of saturated limestone are significantly lower than those of dry limestone. The combined lubrication effect of water and the Vaseline coupling agent intensifies the deformation behavior of the rock.

(2) The presence of the coupling agent weakens the end effect, particularly influencing radial deformation near the specimen ends. At the initial loading stage, all specimens exhibited radial contraction, which gradually diminished from the ends toward the center. With the reduction of end friction constraints, the transient radial-axial strain ratios at different positions along the specimen tended to converge.

(3) Variations in end constraints result in distinct macroscopic failure modes. Specimens without Vaseline exhibited typical X-shaped conjugate shear fractures due to strong end friction confinement, where non-uniform radial stress distribution caused by constrained ends significantly influenced the propagation paths of macroscopic cracks. In contrast, Vaseline-lubricated specimens were dominated by splitting failure, with more severe damage observed on the end surfaces.

(4) Differences in water content significantly affect the ductile-brittle transition behavior of limestone. Dry limestone exhibits higher brittleness, with splitting fractures as the dominant failure mode. Saturated limestone, however, undergoes water-induced weakening, leading to a transition toward a mixed splitting–shear fracture pattern.
