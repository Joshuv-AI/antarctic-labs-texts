Prevalence of hypertension and associated factors among sanitation workers: a community based cross-sectional survey in five urban regions of Nepal
Authors: Samip Khatri, Gita Kumari Shah, Sunita Dhungel, Sweta Koirala, Anshu Varma, Kurt Rasmussen, Erik Jørs, Dinesh Neupane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSanitation workers represent a marginalized occupational group in Nepal often exposed to unsafe working conditions, physically demanding tasks, and occupational hazards. Despite their vulnerability, national surveys such as the Nepal Demographic Health Survey and the STEPwise Approach to surveillance, largely overlook this population. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of hypertension and associated factors among sanitation workers of Nepal.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted in five urban regions of Nepal. A total of 790 sanitation workers were enrolled using nonprobability total enumerative sampling through health camps organized in coordination with metropolitan/sub-metropolitan authorities. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a pre-coded, structured questionnaire, alongside clinical assessments including blood pressure, body mass index, and random blood sugar. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify the factors associated with hypertension.ResultsThe overall prevalence of hypertension among sanitation workers was 30% (95% CI: 26.9–33.3), which is higher than the national estimates as reported by the Nepal Demographic Health Survey 2022 (20%) and the STEPwise Approach to Surveillance 2019 (24.5%). It was found that age (55–75 years) (AOR:7.46, 95% CI: 3.74–14.86), sex (male) (AOR:1.78, 95% CI:1.13–2.80), metropolitan/sub-metropolitan (residing in Janakpur) (AOR: 2.34, 95% CI: 1.53–3.57), alcohol consumption (daily) (AOR: 3.29, 95% CI: 2.02–5.36) and body mass index (obese) (AOR: 3.35, 95% CI: 2.09-5.35) were significantly associated with hypertension.ConclusionSanitation workers in Nepal bear a disproportionately high burden of hypertension, driven by both lifestyle and occupational factors. These findings call for targeted health interventions, routine health screening, and occupational health programs integrated into local health systems to address hypertension among this underserved workforce.Clinical trial numberNot Applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05540-2.Keywords: Hypertension, Non-Communicable diseases, Occupational health, Sanitation workers

Background

Globally, an estimated 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 years are affected by hypertension, with the majority living in low- and middle-income countries. Among them, approximately 46% are unaware of their condition, and fewer than half have received a diagnosis or appropriate treatment [1]. Hence, hypertension remains a leading contributor to premature death.

In South Asia, particularly in Nepal, the rising burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) such as hypertension has become a significant public health challenge specifically in the age group 15–69 years due to low awareness and diagnosis rates, limited treatment coverage, poor medication compliance, reliance on private healthcare, regional disparities, and financial barriers to care [2].

National level surveys such as the Nepal Demographic Health Survey (NDHS) 2022 (15 years and above) and the STEPwise Approach to Surveillance (STEPS) 2019 estimate adult hypertension prevalence at around 20–24%, with notable variations by age, sex and region [2–4]. However, these surveys mainly reflect general population trends [3, 5] and overlook high risk specific occupational groups, who may face elevated health risks due to the nature of their work and socioeconomic conditions.

Sanitation workers represent one such vulnerable group particularly waste pickers, sweepers and drivers of waste transporting vehicles, often exposed to physically demanding labor, hazardous environments, low income, and limited access to healthcare services which increase their likelihood of NCDs, including hypertension. Physically demanding jobs can have complex effects on the risk of NCDs. While general physical activity, such as leisure-time exercise is widely recognized for its protective effect against NCDs, occupational physical activity involving waste picking, sweeping, or driving waste vehicles are often carried out in hazardous, unsanitary conditions and without sufficient rest. These factors can contribute to physical strain and chronic stress, ultimately increasing the risk of NCDs despite the active nature of the work [6, 7]. These occupational and socioeconomic factors may contribute to hypertension through several interconnected pathways. Chronic physical and psychosocial stress [8–10], exposure to unsanitary and hazardous environments [11, 12], irregular healthcare access [13], and behavioral factors such as unhealthy dietary patterns characterized by high salt intake [14], low consumption of fruits and vegetables [13, 14]and substance use [12, 13], all contribute to a higher burden of hypertension among individuals in such high risk occupations.

Several research findings highlight that occupational exposures and work conditions jointly contribute to hypertension in vulnerable groups like sanitation workers due to pollution exposure, psychosocial stress, unhealthy behaviors and physically demanding work. The prevalence of hypertension among the sanitation workers or similar occupational group was found to be quite different in the studies done in low- and middle-income countries especially Africa and India, where it can be as low as 22% [15] and as high as 45% [16]. Despite this growing evidence from India and Africa, population specific data on hypertension among sanitation workers in Nepal largely remain absent, and the national level surveys do not provide disaggregated estimates for this group. Hence, by providing a multi-site assessment across five urban regions and comparing findings with national population estimates, this study offers population specific evidence on the burden of hypertension. A study on workers involved in labor-intensive occupations reported high levels of heat stress, with more than half showing hypertension, particularly among afternoon shift workers and those with longer workweeks [17, 18]. Furthermore, the literature revealed that high temperatures significantly affect the mean temperature (MST), systolic blood pressure (SBP), and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of sanitation workers, with these three indicators contributing the largest proportion to the overall impact [19]. With climate change expected to increase the frequency and intensity of extreme heat, workplace heat exposure is becoming a growing challenge, posing risks of heat-related illnesses contributing to higher risk of hypertension,, particularly among workers in low- and middle-income countries [20].

Despite the critical role of sanitation workers in maintaining public health and hygiene in urban settings, this occupational group remains understudied [21, 22]. Additionally existing literature indicates that sanitation workers face a range of occupational health problems such as musculoskeletal disorders, respiratory problems, gastrointestinal problems, kidney problems, diabetes mellitus, and hypertension, as well as a range of occupational accidents. Many of these conditions are linked to prolonged exposure to unsanitary conditions at open-air dumpsites [13, 16], lack of appropriate protective gears [15, 16] including poor dietary practice [14], substance abuse (alcohol and tobacco) [4, 8], psychosocial stress [9, 10], and poor socio-economic status [8, 23, 24]. Sanitation workers, predominantly middle-aged, illiterate, and overweight women with children endure prolonged exposure to hazardous open-dump conditions, with precarious socioeconomic and health vulnerabilities [8, 25]. Middle-aged workers face increased health risks due to cumulative occupational exposures and biological aging [26], while low literacy limits safety awareness and hazard recognition thereby increasing injury risk [27, 28]. Additionally, psychosocial stress from family responsibilities and overweight status compound susceptibility to NCDs in hazardous work environments [29–31]. Furthermore, studies have revealed that sanitation workers involved in rag picking and landfill waste management are at increased risk of cardiovascular diseases including higher prevalence of hypertension [11, 32]. These risks are further aggravated by several socioeconomic and demographic characteristics such as age, educational background, alcohol consumption, perception of work demands, working hours, and physical work stress [8, 22, 33]. Conceptually, hypertension among sanitation workers may arise from the interaction of occupational and socioeconomic conditions [8] with behavioral and metabolic risk factors. Physically demanding work [34], irregular work schedules, and socioeconomic vulnerability may contribute to psychosocial stress [10] and influence health-related behaviors such as alcohol consumption, tobacco use [4, 8], and dietary practices [14], which in turn are established risk factors for hypertension. Guided by this framework, the present study examines the prevalence of hypertension and its association with socio-demographic, occupational, and lifestyle-related factors among sanitation workers. The National Health Policy 2019 and the Multisectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs 2021–2025 in Nepal suggests the prevention and control of NCDs with a comprehensive integrated health systems approach from individual to community level [35, 36]. As a signatory to global commitments to the World Health Organization and the United Nations, Nepal has committed to achieving its reduction of 25% and 30% by 2025 and 2030, respectively [36]. Despite the provisions under the Local Governance Act 2017, mandating local government to manage the solid waste, it does not specifically recognize and protect the sanitation workers who carry out this vital and high-risk labor [37]. No previous study in Nepal has specifically assessed hypertension prevalence among sanitation workers, a vulnerable yet overlooked occupational group [13, 38].

Sanitation workers from low- and middle-income countries like Nepal face elevated health risks due to unregulated and weakly enforced labor policies, including limited occupational health and safety measures and the lack of routine health screening [21, 39, 40]. These systemic gaps contribute to under recognition of chronic conditions such as hypertension among sanitation workers, despite their exposure to sustained physical, environmental, and psychosocial stressors. Hence, it is essential to recognize their rights, provide them with support, and improve their working conditions, which are crucial steps towards achieving the Sustainable Development Goal 8 of ‘Decent work and Economic growth’ [21]. In the absence of population specific evidence, the hypertension risk among sanitation workers remains poorly characterized in Nepal, limiting the ability of health systems and local governments to design targeted preventive interventions.

This study aims to investigate the prevalence of hypertension and associated factors among sanitation workers across five urban regions of Nepal. By comparing the findings with existing national-level data, this study seeks to highlight potential disparities and inform targeted public health interventions. The burden of hypertension among sanitation workers compared with national estimates and key socio-demographic and lifestyle factors provide a specific evidence base for targeted interventions. Hence, the finding may contribute to evidence-based policy making to improve the prevention and overall control of hypertension among sanitation workers of Nepal.

Methods

Study design

A community-based cross-sectional survey was conducted to assess hypertension and identify the associated factors among sanitation workers.

Settings of the study

The data collection was initiated from February 2024 to July 2024 in five urban regions of Nepal i.e., Biratnagar (Koshi province), Janakpur (Madesh province), Nepalgunj (Lumbini province), Pokhara (Gandaki province), and Bharatpur (Bagmati province). These metropolitan/sub-metropolitan areas were selected to represent diverse urban settings across five out of the seven provinces. In each site, data collection took place during a free health camp organized in coordination with the respective local authorities.

Study participants

Sanitation workers from five urban regions employed by metropolitan/sub-metropolitan or private companies attending the health camp were eligible for the study. The inclusion criteria encompassed individuals engaged in field-based solid waste management within the selected metropolitan/sub-metropolitan, regardless of their duration of employment. Workers who were absent during the free health camp or severely ill were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sampling technique began with purposive selection of provinces representing five out of seven provinces of Nepal, based on urbanization and sanitation workforce presence [37, 41]. Within these provinces, specific urban regions (metropolitan/sub-metropolitan areas) i.e., Biratnagar, Janakpur, Nepalgunj, Pokhara, and Bharatpur were selected considering criteria such as solid waste generation, population density, and number of sanitation workers [37]. However, Kathmandu and Lalitpur were excluded despite their high levels of waste generation. An orientation was conducted in each of the metropolitan/sub-metropolitan prior to conducting the health camp, inviting the local health officials and sanitation workers. The attendance sheet was used as a sampling frame for the sanitation workers. The total number of the sanitation worker from each municipality were Bharatpur (132), Biratnagar (316), Janakpur (180), Nepalgunj (156), and Pokhara (134). A total of 790 sanitation workers engaged in field-based solid waste management in these regions and attending the free health camps were recruited using non-probability total enumerative sampling. This sampling approach ensured representation across multiple provincial urban settings while targeting the relevant worker population available during the health camps. A one-day health camp was organized, in each metropolitan/sub-metropolitan in coordination with the local government authorities, and prior information was delivered to invite the sanitation workers to attend the free health camp.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire via face-to-face interview. The questionnaire was designed to capture socio-demographic information and details of heat stress symptoms. Clinical parameters such as blood pressure, random blood sugar levels, and anthropometric measurements, were collected during the medical examination at health camps. Research assistants with a health education background were trained as data collectors for both the survey and medical examination during a health camp. Blood pressure was measured using a digitally validated Omron device (model HEM-7361T-EBK) by research assistants. Three separate readings (both systolic and diastolic readings) were recorded at intervals of at least 60 s [42], for the calculation of average blood pressure at any time on the day attending a health camp. The study participants were instructed to be seated and rest for about 5 min and then requested to sit on a chair with an upright posture. The cuff was placed on the upper part of the arm, preferably the left, with the arm positioned at the heart level and supported on a table to ensure accuracy [42].

The random blood sugar levels of sanitation workers from Nepalgunj and Biratnagar, were assessed using a validated BeneSphera semi-automated analyzer [43]. Blood samples were collected using vacutainer tubes by experienced laboratory technicians. For BMI calculation, all height and weight measuring instruments (Seca 284) were checked and recalibrated prior to use. The final measurement for each anthropometric parameter was recorded as the mean of three measurements. Study participants were asked to remove excess clothing such as jackets, scarves, and other accessories before the weight measurement. For height measurement, participants were instructed to stand upright with their feet together, knees straight, and head positioned to look straight ahead on a horizontal plane. Data were entered into REDCap using a mobile application. The questionnaire was developed by the research team in alignment with study objectives, extensive literature reviews, and expert consultation (Additional file 1). The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, then translated into Nepali. The questionnaire and medical examination data collection tools were both pretested on approximately 5% of similar participants in Bharatpur sub-metropolitan to ensure consistency and suitability for the field-level data collection. The face validity of the tool was assessed by ensuring that items aligned with study objectives and were easily comprehensible to study participants. Construct validity was ensured by specifying the operational definition for each of the variables as described in the “Study variables” section. Inclusion of study participants from diverse geographical location (metropolitan/sub-metropolitan areas of different provinces), sex and types of sanitation workers ensured external validity of the study [44]. Prior to each health camp, coordination with municipalities and private companies was carried out to disseminate invitations across all categories of sanitation workers.

Study variables

The operational definitions of each of the study variables has been described (Additional file 2).

Dependent variable

The outcome variable for the study is hypertension. The blood pressure reading was based on the systolic and diastolic readings as per the International Society for Hypertension (ISH), which were categorized as non-hypertensive (systolic < 140 or diastolic < 90) and hypertensive (systolic > = 140 or diastolic > = 90). Additionally, for descriptive reporting, blood pressure levels were further categorized into six levels to capture the distribution and severity: optimal (SBP < 120 and DBP < 80), normal (SBP 120–129 or DBP 80–84), high-normal (SBP 130–139 or DBP 85–89), grade 1 hypertension (SBP 140–159 or DBP 90–99), grade 2 hypertension (SBP 160–179 or DBP 100–109), and grade 3 hypertension (SBP ≥ 180 or DBP ≥ 110) [3, 45].

Independent variables

The explanatory variable included socio-demographic factors (age, sex, municipality, ethnicity, marital status, level of education, living circumstances), occupation related factors (types of sanitation workers, employment condition, workdays per week, workhours per day, work experience and heat stress related variables), and life style and health related factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, sniffing glue, chewing tobacco, body mass index and random blood sugar level). The study constituted seven numerical variables: age, workdays per week, workhours per day, work experience (duration in years), BMI, random blood sugar level, and blood pressure readings (systolic and diastolic readings). BMI, random blood sugar, and blood pressure readings were categorized based on existing standards such as national protocols, international guidelines, and peer-reviewed journal articles [46]. Age has been categorized into three levels with equally spaced intervals, 15–34 years, 35–54 years, and 55–75 years. Based on Health Management Information System (HMIS) caste/ ethnic group code, ethnicity in our study was categorized as upper caste (Brahmin and Chhetri), Dalit, Madhesi, Janajati, Muslim and Others [47]. Sanitation workers were categorized as sweepers, waste collectors, transporters, pickers of dumping site and mixed –type workers. Sweepers clean road, streets and public spaces manually [48].Waste collectors collect waste manually from households, industries, hotels, hospitals, streets, market, public bins and thus load in waste transporting vehicles and unload in dumping sites [48]. Transporters are responsible for transporting collected solid waste from producing place to dumping site [49]. Pickers of dumping sites sort out the reusable and recyclable materials in dumping site and sell it to the junk scrap (‘Kawadi’) [50]. Mixed type sanitation workers included those who were directly or indirectly responsible for sanitation work such as billing staff, gardener, painter, manager/supervisor etc. Heat stress in our study was based on the self-reported experience of discomfort due to heat exposure during work. Additional dimensions of heat stress in this study included its frequency, symptoms experienced, onset of symptoms, and drinking water access at the workplace. The BMI was calculated as weight (kilogram) divided by height (squared meter), and categorized as underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2), normal (BMI 18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (BMI 23.0–27.4 kg/m2) and obese (BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m2) based on the Asian-specific BMI cutoff values [51]. Random blood sugar level greater or equal to 200 mg/dl was interpreted as an indication of a diagnosis with diabetes mellitus in the presence of symptoms [52, 53].

Statistical analysis

Each item in the questionnaire was pre-coded with numeric values to facilitate and simplify the data entry process. Data were first reviewed for completeness and accuracy following import from REDCap software into StataSE 18 (StataCorp LP, USA). Missing values were verified and corrected where possible, and remaining missing data were excluded from both descriptive and inferential analyses. An initial exploratory data analysis (EDA) was conducted to understand the variable distributions, detect outliers, and assess the assumptions for parametric testing. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages (rounded to nearest whole number); continuous variables were reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (minimum, maximum) depending on their distribution. Normality was assessed visually (using histogram) and statistically (skewness kurtosis test). Bivariate analyses were performed using chi-square tests for associations between categorical variables, independent t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and Mann–Whitney U tests for non-normal distributions. All underlying assumptions for regression were checked prior to analysis. Variables with p values < 0.2 in simple regression analysis (done for each of the independent variables) [54–56] were included in the multivariable logistic regression model using backward stepwise selection method. Variables were retained in the final model if p < 0.05 and excluded if p > 0.1 [57–59]. Regression diagnostics were performed to assess whether the model met key assumptions necessary to ensure the validity of the analysis. Multicollinearity diagnostics was performed by generating correlation matrix of the independent variables. Furthermore, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test was conducted to assess the predictive capacity of the model for the outcome variable (i.e., the observed and predicted values are close together). The model fit was determined based on the p value of the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test. The adjusted odds ratio and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were used to estimate the magnitude of association. p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Approval letters to conduct health camps were obtained from each participating metropolitan/sub-metropolitan city. Ethical clearance for the study was approved by the ethical review board (ERB) of the Nepal Health Research Council (NHRC) [Ref No. 1260]. The study was conducted in accordance with the National Ethical Guidelines for Health Research in Nepal 2022 to ensure ethical integrity and study participant’s welfare. The participant information sheet and consent form were provided to read or verbally explained by the research assistant, outlining the study’s objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits. The study’s purpose was clearly communicated, and participants were informed of their voluntary involvement and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. Written informed consent was obtained from all literate study participants and from legally authorized representatives of minors (individuals under 18 years of age). Verbal consent was obtained from illiterate participants, witnessed by an independent third party, who also remained present throughout the data collection process. To ensure confidentiality and privacy, all data were de-identified, with personal identifiers removed and stored securely in a password-protected database accessible only to the research team. All the interviews and clinical assessment were conducted in designated private spaces within the health camp to ensure participant privacy. Participants with elevated clinical findings specifically, random blood sugar levels ≥ 200 mg/dl or blood pressure readings ≥ 140/90 mmHg were re-confirmed by the medical doctors available in the health camp and were provided with medicine at free of cost. They were also referred to the nearest tertiary hospitals for further evaluation, confirmation, and appropriate follow-up care.

Results

Total study participants included in the study

A total of 790 study participants attended the health camp. Hence, we included 790 samples for the statistical analysis. The participation rate from each of the metropolitan/sub-metropolitan was Bharatpur (93.2%), Biratnagar (71.2%), Janakpur (92.8%), Nepalgunj (94.9%), and Pokhara (94.8%).

Sample characteristics

Of the 790 sanitation workers included in the study, 74% were male and 26% were female. The median age was 31.5 years (range: 15 to 75 years), with the majority (57.2%) in the 15–34 years age group. The prevalence of hypertension was higher among males (32%) than females (25%) (Table 2). Socio-demographic characteristics by hypertension status

Table 2 depicts the socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants by hypertension status. Hypertension seemed to have a statistically significant association with age, with the prevalence increasing from 19% among those aged 15–34 years to 61% among those aged 55–75 years (p < 0.001). The median systolic blood pressure was 126 mmHg (93–220) for males and 115 mmHg (80–188) for females. Similarly, the median diastolic blood pressure was 80 mmHg for both sexes, with ranges of 55–133 mmHg for males and 57–147 mmHg for females (data not presented in table) respectively. The hypertensive status varied significantly across sanitation workers with different marital status with the highest prevalence (37.5%) among those being single after being married (divorce/widow/widower) (p < 0.001). Literate participants had significantly higher prevalence of hypertension (44%) compared to those with no education (illiterate) or those with primary or higher level of education (p = 0.02). The proportion of hypertension was nearly similar between study participants living alone (31%) and those living with their families (30%) (p = 0.86).

Occupational characteristics by hypertension status

The hypertension level varied significantly across different sanitation workers (p = 0.02). Hypertension was seen highest among the transporters (37%) followed by mixed type workers (33%) and pickers of dumping site (32%) (Table 3). The median systolic blood pressure among sweepers, waste collectors, transporters, and dumping site pickers were 120 mmHg (80–188), 123 mmHg (99–170), 129 mmHg (99–206), and 124 mmHg (90–171), respectively. Similarly, the median diastolic blood pressure was 80 mmHg (56–147) for sweepers, 80 mmHg (55–123) for waste collectors, 84 mmHg (63–133) for transporters, and 81 mmHg (59–106) for dumping site pickers (data not presented in table). Work experience was significantly associated with hypertension status (p < 0.001), with hypertensive participants reporting longer median years of work experience compared to non-hypertensive participants. The average workload of the participants with hypertensive disorder was nearly 8.3 h per day, whereas those with non-hypertensive disorder was nearly 8 h per day. No statistically significant difference was observed in the blood pressure level of study participants with varying degrees of work hours (p = 0.05). Experience of heat stress and the related symptoms did not show any significant relationship with the hypertension status (p > 0.6). The lower sample size for the heat related symptoms may have limited power to detect the association.

Lifestyle and health related characteristics by hypertension status

Table 4 depicts the health-related characteristics and lifestyle factors in relation to the blood pressure status (hypertension versus non-hypertension). Significant differences were observed in the blood pressure level of study participants with varying alcohol drinking habits (p < 0.001). More than half of the daily alcohol consumers (51%) were hypertensive compared to the 36% of weekly drinkers, 19% of monthly alcohol drinkers and 25% of non-drinkers. Chewing tobacco was significantly associated with hypertension (p = 0.02), with 34% of the users being hypertensive compared to the 27% of the non-users. The BMI showed significant association with hypertension status (p < 0.001) with the increased prevalence of hypertension across BMI categories: 21% in participants with normal BMI, 35% in the overweight group, and 42% among obese individuals. Only 2% of the sanitation workers from Biratnagar and Nepalgunj had a random blood sugar level of 200 mg/dl or higher.

Regression analysis

All the independent variables were subjected to the simple logistic regression analysis (Additional file 3). Those variables having p values less than 0.2 in the simple regression analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. The final model showed that age (35–54 years and 55–75 years), sex (male), metropolitan/sub-metropolitan (Janakpur), alcohol consumption (daily drinker, once a week), and BMI (overweight and obese) were significantly associated with hypertension (Table 5).

With reference to the final model, the study participants within the age group from 35 to 54 years were at 2.15 times higher odds of having hypertension compared to those below 35 years of age. Similarly, study participants from the age group 55–75 years had seven times higher odds of hypertension compared to those below 35 years of age, keeping all other variables constant. Male study participants were nearly two times more likely to suffer from hypertension compared to female study participants. Study participants from Janakpur municipality were 2 times more likely to suffer from hypertension compared to those from Bharatpur municipality. Study participants consuming alcohol (once a week) were about 2 times more likely to suffer from hypertension compared to non-consumers. The odds further increased to about 3 times for daily alcohol consumption. Overweight study participants were 2 times more likely to suffer from hypertension compared to study participants with normal BMI. The odds further increased to over 3 times for obese study participants.

A total of 744 observations were included in the final model after excluding records with missing values. The pseudo-R square value of the final model indicates that 15.4% of the variation in hypertension was explained by all the independent variables in the final model. According to the likelihood ratio test (LR χ² (8) = 137.7, p < 0.001), all the independent variables in the final model were significantly predictive of hypertension, i.e., the proposed final model is better than a null model (i.e., a model without any independent variables). None of the individual independent variables in the final model had a correlation ‘r’ value greater than 0.5 (multicollinearity value). The Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness of fit test showed a p-value of 0.19, indicating that the final model could predict hypertension.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive assessment of hypertension prevalence and its associated factors among sanitation workers across five urban regions of Nepal. The findings reveal a substantial burden of hypertension among sanitation workers exhibiting varying degrees of hypertension. The study highlights hypertension as a significant public health concern among sanitation workers; an occupational group that has been underrepresented in various previously conducted epidemiological studies and national health surveys of Nepal.

Hypertension prevalence compared with other studies

This study revealed a concerning burden of hypertension among sanitation workers, with approximately 30% of the study participants classified as hypertensive, which exceeds the national estimates of 20% and 24.5%, as reported by NDHS 2022 [3, 60] and STEPS 2019 [2] respectively. While these national surveys are instrumental in tracking non-communicable disease trends, they reported lower overall hypertension rates and did not specifically analyze vulnerable subgroups such as sanitation workers. Various studies focusing on sanitation workers and similar occupational groups especially from South Asia and Africa reported wide range of hypertension prevalence from as low as 22% among sewage treatment plant workers [15], 26.3% among cleaners [9], 27.7% among sanitary workers [13], 29% among landfill waste pickers [11] to being as high as 36.6% among sanitation workers [8], and 45% among street sweepers [16]. The difference might be due to different socio-cultural, political, economic factors of the countries, and the health care system’s sensitivity in addressing the prevention and control of hypertension. The higher prevalence of hypertension observed in this study underscores the importance of occupation as a key social determinant, particularly in physically demanding and socioeconomically disadvantaged occupational groups. Despite the commitments outlined in the National Health Policy 2019 and the Multisectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs (2021–2025), there is a critical need for these frameworks to explicitly address the unique vulnerabilities of the high-risk occupational groups. Local governments in Nepal, empowered by the 2015 Constitution (Schedule 8) and the Local Governance Act 2017, can leverage their concurrent mandates for basic health services, sanitation, and waste management to integrate routine health screening, education and occupational health programs. These frameworks could be capitalized for creating synergies for sanitation workers who face high risk of hypertension.

Determinants of hypertension

Various factors such as socio-demographic, occupational and life-style factors play an important role in determining the hypertension status. Our study identified key socio-demographic and lifestyle factors, particularly increasing age, daily alcohol consumption, and elevated BMI (obesity) as an important factors associated with hypertension. All established confounders namely, age sex, BMI, and alcohol use were retained in the final model, ensuring that variables with known theoretical and empirical relevance to hypertension were appropriately adjusted for. In addition, occupational exposures inherent to the sanitation work may interact with the demographic and life-style factors further exacerbating the hypertension risk. Prolonged physical workload, repetitive manual labor, and exposure to extreme heat can elevate hypertension, particularly among older workers or those with obesity [17–20]. Continuous exposure to heat stress can induce dehydration, which alters electrolyte balance and activates sympathetic response contributing to high blood pressure [18–20]. Likewise, psychosocial stress stemming from the job insecurity, stigma, and unsafe working conditions may compound the effects of behavioral risk factors such as alcohol use or inadequate rest, further increasing the risk of hypertension [17, 19].

All the variables in the final model contributed to an increase in hypertension of the study participants. Age demonstrated a strong dose-response relationship with hypertension, with older age groups more likely to have hypertension. These findings align with the national and regional evidence. The multivariable analysis from the Nepal Demographic Health Survey 2016 and 2022 reports progressive increase in hypertension outcome with increasing age for both sexes [4]. Furthermore, a study from India among sanitation workers showed that participants with higher age were more likely to suffer from hypertension [8]. The higher prevalence of hypertension among the older age sanitation workers in our study who are often poor and underprivileged, might be due to a combination of accumulated lifestyle, prolonged exposure to occupational stress, unsafe working conditions and inadequate access to preventive health care services. Increasing age contributes to vascular aging and reduced arterial elasticity, leading to higher systolic blood pressure. It is also associated with declining renal efficiency and fluid retention, providing a well-established biological basis for elevated blood pressure with age [61, 62].

Our findings indicate strong dose response relationship between alcohol consumption and hypertension, with higher likelihood of hypertension observed among individuals who consumed alcohol more frequently. This supports the universal scientific consensus that alcohol acts a modifiable factor associated with elevated blood pressure. These findings are consistent with the global and regional literature, including systematic review and meta-analysis that shows a dose-dependent relationship between alcohol intake and hypertension, which are often positive and linear across diverse population [63]. In context of sanitation workers, many of whom experience high occupational stress, social marginalization, and limited access to health education [8, 9, 13], the consumption of alcohol may serve as a coping mechanism, becoming deeply ingrained into their daily habits. This pattern not only contributes to increase likelihood of elevated blood pressure but also complicates effort toward behavioral change. The intake of alcohol, even the moderate level contributes to increased blood pressure due to its effect on sympathetic nervous system, endothelial function, and fluid balance regulation [64–66].

Our findings revealed strong association between elevated body mass index (BMI) and hypertension, reinforcing the well documented link between excess body weight and high blood pressure. Various epidemiological studies along with systematic review and meta-analysis have consistently reflected a strong dose-dependent relationship between overweight/obesity and hypertension, demonstrating a positive and often linear association across diverse populations and age groups [67–69, 70]. These findings are in alignment with national data from NDHS, which shows hypertension prevalence being significantly higher among individual with obesity compared to the general population [3, 5]. Similarly, studies from other low-income occupational groups, such as waste pickers in South Africa, also demonstrate higher likelihood hypertension among those with higher BMI [11]. The sanitation worker often has limited access to nutritious food [14], irregular physical activity patterns [4], and lack of preventive healthcare [15], which may contribute to excess weight and poor hypertension control. Additionally, this population may face unique barriers to lifestyle modification, including work related constraints and minimal health education [8, 9, 11, 13]. Overweight and obese individuals are more likely to have hypertension due to multiple interrelated physiological, hormonal, and structural changes in the body, including insulin resistance, which impairs vascular function and promotes sodium retention in the kidneys, thereby increasing blood pressure [71–73].

Sanitation workers from Janakpur municipality exhibited the highest burden of hypertension compared to those in other study areas. This geographic disparity may reflect a complex interplay of environmental, infrastructural, and social determinants. Differences in urban planning, exposure to pollutants and working condition may contribute to varying levels of hypertension risk across municipalities. In addition, access to healthcare service and information particularly for preventive screening and chronic disease management might be more limited in Janakpur, further exacerbating health inequalities. Cultural and behavioral factors such as local dietary patterns, alcohol use and attitudes towards health seeking behavior, may also influence the elevated hypertension rates observed in this setting.

The burden of hypertension was higher among male participants and were more likely to be hypertensive compared to their female participants. This finding is consistent with national data from Nepal, which consistently report a higher prevalence of hypertension among men compared to women. According to NDHS 2016, 17% of women and 23% of men aged 15 years and above had hypertension [74]. This difference was also reported in recent estimates of NDHS 2022 (women 18% and men 23%, aged 15 years and above) [3]. STEPS 2019 further supported this difference, reporting 20% of women and 30% of men aged 15 to 69 years with hypertension [2]. While our findings reflect national patterns, international studies among similar occupational groups have shown mixed results. Some research from South Africa and India has reported a higher prevalence of hypertension among female sanitation workers [75, 16], which could be possibly due to differences in work condition. This variation highlights the importance of context when interpreting sex specific health outcomes. The difference in hypertension among male and female could be attributed to a combination of biological, behavioral, and social factors. The prevalence of hypertension is usually lower among females, especially before menopause due to a combination of biological and hormonal factors, including the protective effects of estrogen and chromosomal differences [76–80]. Men, on the other hand, may experience earlier vascular aging and are more likely to engage in behaviors such as tobacco and alcohol use, which are associated with elevated blood pressure and other cardiovascular outcomes. Occupational exposure and stress, common in sanitation work, may also differentially affect men due gendered roles in both work and household settings. The study is the first of its kind in Nepal to focus exclusively on hypertension among sanitation workers, a neglected yet high-risk occupational group. The inclusion of a large and diverse sample from five urban metropolitan/sub-metropolitans across different provinces offers robust and geographically representative insights. Additionally, benchmarking our findings against national surveys like NDHS and STEPS underscores the health disparities faced by sanitation workers and reinforces the study’s policy relevance. These findings have implications for the implementation of Nepal’s Multisectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs (2021–2025), which calls for inclusive and equitable health services across all occupational groups. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation of the observed associations. The use of nonprobability total enumerative sampling through voluntary participation in health camps may have introduced selection bias, potentially overestimating hypertension prevalence. The study included only those sanitation workers who were able to attend the health camp, which can be a potential source of bias. Additionally, self-reported behavioral data such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and heat stress symptoms are prone to recall and social desirability biases, despite efforts to minimize the bias through probing. Furthermore, crucial occupational factors including psychosocial and environmental stressors contributing to hypertension were not comprehensively explored, and the assessment of heat stress as a determinant of hypertension lacked statistical power due to the limited number of study participants exposed to heat stress.

Conclusion

Sanitation workers in Nepal experience significant health disparities, as witnessed in this study. This study identifies key demographic and lifestyle factors such as older age, daily alcohol consumption, and obesity as major factors associated with hypertension. Our findings call for tailored public health interventions such as age specific hypertension screening, structured alcohol-reduction counseling, and workplace weight-management initiatives. Integrating routine hypertension screening and follow-up into municipal health services, particularly for older and high-risk workers, could facilitate early detection and management. Moreover, future studies should specifically explore the work specific occupational exposures such as workload, heat stress, ergonomic strain and psychosocial stress to better understand the contribution of occupational conditions to hypertension risk. The key frameworks of Nepal relevant to sanitation workers such as Multisectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs (2021–2025) and Local Governance Act 2017 could be re-visited to recognize and address the unique vulnerabilities of these high-risk occupational groups.
