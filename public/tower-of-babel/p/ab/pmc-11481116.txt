Correlation of programmed death-ligand 1 expression in tumour cells between diagnostic small biopsies performed by radial EBUS and surgical specimens of peripheral lung cancer
Authors: Samy Lachkar, Diane Gervereau, Perrot Loïc, Marielle De Marchi, Helene Morisse, Edouard Dantoing, Nicolas Piton, Luc Thiberville, Mathieu Salaün, Florian Guisier
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveExpression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) in tumour cells (TCs) is predictive of immunotherapy efficacy in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Small biopsy samples collected by bronchoscopy are often used to diagnose peripheral lung cancer. It is questionable whether these small samples from radial endobronchial ultrasonography (r-EBUS) procedures are representative of PD-L1 expression in TCs.MethodsWe retrieved data of consecutive patients who had surgery for NSCLC and previous r-EBUS biopsy sampling, from 2017 to 2019 in our centre. PD-L1 expression in tumour cells was categorised as <1%, 1%–49% and ≥50%. PD-L1 expression was compared between r-EBUS samples and surgical specimens.ResultsAmong 1026 patients who had r-EBUS, 521 had a diagnosis of lung cancer on r-EBUS sample. PD-L1 testing was indicated in 356 cases and results were considered contributive in 325 cases (91%). 82 patients with PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples had subsequent surgical resection of the nodule and were included in the study. PD-L1 expression was identical between r-EBUS samples and surgical specimens in 67% of cases, with kappa 0.44 (p<0.001). 82% of patients with PD-L1≥50% in surgical specimens were identified in r-EBUS samples. Nonetheless, 31% of patients with no PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples had some expression in surgical specimens.ConclusionSmall samples obtained by r-EBUS are adequate for assessment of PD-L1 expression in tumour cells, with moderate concordance compared to surgical specimens. Reassessment of PD-L1 expression in larger samples may be useful to guide therapy in patients with no PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples.Keywords: Bronchoscopy, Lung Cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, accounting for 18% of cancer deaths in 2018.1 It often presents as a peripheral lung lesion, especially for non-squamous tumours such as adenocarcinomas. Various advanced bronchoscopy methods have been developed to reach peripheral nodules such as radial endobronchial ultrasound (r-EBUS).2 Bronchoscopy with r-EBUS achieves high diagnostic performance,3 but one pitfall of this technique remains the small size of the tissue samples. Nonetheless, we and others have previously demonstrated that these samples are suitable for histology subtyping and molecular analysis.4 5

Recently, blockade of the interaction between programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), expressed primarily by T lymphocytes, and its ligand programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) using monoclonal antibodies has been shown to produce durable clinical responses in patients with advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).6

Furthermore, in NSCLC, PD-L1 expression in tumour cells (TCs) has been shown to be predictive of anti-PD1/PD-L1 efficacy in various settings.6 7 For instance, in patients with metastatic NSCLC who received pembrolizumab as first-line treatment, the greatest benefit was observed in patients with a PD‐L1 expression of ≥50% in TCs.8 9 Furthermore, in non-operable stage III NSCLC, approval of consolidation immunotherapy with durvalumab is limited to patients with a PD-L1 expression of ≥1% in some countries. Likewise, adjuvant treatment with atezolizumab is restricted to a PD-L1 expression of ≥50% in some countries, according to the IMpower010 trial results.10 Similarly, the benefit of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in stage II–III NSCLC is also correlated to the expression of PD-L1 in TCs.11

As such, assessment of PD-L1 expression is often necessary to guide lung cancer treatment. The most commonly used technique to assess PD-L1 expression is immunohistochemistry (IHC). PD-L1 protein is expressed on the membrane of cancer cells and immune cells. The spatial heterogeneity of its expression has been described and is thought to impede the predictive value of this biomarker.12,14 Moreover, discrepancies in PD-L1 expression may occur between different types of tissue sample (surgical biopsy, CT-guided core needle biopsy, bronchial biopsy, transbronchial needle aspiration), timepoint of sampling (before therapy, after chemotherapy15 16), age of the sample, fixation and archiving conditions and the antibody clone used for PD-L1 IHC.17

Previous studies reported the feasibility of assessing PD-L1 expression in small samples obtained by r-EBUS-guided bronchoscopy.18,20 Nonetheless, studies raised concerns about the representativity of various types of tumour samples compared with surgical specimens.1821,23 Given the spatial heterogeneity and the technical limitations of PD-L1 IHC, it is questionable whether the small amount of tissue obtained from bronchial biopsies is representative of PD-L1 expression in whole tumours.

There are few data on the reliability of PD-L1 expression in biopsy samples obtained from r-EBUS-guided procedures, with contrasted results in small series. Some authors described a poor correlation of PD-L1 expression in TCs between surgical specimens and matched biopsies,13 22 whereas other studies found a good concordance of PD-L1 testing.18 20 24 25 In a recent study published in 2022, a statistically significant correlation was observed for PD-L1 expression in TCs between biopsy samples and surgical specimens, but of uncertain clinical relevance. Indeed, authors found a moderate agreement when looking at the comparison using a cut-off of PD-L1 of ≥ 50%, which is the most commonly used for treatment decisions.26

The aim of this study was to evaluate PD-L1 expression in TCs between r-EBUS-guided bronchial biopsy samples and surgical specimens.

Methods

This retrospective, single-centre study included consecutive patients who had surgery for NSCLC and prior assessment of PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples, from 2017 to 2019 at Rouen University Hospital, France. Patients were identified by consulting electronic medical records. Patients without a final diagnosis or who had a previous r-EBUS procedure were excluded (figure 1).

Patients or the public were involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research

R-EBUS procedure

Before each r-EBUS procedure, the lesion was located using the planner of the virtual bronchoscopy software to identify the optimal bronchial path to the lesion (LungPoint planner, Broncus Medical, San Jose, California, USA), from a CT scan of the millimetre slice chest. Bronchoscopy was then carried out using local or general anaesthesia with either an MP160F video bronchoscope or a BF-MP60F bronchofiberscope (Olympus Tokyo, Japan), with a 4 mm outer diameter and a 2 mm working channel.

The ultrasonographic probe was the 1.4 mm UM-S20-17S probe (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), introduced into the dedicated 1.9 mm diameter guide catheter (K201, Olympus) with a 1.5 mm biopsy forceps.

The procedure was performed without ‘navigation system’ or fluoroscopy. After having memorised the endobronchial route from the planning, the operator reached the most distal subsegmental bronchus. The r-EBUS probe, covered with the guide sheath, was then inserted into the working channel and gently pushed until a lesion-specific ultrasonographic image could be obtained, as described elsewhere.27 28 R-EBUS views of peripheral lesions were characterised as ‘concentric’, when the radial probe appeared within and completely surrounded by the lesion and ‘eccentric’, when the probe was adjacent to the lesion, without tissue completely surrounding the probe.

Once the nodule ultrasound signal was obtained, the probe was removed and the guide sheath was left in place in the lesion. The sampling, including brush cytology and forceps biopsy, was then performed. Rapid on-site evaluation was not used.

Radiographs of the chest were obtained only in case of chest pain or respiratory symptoms following the procedure.

Specimens were considered diagnostic when a cytologic, histologic or microbiologic diagnosis was confirmed and consistent with the clinical presentation. Patients for whom the procedures were not diagnostic were referred for other sampling methods or surgery or were followed by surveillance CT scan of the chest, when appropriate. Data on the final diagnosis were collected from our centre’s electronic medical records and/or by interviewing the patient’s pulmonologist when clinical follow-up was performed elsewhere.

Pathologic diagnosis

Histologic, molecular and IHC analyses were performed. After cytologic and histologic analysis, unstained slides or paraffin blocks were used for molecular analysis and PDL-1 expression.

Assessment of PD-L1 expression

After histologic analysis, PD-L1 expression was assessed using E1L3N (Cell Signaling Technology) or QR1 (Diagomics) IHC antibodies on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumour slides. Expression was categorised as <1%, 1%–49% or ≥50% after examination by a thoracic pathologist. The number of TCs analysed was also noted: <50, 50–100, >100. Results were considered contributive if >100 TCs were analysed. More details are available in online supplemental appendix 1.

Statistics

Descriptive statistics were performed using Prism (GraphPad, San Diego, California, USA) and R Statistical Software (V.4.0.5; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Qualitative parameters are expressed as numbers, percentages and 95% CIs. Quantitative parameters are expressed as means and SD or medians and IQR whenever appropriate. Comparison between quantitative variable was performed using Mann-Whitney test. A kappa test was performed to assess the concordance between the assessments of PD-L1 expression using the surgical samples and the biopsy samples. Patients with missing data for tumour size were excluded from this analysis. All tests were two sided, with a p value of 0.05 indicating statistical significance. Missing data are indicated.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

Between January 2017 and December 2019, 1026 patients had an r-EBUS procedure (figure 1) in our centre; 67 patients with no follow-up data were not analysed. Finally, 959 patients were included in the study; 62.6% were men and the mean age was 65.9 years (min–max=19–91). The median diameter of the lesion was 29 mm in long axis (IQR=20–43 mm) and 20 mm in short axis (IQR=14–30 mm). The median distance between the nodule and the pleura was 10 mm (IQR=0–29 mm). A bronchus sign was found on CT scan in 71% (360/508) of cases.

The lesions were mainly located in the right (33%) and left (29%) upper lobes.

Analysis of r-EBUS samples

From the 779 cases with a final malignant diagnosis, the diagnosis was obtained in r-EBUS samples in 521 patients (67%). It was obtained in 487/664 samples (75%) in presence of a bronchus sign.

Among these 521 cases, 485 were primary lung cancers (adenocarcinomas: 267 (55%), squamous carcinoma: 80 (16%), NSCLC not otherwise specified: 92 (19%), other: 46 (10%)) and 36 were secondary malignancies.

PD-L1 testing was indicated in 346 patients (340 biopsies, 7 brushes, 1 catheter rinses) corresponding to 265 adenocarcinomas, 80 squamous cell carcinomas, 1 small cell cancer.

PD-L1 expression was contributive in 325 (91.3%) patients: 323/340 biopsies (95%); 2/7 brushes (29%); 0/1 catheter rinses

Expression was <1%, 1%–49% and ≥50% in 188 (58%), 69 (21%) and 68 (21%), respectively.

Correlation of PD-L1 expression between r-EBUS samples and surgical specimens

Among the 325 patients with contributive PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples, 82 patients had surgical resection of the sampled nodule. The median time between r-EBUS-guided bronchoscopy and surgery was 60 days (13–861). Discussion

In an era of personalised medicine in lung cancer, molecular analysis and PD-L1 assessment are necessary to optimise patients’ treatment. In particular, assessing PD-L1 expression in TCs may help to select patients who derive the most benefit from immunotherapy. Peripheral lung lesions are often challenging to sample and r-EBUS is the most popular technique in this setting. However, due to the size of the guide sheath and forceps, samples are smaller than traditional proximal bronchoscopy samples. Moreover, the spatial heterogeneity of PD-L1 expression within tumours may impede the reliability of small bronchial samples such as those performed by r-EBUS for PD-L1 expression.

In this study, PD-L1 assessment was indicated in 356 patients and yielded a contributive result in 325 patients (91%). A study conducted by Tsunoda et al20 including a smaller cohort of 84 patients with NSCLC diagnosed by r-EBUS reported similar feasibility rate of 87% for PD-L1 assessment.

In our study, PD-L1 expression was <1% in 57.8%, 1%–49% in 21%, and ≥50% in 21%. This distribution is broadly comparable to that of other series. Notably, the percentage of specimens with a PD-L1 expression of ≥50% in TCs was similar to that reported in the initial phase I clinical trial that formally validated the use of PD-L1 expression as a biomarker of clinical response to PD-1 inhibition in NSCLC (23%).29

The concordance between PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS biopsy samples and surgical specimens was 67% using three cut-off values (< 1%, 1%–49% and >50%). Kappa test was 0.44 with p value<0.0001, indicating a moderate agreement, yet statistically significant. These PD-L1 expression cut-offs were chosen because they are used in routine treatment decisions. This result is consistent with other studies. Kitazono et al18 reported a higher concordance rate of 92% in 79 surgically resected specimens matched with biopsies. However, they included biopsies performed using a variety of techniques with probably bigger samples including standard bronchial biopsy, EBUS-TBNA (Trans-Bronchial Needle Aspiration) and transparietal puncture. Moreover, PD-L1 expression was only binary (positive/negative). Kim et al21 also studied the concordance of PD-L1 expression in 46 small bronchial biopsy samples obtained by different non-invasive procedures (including 57% of r-EBUS). The correlation with surgical specimens was similar to our results (73% using a threshold of ≥50%). Another recent study26 reported a statistically significant correlation of PD-L1 expression between biopsy samples and surgical specimens, with a concordance rate of 78% in 113 samples, including 13 obtained by transbronchial biopsy.

In contrast, other studies revealed a lower concordance rate. Ilie et al22 reported, as observed in our study, a tendency to underestimate PD-L1 expression in samples obtained by r-EBUS, using a scoring system from 0 to 3 depending on PD-L1 expression (≥50%, ≥5% and <5%).

Median diameter of the sampled nodule was 42 vs 24 mm for cases with or without Pd-L1 assessment concordance, respectively (p=0.10). Although this result is not statistically different, a larger cohort may show that the concordance rate is better for larger nodules. Whether or not using a 1.1 mm cryoprobe would increase correlation was not assessed in our study, as it was conducted on data collected from 2017 to 2019, hence before the availability of this probe.

PD-L1 expression assessment in stage I disease is not currently standard of care. It was however performed in some of our patients. For stage II–III disease, treatment indication is dependent of PD-L1 expression level, with no immunotherapy for PD-L1 negative cases in some countries. Given the underestimation of PD-L1 expression assessment using r-EBUS, as shown in our study, a rebiopsy may be needed.

In a recent meta-analysis,30 authors reported the accuracy of cytologic versus histologic specimens for assessment of PD-L1 expression in NSCLC. 26 articles were included, encompassing a total of 1064 paired histologic and cytologic cell blocks. The pooled sensitivity and specificity for cytologic versus histologic specimens were 0.84 vs 0.88, respectively, for a PD-L1 expression cut-off of 1%, and 0.78 vs 0.94, respectively, for a PD-L1 expression cut-off of 50%. There are major differences between our study and this meta-analysis. In fact, we compared only specimens from r-EBUS acquired small biopsies versus surgical resection specimen of the same lesion, while the studies included in the meta-analysis had variable settings: the cytologic specimens were acquired through various techniques (transthoracic needle aspirations, EBUS-TBNAs, fine needle aspirations, endoscopic brushings, BALs (Broncho-alveolar Lavage), pleural or pericardial fluid samples) as well as the histologic ones (surgical resection specimens, surgical biopsies, transbronchial biopsies, endobronchial biopsies, pleural biopsies). Hence, the setting of the comparison is not the same, notably with >250 cytologic samples acquired from metastatic sites versus primary tumours in the meta-analysis. Results from our studies give a specific assessment of PD-L1 expression accuracy for r-EBUS acquired samples.

In our study, 31% of patients with no PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples had some expression in surgical specimens. This point raises concerns about how to inform therapeutic decision in these patients, mainly in two settings: (1) consolidation treatment with durvalumab after chemoradiation and (2) neoadjuvant treatment with nivolumab and chemotherapy, whose approval is restricted to a PD-L1 expression of ≥1% in some countries. Moreover, in patients who are deemed to have a PD-L1 expression of <1% in presurgery samples, it may be useful to reassess PD-L1 expression in surgical specimens to guide adjuvant treatment, since adjuvant immunotherapy with atezolizumab is restricted to a PD-L1 expression of ≥50% in some countries.

In our study, PD-L1 expression was unchanged in six of seven patients who received chemotherapy between r-EBUS and surgery, even if the median time between r-EBUS and surgery (275 days) was longer than in other studies: 56 days in the report by Gompelmann et al.26 In this latter report, authors found a statistically significant correlation of PD-L1 expression between biopsy samples and surgical specimens in treatment-naïve surgical patients, 0.74 (p<0.001), but a lower correlation of 61% in a subgroup of 27 patients who received neoadjuvant therapy. However, previous studies reported a downregulation of PD-L1 expression in TCs associated with neoadjuvant chemotherapy.15 16

The main strength of our study is the large number of patients and the use of PD-L1 expression categorisation in line with cut-offs used in routine treatment decisions. The main limitation of our study is its retrospective design and the lack of correlation with clinical outcomes, since very few patients received immunotherapy.

Conclusion

Small bronchial biopsy samples obtained during r-EBUS-guided bronchoscopy could be used for the diagnosis of peripheral lung cancer, allowing histologic subtyping and PD-L1 expression in most cases. Overall, the concordance of PD-L1 expression between r-EBUS biopsy samples and surgical specimens was moderate. Of note, 31% of patients with negative PD-L1 expression in r-EBUS samples had some PD-L1 expression in surgical specimens, suggesting that reassessment on larger samples may be relevant to guide therapy in some cases.
