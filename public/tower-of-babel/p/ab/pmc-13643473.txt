Elevated Galvanic Skin Response and Associated Brain Network Patterns in Youth at High Familial Risk for Bipolar Disorder
Authors: Lisha Zhang, Kun Qin, Corey R Jones, L Rodrigo Patino, Nanfang Pan, Ziyu Zhu, Haoran Xu, Thomas J Blom, Jeffrey R Strawn, Qiyong Gong, Manpreet K Singh, Melissa P DelBello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAutonomic nervous system (ANS) dysfunction may be a key neuropathological feature of bipolar disorder. There is limited understanding of how perturbations in the ANS manifest and their relation to brain function in youth with a familial risk for bipolar disorder.MethodGalvanic skin response and functional magnetic resonance imaging data during a continuous performance task with emotional and neutral distractors were collected from high-risk youth with depressive and/or anxiety symptoms and healthy controls (HCs). We compared skin conductance response (SCR) values from galvanic skin response data collected during imaging as an index of ANS arousal between groups. Then, we computed seed-based functional connectivity maps using generalized psychophysiological interaction analysis and applied a general linear model to examine associations between SCR values and functional connectivity patterns within the high-risk group.ResultsWe included 31 high-risk youth and 15 HCs in our analysis. The high-risk group showed significantly higher maximum (1.29 ± 1.32 μS vs 0.65 ± 0.62 μS, p = .048) and mean (0.26 ± 0.26 μS vs 0.14 ± 0.11 μS, p = .028) SCR values compared with HCs. Within the high-risk group, SCR values were not significantly correlated with emotional scores. Mean SCR values in high-risk youth were positively correlated with the functional connectivity between the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex and both the left amygdala and right thalamus, and these associations were not observed in the HC group.ConclusionOur findings provided preliminary evidence of ANS dysfunction in high-risk youth compared with HCs. Furthermore, functional connectivity patterns in prefrontal–limbic regions may be associated with ANS arousal, offering novel insights into ANS-related neural mechanisms and deepening our understanding of emotional dysregulation from peripheral and central perspectives in these youth.Clinical trial registration informationMechanism of Antidepressant-Related Dysfunctional Arousal in High-Risk Youth; https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show; NCT02553161.Diversity & Inclusion StatementWe worked to ensure sex and gender balance in the recruitment of human participants. Bipolar disorder (BD) is a chronic psychiatric disorder that is highly heritable.1,2 In fact, offspring of parents with BD are 10 times more likely to develop the disorder compared with those without a family history of BD.3 These high-risk individuals frequently present with anxiety and mood symptoms, which may further increase their risk of subsequently developing mood disorders, particularly BD.4 Emotional dysregulation and cognitive deficits are common among offspring with mood symptoms.5,6 Moreover, given that early intervention could alter the course of BD and improve longer-term outcomes,7 understanding neurobiological characteristics of individuals at high risk of developing BD has the potential to inform targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysregulation has been associated with BD and other mental conditions8,9 and with the risk of cardiovascular diseases in BD.10 Galvanic skin response (GSR), a measure of the electrical properties of the skin, is a surrogate marker of sympathetic and parasympathetic tone.11 As a noninvasive, convenient, and sensitive marker of ANS activity, GSR can characterize stress response and ANS arousal.12 Previous studies have shown that euthymic patients with BD-I have increased skin conductance compared with healthy controls (HCs) both at rest and during cognitive tests.13 Recently, GSR has been proposed as a biomarker for bipolar depression and as a state-related indicator of treatment response in both depressive and manic episodes.14 In high-risk youth, anxiety and ANS arousal, as reflected by GSR, are correlated during stress-evoking tasks, whereas this relation is not observed in HCs.15 However, ANS arousal remains poorly characterized in high-risk youth with a familial risk for BD.

Importantly, ANS arousal is closely linked to neural activity measured by functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI).16 Prior studies using simultaneous recordings have demonstrated that skin conductance responses (SCRs) covary with blood oxygenation level–dependent activity in key regions involved in emotional processing.17 These findings support the notion that peripheral physiological arousal and central neural responses are tightly interconnected. To understand the neural mechanisms underlying ANS arousal, we used fMRI with a continuous performance task with emotional and neutral distractors (CPT-END)18 to characterize emotional responses during an attentional task in high-risk youth. Generalized psychophysiological interaction (gPPI) analysis was used to examine task-dependent changes in functional connectivity during this emotional processing task.19 Previous fMRI studies have demonstrated abnormal functional connectivity in frontolimbic regions suggesting aberrant emotion processing and regulation in high-risk youth.20,21 High-risk youth with depressive disorders or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder also have increased activation in prefrontal–limbic regions, including the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (VLPFC), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, and subgenual cingulate cortex (sgACC), and cerebellar vermis while processing facial expressions.22 In addition, an fMRI study using an emotion processing task reported hyperactivation in frontolimbic regions in high-risk youth with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder.23

With these considerations in mind, we hypothesized that high-risk youth with a familial risk for developing BD would have increased ANS arousal compared with youth without a familial risk for BD. In addition, we hypothesized that these alterations will be associated with changes in functional connectivity in frontolimbic regions.

Method

Participants

Participants were recruited from the local community and online advertisements and from the Adult and Pediatric Mood Disorders Programs at the University of Cincinnati and Stanford University following criteria for a clinical trial registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02553161). At baseline, before starting any study-related interventions, 56 high-risk youth and 28 HCs were included. High-risk participants were aged 12 to 17 years, had at least 1 first-degree relative with BD-I, were antidepressant-naive, and exhibited current depressive and/or anxiety symptoms. Family history of BD was determined using the Family History-Research Diagnostic Criteria-Modified and the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-III-R or the Kiddie Schedule of Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia. High-risk youths were eligible for inclusion if they had moderate or severe current depressive and/or anxiety symptoms, as determined by a score > 35 on the Children's Depression Rating Scale-Revised (CDRS-R) or >15 on the Pediatric Anxiety Rating Scale (PARS), assessed on the day of fMRI and before the imaging procedure.24,25 The Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS) was used to assess subthreshold manic symptom severity.26 Inclusion and exclusion criteria for high-risk youth have been described previously.27 The 28 sociodemographically matched HCs without any known personal or first or second-degree family history of a mood or psychotic disorder were also included in this analysis.

All participants and their legal guardians provided written informed assent and consent, respectively. The study was approved by the University of Cincinnati and Stanford University institutional review boards.

Data Acquisition

GSR data were recorded using a BIOPAC MP160 Data Acquisition system equipped with the EDA100C module (Biopac Systems, Inc). Before entering the MRI scanner, participants were fitted with pregelled, carbon snap MRI-compatible electrodes (EL508; Biopac Systems, Inc) placed on the palmar skin of the middle phalanges of their index (second) and ring (fourth) fingers. The skin was cleaned with alcohol and allowed to dry before electrode placement. GSR acquisition began at the onset of the fMRI scan and continued continuously across 2 task runs at a sampling rate of 2,000 Hz.

All subjects underwent high-resolution 3-dimensional T1-weighted imaging using a modified driven equilibrium Fourier transform sequence and fMRI scanning with the CPT-END task using a T2∗-weighted gradient-echo echo-planar imaging pulse sequence at 2 sites with 3T Philips/GE MRI systems. Detailed MRI parameters are provided in Table S1, available online.

The CPT-END task uses a visual oddball paradigm that measures sustained attention to emotional and neutral stimuli. Stimuli included unpleasant pictures (10%), neutral pictures (10%), circles (10%), and squares (70%).18 Participants were instructed to press one button for all pictures and squares and a different button for circles. Emotional and neutral pictures were selected from the International Affective Picture System (University of Florida, Gainesville).18 Specifically, emotional distractors depicted unpleasant themes, including human violence, mutilation, and disease, whereas neutral distractors showed everyday activities and were matched to emotional images in terms of mean luminance, chromatic properties, and overall scene complexity. The task consisted of 2 sessions, each displaying 158 visual cues at 3-second intervals for 2.75 seconds. Emotional and neutral pictures were presented pseudorandomly, with a fixation cross appearing for 0.25 seconds between cues.

GSR Data Analysis

GSR data were processed using a custom Python script (version 3.9, available at https://www.python.org/) with the pyEDA module (accessible at https://github.com/HealthSciTech/pyEDA). After removing motion artifacts, the signals were first despiked using a 5-sample median filter and then low-pass filtered with a second-order Butterworth filter (cutoff frequency 1 Hz) to attenuate residual high-frequency noise.28,29 The data were subsequently down-sampled to 10 Hz.29 The GSR signals were then decomposed into 3 components, a tonic component, a phasic component, and additive white Gaussian noise, using a convex optimization approach (cvxEDA, www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/53326-cvxeda).30 The phasic component, known as SCR, reflects rapid changes in skin electrical activity in response to specific stimuli, such as emotional or cognitive triggers.9 SCR is characterized by a quick peak within seconds, followed by a return to baseline, caused by a sudomotor burst.31 In line with previous studies, SCR response was defined as an amplitude change >0.02 μS.32 Despite efforts to optimize participant comfort and compliance with best-practice GSR acquisition protocols, 38 recordings were not analyzable because of signal quality limitations (eg, motion artifact, electrode contact variability, or participant tolerance), including 25 from high-risk youth and 13 from HCs. Finally, we calculated the maximum and mean SCR value of all peaks to serve as the indicators of emotional arousal during the fMRI with the CPT-END task.

Task-Based fMRI Data Preprocessing and gPPI Analysis

Task-based fMRI data were preprocessed and analyzed using SPM 12 software (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) and the CONN toolbox (version 21b; Whitfield-Gabrieli & Nieto-Castanon, 2012). Preprocessing included slice-timing correction, realignment, segmentation, and co-registration based on individual structural T1 images, normalization to standard template, spatial smoothing (6-mm Gaussian kernel), and temporal band-pass filtering (>0.01 Hz). To reduce confounding factors, we removed invalid volumes identified by scrubbing as well as 12 head motion parameters from realignment, white matter and cerebrospinal fluid physiological signals, and event-related effects. An exclusion threshold for motion exceeding 3 mm/3° was applied to all sessions, and none was excluded based on these criteria. The mean framewise displacement values for high-risk youth and HCs were 0.010 ± 0.008 mm and 0.009 ± 0.008 mm, respectively.

A gPPI analysis was performed to examine how task-related functional connectivity patterns are modulated by emotional vs neutral stimuli. This method, implemented using the CONN toolbox, models the blood oxygenation level–dependent response for each task condition with a canonical hemodynamic response function. Each model includes the task regressor for emotional and neutral conditions with the hemodynamic response functions; the seeds/regions of interest blood oxygenation level–dependent time series; and the interaction regressor, which represents the interaction between the task condition and the seed/region of interest time series. Given that previous studies have reported frontolimbic network dysfunction in high-risk youth,20, 21, 22, 23 we selected the bilateral dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (Brodmann area 9/46), VLPFC (Brodmann area 45/47), and the sgACC (Brodmann area 25) as the seeds. The first-level gPPI analysis produced whole-brain functional connectivity (β weights) maps of those seeds for each subject.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0) software and the CONN toolbox. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD and were analyzed using independent-sample t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables, including sex and diagnosis, were compared using the χ2 test. Associations between SCR values and emotional measures were examined using partial correlation analyses controlling for age and sex, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons.

To investigate emotion-relevant brain mechanisms underlying autonomic activity in the high-risk group, a general linear model was used to examine correlations between seed-based functional connectivity, as determined by the gPPI analysis, and mean SCR values, controlling for age, sex, and site. Multiple comparisons were corrected using family-wise error at the voxel level (p < .005) and cluster level (p < .05), followed by Bonferroni correction for the 6 seed regions (p < .05/6).

To assess the robustness of these correlations, partial correlations between mean SCR and region of interest–level functional connectivity were performed in high-risk youth after excluding the participant with the maximum SCR value. Additionally, general linear model analyses were conducted in the HC group to evaluate whether the same associations were observed in HCs. Masks were defined based on the seeds and associated brain regions identified in the main analyses, with adjustments for age, sex, and site, and Bonferroni correction was applied.

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics

Available data from 31 high-risk youth and 15 HCs were included in the analysis, with demographic and clinical characteristics summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were found between the analytical subset and the full sample (Table S2, available online) or between the analytical and excluded subsets, including anxiety symptom severity, except for a higher proportion of anxiety diagnoses among high-risk youth in the analytical subset (Table S3, available online). In the analytical subset, no significant differences were observed between high-risk youth and HC groups in terms of age or sex. However, high-risk youth had significantly higher scores on the CDRS-R (54.32 ± 11.53 vs 18.87 ± 3.27, p < .001), PARS (13.42 ± 5.26 vs 0.93 ± 1.22, p < .001), and YMRS (7.06 ± 4.80 vs 0.40 ± 1.06, p <0.001) compared with HCs. Of 31 high-risk youth, 30 (96.77%) met the diagnostic criteria for an anxiety disorder, 28 (90.32%) met DSM-5 criteria for a depressive disorder, and 27 (87.10%) had both anxiety and depressive disorders. There were no significant differences between high-risk youth and HCs in percentage of correct or reaction times across all stimulus types during the CPT-END task (Table S4, available online).

Skin Conductance Responses

High-risk youth had greater autonomic reactivity compared with HCs, with higher maximum SCRs (1.29 ± 1.32 μS vs 0.65 ± 0.62 μS, p = .048) and higher mean SCRs (0.26 ± 0.26 μS vs 0.14 ± 0.11 μS, p = .028) (Figure 1). Within the high-risk group, SCR values were not significantly correlated with emotional measures, including CDRS-R, PARS, or YMRS scores (p > .05).

Correlations Between SCR and fMRI

In the high-risk group (Figure 2 and Table 2), higher mean SCR was associated with stronger functional connectivity between the right VLPFC seed and both the left amygdala and right thalamus. In addition, for the left sgACC seed, mean SCR was positively correlated with functional connectivity in the left fusiform gyrus. However, these associations were not observed in the HC group (p > .05).

Discussion

Based on a literature review of related search terms, this study is the first to simultaneously examine central and peripheral arousal using task-based fMRI and GSR in high-risk youth and HCs. Compared with HCs, high-risk youth had significantly higher maximum and mean SCR levels, indicating heightened ANS arousal during an emotion-attention processing task. The gPPI analysis further revealed that ANS arousal in the high-risk group predicted functional connectivity between the right VLPFC and both the left amygdala and the right thalamus as well as between the left sgACC and the left fusiform gyrus. These associations were not observed in HCs. Nevertheless, given the small sample size of the control group, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further, the association between SCR and the left sgACC–fusiform gyrus connectivity was no longer statistically significant after excluding an outlier. Collectively, these findings suggest that high-risk youth display increased physiological reactivity that is tightly coupled with alterations in frontolimbic networks supporting emotional processing. The observed synchrony between central and peripheral arousal may represent a distinctive signature of affective dysregulation and highlight broader downstream consequences of frontolimbic network dysfunction in high-risk youth.

Consistent with our hypothesis, high-risk youth had greater ANS arousal during emotion processing compared with HCs, which may reflect exaggerated physiological responses to emotional stimuli. This finding aligns with prior evidence of impaired emotion regulation in high-risk populations33 as well as elevated SCR reactivity in anxiety and BDs34,35 and is further supported by the presence of depressive and/or anxiety symptoms within our cohort. Individuals with BD have been shown to engage in more spontaneous inhibition and cognitive reappraisal strategies in response to emotional stimuli, often exerting greater regulatory effort but achieving lower success rates.36,37 Accordingly, high-risk youth may lack effective regulatory mechanisms, potentially explaining their greater ANS arousal.

Previous studies using emotional tasks in BD have similarly reported increased SCR across emotional conditions, although group differences were not significant,38 possibly because of small sample sizes and the therapeutic effects of antidepressants (which may reduce ANS arousal).39 Furthermore, studies of anxiety disorders have shown that anxiety severity is positively correlated with ANS arousal,40 which is in turn associated with greater perceived intensity of sadness during facial emotion processing tasks.41 This association between anxiety and ANS arousal has also been observed in high-risk youth, both at rest and during mental arithmetic tasks.15 However, we did not observe a significant correlation between SCR values and anxiety scores.

This null finding may be attributable to several factors. Reduced statistical power after the exclusion of low-quality data is a likely contributor. In addition, because of the high prevalence of anxiety symptoms observed in the participants included in this analysis, a potential ceiling effect may have restricted data variability and thereby limited our ability to identify this association. Other contributing factors may include overall sample heterogeneity, inherent variability in sympathetic activity,14 and unmeasured environmental confounders (eg, individual differences in body temperature or daily ambient variations across testing sites). In addition, a temporal dissociation between physiological arousal and symptoms may exist, whereby autonomic dysfunction precedes clinically manifest symptoms; this is consistent with developmental evidence indicating that maturation of prefrontal–limbic circuitry and associated autonomic regulation may occur earlier than fully developed emotion regulation capacities.42,43

A further methodological consideration is that anxiety symptoms were assessed under resting-state conditions, whereas SCR indexed acute task-evoked arousal, a discrepancy that may have attenuated their association. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that ANS dysfunction may emerge earlier in high-risk youth with anxiety and/or depression, preceding the onset of manic symptoms. Moreover, ANS dysfunction may serve as a potential biomarker for emotional dysregulation in this population. However, this requires further validation through comparisons between high-risk youth with and without clinically significant emotional dysregulation.

We explored SCR-related functional connectivity patterns using gPPI analysis and found that ANS arousal was positively correlated with the functional connectivity between the right VLPFC and the left amygdala/right thalamus. This pattern is consistent with prior findings in healthy individuals during fear extinction recall.44

The prefrontal lobe and limbic system play important roles in the regulation of ANS activity.45 Specifically, the VLPFC is involved in emotional reappraisal and regulation, associative emotion memory function, and the integration of goal-directed behavior, whereas the amygdala is central to emotion acquisition, storage, and expression and works with the VLPFC to focus more on negative emotions.46,47 Functionally, the amygdala rapidly detects emotionally salient stimuli—particularly negative cues—and signals to the VLPFC, which in turn modulates amygdala activity, forming a dynamic interaction between bottom-up and top-down processes.48 Increasing evidence suggests that altered VLPFC–amygdala connectivity is associated with affective dysregulation. For example, elevated amygdala–VLPFC coupling has been linked to trait vulnerability to depressive symptoms49 and state anxiety.44 In addition, studies combining fMRI and GSR have shown that altered SCR in patients with BD is linked to dysfunction in corticolimbic regulatory circuits, implicating impaired mechanisms of emotional regulation.50 Complementary evidence from granger causality analyses further indicates enhanced bottom-up effective connectivity from the amygdala to the prefrontal cortex during negative emotion processing.51 Recent studies have also reported greater SCR and abnormal brain activation in the prefrontal cortex in unmedicated patients with anxiety disorders during the early phase of fear learning.52 Our findings highlighted an abnormal VLPFC–amygdala connectivity in high-risk individuals, which may contribute to rapid negative emotional responses and enhanced autonomic reactivity. This pattern may help elucidate the neural mechanisms underlying depressive and anxiety symptoms in high-risk youth from both peripheral and central perspectives. Furthermore, enhanced VLPFC–amygdala functional connectivity during emotional processing may represent a potential risk marker for the subsequent development of BD22 and may aid in distinguishing BD from attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder with externalizing features.53

The thalamus, and particularly its anterior and middle olfactory nuclei, plays an important role in emotion processing by connecting with corticolimbic regions and subcortical limbic structure to integrate and coordinate overall activity within this network.54 As a central relay station in the brain, the thalamus is essential in the cerebello-thalamo-prefrontal pathway55 and plays a pivotal role in the interaction between arousal and cognitive performance by filtering and gating sensory inputs to the cerebral cortex to regulate attention.56 Aberrant thalamic activity has been implicated in a range of affective and cognitive disturbances. In BD, thalamic activity has been shown to correlate positively with depression severity, particularly in individuals with a history of suicidal ideation.57 In addition, dysfunctional thalamic processing has been associated with reward-related abnormalities in both high-risk youth and patients with BD, which may be closely linked to the manifestation of anhedonia during depressive episodes.58, 59, 60 Evidence from anxiety disorders reported that increased functional connectivity of the thalamus may enhance the integration of sensory information, potentially contributing to heightened emotional reactivity and autonomic responses observed in these conditions.61 Consistent with these findings, our results showing a positive association between ANS arousal and VLPFC–thalamus connectivity may reflect aberrant emotional and cognitive processing in high-risk youth. This connectivity pattern may further represent a shared neurobiological mechanism underlying symptom expression across different diagnostic categories. Finally, we found a positive trend between ANS arousal and functional connectivity between the sgACC and the fusiform gyrus. However, this association was no longer statistically significant after excluding an outlier and should therefore be interpreted with caution and validated in larger independent samples. Notably, previous decision-making task-state fMRI studies have reported that SCRs are positively correlated with neural activity in regions including the orbitofrontal cortex and fusiform gyrus in healthy adults.62

This study also has several limitations. First, this analysis excluded participants with unavailable GSR data and so was based on an exploratory investigation of baseline clinical trial data in a limited subsample, although the included sample was demographically and clinically comparable with the full cohort. Second, we assessed overall ANS arousal across the CPT-END task using mean SCR without distinguishing specific stimuli. Future studies can focus on more closely coupled experiments to examine stimulus-specific responses and associated connectivity patterns to better elucidate underlying mechanisms. Finally, generalizability is limited by the inclusion of only high-risk youth with depressive or anxiety symptoms, restricting our ability to disentangle familial risk from current symptomatology and to examine other relevant dimensions such as mood instability.

Our findings provided preliminary evidence of ANS dysfunction in high-risk youth compared with HCs. Furthermore, functional connectivity patterns in prefrontal–limbic regions may be associated with ANS arousal, offering novel insights into ANS-related neural mechanisms and deepening our understanding of emotional dysregulation from peripheral and central perspectives in these youth.
