Behind the fracture: An unusual suspect of mechanical bowel
Authors: Chirantan Suhrid, Sundaresh Prabhakar, Dnyaneshwar Kashinath Mohare, Jayashri Sanjay Pandya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polytrauma with associated pelvic fractures involves significant morbidity and mortality. If they are linked with injuries involving other systems within the body, the order of morbidity and mortality rises severalfold. Isolated pelvic fractures due to trauma are rare and their association with bowel injury is even rarer. Our case, a traumatic pelvic fracture resulting in ileal obstruction due to herniation and entrapment at the fracture site, is one of these rare cases. This case may also be considered as a Traumatic Abdominal Wall Hernia (TAWH) – essentially the herniation of bowel secondary to abdominal trauma - an entity first coined in 1906. If so, our case qualifies as the 21st such case in recorded history. Low-energy force is sufficient to cause pelvic fractures in the elderly population while high-energy forces are required for similar conditions among younger patients. Thus, the multitude of risk factors that determine falls in the elderly population have to be minimized as even a small mishap may result in something grave. Damage Control Surgery was performed. Our patient's entrapped bowel was reduced and the same area was fashioned into a double-barrel ileostomy. While the protocol to be followed in cases of bowel involvement is uncomplicated, the dilemma that exists is whether to subject the patient to operative intervention or adopt a conservative approach vis-a-vis the pelvic bone fracture. In cases of isolated pelvic fractures, a non-operative line may be taken, although a significantly higher survival rate has been seen with those undergoing operative intervention.

Introduction

The very nature of polytrauma leading to pelvic fractures inherently associates them with significant morbidity and mortality [1]. If these fractures are complicated with other injuries involving components of the gastrointestinal tract, the urinary pathway, the genital system, the surrounding vascularity or the peripheral nerves, the order of morbidity and mortality rises severalfold. Thus, it is vital to recognise not only the primary fracture but also the possible associated complication that patients may present with so that timely intervention is possible to prevent adverse outcomes.

While isolated pelvic fractures due to trauma appear to be rare, their association with bowel injury is even rarer. Associated intra-abdominal injuries in cases of pelvic fractures range from 8 to 19%. The incidence of bowel injury, which encompasses simple mesenteric tears to complete disruption of the lumen, is seen in only 1–2% of cases that involve intra-abdominal structures. Within this range, bowel entrapment within the fractured segments accounts for only a very small number of cases. Our case, a traumatic pelvic fracture resulting in ileal obstruction due to herniation and entrapment at the fracture site, is one of them. As of 2019, global reported cases of bowel entrapment in cases of pelvic fractures numbered only 24 [1].

Another concept that may be applied to our case is that of a Traumatic Abdominal Wall Hernia (TAWH) – essentially the herniation of bowel secondary to abdominal trauma - an entity that was first coined in 1906 [2]. While the logic behind entrapment of bowel within fracture segments appears straightforward, the scientific guidelines for diagnosis of such an occurrence was first attempted by Allan Clain way back in 1964, by labelling them “Traumatic Abdominal Wall Hernias” (TAWH). The criteria that they had laid out included that “the hernia must have appeared immediately after trauma” and that “the patient must have consulted a doctor soon enough for signs of trauma to be identifiable” [3]. A modification to these criteria was proposed by Sahdev et al. in 1992 as hernias that appeared as a direct result of trauma were still unaccounted. The modifications were as follows: “(1) absence of a preexisting hernia in the same location (2) evidence of abdominal injury at patient presentation (3) immediate or delayed development of a hernia, usually in proximity to the site of abdominal injury, and (4) variable presence of a peritoneal sac” [4]. Even without these modifications, Clain's criteria for diagnosis of TAWH held true for our patient and thus, add to the annals of existing literature as being the 21st such case in history [5].

Case presentation

Our patient was an 88-year-old female, who suffered from a fall in her bathroom two days prior to presentation at our tertiary care hospital in Mumbai, India. Immediately after the fall, the patient complained of pain in the left groin region which was dull-aching in nature but was sharp and excruciating on touch. While the patient complained of difficulty bearing weight on her left lower limb, there were no apparent bony deformities at the site of pain. In a few hours the patient developed abdominal distension with obstipation that was gradually progressive and associated with bilious vomiting.

On presentation, the patient was tachycardic, hypotensive and hypoxaemic. After adequate resuscitation, a Computed Tomography (CT) scan was done – with intravenous and oral contrast - that revealed a fracture of the superior and inferior pubic ramus on the left side of the pelvis. A loop of the ileum was entrapped within the fractured segments, causing mechanical bowel obstruction as has been shown in Video 1.

The patient was then taken up for an Exploratory Laparotomy where the entrapped portion of the ileum was reduced but had perforated due to the sharp edges of the fractured bone as is clearly visible in Video 2. A double-barrel ileostomy was fashioned from the site of perforation. In addition, the edges of the fractured segment were freshened and a pelvic binder applied post-operatively. The patient had an uneventful recovery.

Discussion

Falls in elderly are notoriously tricky to handle with more than 75% of hip fractures seen in seniors aged 75 years or older. These account for a one-year mortality risk of 27% [6]. In line with existing literature on the subject, our patient fit into the age category that purportedly has a higher risk of falling and suffering from complications that arise thereof.

The study conducted by Suneja et al. depicts a comprehensive picture of the unmodifiable risk factors that play a role in pelvic fractures. Researchers identified that females were at a higher risk of suffering pubic rami fractures than their male counterparts in the 60–100-year age group. Furthermore, the highest rates of pubic bone fractures were identified to be among Asian women and least among native American men and men from the islands in the Pacific region [7]. While this study was aimed at identifying the epidemiology of pelvic fractures in the USA, the parallels that can be drawn with our patient are obvious.

The events that led up to our patient falling have to accounted for as well, as these are responsible for majority of falls in the elderly. Slipping in the bathroom and falling is a known risk factor, as has aptly been described by Vaishya and colleagues in their study on falls in the elderly population. The risk factors that contribute to the prevalent grim statistics can be classified into 3 overarching factors namely extrinsic (inadequate lighting, unsuitable walking surfaces like loose carpets, faulty footwear etc), intrinsic (diseases like Parkinson's disease/vertigo/visual impairments/hypotension and medications that interfere with balance and equilibrium) and situational (running to the bathroom, household distractions, multitasking while walking etc) [6].

The mechanism of injury resulting in isolated pelvic bone fractures differ between the young and the elderly. Invariably, a high energy force is required to cause pelvic fractures in the young while a low energy force (like a fall from an upright position, as was seen with our patient) is enough to cause them among the elderly. The study conducted by Ghosh et al. identified the incidence of traumatic pelvic fractures to be approximately 3% [8]. Within the same study, researchers also recognise that existing literature on the subject report incidence rates as high as 8–9%, as was seen in the study carried out by Turgut and colleagues [8], [9]. These incidences point to a higher rate of pelvic fracture occurrences which are worrisome as the associated morbidity and mortality rises exponentially.

The principles of “Damage Control” that were first contextual in the capacity of naval ships carrying out their missions despite having suffered damage in the US Naval Forces [10] and were later analogized in the context of Surgery – rapidly terminating a surgery after the initial life-threatening haemorrhage or contamination has been contained and followed up with rectifying physiological abnormalities and definitive treatment at a later stage were obeyed [11]. Our patient's entrapped bowel was first extricated and a stomal opening was created. While the protocol to be followed in cases of bowel involvement is uncomplicated, the dilemma that exists is whether to subject the patient to operative intervention or adopt a conservative approach for the pelvic bone fracture. In cases of isolated pelvic fractures, a non-operative line may be taken. There is a significantly higher 2-year survival rate for operatively treated patients – standing at 82% - when compared to conservative management, which accounts for a survival rate of 61% [12].

Conclusion

•An abundance of research exists over the entity that is “Falls in Elderly”, the rising incidence of which is troublesome. Enhanced awareness of this entity and the preventive measures that have been adopted to stifle this rise seems to be inadequate. Statistics on outcomes after intervention offered to such patients, irrespective of conservative or surgical modes, are abysmal, further highlighting the importance of prevention.•At the time of presentation, it is vital to expedite resuscitation and stabilization manoeuvres to prevent deterioration of the patient's condition and thereby equipping them to withstand the added trauma of surgery that may subsequently be required.•The role of CT is incontrovertible in cases that involve fractures and peritoneal disruption. For comprehensive diagnosis of traumatic cases, it is wise to perform a CT scan to prevent oversight of occult injuries.•The rarity of mechanical bowel obstruction secondary to pelvic fractures is one of intrigue. Although it appears logical for displacement of fractured pelvic bone fragments into the peritoneum leading to injury of surrounding structures, reality appears to defy logic, and these injuries are seen in only a few cases.
