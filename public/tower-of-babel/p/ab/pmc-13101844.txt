Biomechanical Model of Outer Retinal Corrugations and Bacillary Layer Detachment in Rhegmatogenous Retinal Detachment
Authors: Sarath Chandra Varma, Isabela Martins Melo, Aurora Pecaku, Shiva Sabour, Arun Ramachandran, Rajeev H. Muni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigated the biomechanical mechanisms underlying outer retinal corrugation (ORC) formation in rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD), focusing on the swelling dynamics of the outer retina in the presence of subretinal fluid (SRF). A mathematical framework was developed to model retinal hydration and quantify associated structural changes.MethodsThe retina was modeled as a composite material in which the outer retina undergoes diffusion-driven hydration. Fluid transport was represented using diffusion equations, retinal thickness and elasticity changes were captured with the Flory–Rehner hydrogel model. The emergence of ORCs was analyzed as a mechanical instability, with an energy minimization approach applied to predict their frequency and morphology. For validation, theoretical results were compared against optical coherence tomography (OCT) imaging data.ResultsThe model predicts time-dependent swelling of the outer retina, accompanied by reductions in elastic modulus due to hydration. These mechanical changes promote the onset of ORCs, with their spacing and morphology determined by the balance between swelling-induced stresses and retinal stiffness. Predicted ORC patterns agree with OCT imaging, supporting the validity of the framework.ConclusionsThis work provides a mechanistic understanding of RRD retinal swelling and ORC formation. By linking fluid absorption, material properties, and instability dynamics, the framework offers clinically relevant insights that may aid in assessing structural alterations and optimizing management strategies for patients with retinal detachment.

Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) is a sight-threatening condition that requires timely surgical intervention to restore retinal anatomy and preserve visual function. In RRD, the neurosensory retina separates from the underlying retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) due to flow of fluid from the vitreous cavity into the subretinal space through a retinal tear/hole. Although current surgical techniques, such as pars plana vitrectomy, scleral buckle, and pneumatic retinopexy, provide acceptable anatomic reattachment rates, post-repair structural abnormalities within the outer retina can significantly influence functional outcomes.1–3 Outer retinal corrugations (ORCs)4 have gained increasing attention as a key morphologic feature observed during retinal detachment, often serving as a precursor to outer retinal folds (ORFs) and worse visual outcomes.5 Nevertheless, ORCs are only part of the spectrum of abnormalities noted in patients with RRD.

Following rapidly progressive RRD, the ensuing morphologic changes in the outer retina can be systematically classified into distinct stages.6 First, the neurosensory retina separates from the RPE with fully intact outer retinal bands (Stage 1). The lack of contact is followed by a rapid thickening of the bacillary (photoreceptor) layer, which most likely occurs due to intra- and extracellular hydration (Stage 2). Stage 3 marks the appearance of ORCs, further subdivided into two substages. As ORCs begin to form in Stage 3a, the bacillary layer starts to undulate, forming low-amplitude ORCs, which will later progress into the classic Stage 3b, with high-amplitude ORCs. Stage 3b is particularly critical, as it indicates significant structural deformation and is associated with increased duration of the detachment and worse functional outcomes.7 When the retina progresses into Stage 4, there is loss of definition of the ORCs and increasing hyperreflective dots, which is the final step before retinal atrophy with complete loss of photoreceptor inner and outer segments (Stage 5). As a result of these structural changes, retinal reattachment at Stage 3b and beyond is associated with worse visual outcomes.7

The formation of ORCs seems to be associated with diffusion of subretinal fluid (SRF) into the photoreceptor layer/interphotoreceptor matrix, which manifests as undulating deformations in the detached outer retina.8,9 This influx of fluid seems to alter the elastic properties of the retina over time, inducing mechanical stress. Recently, Muni et al.4 and Martins Melo et al.10 proposed a mechanical model based on the minimization of elastic energy to predict the frequency of corrugations observed with optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans. Nevertheless, the mechanisms driving the formation and progression of ORCs remain unclear, and elucidating the transport processes and biomechanical behavior that regulate outer retinal hydration is crucial to advancing this understanding.

In this study, we modeled the retina as a composite material of two distinct layers: the outer retina, which corresponds to the bacillary layer, and the inner retina, which includes all the other retinal layers.10 We developed a mathematical framework to describe the diffusion-driven swelling11 of the outer retina, considering fluid uptake from the SRF, and we quantified the increase in outer retinal thickness over time and its associated mechanical consequences. Furthermore, we explored the implications of outer retinal hydration on its elastic modulus, drawing upon the Flory–Rehner model12,13 for hydrogels. Additionally, we analyzed the formation of ORCs, which arise due to mechanical instabilities, and developed a theoretical model to predict their occurrence based on energy minimization principles up to Stage 3b (high-amplitude ORCs).

Using the mechanical instability parameters obtained from our model, we defined a dimensionless parameter that allows us to determine the time points at which the transitions from Stage 2 to Stage 3a (i.e., from bacillary layer thickening to the onset of low-amplitude ORCs) and from Stage 3a to Stage 3b (i.e., from low- to high-amplitude ORCs) occur post-detachment. Finally, we investigated stress distribution within the outer retina, highlighting its potential role in the progression of retinal pathologies such as foveal bacillary layer detachment (BALAD).14 The findings of this study were validated against OCT imaging data, facilitating a comparison between theoretical predictions and experimental observations.

By integrating fluid transport, elasticity, and mechanical instabilities, this study provides a comprehensive understanding of outer retinal swelling dynamics. Understanding the biomechanical mechanisms underlying ORC formation may have significant clinical implications for the management of RRD. The presence and progression of ORCs reflect a continuum of structural alterations within the outer retina that are associated with detachment chronicity in a rapidly progressive (dysregulated RRDs) and functional prognosis.6,7

Methods

Image Dataset

This dataset is associated with a prospective cohort study that examines the morphological features of retinal detachment using real-time swept-source OCT (SS-OCT) at presentation. It includes high-resolution cross-sectional and volumetric scans that characterize retinal detachments with and without ORCs.4 The data were collected from a prospective cohort study involving individuals diagnosed with primary RRD at a specialized vitreoretinal surgery center, St. Michael's Hospital, Unity Health Toronto, Toronto, Canada, between August 2020 and February 2022. Initial imaging assessments were conducted to determine the presence of ORCs, following the methodology described by Muni et al.,4 using the ZEISS PLEX Elite 9000 SS-OCT (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) and the Optos Silverstone with SS-OCT Ultra-Widefield (UWF) Imaging (Optos, Dunfermline, UK). For fovea-involving detachments, SS-OCT scans were obtained with the tracking function disabled, employing either a 51-line protocol with 50-time averaging for a 12-mm scan or a one-line high-definition (HD) spotlight scan with 100-time averaging for a 16-mm scan. Imaging with the Optos UWF SS-OCT was performed using either a 23-mm extended line scan or a 6-mm single-line HD-volume protocol. The dataset consists of 66 OCT images out of which we used images with more than five corrugations.

Results

Swelling of Outer Retina

To understand the temporal evolution of retinal swelling in RRDs, it is essential to characterize the outer retinal fluid absorption with the onset of detachment. This fluid inflow leads to extracellular hydration, here referred to as outer retinal swelling. In this section, we develop a mathematical framework to model this hydration-induced thickening of the outer retina, incorporating diffusion-dominated transport and boundary-driven redistribution mechanisms.

To model the swelling of the outer retina, we assume an initial outer retina thickness of To,0 = 70 µm.15 At time t > 0, the local volume fraction of the fluid within the outer retina is denoted by ϕ(y, t). For simplicity, we assume that the concentration is uniform along the length of the retina. A constraint is imposed on ϕ(y, t) such that ϕ0 < ϕ(y, t) < ϕmax, where ϕ0 represents the initial volume fraction at t = 0 and ϕmax is the maximum volume fraction the outer retina can absorb.

From a clinical perspective, modeling outer retinal swelling dynamics offers a novel approach to estimate the timing of retinal detachment based on OCT-measured retinal thickness. This is particularly valuable in cases where symptom onset is unclear, as it may help to predict the window for timely intervention.6

In addition to the modulus variation induced by hydration, the outer retina also exhibits structural deformations that, as a compensatory response, will form ORCs. The underlying mechanisms responsible for ORC formation have been comprehensively described by Muni et al.4 Moreover, the hydration of the outer retina results in its stretching, thereby increasing its effective length. This change in length is characterized by the strain γ = [L(t) – Li]/Li, where L(t) represents the elongated length at time t, and Li is the initial length. By linking the decrease in the elastic modulus to both fluid uptake and strain, the model offers a comprehensive framework for interpreting corrugations and outer retinal thickening.

Quantifying hydration-induced reductions in retinal stiffness provides valuable insight into why certain retinal detachments develop pronounced morphological changes in the outer retina. The decrease in Young's modulus observed with progressive swelling likely results from the excessive influx of liquefied vitreous into the subretinal space, which overwhelms the absorptive capacity of the RPE pump. This imbalance facilitates the development of ORCs. The latter can turn into folds after reattachment, a feature that has been linked to reduced postoperative visual outcomes.4,5

This analysis highlights that ORCs are biomechanical responses to geometric and elastic constraints arising during detachment. By coupling hydration-driven modulus reduction with the bending and stretching, the model accurately captures the observed frequencies and amplitudes of corrugations in OCT images. The introduction of the dimensionless parameter An/To offers a scalable measure to track morphological transitions during the detachment progression.

Based on this analysis, we estimate that the transition from Stage 2 to Stage 3a occurs over approximately 35 to 39 hours, whereas the progression from Stage 3a to Stage 3b requires about 72 to 81 hours. These findings indicate that Stage 3a initiates roughly 1.5 days post-detachment, whereas stage 3b begins approximately 4 to 4.4 days after detachment. The ability to mathematically predict ORC formation provides clinicians with a mechanistic understanding of the OCT findings. This model may allow clinicians to anticipate the progression from low- to high-amplitude ORCs, refining prognoses and reinforcing the need for early intervention before advanced Stage 3 cases.6,10

This analysis identifies a critical strain range where bending stress decreases and BALAD onset occurs, aligning well with OCT observations. The identification of a critical strain value for BALAD development highlights the biomechanical tipping point beyond which permanent structural changes occur. The critical strain seems to be attained approximately 8 to 10 days following a rapidly progressive RRD. This may help clinicians recognize impending irreversible damage on OCT and expedite treatment.14

Figure 6 clearly indicates that both the reduction in bending stress and the plateau in elastic stress occur at the same strain value, referred to as the critical strain γc. This suggests that, until this strain is reached, the corrugations continue to grow in amplitude, eventually attaining a maximum. At the critical strain, BALAD occurs, allowing the outer retina to stretch further. The critical strain ranges between γc = 0.42 for α = 1 and γc = 1.1 for α = 2 in Equation 5. These values of α are considered to be closely aligned with strain values obtained from OCT images. The critical strain γc was validated against the OCT images shown in Figures 6b to 6d, where γ = 0.8, 0.48, and 0.57, respectively, falls within the predicted range of γc. Furthermore, Figure 3b indicates that these critical strain values are attained approximately 8 to 10 days after detachment. Importantly, this predicted 8- to 10-day timeframe should not be interpreted as a universal temporal threshold applicable to all cases of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment. In fact, clinically, less than 25% of patients with primary fovea-off RRD exhibit BALAD3. The 8- to 10-day timeframe represents the approximate time required to reach a biomechanical critical strain threshold under conditions of sufficiently large detachment height and progressive hydration-induced swelling. Clinical studies have shown that the duration of fovea-off detachment alone is not a significant predictor of BALAD development, whereas detachment height and the presence of cystoid edema are more relevant predictors. This observation is consistent with the present model, in which BALAD formation is governed by the attainment of a critical mechanical strain rather than elapsed time alone. Furthermore, BALAD occurrence likely depends on additional biological and patient-specific factors not included in the present model, such as pathological cell adhesion factors surrounding the Müller cell cone at the foveal pit.

Model Limitations

The present model is subject to limitations arising from its assumptions. The retina is idealized as two uniform, flat layers of constant thickness, neglecting natural curvature, regional variations, and location-specific attachment properties. Also, the transport of the SRF is treated as a one-dimensional diffusion process with a constant diffusion coefficient, ignoring the anisotropic permeability and the role of RPE pumping. Apart from this, the material properties are assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, overlooking the anisotropic and viscoelastic response of the biological tissues. Due to these limitations, the current model cannot predict the occurrence of outer retinal folds. Clinically, this model is limited to Stage 3a and Stage 3b, due to its reliance on average OCT measurements from cases presenting with at least five corrugations. Moreover, the model validation is based on static OCT images of different patients, rather than an OCT image of a single patient at different time intervals, limiting its ability to predict real-time clinical outcomes for a single patient.

Discussion

This study presents a mechanistic framework for understanding the formation and progression of ORCs up to Stage 3b after retinal detachment. The model begins by characterizing the swelling dynamics of the outer retina due to hydration, governed by diffusion and kinetic uptake of SRF. This swelling is then linked to the elastic modulus using the Flory–Rehner model, establishing a relationship between hydration-induced mechanical properties and retinal deformation. By balancing the mechanical stress associated with corrugations against osmotic pressure, the strain distribution in the outer retina is determined. Furthermore, by minimizing elastic energy, the temporal evolution of ORC amplitude is quantified. Using this, we defined a dimensionless parameter, An/To, and determined that the transition from Stage 2 to Stage 3a and from Stage 3a to Stage 3b occurs at 1.5 days and 4 days post-detachment, respectively. Additionally, by analyzing the bending stresses within the corrugations, we established a critical condition for BALAD formation at 8 to 10 days, identified as 0.42 < γc < 1.1. Importantly, this predicted timeframe represents the approximate time required to reach the biomechanical critical strain threshold under conditions of sufficiently large detachment height and progressive hydration-induced swelling and should not be interpreted as a universal temporal threshold.

Together, these insights provide a way to estimate the timing of morphological changes in RRD and deepen our understanding of the mechanical behavior of the detached outer retina. It has been demonstrated that increasing hydration is associated with progressive retinal abnormalities, which are associated with worse postoperative anatomic and functional outcomes. Understanding the biomechanical processes that lead to the outer retinal morphological changes may inform improved diagnostic and treatment strategies for RRD. Clinically, this work highlights the importance of high-resolution OCT imaging in assessing detachment duration and the severity of structural damage through mechanical biomarkers. Recognizing early signs of ORC formation and understanding their progression may inform surgical urgency and prognostication, aligning with prior work by Martins Melo et al.6,7
