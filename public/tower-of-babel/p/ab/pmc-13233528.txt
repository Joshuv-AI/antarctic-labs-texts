Introduction

Extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) is a rare plasma cell neoplasm characterized by a localized monoclonal proliferation occurring outside the bone marrow, in the absence of systemic multiple myeloma. It accounts for approximately 3–5% of plasma cell disorders and predominantly involves the head and neck region, particularly the sinonasal tract [1].

Patients typically present with unilateral nasal obstruction and epistaxis, symptoms that overlap with a wide range of benign and malignant sinonasal conditions. Imaging plays a key role in lesion assessment and staging. Computed tomography may demonstrate a soft-tissue mass with bone remodeling or destruction, while magnetic resonance imaging provides superior evaluation of soft-tissue extension and involvement of adjacent structures. However, imaging findings remain non-specific, and histopathological confirmation is required for definitive diagnosis [2].

A comprehensive systemic evaluation is necessary to exclude multiple myeloma, as this distinction has important therapeutic and prognostic implications. Radiotherapy is the mainstay of treatment for localized disease and is associated with high rates of local control, although long-term follow-up is required due to the risk of recurrence or progression [3].

The aim of this report is to describe a case of sinonasal extramedullary plasmacytoma with orbital extension and to highlight key diagnostic considerations, particularly the role of imaging and early biopsy in the evaluation of unilateral sinonasal masses.

Case presentation

A 40-year-old patient with no significant past medical or surgical history presented with a 6-month history of progressive unilateral nasal obstruction associated with recurrent spontaneous epistaxis. The clinical course was notable for the recent onset of visual symptoms, including blurred vision, progressive reduction in visual acuity, and intermittent diplopia, along with a decline in general condition.

On physical examination, anterior rhinoscopy revealed a left-sided intranasal mass. The lesion appeared exophytic, polypoid, and friable, with a violaceous-red coloration suggestive of a highly vascularized tumor (Fig. 1). The patient also reported ipsilateral facial pain and hyposmia. Nasal septal deviation toward the contralateral side was observed, resulting in reduced airflow. No cervical lymphadenopathy or other abnormalities were detected on examination.

Anterior rhinoscopic view showing a left-sided exophytic, polypoid, and friable intranasal mass with a violaceous-red appearance, suggestive of a highly vascular lesion.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated normocytic anemia with a hemoglobin level of 9.5 g/dL (normal range: 12–16 g/dL), elevated inflammatory markers including C-reactive protein at 51 mg/L (normal <5 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate at 38 mm/h (normal <20 mm/h). Serum protein electrophoresis revealed a monoclonal gammopathy. Other laboratory parameters were within normal limits.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the sinonasal region demonstrated an enhancing soft-tissue mass centered in the left nasal cavity, extending to the maxillary and ethmoid sinuses. Associated bone erosion, particularly involving the nasal septum and medial orbital wall, indicated locally aggressive behavior (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Axial contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) image demonstrating an enhancing soft-tissue mass centered in the left nasal cavity, extending to the maxillary and ethmoid sinuses, with erosion of the nasal septum.

Coronal CT image in bone window showing lytic destruction of the medial wall of the left orbit (arrow), indicating locoregional extension of the lesion.

Magnetic resonance imaging further characterized the lesion as an infiltrative mass with intermediate signal intensity on T1-weighted images and heterogeneous hyperintensity on T2-weighted sequences. The lesion showed marked, heterogeneous enhancement following gadolinium administration. Diffusion-weighted imaging demonstrated restricted diffusion, consistent with high cellularity. The mass measured approximately 31 × 18 mm and extended into the left ethmoid sinus, orbit, and contralateral nasal cavity through septal erosion (Fig. 4).

Coronal T2-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrating an infiltrative sinonasal mass involving the left nasal cavity and ethmoid sinus, with heterogeneous hyperintensity and ill-defined margins, consistent with aggressive local extension.

The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board meeting, and an endoscopic biopsy was performed. Histopathological examination revealed sheets of atypical plasma cells, and immunohistochemical analysis confirmed monoclonal proliferation with kappa light-chain restriction, establishing the diagnosis of extramedullary plasmacytoma (Fig. 5).

Histopathological examination showing sheets of atypical plasma cells with eccentric nuclei and abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm. Immunohistochemistry demonstrates monoclonal plasma cell proliferation with kappa light-chain restriction, confirming extramedullary plasmacytoma.

A systemic work-up was conducted to exclude multiple myeloma, including laboratory evaluation and bone marrow assessment, which did not demonstrate evidence of systemic disease.

Given the extent of local invasion and the radiosensitive nature of the tumor, definitive radiotherapy was selected as the primary treatment. The patient received a total dose exceeding 42 Gy delivered in daily fractions over 6 weeks, with the aim of achieving optimal local control.

The patient was subsequently enrolled in a structured follow-up program with regular clinical and radiological assessments. At the time of last follow-up, no evidence of local recurrence or progression to multiple myeloma was observed.

Discussion

Sinonasal extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) is a rare localized plasma cell neoplasm that arises outside the bone marrow and is distinguished from systemic multiple myeloma by the absence of diffuse marrow involvement at diagnosis. Although EMP accounts for only 3–5% of plasma cell neoplasms, it shows a marked predilection for the head and neck region, particularly the upper aerodigestive tract, where the sinonasal cavity represents one of the most frequently involved sites [4,5].

The pathophysiology of EMP involves a clonal proliferation of plasma cells producing monoclonal immunoglobulins, typically demonstrated by light-chain restriction on immunohistochemistry. While the exact mechanisms underlying extramedullary localization remain unclear, chronic antigenic stimulation of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue has been proposed as a contributing factor. In addition, molecular similarities with multiple myeloma support the concept of a biological continuum between localized and systemic plasma cell disorders, with a potential risk of progression over time [6].

Clinically, sinonasal EMP most commonly presents with unilateral nasal obstruction and epistaxis due to progressive local mass effect. In contrast to systemic plasma cell dyscrasias, constitutional symptoms are usually absent at presentation. In our case, the presence of visual disturbances and orbital involvement reflects locally advanced disease, which is less frequently reported and indicates aggressive locoregional extension [7].

Imaging plays a central role in the initial evaluation by defining tumor extent and guiding biopsy. Computed tomography typically demonstrates a soft-tissue mass with variable bone remodeling or lytic destruction, particularly when adjacent structures such as the orbit or nasal septum are involved. Magnetic resonance imaging provides superior characterization of soft-tissue extension, with lesions usually demonstrating intermediate T1 signal intensity, heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity, and marked post-contrast enhancement. In our patient, the infiltrative pattern, bone erosion, and orbital extension on both CT and MRI were indicative of aggressive local behavior [8].

Diffusion-weighted imaging may further support lesion characterization, as EMP often demonstrates restricted diffusion related to high cellularity. However, these imaging features are not specific and overlap with other malignant sinonasal tumors. Therefore, imaging should be considered complementary to histopathological confirmation rather than diagnostic [8].

Definitive diagnosis relies on tissue sampling, most commonly obtained through endoscopic biopsy. Histopathological examination typically reveals sheets of atypical plasma cells, and immunohistochemistry confirms monoclonality through light-chain restriction and expression of plasma cell markers such as CD138. An essential step in the diagnostic process is the exclusion of multiple myeloma through a comprehensive systemic evaluation, including serum and urine protein studies, bone marrow assessment, and whole-body imaging when clinically indicated [9].

The differential diagnosis of a unilateral enhancing sinonasal mass with epistaxis includes a wide range of benign and malignant conditions, such as inflammatory polyps, inverted papilloma, lymphoma, sinonasal carcinoma, and melanoma. In this context, the presence of persistent unilateral symptoms, recurrent bleeding, bone destruction, and orbital extension should prompt early biopsy, as imaging findings alone are insufficient to establish a definitive diagnosis [10].

Radiotherapy is the cornerstone of treatment for localized EMP due to its high radio sensitivity, with reported local control rates exceeding 80–90%. Standard treatment typically consists of involved-site radiotherapy with total doses ranging from 40 to 50 Gy. Surgical intervention is mainly limited to diagnostic biopsy or selected cases amenable to complete resection. Systemic therapy is reserved for refractory disease or progression to multiple myeloma [11].

The prognosis of sinonasal EMP is generally favorable compared with systemic plasma cell malignancies. However, local recurrence and progression to multiple myeloma may occur, most commonly within the first few years following diagnosis. Identified prognostic factors include tumor size, persistence of monoclonal protein after treatment, and adequacy of local control. For this reason, long-term follow-up combining clinical, radiological, and biological monitoring is essential [12].

Conclusion

Sinonasal extramedullary plasmacytoma should be considered in patients presenting with unilateral nasal obstruction and recurrent epistaxis, particularly in the presence of bone destruction or orbital involvement. Imaging is essential for assessing locoregional extension but remains non-specific, making histopathological confirmation mandatory. A systematic evaluation is required to exclude multiple myeloma, as this distinction directly impacts management. Radiotherapy is the mainstay of treatment and provides excellent local control. Long-term follow-up is necessary to detect recurrence or progression to systemic disease.
