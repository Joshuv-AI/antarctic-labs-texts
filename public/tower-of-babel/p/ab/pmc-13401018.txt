The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study: Ten years of statistical and methodological contributions☆
Authors: Anthony Steven Dick, Samuel Hawes, Mohammadreza Bayat, Marilyn Curtis, Daniel A. Lopez, Pravesh Parekh, Diliana Pecheva, Raul Gonzalez, Steven G. Heeringa, Janosch Linkersdörfer, Robert Loughnan, Laika Aguinaldo, Kenneth J. Sher, Thomas E. Nichols, Susan F. Tapert, Michael Neale, Anders Dale, Wesley K. Thompson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study has substantially advanced developmental neuroscience through its large scale and open-science framework. This review synthesizes the study’s significant statistical and methodological contributions over its first ten years, organized around the pillars of population neuroscience, longitudinal modeling, and causal inference. We first examine how ABCD’s population-based design has prompted a reconsideration of how effect sizes are interpreted, helping to establish new benchmarks for distinguishing stable, biologically relevant signals from trivial associations in large-N contexts. We detail the computational innovations required to process high-dimensional data at scale, specifically highlighting new analytic tools like the Fast and Efficient Mixed Effects Algorithm (FEMA) framework for mass-univariate modeling and advanced strategies for managing selective attrition and missing data in large-scale longitudinal cohorts. In the domain of longitudinal and multilevel modeling, we discuss the transition from traditional cross-lagged designs to sophisticated frameworks – such as random-intercept cross-lagged panel models, latent growth curves, and parallel process models – that disentangle within-person developmental trajectories from stable between-person traits. We further highlight the study’s role in advancing causal inference in observational research through “G-methods”, marginal structural models, and quasi-experimental family-based designs. Finally, we explore how ABCD serves as a critical bridge for cross-cohort generalizability and lifespan validation using datasets like the UK Biobank. By contributing to new standards for reproducibility and methodological rigor, the ABCD Study has helped move neuroscience toward a “big data” era, providing a comprehensive statistical foundation for understanding the complex interplay between biology and environment during the transition to adulthood.

Introduction

The Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study represents one of the most ambitious undertakings in the history of developmental and population neuroscience. Beginning in 2015, this longitudinal cohort has followed nearly 12,000 children recruited at ages 9–10 into early adulthood, combining deep phenotyping, multimodal neuroimaging, genotyping, and extensive behavioral and environmental assessment. The study’s large scale, open-science design, and sustained longitudinal follow-up have influenced how many researchers conceptualize, model, and interpret developmental processes across multiple levels of analysis (Garavan et al., 2018, Dick et al., 2021).

Meaningful inference in this new era requires a conceptual shift. The field of neuroimaging has historically grappled with a “reproducibility crisis”, often attributed to underpowered samples and idiosyncratic analytical pipelines. The ABCD Study was explicitly designed to overcome these limitations by establishing population neuroscience as a cornerstone of biomedical science of the 21st-century (Insel et al., 2010, Paus, 2010). However, the sheer scale of the data presents a new set of challenges. That is, as statistical power increases, the focus must shift from merely detecting significance to accurately estimating effect sizes and ensuring robust out-of-sample generalization (Marek et al., 2022).

Moreover, the study’s complex multilevel design comprising repeated measurements nested within individuals, families, and sites has necessitated rapid innovation in longitudinal modeling. Researchers are no longer just asking “which brain regions correlate with behavior”, but are now tasked with disentangling within-person developmental trajectories from stable between-person traits, all while accounting for genetic and environmental confounding. This has turned ABCD into a critical testbed for advanced statistical frameworks, ranging from Bayesian integrative models to causal structural equation modeling.

The present paper reviews the pivotal role of the ABCD Study in advancing this new empirical framework. We first examine how the study has redefined expectations for population neuroscience, from establishing robust effect size benchmarks to mapping the genetic architecture of the developing brain (Section 2). We then detail the methodological innovations required to model these complex data, covering advancements in longitudinal mixed-effects modeling, causal inference, and high-dimensional data analysis (Section 3), as well as best practices for handling missing data at this scale (Section 4). Turning to interpretation, we discuss frameworks for evaluating meaningful associations in the era of “big data”, emphasizing effect size stability and practical significance over simple null-hypothesis testing (Section 5). Finally, we look forward to the study’s next decade, exploring the frontiers of lifespan cross-cohort validation and the continued evolution of statistical inference in developmental science. Together with related initiatives such as the UK Biobank (Allen et al., 2012) and the nascent HEALthy Brain Child Development (HBCD) Study (Nelson et al., 2024), ABCD is providing the rigorous statistical foundation necessary to construct biologically informed, reproducible models of human development (see Table 1).

Contributions to population neuroscience: From epidemiology to imaging genetics

The ABCD Study’s scale and design have helped advance developmental population neuroscience, supporting a shift from an era of small-sample associations toward one of large-scale precision estimation. Historically, neuroimaging has struggled with reproducibility, often reporting large effect sizes that fail to generalize. ABCD addresses this by providing the statistical power necessary to detect and accurately estimate the modest, widely distributed effects that characterize complex developmental phenotypes. This population-level approach enables more accurate estimates of small effects and greater generalizability to diverse groups (Owens et al., 2021, Dick et al., 2025). By helping to move neuroscience toward a “big data” era, ABCD allows researchers to better distinguish signal from noise, clarifying subtle links between environment, biology, and behavior that are difficult to resolve in smaller samples. These findings underscore that only when N reaches the thousands do results become robust and reproducible (Marek et al., 2022). This allows for a careful interpretation of practical significance that avoids the “tyranny of large N” (where trivial differences become statistically significant) by prioritizing effect size stability and out-of-sample replication (Dick et al., 2025).

The sampling design: Target population versus acquired sample

Before turning to specific methodological contributions, it is important to be precise about what population the ABCD sample does and does not represent, as this conditions the interpretation of every association estimated from the data. A persistent misconception in the broader literature is that ABCD constitutes a probability sample of United States youth and is therefore population-representative per se. It is not. ABCD enrolled approximately 11,800 children aged 9–11 through a school-based, multi-stage recruitment strategy anchored at 21 study sites whose catchment areas were selected to span the demographic and socioeconomic diversity of the national birth cohort, with embedded twin and sibling subsamples (Garavan et al., 2018). This design yields enormous statistical and genetically informative leverage, but the acquired sample departs from the target population of US children in both intended ways (e.g., the deliberate oversampling of twins) and unintended ways (e.g., site catchment areas that do not constitute a national probability frame, and differential enrollment and retention across socioeconomic strata). Notably, this departure is concentrated in socioeconomic rather than core demographic characteristics. The unweighted ABCD baseline matches American Community Survey (ACS) benchmarks closely on age, sex, and the major race/ethnicity categories, yet skews toward more advantaged families, with roughly 40.7% of ABCD children drawn from households earning $100,000 or more versus 27.3% nationally (Heeringa and Berglund 2020, preprint). This skew has an understandable mechanism. Because ABCD sites must be located near major MRI and research facilities, recruitment carries an urban, higher-cost-of-living bias, and both parental consent and the capacity to attend a one-day imaging and assessment session tend to increase with family financial resources (Heeringa and Berglund, 2020).

Recognizing this, the study team and the broader methodological community have developed design-based and model-based tools to align analyses with a national target of inference. Heeringa and Berglund (2020) provide propensity-based population weights that calibrate the ABCD baseline distributions to nationally representative controls from the ACS via raking (i.e., iterative proportional fitting) on key demographic and socioeconomic margins, alongside guidance for treating site as the primary sampling unit and family as a clustering factor. Building on this work, Si (2025) characterizes the study in precisely these terms: the ABCD sample is a nonprobability sample aimed at national representation but subject to selection and nonresponse bias. Using ABCD cognition data, that work further shows that the choice of auxiliary variables in multilevel regression and poststratification (MRP) can materially affect substantive conclusions. Applied appropriately, such weighting and model-based calibration attenuate demographic and socioeconomic selection bias and bring estimates of characteristics such as age, sex, and race/ethnicity into close correspondence with ACS benchmarks. They do not, however, transform ABCD into a simple random sample, nor do they correct for selection on unmeasured characteristics. Weighting trades a degree of statistical efficiency for reduced bias, and the appropriateness of weighted versus unweighted (e.g., multilevel) approaches depends on the estimand and on whether the design features are plausibly ignorable for the question at hand (Heeringa and Berglund, 2020, Si, 2025). We therefore encourage investigators to state explicitly whether their target of inference is the ABCD sample itself or the broader US population of comparably aged youth, and to choose design-based weighting, multilevel adjustment, or both accordingly. Gard et al. (2023) offer practical guidance for navigating this choice, showing through applied analyses of population neuroscience data that whether weighting changes substantive conclusions depends heavily on the analytic goal and on how strongly the outcome relates to the variables driving selection.

One design feature warrants particular care in the analyses that follow. Specifically, the default ABCD weighting scheme pools the special twin oversample, enrolled from state registries in four sites, with the general, school-recruited sample under an assumption of exchangeability. Heeringa and Berglund (2020) found this assumption defensible for the outcomes they examined, but cautioned that it may not hold for analyses of genetic or familial/sibling effects, precisely the imaging-genetics questions we turn to next. This distinction between the population that was sampled and the population to which one wishes to generalize recurs throughout the methodological issues we discuss below, from imaging-genetics heritability estimates to the handling of selective attrition (Section 4).

Methodological advances in imaging genetics

ABCD has become a primary resource for methods development due to its large, diverse sample and genetically-informed design. Traditional twin/family designs are now complemented by genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) data, allowing mixed-effects models that include a genomic relatedness matrix (GRM) to estimate variance components at scale. By integrating twin/sibling information with genome-wide similarity, researchers can decompose trait variance into additive genetic (A), shared environment (C), and unique environment (E) components (Smith et al., 2023).

The multi-ancestry composition of ABCD has also prompted rigorous attention to population stratification and helped catalyze methods for more inclusive imaging-genetics analyses. However, these analyses have revealed a significant “heritability gap” that remains a central topic of biostatistical inquiry. After stringent quality control to account for ancestral admixture, SNP-based heritability (hSNP2) of most brain MRI measures in 9–10-year-olds was found to be low-to-moderate, ranging around 22%–27% for cortical volumes and ∼19%–22% for diffusion indices (Joo et al., 2025). In contrast, using the ABCD twin sample, Maes et al. (2023) demonstrated substantial biometric heritability, with twin-based estimates (h2) for regional subcortical volumes ranging from .34 to .83 and total intracranial volume reaching .94. We emphasize that neither the high twin-based heritability of brain structure nor the gap between twin- and SNP-based estimates is novel to ABCD; both reproduce well-established findings from the behavioral- and medical-genetics literature. High twin-based heritabilities for global and regional brain volumes have been reported for nearly two decades (Peper et al., 2007), and large-scale consortium GWAS of cortical structure have likewise shown that common variants capture only a modest fraction of this heritability (Enhancing NeuroImaging Genetics through Meta-Analysis Consortium (ENIGMA)—Genetics working group, 2020). ABCD’s contribution here is therefore one of reinforcement rather than discovery.

The apparent contradiction between these findings illustrates why the ABCD Study is a valuable resource for biostatistical innovation. From a methodological standpoint, the discrepancy between SNP-based heritability (hSNP2≈25%) and twin-based heritability (h2≈80%) is known as the “missing heritability” gap. By providing both a large-scale genotyped sample and a significant sub-sample of twins and siblings, ABCD allows biostatisticians to triangulate the genetic architecture of the brain from two different vantage points simultaneously. This dual-track design helps researchers quantify how much of the “missing” signal is due to rare variants or complex epistatic interactions that common-variant SNPs fail to capture, a task that is difficult or infeasible in studies containing only one of these data types.

Furthermore, the study’s longitudinal nature transforms these static heritability estimates into a dynamic developmental narrative. Because ABCD follows the same individuals across the transition from childhood to adolescence, it provides a unique testbed for modeling the Wilson Effect (Bouchard, 2013), which describes the longitudinal increase in the heritability of intelligence and brain structure as children gain the autonomy to select environments that align with their genetic predispositions. For the biostatistician, this creates an opportunity to move beyond simple variance decomposition (ACE modeling) toward more complex gene-environment (G×E) interaction models. The scale of the data ensures that researchers have the statistical power to detect these subtle, time-varying interactions without the risk of over-fitting, a common pitfall in smaller neuroimaging cohorts.

Further innovations include BRAINIAC (Bayesian Regularized Annotation-Informed Integrative Analysis of Cognition), a Bayesian multivariate model that integrates imaging features and prior biological annotations (Zablocki et al., 2025). It estimates the total variance in a cognitive outcome explained by all brain features while using “annotation” information to regularize effects without assuming sparse associations. Trained on ABCD resting-state fMRI and cognitive measures, BRAINIAC demonstrated that incorporating relevant annotations substantially improved out-of-study predictive power (Zablocki et al., 2025). Although primarily predictive, BRAINIAC exemplifies a modern approach to modeling distributed brain-behavior associations, providing variance-component estimates that can guide causal hypotheses regarding which networks most drive cognitive development.

Empirical discoveries

Equipped with this power, ABCD has begun to map the genetic architecture of the developing brain and its behavioral correlates. Consistent with the broader genome-wide association literature, which has established that complex traits are highly polygenic and built from many variants of individually small effect (Visscher et al., 2017), analyses in ABCD confirm that complex brain–gene associations are highly polygenic and intertwined with cognitive and mental health traits. A recent analysis of over 8600 ABCD children examined polygenic scores (PGS) for 33 traits against multimodal brain MRI phenotypes (Joo et al., 2025). Most brain measures have significant but small polygenic associations, detectable only through multivariate approaches. Higher PGS for cognitive ability were associated with larger brain volumes and better task performance, whereas PGS linked to psychopathology tended to show the opposite pattern. That is, they showed modest correlations with smaller regional volumes or lower functional activation. Notably, these effects are subtle. Even the strongest single PGS–phenotype relationship (intelligence PGS vs. crystallized IQ) explained only ∼18% of variance, and most gene–brain associations explain far less. Bivariate tests in ABCD reveal that structural MRI metrics are more consistently influenced by genetics than transient functional measures (see Dick et al. this issue), suggesting that stable anatomical features in youth may be more sensitive indicators of genetic liability than momentary brain states.

Beyond broad trends, ABCD has yielded specific insights into gene–brain–behavior pathways. One study integrated PGS and neuroimaging to examine externalizing behaviors and found that children with higher genetic risk for antisocial behavior had subtly lower cortical grey-matter volume and lower white-matter integrity, while tending toward larger subcortical and total white-matter volumes (Teeuw et al., 2023). Phenotypically, externalizing problems were associated with smaller brain volumes overall, but effect sizes were very small: brain metrics and PGS each accounted for only ∼0.5% of variance individually, and even combined with demographic factors, the model explained under 12% of variance. These results highlight both the promise and limits of imaging genetics—most variation in complex behaviors remains unexplained, yet faint genetic and neural signals can now be detected that were formerly obscured by noise. ABCD’s open-data framework has further catalyzed discoveries by enabling researchers worldwide to test gene–environment interactions at scale (Shi et al., 2024) and explore rare variant effects, such as how copy-number mutations impact brain structure (Kopal et al., 2025).

Leveraging large-scale cohorts for discovery and replication

Validation of gene-brain associations requires massive samples, and ABCD increasingly serves as a critical bridge to adult datasets like the UK Biobank (Shadrin et al., 2021, van der Meer et al., 2020). Borrowing from the framework of polygenic risk scores, researchers have generated polyvoxel or polyvertex scores trained within the UK Biobank and validated them within the ABCD cohort (Loughnan et al., 2022b). This approach has demonstrated robust signatures of gene-brain associations genome-wide across both cohorts and established mechanistic insight into genetically driven brain iron dysregulation (Loughnan et al., 2024) of importance to risk for movement disorders (Loughnan et al., 2022a).

Others have established qualitative links between findings of C4 alleles – significant to schizophrenia risk – in both the UK Biobank (O’Connell et al., 2022) and ABCD (Hernandez et al., 2023). This cross-pollination of datasets requires sophisticated harmonization and “meta-analysis” frameworks, positioning ABCD not just as a standalone study, but as a cornerstone of a global, multi-cohort approach to population neuroscience. By providing the raw material for these high-dimensional comparisons, ABCD ensures that the next generation of biostatistical methods will be more robust, inclusive, and developmentally informed (Makowski et al., 2025).

A comprehensive accounting of every methodological development to emerge from ABCD is beyond the scope of a single review, and our emphasis here necessarily reflects contributions in which the present authors have been most directly involved. Several adjacent lines of work deserve explicit mention. In the predictive-modeling literature, for example, groups outside our own have converged on a relatively standard toolkit for whole-brain, individual-level prediction: notably regularized (e.g., ridge and kernel ridge) regression, together with the use of the Haufe transform (Haufe et al., 2014) to convert predictive model weights into interpretable activation patterns. Work led by Yeo and colleagues has applied these methods across imaging modalities and validated them in both the Human Connectome Project and ABCD (Ooi et al., 2022), demonstrating further that distinct brain-network features carry shared and unique predictive information across cognitive, personality, and mental-health outcomes (Chen et al., 2022). Equally important has been the growth of community infrastructure for reproducible analysis and training. Openly documented analytic resources and educational efforts (e.g., the NowIKnowMyABCD resource hub) lower the barrier to entry for new investigators and help standardize practice across the many laboratories now using these data (Ali et al., 2024). We point readers to these efforts as complementary to the contributions emphasized below.

Longitudinal and multilevel modeling: Applications and extensions

One of the central promises of the ABCD Study is its capacity to illustrate how developmental processes unfold over time. Unlike cross-sectional designs, which provide only snapshots, longitudinal data allow researchers to characterize within-person trajectories, distinguish them from between-person differences, and test how exposures, risks, and protective factors alter developmental pathways. Yet, analyzing longitudinal data of ABCD’s scale invites methodological innovation, as conventional models are sometimes ill-suited for the complexity of the design for specific research questions (Hawes et al., 2025). We note an important distinction at the outset. Most of the longitudinal and multilevel work we review below represents the application of well-established statistical frameworks (linear mixed-effects models, latent growth curve models, and random-intercept cross-lagged panel models, among others) that originated in other empirical contexts and are here brought to bear on ABCD data. This is not a limitation. Rather, ABCD has served as an unusually demanding testbed that stresses these methods at a scale and design complexity rarely encountered elsewhere, surfacing practical lessons about convergence, computation, and interpretation. Where genuinely new methodology has emerged, it has typically taken the form of extensions of existing models to ABCD’s scale and nesting structure, most notably the Fast and Efficient Mixed-effects Algorithm (FEMA) for fitting mixed-effects models to massive imaging datasets, and Bayesian Integrative Mixed Modeling (BIPmixed) for multi-view data fusion under nested random effects. The contribution, then, lies less in inventing new estimators than in adapting trusted ones to a scale and structure that older implementations could not accommodate.

The value of longitudinal data

At its core, longitudinal research is motivated by the recognition that development is a process of stability and change. Whereas cross-sectional comparisons confound age, cohort, and period effects, repeated assessments of the same individuals permit more precise estimation of intraindividual change (Singer and Willett, 2003). ABCD is particularly powerful in this respect, as it will eventually provide over ten annual waves of data, spanning late childhood to early adulthood, with the potential for extended data collection into adulthood. This sampling density enables not only the estimation of average developmental trajectories, but also the characterization of individual differences in growth patterns and the identification of nonlinear or discontinuous change processes (Hawes et al., 2025).

A fundamental consideration in longitudinal modeling is the number of repeated assessments. As Rogosa et al. (1982) (p. 744) famously remarked, “two waves of data are better than one, but maybe not much better.” With only two time points, researchers can estimate mean-level change but cannot distinguish linear from nonlinear growth, nor can they adequately model variability in slopes. Three or more measurement occasions permit more nuanced trajectory estimation, and four or more allow the use of advanced models such as latent curve models with structured residuals (Curran et al., 2014). ABCD’s design thus opens the door to sophisticated modeling strategies that are rarely feasible in smaller-scale studies.

Multilevel and mixed-effects models

Linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) are the workhorse of longitudinal analysis in developmental science (Bryk and Raudenbush, 1992, Singer and Willett, 2003). These models treat repeated measures as nested within individuals, enabling estimation of both fixed effects (population-average trajectories) and random effects (individual deviations in intercepts or slopes, or both). For example, Zhou and Neale (2025) applied LMMs to global brain volumes to identify that youth with suicide behaviors exhibited accelerated reductions in cortical gray matter volume compared to their peers. In ABCD, such models can be extended to incorporate multiple levels of nesting, such as participants nested within families, families within sites, and sites within broader regions (Hawes et al., 2025, Dick et al., 2021). This multilevel structure is crucial, as failure to account for clustering can inflate Type I error rates and bias standard errors. They also allow for the testing of complex interactions. For example, Ma et al. (2025) used these models to show that stressful life events impact white matter maturation specifically in youth with a family history of substance use.

LMMs also allow for the inclusion of time-varying covariates, enabling researchers to examine how exposures such as substance use, stress, or sleep vary within individuals across time and predict short-term deviations in developmental trajectories. These models can also link baseline exposures to longitudinal changes in neurobiology. For example, Shou et al. (2025) used LMMs to demonstrate that higher screen time predicted reduced cortical thickness in the superior frontal gyrus, which in turn mediated increases in ADHD symptoms over two years. This approach moves beyond between-person comparisons (e.g., youth who use cannabis vs. those who do not) to assess within-person dynamics (e.g., does a youth show short-term changes in cognition during periods of heavier use?). This is yet another benefit of a large sample longitudinal data set, where covariates and potential moderators of individual trajectories can be modeled at specific time points, or themselves as time-varying variables.

Nonlinear trajectories and growth models

Development is rarely linear. For example, pubertal maturation (Omary et al., 2025), changes in brain volume (Mills et al., 2016), fetal influences on lifespan brain development (Walhovd et al., 2023), and onset of substance use (Gray and Squeglia, 2018) often follow non-linear courses. Latent growth curve models (LGCMs) within a structural equation modeling (SEM) framework allow researchers to model such trajectories flexibly (Duncan and Duncan, 2009). Extensions of LGCMs, such as piecewise growth models, permit different slopes across developmental epochs (e.g., pre- vs. post-puberty), while nonlinear functions (e.g., quadratic, exponential, logistic) can capture accelerating or decelerating change (King et al., 2018). These models can also incorporate predictors of growth parameters, allowing for the testing of risk and resilience factors (e.g., the impact of social determinants of health and vaccination status on mental health trajectories; Xiao et al., 2022).

In ABCD, LGCMs have the potential to characterize developmental trajectories of brain and behavior simultaneously, testing hypotheses about cascading processes (e.g., how changes in executive function predict subsequent substance use trajectories). They are also increasingly used to parse environmental impacts; for example, Shaughnessy et al. (2025) used these models to demonstrate that residential proximity to green and blue spaces predicted lower initial levels of internalizing symptoms. Notably, such models require careful attention to measurement invariance across time to ensure that constructs retain comparable meaning as children age, a prerequisite for interpreting longitudinal change as true development rather than shifting measurement properties (Widaman et al., 2010).

Recent applications in ABCD have used these methods to identify distinct developmental subgroups. For example, Bellaert et al. (2025) identified specific trajectories of irritability, revealing that “increasing” and “stable high” subgroups were at greatest risk for suicidal ideation. Similarly, Gataviņ CČs et al. (2025) applied latent trajectory modeling to characterize depressive symptoms spanning the COVID-19 pandemic, isolating a “depression-susceptible” class that showed marked increases during lockdown phases. Furthermore, parallel process models have been used to map the co-development of ADHD symptoms and inhibitory control (Pang et al., 2025). Expanding this to psychosis risk, Karcher et al. (2025) modeled parallel trajectories of cognition and brain morphometry, finding that declining cognitive trajectories served as a specific predictor for persistent psychotic-like experiences. In a related methodological advance, Funkhouser et al. (2021) used a cross-lagged panel network analysis to show that internalizing symptoms were central predictors of downstream psychopathology.

Separating within- and between-person effects

A critical methodological advance in recent years is the recognition that many widely used longitudinal models conflate within- and between-person effects. For example, the traditional cross-lagged panel model (CLPM) has been criticized for failing to properly disentangle these sources of variance, leading to potentially misleading conclusions about causal dynamics (Curran and Bauer, 2011, Curran et al., 2014, Hamaker et al., 2015, Hoffman, 2015). Alternatives such as the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) and latent curve models with structured residuals (LCM-SR) address this limitation by modeling stable between-person differences separately from within-person fluctuations (Curran et al., 2014).

These distinctions are particularly relevant in ABCD, where questions often concern intraindividual processes. For example, Buss et al. (2025) used random slopes to quantify the within-person coupling between body composition and internalizing problems, finding that this dynamic link was significantly moderated by early-life adversity. Similarly, other work has applied RI-CLPMs to examine reciprocal associations between ADHD behaviors and family functioning (Saulsbury et al., 2025).

These distinctions have proved especially vital for clarifying digital media effects, where between-person correlations often overestimate causal risks. Deng et al. (2025) applied RI-CLPMs to separate within-person effects from stable trait differences, finding bidirectional associations where time spent on social media predicted subsequent attention problems. Similarly, Nagata et al. (2025) found longitudinal associations between social media use and depressive symptoms, identifying gender-specific susceptibilities.

Beyond behavioral modeling, Mattoni et al. (2025) demonstrated that failing to separate these levels has consequences for neuroimaging prediction. They showed that group-averaged functional connectivity models poorly reflect individual-level heterogeneity, limiting their clinical utility. Models that fail to make these distinctions may instead capture only stable trait-like differences, obscuring the dynamic processes that are central to development.

Modeling sensitive periods, snares, and cascades

Longitudinal models in ABCD also allow tests of sensitive periods, developmental snares, and cascading effects (Hawes et al., 2025). For example, adolescence has been framed as a window of heightened plasticity when exposures (e.g., stress, substance use) may exert disproportionate effects on neurobehavioral trajectories (Casey et al., 2019, Fuhrmann et al., 2015). The “snare” concept highlights how experiences such as school disengagement or heavy drinking can trap youth on altered pathways (Moffitt, 2001, Craig et al., 2015). Cascading models extend this perspective by demonstrating how early disruptions in one domain (e.g., academics or behavior) propagate to long-term outcomes across systems (Masten et al., 2005, Masten and Cicchetti, 2010). ABCD’s prospective, multi-wave design enables direct tests of these theories, provided models can capture nonlinear, cross-domain, and reciprocal effects.

While direct experimental tests of adolescent-sensitive periods, snares, or cascade effects remain rare (Fuhrmann et al., 2015), some examples are already appearing in analysis of ABCD data. For example, researchers are probing timing and susceptibility differences within and across participants using prospective designs and natural variation in exposures. Specifically, multi-time point diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with restriction spectrum imaging (RSI) shows that pollutant exposures are related to microstructural development of white matter: PM2.5 relates to baseline microstructure, while O3 (and NO2 in females) predicts changes over time (Cotter et al., 2024). At the same time, neighborhood context appears to tune vulnerability. In longitudinal models, higher PM2.5 is associated with steeper age-related cortical thinning and surface-area contraction, effects that are moderated by neighborhood disadvantage in subsets of brain regions (de Jesus et al., 2025). Complementing risk, greater greenspace exposure may be protective, and it is associated with more favorable structural development (e.g., greater surface-area growth, attenuated thinning) and, indirectly, better mental-health and academic outcomes (Li et al., 2025b). Related symptom-focused work suggests that days above EPA thresholds for PM2.5 are associated with internalizing trajectories over the subsequent year, underscoring near-term sensitivity to exposure burden (Smolker et al., 2024). Taken together with the conceptual framework above, these results show how sensitive-period, snare, and cascade hypotheses can be operationalized as timing- and context-specific predictions about within-person change in brain and behavior and tested prospectively within ABCD.

Alongside the contextual factors reviewed above, pubertal status and timing are potential moderators that indicate when sensitive-period, snare, or cascade effects are most likely to emerge. Longitudinal ABCD analyzes show that pubertal status predicts subsequent structural change beyond age. Baseline pubertal stage predicts faster cortical thinning in females and greater surface area contraction in both sexes, and pubertal development covaries with increases in fractional anisotropy of white matter (Beck et al., 2023). Complementarily, longitudinal brain-age models indicate that more advanced pubertal stage is associated with an “older” brain age, with small parallel changes over time (Holm et al., 2023).

Family history also marks vulnerability: youth with a family history of alcohol/substance problems exhibit faster frontal cortical thinning from ages 9–13, with no accompanying surface-area or volume effects, an effect most evident in boys (Gonçalves et al., 2024). Taken together with the ambient-exposure and neighborhood findings above, these results suggest that timing (puberty), context (neighborhood), and family history jointly shape when and for whom environmental pressures couple with structural brain change in adolescence, showing how ABCD can be leveraged to test sensitive-period, snare, and cascade hypotheses.

Contributions to causal inference: Design and analysis

The ABCD Study provides a powerful framework for moving beyond association toward causal inference in developmental neuroscience. However, because ABCD is an observational cohort rather than a randomized experiment, causal interpretation requires explicit assumptions and careful analytic design. In longitudinal settings, the central difficulty is that exposures, outcomes, and confounders often co-evolve. This structure gives rise to time-varying confounding, where factors that influence exposure at one wave (e.g., psychopathology or stress) may themselves have been affected by earlier exposure (Robins et al., 2000).

In the context of ABCD, three specific threats to causal validity are particularly salient. First, conventional regression adjustment can induce bias by blocking causal pathways or opening spurious “collider” pathways when time-varying confounders are affected by prior exposure (Cole and Hernán, 2008). Second, selection and attrition across waves, such as dropout or unusable imaging scans, can induce selection bias if participation depends on prior symptoms or exposures. Finally, the study’s multi-level structure (nesting within families and sites) introduces interference and clustering, where outcomes are correlated due to shared environments rather than the exposure of interest.

Design-based solutions: Temporal order and family controls

ABCD’s primary design-based contribution to causal inference is its ability to establish temporal precedence through repeated, high-frequency assessments. Tracking intra-individual change across waves allows researchers to distinguish within-person effects from static group differences. For instance, Shen et al. (2020) leveraged this to demonstrate that ADHD symptoms precede sleep disturbances, helping to disentangle the bidirectional ADHD-sleep relationship. As we noted above, structural equation methods, such as the RI-CLPM, explicitly separate stable trait differences from within-individual cross-lagged effects, providing a clearer window into how changes in one domain (e.g., sleep) precede changes in another (e.g., internalizing symptoms) (Hawes et al., 2025).

Furthermore, the inclusion of siblings and twins supports quasi-experimental family-based designs. By using within-family (co-twin) comparisons, researchers can control for all shared genetic and familial confounding variables, even those that are unmeasured (Wilson et al., 2023). This design effectively narrows the “counterfactual” gap, asking whether a child would have developed the same outcome had they not experienced a specific exposure, using their sibling as a biological and environmental proxy.

Estimator-based solutions: G-methods and propensity frameworks

When the design alone cannot eliminate time-varying confounding, ABCD serves as a testbed for “G-methods” and propensity-based adjustments. A practical entry point is the use of Inverse Probability of Censoring Weighting (IPCW) to address differential follow-up. By modeling the probability of being observed at each wave as a function of prior history, observed cases are weighted to represent the original target population, reducing bias from selective attrition (Li et al., 2025a).

For questions regarding cumulative exposure, such as the impact of sustained substance use, Marginal Structural Models (MSMs) provide a principled solution. MSMs use inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting to create a pseudo-population where exposure is independent of measured time-varying confounders. Recent applications to ABCD demonstrate this utility. For example, Visontay et al. (2025) applied MSMs to examine chronotype’s impact on mental health, while Yang et al. (2022) used propensity matching to assess sleep duration effects. These frameworks enable the estimation of causal contrasts, such as dose–response relationships or the effects of initiation during specific developmental windows, that are often unidentifiable using standard mixed-effects regression.

Mechanistic explanation via longitudinal mediation

Finally, ABCD enables questions of mechanistic explanation by identifying the intermediary pathways (e.g., neuroimaging phenotypes) through which an early environmental factor affects later behavior. Longitudinal mediation analysis in ABCD moves beyond cross-sectional “path analysis” by using distinct waves for exposure, mediator, and outcome. Modern approaches increasingly emphasize interventional definitions of direct and indirect effects to avoid problematic “cross-world” assumptions. When paired with the computational efficiency of FEMA or BIPmixed (both methods are described further below), these causal frameworks allow for a high-resolution, mechanistic mapping of the adolescent developmental trajectory.

Challenges and practical considerations in longitudinal data analysis

Despite the innovations described above, several challenges remain. First, model convergence can be difficult in high-dimensional SEMs, requiring careful selection of estimation methods and sometimes Bayesian approaches (Hawes et al., 2025). Second, the sheer size of ABCD increases computational demands, requiring specialized software and high-performance computing resources (Hawes et al., 2025). In the next section, we discuss some statistical innovations developed with ABCD data that have attempted to mitigate or even circumvent these latter two issues. Third, longitudinal models often require a large number of repeated measures for stability, and missing data or attrition can threaten validity (Enders et al., 2020, Li et al., 2025a). We detail some approaches to dealing with missing data in Section 4. Finally, interpretability must remain central: complex models risk producing technically impressive but theoretically opaque results. In addition, the large size of ABCD can render results that are statistically significant, but may have small effect sizes that are not practically meaningful. In Section 5 we discuss approaches to contextualizing and understanding the results gleaned from analyses of ABCD data.

Advanced innovations for longitudinal statistical modeling

Beyond traditional mixed-effects and SEM approaches, the sheer scale and complexity of the ABCD Study have motivated the development of new computational frameworks that extend established mixed-effects and Bayesian methods to this setting. These innovations address the challenges of high-dimensional neuroimaging data while respecting the study’s nested longitudinal design.

FEMA: Statistical advances for ABCD-scale mixed-effects modeling

As neuroimaging datasets have scaled to tens of thousands of scans, workflows built around flat files and serially fitting models have become unwieldy. In longitudinal settings, LMMs are the principled way to capture clustering (e.g., within-subject, family, site). Conventionally, these models are fit using maximum likelihood (ML) or restricted maximum likelihood (REML) approaches which rely on iterative optimization that is computationally expensive. Applied mass-univariately, i.e., one model per voxel or vertex across thousands of participants and multiple waves, these iterations compound, and even on modern clusters, end-to-end runs can take days. This makes the use of mixed-effects modeling across the whole brain computationally intractable.

FEMA was developed to remove this computational bottleneck (Parekh et al., 2024). Designed for large-scale studies like ABCD, FEMA replaces iterative optimization with a method-of-moments approach for estimating variance components, followed by generalized least squares (GLS) estimation for fixed effects. This allows whole-brain voxel-, vertex-, or connectome-wide estimation to be completed in a matter of seconds to minutes, as opposed to hours or days. The FEMA implementation is openly available as a MATLAB toolbox (github.com/cmig-research-group/cmig_tools), and an interactive front-end for many of the models discussed here is provided through the Data Exploration and Analysis Portal (DEAP; described in Section 3.9).

Core estimator and rationale

The FEMA framework relies on a non-iterative regression estimator for variance components. Concretely, the estimation process (1) computes ordinary least squares (OLS) residuals; (2) forms squares and products of these residuals and the random-effects design matrix, followed by a non-negativity constrained regression to estimate variance components; and (3) uses these estimates to perform a GLS estimation for the fixed effects. Simulation studies and applications to ABCD data demonstrate that this approach yields fixed-effects estimates equivalent to ML/REML solutions with well-calibrated Type I error, while achieving dramatic runtime reductions (Parekh et al., 2024).

The algorithm’s massive scalability stems from three key technical innovations. First, the regression-based estimator is fully vectorized, allowing operations across all voxels or vertices simultaneously without loops. Second, FEMA uses a statistical “binning” approach for the GLS step. Instead of re-computing variance–covariance terms for every single voxel, outcomes with similar error structures are grouped on a regular grid (e.g., binning variables with variance components within 0.05 of each other), and the GLS estimation is performed once per bin. Because neuroimaging data exhibit high spatial correlation in variance structures, this reduces the number of expensive matrix inversions by orders of magnitude with negligible loss in precision. Third, the algorithm exploits the natural sparsity of random-effects design matrices in family-structured data (where most entries are zero), enabling highly efficient linear algebra.

This performance shift moves the bottleneck from estimation to design and inference, allowing investigators to focus on selecting appropriate random-effect structures rather than waiting for code to run. Furthermore, by reducing computational time from days to minutes, FEMA significantly lowers the carbon footprint of large-scale analysis, addressing the growing environmental cost of high-performance computing in the “big data” era.

Longitudinal extensions and non-linear trajectories

FEMA is built to mirror ABCD’s specific nesting structure. At minimum, researchers can include random intercepts for participant (to absorb within-person correlation) and family (to capture kinship). However, traditional software often defaults to a “compound symmetry” assumption, implying that the correlation between repeated measures is constant over time. This is often unrealistic in developmental data; for example, the heritability of a trait (the proportion of variance attributable to genetics) may change from childhood to adolescence.

To address this, the recent extension FEMA-Long (Parekh et al., 2025) incorporates unstructured covariance matrices, allowing random effects to vary as a function of the wave. This permits a more nuanced specification where within-person correlations can follow autoregressive or time-varying patterns. Additionally, FEMA-Long integrates natural splines into the fixed-effects design. This enables the estimation of non-linear developmental trajectories (e.g., cortical thinning or surface area expansion) while accounting for nested random effects and properly propagating uncertainty through the spline basis functions (Smith et al., 2025).

Genetic applications: GWAS and heritability

FEMA-Long also introduces support for performing longitudinal genome-wide association studies (GWAS). In a GWAS analysis, the goal is to estimate the effect of SNP on the phenotype. Typically, one examines the effect of several million of these SNPs (taken one at a time) on the outcome of interest. While cross-sectional GWAS analyses are common and computationally efficient methods are widely available, longitudinal GWAS methods that account for time-varying random effects with scalability to multiple outcome variables do not exist. FEMA-Long addresses this gap and importantly, using the spline basis functions, allows users to model the non-linear interaction of every SNP with continuous variables like age, thereby leading to the discovery of SNPs that show time-varying effects. This novel development opens up exciting new discovery opportunities for both the imaging and genetics communities, while being computationally fast and economically cost effective.

As alluded to before, unlike standard linear mixed-effects modeling approaches, FEMA allows incorporating the GRM as a random effect. For behavior-genetic questions, FEMA can be used to specify an ACE/S parameterization—additive genetic (A), common/shared environment (C), and unique/specific/subject environment (E/S). This lets researchers decompose the variance in a trait (or vertex-wise signal) into A/C/E/S while simultaneously adjusting fixed-effects and maintaining the longitudinal/person clustering. When genome-wide data are available, researchers can compute the GRM, and in the absence of available genetic data, this can be substituted with a kinship matrix (e.g., a kinship value of 1.0 for monozygotic twins, 0.5 for dizygotic twins and siblings, and 0 otherwise). Practically, twin/sibling pairs anchor the separation of A vs. C, while the broader sample tightens the A estimate via realized genetic similarity. Singletons still inform fixed effects and total/residual variance even if they carry no pedigree contrast. In ABCD applications to cognitive and anthropometric phenotypes, this setup reproduces canonical benchmarks (e.g., twin-based h2 for height) and yields heritability ranges for cognition consistent with prior literature, all while accommodating repeated measures and scanner covariates at the scale of large datasets (Smith et al., 2023).

Moreover, FEMA’s estimator is outcome-agnostic. Any set of measurements with a common design matrix can be modeled en masse. This has enabled vertex-wise partitions of cortical morphometry variance into genetic, family/shared, and subject-specific components in ABCD, revealing substantial genetic contributions to thickness and surface area and a dominance of subject-specific variance for sulcal depth. Importantly, the vertex-level maps expose heterogeneity within classical ROIs (e.g., subzones of supramarginal gyrus with different A vs. S proportions), underscoring the value of vertex-wise modeling over ROI averages for genetic/environmental inference (Smith et al., 2024). FEMA has also been integrated into 3D surface-based toolkits for subcortical shape (e.g., vertex-wise radial distance or log-Jacobian), where replacing fitlme with FEMA yields order-of-magnitude speedups in processing time while reproducing expected topologies after multiple-comparisons control (Im et al., 2025).

Caveats, diagnostics, and best practices

As a MoM+GLS approach, FEMA trades the last bit of small-sample efficiency for massive scalability. In severely unbalanced designs or very small n, ML/REML may yield marginally tighter intervals. In practice, simulation and empirical benchmarks show controlled false-positive rates and near-ML accuracy when binning is calibrated. The recommended practice in ABCD (which in some cases also applies outside of the FEMA framework) includes: (1) reporting uncertainty on variance components; (2) testing sensitivity to random-effect specifications (e.g., S vs. S+F vs. S+F+Site); (3) validating against ML on reduced problems; and (4) pairing FEMA with principled multiple comparison control suited to mass-univariate maps. Under these guardrails, FEMA operationalizes best-practice mixed-effects modeling at the granularity and scale ABCD was designed to support (Parekh et al., 2024, Parekh et al., 2025, Im et al., 2025, Smith et al., 2023, Smith et al., 2024, Smith et al., 2025).

Infrastructure for scalable and reproducible analysis

To ensure that advanced modeling capabilities like FEMA are accessible to the broader research community, the ABCD Study has developed cloud-native infrastructure designed to bridge the gap between complex statistical theory and practical application. This infrastructure, exemplified by the Data Exploration and Analysis Portal (DEAP), features a tree-based ontology browser that allows users to navigate the complex hierarchy of the data dictionary, viewing variable-level metadata and distributions before analysis. Through a graphical user interface (GUI), investigators can build sophisticated mass-univariate models without requiring extensive programming expertise. Users can intuitively specify dependent and independent variables, apply transformations (e.g., splines for non-linear age effects), and define nesting structures. The platform’s backend automatically generates the design matrix and executes algorithms like FEMA or Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs), returning vertex-wise results that can be visualized interactively or downloaded.

Beyond its utility as an analytic tool, this centralized infrastructure operationalizes consensus best practices to foster reproducible science. Integrated “Data Warnings” guide users toward appropriate interpretation of specific variables, while “dataset locking” allows research teams to freeze a specific subset of variables and filters. This generates a persistent identifier for the analysis set, ensuring that all collaborators are working on the exact same data cut and eliminating the version-control errors common in flat-file sharing.

Finally, this approach represents a significant shift toward efficient, centralized research infrastructure. By performing computations in the cloud where the data reside (“compute-in-place”), the platform optimizes resource utilization and obviates the need for thousands of users to redundantly download terabytes of raw neuroimaging data. This centralization reduces the hardware burden on individual laboratories while ensuring that analyses are run on consistent, up-to-date versions of the dataset.

Supervised multi-view integration

Beyond computational efficiency, the ABCD Study has served as a critical testbed for multi-view data integration, or the statistical challenge of combining heterogeneous data types (e.g., imaging, genetics, and environment) to predict a single outcome. A primary innovation in this area is the BIPmixed framework (Neher et al., 2026). While standard approaches often “fuse” data by simply concatenating matrices (early fusion) or averaging predictions (late fusion), BIPmixed uses a supervised intermediate fusion method. This allows researchers to simultaneously estimate a shared latent structure across different data “views” while identifying the specific variables most predictive of a behavioral outcome.

The development of BIPmixed was specifically motivated by the need to handle the hierarchically nested design of the ABCD Study. Traditional integrative models that treat participants as independent observations risk biased results due to clustering within families and sites. By incorporating two-level nested random effects directly into the outcome model, BIPmixed allows for rigorous biomarker identification while controlling for this complex sampling structure. The model functions as a hierarchical Bayesian factor model, assuming that a common set of latent factors underlies high-dimensional data across views (e.g., sMRI, fMRI, and Early Life Adversity metrics). A “spike-and-slab” prior distribution handles variable selection, automatically “zeroing out” irrelevant features to focus on the pathways that are jointly informative of the outcome. Empirical validation using ABCD baseline data has shown that this approach improves predictive stability for externalizing behaviors compared to models that ignore the nested random effects.

Contributions to missing data best practices

Longitudinal studies of the scope of ABCD inevitably confront extensive missing data. With repeated assessments, multimodal measurements, and large, diverse samples, missingness is an inherent feature of the dataset. Attrition, non-response, skipped items, and modality-specific exclusions (e.g., unusable imaging scans) all contribute to patterns of missingness that, if ignored, can severely bias results and reduce generalizability. Indeed, as (Li et al., 2025a) review, handling missing data appropriately is not a peripheral analytic step but a core component of valid inference in ABCD.

Mechanisms of missingness

Dealing with missing data in any study requires an understanding of the mechanisms that produce it. Rubin’s (Rubin, 1976) taxonomy remains the foundation. In this taxonomy there are three potential mechanisms of missingness. The first, Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) occurs when missingness is unrelated to any observed or unobserved variables. This is rare in practice, but a useful baseline and it does occur (e.g., data lost due to a server malfunction). The second mechanism in this taxonomy is Missing at Random (MAR), which arises when missingness is systematically related to observed covariates, but not to the missing values themselves. For example, imaging data in ABCD are more often missing among younger participants due to excessive head motion, and because age is observed, this mechanism can be accommodated under MAR. Finally, Missing Not at Random (MNAR) applies when missingness depends on unobserved values even after conditioning on observed variables. For example, youth with the most severe externalizing problems may be least likely to complete questionnaires, even after accounting for demographic and clinical covariates.

In ABCD, MAR is often a defensible working assumption. Yet, Li et al. (2025a) highlight that many patterns of missingness in the actual data, particularly for categorical and ordinal measures, create additional challenges. This reality underscores the importance of rigorous sensitivity checks and careful approach to missing data. Historically and even in contemporary investigations, listwise and pairwise deletion are widely used in developmental research. These approaches are simple but yield unbiased results only under MCAR, which is rarely plausible in practice (Enders, 2022). In a dataset of ABCD’s scope, deletion would waste massive amounts of data, disproportionately exclude high-risk participants, and likely yield biased and inefficient estimates. Li et al. (2025) argue that deletion approaches are to be avoided in ABCD and that researchers should adopt modern methods such as multiple imputation, proposensity score weighting, or full information maximum likelihood (FIML).

Modern approaches: Multiple imputation, propensity score matching, and FIML

Multiple imputation.

Multiple imputation replaces missing values with multiple plausible draws from predictive distributions, producing m complete datasets that are analyzed separately and pooled according to Rubin’s rules (Rubin, 1987). Li et al. (2025) applied multiple imputation to actual ABCD data using BLIMP (Enders, 2021), focusing on high-dimensional sets of categorical and ordinal measures (e.g., psychopathology items, socioeconomic indicators). Their analyses highlighted several practical obstacles: sparse contingency tables for low-frequency categories, collinearity among predictors, and instability in fully conditional specification (FCS) models. Simple but principled steps, such as partitioning variables into nominal vs. ordinal groups, collapsing sparse categories, and removing non-overlapping variable pairs, substantially improved convergence and the plausibility of imputed values.

A central empirical lesson from their ABCD analyses was that attrition is non-random. Specifically, they reported that families with lower socioeconomic status were disproportionately missing at follow-up. MI models that incorporated these predictors mitigated bias, whereas naive listwise deletion underestimated associations between risk factors and outcomes. Despite the computational demands, Li et al. (2025a) demonstrated that multiple imputation is feasible at ABCD scale with high-performance computing and careful tailoring of models.

Propensity score weighting.

Propensity score weighting (PSW) offers a complementary strategy. Instead of filling in missing values, PSW re-weights complete cases to approximate the distribution of the full sample under the assumption of MAR given observed covariates. Li et al. (2025a) estimated the probability of being a complete case using sociodemographic covariates (e.g., age, sex, income, parental education, race/ethnicity) and included missingness indicators as predictors. Using gradient-boosted models in the twang R package, they achieved covariate balance, with standardized mean differences below 0.10 across groups.

Applied to ABCD, this method highlighted the same non-random patterns of attrition as MI, with economically vulnerable families and youth more likely to drop out. By re-weighting the analytic sample, PSW reduced bias relative to unweighted complete-case analysis, though the effective sample size remained that of the complete-case subset. This tradeoff makes PSW less efficient than MI, but valuable as a robustness check.

Full information maximum likelihood (FIML).

FIML estimates model parameters directly from incomplete data by maximizing the likelihood of observed data under assumed distributions (Enders and Bandalos, 2001). While Li et al. (2025) emphasized multiple imputation and PSW for handling missing categorical and ordinal measures, they noted that FIML remains a powerful option for structural equation and multilevel models, particularly with continuous outcomes. FIML is computationally efficient, avoids explicit imputation, and in Li et al.’s (2025) re-analyses performed similarly to MI when assumptions were met. However, practical adoption can be challenging since it requires working within a structural equation modeling framework.

Recommendations for missing data in ABCD

Even with careful modeling, some missing data in ABCD are likely MNAR. For example, youth who disengage most from school may be least likely to complete academic questionnaires, even after controlling for observed covariates. In such cases, multiple imputation, PSW, and FIML may still yield biased estimates. Sensitivity analyses are therefore essential: at a minimum, researchers should compare results across multiple missing data strategies (e.g., listwise deletion versus multiple imputation) and assess robustness to alternative model specifications.

Drawing on methodological consensus (Enders, 2022) and empirical insights from Li et al. (2025), we first recommend systematically characterizing missingness. Researchers should visualize patterns and fit logistic (or multinomial) regressions predicting indicator(s) of missingness from observed covariates to identify plausible mechanisms. Second, we recommend abandoning listwise/pairwise deletion in favor of estimators that handle incomplete data directly (e.g., FIML) via principled completion (e.g., multiple imputation), or via PSW, thereby reducing bias and inefficiency. Third, researchers should strengthen the plausibility of MAR by adding well-chosen auxiliary variables as predictors of both missingness and the incomplete outcomes, such as age, scanner/site, socioeconomic indicators, and baseline values so that the imputation and analysis models are conditioned on the drivers of missingness. Fourth, we recommend tailoring the strategy to the data structure. One should distinguish nominal from ordinal variables, collapse extremely sparse categories when feasible, and avoid jointly modeling variable pairs with zero or near-zero cell counts to improve convergence and preserve identifiability. Fifth, researchers should plan for computation at scale by parallelizing imputations and model fitting and, where appropriate, using high-performance resources to accommodate ABCD-sized datasets. Finally, we recommend the prioritization of sensitivity analyses by reanalyzing the same data with alternative reasonable specifications (e.g., different auxiliary sets, with listwise/pairwize deletion) to assess robustness against different analytic decisions.

Missing data is not incidental in ABCD, it is a defining feature. Li et al. (2025) demonstrate that pragmatic solutions, such as partitioning categorical variables, collapsing sparse categories, and tailoring imputation models, are necessary to implement multiple imputation effectively in ABCD. Other modern methods, such as propensity score weighting and FIML, pose different challenges related to covariate balance and model specification. These empirical lessons complement simulation studies (Enders and Bandalos, 2001), underscoring that the challenges are not only theoretical but emerge in real, high-dimensional data. ABCD thus serves as a proving ground for advancing missing data methodology. Addressed rigorously, missing data methods have the potential to remove systematic bias in longitudinal designs. Rather than undermining validity, these algorithms can strengthen inference and ensure that the full scientific promise of ABCD is realized.

Contributions to statistical inference and interpretation: Understanding meaningful associations in ABCD and other large data sets

The ABCD Study offers a level of statistical power and breadth of measurement that has rarely been available in developmental science. With nearly 12,000 participants recruited at ages 9–11-years and followed annually, ABCD affords unusual opportunities to test hypotheses about neurodevelopment, behavior, and health outcomes through adolescence into early adulthood. At the same time, the scale of the dataset creates new statistical challenges that demand a rethinking of conventional analytic practices. In particular, the meaning of statistical significance, the interpretation of effect sizes, and the communication of findings must be carefully reconsidered in the context of “big data” developmental science (Dick et al., 2025).

Effect sizes in ABCD are smaller than predicted

A systematic evaluation of the ABCD baseline cohort by Owens et al. (2021) provides a crucial empirical baseline for effect size expectations in ABCD. By examining distributions across 12,880 correlations derived from 161 varied instruments, the authors demonstrated that effect sizes in this large-scale, population-based study are substantially smaller than traditional psychological heuristics suggest. The median effect size across all associations was approximately |r|=.03, and even under “real-world” analytic conditions, such as controlling for sociodemographics and correcting for multiple comparisons, the median remained approximately r=.05, with the 90th percentile reaching only r=.18. These findings indicate that Cohen’s standard benchmarks (where r=.10 is “small” and r=.30 is “medium”) are severely miscalibrated for the ABCD context, where an effect of r=.05 is empirically “average”. These attenuated effect sizes are not necessarily indicative of measurement failure, but rather reflect the reality of high-powered research within a heterogeneous, non-clinical population. Unlike smaller studies often subject to the “winner’s curse” which artificially inflates estimates in the extant literature, the ABCD Study’s sample size provides more precise estimates of true population parameters, revealing that associations crossing distinct domains (e.g., neuroimaging and behavior) or measurement methods (e.g., cross-reporter variance) are inherently modest. Owens et al. emphasize that in this multi-method framework, “large” effects (e.g., r>.20) are rare and typically reserved for highly interrelated constructs, suggesting researchers must adjust their theoretical expectations to appreciate the cumulative value of small effects.

The problem of statistical significance in big data

The empirical reality of small effects described by Owens et al. directly contextualizes the statistical power dynamics of the ABCD study. In traditional psychological and neuroimaging research, sample sizes have often been relatively small, focusing primarily on whether a study had sufficient N to reliably detect effects of interest (Cohen, 2013). In contrast, because ABCD provides tens of thousands of observations, it possesses the statistical power necessary to detect the small “true” effects characterized by Owens.

We should be clear—this is a substantial benefit of ABCD. However, this power acts as a double-edged sword: there is a risk that many or most associations will yield a statistically significant p-value under the null hypothesis significance testing (NHST) framework, even if the effect is trivially small in magnitude (Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016). This phenomenon has been described as the “tyranny of large N”, where the ubiquity of significance tests risks obscuring the more critical question of whether an effect is meaningful. In ABCD, there is a strong potential that significance testing provides little information about the substantive importance of a finding. This necessitates reorienting inference away from dichotomous decisions about significance and toward the interpretation of effect size and practical meaning (Cumming and Calin-Jageman, 2024, Dick et al., 2025, Cohen, 2013).

Effect sizes: From detection to interpretation

A simple definition of effect size is the magnitude of an observed relationship, independent of sample size (Cohen, 2013, Kelley and Preacher, 2012). In practice, effect sizes come in several interchangeable but context-dependent forms. For group contrasts, standardized mean differences such as Cohen’s d (with small-sample bias correction via Hedges’ g) quantify how far apart two means are in pooled SD units and are most interpretable when the underlying scale is reasonably homogeneous across groups. For continuous associations, the r-family (Pearson r, point-biserial r, semipartial r) provides scale-free indices. When the substantive question is variance attribution, R2 and related measures (e.g., η2; or, in multilevel models, marginal vs. conditional R2) summarize explanatory power, but they are sensitive to model specification, the inclusion of random effects, and shrinkage. Reporting which R2 (and how it was computed) is therefore crucial.

For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) and risk ratios (RRs) communicate changes in event likelihood. Because ORs overstate effects when outcomes are common, RRs or marginal risk differences are often preferable, or ORs should be accompanied by baseline risks and predicted probabilities. Finally, raw (unstandardized) regression coefficients frequently carry the most policy relevance, directly linking predictors to units people care about (e.g., “each additional hour of sleep predicts a 0.5-point increase in GPA”). Accompanying standardized coefficients complement them by placing predictors on a common standard deviation scale, facilitating comparisons across different variables and models. Each of these metrics has advantages and limitations. For example, r facilitates comparisons across studies but is sensitive to base rates (Gignac and Szodorai, 2016). Cohen’s d can be misleading if it does not control for possible confounding covariates. Researchers using ABCD must carefully select and justify their chosen effect size metrics, grounding their interpretation in context.

Smallest effect size of interest (SESOI)

One way to address the interpretation problem is to explicitly define the smallest effect size of interest (SESOI; Lakens, 2017). The SESOI represents a threshold below which effects are considered practically negligible, even if statistically significant. SESOI definitions can be informed by theoretical models, prior meta-analyses, clinical benchmarks, or policy considerations. For instance, in clinical trials of ADHD treatment, an effect explaining at least 5% of the variance in symptom reduction may be considered meaningful, whereas in population neuroscience, effects as small as 1% may be important if they accumulate across multiple risk factors. But this must be justified, and not rest on arbitrary benchmarks. Cohens 2013 conventions for small (d=0.2, r=0.1), medium (d=0.5, r=0.3), and large (d=0.8, r=0.5) effects are widely cited, but were never intended as universal standards. In ABCD, a correlation of r=0.10 might represent an effect with major public health implications if the predictor is modifiable and the outcome consequential. Conversely, a correlation of r=0.20 might be trivial if the predictor is immutable or if the outcome lacks practical importance. The selection of the SESOI must be made a priori and based on firm theoretical and contextual reasoning.

Equivalence testing and estimation approaches

Beyond defining SESOI, researchers can explicitly test whether observed effects are meaningfully different from zero or from trivial thresholds. Equivalence testing allows researchers to reject effects that are smaller than a pre-specified bound, thereby providing stronger evidence about the absence of a meaningful effect (Lakens et al., 2018). For example, one might test whether an observed correlation falls within the interval [−0.1,0.1], concluding that the effect is statistically equivalent to zero if the 90% confidence interval is contained within that range.

Estimation approaches further encourage researchers to present confidence intervals around effect sizes, emphasizing precision and uncertainty rather than dichotomous decisions. Visualization of these estimates through estimation plots, forest plots, or distributional overlays can further enhance interpretability (Cumming and Calin-Jageman, 2024). In ABCD, such approaches are especially important because small effect sizes can nevertheless be highly reliable given the large sample, and visualizations can contextualize whether those effects are meaningful in practice.

Replication, benchmarks, and meta-science

Large-scale datasets like ABCD are invaluable for benchmarking effects across developmental science. By providing high-powered, population-based estimates, ABCD can help calibrate expectations about effect sizes in psychology and neuroscience. This role is particularly important in the context of the replication crisis, where many small-sample studies have produced inflated or unstable effect size estimates (Collaboration, 2015). ABCD offers a corrective by demonstrating that many effects in developmental science are smaller than traditionally assumed. At the same time, replication within ABCD is not trivial: given the breadth of measures, analytical flexibility can lead to “researcher degrees of freedom” and selective reporting (Simmons et al., 2011). Thus, transparent reporting, pre-registration, and sharing of code are important for ensuring reproducibility.

Context-dependent meaningfulness

It is essential to emphasize that the meaningfulness of an effect is not determined solely by magnitude. Instead, context matters. Consider three examples: (1) A correlation of r=0.05 between parental monitoring and adolescent risk-taking may seem small, but if replicated consistently across populations, it could have substantial policy implications when scaled to millions of families; (2) A genetic variant explaining 0.2% of variance in brain volume may be theoretically important if it implicates a novel biological pathway, even though its direct predictive utility is limited; (3) An intervention producing a standardized mean difference of d=0.25 in reading scores may be highly meaningful in education policy, given the downstream impact on academic achievement and life outcomes. In each case, the evaluation of “meaningfulness” depends on the stakes of the outcome, the modifiability of the predictor, and the cumulative effect across multiple factors.

Implications for ABCD and future large-scale studies

The interpretive challenges and opportunities in ABCD exemplify the broader issues of handling big data in developmental science. To maximize the value of such datasets, researchers should (1) define SESOI based on theory, prior evidence, and practical considerations; (2) report effect sizes with confidence intervals, and contextualize them against external benchmarks; (3) use equivalence testing to evaluate the absence of meaningful effects, if effects turn out to be small in magnitude; (4) responsibly visualize results to communicate patterns beyond p-values; (5) emphasize reproducibility through open science practices.

Ultimately, handling big data in developmental science requires a shift from focusing on whether an effect exists to whether it matters. By adopting this perspective, researchers can ensure that the extraordinary statistical power of ABCD translates into meaningful scientific progress rather than misleadingly significant but trivial findings.

Looking forward: The next decade of ABCD

As the ABCD Study moves into its second decade, the focus is shifting from baseline characterization to the modeling of complex developmental transitions. The upcoming waves of data will be critical for capturing the “turning points” of late adolescence and the emergence of adult psychopathology. Statistically, this will require further refinement of time-varying effect models and “G-methods” to handle the increasing complexity of time-varying confounding and cumulative environmental exposures.

A major frontier lies in the integration of ABCD with other “big data” cohorts. As cross-cohort validation methods addressing brain-wide associations become more sophisticated, researchers will be able to map the genetic and neural markers of development across the entire lifespan—from the prenatal period captured by the HBCD study to the aging brain in the UK Biobank. Furthermore, the continued evolution of machine learning and Bayesian hierarchical frameworks will allow for “precision” developmental neuroscience, where individual-level predictions can be made with enough stability to eventually inform clinical intervention. Ultimately, an important part of ABCD’s legacy will be defined not just by the data it collected, but by the rigorous statistical standards it established for the field of human neuroscience.

Finally, the emphasis on large-scale, population-based designs throughout this review should not be read as a claim that “big data” studies are the only, or always the best, path to discovery. Small-N, densely sampled designs remain essential and complementary. Precision-mapping approaches that acquire many hours of data per individual, exemplified by the Midnight Scan Club and related work, can resolve individual-specific functional organization that is obscured by group averaging, and are often far better suited to mechanistic and physiological questions than broad cohorts like ABCD (Gordon et al., 2017). The two paradigms address different goals. ABCD’s strength lies in precise, generalizable estimation of typically modest effects across a heterogeneous population (Marek et al., 2022), whereas dense within-person sampling trades breadth for depth and individual-level fidelity. For many focused questions (e.g., those requiring specific task designs, specialized acquisitions, or fine-grained physiological measures that ABCD’s omnibus protocol cannot include), ABCD is either underpowered for the specific construct or best used in an exploratory, hypothesis-generating mode, with the resulting hypotheses then tested using more targeted measures in smaller, deeply phenotyped samples. We regard these approaches as synergistic. Thus, large cohorts calibrate expectations and identify robust candidate effects, while focused studies probe their mechanisms.
