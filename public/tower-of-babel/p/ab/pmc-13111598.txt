Highly ordered mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes with tunable pore periodicity via self-limiting modular monolayer assembly of monomicelles
Authors: Pengfei Zhang, Liangliang Liu, Wanhai Zhou, Shixiang Ding, Zirui Lv, Linlin Duan, Zaiwang Zhao, Yun Tang, Dongliang Chao, Wei Li, Dongyuan Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrecisely controlling the two-dimensional (2-D) modular assembly of monolayer micelle building blocks into free-standing highly-ordered mesoporous crystalline nanomeshes with adjustable pore periodicity and configuration remains a significant challenge. Here, highly-uniform free-standing ultrathin mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes with periodically arranged pore arrays have been synthesized via a self-limiting modular monolayer assembly of monomicelles. The resultant free-standing mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes are perforated by monolayer long-range ordered hexagonal pore arrays with through mesopores ( ~25 nm in diameter), forming highly periodic 2-D mesostructures with a thickness of ~17 nm and a specific surface area of ~97 m2 g−1. The pore periodicity can accurately be controlled from ~30 to 51 nm by varying the precursor-to-template ratio. As a proof of concept, the TiO2 nanomeshes are deployed as anode protectors in Sn aqueous batteries, with a prolonged cycle life of over 1400 h at 1.0 mA cm–2.Subject terms: Synthetic chemistry methodology, Two-dimensional materials, Molecular self-assembly

Introduction

The isolation of graphene and the demonstration of its unique dimension-related properties have sparked enormous interest in two-dimensional (2-D) materials1–3. Recently, 2-D porous crystalline materials (PCMs) have emerged as a major topic in materials science and nanotechnology due to their size-selective molecular transport channels, tunable electronic bandgap, and boosted edge active sites4–6. The integration of these features into an ultrathin monolayer platform makes 2-D PCMs ideal candidates for high-flux semiconducting nanosieves, suitable for applications in separation, sensing, catalysis, energy storage and conversion7–9. The practical utility of these materials, however, critically depends on the ability to induce periodically highly-ordered nanopores to precisely tailor their surface architectures and electronic properties10–12. In this context, significant advancements have been accomplished in synthesizing ultrathin nanomeshes with inherently periodic micropore arrays, such as porous graphene13,14, zeolite15,16 and metal/covalent-organic framework (MOF/COF)17,18 monolayers. Nonetheless, these nanomeshes face considerable limitations regarding their compositions and electronic structures, particularly in terms of developing desirable pores with large sizes and tunable configurations. Such issues substantially hinder the modulation of mass and charge transport19. Therefore, the development of 2-D ultrathin mesoporous semiconductor nanomeshes with periodically arranged mesopore arrays could offer compelling properties, potentially enabling new opportunities for applications.

The soft micelle-directed assembly approaches have been holding great promise for synthesizing 2-D mesoporous semiconductor nanomeshes, offering an exceptional capability for creating customizable mesopores within 2-D lattices from the bottom level. Numerous efforts to develop the block copolymer lithography have been very successful in fabricating high-quality semiconductor nanomeshes featuring ultrathin thickness and highly ordered mesopore arrays20–22. However, achieving such precise structural control of nanomeshes typically requires the complex multi-step processing with high costs and inefficient throughput23. More critically, these microfabrication strategies remain constrained to successive iterations of 2-D material configurations, which intrinsically restricts the compositional diversity and structural programmability achievable through the nanocrystal-based modular assembly in 2-D superlattice engineering24.

Recent developments have demonstrated a 2-D interface-directed assembly paradigm utilizing surfactant-oligomer composite micelles as modular building blocks. These micellar units can self-assemble in a monolayer manner at two-phase interfaces, facilitating the synthesis of diverse 2-D mesoporous architectures with tunable compositions25,26. Nevertheless, current implementations face two critical challenges. First, the inherent thermodynamic instability of oligomer/micelle assembly units promotes the excessive intermicellar cross-linking, even under dilute conditions, resulting in multilayered micelle structures rather than ideal monolayers27,28. While a set of approaches involving the spatial confinement and interfacial constraint have been implemented to enforce monolayer formation for nanomeshes, realizing the highly structural uniformity across macroscopic dimensions remains elusive so far29. The second challenge originates from compromised mesopore ordering mechanisms. The formation of highly ordered mesoporous nanomeshes requires the regular arrangement of composite micelle units within a monolayer at the interface, which relies on the continuous defect correction through the micellar exchange between interfacial arrays and bulk solution during the assembly process30. However, this self-repair mechanism is severely restricted by the confinement-induced suppression of molecular mobility, resulting in irregular mesopore configurations31. Meanwhile, strong interfacial interactions also hinder the lateral rearrangement and cooperative alignment of micellar building blocks at interfaces, disrupting the long-range ordering in micellar monolayers for highly ordered mesopore arrays32,33. The contradictory requirements of confined growth and ordered organization in nanomesh synthesis cannot be reconciled by conventional synthetic methodologies. Therefore, the modular fabrication of ultrathin mesoporous monolayer nanomeshes with periodically arranged mesopore arrays remains a grand challenge.

Here, 2-D ultrathin mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes with periodically arranged mesopore arrays have been successfully synthesized by a self-limiting modular monolayer assembly method of monomicelles. The principal features of this method include the formation of unique charged titania cluster-copolymer composite monomicelle modules with electrostatic stabilization by using acetic acid (HAc) to mediate the hydrolysis of the titania precursors, and the subsequent manipulation of the monomicelle assembly in a monolayer manner on crystalline NaCl salt substrate by centrifugation. They direct the growth of the 2-D ultrathin mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes with highly ordered pore arrays. The free-standing mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes, obtained after the removal of the copolymer template and NaCl substrate, exhibit a highly-uniform ultrathin nanoperforated 2-D morphology ( ~17 nm in thickness) with a large lateral dimension of several microns and monolayered through mesopores ( ~25 nm in diameter). The in-plane mesopores are arranged hexagonally with a long-range order, forming highly periodic mesopore arrays. The periodicity of the arrays (defined as the center-to-center distance between two neighboring mesopores) can be accurately adjusted from ~30 to 51 nm by varying the precursor-to-template ratio. The resultant mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes possess a large specific surface area of ~97 m2 g−1 and highly crystalline ultrathin perforated frameworks, and are proved to be a good Sn anode protector in Sn aqueous batteries. The modified Sn anode with TiO2 nanomeshes shows an enhanced anti-corrosion ability, accelerated surface charge transfer and rapid ion transport, enabling uniform Sn metal plating/stripping and significantly extending lifespan to over 1400 h (only 48 h for the bare Sn anode).

Results

Synthesis and characterization

The highly ordered mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes were successfully prepared by a self-limiting modular monolayer assembly of monomicelles on NaCl salt surfaces with the aid of centrifugation (Fig. 1). Initially, the polyethylene oxide-block-polystyrene (PEO-b-PS, Fig. S1)/titania spherical composite monomicelles could be formed in a mixed acidic solvent containing tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT) as the Ti precursor after stirring at room temperature (Fig. S2). Subsequently, a monolayer of periodically arranged composite monomicelles could be formed on the NaCl salts (Fig. S3) immersed in the composite monomicelle-containing solution, by centrifuging at 2150 × g for 3 min using a plastic centrifuge tube, followed by an aging process. Ultimately, the highly crystalline, free-standing, periodically mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes were obtained after removing surfactant templates and NaCl substrate in turn by the calcination at 400 °C for 3 h and a subsequent washing step with water.

Schematic illustration of the formation process for the 2-D ultrathin mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes with periodically arranged mesopore arrays via the self-limiting modular monolayer assembly of monomicelles.

The charged titania cluster-copolymer composite monomicelle modules are formed in an acidic THF/H2O solution, added into a centrifuge tube containing NaCl crystals (State 1). During centrifugation, the precursor solution spreads into a thin wet film on the NaCl crystal surface, pressing the monomicelles toward the liquid-solid interface (State 2). The like-charge repulsion together with the limited micelle reservoir suppresses multilayer stacking and leads to self-terminated monolayer deposition while excess monomicelles are removed (State 3). Subsequent solvent evaporation concentrates the monolayer and enables capillary-assisted lateral rearrangement into a hexagonally packed monomicellar lattice (State 4). The crystalline mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes are obtained after removing surfactant templates and NaCl substrate in turn by the calcination and water washing.

The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 2a, b) reveal that the resultant annealed free-standing TiO2 nanomeshes display a highly-uniform 2-D morphology with a mean size of ~1.1 μm (Fig. S4). The in-plane through-mesopores can be clearly observed in the TiO2 nanomeshes, which are hexagonally arranged over the whole 2-D architecture to form periodically ordered arrays. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image (Fig. 2c) shows the 2-D highly-uniform ultrathin structure of the TiO2 nanomeshes with monolayered mesopore arrays arranged in a long-range order manner. The high-magnification TEM image (Fig. 2d) further confirms the periodic monolayer mesopore arrays with a pore size of ~25 nm and a periodicity (the center-to-center distance between two neighboring pores) of ~40 nm. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image (Fig. 2e) shows that the mesopore walls are crystalline TiO2 and the lattice spacing of d = 0.35 nm matches the (101) plane of anatase. The selected area electronic diffraction (SAED) pattern (Inset of Fig. 2e) displays well-resolved diffraction rings and numerous spots, further indicating a crystallized anatase nanomeshes. The high-angle annular dark-field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping images (Fig. 2f) demonstrate that Ti and O elements are uniformly distributed within the whole nanomesh. The thermogravimetry (TG) curve (Fig. S5) of TiO2 nanomeshes shows only a minor, nearly linear mass decrease up to 800 °C without the obvious combustion step, consistent with the removal of the PEO-b-PS template during calcination. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) tomography (Fig. 2g) and multimode (Fig. S6) images reveal that the mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes have rough surfaces with a thickness of ~17 nm (Fig. 2h), which is smaller than the mesopore size ( ~25 nm). It suggests that only one layer of mesopores is close packed in 2-D plane.

The powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern (Fig. 3a) shows well-defined diffraction peaks at 25.3, 37.7, 48.0, 53.8, 55.1, 62.0, 62.6, 68.6, 70.2, 73.9, 74.9 and 76.0°, which can be indexed to the 101, 004, 200, 105, 211, 213, 204, 116, 220, 107, 215 and 301 reflections of anatase, demonstrating the crystalline anatase phase (space group I41/amd, JCPDS card no. 01-071-1167) of the 2-D mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes. The TiO2 grain size is calculated from Scherrer equation to be 2.6 nm, consistent with that (2.8 nm) observed from HRTEM result (Fig. 2e). The Raman spectrum of the TiO2 nanomeshes (Fig. 3b) exhibits a strong band at around ∼144 cm–1, which is attributed to the Eg mode of anatase. The 2-D grazing-incidence small-angle X-ray scattering (GISAXS) pattern (Fig. S7) shows two prominent spots extended along qz on the right of the X-ray beam, indicating the in-plane ordered through-pores. The corresponding 1-D intensity profile (Fig. 3c) further displays three peaks at q-values of ~0.156, 0.274 and 0.317 nm-1 (dhkl/d100 ratios: 1.00:0.571:0.493), indexable as 100, 110 and 200 reflections associated with p6mm hexagonal symmetry. The intense 100 peak reflects a d spacing of ~ 40.3 nm, corresponding to a pore-to-pore distance of ~46.5 nm. Nitrogen adsorption−desorption isotherms (Fig. 3d) reveal type IV curves with a small hysteresis at high relative pressure (P/P0 ≈ 0.9–1.0). The Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) specific surface area is calculated to be about 92 m2 g−1. The corresponding pore size distribution curves (inset) derived from the adsorption branches of the isotherms by using the Barrett−Joyner−Halenda (BJH) method clearly show a centered pore size at 25 nm, in agreement with the TEM results. These results could be attributed to stacking-induced effective channels and inter-sheet slits. The BJH-derived feature size serves as a semi-quantitative reference to the dimension of through-pores, consistent with TEM observations. The X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectrum for Ti 2p (Fig. 3e) shows two bands at 465.2 (Ti 2p1/2) and 459.5 eV (Ti 2p3/2), assigned to Ti4+ oxidation states. For the XPS spectrum of O 1 s (Fig. 3f), the two peaks at binding energies of 530.8 and 532.2 eV can be detected, which is attributed to lattice oxygen atoms in a fully coordinated environment and Ti−OH species, respectively.

The effect of TBOT content on the structural parameters of the mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes was investigated. By such a simply regulating synthetic procedure, the monolayer pore periodicity can be well controlled. TEM images (Fig. 4a–d) show that the monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes with different pore periodicities are all highly uniform, indicating the high control of this self-limiting monomicelle assembly. High-magnification TEM images (Fig. 4e–h) further show that all the mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes possess hexagonally-arranged well-ordered mesopores with a similar diameter ( ~25 nm). While the pore wall thickness increases with the uniform neck width (the smallest edge-to-edge distance between two neighboring pores) ranging from ~5.2 (Fig. 4e) 13.0 (Fig. 4f), 18.2 (Fig. 4g) to 26.4 nm (Fig. 4h) as the amounts of TBOT increase (0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 mL), respectively. With the precisely tunable pore walls, the pore periodicity of arrays (the center-to-center distance between two neighboring pores) can be adjusted from ~30.2, 38.0, 43.2 to 51.4 nm. When the amounts of TBOT reach to 0.6 mL, the pore periodicity of the mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes is about 50 nm, without obvious change compared to that of the nanomeshes prepared by using 0.5 mL of TBOT ( ~5 in precursor-to-template ratio) (Fig. S8). It suggests that excess precursor TBOT in the solution could not be assembled with the surfactant molecules into the mesostructures, removed from the NaCl substrates during the centrifugation process. The thickness of pore walls can be controlled by changing the polymer-to-precursor ratio with the constant mesostructure, which is attributed to the preformed stable monomicelles prior to the assembly process.

Moreover, the centrifugation speed, substrate, solvent and relative humidity effects were also investigated on the assembly process of the mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes. At a low centrifugation speed of 424 × g, the obtained nanomeshes obviously show the hexagonal monolayer mesopore arrays but multilayered mesoporous structures at the edge (Fig. S9). When increasing the centrifugation speed, the highly-ordered mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes with homogeneously hexagonal mesopore arrays can be obtained at the centrifugation speed of 955, 1699, 2654 and 3823 × g (Fig. S10). These results clearly indicate that the centrifugal force plays an important role in controlling the formation of the monolayered mesostructures but does not influence their mesophases. In addition, except for NaCl powders, silicon wafers with hydrophilic surface (Fig. S11) and those coated by carbon with hydrophobic surface (Fig. S12) were used as the assembly substrates. On these substrates, hexagonally ordered mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes can be formed by the spin-coating method, similar to those synthesized on the NaCl substrate by the centrifugation process (Figs. S13 and. S14). It suggests that the substrate property is not the major factor governing the formation of the monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes. Furthermore, when the HAc is not added, many thick mesoporous layers can be observed (Fig. S15). Upon increasing the relative humidity during aging to 60 %, the nanomeshes show elongated channels and larger ring-like pores, indicating the micelle coalescence induced by the strong condensation of TiO2 frameworks (Fig. S16).

The assembly process was studied by terminating reaction at different intervals. The clear orange solution was first generated after being stirred at the room temperature (Fig. S2a), indicating that the precursor TBOT has been hydrolyzed under the chelation of acetic acid assembled with surfactants. In this solution, PEO-b-PS/titania spherical composite monomicelles were formed with PS segments as a core and titania-associated PEO segments as a shell (Fig. S2b–d). Theζpotential of such composite monomicelles was measured to be about +15.7 mV in ethanol, implying the electrostatic repulsive interaction between the monomicelles (Fig. S17). After the centrifugation process at 2150 × g, the composite monomicelles were deposited in a discontinuous distribution on the NaCl substrate, resulting in an irregular micellar monolayer (Fig. S18). In the subsequent solvent-evaporation during drying at 40 °C, the monomicelles consisting of the monolayer were arranged with an almost identical intermicellar distance, forming a very regular hexagonal non-close-packed pattern (Fig. S19). With further drying at 100 °C, the patterned micelle protrusions were turned into a continuous film with hexagonally-arranged monolayer mesopores (Figs. S20 and S21). During calcination at 400 °C, the bottom of pores began to open gradually (Fig. S22), forming ring-like through pores in the end (Fig. S23). The nanomeshes on the NaCl substrates after the calcination show an average size of ~1.4 μm, slightly larger than the free-standing nanomeshes, which implys some sample fragmentation during the NaCl crystal removal (Fig. S24).

Electrochemical performances

The mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes were dispersed in ethanol, forming a film on the surface of a Sn foil (Sn@meso-TiO2), acting as the anode protector for Sn aqueous batteries (Fig. 5a). The SEM images (Fig. S25a, b) show that staggered nanomeshes uniformly covered the Sn foil surface, forming a protective layer with a thickness of ∼3 μm. The contact angle of the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode is measured to be about 55.3°, obviously smaller than that of the bare Sn anode ( ~81.5°) (Fig. 5b). It clearly indicates that the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode exhibits a significantly improved electrolyte affinity compared with the bare Sn anode, which would facilitate the uniform Sn plating/stripping. The cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests at various scan rates were employed to investigate the diffusion kinetics for the Sn-ion diffusion (Fig. S26a, b). The Sn@meso-TiO2 anode shows a higher Sn2+ diffusion coefficient (DSn2+) than the bare Sn electrode (Fig. S26c). The ionic transference number of the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode is 0.74, higher than that (0.43) of the bare Sn anode (Fig. S27). The Tafel curves measured in acidic electrolyte (Fig. 5c) show that the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode yields a corrosion current (Icorr) of 9.5 mA cm−2, which is only about a half of that for the bare Sn foil (18.4 mA cm−2). The markedly decreased corrosion current suggests the enhanced anti-corrosion after coating mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes on Sn foils for anode protectors. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) analyses at various temperatures (Fig. 5d, Figs. S28 and S29) reveal that the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode exhibits a much smaller charge transfer resistance (Rct) value than that of the bare Sn anode (0.5 Ω for Sn@meso-TiO2 vs. 5.2 Ω for bare Sn). The activation energy (Ea) of Sn@meso-TiO2 anode is calculated to be 48.2 kJ mol−1, smaller than that of the bare Sn anode (51.5 kJ mol−1), implying that the meso-TiO2 protector promotes an increased surface electron mobility and enhanced electrochemical activity by improved electrolyte affinity. Also, the ordered mesoporous structures of the TiO2 protectors induce a uniform ion accessibility, inhibiting the uneven Sn deposition and high activation barriers at nucleation sites. The diffusion current for the Sn@meso-TiO2 electrode is much higher than that for bare Sn (Fig. 5e), which further confirms the accelerated ion diffusion behavior benefited from the mesoporous structure and improved electrolyte wettability of the mesoporous TiO2 nanomesh protector. The symmetric cell tests (Fig. 5f) show a prolonged cycle life for the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode of over 1400 h at 1.0 mA cm–2 and a very short one for the bare Sn anode of only 48 h, demonstrating a significantly improved cycling stability. The Sn@meso-TiO2 anode achieves a Coulombic efficiency of 99.8% over 700 cycles at 1 mA cm−2, while the bare Sn anode shows a low Coulombic efficiency of 97% and fails in fewer than 100 cycles due to a short circuit (Fig. S30). Under high areal capacity of 5 mA cm-2, the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode still shows a prolonged cycle life of the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode for over 370 h at 5.0 mA cm–2 (Fig. S31). Moreover, Sn@meso-TiO2 anode exhibits lower electrode polarization (within 4.0 mV for Sn@meso-TiO2 vs. over 10 mV for bare Sn), indicating an accelerated surface charge transfer process and ion diffusion. In-situ side-view optical images upon the plating process (Fig. 5g) show that the Sn nucleation on the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode is homogeneous, and a dense and smooth surface is observed. In contrast, for the bare Sn anode, the initial Sn nucleation is inhomogeneous, leading to apparent dendrites growth after 20 min due to the inhomogeneous concentration field caused by a low diffusion. Laser confocal scanning microscopy (LCSM) tests (Fig. 5h and Fig. S32) show a uniform Sn plating for the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode with an altitude intercept of 5.7 µm and a surface roughness (Sa, arithmetic mean height) within 0.6 µm. Whereas the bare Sn anode exhibits large Sn humps with an altitude intercept of 67.4 µm and a Sa of 4.2 μm.

The Sn anodes with a similarly thick layer of TiO2 nanoparticles (Sn@NP-TiO2) were also prepared (Fig. S25c, d). The Sn@NP-TiO2 anode show a much shorter cycle life (90 h at 1.0 mA cm–2) than the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode (Fig. S33a). The EIS analyses (Fig. S33b) reveal that the Sn@NP-TiO2 anode exhibits a higher Rct value than the Sn@meso-TiO2 anode (2.2 Ω for Sn@NP-TiO2 vs. 0.5 Ω for Sn@meso-TiO2). It indicates that the hexagonally ordered through-pores act as ion channels, enabling a high ionic conductivity and homogeneous ion flux, which cannot achieve by the tortuous ion pathways in the non-uniform porous nanoparticle aggregates.

Discussion

In light of the aforementioned results and analyses, we propose a self-limiting modular monolayer assembly process of monomicelles for the synthesis of ultrathin, long-range ordered mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes with adjustable pore periodicity (Fig. 1). In general, the realization of the self-limiting, long-range ordered monolayer assembly is predominantly determined by the electrostatic interactions among the composite monomicelle building modules. Initially, the microphase separation of the diblock copolymer PEO-b-PS, well-dissolved in THF solution, occurs with the hydrochloric acid (HCl) acting as a poor solvent for the PS segments. It induces the micellization of the copolymers into spherical micelles, characterized by a PS core and a PEO shell34. The PEO shell can interact with titania oligomers formed by the hydrolysis of TBOT through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction35. Notably, the hydrolysis of TBOT is controlled by a balance between chelation by acetic acid (HAc) and HCl as a hydrolysis catalyst36. Upon modification with carboxylic acids, the titania oligomers are, in fact, Ti-O-Ti fragments surrounded by ligands37. They exhibit significantly reduced hydrolytic susceptibility due to an energetic barrier to hydrolysis, stemming from the steric hindrance provided by the hydrocarbon periphery coordinated to metal centers38. Simultaneously, the -OCH2CH2CH2CH3 groups of the metal centers, which have a high accessibility to water, can be primarily hydrolyzed39. They are substituted by surface hydroxyl groups that can be protonated to form Ti-OH2+ under acidic condition40. Then, these multinuclear complexes can be thought as clusters with stable structural motifs and positive surface charges (Fig. S34)41. As a result, the composite micelles with such clusters exhibit strong positive charges. The like-charged composite micelles get repelled via the electrostatic repulsion, avoiding aggregation and then forming electrostatically stable cluster-copolymer monomicelle building modules (State 1)42.

In the subsequent centrifugation procedure, the cluster-copolymer monomicelles are spread out on the NaCl salt crystal surface with the formation of a precursor solution film. The upper surface of the thinning aqueous layer in the wetting film presses dispersive composite monomicelles toward the liquid-solid interface (State 2)43. The positively charged composite monomicelles are trapped on the substrate, coating in a discontinuous manner because the strong intermicellar electrostatic repulsion prevents monomicelles from coming into close contact via the attractive van der Waals force44. Once the monolayer structure is formed, the repulsive interaction also hinders the further stacking of excess monomicelle units. Such excess units can be removed by centrifugal force, leading to the termination of monomicelle deposition in a self-limiting manner, thus forming a uniform micelle monolayer on the NaCl crystal surface (State 3). Besides, the relative dilute sols produce a thin wet layer with a limited micelle reservoir to feed more than one layer, favoring the monolayer formation under the energetic bias induced by intermicellar repulsion toward lateral ordering rather than vertical stacking45.

The arrangement and further assembly of monolayer composite monomicelle modules occur during the solvent evaporation in the drying step. As the thickness of suspension films decreases sufficiently, monomicelle units begin to protrude from the film surface. An immersion capillary force, arising from the deformation of the liquid surface, brings monomicelles into close vicinity46. The strong intermonomicellar electrostatic repulsion induces a significant energy barrier against close contact, endowing individual monomicelles with adequate time and mobility to achieve an energetically favorable configuration. Consequently, the monomicelle building blocks arrange themselves in their minimum free energy positions, forming a hexagonally ordered lattice with a consistent intermicelle distance (State 4)47. Meanwhile, under the high temperature conditions, the titania clusters are driven to hydrolyze and cross-link. The assembly process is also affected by the balance between the cluster condensation and PEO-cluster affinity. Aging at low relative humidity induces a weak condensation and high affinity, yielding a narrow pore-size distribution48. The hydrolyzed solvent (n-butanol) infiltrates into the micelles, inducing the swelling of the copolymer chains of PS cores49. The accumulated pressure from swelling is released at the weakest points, namely, the cap of the PEO coronas associated with clusters, leading to the corona rupture50,51. The swollen core-forming chains then shrink and collapse onto the framework walls, forming pores at locations corresponding to the initial micelle cores. Under the subsequent calcination, the small TiO2 particles initially formed in the thin bottom walls migrate toward the larger particles in the surrounding thick walls, driven by minimization of surface energy, thereby giving rise to the through-mesopore arrays49. Also, the copolymer templates can be removed. Finally, the free-standing highly-ordered mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes are formed after washing to remove the soluble NaCl salt substrate.

In conventional micelle-directed assembly approaches, molecular-level interactions between surfactants and inorganic oligomers are usually the main focus52–54. In contrast, the self-limiting assembly of charged composite monomicelles emphasizes mesoscale organic-inorganic composite intermicellar interactions, where the electrostatic repulsion promotes the monolayer thickness and in-plane ordering without relying on externally engineered confinement or patterning. The nanomeshes composed of ZrO2, Al2O3, HfO2 and Zr0.9Ce0.1O2 can also be synthesized (Figs. S35–S38), indicating the versatility of the approach we proposed. This flexible solution-based route offers the precise tunability of pore periodicity and high compositional diversity, while readily yielding large-area inorganic solid nanomesh films on diverse substrates, thus providing a distinct and versatile pathway to ultrathin 2-D inorganic solid nanomeshes. Further pore-size reduction and more precise structural control could involve engineering the molecular weight and architecture of the block copolymers, especially, the volume of the hydrophobic segments, as well as tailoring the speciation and assembly behavior of the inorganic precursors. Such approaches hold the potential to fabricate the finer nanomesh membrane frameworks with control approaching the molecular/atomic scale, thereby broadening their applications in separation, energy conversion and storage.

In summary, the free-standing mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes with periodically arranged mesopore arrays have been successfully synthesized by a self-limiting modular monolayer assembly approach of monomicelles. The charged titania cluster/PEO-b-PS monomicelle block modules are deposited on the substrate in a monolayer manner by centrifugal force and further assembled into a long-range ordered 2-D mesostructure by capillary force due to the intermonomicellar electrostatic repulsion. The free-standing uniform mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes after removing the template and substrate possess a lateral dimension of several microns, thickness of ~17 nm, specific surface area of ~97 m2 g−1 and mesopore size of ~25 nm. They are composed of only one layer of uniform mesopores aligned hexagonally with a long-range into periodic arrays. The implementation of TiO2 nanomeshes as anodic interface protectors in Sn aqueous batteries demonstrates enhanced electrolyte affinity, corrosion resistance, surface charge transfer and ion transport, which contributes to the uniform Sn plating/stripping and ultralong lifespan of over 1400 h. This self-limiting modular monolayer lithography of monomicelles may offer new opportunities in creating programmable 2-D functional mesoporous superstructures with unparalleled compositional and architectural modularization by controlling multielement monomicelle units.

Monomethyl poly(ethylene oxide) (Mw: 5000 g·mol−1) was purchased from Aldrich. Copper (I) bromide (CuBr), styrene, pyridine, and Al2O3 were purchased from Shanghai Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. N,N,N′,N″,N″-pentamethyl diethylenetriamine (PMDETA) was purchased from Acros. Acetylacetone, tetrahydrofuran (THF), anhydrous ethyl ether, petroleum ether, tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT), zirconium acetylacetonate (Zr(acac)4), aluminum acetylacetonate (Al(acac)3), hafnium chloride (HfCl4), cerium chloride (CeCl3), hydrochloric acid (HCl, 36-38 wt%), and acitic acid (HAC) were purchased from Sino-Pharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used as received without further purification.

Preparation of polyethylene oxide-block-polystyrene (PEO-b-PS) diblock copolymer

The amphiphilic diblock copolymer templates were prepared by a well-established atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) method involving two steps. The first step was the synthesis of macroiniator PEO-Br. 20.0 g of monomethoxy PEO5000 was dissolved in 60 mL of THF. Then, 40 mL of pyridine was added to obtain a homogeneous solution. The solution was cooled in an ice-water bath. To it, 3.00 g of 2-bromoisobutyryl bromide (13 mmol) was added dropwise under stirring for 30 min. The resultant solution was further stirred at 30 °C overnight. After cooling to room temperature, 200 mL of cold ether was added to the solution. The white product of PEO-Br was precipitated from the reaction solution. It was washed with cold ether and further dried in vacuum.

In the second step, 3.00 g of PEO-Br (0.53 mmol), 0.08 g of CuBr (0.57 mmol), 0.10 g of PMDETA (0.57 mmol), and 15.0 g of styrene (144 mmol) were added to an ampoules bottle. The bottle, containing reactants, was fully degassed with three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and sealed under vacuum. It was subsequently immersed in a thermostated oil bath at 110 °C under stirring to allow polymerization of styrene. The reaction continued for 3 h, after which the system was cooled down to room temperature. The gel-like product was dissolved in 50 mL of THF and filtered through Al2O3 column to remove Cu complex. Petroleum ether (200 mL) was poured into the solution to precipitate PEO-b-PS block copolymer. The copolymer was then dried in vacuum without the further purification step. The yield was 70 wt% on the basis of initial styrene amount.

Preparation of highly ordered mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes

In a typical synthesis, 0.4 g of TBOT was added to 10 mL of THF solution of the block copolymer (1.0 wt%, containing 0.1 g of PEO-b-PS) with HCl (1.5 mL, 37%) and acetic acid (0.5 mL, 98%), the solution was stirred for ~2 h at room temperature. The obtained TiO2 sol was poured into a plastic centrifuge tube (100 mL) filled with NaCl crystals (30 g), and the centrifuge tube was shaken so that the TiO2 sol completely soaked the surface of the NaCl particles. The mixture was then centrifuged at room temperature at a rotational speed (2150 × g for 3 min). Then they were transferred to a Petri dish and aged at 30 °C (humidity ~30%), 40 and 100 °C for 24 h, respectively. The mixture was calcined at 400 °C for 3 h in air, with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1. Finally, the free-standing highly-ordered mesoporous monolayer TiO2 nanomeshes were obtained after washing by 100 mL of water to remove the NaCl substrate.

Preparation of the mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes with various mass ratio of TBOT to PS-b-PEO

In a typical synthesis, 0.2 g (0.3, 0.4, 0.5, or 0.6 g) of TBOT was added to 10 mL of THF solution of block co-polymer (1.0 wt%, containing 0.10 g of PEO-b-PS) with HCl (1.5 mL, 37%) and acetic acid (0.5 mL, 98%), the solution was stirred for ~2 h at room temperature. The obtained TiO2 sols were poured into a plastic centrifuge tube filled with NaCl crystals, and the centrifuge tube was shaken so that the TiO2 sols were completely soaked the surface of the NaCl particles. The mixture was then centrifuged at room temperature at a rotational speed of 2150 × g for 3 min. Then they were transferred to a Petri dish and aged at 30 °C (humidity ~30%), 40 and 100 °C for 24 h, respectively. The mixture was calcined at 400 °C for 3 h in air with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1. Finally, the monolayer ordered mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes were obtained after the water washing.

Preparation of mesoporous TiO2 nanomeshes at various centrifugation speeds

The obtained TiO2 sol was poured into a plastic centrifuge tube filled with NaCl crystals, and the centrifuge tube was shaken so that the TiO2 sol completely soaked the surface of the NaCl particles. The mixture was then centrifuged at room temperature at different centrifugation speeds of 424, 955, 1699, 2654 and 3823 × g for 3 min, respectively. The obtained complex was kept at room temperature for 12 h to evaporate THF, followed by further heating in an oven at 40 °C for 24 h and at 100 °C for 24 h. They were calcined at 400 °C for 3 h in air with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1, followed by the subsequent washing.

Preparation of mesoporous monolayer ZrO2 nanomeshes

0.40 g of Zr(acac)4 was added to 10 mL of THF solution of the block copolymer (1.0 wt%, containing 0.10 g of PEO-b-PS) with HNO3 (0.5 mL, 37%) and acetic acid (0.3 mL, 98%), the solution was stirred for ~4 h at room temperature. The following procedures were the same with that for the synthesis of TiO2 nanomeshes.

Preparation of mesoporous monolayer Al2O3 nanomeshes

0.40 g of Al(acac)3 was added to 10 mL of THF solution of the block copolymer (1.0 wt%, containing 0.10 g of PEO-b-PS) with HNO3 (0.5 mL, 37%) and acetic acid (0.3 mL, 98%), the solution was stirred for ~12 h at room temperature. The following procedures were the same with that for the synthesis of TiO2 nanomeshes.

Preparation of mesoporous monolayer HfO2 nanomeshes

0.40 g of HfCl4 was added to 10 mL of THF solution of the block copolymer (1.0 wt%, containing 0.10 g of PEO-b-PS) with HNO3 (0.5 mL, 37%) and acetic acid (0.3 mL, 98%), the solution was stirred for ~2 h at room temperature. The following procedures were the same with that for the synthesis of TiO2 nanomeshes.

Preparation of mesoporous monolayer Zr0.9Ce0.1O2 nanomeshes

0.40 g of Zr(acac)4 and 0.04 g of CeCl3 was added to 10 mL of THF solution of the block copolymer (1.0 wt%, containing 0.10 g of PEO-b-PS) with HNO3 (0.5 mL, 37%) and acetic acid (0.3 mL, 98%), the solution was stirred for ~2 h at room temperature. The following procedures were the same with that for the synthesis of TiO2 nanomeshes.

Characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements were conducted on a JEM-2100 F microscope (JEOL, Japan) operated at 200 kV. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images were taken on a Hitachi Model S-4800 microscope. Samples used for TEM and FESEM analyses were prepared by dropping of the TiO2 dispersion in ethanol on amorphous carbon-coated copper grids and silicon substrates, respectively. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) measurements were performed in tapping mode (Multimode IV, Veeco) after depositing the samples on a mica. A silicon cantilever (TESP-SS, Veeco) with a normal tip radius of <5 nm was used after cleaning in UV/ozone for 15 min. Synchrotron radiation small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) measurements were performed at Beamline BL16B of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The d-spacing values were calculated from the formula d = 2π/q. SAXS measurements were taken on a Nanostar U small-angle X-ray scattering system (Bruker, Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 35 mA). Wide-angle X-ray diffraction (WAXRD) patterns were recorded with a Bruker D8 powder X-ray diffractometer (Germany) using Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 40 mA). Nitrogen sorption isotherms were measured at 77 K with a Micromeritics Tristar 3020 analyzer (USA). Before measurements, the samples were degassed in a vacuum at 180 °C for at least 12 h. The Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) method was utilized to calculate the specific surface areas. The pore size distributions were derived from the adsorption branches of isotherms using the Barrett−Joyner−Halenda (BJH) model.

Electrochemical measurements

In-situ optical observation of Sn plating was conducted on an optical microscope (AW33T-4k, Hangzhou Aoweisi Technology Co., Ltd.) equipped with a homemade transparent symmetric cell. The Tafel curves of Sn electrodes were tested on an electrochemical workstation (DH7000, Jiangsu Donghua Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd) at the scan rate of 5 mV s–1. Chronoamperogram (CA) characterization was conducted at the potential of –0.50 V vs. Ag/AgCl in a home-made three-electrode test cell. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) were obtained in a frequency range of 100 kHz to 100 mHz at open-circuit voltage with an AC voltage of 5 mV. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) images of Sn plating in different electrodes were obtained via the confocal laser scanning microscope (CHOTEST, VT6000). Before image acquisition, the electrodes were plated at 5 mA cm–2 with 1 mAh cm–2. The electrolyte wettability was measured by a contact angle measuring instrument (SDC-100), with an electrolyte of 2 M H2SO4 + 0.2 M SnSO4. Sn symmetric cells were assembled in CR2032 coin cells using bare Sn and/or Sn@meso-TiO2 electrodes (diameter of 12 mm) with glass fiber (GFD, diameter of 16 mm) separator. The cycling performance of symmetric cells was tested by the Neware BTS-4000 batteries test system.
