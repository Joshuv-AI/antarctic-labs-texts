The potential mechanism and immunomodulatory effect of gut microbiota-derived metabolites in the treatment of gastric cancer by integrating network pharmacology and molecular docking
Authors: Yi Chen, Rujia Wang, Shijan Wang, Zhelin Xia, Jiabing Wang, Binhui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer is a malignant tumor that seriously threatens human health. In this study, we explored the potential mechanism and immunomodulatory effects of gut microbiota (GM) metabolites on gastric cancer through network pharmacology and molecular docking. The GM metabolites, targets of GM metabolites and gastric cancer were obtained from the public databases. The cross-targets of GM metabolites and gastric cancer were analyzed and the common key targets were further got. GO function and KEGG analysis were conducted to identify potential functions and regulatory mechanisms. A GMMTGM network was built, and then the drug likeness and toxicity of obtained GM metabolites were screened. The molecular docking was employed to assess binding affinity between GM metabolites and corresponding targets. The expression of key targets was validated, and the relationship between key targets with immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoints was analyzed in gastric cancer. We identified eight overlapping key targets between GM metabolites and gastric cancer. HIF-1 and TNF signaling pathways were the main signaling pathways. Furthermore, nine GM metabolites were expected to be as drug‑likeness and low-toxicity compounds, and six GM metabolites had good binding affinity with corresponding protein, and their binding affinities under − 5 kcal/mol. The expressions of AKT1, CASP3, GAPDH, HIF1A and STAT3 in gastric cancer and normal sample were statistically significant (P < 0.01). Moreover, the results of immune-related analysis showed that key genes may mediate the occurrence and development of gastric cancer by influencing the immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoints. GM metabolites primarily exert their therapeutic effects on gastric cancer through multiple targets, pathways, immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoints. Further in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to validate these findings.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Gut microbiota, Metabolites, Network pharmacology, Molecular docking, Immunomodulatory effect

Introduction

Gastric cancer is a common malignant tumor of the digestive tract. According to the statistics of the World Health Organization in 2022, gastric cancer ranks fifth in the number of new cases worldwide and fourth in the number of cancer-related deaths globally [1]. Due to the high heterogeneity and high recurrence and metastasis characteristics of gastric cancer, the clinical prognosis is often poor and the treatment options are limited. The median survival period of advanced gastric cancer is less than one year [2]. At present, the main treatment methods for gastric cancer are surgery and chemotherapy. However, postoperative complications, chemotherapy resistance and adverse reactions seriously affect the clinical prognosis and quality of life of patients with gastric cancer [2]. Therefore, it is crucial to find alternative and effective treatment methods for gastric cancer.

In recent years, driven by technologies such as gene sequencing and metabolomics, the relationship between gut microbiota (GM), GM metabolites and tumors has received extensive attention [3]. The GM refers to various microorganisms living in the human intestinal tract, and the metabolites derived from the GM are the key hubs connecting the gut microbiome and cancer progression, mainly by reshaping the tumor microenvironment and regulating key signaling pathways in cancer cells and multiple immune cells [3]. Studies have found that the use of GM metabolites in radiotherapy and chemotherapy can alleviate the serious side effects of treatment and improve the therapeutic effect [3]. The combination of immunotherapy and metabolites of GM can effectively activate the immune system to kill tumors and overcome drug resistance [3]. Studies have shown that GM-derived metabolites can mediate the occurrence and development of gastric cancer through multiple pathways and mechanisms. For example, during the process of advanced gastric cancer (AGC), the GM metabolite butyrate production in the intestine was significantly reduced [4]. Moreover, the use of butyrate can inhibit the expression of immunosuppressive factors PD-L1 and IL-10 in gastric cancer tumor-associated macrophages in vivo and in vitro, thereby exerting an anti-gastric cancer effect [4]. Meanwhile, butyrate had also been found to play an anti-gastric cancer role by inhibiting the inactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin/c-Myc signaling pathway, suppressing aerobic glycolysis [5] and inducing apoptosis [6, 7]. In addition, treating gastric cancer cells with Lactobacillus acidophilus and butyrate can lead to the upregulation of tumor suppressor cyclooxygenase (COX)-1, thereby protecting the gastric mucosa [8]. Moreover, the synergistic treatment of butyrate and cisplatin could increase mitochondrial apoptosis in gastric cancer cells and inhibited cell growth [9]. Urolithin A (UA) is a natural metabolite generated by the GM metabolism ellagic acid [10]. Hu et al. found that UA inhibits the proliferation, migration and invasion of gastric cancer cells by acting on the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, while promoting apoptosis and autophagy [11]. Therefore, GM-derived metabolites can serve as a key link between the microbiome and the gut-stomach axis, exerting anti-gastric cancer effects by controlling multiple signaling pathways and mechanisms, which is worthy of further study. Furthermore, the GM, GM metabolites, targets and diseases constitute a complex biological network. Therefore, focusing on specific GM metabolites is helpful for understanding the mechanisms of diseases.

This study adopted the network pharmacology method to explore the relationship between the GM, GM metabolites, targets, signaling pathways and gastric cancer from an overall perspective. Furthermore, we conducted the miRNA-target network, the correlations between key targets expressions as well as immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoints in gastric cancer. Methods and materials

Identification of metabolites and targets of GM

The GM metabolites produced by the reported microbes were retrieved from the gutMGene v2.0 database (http://bio-computing.hrbmu.edu.cn/gutmgene) [12]. The related targets of the metabolites were predicted using the similarity ensemble approach database (SEA) (https://sea.bkslab.org/) [13] and Swiss Target Prediction (STP) database (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/) [14], with the species set as “homo sapiens.” We intersected the targets of GM metabolites in the SEA database and the STP database through Venn diagram, representing the common targets for further analysis.

Identification of common targets of GM metabolites in the treatment of gastric cancer

The targets associated with gastric cancer were extracted from the Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/) [15] and GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org) [16] using the keyword “Gastric cancer.” The prediction of common gastric cancer targets is obtained using Venn diagram. The overlapping targets of GM metabolites and gastric cancer were the common targets that could regulate gastric cancer.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

We obtained the protein–protein interaction (PPI) network data from the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) [17], and the interaction score was medium confidence (0.4). Then, we used Cytoscape software to visualize and analyze this network. The MCODE plug-in in Cytoscape 3.7.1 software was used to perform cluster analysis. Then, the cytoHubba plug-in in Cytoscape 3.7.1 software was used to select the top ten key genes based on degree value, maximum clique centrality (MCC) and maximum neighborhood component (MNC). Finally, the common key genes were obtained after cross-referencing these algorithms and considered as hub genes for GM metabolites against gastric cancer.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways analysis

GO and KEGG pathways were analyzed to perform gene enrichment analysis on the selected common key targets using DAVID database (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) [18]. GO enrichment analysis was mainly used to screen biological process (BP), cellular component (CC) and molecular function (MF). KEGG enrichment analysis is mainly used to discover important signaling pathways involved in biological processes. GO and KEGG data were uploaded to the SRplot platform (http://www.bioinformatics. com) [19] for visual analysis. Furthermore, the target-pathway network was constructed by Cytoscape 3.7.1 software.

GM metabolites-target-GM (GMMTGM) network analysis

A GMMTGM network was constructed to explore the interactions between GM metabolites, target and GM, and the network was visualized using Cytoscape 3.7.1 software.

The evaluation of drug‑likeness and toxicity profiles of GM metabolites

The drug likeness and toxicity of GM metabolites were determined through SwissADME platform (http://www.swissadme.ch/index.php) [20] and ADMETlab 3.0 platform (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com) [21], respectively. Firstly, the drug-likeness properties of GM metabolites meeting Lipinski’s rule were selected out, including (1) Molecular weight ≤ 500 g/mol; (2) The number of hydrogen bonding acceptor ≤ 10; (3) The number of hydrogen bond donors ≤ 5; (4) Moriguchi octanol–water partition coefficient (MLogP) ≤ 4.15; (5) Topological polar surface area (Å2) < 140 [22]. The toxicity of hERG blockers (cardiotoxicity), drug-induced liver injury (DILI) and carcinogenicity are all nontoxic in ADMETlab 3.0 platform. The GM metabolites that meet the above criteria are those with drug-likeness and nontoxic properties, which are further studied.

Molecular docking

The structured data files of the selected GM metabolites were acquired from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [23], and the target protein structure was downloaded from the from the Protein Data Bank (https://www.rcsb.org/) [24], including CASP3 (1RE1), IL6 (4CNI), GAPDH (6M61), STAT3 (4ZIA), and the structures were prepared from X-ray diffraction. The PyMOL software was used to remove water and ligands from the protein, and the AutoDockTools 1.5.6 software was used for hydrogenation and charge treatment. AutoDock was used for molecular docking of ligands and protein to obtain binding energy, and the docking results were finally visualized using PyMOL software.

The relationship between key targets and immune cell infiltration levels and immune checkpoints in gastric cancer

TIMER Database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) is a tool for conducting systematic analysis of various types of cancer expression, immunology, clinical and genomic features, as well as immune infiltration [25, 26]. The TIMER algorithm is used to estimate the abundance of six types of immune infiltrating cells (B cells, CD4 + T cells, CD8 + T cells, neutrophils, macrophages and dendritic cells). In the TIMER database, we evaluate the correlation between key targets and the infiltration of six types of immune cells and immune checkpoints (CD274, CTLA-4 and PDCD1).

Expression of key targets in gastric cancer and normal sample types

The UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) is a comprehensive and interactive tool for analyzing cancer omics data [27]. In the UALCAN database, we identified that selected key genes show differential expression in gastric cancer and normal sample types.

The construction of miRNA-target network

A miRNA-target regulatory network was to be built through miRNet database (https:// www.mirnet.ca/) [28], followed by the application of Cytoscape 3.7.1 for visualization.

Results

The identifications of targets of GM metabolites against gastric cancer

Firstly, a total of 251 metabolites were obtained from the gutMGene 2.0 database. 1,265 and 1,289 GM metabolites-related targets were retrieved from the STP and SEA databases, and then 832 overlapping targets were got after deduplication and consolidation, which were considered the main targets of GM metabolites (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, Fig. 2B indicates that there are 23,220 and 28,457 targets corresponding to gastric cancer from GeneCards and CTD databases, respectively. The 12,448 overlapping targets appeared in the Venn diagram were considered the main gastric cancer-related targets. Finally, the intersection between 832 GM metabolites overlapping targets and 12,448 gastric cancer overlapping targets yielded 698 core targets that were used in the follow-up analyses (Fig. 2C). The 698 overlapping targets were submitted to the STRING platform for PPI network analysis, and the PPI network was obtained after the unconnected nodes were hidden (Fig. 2D).

PPI network construction and cluster analysis

The PPI network was visualized by Cytoscape 3.7.1 software, a total of 694 nodes and 12,265 edges were identified in the network. Moreover, the shade of the color reflects the importance of the node in the PPI network (Fig. 3A). Then, we used MCODE plug-in to interpret the feature of PPI network for further insights into the functional significance of these targets. The PPI network can be divided into 21 clusters, and we selected the top two clusters for further analysis based on their score. As depicted in Fig. 3B and Fig. 3D, Cluster 1 had 81 nodes and 1,126 edges with a score of 29.9, and Cluster 2 had 36 nodes and 410 edges with a score of 23.429. Moreover, the GO-BP analysis of Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 was performed (Fig. 3C and Fig. 3E). The results showed that targets in Cluster 1 were mainly related to response to positive regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, positive regulation of cell migration, response to lipopolysaccharide, inflammatory response and cellular response to lipopolysaccharide (Fig. 3C), and targets in Cluster 2 were mainly involved in positive regulation of transcription by RNA polymerase II, negative regulation of apoptotic process, positive regulation of smooth muscle cell proliferation, negative regulation of gene expression and positive regulation of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B signal transduction. In order to screen the key targets, the top 10 targets based on degree, MCC and MNC algorithms through the cytoHubba plug-in in Cytoscape 3.7.1 software were further analysis. As shown in Fig. 4A–4D, cross-referencing these algorithms resulted in identification of 8 core genes, including IL1B, CASP3, IL6, ALB, GAPDH, AKT1, STAT3 and HIF1A, which may be closely related to GM metabolites against gastric cancer.

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis

In order to further analyze the potential functions and regulatory mechanisms of these genes, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were performed. A total of 82 GO enrichment analysis results involved in GM metabolites against gastric cancer were obtained, including 68 BPs, 7 CCs and 7 MFs. The top 10 items in BP and top 7 items in CC and MF were selected, as shown in Fig. 5A–5D. GO analysis results revealed the top five BPs are linked to positive regulation of vascular endothelial growth factor production, vascular endothelial growth factor production, positive regulation of gene expression, cytokine-mediated signaling pathway and positive regulation of cytokine production involved in inflammatory response. The top five CCs were cytosol, protein-containing complex, cytoplasm, nucleus and RNA polymerase II transcription regulator complex. The top five MFs were related to identical protein binding, protein kinase binding, RNA polymerase II-specific DNA-binding transcription factor binding, protein dimerization activity and cytokine activity. Figure 6A shows that KEGG pathways are mainly enriched in HIF-1 signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, proteoglycans in cancer, pathways in cancer, PD-L1 expression and PD-1 checkpoint pathway in cancer, IL-17 signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway. Moreover, the KEGG enrichment analysis can be categorized into three aspects, including human diseases, organismal systems and environmental information processing (Fig. 6B). The GMMTGM network analysis

To illustrate the intricate relationship between these elements, we use the GM metabolites, target and GM to establish GMMTGM network through Cytoscape 3.7.1 software. As shown in Fig. 7, light purple represented GM, brilliant blue represented GM metabolites, and light pink represented targets. The results showed that the 8 key targets corresponding to 84 GM metabolites and 79 gut microbiotas regulating gastric cancer were observed. Meanwhile, the CASP3 had the most connection with GM metabolites, and ethyl phenyllactate (-)-, ginsenoside Rh2, beta-D-fructofuranose and amylotriose were the top four GM metabolites based on their degree value.

The clinical relevance and immune cell infiltration levels with key genes

The eight key targets were imported into the UALCAN database for clinical relevance analysis. The results showed that the expressions of AKT1, CASP3, GAPDH, HIF1A and STAT3 in gastric cancer and normal sample were statistically significant (P < 0.01), while the expression of the others had no statistical significance (Fig. 10A). These result suggested that GM metabolites may affect the development and progression of gastric cancer patients by regulating targets such as AKT1, CASP3, GAPDH, HIF1A and STAT3.

Clinical relevance and immune cell infiltration levels relevance of key genes. A Expression of key targets in gastric cancer and normal sample types, B The correlation between key targets expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cells

We used the TIMER database to analyze the correlation between the levels of eight key targets and the infiltration levels of immune cells in gastric cancer. The results showed that the levels of GAPDH and IL1B were negatively correlated with B cell, CD4+ T cell and macrophage. AKT1 level was negatively correlated with CD8+ T cell and neutrophil, while it was positively correlated with CD4+ T cell. HIF1A and STAT3 expressions were positive with CD8+ T cell, macrophage, neutrophil and dendritic cell. Moreover, CASP3 level was negatively correlated with CD4+ T cell and macrophage, and the expression of IL6 was negatively correlated with B cell, but macrophage, neutrophil and dendritic cell showed a positive correlation with IL6. In addition, the levels of STAT3 and IL1B were positive with CD4+ T cell and neutrophil, respectively (Fig. 10B). To sum up, except for ALB, other key targets may be involved in the regulation of the immune microenvironment of gastric cancer.

The relationship between key targets and immune checkpoints in gastric cancer

We further evaluated the relationship between the key targets and the immune checkpoints in gastric cancer, and the results are shown in Fig. 11. The results showed that the expressions of HIF1A, IL6 and STAT3 were positively correlated with PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4, AKT1 was positively correlated with PD-1 and PD-L1, and CASP3 had a positive correlation with PD-L1 and CTLA-4. Furthermore, GAPDH and IL1B levels had a positive relation with PD-L1, and ALB was positively correlated with CTLA-4 (P < 0.05). These results suggest that the key genes may mediate the occurrence and development of gastric cancer by influencing the immune checkpoints.

Discussion

Studies show GM and GM metabolites significantly impact gastric cancer [4–11, 30]. In the present study, the purpose was to determine the drug likeness and low toxicity of the GM metabolites that serve as a potential therapeutic agent for gastric cancer through bioinformatics approach. Through PPI network analysis, IL1B, CASP3, IL6, ALB, GAPDH, AKT1, STAT3 and HIF1A are obtained as key targets. In addition, we further conducted a clinical correlation analysis of these eight key genes, AKT1, CASP3, GAPDH, HIF1A and STAT3 are regarded as core genes with research value. AKT1 is a signal transduction protein kinase that plays a significant role in cell proliferation, survival and metabolic regulation, and is involved in the transduction of various signal pathways [31]. Research showed that AKT1 is significantly increased in gastric cancer compared to normal gastric tissue and adjacent non-cancer tissue [32]. Moreover, inhibiting the expression of AKT1 can exert growth inhibitory effects on SGC7901 cells both in vitro and in vivo [32]. CASP3 is an important protease in cell apoptosis [33]; gastric cancer patients with higher CASP3 expressions had poorer prognoses in gastric cancer after surgery [34]. GAPDH is usually studied as a housekeeping gene; however, recent research has revealed that it plays a role in promoting cancer in various tumors [35]. Yang found that the expression of GAPDH in gastric cancer is higher than that in normal tissues, but the inhibitory activity of GAPDH on the growth of gastric cancer has not been reported [36]. HIF1A is a transcription factor; when cells are in an oxygen-deficient state, it can be stabilized and activated, entering the cell nucleus and regulating the transcription of multiple genes, thereby adapting to the oxygen-deficient environment [37]. Nam et al. showed that gastric cancer patients highly expressing HIF1A protein might be prone to tumor progression, poorly differentiated gastric cancer cell types and a high vascular endothelial growth factor expression [38]. Recent studies also have found that HIF1A promotes the transcription of genes related to glycolysis, and silencing the expression of HIF1A in gastric cancer cells can enhance the proliferation and activation of T cells in a co-culture system, induce apoptosis of gastric cancer cells and increase the sensitivity of gastric cancer cells to immune checkpoint inhibitors [39]. STAT3 regulates the expression of various genes; however, its abnormal activation is widely observed in various malignant tumors [40]. Overexpression of STAT3/pSTAT3 was associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer [41], and silencing STAT3 could inhibit viability and induce apoptosis in BGC-823 human gastric cancer cell line [42].

The KEGG enrichment results indicated that HIF-1 and TNF signaling pathways were closely involved in the development of gastric cancer. As mentioned above, HIF1A is closely related to the development of gastric cancer, and targeting HIF1A signaling pathway is a potential tactic for gastric cancer treatment. In response to tumor hypoxia, upregulation of hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1) activates the downstream genes participated in crucial aspects of cancer biology [43]. As a subunit, HIF-1α is responsible for the function of HIF-1 and its overexpression is observed in a variety of cancer tissues compared with the respective normal tissues, including gastric cancer. Tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is a multifunctional cytokine that participates in various biological processes such as inflammatory responses, immune responses, cell proliferation, apoptosis and cell adhesion [44]. Previous studies have shown that high expression of TNF-α can induce precancerous lesions of the stomach, such as chronic gastritis, intestinal metaplasia and atypical hyperplasia, and may eventually progress to gastric cancer [45]. Bounder et al. found that elevated serum TNF-α levels are associated with the occurrence and development of gastric cancer. And the results showed that the TNF-α level in the early gastric cancer group is significantly higher than that in the gastritis group and the precancerous lesion group, and it is an independent risk factor for early gastric cancer [46]. Zhou et al. demonstrated that TNF-α upregulated the expression of IL-33 in cancer-associated fibroblasts through the TNFR2/NF-κB/IRF-1 pathway, thereby promoting the migration, invasion and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) of gastric cancer cells [47].

Our study from the network of GMMTGM analysis demonstrated that there were eight key targets corresponding to 84 GM metabolites and 79 gut microbiotas, and they jointly regulate the occurrence and development of gastric cancer. Furthermore, nine GM metabolites with drug likeness and low toxicity were identified, including 2-hydroxy-3-(5-hydroxy-1H-indol-3-yl)propanoic acid, 3-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-hydroxypropanoic acid, 5-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-4-hydroxypentanoic acid, glycerin, pantothenic acid, 2-hydroxy-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propanoic acid, D-xylose, 10-oxo-11-octadecenoic acid and dopamine. More importantly, six GM metabolite–protein complexes, including (2-hydroxy-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)propanoic acid-CASP3, dopamine-GAPDH, D-xylose-STAT3, 2-hydroxy-3-(5-hydroxy-1H- indol-3-yl)propanoic acid-CASP3, 3-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-hydroxypropanoic acid-CASP3 and 5-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-4-hydroxypentanoic acid-CASP3), had good binding affinity. However, apart from dopamine, the anti-cancer effects and molecular mechanisms of other metabolites remain unclear. Dopamine, as an important neurotransmitter, is abundantly present in the gastric tissue and associated with cognition, emotion, stress and movement [48]. Chakroborty et al. found that dopamine treatment can delay the growth of gastric cancer by inhibiting angiogenesis [49]. Dopamine also inhibited the insulin-like growth factor-I (IGF-I)-induced proliferation of gastric cancer cells through down-regulating the phosphorylation of IGF-IR and AKT [50], suggesting that dopamine is a potential anti-gastric cancer small molecule compound.

The tumor microenvironment (TME), due to its crucial role in cancer progression and drug resistance, has become a potential immunotherapy target for various malignant tumors, including gastric cancer [51]. The cells involved in the TME of gastric cancer are called tumor-infiltrating immune cells (TIICs), mainly including macrophages, lymphocytes, fibroblasts, myeloid suppressor cells and their products (cytokines) [52]. TIICs in the TME can be used as an indicator for evaluating the efficacy of immunotherapy [52]. The relationship between TIICs and gastric cancer is extremely complex and intricate. Immune infiltration analysis using TIMER database demonstrated significant correlations between key genes and immune cells. Notably, apart from ALB, other key targets may be involved in the regulation of the tumor-infiltrating immune cells levels of gastric cancer and thereby mediate the occurrence and development of gastric cancer. The pathways that regulate the immune system are called immune checkpoints [53]. Different types of cancers use these immune checkpoints as an immune evasion mechanism, leading to tumor progression [53]. Three common immune checkpoint molecules, including cytotoxic T lymphocyte associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and programmed cell death 1 ligand 1 (PD-L1), play a significant role in immune evasion [53]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the immune evasion mechanisms are considered a feasible anti-gastric cancer immunotherapy [54]. Our results showed that key targets were positively correlated with CTLA-4, PD-1 and PD-L1, which demonstrated that these key targets may exert its effect by modulating the expression of immune checkpoints. These findings suggest that GM metabolites may exert anti-gastric cancer effects by targeting key genes involved in immune regulation and immune checkpoints, providing further justification for experimental validation.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have been confirmed as key factors related to the pathological progression of gastric cancer [55]. In the present study, six miRNAs were identified as crucial miRNAs involved in the GM metabolites against gastric cancer. There are no studies on these six miRNAs related to the development and progression of gastric cancer. Interestingly, these six miRNAs have been reported to be related to tumors [56–61]. More experimental studies are needed in the future to clarify the protective effect of these miRNAs on gastric cancer.

Although this study has identified the potential role of the GM metabolites in the treatment of gastric cancer, there are still some limitations. This study is based on open database collection, preclinical or clinical experimental verification should be conducted to ensure its accuracy in future research.

Conclusion

Our research has preliminarily confirmed the crucial role of GM metabolites in the pathogenesis of gastric cancer. It is notable that these six key GM metabolites, such as 2-hydroxy-3-(4-hydroxyphenyl) propanoic acid, dopamine, D-xylose, 2-hydroxy-3-(5-hydroxy-1H-indol-3-yl) propanoic acid, 3-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-hydroxypropanoic acid and 5-(3,4- dihydroxyphenyl)-4-hydroxypentanoic acid, may play a regulatory role in the treatment of gastric cancer through mediating corresponding key targets. Meanwhile, these GM metabolites may exert an anti-gastric cancer effect by acting on key genes involved in immune regulation and immune checkpoints. These findings provide new treatment strategies for gastric cancer. Future research should further explore the precise mechanism of GM metabolites in clinical treatment.
