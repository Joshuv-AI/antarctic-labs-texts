Machine learning-based assessment of the healthy human gut mycobiota landscape using ITS1 DNA metabarcoding data
Authors: Giuseppe Defazio, Erika Lorusso, Mariangela De Robertis, Tommaso Mello, Andrea Galli, Graziano Pesole, Bruno Fosso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The human gut microbiome plays a critical role in maintaining host health and homeostasis, and current literature suggests a bidirectional relationship between microbiome ecology and host well-being. DNA metabarcoding has emerged as a powerful tool for investigating microbiome imbalances (i.e., dysbiosis). While the prokaryotic microbiome has been extensively studied, the fungal counterpart – or mycobiome – remains largely unexplored, despite its recognized role from the perinatal stage onward. Here, we present a comprehensive survey based on DNA metabarcoding analysis of approximately 1,500 publicly available ITS1 samples. This survey integrates conventional statistical approaches with Machine Learning (ML) methods coupled with explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI). ML models successfully predicted host health status with accuracies exceeding 80%, and fungal genera such as Eurotium, Aureobasidium, Candida, and Cutaneotrichosporon emerged as key classification features. This study introduces a cutting-edge multiview analytical framework applied to publicly available mycobiome data, highlighting the potential of fungal community profiling as a non-invasive tool to support health diagnostics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13040-026-00532-6.

Introduction

Among the most trending topics in the last fifteen years, the investigation of the human gut microbiome (GM) has gained significant attention in the scientific community [1–3]. The human gastro-intestinal tract (GIT) is where food is metabolized into simple nutrients and then distributed throughout the body via the bloodstream. At birth, the GIT is nearly sterile, but microbial colonization begins immediately during and after delivery. Natural or C-section births expose the newborn to different maternal microbiota, vaginal- or skin-specific, respectively [4, 5]. However, numerous factors such as diet, lifestyle and environmental exposure shape the microbiome’s structure and composition [6]. The GM plays a vital role at the interface between the host and the exposome (i.e. the ensemble of environmental stimuli) [7] and it is now well established that it is crucial in several physiological functions, including immunomodulation, digestion, vitamin metabolism, mood regulation, and protection against pathogens [8–12]. Furthermore, several comparative studies have explored the microbiome’s role in several chronic diseases, ranging from cancer to obesity [13–15]. Increasing evidences support a bidirectional relationship between the microbiome and host, as shown in diseases such as cancer [16, 17], liver and pancreatic disorders [18, 19] and neurologic disfunctions [20]. However, identifying causal links between complex microbial communities and human health remains challenging.

Efforts to exploit microbiome profiling as a clinical biomarker are ongoing. In particular, Gupta and coworkers [21, 22] proposed the Gut Microbiome Health Index (GMHI) as an effective predictor of wellness status. In addition, microbiome modulation is also extensively explored for therapeutic purposes, mainly through dietary interventions and the oral administration of probiotics and prebiotics. While faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has become an approved and effective therapy for Clostridioides difficile infections [23], further personalization approaches aimed at improving FMT donor-recipient compatibility are still under investigation. In this context, a better definition of taxa possibly associated with health and wellness is crucial for rational therapeutic design.

Metagenomics provides a modern approach for profiling microbial communities [24, 25] overcoming the limitations of culture-dependent methods. Among metagenomic strategies, DNA-metabarcoding or amplicon-based sequencing has become widely adopted due to its reduced costs and ability to provide rapid and accurate taxonomic profiling. This method basically relies on PCR amplification and sequencing of a conserved and ubiquitous DNA barcode, flanked by primer-binding sites highly conserved in the taxa of interest [26], while retaining sufficient sequence variability to ensure taxonomic resolution and length compatibility with sequencing platforms [26]. For prokaryotes, the 16S rRNA gene is commonly used, while the 18S rRNA gene and internal transcribed spacers (ITS)1/2, serve as DNA barcodes for eukaryotes [27]. Faecal samples are the preferred matrix for GM studies due to their non-invasive nature and ease of collection, compared to intestinal biopsy [28] and colonic lavage [29]. Over the past two decades, 16S rRNA-based metabarcoding has yielded fundamental insights into the composition and functional roles of the gut prokaryotic community [30]. In contrast, the eukaryotic component of the gut microbiota - mostly fungi and those collectively referred to as mycobiota, has received far less attention. However, research on mycobiota has gained momentum in the last few years. The mycobiome investigation is generally performed by using ITS/18S barcodes, although ITS1 has shown greater reliability compared to ITS2 and 18S rRNA [26, 31, 32].

Although the eukaryotic microbial fraction represents only the 0.1-1% of GM [33], it contributes significantly to the diversity and function of the GM ecosystem [34, 35]. It plays crucial roles such as replacing bacterial commensals in post-antibiotic treatments or producing metabolites that modulate bacterial colonization [34]. The most frequently detected fungal genera include Saccharomyces, Candida, Dipodascus, Aureobasidium, Penicillium, Hanseniaspora, Agaricus, Debaryomyces although their prevalence is strongly influenced by diet [36]. Stable colonizers of the GIT include Candida, Galactomyces, Geotrichum, Saprochaete, Malassezia and Cladosporium [37]. Among these, Candida albicans, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, and Malassezia are most frequently detected in healthy individuals [38]. Alterations in fungal abundance are often associated with a disease condition. For example, Candida spp overgrowth correlates with dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation, while its abundance often inversely correlates with that of Aspergillus, Penicillium, Epicoccum, Galactomyces and Debaryomyces [39–41]. Mycobiome composition may also affect Faecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) outcomes. In Clostridioides difficile infections, high levels of Candida albicans in donors or recipients have been associated with negative outcomes, whereas similar fungal profiles have been linked to positive FMT outcomes in ulcerative colitis [42–44]. Despite the growing amount of data, defining a “healthy” microbiome remains elusive and challenging [45]. Regarding the mycobiota, contrasting evidences suggest a close relationship with bacterial dysbiosis, with GM short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) controlling fungal overgrowth [46, 47]. Conversely, reduced fungal diversity and opportunistic overgrowth of pathogens, such as Candida and Malassezia, have been reported in Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) and alcoholic liver disease [48, 49].

Despite the growing body of literature, the eukaryotic gut microbiota remains poorly characterized, and robust taxonomic markers distinguishing health from disease status have yet to be established. A recent meta-analysis by Zhang et al. (2024) applied Random Forest (RF) models to publicly available ITS data (460 diseased vs. 1912 healthy individuals); however, the unbalanced sample and methodological limitations hindered reproducibility and interpretability of their results [50]. To our knowledge, no previous study has yet combined ITS1-based metabarcoding with machine learning (ML) and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) to systemically compare healthy and non-healthy subjects.

To address these gaps, we analysed publicly available ITS1 DNA metabarcoding data using the BioMaS pipeline [51], integrated in the ITSoneWB docker [52] and using ITSoneDB [27] as the reference database. We performed biodiversity and compositional analyses, trained RF models and applied SHAP-based XAI to comparatively investigate healthy and non-healthy conditions. In addition, this study aims to highlight the value of data reuse from public repositories and to support FAIR (i.e. Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability, Reusability) research practices [53, 54].

Materials and methods

Publicly available raw sequencing data were retrieved by querying the PubMed database and selecting studies with both available sequencing data and associated metadata accessible in public repositories (e.g. the SRA - Short Read Archive, and BioProject/BioSample databases). Only studies comparing the mycobiome of healthy and diseased individuals and using ITS1 as DNA barcode were considered. Additionally, only Illumina sequencing data with a paired-end (PE) sequencing layout were considered. FASTQ files were locally downloaded using the fasterq-dump function from SRA-toolkit (v 3.0.0).

DNA-metabarcoding analysis was then performed using the ITSoneWB [52] docker relying on ITSoneDB (v 1.143) [27] as reference database for ITS1 sequences taxonomic classification. In ITSoneWB different tools for DNA-metabarcoding analysis are available and BioMaS [51] was selected. In brief, BioMaS is an OTU (Operational Taxonomic Unit) and ASV (Amplicon Sequence Variants) unaware method. After quality check, it initially attempts to merge the PE reads. Non-merged reads are trimmed but not discarded. Indeed, the unmerged PE reads are retained to avoid the loss of biodiversity information, which can occur when barcode regions are too long to obtain Illumina PE reads data merging. Then, both merged and unmerged PE reads are aligned against the reference collection. Alignment results are filtered according to similarity (≥ 97%) and query-coverage (≥ 70%). Retained matches are used to perform the taxonomic classification with TANGO [55, 56]. The resulting taxonomic assignments provided by BioMaS docker were managed by extracting classification paths for seven canonical levels (i.e. superkingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species).

Subsequently, taxonomic assignments were filtered to remove potential dietary and environmental contaminants. In particular, the WoRMS database (downloaded on 12/07/2023) [57] was used to exclude marine species, whereas reads assigned to Viridiplantae and Metazoa were also removed.

Samples were rarefied to a uniform sequencing depth for alpha diversity analysis. Rarefaction depths between 1,000 and 20,000 reads were tested in increments of 1,000, with 100 iterations per depth and per sample by using the Mirlyn R package (v 1.4.2) [58]. Shannon index values were computed using the vegan R package (v 2.6-6), and the standard deviation across depth iterations was used to determine the optimal tradeoff between sequencing depth and sample retention. Alpha diversity metrics (i.e., Chao1, Shannon, and Inverse Simpson) were then computed on rarefied data. Differences between healthy and non-healthy conditions were evaluated using Wilcoxon test. Maaslin2 (v 1.16.0) [59] was also used on alpha diversity metrics considering health status as fixed effect and BioProject ID as random effect.

Mycobiome compositional data were represented at genus level, and all subsequent analyses were performed using the quantitative composition of samples at this taxonomic rank.

Prevalence analysis at the genus level was also carried out. Associations between genera and health status were evaluated using LEfSe (v 1.12.1) [60] and Maaslin2 (v 1.16.0). In Maaslin2, health condition was treated as a fixed effect whereas the BioProject ID was included as a random effect. All MaAsLin2 analyses were performed using default settings; in particular, data were log-transformed and normalized usingTotal Sum Scaling (TSS).

Beta diversity was assessed using Jaccard and Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metrics computed on Centered Log Ratio (CLR) transformed data to properly address compositionality of metabarcoding data. PERMANOVA models were constructed using these metrics, with health status and BioProject ID as explanatory variables using the adonis2 function of the vegan R package (v 2.4-6).

Genus-level compositional data were also used to construct co-occurrence networks for healthy and non-healthy samples using the NetCoMi R package. Networks were built by calculating partial correlations among genera (normalized via modified CLR transformation) and converting them into signed dissimilarities using the SPRING method [61]. Hubs were defined as nodes with high centrality (number of adjacent nodes i.e. degree; ability of the network to connect sub-networks i.e. betweenness; measure of how close a node is to other nodes i.e. closeness; centrality of a node and its connected nodes i.e. eigenvector). Fast-greedy clustering was applied, and networks were compared using 200 permutations tests for centrality and global topology metrics.

Supervised classification models were developed using the RF approach applied to genus-level taxonomic counts. Data was randomly split into 70% − 30% training and test sets. Hyperparameters optimization was performed using 10-fold cross validation with 200 repetitions by using the scikit-learn (v 1.5.2) framework and RandomizedSearchCV. The number of estimators (i.e. trees) and the maximum tree depth were the tuned hyperparameters. Further details are available in the GitHub repository. Thirty RF models were trained across combinations of three prevalence thresholds (0%, 0.05% and 0.1%) and ten minimum depth thresholds (0–900 reads, with a 100-read step). Four additional RF models including age and sex as additional covariates were also trained. Feature relevance for each model was assessed using Shapley Additive Explanations with the shap (v 0.45.1) Python package [62]. Independence between prediction outcomes (i.e. correct/incorrect) and the additional covariates (i.e. age, sex) was assessed with a chi-square test. Additionally, age distributions across prediction outcomes were examined for each model variant. Finally, results from LEfSe, Maaslin2 and RF-Shap analyses were compared to identify genera significantly associated with the health status. LEfSe results with |LDA| >3.5, Maaslin2 q-values < 0.01 (considering health status only), and RF results with SHAP values exceeding the overall mean plus one standard deviation, were considered significant.

We fixed some discrepancies between metadata in the published manuscripts and those available in public repository. In particular, for the BioProject CRA01060 [35] the paper reports 498 healthy controls and 72 Alzheimer’s Disease (AD) patients, while metadata only include 353 healthy and 59 AD samples. After request to the authors and to the journal editorial board, metadata were shared through a GitHub repository and subsequently forked into the repository linked to this study (https://github.com/gdefazio/Mycobiome_cohort_analysis) (Supplementary Table 1). BioProject DRA013647 lacks information on sex and age [63]. Similarly, PRJEB42375 and PRJNA607176 do not report sex and age metadata neither in BioSample entries nor in the corresponding publications [40, 67]. Ultimately, sex and age metadata were retrieved for 1,194 and 1,193 samples, respectively, over a total of 1,533 (77.8%) (Supplementary Fig. 1). Regarding the geographic distribution, 358 samples originated from Europe and 1,175 from Asia (Table 2).

Data normalization and alpha diversity

Genus-level counts were normalized by rarefaction. To determine the optimal rarefaction depth minimizing variability in Shannon Index estimation, depths ranging from 1,000 to 20,000 reads were tested using 1,000-reads increments, with 100 replicates per depth. Standard deviation values of the Shannon index were calculated per sample and depth (Supplementary Fig. 1). A depth of 5,000 reads provided the best trade-off between variance and sample retention. After rarefaction, 940 samples and 1,523 genera were retained. Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Shannon and inverse Simpson) were computed (Supplementary Fig. 3). Maaslin2 was applied by using alpha diversity indexes and health condition as fixed effects, and BioProject as a random effect. Chao1 was significantly associated with the healthy status, while the number of observed genera was associated with the non-healthy condition (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Beta diversity analysis

Bray-Curtis and Jaccard beta-diversity indices were calculated on CLR-transformed data and visualized with PCoA (Supplementary Fig. 5). The computation included 1,238 samples with > 1,000 reads and 1,989 genera represented by at least 1 P.E. read assigned. PERMANOVA on genus-level distances revealed that health status explained about 1% of the variation, the BioProject about 20% and their interaction about 2% (Table 4 and Supplementary Table 2). When controlling for age and sex, BioProject remained the dominant explanatory factor (Table 6 and Supplementary Table 3).

Machine learning classification

Finally, supervised Machine Learning classifier, relying on the RF approach, was trained to discriminate healthy vs. non-healthy samples by using gut mycobiota data.

RF models were trained at genus-level data using combinations of prevalence (i.e. 0%, 0.05%, 0.1%) and read-depth thresholds (0-900, step 100). Of the 30 models tested, 4 exceeded 80% accuracy (Table 7, Supplementary Table 4, Supplementary Fig. 7). Feature importance was assessed with SHAP values (Fig. 4). For the 4 models exceeding 80% accuracy we also assessed training and test sets accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score and balanced accuracy within each BioProject as reported in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Additional models incorporating age, sex, or both, improved accuracy especially with age as a covariate (Fig. 5; Table 8 and Supplementary Fig. 8).

Discussion

In this study, we present an integrated comparative analysis of the human gut mycobiome in healthy and non-healthy individuals, using samples available from public repositories. It is important to note that, according to the World Health Organization (WHO), health is defined as a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being, and not merely the absences of disease or infirmity (https://www.who.int/about/governance/constitution). However, this definition does not consider the ecological balance of the GM, which is increasingly recognised as a key component of host health [70]. Consequently, applying a uniform approach to define the health status across subjects enrolled in different studies remains challenging. Therefore, we relied on the original characterization provided by the authors of each included study.

Focusing on fungal members of the microbiome - collectively referred to as mycobiome - it is worth noting that its low biomass requires very deep shotgun metagenomic sequencing for reliable detection. However, even state-of-the-art shotgun approaches often fail to adequately capture fungal diversity. As a result, DNA metabarcoding remains the preferred method for mycobiome profiling. In this study we included all publicly available samples generated using the ITS1 region as molecular barcode, owing to its demonstrated reliability in profiling fungal communities, as compared with ITS2 or 18S rRNA [32], and its widespread use in human gut mycobiome studies [38]. Despite its robustness in taxonomy profiling, the ITS1 region has considerable length variability (i.e. 100–1000 nt) which frequently hampers PE Illumina short-read sequence merging. Such merging failures can lead to data loss in OTU- and ASV-based methods such as QIIME2 [71], which were primarily designed for molecular barcodes with conserved length, such as 16 S rRNA hypervariable regions used for bacterial profiling. To overcome this limitation, we employed BioMaS [51] which is able to equally process both merged and unmerged PE reads. We selected published samples that used ITS1 to study the human gut mycobiome, focusing on comparisons between healthy individuals and those affected by specific diseases. Most samples originated from Asia, followed by Europe and North America. The non-healthy group encompassed a broad range of disease conditions, including melanoma, COVID-19, echinococcosis, and Crohn’s disease.

At the genus level, the most prevalent fungal genera were Aspergillus, Candida, Penicillium, Saccharomyces, and Cladosporium. However, their relative abundances varied markedly among individuals. These observations are consistent with prior studies reporting Candida, Saccharomyces, and Aspergillus as dominant members of the gut mycobiome [38].

Alpha-diversity analyses revealed no statistical differences between healthy and non-healthy subjects. Only the Shannon index showed a trend (i.e. p-value 0.042), suggesting a higher biodiversity in the healthy mycobiome. Maaslin2 analysis further supported this, showing a positive association between health status and richness-based metrics such as the Chao1 index (Supplementary Fig. 4). Overall, these results suggest that the mycobiome exhibits relative stability in terms of richness and evenness across healthy and non-healthy conditions. This aligns with existing literature, which reports limited discriminatory power of alpha diversity in health stratification [22] and highlights that fungal richness is considerably lower than that of prokaryotes [34, 35]. Beta diversity analysis indicated that the main source of variance was related to the BioProjects. Indeed, different BioProjects displayed substantial heterogeneity in terms of the geographical provenance, employed primer-pairs DNA extraction and amplification protocols, sequencing layouts, and Illumina chemistry (i.e. 500 cycles v2 or 600 cycles v3). PERMANOVA analysis confirmed that BioProject accounted for about 20% of total variance thus confirming a remarkable batch effect. We acknowledge that the batch effect may represent a source of bias and can, in principle, be addressed by (i) applying batch-correction strategies, (ii) modelling batch as a covariate, or (iii) treating batch as a random effect within mixed-effect models [72]. Nevertheless, batch- correction strategies have been reported to be detrimental and to introduce additional bias when applied to DNA metabarcoding data [73]. For this reason, alternative approaches were adopted in this study. Moreover, many traditional statistical methods for microbiome analysis and taxa/condition association are not well suited to effectively handle batch effects. Accordingly, we employed a complementary analytical framework combining differential abundance analyses (i.e. LEfSe, Maaslin2), network analysis, and machine learning approaches.

Differential abundance analysis consistently identified Eurotium, Aureobasidium, Geotrichium, Wallemia, and Aspergillus as more abundant in healthy individuals, whereas Cutaneotrichosporon, Candida, and Nakaseomyces were more prevalent in non-healthy individuals. These trends remained stable when age, sex, and BioProject were included as covariates (Figs. 1, 2 and 3). Specifically, Cutaneotrichosporon and Candida were enriched in non-healthy individuals, while Eurotium, Aureobasidium, Wallemia, Aspergillus, Pichia, and Geotrichium were associated with health. Further analysis indicated that the abundance of Candida, Debaryomyces, and Eurotium was primarily influenced by age, whereas Acremonium showed a sex-associated pattern.

Regarding machine learning, RF was selected for its robustness in binary classification and resilience to overfitting [74–76]. RF models based on genus-level data achieved ~ 80% accuracy. Performance assessment indicates that BioProjects with an insufficient number of samples and not primarily related to the gut did not adequately support model fitting (e.g. PRJEB35665 for Melanoma, PRJEB53019 for diabetes and PRJNA317653 for alcoholic liver disease, all vs. healthy; Supplementary Fig. 8). Including age as a covariate increased model accuracy at ~ 87%, while sex alone did not increase accuracy. Interestingly, combined age and sex covariates performed worse than the age-only model. This contrasts with numerous studies linking the prokaryotic composition to sex [77–81], suggesting that mycobiome composition is specifically sensitive to age-related changes, possibly due to immunosenescence [82]. SHAP value analysis confirmed that Age followed by Eurotium and Cutaneotrichosporon were the most influential features (Supplementary Fig. 9). Importantly, ten genera (Eurotium, Aureobasidium, Geotrichum, Aspergillus, Eremothecium, Wallemia, Nakaseomyces, Cutaneotrichosporon, Pichia and Candida) were consistently identified associated with health status by all three methods (LEfSe, Maaslin2 and RF-Shap) making them strong candidates as predictive markers. Notably, Eurotium showed no network co-occurrence in the healthy condition, yet its association with health condition has not been previously reported. Eurotium is known to produce bioactive compounds, such as questin (antimicrobial and antioxidant), physcion (cytotoxic and antiviral), questinol (anti-inflammatory), avariecolortide B (apoptosis modulator), and euroticin H (carbohydrate absorption inhibitor), which may underlie its potential beneficial effect [83]. Aspergillus plays a positive role due to antioxidant, anti-hypertension, immunomodulatory and cognitive-enhancing properties, despite this genus may include mycotoxins-producing species [84]. The functional roles of other genera identified, such as Geotrichium, Eremothecium, and Aureobasidium, remain poorly characterized and warrant further investigation.

Although this study is limited by incomplete technical (e.g. employed primer pair and sequencing layout) and biological metadata, such as BMI, diet, smoking habits, and alcohol consumption, which could account for part of the observed compositional variability of mycobiome, it nonetheless provides valuable initial insights into mycobiome-host interactions. This limitation underscores the persistent need for comprehensive and FAIR-compliant sharing of both data and metadata [54, 85].

Further studies should aim to better elucidate the role of the mycobiome in health and disease by including larger and more diverse cohorts, integrating multi-omics data, and incorporating metabolites profiling. Moreover, machine learning models should be validated across multiple and independent samples to ensure generalizability and robustness [54, 85].

Conclusion

Here we present a reanalysis of publicly available ITS1 DNA metabarcoding samples from stool samples to investigate human gut mycobiome. A combination of approaches identified a consistent set of informative genera including Eurotium, Aureobasidium, Geotrichum, Aspergillus, Eremothecium, Wallemia, Nakaseomyces, Cutaneotrichosporon, and Pichia, capable of discriminating healthy and non-healthy subjects.

Furthermore, this work proposes a robust machine learning model achieving up to 87% classification accuracy in genus-level binary discrimination. This work represents a novel contribution toward the functional relevance of the gut mycobiome its potential role in maintaining health or promoting disease.
