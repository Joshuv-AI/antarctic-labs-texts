Diversity and pathogenicity of Lasiodiplodia species associated with banana crown rot in Northern and Northeastern Brazil
Authors: Kevison Romulo da Silva França, Mayra Machado de Medeiros Ferro, Marcus Vinícius Santos da Silva, Roberto Ramos-Sobrinho, Maruzanete Pereira de Melo, Gaus Silvestre de Andrade Lima, Iraildes Pereira Assunção
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCrown rot is a major postharvest disease affecting bananas (Musa spp.), resulting in substantial economic losses by compromising fruit quality and limiting marketability. In 2023, symptoms of crown rot were observed on banana fruits sourced from ten states across the northern and northeastern regions of Brazil. Pathogen identification was carried out using morphological characteristics and multigene phylogenetic analysis based on the internal transcribed spacer (ITS), translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1-α), β-tubulin (tub2), and RNA polymerase II subunit (rpb2). A total of 116 fungal isolates recovered from diseased fruit exhibited morphological traits consistent with the genus Lasiodiplodia. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on concatenated multilocus data revealed that the new isolates belonged to the species Lasiodiplodia brasiliensis, Lasiodiplodia hormozganensis, Lasiodiplodia iraniensis, Lasiodiplodia laeliocattleyae, Lasiodiplodia pseudotheobromae, and Lasiodiplodia theobromae. Pathogenicity tests confirmed that all Lasiodiplodia species/isolates experimentally induced crown rot symptoms on banana fruits of cultivars ‘Prata’, ‘Pacovan’, and ‘Terra’. Inoculated fungi were re-isolated from symptomatic tissues, which exhibited morphological characteristics identical to those of the original cultures, fulfilling Koch’s postulates. This study represents the first large-scale and comprehensive survey on Lasiodiplodia species associated with banana crown rot in Brazil, revealing that a complex of at least six phylogenetically distinct Lasiodiplodia species are involved in the disease etiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22739-z.Keywords: Musaceae, Botryosphaeriaceae, Phylogeny, Multigene analysis, Pathogenicity, Tropical fruitsSubject terms: DNA sequencing, Fungal pathogenesis, Applied microbiology

Introduction

Bananas (Musa spp.; family Musaceae) are the primary fruit crops produced worldwide, being extensively cultivated across tropical and subtropical regions. In 2023, global production reached 139.2 million tons, harvested from approximately 5.9 million hectares1. Grown in more than 130 countries, bananas are the fourth most important staple food crop in the world, following rice, wheat, and maize2,3. The largest global producers of bananas are India, China, Indonesia, Nigeria, Ecuador, and Brazil, collectively accounting for 59.9% of the world´s production1. In Brazil, the annual production is estimated at 6.8 million tons, representing 4.9% of the global output1. The northern and northeastern regions of the country are responsible for ~ 47.9% of the national production4.

Crown rot is the most significant postharvest disease affecting bananas5. The detachment of fruits from the bunch during harvest creates wounds in the crown, exposing internal tissues to opportunistic pathogens. Although the infection is typically restricted to the crown, it may progress to the fruit stalk6. Symptomatology includes darkening of the stalk and peel, pulp necrosis, and softening of the whole fruit7. In advanced stages, visible mycelial growth appears on the cut surface, often accompanied by fruit detachment8.

Several fungal pathogens have been associated with banana crown rot, including Colletotrichum musae9, Thielaviopsis musarum10, species from the genera Lasiodiplodia11–13 and Fusarium14–16, with fungal community diversity and disease severity varying depending on the geographic region and climatic conditions17,18.

The genus Lasiodiplodia (family Botryosphaeriaceae) includes fungi that are commonly saprophytic, parasitic, or endophytic and are associated with a wide range of cultivated plant hosts19–21. Currently, 55 species are recognized within the genus Lasiodiplodia22, with a global distribution and particularly high prevalence in tropical, subtropical, and temperate climates23,24. In Brazil, Lasiodiplodia brasiliensis, Lasiodiplodia iraniensis, Lasiodiplodia hormozganensis, and Lasiodiplodia laeliocattleyae have recently been identified as causal agents of banana crown rot in commercial growing areas located in the northeastern part of the country12,13,25. However, the true diversity of Lasiodiplodia species involved in banana crown rot is likely still underestimated.

A better understanding of the Lasiodiplodia species diversity associated with banana crown rot and their respective levels of virulence is crucial for the development of effective and sustainable disease management strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize Lasiodiplodia species associated with banana crown rot in cultivation areas in North and Northeastern Brazil, through morphological, pathogenic, and molecular data.

Materials and methods

Plant sampling and fungal isolation

Unripe banana fruits displaying no symptoms of crown rot were collected from most commercial plantations located in the northeastern (states of Rio Grande do Norte, Ceará, Paraíba, Pernambuco, Piauí, Alagoas, Sergipe, and Bahia) and northern (states of Acre and Pará) regions of Brazil. The plant material was superficially disinfested by immersion in a 1% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution for 1 min, rinsed three times in sterile distilled water for 1 min, air-dried, and then stored in plastic trays at 27 °C with a 12-hour photoperiod, until the appearance of crown rot symptoms (Fig. 1a-b). Also, both unripe and ripe banana fruits exhibiting typical symptoms of crown rot were collected (state of Alagoas) (Fig. 1c-e). Fungal isolation was carried out either directly from pycnidia (Fig. 1b) or indirectly from symptomatic tissues. In the absence of visible pycnidia, tissue fragments were excised from the transition zone between healthy and diseased areas. They were superficially disinfected by immersion in 70% ethanol for 1 min, followed by 1% NaOCl for 1 min, and then rinsed three times in sterile distilled water for 1 min. The disinfested fragments were dried, transferred to Petri dishes containing potato-dextrose-agar (PDA) medium, and incubated at 25 °C in the dark. After five days, colonies displaying morphological features typical of the family Botryosphaeriaceae19 were transferred to fresh PDA plates and incubated for 15 days under the same conditions.

Symptoms of crown rot on banana fruits. (a) crown rot symptoms on a fruit collected still unripe and kept in plastic trays; (b) pycnidia on the surface of a naturally infected symptomatic fruit; (c) typical postharvest symptoms of the disease. (d-e) during field observations, ‘atypical’ symptoms of crown rot were identified on developing fruits.

Following the observation of reproductive structures characteristic of the genus Lasiodiplodia19, the new isolates were individually transferred to Petri dishes containing water-agar (WA) medium. To ensure the genetic purity of the fungal isolates, hyphal tips were then transferred to PDA plates and maintained at 25 °C in the dark. To induce pycnidia formation, the isolates were grown on PDA supplemented with sterilized pine needles and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 30 days. Conidia were subsequently analyzed for morphological characterization. The isolates were cataloged and deposited in the phytopathogenic fungal Culture Collection of the Federal University of Alagoas (COUFAL).

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from mycelial growth of 7-day-old cultures of representative isolates collected from each sampling location, using a modified CTAB protocol26. Extracted DNA was used as a template for PCR amplification of different genomic regions: translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1-α), internal transcribed spacer (ITS), β-tubulin (tub2), and RNA polymerase subunit II (rpb2).

The PCR reactions were performed in a final volume of 30 µL, including 3.0 µL of 10× buffer (100 mM KCl, 100 mM Tris-HCl at pH 9.0, 1% Triton-X), 2.4 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.9 µL of 50 mM MgCl2, 0.2 µL of Taq DNA polymerase, 1.0 µL of each oligonucleotide (10 µM), 1.0 µL (50 ng) of total DNA, and 17.0 µL of PCR-grade water. The amplification conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 52–55 °C (52 °C for tef1-α; 55 °C for tub2 and ITS, and 54.8 °C for rpb2) for 30 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. The tef1-α, ITS, tub2, and rpb2 target regions were amplified using the specific primer pairs EF1-688 F/EF1-1251R27, ITS1-F/ITS428, Bt2a/Bt2b29,30, and rpb2-LasF/rpb2-LasR31, respectively. The PCR products were purified and bidirectionally Sanger sequenced by ACTGene Análises Moleculares (Alvorada, RS, Brazil).

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

The tef1-α, ITS, tub2, and rpb2 partial sequences were assembled and manually edited using CodonCode Aligner v. 4.1.1 (Codon Code Corporation, Dedham, MA, USA) and subsequently analyzed with the BLASTn algorithm against the GenBank non-redundant nucleotide database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov). The most closely related Lasiodiplodia sequences were then retrieved from GenBank based on the BLASTn results and relevant peer-reviewed publications32–34. The taxa and their corresponding GenBank accession numbers are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Multiple sequence alignments were obtained for each marker using MEGA v. 11.0.1335.

Bayesian phylogenetic inference was conducted using the Markov chain Monte Carlo approach via the CIPRES Science Gateway36. Phylogenetic analyses included the sequences from each locus (tef1-α, ITS, tub2, and rpb2), both individually and concatenated, and were performed using MrBayes v. 3.2.337. The best-fit nucleotide substitution model for each genomic region was identified using MrModeltest v. 2.338, based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) implemented in PAUP v. 4.0b1039. The analysis was run for 10 million generations with four chains, sampling every 1,000 generations, resulting in a total of 10,000 trees. After discarding the first 25% of trees as a burn-in phase40, a consensus Bayesian tree and Bayesian posterior probabilities (BPP) were determined based on the 7,500 remaining trees. A BPP ≥ 0.95 was considered a significant value. The trees were visualized and edited using FigTree v. 1.441 and Adobe Illustrator v. 25.4.1 (Adobe Inc., San Jose, CA).

Pathogenicity test

Preliminary crown inoculation trials resulted in rapid lesion coalescence, which, combined with the anatomical complexity of the crown tissue (floral remnants and fruit peduncles), led to inaccurate quantification of aggressiveness. Therefore, pathogenicity and aggressiveness were assessed by inoculating the wounded epicarp of individual fruits to avoid misleading results. Although Lasiodiplodia species generally sporulate well, many isolates used in this study showed significant variability in sporogenic capacity, making it labor-intensive and time-consuming to prepare standardized conidial suspensions at uniform concentrations for large-scale inoculation tests. In this context, the mycelial plug approach was adopted, as it has been widely used due to its feasibility and reproducibility in simulating postharvest infection conditions in climacteric fruits such as bananas, while also overcoming the high variability in sporogenic capacity observed among fungal isolates.

Representative isolates obtained in this study were tested for pathogenicity and aggressiveness on banana fruits of cultivars ‘Prata’, ‘Pacovan’, and ‘Terra’ at stage 2 of ripening, according to the Von Loesecke scale42. The fruits were washed with detergent and running water, surface-sterilized in 1% NaOCl for 3 min, and rinsed with sterile distilled water. After air drying, each fruit was wounded at three equidistant points on the epicarp to a depth of 3 mm using sterile needles, and mycelial plugs (5 mm in diameter) obtained from 7-day-old cultures grown on PDA were placed on the wounded area. Control treatments consisted of PDA plugs without fungal growth. The inoculated and control fruits were placed in plastic trays with moistened paper towels, enclosed in plastic bags to maintain high relative humidity, and incubated at 27 °C with a 12-hour photoperiod for 48 h. Afterwards, the humidity chamber was removed, and the fruits were kept at the same temperature for another 48 h. The experiment included five replicates for each isolate, with each replicate comprising one fruit.

Symptom development was evaluated at four days post-inoculation (dpi), and pathogenicity was determined based on the presence or absence of disease symptoms. Further, isolate aggressiveness was assessed by measuring the lesion diameter (mm), calculated as the average of two perpendicular measurements for each replicate. Statistical analyses were conducted using Permutational Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA), followed by multiple comparisons using Student’s t-test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed in R v. 4.4.143.

Morphological and cultural characteristics

Colony morphology, including color and hyphal structure, was observed after 7 and 15 days of incubation on PDA at 25 °C in the dark, following the color standards established by Rayner44. Conidial morphology was examined from pycnidia, formed on PDA supplemented with sterilized pine needles, by crushing them on microscope slides in a drop of lactoglycerol. Measurements of length, width, and length-to-width (L/W) ratio were taken for 50 mature and 50 immature conidia per isolate using an Olympus CKX41 inverted microscope equipped with a digital camera and cellSens Standard software v. 4.2 (CS-ST-V4.2).

To determine the optimal growth temperature, mycelial plugs (5 mm diameter) were excised from the margin of 7-day-old cultures and transferred to the center of fresh PDA plates and incubated at 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, or 35 °C in the dark. The experiment followed a completely randomized design in a 6 × 6 factorial scheme, with six Lasiodiplodia species and six incubation temperatures, and each treatment comprised five replicates. Colony diameter was measured daily for up to 7 days until the Petri dish was fully covered. Mycelial growth was quantified by measuring the colony diameter along two perpendicular axes using a digital caliper. The temperature corresponding to maximum growth was determined from the regression model. These analyses were performed using Past software v. 4.1146.

Results

Lasiodiplodia isolates

A total of 466 unripe and 34 ripe banana fruit samples were collected from commercially growing areas in 34 municipalities across ten states in Northern and Northeastern Brazil. A total of 116 Lasiodiplodia-like isolates were obtained from fruits exhibiting crown rot symptoms. Typical disease symptomatology was similar when comparing plant-attached ripe fruits exhibiting initial crown rot symptoms and those collected while still green and subsequently ripened under laboratory conditions (Fig. 1a, c). Symptoms included darkening of the crown and peduncles, which rapidly progressed to the fruit. In advanced stages, mycelial growth was visible on the cut and fruit surfaces, often followed by intense pycnidia production (Fig. 1b). Interestingly, ‘atypical’ symptoms were also recorded in field conditions. These were characterized by dry rot affecting the crown and peduncles of green bunches, which spread to the developing fruits in later disease development stages (Fig. 1d, e). Early ripening of fruits still attached to the bunch was also frequently recorded (Fig. 1e). The new fungal isolates were cataloged and deposited in the phytopathogenic fungal Culture Collection of the Federal University of Alagoas under accession numbers COUFAL0523-COUFAL0533, COUFAL0537-COUFAL0566, and COUFAL0651-COUFAL0652 (Table 1).

Molecular phylogenetic analysis

To perform an initial species identification of the Lasiodiplodia isolates (n = 76) representing each sampling locality, partial sequences of the tef1-α gene were obtained. The seventy-six new tef1-α sequences comprised 572 characters, from which 518 and 33 were classified as conserved and parsimony informative sites, respectively, after alignment. Based on phylogenetic analysis using a haplotype network, 43 isolates were selected for sequencing additional molecular markers such as ITS, rpb2, and tub2 to provide stronger support for species classification. The forty-three new ITS, rpb2, tef1-α, and tub2 sequences were 579, 630, 568, and 455 in length, respectively, and the concatenated aligned data set contained 2,232 characters, which included 307 parsimony-informative sites, 1,692 conserved sites, and 536 variable sites. The boundaries for each locus in the alignment were as follows: 1-579 for ITS, 580–1209 for rpb2, 1210–1777 for tef1-α, and 1778–2232 for tub2. The Bayesian Inference phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using the following models based on AIC: SYM + I for ITS, GTR + G for rpb2, HKY + I + G for tef1-α, and GTR + I for tub2. The dataset included sequences from 43 isolates in this study, 91 taxa of Lasiodiplodia spp., and Neodeightonia phoenicum (CBS 122528) as an outgroup.

Phylogenetic analysis of the multilocus concatenated dataset (ITS, tef1, tub2, and rpb2) revealed that the isolates from this study belong to six species within the genus Lasiodiplodia. Most of the isolates (n = 29) (COUFAL0526, COUFAL0541-0566, and COUFAL0651-0652) clustered with type sequences and reference specimens of Lasiodiplodia theobromae, with strong statistical support (Bayesian posterior probability, BPP = 1.00). The second most commonly found species was Lasiodiplodia pseudotheobromae (n = 9), represented by the isolates COUFAL0527, COUFAL0530-0533 and COUFAL0537 (BPP = 1.00), followed by Lasiodiplodia brasiliensis (n = 4), isolates COUFAL0528 and COUFAL0538-0540 (BPP = 1.00), Lasiodiplodia laeliocattleyae (n = 2), isolates COUFAL0523-0524 (BPP = 0.99), Lasiodiplodia hormozganensis (n = 1), isolate COUFAL0525 (BPP = 1.00), and Lasiodiplodia iraniensis (n = 1), isolate COUFAL0529 (BPP = 1.00) (Fig. 2). Interestingly, all six species were recovered from symptomatic banana fruits in the northeastern region, while only L. theobromae, L. brasiliensis, and L. pseudotheobromae were identified in the northern states (Fig. 3). We observed a significant predominance of L. theobromae associated with crown rot, except for the state of Acre (Northern Brazil), where this species was not found.

Pathogenicity test

The Lasiodiplodia isolates (n = 43) were pathogenic to the banana cultivars ‘Pacovan’, ‘Prata’, and ‘Terra’ evaluated in this study, and a wide range of lesion sizes were observed between fungal isolate and banana cultivar combinations (Fig. 4c-e). Necrotic, dark lesions developed on the fruit surfaces within two days post-inoculation, progressing rapidly by the fourth day (Fig. 4a). Internally, the infected tissues displayed characteristic signs of crown rot, including water-soaking, softness, and darkened pulp (Fig. 4b). Although the limitation of employing morphology for demarcation of Lasiodiplodia cryptic species, it is important to note that the isolates used here were identified based on sequences of four molecular markers (tef1-α, ITS, tub2, and rpb2), and morphological characteristics were applied only to support the pathogenicity tests. Therefore, Koch’s postulates were considered fulfilled when the Lasiodiplodia specimens successfully re-isolated from experimentally inoculated symptomatic tissues showed morphological traits consistent with those of the original cultures.

(a) Necrotic lesions on the surface of the fruit, starting at the point of inoculation (indicated by a red arrow). (b) Internal appearance of the fruit shows signs of darkening and water-soaking of the pulp. c-e. Pathogenicity tests on ‘Prata’, ‘Pacovan’, and ‘Terra’ with isolates of L. theobromae (COUFAL0541; 0546; 0548; 0558; 0559; 0561; 0563; 0564 and 0565), L. pseudotheobromae (COUFAL0527 and 0532), L. hormozganensis (COUFAL0525), and L. iraniensis (COUFAL0529).

Statistically significant interaction between fungal isolate and banana cultivar (df = 84; F = 18.602; P < 0.001) was observed when evaluating the lesion sizes induced by Lasiodiplodia spp. on inoculated fruits (Supplementary Table S2). Due to the high number of isolates representing some of the Lasiodiplodia species described here, the mean lesion sizes were calculated at a pathogen species-level for each banana cultivar (Fig. 5). Overall, the results indicate clear differences in aggressiveness among Lasiodiplodia species, with L. pseudotheobromae and L. hormozganensis inducing the larger mean lesions on both ‘Pacovan’ and ‘Prata’, and L. iraniensis showing the smallest mean sizes on ‘Pacovan’ and ‘Terra’ (Fig. 5). Since L. hormozganensis and L. iraniensis were represented by only one isolate, virulence diversity in these species is probably underestimated. When analyzing the Lasiodiplodia species represented by multiple isolates, L. pseudotheobromae seems to be the most aggressive species on ‘Pacovan’ and ‘Prata’ fruits, followed by L. theobromae, while the mean lesion sizes induced by L. pseudotheobromae, L. theobromae, L. brasiliensis, and L. laeliocattleyae were similar on the cultivar ‘Terra’ (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table S3). Lesions on ‘Prata’ and ‘Pacovan’ were comparable but significantly larger than those on ‘Terra’ (Supplementary Table S3).

Isolates of L. pseudotheobromae (n = 4), L. hormozganensis (n = 1), and L. theobromae (n = 9) caused the largest lesions on the three banana cultivars. However, a few L. theobromae isolates (n = 7) produced the smallest lesions observed, indicating different levels of aggressiveness among isolates of the same species. On the banana fruit of cultivar ‘Prata’, lesion sizes ranged from 14.8 mm to 52.0 mm. The largest lesions were caused by the isolates COUFAL0565 (L. theobromae) and COUFAL0561 (L. theobromae), measuring 52.0 mm and 50.2 mm in diameter, respectively. In contrast, the smallest lesions were induced by the isolates COUFAL0563 (L. theobromae) and COUFAL0524 (L. theobromae), with 12.8 mm and 14.6 mm in diameter, respectively. On ‘Pacovan’ fruits, lesion diameters ranged from 8.2 mm to 63.3 mm, with COUFAL0541 (L. theobromae) and COUFAL0532 (L. pseudotheobromae) being the most aggressive isolates, at 63.3 mm and 62.7 mm in diameter, respectively. At the same time, COUFAL0563 (L. theobromae) and COUFAL0558 (L. theobromae) were the least aggressive isolates, inducing lesions of 8.2 mm and 9.3 mm in diameter, respectively. Finally, on ‘Terra’ inoculated fruits, the disease lesions range in size from 6.7 mm to 46.7 mm. The inoculations with isolates COUFAL0566 (L. theobromae) and COUFAL0525 (L. hormozganensis) resulted in statistically significantly larger lesions, at 46.7 mm and 42.3 mm in diameter, respectively. Conversely, the smallest lesions were observed when ‘Terra’ fruits were inoculated with isolates COUFAL0563 (L. theobromae) and COUFAL0555 (L. theobromae), at 6.7 mm and 8.2 mm in diameter, respectively (SI3).

Morphological and cultural characterization

A representative isolate from each molecularly identified Lasiodiplodia species was selected for morphological and cultural characterization: COUFAL0523 (L. laeliocattleyae), COUFAL0525 (L. hormozganensis), COUFAL0529 (L. iraniensis), COUFAL0527 (L. pseudotheobromae), COUFAL0526 (L. theobromae), and COUFAL0528 (L. brasiliensis). All species formed colonies on PDA that were initially grayish-white with moderately dense aerial mycelia (Fig. 6a). After 15 days of incubation, colony colors varied from gray and olive green to grayish-black, with reverse sides ranging from olive green to black (Fig. 6c-h). Mycelial growth rates varied from 21.9 mm to 42.5 mm per day, with colonies reaching a diameter of 9 cm between the second and fourth day. Pycnidia developed after 3 to 4 weeks on PDA supplemented with pine needles and appeared as solitary, globose structures with gray to black pigmentation (Fig. 6b). Conidia were ellipsoid to ovoid. Immature conidia were hyaline, with granular contents and thick cell walls, while mature conidia were dark brown, featuring a median septum and longitudinal striations (Fig. 6c-h). The conidial dimensions of the species examined here were slightly larger than those reported in previous literature (Table 2).

Discussion

This study provides the first large-scale survey on Lasiodiplodia species diversity associated with banana crown rot in commercial plantations located in the northern and northeastern parts of Brazil, integrating morphocultural, pathogenic, and molecular characterization. Six Lasiodiplodia species were identified: L. brasiliensis, L. hormozganensis, L. iraniensis, L. laeliocattleyae, L. pseudotheobromae, and L. theobromae. The genus comprises numerous phytopathogenic species with a broad geographic distribution34 known to cause socioeconomically important diseases, such as dieback, canker, gummosis, stem blight, collar and root rot, stem-end rot, and leaf spots51–57. Further, these fungi may act as secondary pathogens or endophytes that shift to a pathogenic state in response to abiotic stress23,58,59.

Historically, Lasiodiplodia was considered a monotypic genus, with L. theobromae as the type species60. However, advances in molecular and phylogenetic techniques have revealed considerable species-level diversity and the presence of cryptic species within the genus associated with a wide array of hosts27. In this study, 116 isolates obtained from bananas exhibiting crown rot symptoms were initially identified as Lasiodiplodia sp. based on the morphology of mature conidia. Although morphological characterization remains a foundational approach in fungal taxonomy, relying solely on this method is insufficient for accurate species delimitation within the genus Lasiodiplodia due to a high degree of morphological similarities among species61. Therefore, a polyphasic strategy that combines morphological, molecular, and phylogenetic data is essential for robust and accurate species identification62.

Multilocus phylogenetic analyses are essential for resolving species boundaries in Lasiodiplodia32,63,64. In this study, sequence data from four loci (ITS, tef1-α, tub2, and rpb2) were used to characterize the new isolates. While preliminary species differentiation was achievable using tef1-α alone, which supports its utility as a diagnostic marker for known species, single-gene analysis is not sufficiently robust for taxonomic resolution or identification of novel taxa19,65,66. Therefore, additional markers (ITS, tub2, and rpb2) were employed to enhance phylogenetic resolution as recommended for species-level delimitation in Lasiodiplodia62.

Species of Lasiodiplodia are commonly detected as endophytes and are regarded as generalist pathogens that can shift to a virulent lifestyle under environmental stresses such as drought, frost, or waterlogging67,68. Population genetic and biogeographic studies of L. theobromae indicate that its global dissemination has likely been facilitated by human-mediated movement of infected plant material69. In the present study, L. theobromae was the predominant species associated with crown rot across most sampled sites, underscoring its broad ecological adaptability and dispersal capacity. Conversely, L. theobromae was not identified in the state of Acre, Northern Brazil. The absence of L. theobromae in Acre may be linked to local ecological or climatic conditions, host distribution, or competition with other Lasiodiplodia species. Also, historical or phytosanitary factors could play a role. Although ecological, climatic, host-related, and historical facts could explain the absence of L. theobromae isolates in Acre, it is important to note that our survey was restricted to a single municipality in this state. Therefore, a large-scale study comprised by different cultivation areas and banana cultivars growing in Acre is needed before any definitive conclusion can be drawn.

Lasiodiplodia was first associated with banana fruits in Jamaica in 1931, where rapid fruit deterioration under storage conditions was observed following inoculation70. Lasiodiplodia theobromae, a cosmopolitan and opportunistic pathogen, is prevalent in tropical and subtropical regions and has been associated with more than 500 host plants71,72. In Brazil, the pathogen was previously reported to cause banana crown rot, however, identifications were based solely on morphological characteristics73, a method now recognized as inadequate for distinguishing Lasiodiplodia species66.

Lasiodiplodia pseudotheobromae, a cryptic species within the L. theobromae complex, differs morphologically in the size and structure of its conidia27. Originally described in Costa Rica, it is now known to infect a broad spectrum of host plants50,74–81. In Brazil, it has been reported on coffee, Platonia insignis (bacurizeiro), dragon fruit, persimmon, papaya, and mango47,82–85. To our knowledge, this study represents the first report of L. pseudotheobromae causing crown rot on bananas (Musa spp.) in Brazil.

Lasiodiplodia hormozganensis is another widespread species with a wide host range. It was first described in Iran causing branch dieback in Mangifera indica and Olea sp48. , later associated with fruit rot in eggplant in Haiti86, and with leaf spots on betel nut palm and beach cabbage in China79,82. In Brazil, this species has been reported associated with leaf spot of aloe vera87, dry rot of cassava88, stem rot of papaya47, and mango82. Recently, it was identified as the causal agent of crown rot in the banana cultivar ‘Prata’25.

Lasiodiplodia laeliocattleyae was originally described as Diplodia laeliocattleyae in Italy, associated with orchids of the genus Laeliocattleya (Sibilia 1927). A subsequent taxonomic revision merged this species with L. egyptiacae, previously reported to cause dieback in Mangifera indica49,50. Lasiodiplodia laeliocattleyae shows a narrow host range and has commonly been linked to dieback symptoms49,50,61,63. In Brazil, it has been reported as a pathogen of root rot in purging nuts65, peduncular rot in coconut89, and more recently associated with crown rot of bananas25.

Lasiodiplodia iranensis (syn. L. iraniensis) was first identified on Salvadora persica in Iran48. The species has been associated with dieback, gummosis, and various rot types across a wide host range49,52,55,64,90,91. In Brazil, it has been found causing disease on cashew, mango, coffee, and cassava82,88,92,93, and more recently, inducing banana crown rot in cultivar ‘Prata’ in the state of Ceará, Northeastern Brazil13. Lasiodiplodia brasiliensis (syn. L. brasiliense) was first reported in Brazil associated with papaya and mango47. Its distribution is concentrated in the Northeastern region, where it has been reported in association with various hosts including jackfruit, coconut, melon, watermelon, corn, custard apple, grapes, and bananas12,56,94–99.

In this study, optimal mycelial growth rates for all Lasiodiplodia species were determined between 25 °C and 30 °C on PDA medium. Previous studies have demonstrated minimum growth near 5 °C and above 35 °C, with optimal growth typically ranging from 15 °C to 25 °C under controlled conditions19. From an epidemiological standpoint, temperature is a key factor in Lasiodiplodia pathogenicity, particularly under projected climate change scenarios. Rising temperatures may not only favor faster growth but also modulate virulence traits, as shown in other fungal pathogens100. Indeed, proteomic and metabolomic analyses in related fungi revealed that elevated temperatures enhance the expression of genes involved in cell wall degradation, toxin production, mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, and the velvet protein complex101–104. These findings suggest that similar mechanisms may operate in Lasiodiplodia, potentially explaining the pigment production we observed at 35 °C. Although pigment production is variable among species and isolates27,47,48,50,105, it may reflect the activation of specific metabolic pathways under heat stress and could contribute to pathogenicity and adaptability106,107. Altogether, these results indicate the potential of combined metabolomic and proteomic studies to unveil putative influence of environmental changes in the epidemiology of crown rot disease in bananas.

The banana-associated Lasiodiplodia isolates characterized in this study were pathogenic when inoculated on banana fruits of cultivars ‘Prata’, ‘Pacovan’, and ‘Terra’, with L. theobromae and L. pseudotheobromae being the most aggressive species based on lesion growth rates. The pathogenicity assays revealed typical symptoms of crown rot, including fruit darkening and softening, both of which are associated with reduced quality and shelf life108. Under natural field conditions, the disease manifested as dry rot originating at the stalk and progressing into the developing fruit, differing from the typical postharvest symptoms. Additionally, early ripening was observed in affected fruits that remained attached to the bunch, likely as a result of pathogen-induced physiological changes. To our knowledge, this is the first report of these symptoms associated with banana crown rot disease caused by Lasiodiplodia spp. This ‘atypical’ manifestation might have important implications for disease epidemiology and production losses as it suggests that infection can compromise fruit quality even before harvest. Premature ripening may be related to pathogen-triggered hormonal or metabolic alterations, such as changes in ethylene biosynthesis or stress-induced signaling pathways, which accelerate fruit senescence. Further studies integrating physiological, hormonal, and metabolic approaches will be essential to elucidate the mechanisms underlying this phenomenon and to assess its impact on banana production systems.

This study provides the first systematic identification of Lasiodiplodia species associated with crown rot disease on bananas in Brazil. Based on morphological characteristics and multilocus phylogenetic analysis, our findings revealed that at least six species of Lasiodiplodia (L. brasiliensis, L. hormozganensis, L. iraniensis, L. laeliocattleyae, L. pseudotheobromae, and L. theobromae) are responsible for banana crown rot in the northern and northeastern regions of the country. Additionally, these results expand the current understanding of this pathosystem and provide important insights for the development of long-term, integrated disease management strategies.
