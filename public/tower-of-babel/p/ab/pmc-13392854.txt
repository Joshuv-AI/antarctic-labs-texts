Substance use and mental health outcomes among gay and bisexual men in South Korea: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Seong-Jo Jeong, Bomi Kim, Heeyoung Lee, Hyemin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigated the prevalence of substance use among gay and bisexual men in South Korea and examined its association with mental health outcomes.MethodsData were drawn from a nationwide cross-sectional survey of 1407 Korean gay and bisexual men aged 19–34 conducted in 2021. Participants completed measures assessing lifetime substance use (e.g., methamphetamine, ketamine, and cannabis) and mental health outcomes including depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and psychiatric service utilization. Multivariate regression analyses examined associations between substance use and mental health outcomes.ResultsAmong participants, 17.7% reported lifetime substance use. Substance use was significantly associated with increased risks of suicidality and psychiatric service utilization.ConclusionThis study found significant associations between substance use and adverse mental health outcomes among Korean gay and bisexual men. These findings highlight the urgent need for integrated interventions addressing both substance use and mental health in this population.

Introduction

Substance use represents a critical global public health challenge associated with a wide range of adverse health outcomes (World Health Organization, 2024). Existing literature has documented that substance use is closely linked to psychiatric morbidities, including depression and anxiety, and increases the risk of suicidal ideation and attempts (Lai et al., 2015; Poorolajal et al., 2016). Additionally, substance use is associated with increased psychiatric service utilization (Hoblyn et al., 2009; Jackson et al., 2001), reflecting both the severity of mental health burden and help-seeking behaviors among affected individuals (Medley et al., 2016). Given these well-established associations, investigating the intersection of substance use and mental health outcomes is particularly imperative for populations experiencing disparities in these outcomes.

The relationship between substance use and mental health among gay and bisexual men (GBM) involves distinct dynamics. GBM experience disproportionate rates of substance use and adverse mental health outcomes due to oppression-based stressors (e.g., minority stress) related to their sexual orientation. According to the minority stress model, chronic exposure to such minority stressors increases engagement in risk behaviors and exacerbates both physical and mental health burdens (Meyer, 2003).

Furthermore, syndemic theory posits that substance use, mental health burdens, and psychosocial stressors synergistically interact to amplify adverse health outcomes in marginalized populations (Stall et al., 2003; Turpin et al., 2021). This synergistic interaction is exemplified by chemsex (i.e., substance use specifically intended to facilitate or enhance sexual activity) among GBM. This population is more likely to be exposed to such behavior due to the convergence of limited access to safe public social spaces, specific subcultural modes of socialization, and a profound desire to mitigate social isolation by fostering intimacy within the community (Halkitis & Singer, 2018; Maxwell et al., 2019). Despite growing evidence, research on substance use and mental health among GBM in Asia remains limited.

South Korea (hereafter Korea) represents a particularly important context for two reasons. First, in Korea, where patriarchal social norms predominate and comprehensive anti-discrimination protections remain absent, GBM face compounded stigma and marginalization (e.g., internalized homophobia, sexual orientation discrimination) (Lee et al., 2019; Lee, Streed Jr., Yi, Choo and Kim, 2021a). Recent improvements in public awareness toward lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender, queer/questioning, and other identities (LGBTQ+) communities have been countered by the political mobilization of homophobia rooted in conservative evangelicalism (Kim, 2021; Lin & Lee, 2024). Moreover, public acceptance remains among the lowest in high-income countries, with less than half of the population expressing support for LGBTQ+ individuals (Ipsos, 2025; Korea Institute of Public Administration, 2025).

In this polarized sociopolitical climate, compounded by the absence of legal protections (Na et al., 2014), many Korean GBM remain closeted due to fear of social rejection. Korean GB men experience discrimination, internalized homophobia, and coming out challenges that contribute to elevated rates of depression and suicidal behaviors (Lee & Jeong, 2025; Lee et al., 2019; Lee, Park, Choi, Yi and Kim, 2021b). These mental health disparities are particularly concerning given that Korea has recorded the highest suicide rate among Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development nations since 2003 (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2025).

Secondly, substance use has emerged as a growing concern in Korean society. In Korea, substances such as cannabis and poppers have been prohibited under strict regulatory laws for decades, yet reported illicit substance use has more than doubled over the past decade (Supreme Prosecutors Office, 2025). Although prior research has documented elevated substance use among adolescents with same-sex sexual experience in Korea (Joung et al., 2024), substance use patterns in adulthood when accessibility to substances increases and their associations with mental health outcomes remain largely unexplored.

Addressing this critical gap and guided by minority stress and syndemic frameworks, this study examines associations between substance use and mental health outcomes among Korean GBM. Specifically, we investigate relationships between substance use and depressive symptoms, suicidal behaviors, and psychiatric service utilization. We hypothesize that substance users will report higher rates of depressive symptoms, suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, psychiatric service visits, and psychiatric medication use compared to nonusers.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedures

Data were drawn from a 2021 nationwide cross-sectional online survey of Korean LGBTQ+ young adults, conducted by Dawoom, a South Korean LGBTQ+ activist organization (Dawoom, 2022). Data were collected via Qualtrics between August 11 and September 7, 2021. Eligible participants were individuals aged 19–34 who had lived in South Korea within the past 10 years and self-identified as a sexual or gender minority. Given the absence of population-based sampling frames for sexual and gender minority individuals in South Korea, participants were recruited using convenience sampling through multiple online channels, including social media platforms (Facebook, Instagram, and Twitter), online SGM communities, SGM-specific dating applications, university-based online SGM forums, SGM-focused YouTube channels, and other related platforms. After providing informed consent, participants completed an online questionnaire assessing their social needs and health status. All participants received 4000 Korean won (∼$2.70 United States Dollars) as compensation.

Although the parent survey included a broad spectrum of sexual and gender minority individuals (n = 3911), the current study analyzed data from participants who identified as cisgender gay or bisexual men (n = 1407). This subsample had no missing values on any key study variables. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Oklahoma.

Measures

Lifetime substance use was assessed by asking participants whether they had ever used any of the following substances: ice (methamphetamine), ecstasy (3,4-Methylenedioxymethamphetamine), ketamine, erectile dysfunction medications (e.g., Viagra), cannabis, poppers (alkyl nitrites), gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB), or other substances. Participants who reported using any substance were classified as substance users, while those who reported no use were classified as substance nonusers.

Depressive symptoms were measured using the 11-item Korean translation of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-11; Kohout et al., 1993; Radloff, 1977). Items assess frequency of symptoms in the past week on a 4-point scale (0 = rarely/none to 3 = most/all of the time), with total scores ranging from 0 to 33. Scores ≥9 indicated clinically significant depressive symptoms (Kohout et al., 1993).

Two items assessed suicidality within the past 12 months. Suicidal ideation was measured by asking participants: “Have you seriously thought about dying by suicide within the past 12 months?” (yes/no). Suicide attempt was measured by asking: “Have you attempted suicide within the past 12 months?” (yes/no).

Two indicators assessed psychiatric service utilization within the past 12 months. First, participants reported whether they had sought professional help (e.g., physician, counselor) for mental health concerns such as depression, anxiety, insomnia, or stress within the past 12 months (yes/no). Second, participants reported whether they had been prescribed and used psychiatric medications (e.g., antidepressants, anxiolytics, sleep medications) for such mental health concerns, excluding cold medicine or diet pills (yes/no).

Sociodemographic characteristics were assessed using single-item questions including “What is your year of birth?” (converted to age; continuous), “Which of the following best describes your sexual orientation?” (gay vs. bisexual), “What is your highest level of education?” (high school or less, associate degree, bachelor's degree, graduate degree), “What is your average monthly total income?” (in 10,000 Korean won: no income, <100, 100–250, 250–450, >450), “What is your employment status?” (student, employed, self-employed), “What is your current residential area?” (metropolitan vs. non-metropolitan), and “How did you hear about this survey?” (social network services, online LGBTQ+ community, offline recruitment, and others).

Participants were identified as cisgender men by asking “What is your sex assigned at birth?” with response options including male and female and “What is your current gender identity?” with response options including man, woman, and nonbinary/genderqueer; only those who reported being assigned male at birth and selected “man” for gender identity were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses assessed the distribution of the participant characteristics, and the prevalence of lifetime substance use across sociodemographic variables. To examine associations between lifetime substance use and mental health outcomes, we conducted multivariate log-linked Poisson regression models with robust sandwich variance estimators. This approach was selected because the prevalence of most mental health outcomes exceeded 20%, making prevalence ratios (PRs) more appropriate than odds ratios (Barros & Hirakata, 2003). Models were adjusted for age, sexual orientation, educational attainment, monthly individual income, employment status, residential area, and participating channel. Analyses were conducted using STATA/SE version 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX).

Discussion

The current study provides empirical evidence on the prevalence and mental health correlates of substance use among Korean GBM. Approximately one in six participants (17.7%) reported lifetime use of any assessed substance, including 12.8% who reported a controlled drug (methamphetamine, ecstasy, ketamine, cannabis, poppers, or GHB). While this prevalence is lower than rates commonly observed in GBM populations in other countries (e.g., United States, European countries) (Medley et al., 2016; The European Men-who-have-sex-with-men Internet Survey Network, 2019), roughly 4.6 times the 2.8% lifetime controlled-drug use reported among Korean adults in a 2024 national survey (Korean Institute of Criminology and Justice, 2025), where lifetime substance use remains low due to strict legal penalties and pervasive social stigma (National Center for Mental Health, 2022). This disparity underscores that substance use persists as a significant behavioral health concern within the Korean GBM community despite the country's punitive legal environment. These patterns likely reflect minority stress processes, including discrimination and identity concealment, which may lead some GBM to use substances as a coping mechanism even in the face of legal risks.

The associations we observed between substance use and both suicidality and psychiatric service utilization align with the minority stress model (Meyer, 2003) and syndemic theory (Stall et al., 2003; Turpin et al., 2021), which posit that substance use and suicidality co-occur and mutually reinforce one another within marginalized populations. The elevated risk of suicide attempts among substance users may reflect the acute disinhibiting effects of substances, which can facilitate the transition from suicidal ideation to lethal action. Furthermore, given that substance use is criminalized in Korea, the fear of legal repercussions and the double stigma of being both a sexual minority and a substance user may exacerbate psychological distress, potentially contributing to elevated suicide risk.

Interestingly, while substance use was associated with psychiatric service utilization, it was not significantly associated with depressive symptoms. This apparent discrepancy may be partially explained by psychiatric treatment effects. The high rate of psychiatric intervention among substance users may effectively mitigate depressive symptoms, thereby masking an underlying association in this cross-sectional analysis. Alternatively, this pattern may suggest that substance use among Korean GBM is more strongly driven by socio-sexual contexts (e.g., chemsex) rather than solely as a coping mechanism for depressive affect (Power of Pleasure, 2022). Future research is needed to disentangle these competing explanations.

These findings carry several implications for intervention. Because substance use co-occurred with suicidality and elevated psychiatric service use, integrated programs that jointly address substance use and mental health—rather than siloed services—are likely to be most effective for this population. Such programs should be LGBTQ+-affirming and attentive to the minority-stress and socio-sexual (e.g., chemsex) contexts in which substance use occurs. Given Korea's punitive legal environment and the double stigma of being both a sexual minority and a substance user, confidential, non-criminalizing, harm-reduction–oriented services delivered through trusted community and online channels may be needed to reach those at highest risk. Routine screening for substance use and suicidality in these settings could support early identification and linkage to care.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design and discordant timeframes between substance use (lifetime) and mental health measures (past week for depressive symptoms; past 12 months for suicidality and service utilization) preclude establishing temporal causality between them. In addition, our lifetime (“ever use”) measure is inherently age-dependent and reflects cumulative exposure rather than current behavior; although we adjusted for age and the narrow age range (19–34 years) limits this differential, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and the associations may partly reflect cumulative rather than current use.

Second, generalizability is limited by the use of online non-probability sampling and the restriction of the study population to young adults aged 19–34. Because recruitment relied on social media and LGBTQ+ community networks, the sample likely over-represents community-connected and digitally engaged GBM and under-represents more isolated or concealed individuals; the resulting selection bias could either inflate or deflate our estimates of both substance use and mental health, and its direction is uncertain.

Third, our exposure combined pharmacologically and behaviorally heterogeneous substances—ranging from psychoactive recreational drugs (e.g., methamphetamine, ketamine, GHB) to sex-facilitating agents such as erectile dysfunction medications, which are clinically distinct from drugs of misuse per se. This aggregation may obscure substance-specific associations and should be interpreted accordingly. In a sensitivity analysis excluding erectile dysfunction medications, the associations with suicide attempts, psychiatric visits, and psychiatric medication use remained robust, the null association with depressive symptoms was unchanged, whereas the more modest association with suicidal ideation was attenuated and no longer significant, indicating that our findings reflect a broad pattern of substance use rather than the effect of any single agent.

Fourth, the exposure assessment had several additional constraints. The parent survey did not include measures of commonly used substances such as alcohol or tobacco, nor did it assess the frequency, recency, or quantity of use for any substance; this precludes substance-specific comparisons and dose-response analyses. Detailed contextual data on specific engagement in chemsex were also not collected. Finally, the analysis was confined to cisgender GBM, excluding other sexual and gender minority groups (e.g., sexual minority women, transgender individuals).

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine substance use and its mental health correlates among adult GBM in Korea, and one of few in Asia, extending a literature dominated by Western and adolescent samples. Beyond documenting elevated risk, it provides an empirical basis for clinical and public health responses tailored to a population that has remained largely invisible in both research and services. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish causality and collect detailed contextual data, such as substance use frequency/recency and use within specific socio-sexual settings, to better inform the development of targeted prevention and intervention strategies for this population.

Conclusion

This study found that substance use was significantly associated with increased suicidality and psychiatric service utilization among Korean GBM. Our findings underscore the urgent need for integrated clinical and public health approaches that recognize the interconnected nature of substance use and mental health challenges, rather than treating them as separate issues. Tailored prevention and treatment programs that simultaneously address both concerns may be particularly effective for improving Korean GBM's health.
