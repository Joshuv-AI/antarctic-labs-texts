Case series: Arterial tortuosity syndrome confirmed by homozygous SLC2A10 variants
Authors: Abdullah AlBathi, Bader A AlHariqi, Tasneem Rashid, Moayad AlMaimani, Omar AlKhajah, Ahmed AlDraihem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArterial tortuosity syndrome (ATS) is a very rare inherited connective tissue disorder caused by pathogenic variants in Solute Carrier Family 2 Member 10 (SLC2A10) gene. It primarily affects the large and medium-sized arteries, leading to abnormal elongation and tortuosity of the vascular tree. In children, the condition can present in markedly different ways, making early diagnosis challenging. We describe 3 children with genetically confirmed ATS who demonstrated strikingly different clinical courses. They underwent comprehensive cardiovascular evaluation using echocardiography, CT angiography, and whole-body MRI. Imaging in these cases revealed diffuse arterial tortuosity involving the aorta and its major branches, without aneurysm formation, dissection, or significant obstruction. Clinically, however, the presentations differed substantially. One patient, a neonate, presented early with respiratory distress and severe feeding difficulties and showed extensive systemic and pulmonary arterial involvement. The second patient, a 15-month-old infant, was clinically well and demonstrated a much milder vascular phenotype with preserved cardiopulmonary function. The third patient, a 6-month-old male, was referred for surgical management of a diaphragmatic hernia and evaluation of suspected ATS with an associated atrial septal defect (ASD) based on initial echocardiography. These cases highlight the wide clinical and imaging spectrum of ATS, even among patients with the same genetic diagnosis. Awareness of characteristic imaging features is essential for radiologists, as early recognition can lead to timely genetic confirmation, appropriate multidisciplinary care, and tailored long-term follow-up.Keywords: Arterial tortuosity syndrome, SLC2A10, CT angiography, Connective tissue disorder, Pediatric imaging

Introduction

Arterial tortuosity syndrome (ATS) is an extremely rare autosomal recessive connective tissue disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in Solute Carrier Family 2 Member 10 (SLC2A10) gene, which encodes the facilitative glucose transporter GLUT10 [1]. It is characterized by elongation and tortuosity of the aorta and medium-sized arteries, often accompanied by focal stenosis and aneurysmal dilation, and may be associated with systemic features such as skin laxity, joint hypermobility, diaphragmatic or hernia defects, and ophthalmologic involvement [1]. Despite increasing recognition, ATS remains exceptionally uncommon; recent cohorts suggest that just over 100 individuals with genetically confirmed disease have been described worldwide, underscoring its ultra-rare status [2].

The clinical spectrum of ATS is broad, ranging from severe neonatal presentations with respiratory compromise, feeding difficulties, and early vascular complications to milder childhood or even adult-onset disease discovered incidentally or during evaluation for hypertension or vascular anomalies [1]. This heterogeneity poses challenges for early diagnosis, risk stratification, and longitudinal surveillance—particularly in infants in whom overlapping features with other heritable thoracic aortic diseases may delay recognition [3].

Cross-sectional imaging is central to the diagnosis and characterization of ATS. Multidetector CT angiography (CTA) and MR angiography enable comprehensive assessment of the entire aorta and its branches, depicting hallmark findings such as marked aortic elongation, pronounced tortuosity of supra-aortic trunks, and involvement of pulmonary arteries, which may assume an early bifurcation pattern with an inverted “V” configuration [4]. Radiologists, therefore,play a pivotal role in raising the initial suspicion of ATS by recognizing these distinctive vascular patterns and prompting confirmatory molecular testing [4]. In this context, we report 3 pediatric patients with homozygous SLC2A10 pathogenic variants who illustrate striking phenotypic divergence. Through multimodality imaging, we highlight how similar genetic findings can be associated with very different clinical and radiologic presentations.

Case series

Case 1

A full-term male infant, aged 3 weeks, was referred from a peripheral hospital for evaluation of bronchiolitis, suspected congenital heart disease, and possible ATS. He was born via uncomplicated spontaneous vaginal delivery and required supplemental oxygen in the immediate postnatal period due to desaturation episodes. An echocardiographic assessment had been planned at the referring hospital, but the family discharged the infant before it could be performed. He re-presented 1 week later with cyanosis and increased work of breathing, necessitating oxygen therapy, nasogastric tube (NGT) feeding, and empiric antibiotic therapy.

There was a family history of congenital heart disease in 2 cousins. Chest radiography showed only mild peribronchial wall thickening, with no evidence of consolidation, atelectasis, pleural effusion, or pneumothorax, and demonstrated normal cardiac size and pulmonary vasculature (Fig. 1). Cardiac CT angiography revealed marked elongation and tortuosity of the aorta extending from the arch to the abdominal aorta, with mild dilation of the ascending aorta. The brachiocephalic trunk, left common carotid artery, and left subclavian artery showed pronounced tortuosity, which extended into their distal courses. Systemic and pulmonary venous drainage were otherwise normal. Collectively, these findings were highly suggestive of arterial tortuosity syndrome (Fig. 2).

Initial echocardiography was technically limited but identified a small muscular ventricular septal defect with a left-to-right shunt and preserved biventricular function. A follow-up study demonstrated a patent foramen ovale with an aneurysmal interatrial septum, a small mid-muscular ventricular septal defect, and a tortuous but unobstructed aortic arch. A modified barium swallow study revealed severe pharyngeal dysphagia (Dysphagia Outcome and Severity Scale [DOSS] score of 1), indicating that the infant was unsafe for oral feeding and required continued NGT nutrition. Whole-body MRI demonstrated diffuse tortuosity of the aorta and major branch vessels, including the lumbar arteries, without evidence of aneurysm or dissection (Fig. 2). Genetic testing confirmed a homozygous pathogenic variant in SLC2A10, establishing the diagnosis of autosomal recessive arterial tortuosity syndrome.

Case 2

A 15-month-old boy, an infant of a diabetic mother, was referred from a peripheral hospital for evaluation of a known tortuous aortic arch and left ventricular hypertrophy. He was electively admitted for advanced cardiac imaging. According to caregivers, he was feeding normally, passing urine and stool without difficulty, and showed no symptoms suggestive of cardiac or respiratory compromise.

On examination, he was active, well-appearing, and well-perfused. Cardiac examination demonstrated normal heart sounds without murmurs. Echocardiography demonstrated trivial tricuspid regurgitation, normal ventricular size and function, and an intact interventricular septum. The pulmonary artery branches were tortuous but appropriately sized for age, with laminar flow across the left pulmonary artery and mild flow turbulence across the right pulmonary artery (pressure gradient of 14 mmHg). Computed tomographic angiography confirmed a tortuous but unobstructed aorta, without evidence of stenosis or aneurysm. The pulmonary arteries were elongated with an inverted V-shaped bifurcation, and mild tortuosity of the pulmonary veins was present (Fig. 3). Genetic analysis identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in SLC2A10, confirming the diagnosis.

(D–G) CTA shows the tortuous appearance of the ascending aorta and its major branches including the celiac artery, superior mesenteric artery, bilateral main renal arteries (green arrows), and inferior mesenteric artery. (H) Global cardiomegaly with marked enlargement of the left side of the heart. Pectus excavatum is noted (black arrow).

Case 3

A 6-month-old male from Najran was referred for surgical management of a diaphragmatic hernia and evaluation of suspected ATS. He was born at full term via cesarean section following a pregnancy complicated by polyhydramnios. His medical history was notable for a right inguinal hernia repair at age 3 months, during which initial echocardiography first raised suspicion for ATS and identified an atrial septal defect (ASD).

At 6 months of age, the patient presented to the emergency department with a respiratory tract infection. Chest radiography identified an abnormal retrosternal shadow (Fig. 4), and a subsequent contrast enema confirmed the presence of the colon within the thoracic cavity, consistent with a Morgagni hernia.

(A & B) Frontal and lateral chest radiographs demonstrate a herniated bowel loop seen in the retrocardiac region (blue arrows), in keeping with an anterior diaphragmatic hernia.

Upon admission, the patient was clinically stable. Physical examination revealed a healed inguinal scar, a bulging sternum (pectus deformity), and generalized hypotonia. CT imaging demonstrated diffuse and extensive arterial tortuosity involving major vascular structures (Fig. 5). Additionally, it confirmed a large anterior diaphragmatic hernia (Morgagni type) containing bowel loops and a small left posterior fat-containing hernia (Fig. 6). Genetic analysis identified a homozygous pathogenic variant in SLC2A10, confirming the diagnosis of autosomal recessive arterial tortuosity syndrome. The patient was referred for laparoscopic repair and physical therapy for hypotonia.

(A–F) Axial and coronal CT of the chest demonstrates diffuse tortuous appearance of patent large and medium vessels, including the aortic arch (orange arrows), thoracic aorta, and its major branches including the left subclavian (green arrow) and brachiocephalic artery (red arrow).

(A–F) Large anterior diaphragmatic hernia spanning 6.3 cm, containing bowel loops, causing a mass effect pushing the heart posteriorly and causing a leftward midline shift (blue arrows). A non-complicated left posterior diaphragmatic hernia is also noted containing a portion of the liver (red arrows). A small left posterior fat-containing diaphragmatic hernia is indicated (green arrows).

Comparative phenotypic summary

All 3 patients demonstrated diffuse arterial tortuosity involving the aorta and major branch vessels, consistent with arterial tortuosity syndrome secondary to homozygous SLC2A10 pathogenic variants.

The neonatal patient exhibited a more severe and early-onset phenotype, including respiratory symptoms, significant feeding dysfunction due to severe pharyngeal dysphagia, and widespread arterial involvement affecting both systemic and pulmonary circulations. In contrast, the older infant demonstrated a milder phenotype with isolated vascular tortuosity, preserved cardiac function, and an absence of feeding or respiratory difficulties. They had non-obstructive aortic pathology and normal ventricular function. The third patient illustrated the systemic connective tissue involvement characteristic of diffuse arterial tortuosity, with multiple hernia defects, including an inguinal hernia and a Morgagni-type diaphragmatic hernia. These findings emphasize that ATS should be considered a multisystem disorder, particularly in infants presenting with congenital hernias or diaphragmatic anomalies.

Collectively, these cases demonstrate the broad phenotypic variability of arterial tortuosity syndrome, even among patients with identical molecular diagnoses. Recognition of this variability is essential for radiologists and clinicians, as imaging often provides the first clue to diagnosis and facilitates timely genetic confirmation and multidisciplinary management. Discussion

These 3 cases illustrate the striking heterogeneity of arterial tortuosity syndrome. Despite sharing the same genetic diagnosis, the patients differed significantly in age at presentation, symptom burden, and extent of vascular involvement. The neonatal case represented the severe end of the spectrum, with early respiratory compromise and feeding dysfunction, while the 15-month-old infant demonstrated a milder, largely asymptomatic phenotype.

Case 3 highlights that ATS is a systemic connective tissue disorder, not merely a vascular one. The literature notes that ATS may be associated with features such as skin laxity and hernia defects [1]. Case 3 exemplifies this through the presence of both an inguinal hernia and a Morgagni-type diaphragmatic hernia. Furthermore, prenatal findings such as polyhydramnios, seen in Case 3, may serve as an early clinical red flag.

Imaging was crucial for diagnosis in all patients. CT and MR angiography allowed comprehensive assessment of the vascular tree and exclusion of complications such as aneurysm or dissection. In the neonatal patient, whole-body MRI was particularly valuable as a radiation-sparing method for evaluating systemic involvement. ATS can mimic other heritable connective tissue disorders, including Loeys–Dietz syndrome and vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome [3]. However, the predominance of arterial tortuosity relative to aneurysm formation is a distinguishing feature [4].

Conclusion

Arterial tortuosity syndrome is a rare but vital cause of diffuse vascular and connective tissue abnormalities in children. These cases demonstrate that disease severity and clinical presentation vary widely, ranging from severe neonatal respiratory distress and feeding dysfunction to incidental findings of vascular tortuosity or presentation with multiple surgical hernias.

Radiologists play a pivotal role in early detection by recognizing characteristic vascular patterns and associated extravascular findings, such as diaphragmatic defects. Recognizing these clusters of symptoms enables timely genetic confirmation, appropriate surgical planning for connective tissue defects, and coordinated long-term cardiovascular surveillance.
