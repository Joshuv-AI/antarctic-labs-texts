Risk-dependent conditional survival analysis and annual hazard rate of primary ocular adnexal lymphoma
Authors: Jiali Song, Shicheng Chen, Jiajiang Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe real-time prognosis of patients with primary ocular adnexal lymphoma (POAL) who have survived for several years remains uncertain. Our objective was to assess survival dynamics over time in POAL using conditional survival (CS) analysis and annual hazard functions.MethodsWe utilized data from the SEER database (2004–2019) to conduct CS analysis on POAL patients. A total of 1901 patients were included. CS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the annual hazard rate (AHR) was calculated to examine changes in mortality risk over time. A predictive nomogram incorporating CS analysis was developed using a random survival forest (RSF) approach, with model performance evaluated through calibration, concordance index (C-index), receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsCS analysis revealed a significant improvement in survival over time, with 10-year CS rates increasing from 69% at diagnosis to over 96% for patients surviving 1–9 years. The AHR peaked at 4.1% in the first year, declining progressively to 1.7% by year 10. The RSF algorithm identified five key prognostic factors—age, tumor site, histology, tumor stage, and radiotherapy—as important predictors. A CS-based nomogram was developed, and its reliability was further confirmed through calibration curves, C-index values, and ROC analysis, with AUC values exceeding 0.80 for 3-, 5-, and 10-year survival predictions.ConclusionsThis study outlined the changes in CS prognosis and AHR for patients with POAL. Furthermore, we developed a CS-based nomogram using SEER data, which effectively integrated dynamic survival improvements and time-dependent risk factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04800-z.

Introduction

Primary ocular adnexal lymphoma (POAL) is a rare form of extranodal lymphoma that primarily affects the orbit, eyelids, conjunctiva, and lacrimal glands, with an annual incidence rate of 0.307 per 100,000 [1–3]. The majority of POAL cases are histologically classified as extranodal marginal zone lymphoma of mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue (MALT) [4, 5]. Clinically, POAL often presents with painless orbital swelling, proptosis, or conjunctival masses, making early diagnosis challenging. Prognostically, while the overall survival (OS) rate is relatively favorable compared to other extranodal lymphomas, with an estimated 5-year relative survival rate ranging from 74.3% to 89.7%, survival outcomes vary significantly depending on factors such as age, stage, and histological subtype [3, 6, 7]. However, despite existing studies on the prognosis of this tumor type, they all rely on traditional static survival data, which limits their ability to accurately assess and communicate improved survival prospects for patients who have already survived several years. This underscores the critical need for dynamic survival estimation.

Traditional survival analysis estimates, such as the Kaplan–Meier method, offer survival probabilities calculated from the time of diagnosis but fail to account for the changing risk profile of patients as they survive longer [8]. A patient’s risk profile is not fixed and has been reported to diminish with increasing years of survival, leading to an improved probability of survival over time [8]. Consequently, static survival data often underestimates these dynamic changes, underscoring the importance of dynamic survival analysis [8–11]. Conditional survival (CS) analysis and the annual hazard function, on the other hand, effectively address the limitations of traditional survival estimates by incorporating time-dependent changes in risk, offering a deeper understanding of survival dynamics [8, 10, 11].

To further enhance clinical utility, integrating CS analysis into a predictive nomogram provides a user-friendly tool for individualized risk assessment. Nomograms are graphical representations of statistical models that combine multiple prognostic factors to generate personalized survival probabilities [12, 13]. Incorporating CS into a nomogram allows for dynamic survival predictions, improving decision-making for both clinicians and patients, especially in the context of POAL, where individualized prognostication is crucial due to the disease’s heterogeneity.

In this study, we utilized the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to perform CS analysis on POAL patients. Our analysis aimed to dynamically describe survival outcomes over time, identify critical periods of increased mortality risk, and develop a CS-based nomogram to improve the precision of survival predictions. This approach provides a robust framework for addressing the limitations of traditional survival analysis, offering a more comprehensive understanding of POAL prognosis.

Methods

Study population

The SEER database, one of the world’s largest publicly available cancer surveillance resources, encompasses participants from 22 geographic regions across the United States. For this study, data were obtained from the SEER*Stat, incorporating information from 17 population-based cancer registries.

Patients included in the study were selected based on the following criteria: (1) diagnosed between 2004 and 2019; (2) histologically confirmed B-cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, classified according to the 2021 revision of the Lymphoid Neoplasm Recode (derived from the third edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, ICD-O-3); (3) primary tumor sites restricted to specific ocular adnexal locations, including the eyelid (C44.1), conjunctiva (C69.0), lacrimal gland (C69.5), and orbit (C69.6); (4) pathologically confirmed as POAL; and (4) diagnosis of primary or initial ocular adnexal lymphoma. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) insufficient clinical or pathological data; and (2) missing follow-up information.

Variables and primary outcomes

A total of twelve variables, including demographic, clinical, and treatment-related factors, were chosen for the analysis: age at diagnosis, sex, race, tumor site, laterality, histological subtype, tumor stage, surgery type, radiotherapy (RT), chemotherapy, marital status, and household income. The primary outcome measures were overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS). OS was defined as the interval from the date of diagnosis to death from any cause. For CSS, the primary event of interest was death attributable to POAL, whereas deaths from unrelated causes were treated as censored observations.

Statistical analyses

Conditional survival

OS in POAL patients was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, which also facilitated the calculation of CS. The formula CS(y∣x) = OS(y + x)/OS(x) was applied to estimate CS, where CS(y∣x) indicates the likelihood of surviving y additional years after already living x years post-diagnosis [9, 14]. Here, OS(y + x) and OS(x) correspond to Kaplan-Meier survival probabilities at y + x years and x years, respectively. The calculation for conditional cancer-specific survival (CCSS) followed the same methodology.

Annual hazard rate

The AHR is a measure of the mortality risk within a specific one-year interval. It is calculated by dividing the number of deaths that occurred during a given year by the total number of patients at risk (cumulative follow-up patients) at the beginning of that year. The formula is expressed as: AHR=Number of deaths in the year​/Cumulative follow-up patients in the year [10]. The AHR curve provides a clear visualization of how a patient’s mortality risk evolves over time, helping to identify dynamic changes in risk throughout the course of the disease.

Landmark method

A landmark analysis was performed to evaluate survival patterns before and after the 5-year time point. Patients were divided into two intervals: diagnosis to 5 years, and beyond 5 years. For the analysis beyond 5 years, only patients who survived the initial 5-year period were included. Survival rates were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between subgroups were compared using the log-rank test.

CS-nomogram model development and evaluation

The entire cohort was randomly divided into a training group and a validation group at a 7:3 ratio. In training cohort, we utilized variable importance (VIMP), an internal statistical metric of the RSF algorithm, to identify prognostic factors for feature selection. To enhance robustness for VIMP, the bootstrap resampling method with 1,000 iterations was employed. Variables with a VIMP greater than 0.01 were used to build a nomogram model through multivariable Cox regression analysis. Additionally, the concept of CS was incorporated into the nomogram to develop a CS-based nomogram, enabling the creation of a personalized model for final development.

Model performance was evaluated in both training and validation cohorts. The accuracy of the CS-nomogram was assessed using calibration curves. To evaluate its discrimination and stability, the concordance index (C-index) and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) with area under the curve (AUC) were applied. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to confirm the clinical value by calculating the net benefit of implementing medical interventions based on the CS-nomogram.

Statistical analysis for this study was performed using R (version 4.4.1). A two-tailed test was applied, and P values below 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the 1901 patients included in this study are presented in Table 1, with 1330 patients assigned to the training cohort and 571 to the validation cohort. The majority of patients, accounting for 77.7%, were over the age of 50. The cohort comprised 45.8% male and 54.2% female patients. Regarding racial distribution, White patients represented the majority (79.0%), while 21.0% were classified as other racial groups. The primary tumor sites included the conjunctiva (28.5%), orbit (52.2%), lacrimal gland (12.9%), and eyelid (6.5%). Histologically, MALT lymphoma was the most prevalent subtype (61.2%), followed by diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL, 12.2%). The majority of cases (79.0%) presented with localized tumors, and unilateral involvement was observed in 91.5% of patients. Regarding treatment modalities, 40.6% of patients underwent surgery, 62.8% received radiotherapy, and 25.5% were administered chemotherapy. Statistical analysis indicated that the two cohorts were comparable (P > 0.05) across all baseline variables, except for marital status (P = 0.044).

Conditional survival and annual hazards over time in patients with POAL

The CS analysis reported a dynamic improvement in survival over time, indicating that patients who had survived longer after diagnosis exhibited better survival prospects for subsequent years. The 10-year CS rates showed a steady increase over time, climbing from around 69% at diagnosis to over 96% for patients who had survived 1 to 9 years (Fig. 1A). This reflected the decreasing cumulative mortality risk for long-term survivors. Regarding CSS, the 10-year CCSS rates increased from 90% to 99% as the duration of survivorship increased from 1 to 9 years (Figure S1). CS analysis across DLBCL, FL, and MALT subtypes demonstrated that prognosis consistently improved with increasing survivorship duration. While FL and MALT maintained high stable rates, DLBCL showed the most dramatic improvement in long-term survival probability after surviving the initial high-risk years (Figure S2). Meanwhile, the CS(1 | x) curve exhibited stability, reinforcing the idea of progressively improved survival over time (Fig. 1B). The smoothed AHR illustrated the variable nature of survival improvements in POAL patients. Initially, the AHR reached its peak at 4.1% during the first year, gradually declined, and showed a slight fluctuation around the fifth year before leveling off to 1.7% by the tenth year (Fig. 1C). This pattern underscored an elevated early mortality risk, followed by a stabilization and eventual decrease in hazards among long-term survivors.

CS-nomogram development and evaluation

Using the training data, the RSF algorithm identified 5 out of 12 factors with a variable VIMP score exceeding 0.01 including age, tumor site, histology, tumor stage and RT (Fig. 2). These factors, validated through the bootstrap method, were selected for constructing the final model. By integrating the multivariable regression model with CS analysis, we successfully developed a CS-nomogram for dynamically predicting the survival prognosis of POAL (Fig. 3).

Model performance was assessed in both training and validation cohorts. The calibration curves demonstrated high model accuracy, with predicted survival rates closely aligning with observed outcomes along the 45-degree line in both training and validation cohorts (Fig. 4A and B). The model’s discriminatory ability was further evaluated using the C-index and ROC curves. The C-index values for training and validation predictions were 0.800 and 0.819 respectively. The AUC values for 3-, 5-, and 10-year predictions were 0.82, 0.81, and 0.83 in the training set (Fig. 4C), and 0.84, 0.86, and 0.87 in the validation cohort (Fig. 4D). DCA demonstrated a substantial net benefit of employing the CS-nomogram as a tool for guiding medical interventions compared to treating all patients or none (Fig. 5A and B).

Risk-dependent conditional survival and annual hazards over time

Building on our earlier analysis, which revealed an elevated early mortality risk within the first five years and identified five key prognostic factors, we then used the landmark method to estimate variables that affect subsequent survival in patients who have survived five years. And we further explored risk-dependent CS and AHR trends over time. The Kaplan–Meier curves revealed that while all variables showed significant differences between subgroups at the 5-year landmark and beyond, only patients with regional or distant tumors exhibited borderline significance compared to those with localized tumors after 5 years (Fig. 6A–E). The 10-year CS curves indicated significant improvement in survival rates over time for the subgroup of patients with poorer prognoses. The gap in CS rates between this subgroup and those with better prognoses narrowed each year (Fig. 6F–J). Additionally, the AHR curves demonstrated a gradual decrease over time in the AHRs associated with these risk factors (Fig. 6K–O).

Discussion

Accurate prognostic evaluation plays a pivotal role in clinical decision-making, guiding treatment strategies, and improving patient outcomes. Traditional survival analysis provides static estimates from diagnosis, overlooking the dynamic changes in survival probabilities over time. This limitation may lead to inaccurate assessments, particularly for long-term survivors or those with poor early outcomes [15, 16]. This study utilized CS analysis and AHR calculations to address the limitations of traditional OS analysis and supporting improved clinical decision-making for patients with complex survival trajectories.

By incorporating CS into our analysis, we were able to illustrate the progressive improvement in survival probabilities for POAL patients who had already survived several years. A notable finding in our study is the significant divergence between the CS estimates based on OS and the CCSS rates. While the 10-year CCSS remained excellent (> 90%), reflecting the indolent nature of the predominant MALT subtype and effective disease control, the corresponding 10-year CS was considerably lower (~ 69%). This discrepancy underscored the substantial impact of competing risks within the POAL population. Since a large proportion of these patients are older (with 77.7% aged > 50 years), they are more vulnerable to non-cancer-related mortality, such as cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases, rather than the lymphoma itself. Consequently, the divergence between CS and CCSS suggested that the lymphoma effectively becomes a manageable chronic disease. For long-term survivors, overall prognosis is driven less by the cancer itself and more by other age-related health issues. This suggested that clinical management for long-term POAL survivors should shift from a purely oncological focus to a holistic approach that rigorously manages concurrent health conditions. The smoothed AHR peaked at 4.1% in first year, decreased progressively, showed minor variations around the fifth year, and leveled off at 1.7% by the tenth year. The steady increase in 10-year CS rates and the decline of AHR underscored the dynamic changes in survival risks over time. These findings emphasized that patients with longer survival durations experienced a marked reduction in mortality risk, enabling more optimistic prognostic predictions for long-term survivors. These detailed prognostic dynamics provided patients with a comprehensive understanding of the outlook for POAL, fostering greater confidence and hope in their prognosis.

Subsequently, we employed the RSF method to identify prognostic factors and integrated them into a CS-based nomogram model, demonstrating both methodological innovation and clinical relevance. The RSF approach excels in handling complex, non-linear relationships and interactions among variables, enabling the accurate selection of key risk factors [17, 18]. This data-driven technique ensures robustness and minimizes bias, making it particularly suitable for survival analysis in heterogeneous populations. Although several prognostic models for POAL tumors have been established [3, 19], they are limited to predicting static outcomes. As a result, survival probabilities for patients who have already lived beyond a certain period are often underestimated. The CS-nomogram developed in this study represents a significant step forward from static to dynamic prognostication. Unlike traditional models that freeze a patient’s risk assessment at the moment of diagnosis, our tool dynamically recalibrates survival probabilities as patients surpass critical survival milestones. This feature is particularly vital for POAL, where the prognosis for long-term survivors typically improves. By reflecting this evolving risk profile, the nomogram provides clinicians and patients with updated, real-time survival estimates, avoiding the inaccuracy of applying baseline statistics to patients who have already survived several years. Consequently, this dynamic approach offers not just better accuracy, but also valuable reassurance for long-term survivors.

Our RSF-based analysis identified age as the most significant prognostic factor, likely reflecting the predominance of POAL in middle-aged and elderly patients, as well as its relatively favorable overall prognosis. Given the high heterogeneity of this tumor group, histological subtypes significantly influenced survival outcomes. Among these, DLBCL demonstrated the poorest prognosis, consistent with previous studies [20, 21]. Interestingly, within our CS-nomogram, the variable Histology (specifically DLBCL) was assigned a relatively lower prognostic weight compared to Age, despite the known biological aggressiveness of DLBCL. This apparent discrepancy reflected the distinct nature of OS prediction in an aging cohort, as opposed to CSS. Given that nearly 80% of our patients were over 50 years old, competing risks from non-cancer comorbidities (e.g., cardiovascular events) play a dominant role in determining all-cause mortality, thereby elevating the prognostic weight of age while diluting the impact of histological subtypes. Furthermore, this finding aligned with our CS analysis (Figure S2), which demonstrated that DLBCL patients who survived the initial high-risk period (1–2 years) achieved a “conditional cure”, with long-term survival probabilities converging with those of indolent subtypes. Thus, the nomogram faithfully captured the treated natural history of POAL, where the long-term threat to life often shifts from the malignancy itself to age-related competing risks.

The anatomical site of POAL also played a critical role in determining survival, with notable variability in outcomes. Furthermore, tumor staging based on the SEER database revealed significantly worse prognoses for regional and distant-stage tumors. Therapeutic strategies for POAL include radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, antibiotic treatment, and surgical resection. Most POAL cases are localized to the periocular region at diagnosis, where radiotherapy remains the most widely utilized and well-established treatment, with our study confirming its significant survival benefits [7]. Given our focus on dynamic prognostic evaluation and precise survival prediction, the impact of surgery and chemotherapy on overall survival was relatively limited within our cohort. The relatively limited impact of surgery and chemotherapy on overall survival can be largely attributed to the indolent biological behavior and inherently favorable prognosis of POAL. Since the majority of our cohort consisted of MALT lymphoma, a subtype characterized by slow progression and high survival rates, standard locoregional control (such as radiotherapy) is often sufficient. To streamline the model, these treatments were excluded from the final model construction. However, their potential benefits in specific subgroups should not be overlooked. Future research is needed to identify patient subpopulations for whom chemotherapy, surgery, and related therapeutic strategies may be most appropriate.

Risk-dependent CS and AHR analyses provided further insights into the temporal impact of key prognostic factors on survival. For example, tumor stage demonstrated borderline significance beyond the five-year mark, indicating that the adverse effects of advanced stage diminish with increasing survival time. The narrowing gap in CS rates between regional/distant and localized subgroups highlighted the potential for time-dependent improvements in prognosis, even for patients initially classified as high-risk. These findings may significantly alleviate patients’ fear of poor tumor staging, fostering confidence in treatment and enhancing adherence to both therapy and follow-up care.

Owing to the large sample size of the SEER database, we were able to describe the dynamic survival outcomes and AHR variations of this tumor type, as well as develop a relatively robust predictive model. However, this study also has limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis based on SEER data, selection bias may exist, and the findings may not generalize beyond the U.S. population. Second, the SEER database lacks critical details such as comorbidities, specific treatment regimens, the use of immunotherapy, and recurrence status, all of which could further improve prognostic accuracy. Third, treatment advancements during the study period were not fully accounted for, limiting insights into dynamic treatment effects. Fourth, our CS-based nomogram was internally validated; external validation in independent cohorts is necessary to confirm its applicability. Finally, although we adjusted for histology in our model, the distinct biological behaviors of MALT and DLBCL ideally warrant separate prognostic models. In our study, a unified model was constructed to ensure sufficient statistical power. Future research with larger, multi-center sample sizes should aim to develop histology-specific nomograms to further optimize predictive precision for these distinct subtypes.

Conclusions

This study conducted a CS analysis and developed a dynamic nomogram for POAL using SEER data. The model, incorporating key prognostic factors, provided accurate, personalized survival predictions and revealed dynamic survival improvements and decreasing hazard rates over time. Risk-dependent analyses highlighted the diminishing impact of advanced stages. Despite limitations, this study offered valuable insights into POAL prognosis and underscored the clinical utility of CS-based models for guiding tailored treatment decisions and improving patient counseling.
