Partial Splenic embolization combined with endoscopic variceal ligation in variceal rebleeding prevention among liver cirrhosis patients complicated with hypersplenism, thrombocytopenia, and esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage: a prospective observational study
Authors: Bin Yue, Di Yang, Zhongqiao Lu, Shiyou Zhao, Zhenhong Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with liver cirrhosis often suffer from complications such as splenomegaly, thrombocytopenia, and esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage, necessitating effective preventive measures. This study aimed to investigate the outcome of partial splenic embolization (PSE) combined with endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL) in variceal rebleeding prevention among liver cirrhosis patients complicated with hypersplenism, thrombocytopenia, and esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage.MethodsThis prospective observational study enrolled liver cirrhosis patients complicated with hypersplenism, thrombocytopenia, and esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage who received PSE combined with EVL (PSE & EVL) or EVL alone based on informed patient’s choice at the Affiliated Wenshan Hospital between November 2019 and October 2021. The primary outcome was the variceal rebleeding rate at postoperative 2 years, secondary outcomes included changes in platelet, white blood cell (WBC) counts and complications.ResultsThe baseline characteristics between patients who received PSE & EVL (n = 26, aged 54.12 ± 9.39, 21 males) and EVL alone (n = 40, aged 52.08 ± 11.64, 33 males) were comparable (all P > 0.05). Two years after surgery, patients received PSE & EVL were more likely to have lower rate (23.3% vs. 53.5%, P = 0.020) and risk of variceal rebleeding [HR (hazard ratio) = 0.36, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.17–0.77, P = 0.020] compared to those received EVL alone. The platelet (60.58 ± 13.55 vs. 48.84 ± 11.41, P = 0.007) and WBC (5.12 ± 0.91 vs. 4.18 ± 0.92, P = 0.002) levels in patients received PSE + EVL at postoperative 2 years were significantly higher than those received EVL alone, while the occurrence of postoperative complications, such as abdominal pain, abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, postoperative fever, and palpitations between patients who received PSE & EVL and EVL alone were similar (all P > 0.05).ConclusionCombining PSE with EVL may reduce variceal rebleeding risk and boosts postoperative platelet and WBC counts without increasing complications. Background

Esophagogastric variceal bleeding (EVB) is a prevalent complication caused by portal hypertension in decompensated cirrhosis. It is characterized by a sudden onset and a high mortality rate, with statistics showing that 10–20% of patients die within six weeks of the initial hemorrhage [1]. Furthermore, patients who do not receive secondary prophylaxis have a significant risk of experiencing recurrent bleeding and mortality within two years of the first hemorrhage [2]. In addition to common symptoms such as appetite loss, weight loss, and fatigue, EVB can also lead to severe upper gastrointestinal bleeding, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), abdominal pain, and discomfort [3]. Moreover, the fear of experiencing recurring bleeding and life-threatening complications can trigger emotional distress and anxiety in EVB patients [2, 4]. Collectively, these factors have a significant impact on quality of life of individuals with EVB. EVB often requires hospitalization and medical intervention like endoscopic procedures [3, 5], medication [6], and sometimes liver transplantation in severe cases [7]. These treatments aimed at controlling bleeding or preventing rebleeding can be associated with complications like post-procedure pain, discomfort, side effects, risk of infection, and perforation in addition to their high cost and invasiveness, leading to a significant burden on the patient. Therefore, preventing rebleeding is crucial in reducing morbidity and mortality rates, as well as improving the quality of life for individuals with cirrhotic patients after the initial hemorrhage. The current methods of secondary prophylaxis of EVB in cirrhosis like nonselective beta-blockers (NSBB), endoscopy, vascular intervention, and surgery, have shown limited success [8] especially in cirrhotic patients with hypersplenism.

Partial splenic embolization (PSE) [9], involving the selective embolization of the splenic artery branches to reduce blood flow to the spleen, has been used for the treatment of hypersplenism in cirrhosis patients. This procedure may increase the platelet count, and reduce hypersplenism related complications like thrombocytopenia and leukopenia, thus contributing to decreased risk of bleeding. It also may decrease portal pressure and improve liver function [10, 11]. However, this procedure can result in side effects such as splenic infarction, abscess formation, sepsis, and portal vein thrombosis [12]. Similarly, endoscopic variceal ligation (EVL) is a widely used endoscopic therapy for the management of esophagogastric varices that involves placing bands over the bleeding varices to achieve homeostasis and prevent rebleeding in cirrhosis patients [13]. Although this technique has a lower risk of complications than other approaches, it needs repeated endoscopic sessions to achieve variceal eradication. In addition, it may have some associated complications like esophageal strictures, ulceration, and perforation, and unable to address hypersplenism [14].

Although there is limited research available that evaluated the efficacy and safety of PSE combined with EVL [15, 16], it is hypothesized that combination may potentially address esophagogastric varices and hypersplenism related complications. Furthermore, by reducing the spleen size and splanchnic blood flow, PSE may potentially enhance the efficacy of EVL in variceal rebleeding prevention. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the outcome of PSE combined with EVL in variceal rebleeding prevention among liver cirrhosis patients complicated with hypersplenism, thrombocytopenia, and esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage.

Methods

Study design and patients

This prospective observational study enrolled liver cirrhosis patients complicated with hypersplenism, thrombocytopenia, and esophagogastric variceal hemorrhage who received PSE combined with EVL (PSE & EVL) or EVL alone based on informed patient’s choice at the Affiliated Wenshan Hospital between November 2019 and October 2021. The inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18–75 years old; (2) recovered from first-time or acute variceal bleeding; (3) diagnosed with liver cirrhosis with hypersplenism and thrombocytopenia (platelet < 100 × 109/L); (4) patients who received PSE combined with EVL (PSE & EVL) or EVL alone. Exclusion criteria: (1) had previously received treatment such as splenectomy, PSE, EVL, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), or tissue glue injection (other than reasons for oral NSBB treatment); (2) had hepatocellular carcinoma or other malignant tumors; and (3) had hepatic failure, hepatic function grade C, severe ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, or abnormal coagulation function [prothrombin time activity (PTA) < 40%]. This study was approved by the Ethic Committee of The People’s Hospital of Wenshan Prefecture, Affiliated Wenshan Hospital, KunMing University of Science and Technology (No. YBXM2021027), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Procedures

We routinely recorded the baseline clinical characteristics, preoperative peripheral blood leukocyte and platelet counts, and liver function indexes, including total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), albumin (ALB), and coagulation function like prothrombin time (PT) and calculated liver function scores.

The width of portal and splenic veins was determined using computed tomography (CT) portal vein angiography and assessed the severity of varicose veins during endoscopy. Esophageal varices were categorized into three grades based on their morphology, diameter, and risk of bleeding: Grade 1 for straight and small caliber varices, grade 2 for moderately enlarged, beady varices covering less than one-third of the lumen, and grade 3 for markedly enlarged, nodular or tumor-shaped varices occupying more than one-third of the lumen [17]. Gastric varices were classified as gastroesophageal varices (GOV), and furtherly subdivided into GOV-1 (characterized by continuous esophageal varices and extending 2–5 cm along the lesser curvature of the stomach), and GOV-2 (characterized by varices extend beyond the gastroesophageal junction into the fundus of the stomach). In addition, isolated gastric varices (IGV), defined as gastric varices occurring in the absence of esophageal varices, were classified into IGV-1 and IGV-2 following Sarin’s classification [18].

Partial Splenic embolization

The presence of splenomegaly and details of the blood supply to the spleen were confirmed through abdominal-enhanced CT and portal venous imaging. All PSEs were performed within 14 days after stabilizing the initial acute gastrointestinal bleeding. A 5 F puncture sheath (Terumo Medical Corporation) was used to implant a microcatheter guidewire system (Suzhou Hengrui Disheng Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). This was done by accessing the femoral artery with a percutaneous femoral artery puncture cannula using Seldinger’s puncture technique. After identifying the splenic artery through imaging, the target vessel was embolized using fiber platinum coils (Boston Scientific Corporation). The appropriate vessels for embolization were selected based on the distribution of the splenic artery blood supply, with the embolization covering 50% to 70% of the splenic volume, as guided by intraoperative real-time DSA imaging. A CT scan was performed 72 h post-operation to further confirm efficacy.

Endoscopic variceal ligation

During the period of hemodynamic stabilization, the patient underwent an endoscopic examination and treatment while under anesthesia from endotracheal intubation. All procedures were conducted by physicians and trained assistants who have extensive experience. Olympus CV-290 or FUJINON ELUXEO-7000 endoscopic systems were used. Initially, a complete gastroscopy was performed to determine the location of the esophagogastric variceal bleeding and rule out any other gastrointestinal bleeding disorders. After the gastroscope was withdrawn, endoscopic ligation was carried out using the Multi-Band Ligator endoscopic ligating instrument (TIANJIN T Y. Medical Organism Material Research Co. Ltd.). Priority was given to ligating varices that showed clear signs of red coloration and vascular rupture. The remaining varices were sequentially ligated until they were either eradicated or considered to have a low risk of bleeding. The gastroscopy was repeatedly reviewed, and additional ligations were performed as required until variceal eradication was achieved or until the diameter of varices was reduced to less than 5 mm and they disappeared completely within four weeks. Carvedilol (6.25–12.5 mg daily), a type of NSBB drug, was administered after the first EVL treatment to reduce portal pressure, with the dosage adjusted according to heart rate and blood pressure variations.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the variceal rebleeding rate after 2 years, secondary outcomes included changes in platelet and white blood cell (WBC) counts, and complications. Complications included postoperative abdominal pain, abdominal distension, vomiting, fever, hepatic encephalopathy, and portal vein thrombosis. The abdominal pain reported was generally mild, as indicated by the symptom intensity pain rating scale (VRS) [19].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.3 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Continuous data with normal distribution was described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and analyzed using Student’s t-test; otherwise, they were presented as medians (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical data were described as n (%) and analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The risk of variceal rebleeding rates between the two groups was conducted using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test. Changes in WBC and platelet were measured by repeated ANOVA. Two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 77 patients were enrolled, 5 patients rejected EVL, 3 rejected follow-up examination, 2 changed treatment and 1 died from other causes. Finally, there were 26 patients received PSE + EVL (mean age: 52.08 ± 11.64 years), and 40 patients received EVL alone (mean age: 54.12 ± 9.39) (Fig. 1; Table 1). There were no significant differences observed in terms of demographic characteristics (including gender, age, height, weight, heat rates, and cirrhosis etiology), image characteristics (portal vein and splenic vein width), endoscopic characteristics (grades of esophageal varices and gastric varices), liver function indexes (Child-Pugh stage, ALT, AST, TBIL, and DBIL), routine hematology (WBC and platelet), PT, time from bleeding to first EVL and number of EVL rings between patients received PSE + EVL or EVL alone (all P > 0.05) (Table 1). Two years after surgery, patients received PSE & EVL were more likely to have lower rate (23.3% vs. 53.5%, P = 0.020; Table 1) and risk of variceal rebleeding [HR (hazard ratio) = 0.36, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.17–0.77, P = 0.020; Fig. 2] compared to those received EVL alone. There were no significant differences in the preoperative platelet counts between patients received PSE + EVL or EVL alone (all P > 0.05). The platelet levels in patients received PSE + EVL after surgery (70.11 ± 10.83 vs. 55.47 ± 18.63, P = 0.005), postoperative 1 week (68.63 ± 13.69 vs. 57.21 ± 17.99, P = 0.034), postoperative 6 months (66.31 ± 15.63 vs. 55.58 ± 17.05, P = 0.045), postoperative 1 year (63.95 ± 14.51 vs. 50.26 ± 12.37, P = 0.003) and postoperative 2 years (60.58 ± 13.55 vs. 48.84 ± 11.41, P = 0.007) were significantly higher than those received EVL alone (Table S1, Fig. 3). There were no significant differences in the preoperative and postoperative WBC counts between patients received PSE + EVL and EVL alone (P > 0.05). The WBC levels in patients received PSE + EVL at postoperative 1 week (5.57 ± 2.45 vs. 4.25 ± 1.07, P = 0.039), postoperative 6 months (5.51 ± 1.48 vs. 4.60 ± 1.11, P = 0.040), postoperative 1 year (5.25 ± 1.37 vs. 4.26 ± 0.81, P = 0.011), and postoperative 2 years (5.12 ± 0.91 vs. 4.18 ± 0.92, P = 0.002) were significantly higher than those received EVL alone (Table S1, Fig. 3). There was no significant difference in the occurrence of postoperative complications, such as abdominal pain, abdominal distension, nausea, vomiting, postoperative fever, and palpitations between patients received PSE + EVL or EVL alone (all P > 0.05). Notably, neither group experienced severe complications, such as hepatic encephalopathy, sepsis or deaths in the postoperative period or during the follow-up period (Table 2).

Discussion

The results indicated that combining PSE with EVL might effectively reduce variceal rebleeding risk, while also enhancing postoperative platelet and WBC levels without increasing complications, offering a promising therapeutic avenue in variceal management.

EVB is a common and serious complication of decompensated cirrhosis that requires immediate attention. About half of newly diagnosed patients with decompensated cirrhosis have varying degrees of GOV, with a 5–15% annual risk of initial bleeding [20]. The risk of rebleeding within 1–2 years is 60%, and patients without prophylaxis have a mortality rate as high as 33% [21]. Bleeding can also lead to serious complications such as ascites and hepatic encephalopathy, worsening the overall condition. Managing EVB is essential to reduce the likelihood of future bleeding and improve patient survival. Various treatment options are available for EVB, including medication, endoscopic procedures, interventional techniques, surgery, or a combination of these approaches. However, there is inconsistency in the findings of multiple studies regarding the effectiveness and safety of these treatments [2, 22]. Combining two treatments may result in better outcomes in preventing rebleeding and enhancing long-term survival compared to using EVL or NSBB alone [23]. As a result, both domestic and international guidelines recommend a combination of EVL and NSBB as the standard regimen for secondary prophylaxis of EVB. It is important to note that potential drug side effects may restrict treatment options [24, 25].

Cirrhosis can lead to changes in the hemodynamics of the portal system, resulting in a three to four-fold increase in blood flow in the splenic artery and the splenic vein. This often causes an enlargement of the spleen and hypersplenism. Studies have shown that the size of the spleen is correlated with portal venous pressure, and splenomegaly is commonly associated with hypersplenism, which can worsen the progression of cirrhosis [26–28]. PSE can directly or indirectly reduce the functional volume of the spleen by decreasing splenic blood flow and lowering portal venous pressure, which helps prevent bleeding in varices. Current studies consistently show that embolizing 60–70% of the spleen significantly improves hypersplenism, while still maintaining effective immune function with the remaining 30–40% of splenic tissue [29]. Han et al. demonstrated that PSE can reduce hepatic venous wedge pressure by 30–50%, indirectly confirming its ability to lower portal venous pressure [30]. PSE has been proven to reduce the risk of variceal rupture bleeding and recurrence in cirrhotic patients, either as a standalone treatment or in combination with drugs and endoscopic procedures [31]. A recent study [32] investigated the combination of PSE and endoscopic treatment for secondary prophylaxis in cirrhotic patients with hypersplenism and found significantly lower rates of rebleeding and variceal recurrence compared to those receiving only endoscopic treatment, which aligns with the findings of this study. In this study, the postoperative rebleeding rate in patients received PSE combined with EVL was significantly lower than that of patients received EVL alone. Additionally, other studies have reported successful emergency endoscopic treatment accompanied by ultrasound-guided transgastric puncture embolization of the splenic artery in acute EVB cases, showing effective control of bleeding and decreased risk of rebleeding [33, 34]. However, we noticed that the two-year rebleeding rate, particularly in the EVL group (53.30%), was relatively high in our study, which may be associated with significant splenomegaly and thrombocytopenia often indicate a high risk of bleeding. Additionally, a large proportion of patients had severe varices, and our EVL target was variceal diameter < 5 mm or eradication, which may have also contributed to this outcome.

There was a reduction in the blood supply and effective volume of the spleen, as well as a decrease in splenic venous flow and pressure after PSE. This led to a decrease in portal venous and gastric coronary venous pressure, which contributed to the development of esophagogastric varices. Some studies also demonstrated that PSE can reduce hepatic arterial pressure and improve liver function, which may be associated with a decreased risk of EVB in patients with liver cirrhosis [35]. Overall, improved portal hemodynamics usually result in reduced bleeding from esophageal or gastric varices. Cirrhosis patients with hypersplenism often have lower platelet counts, which may furtherly increase the risk of rebleeding. However, after PSE, the reduced splenic phagocytosis of leukocytes and platelet helps elevate peripheral blood counts, reducing the risk of EVB. This study revealed that WBC counts increased rapidly after the operation in all patients, but one week later, patients received EVL alone returned to preoperative levels. In contrast, patients received PSE combined with EVL showed a decrease in leukocyte levels but remained higher than before the operation for at least 2 years. This may be due to the release of leukocytes from the spleen due to the acute inflammatory response. Further, this study suggested that the long-term improvement of WBC counts in patients received PSE and EVL may be attributed to improved bone marrow hematopoiesis after PSE [36]. The long-term increased platelet counts in patients received PSE and EVL may be associated with enhanced hepatic thrombopoietin production, as well as a decrease in the splenic platelet pool and an increase in platelet survival time. Additionally, the decrease in splenic phagocytosis contributes to the increased platelet survival time [37]. Therefore, PSE combined with EVL may be more advantageous for secondary prophylaxis of EVB in cirrhosis patients with hypersplenism.

However, this study had several limitations. Firstly, it is a single-center study with a small sample size, which limits the generalizability of the study. Secondly, the study lacks randomization, which may introduce selection bias and confounding factors that could affect the study outcomes. Thirdly, the study has a limited follow-up time of only two years. Fourthly, the study lacked a control group, thus limiting its ability to directly compare the interventions to standard care and assess their relative effectiveness. Fifth, the study did not adequately monitor the changes and differences in postoperative liver function indices between the two groups. Finally, the study did not analyze potential confounding factors such as comorbidities, medication use, or other interventions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study suggested that PSE combined with EVL could effectively reduce variceal rebleeding risk and increase postoperative platelet and WBC levels without raising complications. Larger studies with longer follow-up are recommended to validate these findings.
