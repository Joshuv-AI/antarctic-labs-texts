Background

Pulmonary chondroma is a rare benign tumor consisting in cartilage with various degrees of calcification [1–3]. Radiological presentation includes solitary or multiple pulmonary nodules [3, 4].

Chondroma rarely occurs in lung parenchyma. It can be encountered within a medical triad called Carney’s triad, found in 22% of the patients, also including gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST) and extra-adrenal paraganglioma [5].

We present herein a case of solitary pulmonary chondroma in a middle-aged Caucasian woman with atypical radiological features.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old Caucasian woman was evaluated at the University hospital of Reims for chronic dyspnea and incidental finding of a pulmonary nodule in the left lower lung on chest x-ray. Her medical history included peripheral arterial occlusive disease with stent implantation in the left common iliac artery, and medical treatment with clopidogrel and history of intestinal polyp removal. She worked as a farmer and stopped smoking 9 years ago (40 pack-years). Her clinical exam was unremarkable.

A thoraco-abdominal computed tomography (CT))-scan was performed, showing a 2 cm non-calcified pulmonary nodule on the left lower lobe, with a mean density of 12 Hounsfield units (HU) varying between − 40 and 20 HU, centrilobular emphysema (Fig. 1), and a 4-cm hepatic cyst. These radiological features contrast with those typically reported in pulmonary chondroma, where the lesions are typically larger (mean size ~ 3.9 cm), exhibit well-defined margins, and most notably show various patterns of calcification (strip-like, punctate, or ring-like) in approximately 87.5% of cases. A follow-up CT-scan performed 6 weeks after a course of antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanic acid), showed no change in size or appearance of the pulmonary nodule. Blood tests did not reveal any evidence of systemic inflammation.

A fiberoptic bronchoscopy was performed and revealed no abnormalities. Microbiological and cytological analyses were unremarkable. Pulmonary function tests demonstrated moderate chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) related to tobacco exposure, with a forced expiratory volume in the first second of expiration (FEV₁) of 1.24 L (55% of predicted), a FEV₁/FVC ratio of 0.51 with FVC corresponding to the Forced Vital capacity, a total lung capacity (TLC) of 7.41 L (155% of predicted), and a diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) at 44% of predicted.

The patient underwent an [18F] fluoro-2-deoxy-D-glucose (18F-FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) scan, revealing a low uptake of the left lower lobe nodule (Fig. 1; SUV max: 1.1). Brain MRI did not find any abnormality. Given the tobacco-smoking history and persistence of the pulmonary nodule, a surgical resection was proposed. An atypical resection of the left lower lobe nodule was performed three months after the initial specialist consultation. Histological examination revealed a benign pulmonary chondroma, measuring 1.5 × 1.5 × 2 cm composed of mature hyaline cartilage with chondrocytes, lacking cytological atypia, and surrounded by normal lung parenchyma (Fig. 2).

Abdominal Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) did not reveal extra adrenal paraganglioma or GIST but confirmed the presence of the previously identified benign hepatic cyst. During the two-year follow-up, which included clinical evaluations and thoraco-abdominal CT scans, no recurrence or evidence of GIST was observed (Table 1).

Discussion and conclusions

Pulmonary chondroma is a rare tumor accounting for approximately 0.1% of all benign lung tumors [6]. Although it is mostly observed in young women during their second decade of life, it has also been described in older women, men, and children [2, 7].

Typically, pulmonary chondroma appears on chest CT scan as a solitary, peripheral, lobulated, round or oval mass with well-circumscribed margins, and varying degrees of calcification (ranging from absent to punctate, ring, or strip-like patterns), with a density usually below 15 HU, most often located in the right or left lung with no side predominance. In some cases, particularly with larger lesions, chest wall adhesion may be observed [3]. Punctate calcification can be associated with malignancy. Nevertheless, in a context of a benign tumor, it is highly suggestive of a pulmonary chondroma [3]. These lesions are generally not associated with satellite nodules or mediastinal lymph nodes [3]. Previous case series indicate no predilection for either lobe [1, 3, 8]. A Chinese study [3], including 8 patients, reported a mean size of 3,9 cm (range from 0,3 cm to 10,7 cm). The [18F] -FDG PET typically reveals a low metabolic activity in pulmonary chondroma which was also observed in this case. Surgical resection was proposed in order to rule out the hypothesis of a lung cancer, particularly in the context of tobacco exposure and underlying COPD.

In contrast, our case exhibited a smaller (2 cm), non-calcified nodule with atypical low attenuation values ranging from − 40 to 20 HU, an uncommon radiological profile for this tumor. This absence of calcification and heterogeneous low density may reduce diagnostic confidence based solely on imaging, necessitating histological confirmation. Furthermore, the lack of typical calcification patterns, such as strip-like or ring-like forms, differentiates this case from the majority of reported pulmonary chondromas. The clinical context—a middle-aged patient with a history of smoking and COPD—adds to the atypicality, as pulmonary chondromas are more frequently described in younger female patients. These radiological and clinical features highlight the importance of including pulmonary chondroma in differential diagnosis, even when classical imaging signs are absent.

Regarding treatment, management in all reported cases has relied on surgical resection. Given the benign nature of the lesion, most cases have been diagnosed postoperatively. A preoperative diagnosis could lead to therapeutic abstention, except in rare symptomatic cases. Although, the prognosis of pulmonary chondroma is generally considered favourable, rare cases of malignant transformation into a giant chondrosarcoma have been reported [3, 9].

Pathological analysis of pulmonary chondroma typically reveals a tumor composed of a myxoid stroma, bone cells, stellate mesenchymal cells, peripheral calcifications, and a fibrous pseudo capsule that separates the tumour from the surrounding lung parenchyma [1, 10]. In our case, the tumor consisted of mature cartilage, surrounded by a fibrous pseudo capsule, which separated it from normal lung tissue. In 1977, Carney’s triad was described as the association of pulmonary chondroma, extra-adrenal paraganglioma, and GIST. The median time between the occurrence of these tumors is estimated to be approximately 8,4 years [3, 11], justifying a close long-term clinical and radiological follow-up, as GIST may carry a poor prognosis. Given the potential heterochronous development of these tumors, lifelong surveillance is recommended for patients with pulmonary chondroma, although clear guidelines are lacking due to the rarity of the condition. Abdominal MRI and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy may be useful to exclude associated lesions. Considering the low frequency of these events, non-irradiating imaging modalities—particularly MRI—should be preferred for follow-up whenever possible.

In summary, we report the case of a pulmonary chondroma, a rare benign cartilaginous tumor, in a middle-aged woman, characterized by the absence of calcification on chest-CT scan, no sign of Carney’s Triad and no evidence of recurrence after a 2-year follow-up.
