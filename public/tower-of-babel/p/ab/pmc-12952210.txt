Association of cardiac sarcoidosis with coronary slow flow: a case report unraveling the enigma
Authors: Hossameldin Hussein, Ashwin Radhakrishnan, Uzma Gul, Tarvinder Dhanjal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe exact mechanism of slow coronary flow (SCF) is still not well understood. SCF can manifest as acute coronary syndrome, or in rare scenarios, sudden cardiac death (SCD). Regional microvascular dysfunction (MVD) has been reported in inflammatory cardiomyopathies with a correlation between coronary flow reserve and active myocardial inflammation.Case summaryA 52-year-old gentleman presented with refractory ventricular tachycardia (VT) following an aborted out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Apart from one prior episode of uninvestigated syncope, there was no history of prior cardiac symptoms or comorbidities. Initial blood tests were unremarkable with a normal troponin level. Coronary angiography identified no obstructive epicardial coronary artery disease but demonstrated SCF that was marked in the right coronary artery (RCA). Subsequent cardiac magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated transmural late gadolinium enhancement in the RCA territory, which correlated to VT origin on the surface electrocardiogram. With no reversible cause of VT identified, an implantable cardioverter defibrillator was implanted for secondary prevention. Following discharge, an out-patient 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography scan revealed increased metabolic activity suggestive of active inflammatory myocardial involvement, most likely due to cardiac sarcoidosis (CS).DiscussionThis case highlights the importance of SCF as a surrogate marker for underlying MVD in relation to active myocardial inflammation. Studies are required to understand the role of MVD as a causal link between SCF and CS. Whether MVD should be considered in risk stratification for primary prevention of SCD in CS patients is to be explored.

Introduction

Slow coronary flow (SCF) is a commonly reported angiographic finding, with a prevalence ranging from 1% to 7% in subjects referred for coronary angiography (CAG).1 It has a wide scope of presentations, comprising effort angina, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), ventricular arrhythmia (VA), and sudden cardiac death (SCD). The mechanism of VA in SCF is usually attributed to acute ischaemic repolarization changes and abnormal QT interval, resulting in ventricular tachycardia (VT).2

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is a rare condition that may present with VA and SCD. Diagnosis can be challenging due to diverse clinical presentation, non-specific findings on multi-modality imaging, and the low diagnostic yield of endomyocardial biopsy (EMB). Recent non-invasive studies have shown that CS is associated with impaired coronary microvascular function,3 which is correlated to disease progression.

Herein, we report a case of aborted cardiac arrest with refractory VT, associated with right coronary artery (RCA) slow flow and regional late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) mimicking ischaemic cardiomyopathy, with a subsequent diagnosis of active CS.

Case presentation

A 52-year-old gentleman was transferred to our institution following a cardiac arrest. The patient collapsed whilst in the gym with VT for which he received immediate defibrillation and was successfully resuscitated. On transit to the hospital, he developed recurrent haemodynamically unstable VT, requiring repeated direct current cardioversion (DCCV).

In the emergency department, he was conscious and well-oriented. His blood pressure was 80/50 mmHg, heart rate 228 beats per minute, respiratory rate 22 cycles, and oxygen saturation 97%. Cardiac and chest auscultation were normal. The initial 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) showed wide complex tachycardia consistent with monomorphic VT, with right bundle branch block (RBBB) morphology and a superior axis, rS wave in lead I, suggestive of a basal endocardial inferolateral or lateral left ventricle (LV) exit site (Figure 1).

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram on presentation, showing monomorphic ventricular tachycardia with evidence of atrio-ventricular dissociation, superior axis, and right bundle branch block morphology.

He received a total of four synchronized DCCV, with resistant VT despite receiving 300 mg of intravenous (IV) amiodarone and 1 g of IV magnesium sulphate. Subsequently, a bolus dose of IV lidocaine (1 mg/Kg) was given with successful cardioversion to sinus rhythm. ECG post-cardioversion showed sinus rhythm, a corrected QT interval of 380 msec, with no signs of channelopathy (see Supplementary material online, Figure S1).

He denied any history of chest pain; however, one episode of syncope occurred 4 months prior, for which he did not seek medical advice. Risk factors for coronary artery disease (CAD) were limited to a family history of premature CAD. There was no history of inherited cardiomyopathy or premature SCD in the family.

Initial laboratory workup revealed normal full blood count, renal function, and C-reactive protein (CRP). The electrolyte panel showed a serum potassium level of 3.8 mmol/L, calcium 2.34 mmol/L, and magnesium 1.23 mmol/L. Cardiac high-sensitive troponin was normal (66 ng/L), with a mild elevation on the serial test after repeated DCCV (236 ng/L). HbA1C was 38 mmol/mol, with lipid profile in the high range (HDL-C 1.3 mmol/L, LDL-C 3.5 mmol/L, TC 5.1 mmol/L, and TG 0.6 mmol/L).

Due to the family history of premature CAD and refractory VT, he was transferred for urgent CAG. His angiogram showed no evidence of atherosclerotic CAD, with the left main trunk trifurcating into left anterior descending (LAD), left circumflex, and ramus arteries. The RCA was dominant, with evidence of significantly slow flow, in the absence of ectasia or aneurysms (Figure 2, Supplementary material online, Videos S1–S4). The corrected thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) frame count was 54 in the RCA and 27 in the LAD.

Coronary angiography showing normal right coronary artery in the left anterior oblique (A) and posterior-anterior (B) cranial views. The left coronary arteries were normal with no evidence of atherosclerotic plaques or thrombi in the posterior-anterior caudal (C), left anterior oblique caudal (D), right anterior oblique cranial (E), and posterior-anterior cranial (F) views.

Bedside echocardiography revealed preserved LV ejection fraction (LVEF), with an akinetic thinned basal inferolateral segment. Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) showed an LV of normal size, mass, and function (LVEF 57%), right ventricle (RV) of normal size and function (RVEF 53%). The basal-to-mid inferolateral segments were thinned, akinetic, with full-thickness enhancement in LGE images and elevated myocardial T2 consistent with oedema. (Figures 3, 4, Supplementary material online, Video S5). There were no lung abnormalities or lymphadenopathy on low-resolution planning images.

Cardiac magnetic resonance, three-chamber view with late gadolinium enhancement image (A), T2 map (B), and T1 map (C), showing a transmural late gadolinium enhancement in the basal/mid inferolateral wall with evidence of mild oedema.

Cardiac magnetic resonance, short-axis slice late gadolinium enhancement images showing transmural late gadolinium enhancement in the basal/mid inferolateral wall.

With no reversible cause of VT identified, a dual-chamber implantable cardioverter defibrillator was inserted in-hospital for secondary prevention. He was discharged on aspirin, atorvastatin, bisoprolol, amiodarone, and ramipril. Due to a high index of suspicion, an outpatient 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG-PET/CT) scan was scheduled after discharge, revealing moderate uptake (SUV max 4.4) within the mid, basal inferolateral and lateral left ventricular walls consistent with a diagnosis of active cardiac sarcoidosis (Figure 5).

18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography, axial section, showing moderate uptake (SUV 4.4) in the basal-mid inferolateral left ventricular wall (yellow arrow).

Discussion

The exact mechanism of SCF remains unclear, with different proposed theories suggesting multi-factorial pathogenesis.4 Endothelial dysfunction is believed to play a role in SCF through an imbalance between nitric oxide and endothelin-1, leading to elevated resting microvascular resistance (MVR).5 Additionally, mechanical microvascular dysfunction (MVD) was suggested by Mangieri et al.6 who identified thickened arteriolar walls and luminal narrowing in EMB from SCF patients. An inflammatory hypothesis is supported by elevated high-sensitivity CRP, interleukins, and other inflammatory markers that promote vascular remodelling and increase the MVR.7 SCF has also been seen in vessels with an abnormal anatomical course, such as an extremely acute angle of origin from the aorta or excessive arterial tortuosity.

ACS is reported as the most frequent presentation for SCF, with a rate of 42.5% compared to 22.5% for stable angina.8 It may result in repolarization changes and subsequent increased QT dispersion,9 which increases the risk of polymorphic VT and SCD.10 In our case scenario, VT was monomorphic, QT interval was normal, troponin was borderline elevated, and the presence of focal transmural LGE was unusual with SCF. Although Candemir et al.11 reported an association between SCF and LGE in a CMR study, this finding was not reproduced in other studies.

Isolated CS may manifest with palpitation, syncope, or overt heart failure. On the contrary, it may be clinically silent with no obvious symptoms. ECG findings are usually non-specific, including a wide or fragmented QRS complex, bundle branch block, or different stages of atrioventricular block, whereas echocardiography is not routinely recommended for CS screening in systemic sarcoidosis due to low sensitivity. In our patient, the resting ECG was unremarkable. However, echocardiography was consistent with regional wall motion abnormalities suggestive of myocardial ischaemia.

The only definitive way to diagnose CS is through histopathological recognition of a non-caseating granuloma. Given the patchy infiltration pattern, EMB has a low sensitivity, even when guided by voltage-mapping and CMR or 18F-FDG-PET/CT scan.12

CMR remains paramount to CS diagnosis. Although certain LGE patterns are reported as pathology-frequent locations, the tissue characterization patterns can be non-specific and mimic other myocardial disease processes. The typical involvement includes basal septal, subepicardial, multifocal, or RV free wall involvement.12 On the other hand, 18F-FDG-PET/CT has the advantage of evaluating the whole body for extra-cardiac sarcoidosis. The quantification of increased metabolic activity is useful for predicting prognosis and evaluating the response to treatment. A rubidium/ammonia perfusion followed by an FDG scan allows detection of myocardial inflammation with or without scar (perfusion defect with FDG intense uptake).

Whilst a tandem analysis of CMR and PET images has been proposed to categorize the likelihood of a CS diagnosis, this imaging-focused approach has not proven sufficiently specific for confirming a definite CS, compared to a histological diagnosis.13 Recently, a hybrid PET/CMR fusion imaging has emerged as a more robust indicator of disease severity and a better tool for risk stratification in suspected CS,14 serving as a more effective predictor of clinical outcomes via combining a patchy 18F-FDG uptake pattern and quantitative LGE analysis.

In the absence of systemic disease, the criteria for CS diagnosis are stringent, and since infarct remains a common occurrence, this was our initial diagnosis. There was, however, a disparity between the transmural LGE, with oedema, and the peak troponin pattern. Despite the absence of epicardial obstructive CAD, coronary flow was extremely slow in the RCA, matching the regional wall motion abnormalities in echocardiography. Moreover, the CMR findings were not typical for CS, with LGE being transmural in the basal inferolateral wall. In the presence of a positive family history of premature CAD, our initial diagnosis implied a missed inferolateral ST-elevation myocardial infarction with late spontaneous recanalization, resulting in scar-related monomorphic VT.

Recent evidence is mounting for MVD in patients with CS. Whilst acute inflammation is proposed to induce endothelial dysfunction and subsequent reduction of nitric oxide, it may also enhance coronary microvascular spasm.15 Kruse et al.3 were able to establish a relation between sarcoidosis-mediated inflammation and a worse regional hyperaemic myocardial flow reserve, along with a higher coronary vascular resistance. A non-invasive echo-Doppler study found that patients with sarcoidosis had lower coronary flow velocity reserve in the LAD, compared to healthy subjects.16 Interestingly, MVD was found to be localized to the area of active inflammation in early stages of the disease (without perfusion defects). However, in advanced stages, MVD was more global, affecting remote non-inflamed areas. To date, there is no study confirming MVD in CS using invasive functional assessment. Notably, immune suppression treatment in CS has been shown to reduce inflammation, alongside improvement in MVD parameters by Cardiac PET studies.17

Studies on the long-term prognosis of SCF reported worse outcomes. Our case report highlights that poor prognosis may be related to undiagnosed cardiac conditions, including inflammatory cardiomyopathies.

Conclusion

SCF is not a benign condition as previously thought, with most cases presenting with ACS, carrying a risk of VA and SCD. This case highlights the recognition of SCF as a surrogate marker for MVD, which may denote an underlying myocardial inflammation and/or fibrosis. Further research is still required to identify the mechanism(s) of MVD in cases of active inflammatory sarcoidosis.
