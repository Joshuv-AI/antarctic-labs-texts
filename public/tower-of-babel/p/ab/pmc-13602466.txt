Scoping review on orofacial pain management in older adults with dementia
Authors: Liang Kong, FengLi Zhu, LingHui Zhang, Qian Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/ObjectivesOrofacial pain in older adults with dementia is often overlooked due to cognitive decline and impaired communication, impairing patients’ quality of life and exacerbating neuropsychiatric behavioural symptoms. This scoping review aimed to map available evidence regarding prevalence, assessment tools, clinical management strategies and research gaps, with the aim of informing clinical practice and guiding future research.MethodsSystematic literature searches were performed in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and Cochrane Library from database inception to December 2025. Two independent reviewers performed literature screening and data extraction, and any disagreements were resolved via consensus discussion. Narrative synthesis was adopted to summarise findings, and the review strictly followed PRISMA-ScR guidelines. This review was retrospectively registered on the Open Science Framework on 5 March 2026 (OSF: https://osf.io/qmg64). The literature search was completed prior to registration.ResultsTwenty one studies were included. Orofacial pain prevalence varied: 7.4%–21.7% in community-dwelling older adults; 11.9% (rest) and 21.9% (mastication) in hospitalised patients; up to 48.8% in nursing home residents with severe dementia. A meta-analysis (n = 6115) reported a pooled prevalence of 19.0%. Nearly half (50.3%) of patients without self-reported pain had underlying oral pathological lesions. Based on limited validation studies, the OPS-NVI demonstrates relatively stronger psychometric properties compared to other available tools, though evidence remains preliminary and head-to-head comparisons are lacking, while the OHAT-NL may be suitable for community screening by caregivers. Paracetamol is frequently recommended as a first-line option for mild to moderate pain based on general geriatric pain guidelines, but direct evidence for orofacial pain in dementia is limited. Multidimensional non-pharmacological strategies, including staged oral care, caregiver training and multidisciplinary collaboration, are generally suggested. Major systemic barriers include limited dental service access, insufficient caregiver training, and information fragmentation.ConclusionsOrofacial pain represents a substantial underrecognised burden among older adults with dementia. Immediate priorities include integrating oral health assessment into routine care, promoting scenario-specific use of validated tools, and strengthening caregiver education. Future research should prioritise subtype-specific investigations and translate existing evidence into implementable clinical protocols.

Introduction

Dementia comprises a heterogeneous group of neurodegenerative disorders characterised by progressive cognitive decline and behavioural disturbances, with Alzheimer’s disease accounting for 60% – 80% of all cases [1]. Global population ageing has led to a continuous rise in dementia prevalence, rendering oral health problems and orofacial pain key unmet clinical needs in this vulnerable older population.

Orofacial pain is frequently underestimated in dementia patients. Cognitive and verbal impairment prevents patients from adequately expressing physical discomfort through speech, leading to behavioural changes such as food refusal, irritability and aggression. Such behavioural manifestations may often be misattributed to dementia-related neuropsychiatric symptoms rather than unrelieved orofacial pain. This may be partly explained by evidence that pain itself can directly impair attentional and executive cognitive functions [2]. Conditions such as burning mouth syndrome have been associated with increased anxiety and depression risk in general populations [3], though evidence specific to dementia is lacking, further complicating clinical differentiation.

Reported orofacial pain prevalence ranges from 7.4% to 48.8% across different populations [4, 5]. Persistent unrelieved orofacial pain may increase the risk of malnutrition, dysphagia and impaired quality of life. Poor oral conditions including caries, residual roots and periodontal disease are frequently reported as common causes of orofacial pain [6, 7]. Decline in self-care ability and inadequate caregiver oral health knowledge further increase pain risk [8]. Notably, oral health status may have a bidirectional correlation with dementia progression [9].

Existing evidence on orofacial pain in dementia remains fragmented, lacking systematic categorization of assessment tool application scenarios and differences across dementia subtypes. This scoping review aimed to synthesise available literature to clarify pain prevalence, validated assessment tools, whole-course management strategies and research gaps, with the goal of offering practical references for geriatric oral healthcare.

Methods

Literature search

Systematic searches were conducted across PubMed, Web of Science, Embase and the Cochrane Library for literature published from database inception to December 2025. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and free-text keywords focusing on dementia, orofacial pain and pain management. Manual reference screening of included articles was also performed. Literature searches were restricted to English-language human studies only; non-English publications were excluded, which may have introduced subtle geographical and language bias.

PubMed search strategy: ((“dementia“[Mesh] OR “Alzheimer disease“[Mesh])) AND ((“pain“[Mesh] OR “pain management“[Mesh] OR “analgesia“[Mesh] OR “pain measurement“[Mesh])) AND ((“dental“[Title/Abstract] OR “oral“[Title/Abstract] OR “tooth“[Title/Abstract])).

Embase search strategy: (‘dementia’/exp OR ‘Alzheimer disease’/exp OR ‘cognitive impairment’/exp OR dementia: ti, ab OR alzheimer: ti, ab) AND (‘dental care’/exp OR ‘oral health’/exp OR ‘tooth’/exp OR dental: ti, ab OR oral: ti, ab OR tooth: ti, ab) AND (‘tooth pain’/exp OR ‘facial pain’/exp OR ‘orofacial pain’/exp OR ‘dental pain’:ti, ab OR toothache: ti, ab).

For the Web of Science and Cochrane Library, the search strategies were adapted from the PubMed and Embase strategies by converting subject headings and syntax according to each database’s specifications. The core concept blocks and Boolean combinations remained consistent across all databases. The database searches were conducted in February 2026 (prior to protocol registration). Grey literature (e.g., dissertations, conference proceedings, government reports) was not searched. The initial manuscript draft was prepared based on the results of this search. After registration, the manuscript was submitted. No additional studies were identified between the search and submission.

Literature screening and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened titles, abstracts and full texts according to unified inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible study designs included cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, qualitative research, systematic reviews and narrative reviews. Case reports, letters, conference abstracts and publications with unavailable full texts were excluded.

In total, 319 records were retrieved from four electronic databases. After removing 25 duplicate records, 294 unique records remained for initial screening. A total of 250 irrelevant records were excluded based on titles and abstracts, leaving 44 articles eligible for full-text assessment. Of these 44 articles, 4 could not be retrieved, primarily due to institutional paywall restrictions and no response from corresponding authors. The remaining 40 full texts were assessed for eligibility, among which 19 were excluded for the following reasons: study populations inconsistent with the review’s inclusion criteria (n = 10), irrelevant pain locations not limited to orofacial regions (n = 6), and duplicate data overlapped with previously published systematic reviews (n = 3). Ultimately, 21 studies were included in this scoping review.

A pilot-tested standardised form was used for independent data extraction by two reviewers. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or third-party consultation, and full consensus was achieved for all extracted items without calculating formal inter-rater reliability statistics.

Data synthesis and analysis

The methodological framework for this scoping review was guided by the foundational work of Arksey and O’Malley, as further advanced by Levac et al. and the updated JBI guidance for scoping reviews [10–12]. This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) recommendations, with a focus on mapping the overall research landscape, rather than conducting quantitative meta-analysis or formal risk-of-bias assessment [13]. Descriptive quality assessment was conducted based on study design, sample representativeness and adoption of formal dementia diagnostic criteria. The methodological quality of observational studies was assessed using Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) criteria, though no numerical scoring or study exclusion was performed. Narrative synthesis was adopted due to substantial methodological heterogeneity across included studies. This review was retrospectively registered on the Open Science Framework on 5 March 2026 (OSF: https://osf.io/qmg64). The literature search was completed prior to registration. A published protocol proposing a multidisciplinary pain assessment framework for dementia informed the methodological approach of the present review [14]. Results

Basic characteristics of included studies

A total of 21 eligible studies published from 2002 to 2025 were included, comprising 7 cross-sectional studies, 2 cohort studies, 6 systematic reviews/umbrella reviews, 3 narrative reviews, 1 qualitative study, and 2 research protocols. Studies covered European, American and Asian regions, including community, hospital and nursing home patients spanning mild cognitive impairment to severe dementia. Twelve studies (57.1%) adopted formal diagnostic frameworks such as the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) or the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) criteria [15, 16].

Quality overview showed most cross-sectional studies had limitations in sample representativeness. Three of the six systematic reviews demonstrated high methodological quality [5, 9]. Core conclusions on pain prevalence and tool validity were consistently supported by the available high-quality evidence, while most remaining studies were of moderate to low quality, requiring cautious interpretation. Orofacial pain burden and oral health status

Orofacial pain prevalence differed significantly by care setting: 7.4% – 21.7% in community-dwelling older adults, 11.9% at rest and 21.9% during mastication in hospitalised patients, and up to 48.8% in nursing home residents with severe dementia [15]. A meta-analysis of 6115 participants reported a pooled prevalence of 19.0% [5].

Nearly half (50.3%) of patients with no subjective pain complaints presented with underlying oral pathological changes including coronal caries, root caries, residual roots and oral ulcers, suggesting possible substantial under-detection of silent orofacial pain [16].

Dementia patients showed poor overall oral health: plaque index up to 90.1%, gingival bleeding index ranging from 43.8% to 53.8%, 73.8% with periodontal pockets ≥ 4 mm, and xerostomia incidence of 22.0% [6, 17]. Compared with non-demented older adults, dementia patients had fewer remaining teeth, more residual roots, higher Decayed, Missing, and Filled Teeth (DMFT) indices and poorer denture hygiene [15].

Clinical practice recommendations emphasize that observational pain assessment is the standard of care for individuals with dementia who cannot verbally communicate their discomfort [18].

Pain recognition by caregivers

Informal caregivers mainly identify potential pain through daily behavioural observations such as food refusal, altered facial expression and sleep disturbance [19]. However, busy nursing home environments hinder standardised documentation of subtle behavioural cues. Caregivers emphasised that dental pain assessment should be undertaken by professional staff rather than untrained family members, and pain monitoring should be integrated into routine long-term care plans [8].

Pain management strategies

Pharmacological management

Unless otherwise specified, the following pharmacological recommendations are extrapolated from general geriatric pain management guidelines, as direct evidence for orofacial pain in dementia is lacking.

Paracetamol is frequently recommended as a first-line option for mild to moderate pain in general geriatric pain guidelines (≤ 3 g/day to avoid hepatic injury), but specific evidence for orofacial pain in dementia is limited. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are advised to be used cautiously with short-term and low-dose regimens with gastroprotective agents. Opioids are generally recommended with low initial dose and slow titration. Clinicians should remain vigilant for potential drug–drug interactions between tramadol and selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), as well as NSAIDs combined with angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or diuretics [20]. Liquid formulations may be preferable for patients with dysphagia.

Analgesic utilisation was 82.9% in nursing home residents and 61.4% in hospitalised patients. Sedatives and opioids used in end-of-life care may aggravate xerostomia, requiring enhanced supportive oral care [21].

Non-pharmacological management

Non-pharmacological interventions include staged oral care, caregiver education, environmental adjustment and multidisciplinary collaboration. The whole-course management framework has been proposed to include prevention in early dementia, adaptive care in the middle stage and palliative care for patients with advanced dementia [22, 23]. Quiet familiar environments and music intervention may help relieve patient irritability. For terminally ill patients, oral care has been suggested every 2–3 h to alleviate dry mouth and discomfort [21]. Mechanism of the association between oral health and dementia

Periodontal pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis may cross the blood–brain barrier, activate microglia, promote amyloid-β deposition and tau phosphorylation, and play a role in Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis [9]. Periodontal disease and tooth loss are correlated with cognitive decline, and active periodontal intervention has been associated with reduced dementia risk in some studies [6]. Such evidence is mainly derived from high-level umbrella reviews, systematic syntheses, and recent narrative reviews [24]. Beyond these direct pathological pathways, the experience of persistent orofacial pain may independently contribute to cognitive decline. The proposed mechanisms include competing neural resources and neuroplastic changes in brain regions that are shared by pain processing and higher-order cognition [2].

Systemic barriers and recommendations

Longstanding systemic barriers include insufficient dental insurance coverage, limited access to professional dental services and inadequate caregiver training [25]. Caregivers also reported difficulties including unfamiliarity with assessment tools, fluctuating patient cooperation and fragmented medical information sharing [8]. Research output on dental pain assessment tools remains scarce [19]. Best practice examples from Scotland offer potential models, including free dental examinations for older adults, dementia-oriented dental training and designated oral health coordinators in nursing homes.

Discussion

Key findings

This scoping review synthesised 21 relevant studies and identified a heavy, widely under-recognised orofacial pain burden across community, hospital and nursing home settings. Among the limited validation studies available, the OPS-NVI has shown relatively stronger psychometric properties compared to other tools, though evidence remains preliminary and head-to-head comparisons are lacking, while dedicated simplified tools for dental clinics are still lacking. Current pharmacological and non-pharmacological management frameworks have been formed, yet clinical implementation is restricted by systemic service barriers and insufficient caregiver capacity.

Novelty compared with existing reviews

Previous systematic and narrative reviews mostly focused on overall oral health status or general pain prevalence. Unlike previous meta-analyses that focused only on pooled prevalence or solely on pathological mechanisms [5, 9], the present review offers a potential contribution by systematically classifying assessment tools according to clinical scenarios, highlighting research gaps in dementia subtype heterogeneity, and linking empirical evidence to clinical and policy guidance.

Critical interpretation of prevalence heterogeneity

The wide prevalence range reflects inherent differences in study population, disease severity and assessment methods. Higher detection rates in nursing homes are closely related to advanced dementia, loss of verbal expression, and poor baseline oral health. In contrast, community samples mainly include mild cognitive impairment and early dementia patients with retained communication ability. Such heterogeneity suggests that pooled prevalence should be interpreted cautiously, and setting-targeted screening may be more clinically practical.

Assessment tool application and limitations

In the limited available studies, the OPS-NVI has demonstrated relatively reliable sensitivity and specificity, suggesting it may be suitable for systematic assessment of nonverbal patients in hospitals and nursing homes; however, validation evidence remains preliminary and head-to-tool comparisons are lacking [26], though its relative operational complexity limits wide community application. The OHAT-NL appears simple for lay caregiver screening with good overall consistency and specificity [27], but has demonstrated limited performance in evaluating gingival and salivary items. Emerging facial recognition technologies remain in the research stage with limited clinical practicability [28]. Consensus recommendations emphasize that observational pain assessment is essential for nonverbal older adults, and that assessment should be embedded within a multidisciplinary framework [29]. Future research may prioritise the development of clinic-oriented simplified scales and the optimisation of community screening tool performance.

Critical appraisal of tool performance inconsistencies

While the OHAT-NL demonstrated high overall specificity (100%) in one validation study [27], its performance on individual items—particularly gingival and salivary assessment—was limited. This discrepancy suggests that while the tool is useful for basic community screening, it may miss subtle but clinically significant localised pathology. Such inconsistencies across validation studies suggest a need for head-to-head comparisons of available tools within the same population, a comparison that is currently lacking in the literature.

Dementia subtype heterogeneity and clinical implications

Existing literature is heavily concentrated on Alzheimer’s disease, with limited evidence on vascular dementia, frontotemporal dementia and dementia with Lewy bodies. Distinct neuropathological and behavioural features lead to differences in pain perception and care cooperation. A unified management model may be insufficient to meet individualised needs, and subtype-specific research warrants further investigation. This represents a critical evidence gap, as patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD) often display oral stereotyped behaviours and self-harm tendencies, while those with dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB) are prone to orofacial dyskinesia from neuroleptic sensitivity.

These distinct phenotypes underscore that a one-size-fits-all pain management protocol is likely inadequate, and future clinical protocols may need to account for subtype-specific behavioural and motor profiles. Consequently, pain assessment protocols for FTD may need to prioritize screening for self-inflicted oral trauma, while those for DLB should focus on distinguishing pain-induced agitation from medication-related dyskinesia.

For patients with frontotemporal dementia (FTD), who often display oral stereotypies and self-injurious behaviors, pain assessment should include routine oral mucosal inspection for trauma, and behavioral cues such as repetitive lip/cheek biting should be specifically queried. For dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), orofacial dyskinesia may mimic pain-related grimacing; video-recording with subsequent behavior analysis or a trial of neuroleptic dose reduction (if clinically appropriate) may help differentiate pain from medication-induced movements. Future research should validate subtype-specific pain assessment protocols.

Management implementation and systemic barriers

Based on expert opinion and indirect evidence, pharmacological schemes may need individualised adjustment according to patients’ cognitive level, underlying diseases and polypharmacy risks. Multi-dimensional non-pharmacological strategies may require multidisciplinary implementation. As a scoping review, this study offers preliminary policy considerations rather than mandatory regulations: Mandatory inclusion of a validated observational pain tool (e.g., OPS-NVI) in nursing home admission assessments for residents with moderate-to-severe dementia. A brief (2-hour) online training module for informal caregivers on recognizing orofacial pain cues (food refusal, altered facial expression, sleep disturbance). Establishing a standardized dental-medical communication template to share oral health findings between primary care providers and dental services.

Study limitations

Most included studies employed a cross-sectional design, which cannot confirm causal relationships between oral health and dementia. There is substantial methodological and population heterogeneity across the literature. Only 57.1% of studies applied formal dementia diagnostic criteria, and subtype-related research is insufficient. The review only included English-language literature and did not search grey literature (e.g., dissertations, conference proceedings, government reports), which may have resulted in geographical, linguistic and publication bias. All clinical recommendations should be applied prudently in combination with local circumstances.

This review was retrospectively registered, which deviates from the ideal of prospective registration and may introduce reporting bias. Additionally, the systematic literature search was completed prior to protocol registration, and the initial manuscript was submitted before registration. This reversed workflow does not conform to standard scoping review methodology. However, the search was performed systematically, and all findings were reported transparently. Future reviews should ensure prospective registration and completion of the systematic search before manuscript submission.

Conclusion

This scoping review systematically mapped the burden of orofacial pain, clinical application of assessment tools, and current status of full-course management strategies in older adults with dementia, highlighting prominent underdiagnosis and multi-level clinical and systemic challenges. This review suggests that resolving orofacial pain in this population may require not only better assessment tools but also the structural integration of dental and medical care pathways. By categorizing scenario-based tool characteristics and integrated management frameworks, this review may serve as a practical reference for interdisciplinary geriatric oral healthcare.

Clinically, oral health assessment could be integrated into routine dementia care, and validated tools such as OPS-NVI may be standardised in institutional settings. Combined interventions could be implemented by multidisciplinary teams. From the policy perspective, optimising dental insurance, home dental services and nursing home oral health management models may help address systemic barriers. Immediate next steps include integrating validated observational pain tools into institutional protocols, providing caregiver training on pain cue recognition, and establishing dental-medical communication pathways.
