Boosting Selectivity toward Long-Chain α‑Olefins in CO2 Hydrogenation through Strengthening ZnO–Fe Interactions with Favorable Iron Phase Regulation for Fischer–Tropsch Synthesis
Authors: Zhaobo Fan, Zhangshi Li, Yufeng Li, Qiang Li, Siyang Yan, Guanze Nie, Dezheng Li, Changxu Wang, Huanhuan He, Jiaxu Liu, Bing Liu, Xiaohao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Developing excellent catalysts for CO2 hydrogenation to long-chain linear α-olefins (LAOs) offers a promising route toward value-added chemicals. However, it remains a considerable challenge to achieve high CO2 conversion along with superior selectivity to LAOs. In this study, we prepared a series of Zn-modified iron-based catalysts using urea precipitation for the CO2 hydrogenation reaction, and they exhibited a much higher selectivity to C4+ LAOs of 61.7%, with a CO2 conversion of 38.5%, over Fe2Zn1-U catalyst, compared to the selectivity of 50.5% over Fe2Zn1-A catalyst with same elemental composition prepared using ammonia precipitation. A lower selectivity to methane was obtained, from 13.7% to 8.8%. Moreover, this process resulted in a record-breaking space-time yield (STY) of 0.544 gC4+LAOs·gcat –1·h–1. HRTEM characterization over spent catalysts showed that Fe2Zn1-U exhibited smaller-size mixed iron-phase particles (Fe3O4, χ-Fe5C2, and θ-Fe3C) where the ZnO phase was more homogeneously dispersed and had stronger interactions with Fe species compared to the observations for Fe2Zn1-A. In situ XPS analysis suggested that this structure promoted Zn-to-Fe electron donation along with intimate interaction over Fe2Zn1-U. XANES spectra demonstrated a more difficult reduction of Fe species with weaker Fe–C coordination for carburization, thereby resulting in a higher Fe3O4 content in spent Fe2Zn1-U. In situ IR spectroscopy under reaction conditions confirmed that significantly more surface species of CO*, HCOO–, *HCO, *CH x , and *CCH were present over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst, derived from strengthened synergistic catalysis between ZnO, Fe3O4, and iron carbides. Also, Mössbauer spectroscopy analysis revealed that more θ-Fe3C phase was observed over spent Fe2Zn1-U, which was beneficial for carbon–carbon coupling, owing to its wavy protrusion structure, demonstrated by DFT calculations to be like *C5H10 and *C6H12 coupling with *CH2 species, showing a much lower energy barrier to form longer α-olefins. In addition, it was found that the introduction of Cu could enhance the CO2 conversion, selectivity to LAOs, and catalyst stability. As a result, the Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst gave stable production of LAOs with a high selectivity from CO2 hydrogenation over 400 h reaction time on stream.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of global industrialization has led to a sharp increase in the consumption of conventional carbon-based energy sources, resulting in excessive emissions of carbon dioxide (CO2), a predominant greenhouse gas. , The conversion of CO2 into value-added fuels and chemicals presents an attractive strategy not only to reduce reliance on traditional fossil fuels but also to mitigate the greenhouse effect. − As CO2 can be readily recovered from industrial off-gas streams, hydrogen (H2) can be produced via water electrolysis powered by renewable energy sources such as solar and wind, yielding carbon-neutral “green hydrogen”. , Olefins serve as crucial feedstock for a wide range of high-value commodities in the chemical industry which are vitally important. Short-chain olefins (C2 =–C4 =) are indispensable, acting as key building blocks for use on a massive scale, and can be produced via various routes such as petroleum cracking, dehydrogenation of lower alkanes, and the methanol to olefins (MTO) process; the Fischer–Tropsch synthesis (FTS) is also used but is less selective.

In contrast, it is relatively difficult to apply the above-mentioned routes for the synthesis of long-chain linear α-olefins (LAOs). Long-chain olefins are key raw materials for producing various high-value products, such as the copolymerization of monomers to upgrade the properties of polyolefins, the production of Guerbet alcohols for synthesis of surfactants, C8–C12 (especially C10) catalytic polymerization for the production of poly-α-olefins as lubricant base oils, and cosmetics. Currently, several petroleum-based routes are applied for long-chain olefins production, such as wax cracking, dehydrogenation of long-chain n-alkanes, and ethylene oligomerization. , However, these processes generally suffer from low conversion and high energy consumption. Alternatively, the FTS route offers a promising pathway to directly produce long-chain α-olefins through CO hydrogenation with high activity and selectivity. Liu et al. designed Na2S-modified Fe-based catalysts for the CO-FTS reaction. The addition of a small amount of Na2S can efficiently regulate the CO adsorption/dissociation behavior during the CO-FTS reaction and largely suppress coke deposition, thus contributing to ultrahigh CO conversion of ∼95%, high C4+ olefin selectivity up to ∼65%, and excellent stability. This reaction’s selectivity to C4+ LAOs is significantly higher than that obtained with the ultra-active and stable cylindrical iron carbide nanocapsules catalyst we developed. Ding et al. proposed a hydrophobic modification strategy for an Fe-based FTS reaction. The introduced hydrophobic shell can reduce the water concentration around the Fe catalyst core during FTS, thus suppressing the aqueous oxidation of iron carbide species and promoting C–C coupling for the formation of long-chain olefins. Recently, Wang et al. developed phase-pure χ-Fe5C2 for synthesis of LAOs, which achieved a higher carbon utilization efficiency by 1–2 orders than previous reports. The selectivity to C2–C10 LAOs was 51%, along with a low selectivity to CO2 of 9%. Furthermore, Wen et al. achieved near zero CO2 emission by adding trace-level halogen in iron-based FTS.

Compared with CO-FTS, the selective production of long-chain α-olefins via CO2-FTS is more challenging, particularly due to the lower surface concentration of CO* and CH x * intermediates, which results in a weaker chain-growth ability. Various research efforts have focused on the production of short-chain olefins in the CO2-FTS reaction through promoter effects. Liu et al. revealed the crucial roles of Na and Mn promoters in the hydrogenation of CO2 to light α-olefins. Sodium facilitates the reduction of Fe3O4 by lowering the oxygen vacancy formation energy via electron transfer, whereas manganese enhances reducibility by enabling spontaneous oxygen spillover from iron oxides, mediated by oxygen vacancies in Mn oxides. Meanwhile, the incorporation of Mn generates steric hindrance for carbon chain growth, which thus significantly increases the selectivity to light α-olefins. Subsequently, Ma et al. reported that a Na promoter enhanced Fe and Mn interactions and provided strong basic sites for CO2 hydrogenation selectively to light α-olefins in the range of C2 =–C6 = with a high space-time yield (STY). Also, Li et al. used a Mn- and Na-promoted Fe5C2 catalyst to hydrogenate CO2 to light α-olefins. It is concluded that the high surface density of sodium suppresses olefin chain growth via steric hindrance, significantly enhancing the selectivity to light α-olefins.

In the case of long-chain olefin production via CO2-FTS, it is required to stimulate surface C–C coupling processes. However, this is a big challenge for CO2-FTS owing to the limitation of the surface reaction environment and Anderson–Schulz–Flory (ASF) distribution. Ma et al. successfully utilized a co-feeding approach involving ethylene in the CO2 hydrogenation reaction for a higher C3+ olefins formation over a FeZnNa catalyst. In this case, it was also facile to hydrogenate the resulting olefins into the corresponding alkanes in spite of promoting the chain growth. , For direct synthesis of long-chain olefins, it was interestingly found that similar active components and promoters such as K, Mn, Zn, and Fe can result in higher selectivity toward long-chain carbon olefins, while most cases exhibited facile formation of light olefins. For instance, Tsubaki et al. constructed a K-FeMn catalyst via a pre-coordination combustion method. By leveraging the stabilized active phase at the MnCO3–Fe5C2 interface and synergistic catalysis between Fe3O4 and Fe5C2, they achieved 36.6% CO2 conversion with 38.1% selectivity to C4+ LAOs. Gao et al. synthesized a, FeZnK catalyst via a citrate-mediated combustion method for selective production of LAOs. The Fe1Zn0.1K0.1 catalyst, featuring a well-balanced Zn–O–Fe interface and active Fe5C2 species, achieved a CO2 conversion of up to 43.3% with a selectivity of 44.7% to C4+ α-olefins.

Looking back to the above-summarized synthesis of long-chain α-olefins from both CO-FTS and CO2-FTS, it seems that the same promoter often shows opposite effects on the selectivity to the product. We reported that Na2S played a role in enhancing the high selectivity to LAOs; however, de Jong et al. achieved a remarkably high selectivity to light olefins (C2 =–C4 =) by a synergistic effect of Na and a S promoter. Sun et al. demonstrated that a Fe3C@C catalyst with a nitrogen-rich surface is beneficial for strengthening Fe–N coordination and reducing electron loss to the carbon shell layer, which can promote CO adsorption and activation but not increase selectivity to C5+ products. However, some literature showed that a Fe3C phase is favorable for promoting carbon chain growth ability to produce heavier products. , These seemingly contradictory conclusions might originate from changing the microenvironment in the FTS reaction, such as supported or non-supported catalyst, different feed gas CO/H2 or CO2/H2, and the presence of a carbon-wrapped layer. These factors could possibly weaken and even reverse the effects of the promoter and the iron phase itself.

In this study, we tried to eliminate the effects of these variables to avoid misguided scientific judgments in exploring the origin of the resulting catalytic performance. In detail, we investigated a series of FeZnNa catalysts for the CO2 hydrogenation reaction to observe changes in selectivity to short-chain light olefins and LAOs. The synthesized catalysts were obtained by using co-precipitation methods with same elemental ratio of Fe/Zn. The only difference between them is that one co-precipitation process used ammonia as the precipitant and the other used urea. The Na promoter was then introduced by using a conventional impregnation method. Interestingly, by changing the precipitant from ammonia to urea, totally different catalytic performances were observed over the two obtained Fe catalysts in the CO2-FTS reaction, despite their identical composition. In detail, the catalyst from urea precipitation (denoted as Fe2Zn1-U) exhibited a high selectivity to C4+ LAOs of 61.7%, accompanied by a CO2 conversion of 38.5%. In comparison, the catalyst synthesized by ammonia precipitation (denoted as Fe2Zn1-A) showed only 50.5% selectivity to C4+ LAOs. Detailed characterizations such as TEM, in situ XPS, in situ FTIR of reaction conditions, XANES, etc. suggested that the enhanced long-chain growth originates from smaller-size mixed iron-phase particles, more dispersed ZnO species, and stronger ZnO–Fe interactions. The catalyst structure obtained by urea precipitation resulted in more facile electron transfer between Zn and Fe species and different iron-phase compositions in Fe3O4, Fe5C2, and θ-Fe3C. The content of θ-Fe3C was obviously higher over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst, which showed a higher chain growth ability for C–C coupling into long-chain olefin products, as confirmed by DFT calculations. This conclusion was also demonstrated by in situ FTIR experiments under the reaction conditions, where the surface intermediates CO*, HCOO–, *HCO, *CH x , and *CCH were obviously higher over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. Moreover, the catalyst was further modified with a Cu promoter, which resulted in improved CO2 conversion, selectivity to LAOs, and catalyst stability.

Results and Discussion

Catalytic Performance for CO2 Hydrogenation

The Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts were synthesized by precipitating specific amounts of Fe­(NO3)3·9H2O and Zn­(NO3)2·6H2O using the ammonia precipitation and urea precipitation methods, respectively. Details of preparation procedures are provided in the Experimental Section in the Supporting Information. Under the same reduction and evaluation conditions, CO2 hydrogenation performance tests were conducted over the two catalysts (Figure a-d). As shown in Figure a, the two catalysts demonstrate similar CO2 conversion. As for product selectivity, compared with Fe2Zn1-A, Fe2Zn1-U demonstrates much lower selectivity to short-chain products such as CH4 (Figure b), CO (Figure S1c), C2 =–C4 = (Figure c), and C2 0–C4 0 (Figure S1a). As shown in Tables S1 and S2, the selectivity to CH4 and C2 =–C4 = was reduced from 13.7% and 36.0% to 9.5% and 26.6%, respectively. Consequently, the selectivity to C5+ hydrocarbons was quite, high up to 60.8% (Figure d), and the selectivity to desired long-chain linear α-olefins (C4+ LAOs) was 50.6% and 61.1% over Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U, respectively. This finding suggests that the urea precipitation method is much more favorable for the formation of long chains (Figure i, j), which are rich in C4+ LAOs. In addition, compared to Fe2Zn1-A, the selectivity of CO for Fe2Zn1-U was slightly lower (14.3% vs 15.5%), indicating that Fe2Zn1-U is more conducive to the adsorption and activation of CO. This might be one of the reasons for the higher selectivity to long-chain olefins over Fe2Zn1-U.

In order to enhance the selectivity for long-chain olefins and the stability of the catalyst, two strategies were selected to further optimize the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. The first strategy was to regulate the Fe/Zn ratio. The second one was to impregnate 12 wt% copper into the catalyst with an optimized Fe/Zn ratio. The results from using the first strategy indicated that when the Fe/Zn ratio was increased from 2 to 3, the conversion rate, selectivities to CH4 and CO, and carbon number distribution remained virtually unchanged (Figure e, f, Figure S1f, and Figure j, k). However, when copper was impregnated into Fe3Zn1-U, the stability of the catalyst was obviously improved (Figure e). At the same time, the selectivity to light olefins (C2 =–C4 =) was further suppressed from 27.4% to 24.6%, along with almost unchanged selectivity to CH4 at about 10% (Figure f, g and Table S1). Correspondingly, the selectivity to the desired C4+ LAOs was 58.2% and 60.8% over Fe3Zn1-U and Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu, respectively (Table S2). The selectivity to CO was only slightly reduced from 12.8% to 12.2%.

Furthermore, the effects of gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) were further investigated over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst (Figures S2, S3 and Tables S3, S4). Under constant pressure and temperature, an increase in GHSV led to a decrease in CO2 conversion and an increase in selectivity to CO from 38.5% to 33.7%, and from 14.3% to 18.6%, respectively, when the GHSV increased from 1200 to 4800 mL·gcat –1·h–1. Interestingly, a quite high GHSV at 20 000 mL·gcat –1·h–1, along with increasing pressure from 1 to 2 MPa and temperature from 320 to 340 °C, resulted in a record-breaking STY of 0.544 gC4+LAOs·gcat –1·h–1. As expected, such a high GHSV for the CO2-FTS reaction did not result in a notable decrease in CO2 conversion, with a value of 35.9%, and it still gave a low selectivity to CH4 at 8.8% and a suppressed selectivity to light olefins (C2 =–C4 =) at about 24%. The results show that the iron-based Fe2Zn1-U catalyst gave a relatively high CO2 conversion rate and selectivity to long-chain C4+ LAOs, even though under extremely high GHSV conditions. Notably, the extremely high STY of 0.544 gC4+LAOs·gcat –1·h–1 demonstrates its strong potential for industrial applications. Among comparable catalysts, this value ranks as one of the highest reported in the recent literature (Table S12). Although the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst gave a relatively high STY for LAOs, there were still significant byproducts, including CO, CH4, and light olefins. Future research may consider the moderate addition of Mn or the implementation of hydrophobic treatment of the catalyst to achieve the dual objectives of reducing byproduct formation and enhancing the stability of the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. In this work, we further optimized the effects of reaction pressure in the CO2-FTS (Figures S4, S5 and Tables S5, S6). It was found that a higher reaction pressure of 3 MPa decreased the selectivity to C4+ LAOs from 61.7% to 54.5%, which originated from the promoted hydrogenation to the corresponding alkanes. Indeed, the selectivity to C4+ alkanes was enhanced from 11.7% to 16.4%. This indicates that a suitable reaction pressure enables efficient CO2 conversion to maximize the selectivity toward long-chain olefins. ,

A 400-h stability test was conducted over the Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst (Figure a). The results demonstrate that the catalyst exhibits excellent stability. During the continuous 400 h reaction, the CO2 conversion remained almost constant at 39.1%. Meanwhile, the selectivity toward CO, CH4, C2 =–C4 =, and SC5+ was stable at 10.6%, 10.4%, 26.8%, and 59.3%, respectively. The average conversion and product selectivity over Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst during the 0–50 h and 0–400 h reaction periods were compared (Figure b). It was found that both the CO2 conversion and product selectivity were almost the same. The robust stability of the Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst indicates promising potential for industrial applications. Furthermore, the average carbon number distribution for the 400-h CO2-FTS reaction revealed relatively high selectivity toward SC4+ olefins at 59.8% (Figure c).

Structure Evolution and Surface Chemical States Characterization

To elucidate the fundamental reasons for the impact of different precipitation methods on catalyst performance, we conducted systematic characterizations of the phase composition and microstructure of the catalysts. First, the phases of the fresh and reduced Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts were analyzed by XRD. Fresh Fe2Zn1-A primarily consists of ZnFe2O4 (PDF#70-6493) spinel (Figure a). The average particle size of ZnFe2O4 is 8.3 nm, determined using the Scherrer formula. This suggests that the fresh spinel phase exhibits excellent crystallinity and small particle size. By comparison to Fe2Zn1-A, the fresh Fe2Zn1-U catalyst demonstrates a mixed phase of Fe2O3 and ZnO (Figure b). The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms and pore size distribution plots for the Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts display hysteresis loops in both fresh catalyst samples (Figure S6), indicating that these catalysts possess mesoporous structures. The Fe2Zn1-A catalyst exhibits a typical H2-type hysteresis loop. In contrast, the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst reveals an H3-type hysteresis loop, indicating the potential presence of large sheet-like particle aggregates that form mesopores. Table S7 provides a summary of the specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size of the fresh catalysts. The two catalysts show similar surface areas and pore diameters. Quantitative analysis of the elemental composition of catalysts was done by using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) (Table S8). The contents of Fe, Zn, and Na did not exhibit obvious differences between Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts. After reduction, both Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts were primarily comprised of metallic Fe0 (PDF#87-0722) and ZnO (PDF#75-1526) (Figure a, b). In contrast, the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst may be more difficult to reduce to metallic Fe, as the diffraction peak of FeO (Fe2+) located at 41.9° is present. This suggests a stronger interaction between iron and ZnO species over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst.

Structural characterizations of various catalysts. XRD patterns of (a) Fe2Zn1-A and (b) Fe2Zn1-U catalysts. SEM images of (c) fresh Fe2Zn1-A and (d) fresh Fe2Zn1-U catalysts. TEM, particle size distribution, HRTEM, HAADF-STEM, and elemental mapping of (e-h) fresh Fe2Zn1-A, (i-l) reduced Fe2Zn1-A, (m-p) fresh Fe2Zn1-U, and (q-t) reduced Fe2Zn1-U catalysts.

From the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Figure c, d), it seems that fresh Fe2Zn1-A catalyst shows the morphology of a larger size in the bulk, whereas the morphology of fresh Fe2Zn1-U catalyst involves the agglomeration of small particles around 40–50 nm. Furthermore, the transmission electron microscope (TEM) images exhibit a much larger particle size of about 42.2 nm over fresh Fe2Zn1-U compared to that for the fresh Fe2Zn1-A catalyst at about 8.3 nm (Figure e, m). For fresh Fe2Zn1-A catalyst, the particle size is in accordance with XRD data. It is obvious from elemental mapping that all elements (Fe, Zn, O, and Na) are uniformly distributed over the two fresh Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts (Figure h, p). After reduction, their morphology and structure evolution are somewhat different. First, the reduction process results in serious sintering, with their particle size growing from 8.3 and 42.2 nm to 206.7 and 293.9 nm over Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U, respectively (Figure i, q). Second, the evolution of the elemental distribution is different. In detail, the Fe2Zn1-A catalyst leads to much more significant phase separation between Fe and Zn/Na. The Fe phase is located at the core layer, and the Zn and Na migrate and aggregate together to distribute around the Fe particles (Figure l). However, in the case of Fe2Zn1-U catalyst, most of the Fe, Zn, and Na are still gathered together, which indicates that a stronger interaction between Fe and Zn is formed via the urea co-precipitation method (Figure t). This result is consistent with the conclusion from XRD characterization. In general, Na is prone to get together with Zn. After the CO2-FTS reaction, the catalyst morphology, Fe phase state, and elemental distribution are further evolved. For spent Fe2Zn1-A catalyst, the particle size increases from 206.7 to 428.2 nm, and the iron-phase particles are surrounded by quite large ZnO particles (Figure S9e). However, over spent Fe2Zn1-U catalyst, the particle size increases only slightly from 293.9 to 350 nm. It should be especially pointed out that the ZnO phase is much more dispersed and leads to much smaller particles located on the large Fe-phase particle surface (Figure S10e). When copper was introduced into the Fe3Zn1-U catalyst by the impregnation method, fresh, reduced, and spent Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalysts showed corresponding particle sizes of 30.1, 167.7, and 284.6 nm. This suggests that spent catalyst with addition of the Cu component can largely decrease the particle size (Figures S11, S12, and S13), and its addition is also beneficial for preventing phase separation of Na and Zn from the Fe phase (Figure S13e, f, h, and i). As mentioned above, based on the observation from all samples, it seems that elemental Na is always closely combined with ZnO. This phenomenon has been explained by DFT calculations. The results indicate that the binding energy between Na2O and ZnO (−6.24 eV) is lower than that between Na2O and χ-Fe5C2 (−5.53 eV).

In situ XPS was employed to probe the interaction between zinc and iron phases and to elucidate the electronic transfer at the catalyst surface. The obtained spectra of the fresh, reduced, and spent catalysts all reveal the presence of Fe, Zn, O, and Na (Figure S14), consistent with the elemental compositions summarized in Table S8. Fe 2p XPS spectra of fresh Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U are presented in Figure a, d. The spectra can be deconvoluted into eight peaks, corresponding to Fe2+ and Fe3+ species and their satellite peaks. XPS spectra of fresh Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts are mainly composed of Fe3+ species, which indicates that most of the Fe in the two catalysts is in a completely oxidized state. This is consistent with XRD (Figure a, b) and TEM (Figure f, n) results. Zn LMM auger electron spectra (AES) of fresh Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U are shown in Figure g, j, where only Zn2+ species are observed at 989.0 and 988.8 eV, respectively. ,

In situ Fe 2p XPS spectra of Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts: (a, d) fresh, (b, e) reduced, and (c, f) spent after 4 h reaction. In situ Zn LMM auger XPS spectra of Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts: (g,j) fresh, (h,k) reduced, and (i, l) spent after 4 h reaction.

The reduced Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts were resolved into eight peaks (Figure b, e), assigned to Fe0, Fe2+, and Fe3+ species and their satellite peaks. The Fe 2p3/2 bonds of Fe0 species in the reduced Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts have binding energies of 706.8 and 706.7 eV, respectively. The ratio of metallic iron to iron oxides (Fe0/FeO x ) for reduced Fe2Zn1-A was 2.45, which is higher than the value of 1.11 for reduced Fe2Zn1-U, indicating a higher extent of reduction in Fe2Zn1-A. As for Zn (Figure h, k), the Zn species in the reduced Fe2Zn1-A catalyst consisted of Zn2+ and Zn0, whereas the reduced Fe2Zn1-U catalyst contains dominant Zn2+ species. This indicates a stronger interaction between Zn and Fe species in the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. Furthermore, considering that the decline in binding energy of Fe0 in Fe2Zn1-U, this phenomenon should be related to the electron transfer from Zn species to metallic Fe. This transfer reasonably results in the decline in Zn0 along with the increase in Zn2+.

After 4 h of the FTS reaction, FeC x species can be found over the spent Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts (Figure c, f). ,, Similar to the reduced catalysts, the binding energy of FeC x species in spent Fe2Zn1-U is slightly lower than that of spent Fe2Zn1-A. Meanwhile, the ratio of FeC x to FeO x for the spent Fe2Zn1-A catalyst (0.20) was higher than that of spent Fe2Zn1-U (0.11). As for Zn in spent catalysts (Figure i, l), the results align with those of the reduction catalyst, further indicating a stronger interaction between Zn and Fe species over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. In summary, these results effectively demonstrate the electron donation from surface Zn species to surface metallic Fe and FeC x species over the reduced and spent Fe2Zn1-U catalyst, which is derived from their strong interaction. Such a strong interaction could also slightly suppress the carburization of the iron phase during the CO2-FTS reaction.

Characterization of Redox Behavior, Surface Adsorption, and Structural Properties

H2 temperature-programmed reduction (TPR) profiles of various catalysts are shown in Figure a. A weak peak at 300 °C can be found over both the Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts, corresponding to the reduction of surface oxygen in ZnO. For the Fe2Zn1-A catalyst, the reduction peak at 370 °C is attributed to the reduction of ZnFe2O4 to Fe3O4 and ZnO. The peak at 555 °C is associated with the reduction of Fe3O4 to FeO. Furthermore, the broad high-temperature peak at 702 °C is related to the slow reduction of FeO to Fe0. In the case of Fe2Zn1-U, the peak at 415 °C is ascribed to the reduction of Fe2O3 to Fe3O4. This peak temperature is higher than the reduction temperature of ZnFe2O4, suggesting a more facile reduction of ZnFe2O4 than Fe2O3. Meanwhile, the reduction peaks for Fe3O4 to FeO and FeO to Fe0 are found at 550 and 752 °C, respectively. The reduction behavior of the Fe3Zn1-U catalyst is quite similar to that of Fe2Zn1-U. As for Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu, the lower reduction temperature at 365 °C is attributed to the overlapping reduction peaks of CuO to Cu0 and Fe2O3 to Fe3O4. In addition, the reduction peaks for both Fe3O4 and FeO shift slightly to lower temperature, owing to the hydrogen spillover effect of metallic Cu.

CO temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) profiles of Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U are shown in Figure b. Fe2Zn1-U exhibits a higher CO desorption peak area compared to that of Fe2Zn1-A, suggesting that it is more favorable for CO adsorption. CO2-TPD profiles are presented at Figure c. Obviously, the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst exhibits a larger number of moderately basic sites (200–400 °C) and a moderate number of strongly basic sites (>400 °C), which facilitate adsorption and activation of CO2 over the catalyst surface and subsequent carbon chain growth. , CO-TPD was also carried out over Fe3Zn1-U and Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalysts (Figure S7a). Clearly, the addition of Cu also significantly increases the adsorption strength and amount of CO, which are favorable for carbon chain growth. Moreover, H2-TPD tests were conducted on Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U (Figure S7b). Fe2Zn1-U exhibits weaker H2 adsorption ability compared to Fe2Zn1-A, which can prevent excessive hydrogenation of surface CH x * species and thus promote their C–C coupling. ,

After 50 h of evaluation, the spent Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts primarily consist of Fe3O4, Fe5C2, and ZnO (Figure d). Compared with spent Fe2Zn1-U, spent Fe2Zn1-A catalyst exhibits a significantly higher diffraction peak for ZnO, suggesting the presence of larger-size ZnO particles. This result is consistent with the TEM test results (Figure S9). In the thermogravimetric (TG) profiles of Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U (Figure S8a), the mass percentage (TG%) increased with temperature due to the gradual replacement of carbon atoms in the iron carbides by oxygen atoms, which have a higher atomic mass. The sharp mass gain observed at approximately 380 °C is attributed to the abrupt decomposition of the dominant Fe5C2 phase in the catalyst at its critical temperature. The mass stabilized after all carbon atoms in the catalyst were completely replaced by oxygen. The total mass change percentage for Fe2Zn1-A was 4% greater than that for Fe2Zn1-U, indicating a higher Fe x C content in the Fe2Zn1-A catalyst. Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to examine the spent Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts (Figure e, f). The parameters corresponding to the different iron phases are listed in Table S9. Accordingly, the iron phase composition of the spent Fe2Zn1-A catalyst is 5.2% θ-Fe3C, 25.9% Fe3O4, and 67.1% χ-Fe5C2, while Fe2Zn1-U consists of 11.3% θ-Fe3C, 35.9% Fe3O4, and 52.8% χ-Fe5C2. Clearly, spent Fe2Zn1-U catalyst exhibits a 2-fold higher content of the θ-Fe3C phase compared to Fe2Zn1-A. This might indicate that the θ-Fe3C phase is favorable for the formation of long-chain olefins. Even so, we thought that the presence of the θ-Fe3C phase is not a must for stimulating carbon–carbon coupling in the CO2-FTS reaction. Indeed, the carbon–carbon coupling regulation is quite complicated, which depends on many factors rather than the iron carbide phase alone. This view reflects why we would like to design two catalysts with same elemental ratio and unsupported catalysts to eliminate the effects of support. Only in this way is a more reliable for discussion of the effects of Zn–Fe interaction and iron carbide phase on the product selectivity possible.

According to previous reports, the θ-Fe3C phase is an important active phase for the formation of long-chain olefins in the FTS reaction. , In general, the θ-Fe3C phase could be stable only under high-temperature and low-carbon-potential conditions. However, in this work, Fe2Zn1-U exhibits a stronger ability to adsorb and activate CO, indicating a higher surface carbon potential. Therefore, the higher θ-Fe3C content in the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst indicates that the θ-Fe3C phase is more stable in Fe2Zn1-U. The stable effect of this phase may be related to the stronger interaction between iron and zinc particles over the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. In addition, the Fe3O4 phase is well-accepted as the active phase for the reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) reaction. It higher content in Fe2Zn1-U is favorable for the generation of *CO from CO2 hydrogenation, which is crucial for subsequent C–C coupling reaction over iron carbide phases such as χ-Fe5C2 and θ-Fe3C.

As for the Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst, it exhibits excellent catalytic stability, making it essential to investigate the influence of Cu promotion on the phase composition of the FeZnNa catalyst. For the spent Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst (Figure S8b), the notable diffraction peaks for the Cu promoter appeared and shifted to larger diffraction angles. This indicates that Cu is stable in the metallic Cu state in this system. The Cu0 nanoparticles are in close contact with the surrounding Fe5C2 and ZnO grains. Some Fe and Zn atoms migrate to the interstitial spaces of the Cu lattice, causing the lattice constant to decrease and resulting in the XRD peaks shifting to higher angles. Mössbauer spectroscopy was employed to evaluate the iron-phase composition of the Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst (Figure S8c, Table S9). The results indicate that the spent catalyst consists of 21.5% Fe3O4, 5.8% θ-Fe3C, and 72.6% χ-Fe5C2. This demonstrates that the addition of Cu does not increase the content of θ-Fe3C. Compared with the Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts, the spent Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst exhibits a lower Fe3O4 content and a higher proportion of the χ-Fe5C2 phase. This indicates that the introduction of Cu promotes the carburization of iron and slightly inhibits its oxidation. This finding is consistent with the H2-TPR results (Figure a), where a lower reduction temperature over the Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst was observed, suggesting that Cu facilitates the reduction of iron species and their subsequent carburization. Combined with the CO-TPD result in Figure S7a, the promotional effects of Cu on chain growth in the CO2-FTS reaction should contribute significantly to the enhanced adsorption and activation of CO, leading to the increase of surface *CH x concentration.

XANES and EXAFS Analysis

X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analysis were conducted for the iron and zinc components in spent Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts after 4 h of CO2-FTS reaction. Figure a shows the Fourier-transform EXAFS (FT-EXAFS) spectra of Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U at the Fe K-edge. From the absorption edge at about 7120 eV, the profile of spent Fe2Zn1-U is close to that of Fe3O4, indicating a higher proportion of the Fe3O4 phase. This is consistent with the Mössbauer spectroscopy result (Table S9). The coordination environment of Fe was further investigated by using EXAFS analysis (Figure b). Notably, the spent Fe2Zn1-U catalyst exhibits more pronounced Fe–O (1.5 Å) and Fe–O–Fe (3.0 Å) coordination peaks compared to the Fe2Zn1-A catalyst, further confirming a higher Fe3O4 content. Additionally, a distinct peak at approximately 1.6 Å can be assigned to Fe–C coordination. , It can be found that Fe2Zn1-A exhibits a stronger Fe–C scattering feature compared to Fe2Zn1-U, suggesting a higher proportion of Fe5C2 species. This is also associated with Mössbauer spectroscopy and in situ XPS results (Table S9, Figure c, f). Furthermore, the EXAFS fitting parameters at the Fe K-edge for spent Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts are summarized in Table S10. The Fe–C coordination number (CN) over spent Fe2Zn1-A is 6.14 ± 0.54, which is higher than that of spent Fe2Zn1-U (3.15 ± 0.46). This indicates a higher degree of carburization of the iron phase over spent Fe2Zn1-A.

Reaction Mechanism of Selective CO2 Hydrogenation to Long-Chain α-Olefins

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) measurements were conducted over Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts under the reaction conditions to investigate the formation and dynamic evolution of surface reaction intermediates (Figure a, b). The peaks with the highest intensity appear in the 2200–2400 cm–1 region, corresponding to gaseous CO2 with a high partial pressure under the reaction conditions. , The bands at 1395 and 1455 cm–1 are attributed to the O–C–O stretching vibrations of monodentate carbonate species (m-CO3 2–), while those at 1295, 1311 1624, and 1653 cm–1 can be assigned to bidentate carbonate species (b-CO3 2–). These carbonates originate from the adsorption and activation of CO2 on the catalyst surface. Clearly, Fe2Zn1-U exhibits stronger intensities for carbonates, suggesting a higher surface carbonate concentration under reaction conditions. This should be related to its stronger interaction with CO2 as discussed in relation to the CO2-TPD (Figure c). In addition, the bands at 1436 and 1473 cm–1 are ascribed to the symmetric and asymmetric vibrations of O–C–O in bicarbonate species (HCO3 –), while those at 1362, 1374, 1536, 1541, and 1560 cm–1 are assigned to the formate species (HCOO–), and that at 1256 cm–1 belongs to the vibration of the C–O group of the above surface species. ,, These surface species are generated from the hydrogenation of carbonates (CO3 2– → HCO3 – → HCOO–). The peaks observed at 1665, 1695, 1727, 1746, and 1761 cm–1 indicate the presence of intermediates containing CO groups, such as formaldehyde (H2CO*) or formic acid (HCOOH*) derived from the hydrogenation of HCOO– species. Owing to the higher surface carbonate concentration, Fe2Zn1-U also displays higher intensities for these surface species.

High-pressure in situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (in situ DRIFTS) of (a) Fe2Zn1-A and (b) Fe2Zn1-U catalysts under H2/CO2 = 3, 1.0 MPa, and 320 °C.

CO is the key intermediate product in the CO2-FTS reaction. The gaseous CO peaks are found around 2090 and 2112 cm–1. Meanwhile, the bands between 1897 and 1962 cm–1 and at 3031 cm–1 correspond to bridge-adsorbed CO* and CO* adsorbed at multiple surface sites, respectively. ,, It can be found that the peak intensities for adsorbed CO* are stronger over Fe2Zn1-U, which is also consistent with the CO-TPD result (Figure b). This further confirms the stronger interaction between CO and the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst. Derived from the hydrogenation of CO*, various hydrocarbon species are detected in the regions of 2700–3000 cm–1 and 1600–1720 cm–1. Specifically, the peaks at 3087, 2962, 2875, 2813, and 2709 cm–1 are assigned to stretching vibrations of hydrocarbon intermediates (CH x ). Additionally, the peaks observed at 1643 and 1706 cm–1 are attributed to the stretching vibration of the double bond (*CCH) in olefins and the H–C–H scissoring vibration, respectively. , Apparently, the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst also exhibits a higher concentration for these surface hydrocarbon species.

To sum up, the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst demonstrated superior adsorption and activation ability for both CO2 and CO compared to the Fe2Zn1-A catalyst, which is related to the presence of more electron-rich active iron phases derived from the electron donation from the intimate ZnO species. This largely increases the concentration of surface intermediates in the CO2-FTS reaction, such as CO*, HCOO–, *HCO, *CH x , and *CCH, and thus efficiently promotes carbon chain growth via the enhanced surface C–C coupling reaction.

Apart from the impact on the concentration of surface reaction intermediates, the different extents of Fe–Zn interaction also have a significant impact on the composition of iron phases, where the Fe2Zn1-U catalyst with a stronger carbon-chain-growth ability shows a higher Fe3C concentration at about 11.3%. Meanwhile, the content of Fe3O4 phase increases from 25.9% to 35.9%, which is beneficial for enhancing the RWGS reaction to give desired the CO, and the content of Fe5C2 decreases from 67.1% to 52.8%, which is well accepted as the key active phase for FTS. Fe3O4 is the key initial phase after the reduction of the precursor, which is mainly responsible for catalyzing the RWGS reaction to activate CO2 to CO. In a suitable reduction–carbonization atmosphere, Fe species (such as Fe3O4 or intermediate metallic iron) can be gradually converted through carburization to Fe5C2, which is widely regarded as the main highly active phase for catalyzing CO hydrogenation and C–C chain growth to generate hydrocarbon products. The Fe3C often appears as an intermediate in this process, and its intrinsic FTS activity remains controversial. Guo et al. believe that Fe5C2 is the main active phase for chain growth and hydrocarbon formation and that the activity of the Fe3C phase is much lower than that of the Fe5C2 phase. However, Sun et al. designed Fe@NC-S catalysts that showed strong CO adsorption/dissociation capacity by catalyst modulation, resulting in higher electron density in the Fe3C core. Furthermore, Li et al. demonstrated that θ-Fe3C had FTS activity, exhibiting superior carbon chain growth, i.e., higher C5+ selectivity. In summary, the physical phase composition and catalytic mechanism of iron-based catalysts constitute a complicated dynamic system. Therefore, precise regulation of the kinetic equilibrium of key iron phases (e.g., Fe3O4/Fe5C2/Fe3C) in the CO2-to-olefin reaction is at the core of realizing the directional distribution of the products, which is also an important and challenging research direction.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were further employed to compare and confirm the chain growth capabilities of Fe5C2 and Fe3C in the FTS reaction (Figures , S15–S25). From the results of calculations on Fe5C2 and Fe3C, it can be seen that the reaction energy barriers for *C n H2n coupling with *C2H4 (Figure a) are much higher than those for *C n H2n coupling to *CH2 (Figure b). This is because the adsorption energy of *C2H4 on the surfaces of Fe5C2 and Fe3C is relatively high, which makes it unfavorable for it to be desorbed at the active sites and then undergo insertion reactions with *C n H2n (Figure S22). The energy barrier for the coupling of *C n H2n with *C2H4 on the Fe3C phase is higher than that on the Fe5C2 phase. This is attributed to a higher adsorption energy of *C2H4 on the Fe3C phase than that on the Fe5C2 phase (−1.51 eV vs −1.22 eV). Considering the high adsorption energy of *C2H4 on Fe5C2 and Fe3C, it is more likely that *CH2 will be inserted stepwise into the carbon chains during the FTS chain growth process. Furthermore, several C–C coupling steps between *CH2 and *C n H2n (n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6) are considered (Figure a. Compared to Fe5C2, Fe3C exhibits a slightly lower energy barrier for *C2H4 formation, higher energy barriers for *C3H6, *C4H8, and *C5H10 formation, but notably lower barriers for both *C6H12 and *C7H14 formation. The high barrier for the coupling between *CH2 and lower olefin species (*C2H4, *C3H6, and *C4H8) over the Fe3C phase should be related to its stronger interaction with lower olefin species (Figures S22–S24). The increase of carbon number over olefin species results in weaker adsorption with the Fe3C surface. On the other hand, it also leads to an increase in the steric hindrance. Compared with the plane-shaped surface of the Fe5C2 phase (Figure S25a), the surface of the Fe3C phase (Figure S25b) has a wavy protrusion structure. The protrusions, with their unsaturated coordination and step sites, are more conducive to stabilizing *CH2 and the transition state of C5+ long-chain olefin species in the carbon–carbon coupling reaction with high reactivity, thereby obtaining longer chain olefins. Thus, in the case of Fe5C2- and Fe3C-phase mixtures over practical Fe-based FTS catalysts, an increase in the proportion of Fe3C is reasonably conducive to a higher selectivity to long-chain olefins. Furthermore, Fe3C also exhibits a slightly higher energy barrier for the hydrogenation of *CO into *CHO but a much lower barrier for its dissociation into *CH and *O (Figures S19–S21). It is favorable for the generation of *CH2 and its subsequent insertion into carbon chains. Moreover, Fe3C exhibits stronger bonding to these olefin intermediate species, which is beneficial for the subsequent chain growth reaction (Figures S22–S24).

Conclusions

CO2-FTS is an excellent route for producing α-olefins. However, the selective production of high-value long-chain α-olefins (LAOs) is still quite challenging owing to the limitations of carbon chain growth. In this study, the well-designed Fe2Zn1-A and Fe2Zn1-U catalysts were prepared through ammonia precipitation and urea precipitation methods, respectively. Despite having the same elemental composition, the two catalysts display significantly different catalytic performances for α-olefins production from the iron-based CO2-FTS. Fe2Zn1-U exhibited a much higher selectivity toward LAOs than that of Fe2Zn1-A. Intensive characterizations including in situ XPS, Mössbauer spectroscopy, H2/CO2/CO-TPD, TEM, in situ DRIFTS of the reaction conditions, and theoretical calculations demonstrate that the enhanced chain growth ability over Fe2Zn1-U originates from the stronger Fe–Zn interaction along with significantly improved ZnO dispersion, which enable more facile electron donation from ZnO to Fe, leading to electron-rich active Fe sites that further promote the adsorption and activation of CO2 and CO, increase the concentrations of surface *CH x and *CCH, and thus facilitate their coupling into long-chain α-olefins.

More importantly, the Fe–Zn interaction also shows a significant impact on the iron phase evolution and composition. It increases the content of the Fe3O4 phase to 35.9% for the RWGS reaction over Fe2Zn1-U, thereby favoring CO generation. Simultaneously, the content of the Fe3C phase with stronger chain growth ability than Fe5C2 is also enhanced about twice higher than that over the Fe2Zn1-A catalyst. The improved chain growth ability over the Fe3C phase is well revealed by DFT calculations. Owing to its unsaturated coordination and step sites in the wavy protrusion structure, this phase is more conducive to stabilizing *CH2 and the transition state of C5+ long-chain olefin species, with a suitable interaction with active Fe sites for the carbon–carbon coupling reaction. In contrast, the plane-shaped surface of the Fe5C2 phase is more beneficial for the production of light olefins. Such desired regulation of the iron phase composition (Fe3O4, Fe3C, and Fe5C2) contributes to the efficient and selective hydrogenation of CO2 into long-chain α-olefins. Moreover, through the modulation of reaction conditions, a record-breaking space-time yield (STY) value for C4+ LAOs was obtained at 0.544 gC4+LAOs·gcat –1·h–1 along with high selectivity to C4+ LAOs (61.7%) and low selectivity to CO and CH4 at 18.1% and 8.8%, respectively. In addition, the designed Fe3Zn1-U-12Cu catalyst resulted in excellent stability for the production of LAOs over 400 h of reaction time on stream. The proposed strategy of strengthening Fe–Zn interactions for the regulation of favorable electron transfer and Fe-phase evolution holds great potential for industrial applications in high-value LAOs production from the iron-based CO2-FTS reaction.
