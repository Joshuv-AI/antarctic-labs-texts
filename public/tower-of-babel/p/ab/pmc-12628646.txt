The Indirect Effect of a Brief Couple Intervention on Child Mental Health Challenges via the Interparental Relationship
Authors: Maya Koven, Jazzmin Demy, Jasmine Zhang, Mark Wade, Heather Prime
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChild mental health challenges have long‐term implications for social and emotional functioning. The quality of the interparental relationship is an important contributor to children's mental health challenges. Evidence supports the use of brief couple interventions to enhance couple functioning, though secondary benefits to child outcomes are unknown. The current study examines whether changes to the interparental relationship following participation in a brief couple intervention, in turn, lead to changes in child mental health challenges.MethodsParticipants come from a secondary dataset from a randomized controlled trial of Love Together, Parent Together, a brief couple intervention, and included 267 parents (140 couples) with at least one child under 6 years old. Parents reported on 10 indicators of the interparental relationship and their child's mental health challenges at baseline, 1 week post‐intervention and 1‐ and 3‐month follow‐ups.ResultsBased on an exploratory factor analysis including all 10 indicators of the interparental relationship, a two‐factor model emerged, which included interparental conflict and general relationship quality. Structural equation modelling was used to test indirect effects with interparental conflict and relationship quality, respectively, as mediators, and child mental health challenges at 1‐ and 3‐month follow‐ups, respectively, as outcomes. The intervention did not significantly predict couples' T2 conflict, nor did T2 conflict predict follow‐up child mental health challenges. The intervention significantly improved the couples' T2 relationship quality, though there were no reliable effects found on child mental health challenges.ConclusionsIn sum, though findings are consistent with the idea that conflict and relationship quality are unique factors of the interparental relationship, there is no evidence for benefits of a brief couple intervention to child mental health challenges. Future studies should carefully consider measurement selection and assessment schedules to detect developmental cascades following couple interventions.Keywords: brief couple intervention, child mental health challenges, conflict, interparental relationship, relationship quality

Introduction

Child mental health challenges, which develop within the family context (Newland 2014), lay the foundation for future educational attainment (Schlack et al. 2021), social relationships (Lay‐Yee et al. 2021) and cognitive functioning (Caspi et al. 2020). Promoting healthy patterns of interaction within the family is important to build early foundational skills in children. This study explores the potential secondary benefits of a couple‐focused intervention to children's mental health challenges. The intervention is very brief and self‐directed, to address barriers to meeting family mental health needs (Carbonell et al. 2020).

The current study draws on systems theory, which emphasizes interdependencies in the well‐being of family members and relationships. Specifically, child mental health challenges emerge and/or are maintained, in part, from complex and dynamic interactions between family members and the overall health of the family system (Carr 2016; Minuchin 1985). Dimensions of the interparental subsystem—including relationship quality, conflictual and hostile behaviour, disengagement, constructive behaviour and child‐related conflict—play a central role in the well‐being of children and families (McHale et al. 2019; van Eldik et al. 2020; Yap and Jorm 2015). For example, harsh, violent and unresolved conflict between parents is associated with children's mental health challenges (Cummings and Davies 2002; Grych and Fincham 1990; Harold and Sellers 2018), while constructive, resolution‐focused conflict can promote working memory and inhibitory control in children (Swerbenski et al. 2023). Causal support for these associations comes from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), which show that strengthening the interparental relationship through couples' programming can reduce child mental health challenges (Cummings et al. 2008; Zemp et al. 2016). Thus, one way to produce sustainable change for children's mental health challenges is to strengthen the interparental relationship.

Notably, to date, positive benefits of couple interventions to children have been shown with lengthy programs addressing marital satisfaction, interparental conflict, and/or coparenting coordination (Cowan and Cowan 2014). Though not focused on the couple, specifically, a review of eight brief parenting interventions indicated significant improvements in parent‐reported child externalizing behaviours, parenting skills (i.e., harsh parenting) and parenting self‐efficacy (Tully and Hunt 2016), demonstrating the potential utility of brief family‐centred interventions to children. Newer research has focused on brief couple interventions (BCIs), short‐term instructional or relationship‐enhancing couple‐based activities that target specific, precise psychological processes—rather than having comprehensive goals. There is growing evidence for the effectiveness of BCIs for enhancing couples' relationship quality, as seen in a recent review of 12 BCIs (Kanter and Schramm 2018). Despite the evidence supporting the use of BCIs to enhance couple functioning, they have not been studied in relation to family outcomes, more broadly.

In a BCI tailored for couples with young children—the Love Together Parent Together program (L2P2; Prime et al. 2023), couples engage in a brief writing intervention promoting the use of conflict reappraisal (i.e., viewing disagreements from the perspective of a neutral, third party). The intervention was developed based on Gottman's (1998) work on negative reciprocity, wherein observations of dissatisfied couples show that negative affect in one partner is often responded to with negative affect from the other. This leads to the escalation of negativity and an absence of productive (or constructive) conflict interactions. There is evidence for the effectiveness of L2P2 in producing positive change in couples' relationship quality and partner responsiveness (Prime et al. 2023), preventing relationship quality decline (Prime et al. 2024) and producing positive spillover effects on the coparenting alliance (Demy et al. 2025). Given robust links between the interparental relationship and child mental health (Davies et al. 2004; van Eldik et al. 2020), L2P2 has promise for promoting child mental health through changes to the interparental relationship. Testing this hypothesis is the central goal of the present study.

Current Study

The current study is a secondary analysis from a longitudinal RCT examining the impact of L2P2 on relationship quality in couples with young children (Prime et al. 2024). In line with a multidimensional approach to examining the interparental relationship (van Eldik et al. 2020), we examined indirect effects of L2P2 on child mental health challenges using two dimensions of the interparental relationship, relationship quality and conflict. A previous study using the same data showed that couples in the L2P2 group had enhanced relationship quality at post‐intervention compared to those in the control group (Prime et al. 2024). We further hypothesized that children from families with an enhanced interparental relationship at post‐intervention would be reported as having fewer mental health challenges at 1‐ and 3‐month follow‐ups and that the beneficial effect of the intervention on child mental health would partially operate through improvements in the interparental relationship.

Methods

Study Design

The primary study followed Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for randomized pilot and feasibility trials (Eldridge et al. 2016) and was pre‐registered on ClinicalTrials.gov and Open Science Framework.

Participants

Participants were recruited from community spaces in Ontario, Canada, between May and November 2022. Participants had to be at least 18 years old, living together and have a child younger than 6 years old living in the home. Both partners in a couple had to consent to be eligible. Exclusion criteria included having plans or history of separation or divorce (from each other) and being located outside of Canada.

Procedure

The longitudinal RCT was approved by York University Ethics (#2022‐076). All study procedures were conducted using Qualtrics. After both partners were consented and enrolled, they were sent baseline questionnaires. Randomization occurred after both partners completed baseline questionnaires. Thereafter, participants completed questionnaires at 1‐week post‐intervention (PI) and at 1‐ and 3‐month post‐intervention follow‐ups (FU1 and FU3, respectively). In addition, all participants completed three writing sessions, 4 weeks apart, between baseline (B) and PI (elaborated in ‘Intervention’). Each participant was remunerated up to $80. Interparental Relationship (Mediator)

Parents completed measures relating to their interparental relationship at baseline and PI. Ten indicators were subjected to an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to establish the factor structure for the relationship quality mediator(s), based on the below parent‐reported measures.

Perceived Relationship Quality Component Scale (PRQC; Fletcher et al. 2000)

Six 3‐item subscales from the PRQC were used to assess satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion and love. Each item was rated using a scale from not at all (1) to extremely (7).

Coparenting Relationship Scale (CRS; Feinberg et al. 2012)

Four items from the CRS were used to assess Coparenting Agreement (2 items; Relationship Quality) and Child Exposure to Conflict (2 items; Conflict) using a scale from never (0) to several times a day (6).

Conflict Frequency

One item assessed the frequency of conflicts over the previous 4 weeks using a scale from not very often (1) to very often (7; Braiker and Kelley 1979).

Conflict‐Related Distress

Following each writing session, participants completed two items that assessed their anger and distress relating to the conflict described in their writing sample using a scale from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (7; Finkel et al. 2013).

Child Mental Health Challenges (Outcome)

Parents reported on their child's mental health challenges at baseline, FU1 and FU3 using one of three versions of the Pediatric Symptom Checklist, a psychosocial tool used to screen emotional and behavioural challenges. To create a composite measure across age groups, scores were standardized within age groups and combined across age groups into one variable of child mental health challenges.

Children < 19 Months (n = 39)

Parents completed the 12‐item Baby Pediatric Symptom Checklist to rate their child's social and emotional behaviours (e.g., cries) over the previous 2 weeks using a scale from not at all (0) to very much (2; Sheldrick et al. 2013).

Children 19–60 Months (n = 78)

Parents completed the 18‐item Preschool Pediatric Symptom Checklist to rate their child's social and emotional behaviours (e.g., afraid) over the previous 2 weeks using a scale from not at all (0) to very much (2; Sheldrick et al. 2012).

Children > 60 Months (n = 23)

Parents completed the 18‐item Pediatric Symptom Checklist to rate their child's psychosocial behaviours (e.g., trouble concentrating) over the previous 2 weeks using a scale from never (0) to often (2; Gardner et al. 1999).

Intervention (Predictor; Random Assignment to L2P2 Versus Control)

The L2P2 program is an online brief writing intervention for couples intended to promote couples' relationship quality. At each of the three writing sessions (which occurred 4 weeks apart), participants were asked to write a fact‐based summary of a significant recent disagreement with their partners and respond to two conflict‐related distress items. They were then taught conflict reappraisal strategies via a psychoeducational video (3.5 min). Finally, they were asked to rewrite their conflict using a series of conflict reappraisal prompts (9 min). For an elaboration on the intervention, see Prime et al. (2024). Emails were sent 2 weeks after each writing session to remind the L2P2 group to practice conflict reappraisal. With respect to adherence and uptake, of the participants in the intervention group, 90% completed two of the three writing sessions and 80% of participants reported using at least ‘some’ conflict reappraisal skills outside of the writing sessions.

The control group completed the fact‐based summary, only, at each writing session. They received scheduling reminders between writing sessions. They were offered the L2P2 program at the end of the study period.

Analytic Plan

Descriptive statistics were calculated in SPSS 29.0.2 (IBM Corp 2023), and primary analyses were conducted in R (R Core Team 2021) using the Lavaan package (Rosseel 2012).

We first conducted an EFA with baseline data using 10 parent‐report indicators to identify appropriate dimensions of the interparental relationship. In addition, a confirmatory factor analysis at PI was used to confirm the baseline structure identified by the EFA. EFA and CFA results support a two‐factor model: conflict and relationship quality. These analyses are presented in Table S1 in Supporting Information S1.

Four structural equation models (SEMs) were used to examine the indirect effects of the L2P2 intervention (baseline) on child mental health challenges (assessed at FU1 and FU3, respectively) via interparental conflict and relationship quality mediators (assessed PI), respectively. Models 1A and 1B examined the indirect effect through interparental relationship quality on child mental health challenges (at FU1 and FU3, respectively). Models 2A and 2B examined the indirect effect through interparental conflict on child mental health challenges (at FU1 and FU3, respectively). All models controlled for the stability of the mediator/outcome (e.g., Model 1A included paths from baseline relationship quality to PI relationship quality and baseline child mental health challenges to FU1 child mental health challenges). All models also controlled for baseline child age. Total, direct and indirect effects were examined, with p < 0.05 (direct effects) or bootstrapped confidence intervals (CIs) across 10 000 draws, with CIs not containing zero indicating a significant indirect effect.

Five model fit indices were used to determine model appropriateness (values indicating moderate/good model fit in brackets): model chi‐square (< 3), Comparative Fit Index (> 0.9), Tucker Lewis Index (> 0.9), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (> 0.95) and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (< 0.1; Hu and Bentler 1999).

Missing Data

Rates of missing data range from 0% to 13.6%. Details on attrition are reported in the primary paper (Prime et al. 2024). In brief, 35 participants (12.5%) did not complete any post‐intervention/follow‐up assessments (deemed ‘lost to follow up’). Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) was used to handle the missing data.

Exploratory Analyses

There was an unexpected finding in Model 1A, wherein higher relationship quality was related to more child mental health problems (though it did not meet the standard threshold of significance). The child mental health challenge variable is highly autocorrelated over time (rs > 0.67), which could lend itself to too much shared variance to accurately predict the outcome. To explore this further, we removed the control for baseline child mental health challenges and re‐ran all models (See Supporting Information S2). Findings for the relationship quality mediated models no longer indicated a marginal positive effect linking the mediator and FU1 outcome. In contrast, there was emerging evidence for a negative effect linking this PI relationship quality to child mental health challenges at FU3, though the effect did not meet the standard threshold for significance (p = 0.055) (Table S2 and Figures S1 and S2). No changes were seen in the results for the conflict mediated models (Table S3 and Figures S3 and S4).

Discussion

Healthy functioning of the interparental relationship has the potential to benefit other family subsystems and family members, especially children (van Eldik et al. 2020). Most couple‐focused interventions do not examine downstream effects on child functioning (Eira Nunes et al. 2021; Harold and Sellers 2018). The goal of the current study was to examine secondary benefits of a BCI on child outcomes, through two dimensions of the interparental relationship, relationship quality and conflict. The present study did not find evidence to support pre‐registered hypotheses.

In line with the primary paper using the same data, couples in L2P2, as compared to the control group, reported enhanced relationship quality following the intervention (Prime et al. 2024). However, we did not find evidence that strengthened relationship quality subsequently benefitted child functioning. In the pre‐registered models, which controlled for baseline child mental health challenges, we found unexpected results, with weak, non‐significant evidence that strengthened relationship quality predicted more child mental health challenges (FU1). The child mental health challenges variable was highly autocorrelated over time. Moreover, because of the use of RCT methodology, we can assume that any differences detected between groups over time are due to the intervention. Thus, as an exploratory analysis, we removed the control for stability of child mental health challenges. Findings indicated weak, non‐significant evidence that strengthened relationship quality predicts fewer child mental health challenges (FU3; Supporting Information S2). These contrasting marginal findings indicate an unreliable pattern.

Overall, null findings may indicate there are no intervention benefits to children. It may be that children are too distal to the intervention, and thus, triadic interventions including the child (e.g., coparenting interventions) would be more beneficial (Feinberg 2002). Or perhaps, it may not be that there are no benefits to children, which would be surprising given the theoretical evidence, but instead these findings may emerge due to measurement selection. Perhaps constructs capturing children's interpretations or internal experiences, such as child cognitive appraisals and/or emotional security (Grych and Fincham 1990; Davies and Cummings 1994), would be more sensitive to intervention effects. This is an important intermediary step to test in future studies. Relatedly, with respect to more distal child outcomes, most research on child outcomes in the context of couple interventions focuses on behavioural outcomes, ignoring emotional competencies and internalizing behaviours (Davies, Martin, Sturge‐Apple, Ripple, and Cicchetti 2016). A measurement approach that better differentiates these subcategories may be more sensitive to the interparental relationship environment. Further, research should consider other methods of delivering intervention when exploring distal outcomes, as the brief, virtual nature of L2P2 could have limited effects on children of participants. Finally, spillover through relationships/interactions that involve the child, such as the coparenting and/or parent–child relationship, may be an important intermediary step in this pathway (Harold and Sellers 2018). Taken together, it is crucial to measure a variety of constructs related to child functioning that are carefully tailored to when change is expected. Testing developmental cascades that unfold over longer periods of time will be important in ascertaining which constructs are sensitive to BCIs, and when those effects are most impactful.

With respect to the second mediator, there was no evidence of an intervention effect on conflict, which is consistent with a recent study using this dataset and a different assessment of couple conflict (Demy et al. 2025). It remains unclear why a BCI aimed at conflict reappraisal did not significantly impact conflict dynamics in couples, as assessed in our study. Perhaps the intervention addressed a different type of conflict than we assessed—that is, we assessed parent reports of conflict frequency and distress, and child exposure to conflict; however, we do not know about couples' abilities to problem‐solve, communicate, and/or resolve their conflicts. Analysis of the writing samples is currently underway. Examining these writing samples will shed light on individual differences in understanding and applying the concepts of conflict reappraisal, and how that relates to changes in couple and child functioning following the intervention.

Additionally, interparental conflict did not significantly predict child mental health challenges. We did not assess destructive behaviours such as interparental hostility and disengagement, which have the strongest risks to child outcomes (Davies, Martin, and Sturge‐Apple 2016). It may be that more precise measurement of the multidimensional nature of the interparental relationship, such as a distinction between destructive and constructive conflict, would yield effects on children (Swerbenski et al. 2023; van Eldik et al. 2020). Future research should therefore include measurement of these critical conflict constructs in evaluating the benefits of BCIs on children.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations of the current work are noteworthy. First, all study measures were based on parent report. There are some validity concerns of self‐reported marital conflict, communication, and child mental health challenges (De Los Reyes et al. 2011; Sanford 2010), which could be addressed using a multi‐informant and multi‐modal approach. Second, our measurement of child mental health challenges had limitations due to its three age forms. Each measure asked different questions, making comparison of composites across age groups hard to interpret. Future research should recruit a larger sample that can systematically examine age differences. Third, the internal consistency of the coparenting agreement scale ranged from poor to acceptable, which may be due to having few items. Fourth, our sample consisted of participants who were highly educated, limiting generalizability to a more diverse educational sample.

Conclusions

There is significant potential for BCIs to provide an accessible approach to improve couple functioning, with benefits to other family members and relationships. However, the current study does not provide support for this; there were no detected secondary benefits to child mental health challenges, despite improvements amongst participating couples' relationship quality. Future studies should continue to evaluate the multitude of benefits of couple interventions across the family, making careful considerations about the assessment schedule and measurement selection.
