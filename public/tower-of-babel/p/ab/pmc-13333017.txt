The burden of antimicrobial-resistant bacterial infections: a causal perspective
Authors: Cherry Lim, Koen B Pouwels, Sue J Lee, Yin Mo, Rogier H van Doorn, Paul Turner, Ben S Cooper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEstimates of preventable antimicrobial resistance (AMR) burden are important to inform local, national, regional, and global policies, targets and research priorities. Such estimates rely heavily on model assumptions and several analytical approaches have been used. In this perspective article, we outline key conceptual and practical challenges in estimating AMR burden, and propose strategies for building on existing work to obtain more policy-relevant burden estimates. We highlight how new approaches taking an explicitly causal perspective are tackling these problems and have the potential to improve the way results are combined from individual studies to estimate national and regional AMR burden.Subject terms: Epidemiology, Bacterial infection, Statistical methods, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

Reliable estimates of the health impact of antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) infections are needed to inform policy decisions at local, national and international levels about both interventions with potential to have almost immediate impact (such as hospital antibiotic stewardship and hygiene measures), as well as longer-term initiatives (such as new drug and vaccine development programmes) to combat such infections. When the objective of estimating AMR burden is to monitor such temporal trends or measure progress towards targeted reductions, an explicitly causal perspective is not needed; for such applications, the key concern is consistency of measurement across time and space. However, when AMR burden estimates are intended to inform decision-making, set priorities and provide input into health economic evaluations of potential control measures, then causally interpretable burden estimates are needed because these applications depend on a causal understanding of the relationship between AMR infection and patient outcomes. A key challenge in the interpretation of published AMR burden estimates is the large variation in patient outcome measures reported, study designs employed, and analytical approaches used. A consequence of this is that reported estimates are often not directly comparable or causally interpretable1–3. Moreover, the large heterogeneity in methodological quality of the studies evaluating AMR burden further contributes to the challenging task of interpreting and combining estimates from individual studies to generate combined estimates of AMR burdens4.

Attributable and excess mortality are common measures used to quantify the health impact of AMR infection5–8, but the terms have not always been used consistently, creating a potential source of confusion for policy-makers and researchers6. Moreover, there are several issues that specifically relate to the problem of quantifying the burden of AMR infections that have not been widely recognised in the AMR burden literature. We address these below.

Observational studies and routinely collected surveillance data are the key sources of data used to quantify the health impact of AMR infections, yet they are vulnerable to several important biases that, if unaccounted for, may substantially distort burden estimates. In this paper, we will first consider the fundamental problem of making causal inferences about the burden of AMR, then introduce standard definitions of common terms used in the current literature and highlight their causal interpretation. We then discuss the assumptions and potential pitfalls underlying the existing observational study designs and analytical approaches. We propose solutions to reduce biases relating to the design, analysis and synthesis of studies on the health burden of AMR infections. Finally, we outline potential future work aimed at improving the generalisability and transportability of estimates, particularly in data-limited settings.

The fundamental problem of causal inference

When considering the potential impact of interventions to reduce the burden of AMR, we need to take an explicitly causal perspective. We want to know: if an intervention were able to reduce AMR infections, what effect would this have on deaths and DALYs? The potential outcomes framework is a natural approach for conceptualising this9. Within this framework, we compare the health outcomes of a patient with a resistant infection with the potential health outcomes that would have occurred in the same patient had the resistant infection not occurred. Since outcomes under both conditions cannot be observed in the same patient at the same time (this is the fundamental problem of causal inference), the causal contrast of interest cannot be directly observed. Causal inference methods must therefore make use of indirect approaches which, if certain assumptions hold, allow us to estimate this causal contrast using real-world data.

Types of comparators

The cohort of individuals to which AMR infections are compared is often called the 'unexposed' group or sometimes the 'exposure counterfactual'. In the context of AMR infection, there are two types of comparators that are often used to quantify outcomes, and these correspond to two distinct definitions of 'unexposed': the antimicrobial-susceptible infection counterfactual and the AMR infection-free counterfactual. For the former, the exposure counterfactual is an otherwise equivalent individual with the same type of infection with the same bacterial species, but with the infection caused by a susceptible instead of a resistant phenotype. For the latter, the exposure counterfactual is an otherwise equivalent individual but without the particular infection under consideration (though note that this counterfactual does not exclude individuals with infections caused by other pathogens or take into consideration infections subsequent to the one under consideration). A large body of literature attempts to estimate the burden defined by the first counterfactual, comparing outcomes of AMR and antimicrobial-susceptible infections caused by the same bacterial species1,3,10. This comparison is relevant for policymakers when interventions causing decreases in resistant infections lead to concomitant increases in susceptible infections (and vice versa). This would be expected for interventions affecting carriage of resistant strains when resistant and susceptible strains are in direct competition with complete niche overlap11. As a thought experiment, we can think of this counterfactual as appropriate for measuring the change in disease burden that would be caused by a hypothetical intervention to inoculate individuals with a vaccine that prevents infections with resistant strains of a given pathogen but causes a matching increase in the risk of a susceptible infection with the same pathogen. This susceptible infection counterfactual can also be relevant for policymaking if we want to quantify the burden of disease we could prevent by avoiding delays in effective antibiotic treatment caused by resistance, provided we assume that any additional burden of drug-resistant bacteria compared with susceptible bacteria is solely a consequence of the resistant phenotype. In contrast, the AMR infection-free comparator is relevant when two conditions simultaneously hold: (1) we are interested in quantifying the burden of disease we could avert by an intervention that prevents resistant infections; and (2) the intervention of interest has no effect on the number or severity of susceptible infections. This second condition would hold for interventions reducing carriage of resistant strains when there is no competition between resistant and susceptible strains (i.e. no niche overlap). We can think of this counterfactual as appropriate for measuring the change in disease burden that would be caused by a hypothetical intervention to inoculate individuals with a vaccine that prevents infections with resistant strains of a given pathogen but has no effect on susceptible infections. These two counterfactuals can lead to widely differing burden estimates. In situations where there is partial niche overlap between resistant and sensitive strains of the same species or for certain interventions, the most relevant counterfactual may be one that is intermediate between the two. Currently, evidence to inform the choice of appropriate counterfactual is limited, but we are aware of four relevant studies. Ammerlaan et al. considered nosocomial bloodstream infection (BSI) data for multiple bacterial species from 1998 to 2007 from 14 hospitals across 9 countries and three continents and concluded that 'increasing incidence of nosocomial BSIs caused by antibiotic-resistant bacteria adds to the total burden of disease without replacing [nosocomial] BSIs caused by more susceptible bacteria' suggesting little niche overlap12. In a systematic review of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) trends, Mostofsky et al. reached a similar conclusion for hospital-acquired MRSA, noting that 'studies typically found that the incidence of MSSA infections did not decrease in the face of rising MRSA prevalence' and that infection control efforts led to declines in MRSA with no reciprocal increase in methicillin-sensitive S. aureus (MSSA) infections13. For community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA), the authors also concluded that CA-MRSA most likely increased the total burden of S. aureus infections rather than displacing MSSA, though noted that one study provided evidence of CA-MRSA colonisation displacing MSSA colonization13. This observation is relevant as although colonisation does not always lead to infection (and thus is not a direct measure of burden), it does typically precede clinical infection. In an analysis of resistance trends for five major bacterial pathogens from 27 European countries (2002–2008), de Kraker et al. also concluded that 'It appears that infections with resistant clones add to rather than replace infections caused by susceptible bacteria'14. In an analysis of Escherichia coli trends from Oxfordshire, United Kingdom (1999–2011), Schlackow et al. similarly concluded that the observed increase in E. coli bacteraemia rates was due to rising rates of resistant E. coli, while little change was observed in susceptible E. coli15. Overall, these analyses suggest that the AMR infection-free comparator may be more relevant for hospital-acquired drug-resistant infections when considering many infection prevention interventions, but are less conclusive for community-acquired infections. The antimicrobial-susceptible infection counterfactual may be more relevant when considering interventions to reduce delays in effective treatment caused by resistance. De Kraker and Lipsitch16 proposed selecting the comparator group of AMR infection using an intervention-based causal approach, which encourages the choice of a comparator based on the type of preventive control intervention in mind16. Such choices will also depend on a causal understanding or assumptions about mechanisms through which the intervention works and the nature of the ecological interactions between the different strains.

Defining outcomes

Mortality is an objective health outcome to assess. Commonly used metrics include 14-day (since onset of symptoms) mortality, 28-day mortality, 30-day mortality, hospital discharge mortality and ICU discharge mortality17. In settings such as parts of Southeast Asia, where moribund patients are often discharged to die at home, using hospital discharge mortality without appropriate adjustment could considerably underestimate the burden and lead to difficulties in comparing estimates across different settings. Common approaches in the literature include defining moribund as a death event or performing sensitivity analyses17.

When making decisions about investment in measures to reduce AMR infections, considerations of the number of life years lost (YLL) are of central importance. Such approaches can be extended to consider infection-related morbidity in addition to mortality by using quality-adjusted life years gained by preventing or better treating AMR infections (where a higher number indicates a greater benefit) or disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) that could be averted18. In the context of AMR infections, challenges in defining YLL lie in knowing the distribution of the age of death in a counterfactual scenario without AMR infection18. Specifically, hospitalised patients will be expected to have a different distribution of the age of death compared to the general population, and calculations of YLL due to hospital-acquired infections should account for this. For instance, a study from Thailand estimated the life expectancy post-discharge from ICU to be seven years lower than would be expected for a cohort with the same age distribution in the general population19.

The excess length of hospitalisation due to AMR infection is an important outcome for estimating economic costs due to AMR, but careful analysis is needed to address time-dependent bias when analysing hospital-acquired infection data (AMR infections may lengthen length of stay, but longer length of stay also increases the chance of acquiring an infection). The majority of published studies have been found to inappropriately apply time-fixed methods, which are known to substantially overestimate this quantity20. More appropriate methods accounting for the time-dependent exposure using exposure density sampling and multistate models overcome the limitations of these naïve analyses20. More recently, inverse probability weighted survival curves have been applied to the problem; this has the advantage of being able to address both time-dependent bias and time-varying confounding20,21.

Assumptions and potential pitfalls underlying the existing observational study designs and analytical approaches

Directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) have emerged as a key tool to support the estimation of causal risk ratios and are now increasingly applied to AMR. DAGs provide a formal approach for describing assumed causal relationships based on domain-specific knowledge. They can be used to identify when these relationships will introduce bias, and allow the identification of sets of variables which, if fully adjusted for, will eliminate such bias. An important insight from such causal tools is that the adjustments required to estimate causal risk ratios for hospital-acquired infections will, in general, differ from those for community-acquired infections due to differences in causal relationship structures (Fig. 1). A particular challenge when estimating the burden of community-acquired AMR infections is the risk of collider bias. This can occur when available data come exclusively from patients admitted to the hospital. In contrast, for hospital-acquired infections, one of the main challenges is appropriately accounting for the time-dependent nature of the exposure and time-varying confounding. In this section, we summarise the major sources of bias in observational data used to estimate the health consequences of AMR infection, discuss their implications for these estimates, and describe how causal analyses are increasingly being applied to address these challenges.

Confounding bias

Confounding bias can influence the estimate of cRR in Eqs. 2 and 4 and, consequently, lead to bias in the AFE and PAF estimates. Adjusting for confounders is often challenging in the context of AMR infection. Confounders are context-based (Fig. 2). For instance, if comorbidities and previous antibiotic use both affect the risk of AMR infection and, through another pathway, risk of an outcome such as mortality, then it will be necessary to adjust for or condition on both to get unbiased estimates of the cRR (Fig. 2A). The precise causal relationship and confounders cannot be known with certainty, and key confounders may either be unrecorded or measured only imperfectly, meaning that some degree of residual confounding is expected. Analysis of a previously published prospective surveillance study of hospital-acquired infections in Thailand showed that adjusting for recorded confounders reduced the estimate of the excess deaths attributable to AMR infection by 11–30% (Supplementary Table 1)22. In practice, these challenges mean that estimates of the excess fraction will, in most cases, be dependent on strong causal assumptions and will, to a greater or lesser degree, be distorted by residual confounding. Nevertheless, careful consideration of likely causal pathways for the specific context under consideration can help to identify plausible confounders, which should be accounted for in the analysis. For complex DAGs, tools such as DAGitty can be useful to identify the sets of confounders that need to be adjusted for, assuming a given DAG23.

Measurement bias due to delayed sampling

Measurement bias occurs when the estimated association between exposure and outcome deviates from the true causal association as a result of the process by which the study data are measured (Fig. 3). In the context of estimating the health burden of AMR infection, delays in or absence of clinical sample collection would result in measurement error in identifying AMR infections. The direction and magnitude of biases depend on the type of patients affected by measurement bias, and could pose challenges when attempting to estimate AFE and PAF. In the context of AMR burden, measurement error would, in general, mean that the estimated association between measured exposures and measured outcome would represent biased estimates of the true association between exposures and outcomes24,25. Such measurement error may be particularly important in LMIC settings where, when microbiological testing is possible, it is typically performed much less frequently than in high-income settings. Indeed, in many LMIC settings, there are only microbiology laboratories in higher-level/referral hospitals, and clinical cultures are routinely taken only when patients do not improve with initial antibiotic treatment. If such a lack of improvement can be caused by both AMR infection (due to non-response to initial antibiotic treatment) and underlying patient illness, biased estimates of the impact of AMR infection on patient outcomes will result. Such bias can also be introduced by recent antibiotic treatment if this affects both testing sensitivity and patient outcomes. Such differential microbiological testing for patients depending on responsiveness to empirical antibiotic treatment will also distort estimates of the prevalence of resistance. Possible approaches to reduce measurement bias include (i) the use of prospective studies where microbiological data used in estimating AMR burden come from testing that does not depend on responsiveness to initial treatment; (ii) analysis that adjusts for factors influencing both microbiological testing and patient outcomes (Fig. 3); and (iii) explicitly modelling the size and direction of the potential bias24,25.

Collider bias in community-acquired infections

Though the importance of collider bias has been recognised since the 1940s26, it came to prominence during the COVID pandemic and is now widely recognised as a key obstacle to making causal inferences from common types of observational data27. In the context of AMR infections, it has hitherto been largely neglected. A collider is a variable that is influenced by two or more other variables (Fig. 4). Collider bias refers to a distortion in the association between variables when conditioning on the collider. Here, conditioning means adjusting for or stratifying on the collider in the analysis or restricting the analysis to records with particular values of the collider variable. To illustrate this, consider the excess mortality due to AMR infection among patients with community-acquired infections, assuming we are interested in using no AMR infections as the comparator. If antibiotics can be readily obtained by community members, then those who acquire antimicrobial-susceptible infections are more likely to recover without the need for hospital admission than those with resistant infections. In contrast, for community members who acquire AMR infections, initial antibiotic treatment purchased from the local pharmacy would be more likely to fail, and these individuals would be more likely to require hospitalisation. When the analysis is conditioned on the cohort of patients who visited the hospital (i.e. by restricting the analysis to these patients, as will occur if microbiological testing occurs only for admitted patients), it can result in a distortion in the association between community-acquired AMR infection and outcome. This occurs because (i) hospitalisation is a collider as a result of being causally influenced both by the resistance phenotype and the underlying health condition, and conditioning on it creates a non-causal association between resistance and the underlying health condition; and (ii) the underlying health condition is causally linked to patient outcome. In a fully adjusted analysis, the magnitude of this bias depends on the strength of the relationship between the collider (hospital admission) and unmeasured causes of both the collider and the outcome. In the context of community-acquired infection, the strength of association between hospital visits and unmeasured confounders might be expected to depend on the availability of healthcare resources in different settings. In a resource-limited setting where only the most severely ill patients access the healthcare system or antibiotics are commonly used in the community, then collider bias could have important detrimental impacts on the reliability of burden estimates when analysis uses hospital data alone. Our own simulation study shows that in the presence of collider bias, underestimation of excess deaths attributable to AMR infection compared to antimicrobial-susceptible infection would be expected (from a causal odds ratio of 2.0 to an estimated odds ratio of 1.65–1.78 in the presence of a strong correlation between collider and exposure in a simulation study; Supplementary Table 2). The magnitude of underestimation depends on the strength of the relationship between antibiotic exposure in the community and access to healthcare facilities in the community from which surveillance data is collected.

Study designs and statistical methods

Observational studies, including cohort and case-control studies, have been used to estimate the burden of AMR in terms of the excess fraction and PAF3. Electronic databases holding routinely collected microbiological and patient outcome data can be a sustainable and useful data source28. An ideal electronic database for estimating the AFE and PAF of community-acquired AMR infection would involve longitudinal surveillance of the entire target population, including individuals who were hospitalised, attended primary care facilities and healthy community members. Data should include accurate information about the date of infection onset, causative organisms, including resistance phenotype, date of death or censoring and temporarily resolved data on potential confounders.

If such ideal data sources existed, estimating the burden of community-acquired infections would require estimating the AFE and PAF, which in turn requires estimating the cRR associated with resistance (whether for the susceptible or no infection comparator). Provided confounders could be fully adjusted for, and in the absence of time-varying confounders, this could be achieved using standard statistical approaches (e.g. survival analysis or logistic regression). Because this analysis includes individuals from the entire target population rather than conditioning on the hospitalised population or adjusting for other colliders, it avoids collider bias. Data sources currently used to estimate AMR burden fall considerably short of this ideal, and in most cases include only data from patients with hospital contact. When only hospital data are available, there are no simple solutions to the problem of collider bias, though a number of approaches to explore the sensitivity of results to such bias have been proposed27,29.

The AMR burden due to hospital-acquired infections can, however, be estimated using only hospital data without distortions due to collider bias, but a central challenge is accounting for the time-dependent nature of the exposure. Data sources used for estimating AMR burden due to hospital-acquired infections typically include date of admission, date of discharge or death, date of infection and may include associated time-dependent variables (such as measures of patient severity) and non-time-varying variables (such as age and sex). Since the exposure (the hospital-acquired infection) is initially not present, it represents a time-dependent exposure. Analyses such as standard logistic regression models and survival analyses that fail to account for this and treat the exposure as if it were known at the day of admission are widespread in the AMR burden literature but have been shown to lead to substantial biases30. Appropriately accounting for the time-dependent exposure using full cohort data (for example, using a survival analysis with time-dependent covariates) can eliminate this bias. When full cohort data are not available or are prohibitively expensive to collect, matched designs offer an appealing alternative but also have pitfalls. A widely used approach is to match cases (patients with hospital-acquired infections) with controls chosen from patients without hospital-acquired infections during their entire hospital stay who stayed at least as long as the unexposed period for cases (i.e. time from admission to infection). While superficially appealing, because this approach selects controls conditioning on a future event (lack of hospital-acquired infection), it will still yield substantially biased results (though less so than approaches that entirely ignore the time-dependent nature of the exposure). Exposure density sampling is a modified matching approach proposed by Wolkewitz et al. (and now used by the US Centers for Disease Control)30,31 that overcomes this problem by selecting matched controls based only on their exposure status at the time of the infection in corresponding cases, without regard to their future status. Allowing the matched controls to subsequently change their exposure status (i.e. acquire infections) avoids conditioning on a future event and allows unbiased estimates. Examples of applying exposure density sampling include AMR burden studies on healthcare-associated infections using data from the US Department of Veterans Affairs system32,33 and from hospitals in Germany30. Alternatively, applying multistate models to the entire target population has been used to account for time-dependent exposure. A limitation of such approaches is that adjusting for time-varying confounding is challenging. Time-varying confounding will occur when there are time-dependent covariates which are affected by past infection status but also affect future infection status and the patient outcome. An increasingly common approach to adjust for this type of confounding is to use marginal structural models estimated using inverse probability of treatment weighting34,35. Such approaches have recently been used to estimate the excess length of hospitalisation due to hospital-acquired infection21. Inverse probability weighting techniques have also been applied to data from different designs, including case-cohort, nested case-control and point prevalence studies to adjust for unequal selection of controls in nosocomial infection36.

Generalisability and transportability of the health impact of antimicrobial-resistant infection

In the above discussion, we focused on internal validity, considering biases in the estimation of the AMR burden in a given population. In this section, we focus on external validity and consider how results from one population can be extrapolated to other populations. A distinction between generalisability and transportability has been made37,38, but it has yet to be explored in the context of AMR infections. In brief, generalisability refers to the ability to extend causal inferences (i.e. an estimate) from an analysed study sample to a target population with individuals who meet the same inclusion criteria for the analysis. Transportation, on the other hand, refers to the ability to extend causal inferences (i.e. an estimate) to a target population with some individuals who do not meet the inclusion criteria for the analysis (Supplementary Text 1)38. A key challenge in generalising or transporting (or both) estimated AMR burdens from one context to another lies in appropriately accounting for effect modifiers. Effect modifiers are factors that can lead to different health impacts of AMR infections in different populations. Possible effect modifiers include age, immunocompetence, virulence of the dominant lineages of causative organisms and healthcare provision, including access to appropriate second-line antibiotics. Additionally, variation in age structure across populations plays a role in differences in disease severity, as observed in pneumococcal infections, where more severe illnesses are seen in extreme age groups. Similarly, observation of geographic variation in the distribution of different clones of S. aureus, each hosting distinct virulence factors that contribute to the pathogenicity of the bacteria, has been reported39. However, studies on clonal distributions of the major pathogenic bacteria in resource-limited settings are still limited, and this contributes to difficulties in generalising and transporting the estimated health burden of AMR infection from data-rich settings to data-limited settings.

Innovative methods, including the use of a federated target trial emulation framework, which overcomes the need for centralised data storage and analysis of patient-level data, could be a useful approach to maximise usage of different electronic health database to improve generalisability while overcoming what are often otherwise insurmountable data-sharing constraints40.

Future studies designed to estimate the burden of AMR

Producing policy-relevant estimates of the burden of AMR infections involves asking causal questions which require consideration of counterfactuals such as, 'What would the health outcomes in patients with AMR infections have been if all resistant infections had been replaced with otherwise equivalent antimicrobial-susceptible infections?' and 'What would the health outcomes in patients with AMR infections have been if the infections had not occurred?'. Therefore, causal considerations are crucial for interpreting, understanding, and re-using the derived estimates41. An important consideration when designing an AMR burden study is the selection of comparators. In general, this should be informed by both the nature of the ecological interactions between resistant and sensitive phenotypes and the policy questions such burden estimates are intended to address. Consideration of the existing evidence, which suggests that reducing hospital-acquired AMR infections has not led to replacement by AMS infections and increases in hospital-acquired AMR infections have not led to reductions in AMS infections, suggests that the no-infection counterfactual is more relevant when considering interventions to prevent AMR burden of hospital-acquired infections. The susceptible infection counterfactual is more relevant if considering the potential reduction in burden due to interventions that remove delays in effective treatment caused by resistance, but which do not prevent infections. However, almost all research into the appropriateness of different counterfactuals comes from high-income settings, and more research is needed in understanding the extent to which conclusions can be generalised to LMIC settings. There is also a need to understand the extent to which this is true for community-acquired infections, though in this case, analysis is complicated by the fact that most observed community-acquired infections are those where patients are admitted to hospitals.

The different sources of biases operating and the different analytical approaches needed provide a clear rationale for estimating and reporting AFE and PAF separately for community-acquired and hospital-acquired bacterial infections. There is also a clear public health and scientific rationale given that different types of interventions are needed in the two contexts. Care is needed when selecting appropriate cohorts from which comparators are sampled for the two contexts. In the case of community-acquired infections and using antimicrobial-susceptible infections as a comparator, collider bias may be impossible to avoid if outcome and exposure data are collected only from hospitalised patients. Ideally, in this case, surveillance data should therefore include both hospitalised and non-hospitalised patients together with microbiological testing results and clinical symptoms of bacterial infection from both settings. An example of such a database system is the combined United Kingdom electronic health database and primary care database42. An ideal data set would include either full cohort data or a sample of randomly selected individuals in the community, and include microbiological testing results for all bacterial infections with the potential to lead to hospitalisation. While this would be challenging, the success of large-scale prospective cohort studies for understanding the epidemiology and burden of SARS-CoV-2 highlights the considerable advantages of appropriately resourced prospective studies compared to using noisy surveillance data collected for other purposes43,44. Importantly, in the case of using the AMR infection-free counterfactual as the comparator for community-acquired AMR infections, patients admitted to hospital without such infections will not be an appropriate comparator for quantifying burden; by virtue of being admitted to hospital for something else, these uninfected but hospitalised patients would be expected to have a higher mortality risk than the counterfactual of interest (i.e. the mortality risk that patients with community-acquired AMR infections would have had had they not had these infections). Instead, estimation of the burden of community-acquired AMR infection with the AMR infection-free counterfactual requires comparing outcomes between patients with community-acquired AMR infections and members of the same community without such infections after adjustment for potential confounders using an appropriate method such as inverse probability weighting45.

For hospital-acquired infections, measurement bias can be minimised by collecting clinical samples for microbiological testing before antibiotics are prescribed46. In practice, while avoiding delaying clinical sample collection for microbiological testing is essential to minimise measurement bias, this can be challenging for many resource-limited settings46,47.

Additionally, there are few studies on recurrence of AMR infection and on hospital readmission, especially from low and middle-income countries. Understanding the long-term impact of AMR is important for estimating the overall health and economic burden, which are key quantities for cost-effectiveness analyses when comparing different intervention strategies.

To be most useful to inform policy-making, distinct estimates for the burden of AMR linked to community-acquired infections and hospital-acquired infections are needed. While there is value in considering both the no-infection and susceptible-infection counterfactuals, when using results from burden estimation to inform policy, careful consideration of the appropriate counterfactual is needed. This will depend both on the interventions under consideration and the population biology of the targeted bacterial pathogens.

Published studies or data

When combining estimates of excess fraction, cRR and PAF across settings with different patterns of microbiological testing in addition to considerations of transportability discussed above, it is important to make appropriate adjustments for the magnitude and direction of potential biases in the estimates. Use of weighting by quality scores is one possible approach, but this fails to account for the direction of bias and is not recommended48. Another possible approach would be to use an existing risk of bias assessment scoring system, such as ROBINS-I49, to evaluate published studies with the evidence synthesis restricted to studies with low risk of bias. A limitation here, though, is that with almost all AMR burden estimation studies vulnerable to at least one important source of bias, this would leave few studies to analyse. Several more sophisticated model-based approaches to synthesise evidence while adjusting for biases have been described, and these provide the most pragmatic pathways to fully bias-adjusted burden estimates50. Ideally, such bias adjustment should be informed by empirical evidence, but elicited opinion and simulation studies may also inform such bias modelling50.

Estimating the burden of AMR infections globally is a challenging but important task, and many important methodological improvements have been described in recent years, ranging from large-scale efforts to integrate multiple sources of data to produce regional and global burden estimates to improved ways of tackling important biases applied to single hospital studies20. In this paper, we have sought to define what we mean by the burden in explicitly causal language, summarise what we believe to be the main challenges in obtaining reliable estimates for different components of this burden, and outlined strategies and recommendations for obtaining more accurate and more useful burden estimates. We believe these findings will be relevant for future studies attempting to estimate AMR burden, whether at a single institution or at a larger spatial scale. In particular, we argue that taking an explicitly causal approach can be useful in helping to clarify what we mean by the burden, help to support the design and analysis of future studies intended to estimate this burden, and help to clarify which burden estimates are relevant for different policy questions.
