The Unfinished Breath: Caregiver Perceptions of Terminal Events and Gaps in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Care in India
Authors: Darshan Chikktimmegowda, Muddasu Suhasini Keerthipriya, Seena Vengalil, Saraswati Nashi, Dipti Baskar, J Jobi Mol, Soja Joseph, SY Aishwarya, PB Binesha, B Dinesh Kumar, H Chandrika, Mugdha Mehta, Ajmal Roshan, Yamini Keshavaprasad Belur, Atchayaram Nalini, Priya Treesa Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objectives:Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder with a high symptom burden and limited survival. Little is known about the terminal phase experiences, symptom prevalence, and end-of-life care patterns of people with ALS (PALS) in India. This study aimed to assess terminal events and caregiver-reported outcomes in PALS to identify gaps in ALS care delivery in India.Methods:A cross-sectional telephonic survey was conducted among bereaved caregivers of PALS enrolled in the Neuropalliative and Supportive Care project between December 2021 and May 2024. A structured, validated questionnaire was used to collect data on demographics, terminal-phase symptoms, medical interventions, and the nature of death as perceived by primary caregivers. Descriptive statistics and appropriate statistical analyses were performed.Results:A total of 130 caregivers participated in the survey; the majority (57.7%) were sons or daughters. Among the 130 PALS, 76 (58.5%) were men; 56.2% had limb onset and 43.8% had bulbar onset. The mean age at death was 53.5 ± 11.4 years. Most patients (57.7%) died at home, and 29.2% experienced sudden death. Patients who died in the hospital were more likely to be on invasive mechanical ventilation (P < 0.001). The most common terminal symptoms were breathlessness (79.2%), excessive oral secretions (54.6%), followed by anxiety or restlessness (44.6%). Only 20% received bilevel positive airway pressure, and 25.4% were on percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy. A significant association was found between bulbar onset and assisted feeding (P = 0.002).Conclusions:This study highlights the need for proactive, community-integrated palliative care services and emphasizes the urgency of early intervention and caregiver support to improve end-of-life experiences in PALS in India.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as motor neuron disease, is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by degeneration of motor neurons,[1] leading to muscle weakness, atrophy, and eventual death within 2 to 4 years.[2] The disease progresses from focal symptoms to involve multiple regions, including the upper and lower limbs, speech, swallowing, and respiratory muscles, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life (QOL) and placing a considerable burden on caregivers.[3] In India, the median age at ALS onset is lower than in Western populations, with slower disease progression in many cases.[4] This highlights the importance of region-specific studies to understand the unique challenges faced by people with ALS (PALS).

The terminal phase of ALS is associated with a high symptom burden, including breathlessness, excessive oral secretions, pain, anxiety, and cough,[56] which contributes to significant distress for patients and their caregivers.[7] Respiratory failure, exacerbated by ineffective management of oral secretions and progressive muscle weakness, remains the primary cause of mortality in ALS.[78910] Many patients require assistive devices such as noninvasive ventilation (NIV), home invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), and percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) during advanced stages of the disease.[11] The nature of death in ALS, whether expected or unexpected, also influences caregivers’ emotional and psychological experiences during the end-of-life (EOL) phase.[5] Higher caregiver burden is associated with greater patient physical and behavioral impairment.[12] According to a study by Stutzki et al.,[13] up to 50% of patients consider hastening death, influenced by feelings of being a burden, anxiety, depression, loneliness, and poor QOL.

The circumstances surrounding death in PALS remain poorly characterized in the Indian context. This report aims to provide the key aspects of death among PALS in India as perceived by primary caregivers of ALS patients (CALS). Specifically, the study examines terminal events, focusing on critical factors such as symptom burden, EOL care patterns, use of assistive devices, and the nature of death. By addressing these critical areas, the study aims to identify gaps in ALS care delivery and advocate for palliative care strategies tailored to the unique needs of PALS in India.

Methods

The Neuropalliative and Supportive Care (NPSC) project was initiated in 2021 at a quaternary care center for neurological conditions in southern India to evaluate the palliative care needs of patients with chronic life-limiting neurological conditions, including ALS, and to develop a palliative care model for these patients. PALS who avail outpatient or inpatient services at the department of neurology are registered under the NPSC project and receive multidisciplinary care. This study was conducted among bereaved caregivers of PALS registered under the project between December 2021 and May 2024, providing a reliable source for identifying eligible participants. A cross-sectional, telephonic survey was conducted in 2024 and 2025 to assess terminal events in PALS based on caregiver experiences. CALS who were directly involved in patient care, were above 18 years of age, had sufficient recall of terminal-phase events, and provided consent to participate in the study were included. All consecutive bereaved caregivers meeting the inclusion criteria were recruited during the defined study period. No a priori sample size calculation was performed. Patients’ clinical and demographic data were collected from meticulously maintained medical records. Data on caregiver demographics, terminal-phase symptom burden, use of assistive devices such as NIV, PEG, or Ryle’s tube, place of death, and nature of death were collected using a structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was developed based on themes derived from a prior qualitative study exploring caregiver experiences of EOL care in ALS in India, which is yet to be published. The draft instrument was reviewed by experts in neuromuscular disorders and palliative care to establish face and content validity. A pilot test was subsequently conducted among caregivers to assess clarity and feasibility. Minor modifications were made to improve clarity and reduce the length of the questionnaire before final administration. The use of a structured questionnaire helped minimize missing data. The telephonic survey was conducted by trained interviewers and was chosen to enable caregivers to participate from home. Each interview lasted approximately 20–30 minutes, ensuring caregivers had sufficient time to recall details without experiencing undue distress. The telephonic approach also allowed interviewers to provide support if caregivers experienced distress. The terminal phase was defined as the last 1 week to 1 month of life. Sudden death was recorded as any unexpected and rapid deterioration in clinical condition resulting in death within a few hours (<24 hours) in an otherwise stable PALS, as reported by the caregiver.

Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants before the interview. Caregivers were given the option to withdraw at any time. The study adhered to ethical guidelines for research involving bereaved individuals, ensuring sensitivity and confidentiality. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical data, including frequencies, percentages, mean and standard deviation (SD), median, range, and interquartile range (IQR). Chi-square tests were used to examine relationships between categorical variables, and analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables. Independent-samples t-tests were used to compare continuous variables. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 860 PALS were registered under the project during the study period, of whom 153 had died. Twenty-three CALS who did not consent to participate were excluded, and responses from the remaining 130 CALS were documented and analyzed.

Patient characteristics

Among the 130 PALS, 76 (58.5%) were men, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.4:1. The mean age at disease onset was 53.5 ± 11.4 years. Limb onset was observed in 73 (56.2%) and bulbar onset in 57 (43.8%) PALS. The mean age at death was 55.7 ± 11.3 years, with no significant difference based on gender or type of onset. Place of death, nature, and circumstances of death

Seventy-five (57.7%) PALS died at home, while 51 (39.2%) died in a hospital setting. Three (2.3%) PALS died on the way to the hospital, and one (0.8%) died in a residential care home. Overall, the majority of PALS (79 of 130, 60.8%) died outside hospital settings, with home being the most frequent place of death. Age at death, gender of PALS or CALS, and age of CALS did not influence the place of death. Sudden death occurred in 38 (29.2%) PALS, whereas 92 (70.8%) experienced a more gradual decline. A Chi-square test revealed no significant association between gender, type of onset, place of death, and sudden death. Among those who died in the hospital, 14 (27.5%) were on IMV. Of these, 11 were initiated on IMV in the terminal phase, meaning they died within 1 month. In contrast, only 2 of 79 (2.5%) PALS who died outside hospital settings had received IMV—one had been ventilated for five months, and the other was discharged home after 5 days of IMV initiation and died within hours. There was a significant association between place of death and IMV use (χ² =19, p < 0.001), indicating that patients who died in the hospital were more likely to be on IMV.

Symptoms in the terminal phase

The study revealed that breathlessness was the most common terminal symptom in PALS, affecting 103 (79.2%) individuals, highlighting severe respiratory muscle involvement in the advanced stage. Excessive oral secretions were also prevalent (n = 71, 54.6%), emphasizing the need for effective secretion management. Anxiety or restlessness was frequently reported (n = 58, 44.6%), reflecting significant psychological distress. Pain affected one-third of PALS (n = 39, 30%), likely due to muscle stiffness and immobility. Cough was reported in one-quarter (n = 33, 25.4%). These findings underscore the need for comprehensive multidisciplinary care that addresses both respiratory and psychological challenges in ALS management.

Use of NIV, IMV, and assisted feeding

A relatively small number of PALS (n = 26, 20%) were using NIV in the form of bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), of whom 4 progressed to IMV in the terminal phase. An additional 12 PALS were managed exclusively with IMV. There was no significant association between the type of onset and the use of NIV or IMV. Overall, 12 of 16 (75%) PALS on IMV were initiated on it in the terminal phase. Fifty-four (25.4%) PALS were on PEG. Among those with bulbar-onset disease, 16 (28.1%) received PEG feeding, 11 (19.3%) used a nasogastric (Ryle’s) tube, and 30 (52.6%) maintained oral intake. By contrast, PALS with limb-onset disease predominantly continued oral feeding (n = 52, 71.2%), with only 4 (5.5%) receiving PEG and 17 (23.3%) using a Ryle’s tube. This difference was statistically significant, as shown by the Pearson Chi-square test (χ² =12.610, df = 2, p = 0.002), indicating that patients with bulbar-onset ALS were considerably more likely to undergo invasive or semi-invasive interventions for nutritional support than those with limb-onset ALS [Table 3].

Discussion

This study provides rare and important insight into terminal events among PALS in India, highlighting both consistencies and differences compared with international research. Very few studies have examined EOL in ALS in India.

Family members played a significant role in caregiving, with over 80% of patients receiving care from a close family member. The majority were children of PALS, followed by spouses, which differs from the previous Indian studies that showed a preponderance of spouses as primary caregivers.[1415] However, within the Indian cultural context, it is common for aging parents to live with their children, and caregiving responsibilities are often shared among family members. This arrangement has positive implications. In one study exploring the wish to die (WTD), lower proportions of PALS expressed WTD compared with Western studies, and strong family support was one of the contributing factors, along with faith in religion, spirituality, and karma.[16] A qualitative study exploring the death and bereavement experiences of family caregivers of PALS in India highlighted that the care of PALS predominantly takes place at home, as also observed in this study, where the majority of PALS (57.7%) died at home.[14] The proportion of patients dying at home varies across countries: 85.2% of Italian, 82% of Chinese, 64% of American and Canadian, 37% of French, 31% of German, and 15% of Japanese cohorts.[5917181920] Cultural and economic factors, as well as the availability of palliative and hospice care support at the community or institutional level, influence the place of death.[1718] One Indian study reported that 77.5% of terminally ill cancer patients preferred to die at home.[21] With the majority of patients dying at home or preferring to die at home, it is important to strengthen home health care services and enhance collaboration with local practitioners to improve EOL care for PALS.[2022] The mean age at death in our cohort is comparable to previous reports from India and China,[418] but lower than that reported in European, Western, and Japanese populations, where the mean age at death ranges from 62 to 72 years.[59171920] An earlier age at disease onset, along with genetic and environmental factors unique to the Indian population, could contribute to differences in disease phenotype and progression.[4] Sudden death occurred in 29.2% of PALS. Studies using a similar definition of sudden death, characterized by rapid and unexpected decline, have reported rates of 32.2% in a Swedish study, 6.04% in an Italian cohort, and 45% in a Japanese series.[172023] This variation may be due to differences in access to multidisciplinary care, which enables regular respiratory and cardiac monitoring, or, as highlighted by Ushikubo and Okamoto, could be due to the family’s inability to recognize clinical deterioration and possible misinterpretation of sudden death.[20] In the absence of medical verification, reports of sudden death should be interpreted cautiously. Respiratory failure remains the major cause of death in ALS, whether sudden or anticipated, as described by Sennfält et al.[23]

Our findings demonstrate a clear association between place of death and the use of IMV, which was often initiated as a late intervention. In India, where there is no common practice of advance care planning and living wills, families may opt for aggressive interventions for their loved ones when faced with life-threatening situations, particularly in hospital settings. In contrast, the lower proportion of IMV among non-hospital deaths reflects limited access to long-term ventilatory support at home.

In this study, the symptom burden in the final stages of life was high, with breathlessness being the most frequently reported symptom (79.2%), consistent with prior findings from Germany, where respiratory distress was a major symptom during the terminal phase of ALS.[10] Similarly, Ganzini et al.[6] showed that dyspnea is prominent during the final month of life. Excessive oral secretions (54.6%) and anxiety or restlessness (44.6%) were also highly prevalent, mirroring findings from a Swedish study that identified secretion management and psychological distress as key EOL concerns.[23] Despite the prevalence of respiratory distress, only 20% of patients were using BiPAP in the terminal phase, which contrasts with Western studies where usage often exceeds 60%–80%.[711] Among our PALS, it was common practice to use nebulization or supplemental oxygen at home to manage breathlessness. International guidelines for ALS care emphasize the importance of early respiratory and nutritional monitoring, along with regular discussions about NIV and PEG.[24] Early communication allows patients and families sufficient time to make informed decisions rather than being compelled to decide during an acute crisis. Cognitive impairment and communication difficulties may emerge as the disease progresses, further complicating the decision-making process. Healthcare professionals (HCPs) play a pivotal role in shaping ALS patients’ decisions about interventions such as NIV or PEG. Patients often rely heavily on their doctors, nurses, and therapists for guidance, reassurance, and expertise.[25] In addition to comprehensive knowledge of the illness, such discussions require serious illness communication skills, a core component of palliative care. Strengthening primary palliative care skills among neurologists and other HCPs involved in motor neuron disease care, along with integrating neuropalliative services into routine neurological practice, may help address these challenges.[26] The low uptake of NIV and PEG among our PALS, even with neuropalliative involvement, may be attributed to multiple factors, including lack of local services or resources or expert guidance, financial constraints, difficulties managing respiratory devices at home, and lower acceptance rates. Despite evidence that NIV and PEG improve QOL and survival, practical challenges often hinder their use in low-resource settings such as India.[27] There is also a lack of specialized ALS clinics and neuropalliative care services in the country. In the absence of adequate supportive care services, families are often insufficiently equipped to manage complex symptoms.[28] Local HCPs may provide symptom management but are frequently inexperienced in managing a rare and heterogeneous condition such as ALS. This highlights a critical gap in care for PALS in the country. Previous research in Western countries has shown that many PALS die peacefully.[510] Flemming, in a review, emphasized the benefits of anticipatory palliative planning, which appeared limited in our cohort.[29] Palliative care services support informed shared decision-making, ensure timely provision of home-based symptom relief measures (e.g., opioids, anticholinergics, suctioning devices), and equip caregivers with anticipatory guidance and emergency support plans. In Western and European contexts, neuropalliative care for ALS is structured, guideline-driven, and multidisciplinary, with early integration from diagnosis shown to improve quality of life and, in some cases, survival. Teams typically include neurologists, nurses, dietitians, physiotherapists, speech and language therapists, respiratory specialists, and palliative care experts, ensuring coordinated interventions such as NIV and gastrostomy, along with psychosocial and spiritual support.[30] India is still at an emerging stage of neuropalliative care. Family caregivers shoulder most of the burden, with psychosocial support largely informal, and cultural sensitivities often delay EOL discussions, contributing to high levels of terminal distress.[14] The NPSC project was initiated to explore the palliative care needs of patients with chronic neurological conditions in the country as an initial step toward addressing neuropalliative care needs. However, much needs to be done nationwide to overcome practical challenges and ensure equitable access to neuropalliative care support for PALS and their families. Integrating trained palliative care nurses and telehealth consultations may help improve the quality of life and dying in PALS.

On a related note, the Supreme Court of India, in its landmark judgment in 2018, recognized that the right to life under Article 21 of the Constitution includes the right to die with dignity and made legal provisions for advance medical directives or living wills. The legal process was further simplified in 2023 to reduce bureaucratic procedures and facilitate the implementation of EOL decisions in terminal stages when the patient is incapacitated to make any decisions and no living will exists to guide treatment.[31] However, cultural barriers and limited awareness among patients and HCPs continue to hinder its implementation.[32] In a disease as heterogeneous as ALS, early integration of palliative care, ideally from the time of diagnosis, is critical for improving symptom management, facilitating advance care planning, supporting families in crucial decision-making, and providing better EOL care.

Strengths of this study are a large, diverse sample and the use of a validated questionnaire administered by trained interviewers, ensuring sensitivity and consistency. However, limitations include the cross-sectional design and reliance on caregiver recall, which may introduce bias. The absence of detailed medical records prevented analysis of the length of hospital stays, co-occurring medical complications, and the cause of death. Telephonic interviews limited clinical verification, and the single-center sample may reduce generalizability. The lack of objective symptom scales and caregiver distress assessments, as well as unexamined factors such as caregiver education and regional health care access, also constrain the findings. Future research incorporating prospective designs, multicenter sampling, and detailed caregiver psychological assessments would strengthen the evidence base and help design more effective EOL care strategies for ALS patients in India.

Conclusions

The findings from this study collectively emphasize the complexity of ALS in its final stages and highlight the need for proactive symptom management, caregiver training, and palliative interventions tailored to individual needs, regardless of the site of care. This study provides valuable insights into terminal events and symptom burden among PALS in India, an area that has been previously underexplored.
