Combined low-volume high-intensity interval training and inspiratory muscle training in patients with metabolic syndrome: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Hilal Uyar, Naciye Vardar-Yagli, Melda Saglam, Ebru Calik, Oguz Abdullah Uyaroglu, Murat Ozdede, Mine Durusu Tanriover
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Low-volume high-intensity interval training (LVHIIT) is known to improve cardiometabolic health; however, the potential additive effects of combining LVHIIT with inspiratory muscle training (IMT) in reducing the severity of metabolic syndrome (MetS) have not been fully explored. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of 8 weeks of IMT combined with LVHIIT on cardiorespiratory fitness, MetS parameters, and respiratory muscle function in individuals with MetS.Methods:This randomized, controlled, double-blind trial included 38 adults with MetS, aged 42 to 60 years, who were randomly assigned to 3 groups: LVHIIT (n = 12), LVHIIT + IMT (n = 14), and control (n = 12). The primary outcome was the change in maximal oxygen consumption (VO2max) after 8 weeks. Secondary outcomes included MetS z-score, anthropometric measures, metabolic blood markers, and respiratory muscle strength and endurance. All outcomes were assessed at baseline and after the 8-week intervention.Results:The mean age of participants was 50 ± 5 years, and 67% were women. Significant group × time interactions were observed for VO2max, oxygen uptake efficiency slope, heart rate recovery at 1 minute, waist circumference, hip circumference, visceral fat ratio, fasting plasma glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin A1c, and MetS z-score (P < .05). Both LVHIIT and LVHIIT + IMT groups showed significant improvements in VO2max (P < .001). Respiratory muscle strength and endurance increased significantly only in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P < .001).Conclusion:Eight weeks of LVHIIT combined with IMT led to improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness, respiratory muscle function, and key MetS components, including MetS z-score. Integrating IMT into LVHIIT may be an effective, clinically feasible, and time-efficient approach to improve cardiometabolic health in individuals with MetS in cardiac rehabilitation.

1. Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) refers to a cluster of interrelated cardiometabolic risk factors, including abdominal obesity, hypertension (HTN), insulin resistance, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and a chronic low-grade pro-inflammatory state. The coexistence of these abnormalities markedly increases the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), several types of cancer, and premature mortality. The development of MetS is mainly attributed to a chronic imbalance between energy intake and expenditure, with physical inactivity, suboptimal dietary habits, and genetic and epigenetic susceptibility playing important contributory roles.[1]

Lifestyle modification, particularly regular physical activity (PA) and structured exercise prescription, is recommended as the first-line strategy for the management of MetS.[2,3] Exercise interventions have consistently demonstrated beneficial effects on cardiometabolic risk in individuals with MetS.[4] Regular exercise contributes to the control of body mass and blood pressure (BP) and improves lipid metabolism by favorably altering triglyceride (TG) and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels.[3] Current PA guidelines recommend at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise (approximately 40%–60% of maximal oxygen consumption [VO2max]) or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity exercise (approximately 60%–85% of VO2max) per week to improve metabolic health.[3] In addition, higher levels of PA and greater cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) are strongly associated with improvements in all components of MetS.[3,4]

Despite the well-established benefits of aerobic exercise for cardiometabolic health and healthy cardiovascular aging, a lack of time is consistently reported as one of the most important barriers to exercise participation and long-term adherence, both in the general population and in clinical settings.[5,6] This limitation has stimulated increasing interest in time-efficient exercise strategies that can elicit meaningful physiological adaptations with a lower total training volume.[7–9] High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has emerged as a promising and increasingly popular alternative. HIIT has been shown to improve body composition, CRF, and several cardiometabolic risk factors, often producing effects comparable to, or even superior to, those achieved with traditional moderate-intensity continuous training, despite requiring substantially less time.[6] HIIT has been associated with significant improvements in lipid profile, glycemic control, systolic and diastolic BP, waist circumference (WC), and body mass and/or body mass index (BMI) in individuals with MetS.[10–12]

More recently, particular attention has been directed toward low-volume high-intensity interval training (LVHIIT) protocols, which are characterized by a markedly reduced total exercise duration while maintaining high exercise intensity. LVHIIT has been proposed as a highly time-efficient strategy to improve CRF and selected cardiometabolic risk factors in individuals with MetS.[5,6]

Inspiratory muscle training (IMT) has also gained attention as a simple and time-efficient non-pharmacological intervention with potential cardiovascular and functional benefits. Previous studies have demonstrated that IMT can improve vascular endothelial function, autonomic regulation, and BP control, thereby potentially reducing overall CVD risk.[7,13,14] Furthermore, studies conducted in different cardiac populations have shown that IMT, applied either as a stand-alone intervention or in combination with conventional exercise-based rehabilitation, can improve exercise capacity and respiratory muscle function, including in patients with heart failure (HF) and in individuals with cardiac implantable devices.[15–19] These findings suggest that IMT may represent a valuable adjunct to exercise training in populations characterized by elevated cardiometabolic risk. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the effects of IMT specifically in individuals with MetS remains scarce. To date, only 1 randomized controlled study has investigated IMT in older women with MetS and reported improvements in pulmonary function, autonomic modulation, and selected hemodynamic variables.[20] Consequently, the effects of IMT on CRF, metabolic outcomes, and respiratory muscle performance in broader and mixed-sex MetS populations remain largely unknown.

While LVHIIT has proven effective in improving CRF and cardiometabolic risk profiles in individuals with MetS[5–7] and IMT has yielded favorable outcomes in several cardiac and metabolic disorders, no randomized controlled trial has yet evaluated the combined impact of LVHIIT and IMT on cardiometabolic and functional outcomes in patients with MetS. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial aimed to determine whether combining IMT with an LVHIIT program would result in greater improvements in CRF, metabolic blood parameters, and respiratory muscle function compared with LVHIIT alone in individuals with MetS. We hypothesized that combining LVHIIT with IMT would lead to superior improvements in respiratory muscle function, CRF, and selected metabolic parameters compared with LVHIIT alone.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and procedures

This interventional, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial was conducted from April 2022 to March 2023 in the Cardiopulmonary Rehabilitation Unit at the Hacettepe University, Faculty of Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation. The trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (Identifier: NCT05413434; June 10, 2022).

Group allocation was performed by an independent researcher who was not involved in outcome assessment or intervention delivery. On day 1, blood sampling, anthropometric measurements, and cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) were conducted. On day 2, physical fitness and respiratory muscle strength tests were performed. The primary and secondary outcome measures were assessed at baseline (week 0) and after the intervention (week 8).

All assessments and interventions were conducted at the Cardiopulmonary Rehabilitation Unit of Hacettepe University, Faculty of Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation (Ankara, Türkiye). No important changes to the trial methods were made after study commencement, including eligibility criteria, outcomes, or intervention procedures. The study flow diagram is shown in Figure 1. The study design and reporting were conducted in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for randomized controlled trials.

Flow diagram of the study. LVHIIT = low-volume high-intensity interval training, LVHIIT + IMT = low-volume high-intensity interval training combined with inspiratory muscle training.

2.2. Randomization

A computer-based random-number (https://www.sealedenvelope.com) generator was used for stratified block randomization by sex. The participants were randomized into 3 groups after the assessment period as follows: LVHIIT (n = 12), LVHIIT combined with IMT (n = 14), and control (n = 12). The allocation sequence was concealed using a web-based system (SealedEnvelope, Sealed Envelope Limited, London, England, UK) and was not accessible to the investigators responsible for assessment or enrollment. The random allocation sequence was generated by an independent researcher who was not involved in data collection or intervention delivery. Participants were enrolled by the clinical team, and group assignments were automatically provided through the SealedEnvelope system.

2.3. Blinding

In this double-blind trial, participants were blinded to their group assignment (LVHIIT, LVHIIT + IMT, or control), and outcome assessments and exercise sessions were conducted at distinct time periods to reduce the risk of contamination between groups. The same physiotherapist performed both the assessments and the exercise interventions. Before statistical analyses, group assignments were coded, and the biostatistician remained blinded to group identity.

2.4. Eligibility criteria

Participants who met the following criteria were included: having a MetS diagnosis based on the National Cholesterol Education Program–Adult Treatment Panel III criteria[20]; being between 18 and 65 years of age; not having recently participated in a scheduled exercise program; being clinically stable with no changes in medical therapy in the 2 months before the study; and having no recent cardiac events, HF, renal failure, peripheral vascular disease, or exercise intolerance for any reason, and no cognitive impairment that would interfere with participation.[6,20,21] In addition, participants were not following any specific dietary program and maintained the same dietary habits for the past 3 months.

2.5. Sample size calculation

A power analysis using G*Power (v3.1.9.4; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany) indicated that 33 participants were required to detect significant changes in VO2max, based on a previous study in patients with MetS, with 80% power and α = 0.05.[22] To account for potential dropouts (~15%), 38 participants were recruited. No interim analyses or stopping guidelines were planned for this trial.

2.6. Primary outcomes

2.6.1. Cardiopulmonary exercise testing

CRF was evaluated by CPET on a treadmill (Cosmed Quark CPET, Rome, Italy) using the modified Bruce protocol. VO2max was recorded as both mL/min and mL/kg/min. Percentages of predicted values were calculated automatically by the system software using Wasserman’s equations.[23]

2.7. Secondary outcomes

2.7.1. Blood sampling

Blood samples were obtained after 8 to 12 hours of fasting to measure TG, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), HDL-C, total cholesterol (TC), fasting plasma glucose (FPG), C-reactive protein, and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Blood samples were obtained 1 week before and 1 week after the study using standardized procedures.

2.7.2. Anthropometric and body composition measurements

A Mi Body Composition Scale 2 (Anhui Huami Information Technology CO., Ltd., Anhui, China) was used for body composition analysis.[24] Although the Mi Body Composition Scale 2 is not a gold-standard method, its validity and clinical usability have been shown. The participants’ height (cm), body mass (kg), WC (cm), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR), BMI (kg/m2), visceral fat ratio (VFR, %), body fat percentage (%), fat-free mass (FFM, kg), and were recorded.

2.7.3. Resting BP measurement

After resting for 5 minutes, BP was measured on the right upper arm using a validated automatic BP monitor (BP710, Omron, Tokyo, Japan). A total of 3 measurements were taken, with at least 2 minutes between readings. The mean systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) values were recorded.

2.7.4. Determination of MetS score

MetS z-scores were determined using separate equations for male and female patients and were based on mean arterial BP, WC, and serum blood glucose, TG, and HDL-C levels.[25]

2.7.5. Respiratory muscle strength

Maximal inspiratory and expiratory pressures (MIP/MEP) were measured in accordance with the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines using a mouth-pressure device (MicroRPM; Micromedical, Kent, United Kingdom). The highest values at peak inspiration and peak expiration from 3 trials with <10% variation were recorded as MIP and MEP, respectively. Respiratory muscle endurance was evaluated using a PowerBreathe Classic device (HaB International Ltd., Southam, UK) through a constant threshold loading method. Participants were instructed to breathe against a fixed inspiratory load equivalent to 50% of their initial MIP and to continue until task failure. The endurance outcome was defined as the total time sustained under these conditions.[26]

2.7.6. Physical fitness tests

Physical fitness was assessed in terms of lower-limb functional strength and flexibility. To assess lower-limb strength, participants performed the 1-minute sit-to-stand test (1-MSTST). In this test, the participant was seated in an armless chair with its back against a wall. With their arms crossed over their chests, they were asked to stand up and sit back down as many times as possible within 1 minute. The number of stand/sit cycles completed during this time was recorded as the 1-MSTST score.[27] Flexibility, which is essential for effectively performing activities of daily living, was assessed using the sit-and-reach and functional reach tests (FRTs).[28,29]

2.8. Interventions

Participants in both the LVHIIT and LVHIIT + IMT groups completed an 8-week treadmill-based aerobic exercise program, performed 3 days per week and supervised by the same expert physiotherapist with experience in cardiac rehabilitation. Rate of perceived exertion and heart rate (HR) were monitored continuously and recorded in each training session. LVHIIT sessions were 26 minutes in duration, consisting of a 5-minute warm-up (30%–50% of VO2max), followed by 6 1-minute high-intensity (90%–100% of VO2max) intervals, separated by 2-minute active recovery periods (50%–70% of VO2max) and concluding with a 5-minute cool-down period (30%–50% of VO2max; Fig. 2). This protocol was based on that developed by Gallo-Villegas et al.[7]

Low-volume high-intensity interval training protocol for the intervention.

The LVHIIT + IMT group performed IMT using a Powerbreathe Classic–Light Resistance device (HaB International Ltd., Southam, UK) in addition to the HIIT sessions. Participants in this group performed 60 inspiratory maneuvers twice a day for 8 weeks (6 sets of 10 repetitions with 1 minute of rest between sets). Training intensity targeted 50% of MIP and was maintained by reassessing MIP during supervised sessions and adjusting for improvements in inspiratory muscle function over the course of the intervention. IMT sessions were supervised 3 times each week to ensure participants maintained proper technique. The remaining sessions were performed at home without supervision, and patients were asked to keep daily records.[13]

The control group received standardized recommendations regarding appropriate PA and lifestyle modifications after the baseline assessments. These recommendations were reinforced every 2 weeks by telephone during the 8-week follow-up. The PA recommendations provided to the control group were based on current public health guidelines and included at least 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, such as brisk walking, cycling, or swimming.[2] Participants were also advised to incorporate lifestyle modifications such as adopting a balanced diet, maintaining a healthy body weight, reducing sedentary behavior, and avoiding smoking. These recommendations were verbally reinforced every 2 weeks via telephone follow-up. No structured or supervised exercise training was applied to the control group during the study period. The participants in the control group were not enrolled in any exercise program. At the end of the study, patients in the control group were offered the opportunity to participate in the combined IMT and LVHIIT intervention.

2.8.1. Safety monitoring

Adverse events were monitored throughout the study period. During each supervised exercise session, the physiotherapist monitored participants for signs of adverse events. Participants were also instructed to immediately report any symptoms, discomfort, or unusual events experienced during or after training sessions. No serious adverse events were reported throughout the study.

2.9. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24 (IBM Corp., Armonk). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Changes from baseline are presented with 95% confidence intervals, where appropriate. Baseline characteristics were compared among the LVHIIT, LVHIIT + IMT, and control groups using 1-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. A 2-way repeated-measures analysis of variance was used to examine the main effects of time, group, and time × group interaction. When significant interactions or main effects were observed, post hoc analyses were performed to explore within- and between-group differences. To reduce the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, Bonferroni-adjusted significance thresholds were applied for post hoc analyses. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

Eighty-eight individuals with MetS were assessed for eligibility; 38 declined to participate in the study, and 12 were excluded based on eligibility criteria. The remaining 38 participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 3 groups: the LVHIIT group (n = 12), the LVHIIT + IMT group (n = 14), or the control group (n = 12). One participant withdrew from the study during the intervention period. Therefore, a total of 37 participants completed the study and were included in the final analyses. The study groups were comparable in terms of physical and clinical characteristics at baseline (Table 1). Post hoc power analysis was carried out on our VO2max (mL/kg/min) data (d = 0.61, α = 0.05 [two-tailed]). Power (1−β) was 91%.

Although significant interaction effects were detected, post hoc comparisons did not reveal statistically significant between-group differences after Bonferroni correction. Within-group comparisons showed significant reductions in body weight in the LVHIIT group (P = .009) and in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P < .001), whereas a significant reduction in BMI was observed only in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P < .001). WC (P < .001 for both groups) and hip circumference (P = .011 and P < .001 for the LVHIIT and LVHIIT + IMT groups, respectively) significantly decreased, whereas no significant changes were observed in the control group (P > .05). VFR (%) significantly decreased in both the LVHIIT (P = .026) and LVHIIT + IMT groups (P = .008), with no significant change in the control group (P = .339). No significant within-group changes were found for FFM or WHR values (P > .05).

3.2. Cardiometabolic risk markers

Significant main effects of time and significant group × time interactions were observed for FPG (P = .028 and P = .010, respectively), HbA1c (P = .041 and P = .018, respectively), resting DBP (P = .001 and P = .040, respectively), and MetS z-score (P = .001 for both). A significant group × time interaction was also found for TC (P = .013), whereas no significant main effect of time was observed. No significant group × time interactions were found for C-reactive protein, LDL-C, HDL-C, TG, or resting SBP (P > .05; Table 2). Within-group analyses showed significant reductions in resting DBP in the LVHIIT and LVHIIT + IMT groups (both P < .001), with no significant change in the control group (P = .609). Similarly, FPG levels decreased significantly in the LVHIIT group (P = .038) and in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P = .006), but not in the control group (P = .381). HbA1c levels were also significantly reduced in the LVHIIT group (P = .012) and the LVHIIT + IMT group (P = .006), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (P = .381).

3.3. Respiratory functions

Table 2 shows the effectiveness of different exercise protocols on respiratory function outcomes within groups, over time, and for time × group interactions. A significant main effect of time and a significant time × group interaction were observed for MIP (P = .003 and P = .049, respectively), %MIPpred (P = .004 and P = .001, respectively), MEP (P = .023 and P = .038, respectively), and respiratory muscle endurance (P = .011 and P = .001, respectively). Neither a significant main effect of time nor a time × group interaction was observed for forced expiratory volume in 1 second (%), forced vital capacity (%), or %MEPpred values (P > .05, Table 2). Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni adjustment revealed no significant between-group differences in respiratory function parameters.

Based on within-group comparisons, significant improvements in MIP and %MIPpred levels were observed only in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P < .001 and P = .001, respectively), whereas no significant changes were found in the LVHIIT or control groups (P > .05). Similarly, significant increases in MEP and %MEPpred levels were detected only in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P = .014 and P = .010, respectively), while no significant within-group changes were observed in the LVHIIT and control groups (P > .05). In addition, respiratory muscle endurance increased significantly in the LVHIIT + IMT group (P < .001), whereas no significant changes were found in the LVHIIT group (P = .911) or in the control group (P = .367).

3.4. Physical fitness tests

Although there was no significant group × time interaction for STR (P = .150), significant group × time interactions were observed for the 1-MSTST score (P = .033) and FRT (P = .004; Table 2). Within-group analyses showed that only the LVHIIT + IMT group had a significant improvement in the 1-MSTST score (P < .001), while only the LVHIIT group had a significant improvement in FRT (P = .012).

3.5. Cardiorespiratory fitness

Table 3 shows the effectiveness of different exercise protocols on CPET parameters within groups, over time, and in time-by-group interactions. A significant main effect of time and a significant group × time interaction were observed for VO2max (mL/min; P < .001 and P = .041, respectively), VO2max (mL/kg/min; P < .001 and P = .005, respectively), heart rate recovery at 1 minute (HRR1min) (bpm; P < .001 and P = .022, respectively), and oxygen uptake efficiency slope (OUES) (mL/min/L/min; P = .003 and P = .020, respectively; Table 3). Post hoc analyses with Bonferroni adjustment revealed no significant between-group differences for CPET parameters. Within-group comparisons showed significant increases in VO2max (mL/min) in the LVHIIT (P = .007) and LVHIIT + IMT groups (P = .012), and in VO2max (mL/kg/min) in the LVHIIT (P = .001) and LVHIIT + IMT groups (P = .002), whereas no significant changes were observed in the control group (P > .05; Fig. 3). Within-group comparisons showed significant improvements in OUES (mL/min/L/min) in the LVHIIT (P = .028) and LVHIIT + IMT (P = .006) groups, whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (P = .657). Similarly, HRR1min (bpm) improved significantly in the LVHIIT group (P = .006) and the LVHIIT + IMT group (P = .001), while no significant within-group change was found in the control group (P = .095). A significant main effect of time was observed for peak HR (bpm; P < .001), O2 pulse (mL/beat; P = .012), minute ventilation (L/min; P = .012), respiratory exchange ratio (P = .000), and ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide (VE/VCO2) slope (P = .043; Table 3). Although a significant main effect of time was observed for VE/VCO2 slope (P = .043), the time × group interaction was not statistically significant (P > .05; Table 3). A post hoc comparison showed a difference between LVHIIT + IMT and control groups; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, given the nonsignificant interaction effect.

3.6. Patients adherence and adverse effects

During the intervention, 1 participant in the LVHIIT + IMT group withdrew from the study due to knee pain. Therefore, the analysis included 37 participants (LVHIIT n = 12, LVHIIT + IMT n = 13, and control group n = 12). No serious adverse events were observed during or after training, and all participants completed at least 21 (87.5%) of the 24 training sessions.

4. Discussion

This randomized controlled trial investigated the combined effects of LVHIIT and IMT on cardiometabolic and respiratory outcomes in individuals with MetS. The findings indicate that LVHIIT significantly improved CRF and several metabolic parameters, while the addition of IMT resulted in further improvements in respiratory muscle strength and endurance. These results partially support our hypothesis and suggest that IMT may serve as a complementary intervention to optimize respiratory muscle performance in this population. Importantly, our study demonstrates that LVHIIT is a time-efficient and accessible approach to achieving significant health benefits for patients with MetS, potentially facilitating the development of integrated cardiac rehabilitation protocols for this population.

Enhancing CRF in patients with MetS could help address the mismatch between the increased oxygen demand of expanded adipose tissue and the available blood supply.[30] Preventing this imbalance could reduce the occurrence of hypoxia-induced adipose tissue necrosis, thereby mitigating downstream effects such as inflammation, insulin resistance, and oxidative stress – key contributors to the clustering of cardiometabolic risk factors observed in MetS.[30] It is widely recognized that VO2max is a crucial determinant of future CVD risk and overall mortality in MetS.[3,31] Recent evidence suggests that improvements in CRF through exercise training positively influence the underlying factors of MetS.[3] A mean increase of 1 mL/kg/min in VO2max is estimated to be associated with a 9% reduction in CVD mortality risk.[3] According to Wilkinson et al, an improvement of approximately 2.0 mL/kg/min (~19%) in VO2max represents the proposed minimum clinically important difference for populations with comorbidities.[32] Two previous studies examining the effects of LVHIIT interventions in patients with MetS reported increases in VO2max of approximately 15% (3.1 mL/kg/min) and 6% (1.8 mL/kg/min) after 12 and 16 weeks of training, respectively.[5,6] Similarly, Madsen et al observed a significant improvement in VO2max (3.0 mL/kg/min, ~13%) in patients with T2DM and obesity following an 8-week low-volume HIIT protocol consisting of 3 sessions per week, each involving 10 1-minute intervals at 90% HRmax.[33] Improvements in VO2max (4.3 mL/kg/min, ~28%) observed in both intervention groups exceeded the proposed minimum clinically important difference reported for populations with cardiometabolic comorbidities. Given that CRF is a well-established surrogate marker of cardiovascular risk and mortality, these short-term improvements may have clinically relevant implications. However, caution is warranted when extrapolating short-term changes in surrogate outcomes to long-term cardiovascular events, and future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm sustained clinical benefits.

Notably, the total exercise duration in our study was approximately 78 minutes per week – about 30% lower than the average exercise volumes reported in previous studies, typically ranging from 51 to 113 minutes per week (including warm-up and cool-down periods). Furthermore, the 8-week intervention period in our LVHIIT protocol was approximately 50% to 60% shorter than those used in earlier research. Considering that a perceived lack of time is a major barrier to adherence to regular exercise, our findings highlight that LVHIIT, with reduced overall time commitment, remains effective in enhancing CRF in individuals with MetS.

Beyond VO2max, favorable adaptations were observed in submaximal and ventilatory efficiency indices, including OUES and HRR1min. This is clinically relevant, as individuals with MetS typically present with reduced OUES and VO2 at the ventilatory threshold, together with an increased VE/VCO2 slope, reflecting impaired ventilatory efficiency and cardiovascular–respiratory coupling.[34] These parameters reflect cardiopulmonary integration and autonomic recovery, and may provide complementary insight into functional improvements not fully captured by maximal indices alone. Although improvements in VE/VCO2 slope were observed over time, the interaction effect between groups was not statistically significant, suggesting that further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify potential additive effects of IMT on ventilatory efficiency.[35]

Feriani et al demonstrated that 7 days of IMT significantly improved respiratory muscle function, cardiac autonomic modulation, and SBP levels in older women with MetS and concluded that IMT is an effective non-pharmacological strategy.[20] In a study investigating combined IMT and HIIT in patients with HF, Hornikx et al suggested that incorporating IMT into HIIT could attenuate the inspiratory muscle metaboreflex, thereby enhancing peripheral blood flow during exercise and leading to greater improvements in exercise capacity.[17] Their findings indicated that both HIIT alone and HIIT combined with IMT were equally effective in improving overall exercise capacity in HF, although the HIIT combined with IMT group exhibited greater gains in both peripheral and inspiratory muscle strength.[17] Muammer et al reported that 6 weeks of IMT improved respiratory function, enhanced exercise capacity, and favorably altered biochemical markers in patients with coronary artery disease and MetS.[36] In our study, baseline MIP values were within normal limits according to the American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines.[26] Consistent with previous findings, the mean MIP and MEP increased significantly by 13% and 16%, respectively, following the HIIT + IMT intervention. Additionally, respiratory muscle endurance increased by 138% in the LVHIIT + IMT group. Although VO2max increased comparably in both intervention groups, clinical improvements in respiratory muscle performance were slightly more pronounced in the LVHIIT + IMT group. The additional improvements in respiratory muscle strength observed in the LVHIIT + IMT group may be explained by specific adaptations of the inspiratory muscles. Previous studies in other cardiometabolic populations have suggested that IMT may attenuate the inspiratory muscle metaboreflex and improve autonomic regulation. However, these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study and therefore remain speculative.

Reljic et al demonstrated that very low-volume HIIT interventions significantly improved cardiometabolic markers and liver health in patients with MetS.[37] Additionally, a recent meta-analysis by Yin et al supports the time efficiency and cardiometabolic benefits of low-volume HIIT across diverse populations.[38] Wiley and Carrington reported that a reduction of 0.15 in the MetS z-score corresponds to approximately a 10% improvement in a single MetS component and indicates a clinically meaningful reduction in MetS severity and subsequent CVD risk.[25] In the present study, we observed a significant mean decline of 4.2 units in the MetS z-score following LVHIIT and LVHIIT + IMT interventions, which notably exceeds the mean declines reported by Ramos et al (−1.6 units) and Reljic et al (−1.8 units).[5,6] Another study demonstrated that each standard deviation increase in the MetS z-score at a mean age of 38 years was associated with a sevenfold increase in CVD risk and a 5.2-fold increase in T2DM risk by the age of 50.[39] A substantial reduction in MetS z-score was observed following both intervention protocols. While this finding indicates an overall improvement in MetS severity, the magnitude of change observed in this study was greater than that reported in previous trials. Differences in calculation methods, baseline characteristics, and intervention protocols may partly explain this variation. Therefore, replication in larger, multicenter cohorts is necessary before drawing firm conclusions regarding the magnitude of metabolic improvement.

A reduction of 5 mm Hg in DBP or 10 mm Hg in SBP has been associated with a 22% lower risk of CVD and a 41% lower risk of stroke.[40] In addition, a 10% reduction in WC has been associated with an approximately 1.5-fold reduction in mortality risk.[40] In the present study, mean reductions in SBP, DBP, and WC were approximately 15 mm Hg, 13 mm Hg, and 3 cm, respectively, in the HIIT + IMT group and approximately 13 mm Hg, 14 mm Hg, and 4 cm, respectively, in the HIIT group. These findings reflect short-term favorable changes in key cardiometabolic risk markers.

However, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for SBP. The potential mechanisms underlying these improvements may be related to modulation of the autonomic nervous system, as suggested by previous studies conducted in other populations.[14,20,41]

Alvarez et al reported that FPG, HbA1c, and TG levels decreased by 19.8 ± 1.9 mg/dL, 0.9 ± 0.1%, and 22.9 ± 3.4 mg/dL, respectively, following 12 weeks of LVHIIT in patients with T2DM.[42] Although no significant changes were observed in TC and LDL-C, HDL-C levels increased by 10.1 ± 1.1 mg/dL. In contrast, Reljic et al[6] reported no significant changes in markers of glucose or lipid metabolism after a similar 12-week LVHIIT intervention.

In the present study, both the LVHIIT and LVHIIT + IMT groups showed significant improvements in markers of glucose metabolism, reflected by reductions in FPG and HbA1c. However, no significant group × time interactions were detected for lipid parameters, including TGs, LDL-C, HDL-C, and no between-group differences were observed for these parameters. Within-group analyses showed a significant reduction in TC only in the LVHIIT + IMT group, whereas no significant change was detected in the LVHIIT group. Although reductions in TG and LDL-C levels were observed in the intervention groups, these changes did not reach statistical significance at the interaction level. Nevertheless, the reductions in TG (−36 mg/dL and −53 mg/dL, respectively) exceeded the proposed minimal clinically important difference (8 mg/dL).[43] Although improvements in glucose metabolism were statistically significant, lipid parameters did not demonstrate consistent between-group differences. This may suggest that longer intervention durations or higher training volumes are required to induce substantial changes in lipid metabolism.

The improvements in glycaemic control observed with both exercise protocols may, at least in part, be related to exercise-induced adaptations in skeletal muscle. Previous studies conducted in other populations have demonstrated that exercise can reduce 24-hour glucose concentrations and increase the protein content and nuclear translocation of glucose transporter type 4, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α, and mitochondrial oxidative enzymes.[44] However, as these molecular mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study, these interpretations should be considered indirect and based on evidence from previous studies.

Furthermore, the absence of significant group × time interaction effects for HDL-C suggests that longer intervention periods or different exercise volumes may be required to achieve more consistent improvements in the lipid profile.

5. Study limitations and strengths

This study has some limitations. The first limitation of this study was that the sample consisted predominantly of women, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to male patients with MetS. Another important limitation is the absence of an IMT-only group, which precludes determination of the independent effects of IMT. Finally, the relatively short intervention period and lack of long-term follow-up prevent conclusions regarding the sustainability of the observed adaptations.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to investigate the combined effects of LVHIIT and IMT on both metabolic and respiratory outcomes in individuals with MetS. Additional strengths include the use of standardized and clinically applicable exercise and IMT protocols, the stability of medication use before and during the intervention period, and the double-blind design of the study. Moreover, the sample size was determined a priori by power analysis, and appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison procedures were applied, supporting the robustness of the statistical findings.

6. Conclusion

In conclusion, an 8-week LVHIIT program produced clinically meaningful improvements in CRF and metabolic parameters in individuals with MetS. The addition of IMT further enhanced respiratory muscle performance and was associated with improvements in selected CPET-derived indices. These findings suggest that combining LVHIIT with IMT may represent a feasible and time-efficient strategy to enhance cardiopulmonary function in individuals with MetS. However, larger, multicenter trials with longer follow-up periods are needed to determine the long-term clinical impact of this combined approach.
