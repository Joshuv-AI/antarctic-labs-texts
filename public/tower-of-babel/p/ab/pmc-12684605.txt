Population‐Based Study on the Coexistence of Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Steatotic Liver Disease and Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Rachel Sze Jen Goh, Jaycie Koh, Made Ayu Utami Intaran, Yiphan Chin, Gwyneth Kong, Bryan Chong, Jobelle Chia, Mark Y Chan, Anurag Mehta, Mark Muthiah, Muhammad Shahzeb Khan, Nicholas WS Chew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are important cardiovascular risk factors. However, the prognostic impact of coexisting MASLD and CKD remains understudied.MethodsThis study cohort used the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) 2007 to 2018 database, examining outcomes in adults with varying MASLD and CKD statuses. The primary outcome was all‐cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included coronary heart disease, heart failure, stroke, and cancer. Cox regression model was constructed to investigate the relationship between MASLD/CKD and all‐cause mortality, adjusted for age, prior coronary heart disease, body mass index, smoking, poverty‐to‐income ratio, lipid‐lowering and glucose‐lowering medications. Sensitivity analysis was performed with hepatic fibrosis and CKD.ResultsAmong 14 818 participants (mean follow‐up: 6.9 ± 3.4 years), a majority of participants had MASLD(−)/CKD(−) (50.8%), followed by MASLD(+)/CKD(−) (34.8%), MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (7.7%), and MASLD(−)/CKD(+) (6.7%). MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (n = 1142) had the highest rates of obesity (77.6%), hypertension (77.5%), dyslipidemia (67.0%), and diabetes (49.7%), with the highest risk of coronary heart disease (risk ratio [RR], 1.79 [95% CI, 1.13–2.82],P = 0.013) and heart failure (RR 2.33 [95% CI, 1.07–5.08], P = 0.033). Socioeconomic disparities were observed, with lower‐income individuals predominantly in the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (P < 0.001). MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 3.28 [95% CI, 1.89–5.70], P < 0.001) and MASLD(−)/CKD(+) (aHR, 2.18 [95% CI, 1.33–3.66], P = 0.002) phenotypes were independent mortality predictors. Although MASLD(−)/CKD(+) and MASLD(+)/CKD(+) had unfavorable 10‐year prognoses, survival was worse in those with both hepatic fibrosis and CKD (P < 0.001).ConclusionsMASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotype increases the risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity and independently predicts mortality. Mortality risk increased progressively in individuals with both advanced hepatic fibrosis and CKD.

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are the leading cause of mortality, accounting for 32% of all global deaths in 2019, 1 with the CVD epidemic anticipated to rise by 2‐fold from 2025 to 2050. 2 , 3 Although standard modifiable risk factors, including high body mass index (BMI), hyperlipidemia, hypertension, smoking, and type 2 diabetes (T2D) contribute to the majority of the global CVD burden, 4 approximately 42.8% to 47.4% of residual cardiovascular risk is attributed to other underrecognized risk factors.

Metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are chronic conditions estimated to affect 32.4% and 13.4% of the global population respectively, 5 , 6 both of which are contributors of premature cardiovascular‐related deaths. 6 , 7 In silos, early stage CKD increases the risk of incident CVD and precedes end‐stage kidney failure 8 , 9 ; on the other hand, MASLD is associated with increased incident CVD 10 and is a driver of new onset CKD 11 , 12 and metabolic dysfunction. 13 Kidney and liver dysfunction perturbs the maintenance of homeostasis systemically, and has a deleterious impact on the cardiovascular system. 14

There is increasing attention on the interorgan crosstalk between the heart, kidneys, and liver, with evidence demonstrating the bidirectional and synergistic relationship between MASLD and CKD. The 2023 scientific statement on the proposed definition of cardiovascular‐kidney‐metabolic syndrome 15 and the 2024 consensus on the cardiovascular‐liver‐metabolic health 16 have provided much insight into this topic. The coexistence of MASLD and CKD has been associated with increased cardiovascular events and mortality across several cohort studies in both Eastern 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 and Western 12 , 21 populations, compared with MASLD or CKD in silos. However, there remains a paucity of data on the long‐term survival, as well as the social correlates, across the 4 MASLD‐CKD phenotypes in a real‐world community‐based setting. Although key mediators of survival such as obesity, T2D, and sex have been described in the individual populations with MASLD 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 and CKD 26 , 27 , 28 , little is known about their mediation effects on survival in the population with coexistent MASLD and CKD. Moreover, it is unclear if the synergistic effect of MASLD and CKD on cardiovascular outcomes is driven by MASLD or by its more severe form, advanced hepatic fibrosis. To address these gaps, this study has 3 specific aims: (1) to examine the clinical and social correlates across the MASLD and CKD phenotypes; (2) to evaluate the prognostic impact of coexistent MASLD and CKD on cardiovascular outcomes and mortality, as well as the mediation effects of obesity, T2D, and sex on survival; and (3) to examine the dose–response relationship across the MASLD severity stages and mortality. 12 Thus the study findings can inform the prioritization of liver‐ and kidney‐related biomarkers for risk stratification and guide primary and secondary preventative strategies in higher risk populations to mitigate CVD and mortality in this comorbid context.

METHODS

Study Design and Population

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The data used in this study were extracted from NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) 2007 to 2018, a health and nutritional database of adults and children in the United States. 29 The NHANES is a prospective cohort study of adults aged ≥18 years and is a nationally representative study conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention in the United States. NHANES is approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided informed consent. The study design has been described previously (www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about_nhanes.htm). The longitudinal data on death and death date were identified through linkage to the National Death Index. 30 Follow‐up duration was derived from the date of examination until the date of death or the end of the study (December 31, 2019). The mortality causes were classified based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD).

Study Definitions

The study inclusion criteria were adults, aged ≥18 years, with complete data on MASLD and CKD status. Participants with missing MASLD or CKD status, missing mortality data, or age <18 years were excluded from the study analysis (Figure S1). Individuals with significant alcohol consumption—defined as >2 drinks per day for men and >1 drink per day for women—were also excluded from the study. 31

In accordance with the 2023 Delphi Consensus, MASLD was defined as the evidence of hepatic steatosis and the presence of at least 1 of 5 cardiometabolic criteria: (1) BMI ≥23 kg/m2 (Asian) or ≥25 kg/m2 (White), or waist‐circumference >94 cm for men or >80 cm for women; (2) fasting plasma glucose ≥5.6 mmol/L, or 2‐hour postload glucose ≥7.8 mmol/L or hemoglobin A1C ≥5.7%, or T2D, or on glucose‐lowering treatment; (3) hypertension (blood pressure ≥130/80 mm Hg from 3 readings or on antihypertensive treatment); (4) hypertriglyceridemia (plasma triglyceride ≥1.7 mmol/L or lipid‐lowering treatment) and (5) plasma high‐density lipoprotein ≤1.0 mmol/L for men or ≤1.3 mmol/L for women, or lipid‐lowering treatment. 32 As liver biopsy data were unavailable, Fatty Liver Index was used for the definition of hepatic steatosis, with the threshold of ≥60 determining the presence of hepatic steatosis. This has been externally validated in population studies with ultrasound‐proven hepatic steatosis, 33 , 34 with a sensitivity of 61%, specificity of 86%, and positive likelihood ratio of 4.3. 35 The equation 35 used was Fatty Liver Index=(e0.953×loge (triglycerides [mg/dL])+0.139×BMI [kg/m2]+0.718×loge (gamma‐glutamyl‐transferase [U/L])+0.053×waist circumference [cm]–15.745)/(1+e0.953×loge (triglycerides [mg/dL])+0.139×BMI [kg/m2]+0.718×loge (gamma‐glutamyl‐transferase [U/L])+0.053×waist circumference [cm]–15.745)×100. Advanced hepatic fibrosis was determined based on the serum‐based Fibrosis‐4 Index (FIB‐4), which includes alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, age, and platelet count. The formula 36 used was FIB‐4=age [years]×aspartate aminotransferase [IU/L]/(platelet [×109/L]×alanine aminotransferase [IU/L]1/2). A FIB‐4 threshold of ≥2.67 was defined as advanced fibrosis, with a 80% positive predictive value and 83% negative predictive value. 37 , 38

CKD was defined based on the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes definition, which encompasses kidney damage for >3 months, defined by structural or functional abnormalities of the kidney with or without decreased glomerular filtration rate (GFR), manifesting by either (1) pathologic abnormalities; (2) markers of kidney damage, including abnormalities in the composition of blood or urine, or abnormalities on imaging (3) GFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for ≥3 months with or without kidney damage; or (4) presence of albuminuria defined as urine albumin–creatinine ratio >30 mg/g. 39 The estimated GFR was calculated according to the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration 2021 formula. 39 An estimated GFR of <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, urine albumin–creatinine ratio >30 mg/g, or individuals on dialysis were used to define CKD. A full list of all other study definitions is appended (Table S1).

Participants were stratified into 4 groups based on presence of MASLD and CKD: without MASLD and CKD [MASLD(−)/CKD(−)], with MASLD and without CKD [MASLD(+)/CKD(−)], without MASLD and with CKD [MASLD(−)/CKD(+)], and with MASLD and CKD [MASLD(+)/CKD(+)].

Socioeconomic correlates were examined using the poverty‐to‐income ratio (PIR), and categorized into low (PIR <1), middle (1≤PIR≤4), and high income (PIR >4). 40 , 41 The ratio is determined by the annual family income relative to federal poverty level, with 1 representing the poverty level, and PIR <1 demonstrating that the household income is below the poverty line. The PIR is comparable across survey years as income thresholds were annually updated for inflation. 42 The thresholds selected for middle‐ and high‐income individuals were in accordance with the Patient Protection Affordable Care act, in which individuals with PIR between 1 and 4 were eligible for insurance subsidies, whereas those with PIR >4 were not eligible for subsidies. 40

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was all‐cause mortality during follow‐up. Secondary outcomes were coronary heart disease (CHD), heart failure, stroke, and cancer. All‐cause mortality was categorized as any death regardless of cause. These outcomes were adjudicated based on previous primary and secondary prevention studies on cardiovascular‐kidney‐liver‐metabolic health, from prior NHANES studies 43 , 44 , 45 as well as other study cohorts. 19 , 21 , 46 , 47 In the NHANES study, mortality was the longitudinal study outcome, and data on CHD, heart failure, stroke, and cancer were obtained at baseline.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted on R 4.4.1 software in RStudio. Continuous variables were presented as mean±SD, and categorical variables as number (%). In order to examine the clinical and social correlates across the MASLD and CKD phenotypes (aim 1), continuous variables were compared by ANOVA, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson chi‐square test. Next, to examine the prognostic impact of coexistent MASLD and CKD phenotypes (aim 2), Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed for long‐term all‐cause mortality stratified by MASLD/CKD status, with sex subgroup analysis performed, and compared using the log‐rank test. Further subgroup analysis of participants based on fibrosis/CKD status, T2D status, sex, and obesity were performed.

Cox proportional hazard regression model was used to investigate the relationship between MASLD/CKD and all‐cause mortality, adjusted for age, prior CHD, BMI, smoking, PIR, lipid‐lowering drugs, and glucose‐lowering medications. These covariates included in the Cox regression model were adjudicated based on their significant associations with mortality in individuals with cardiovascular‐kidney‐liver‐metabolic diseases, as described in prior studies. 12 , 17 , 48 , 49 The final model selection underwent a test of proportionality, conducted with the Grambsch–Therneau test for a nonzero slope, Schoenfeld residuals plot and log–log plot. The model was also examined for model fit and collinearity (Supplementary Methods, Table S2, Figures S2–S5). In addition, the interaction terms of sex, obesity, and T2D were tested as these are key mediators of survival in individuals with MASLD and CKD, as reported in previous studies. 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 To assess whether sex, obesity, or T2D influences the association between MASLD/CKD or fibrosis/CKD status with long‐term all‐cause mortality, their interactions were tested within the Cox regression model.

Lastly, to examine that the prespecified hypothesis of unfavorable prognosis in the more severe form of MASLD (aim 3), subgroup analysis of participants based on advanced hepatic fibrosis/CKD status was performed. Individuals in the MASLD(+) subgroup were stratified into 4 groups based on the presence of hepatic fibrosis and CKD: without fibrosis and CKD [fibrosis(−)/CKD(−)], with fibrosis and without CKD [fibrosis (+)/CKD(−)], without fibrosis and with CKD [fibrosis (−)/CKD(+)], and with fibrosis and CKD [fibrosis (+)/CKD(+)]. The R‐package “sandwich” was used to attain the robust error. Risk ratios (RR) and their 95% CIs were calculated. A P value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

Data on 59 842 participants were extracted between 2007 to 2018. After excluding participants with missing MASLD or CKD status, missing mortality data, or individuals with significant alcohol consumption, a total of 14 818 individuals were included in the study analysis (Figure S1). The mean age of the study cohort was 48±18 years old, with 51.3% being female. The mean study follow‐up duration was 6.9±3.4 years. When stratified based on CKD‐MASLD status, the majority were in the group with MASLD(−)/CKD(−) (n=7526, 50.8%), followed by the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(−) (n=5157, 34.8%), the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (n=1142, 7.7%), and the group with MASLD(−)/CKD(+) (n=993, 6.7%) (Table 1).

MASLD and CKD Phenotypes

Individuals with CKD tended to be older (MASLD(−)/CKD(+): 63±19 years, MASLD(+)/CKD(+): 61±16 years, P<0.001), with a larger proportion from low‐ and middle‐income groups (P<0.001), compared with those without CKD. Notably, the groups with CKD had higher rates of stroke (P<0.001), CHD (P<0.001), heart failure (P<0.001), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (P<0.001), and cancer (P<0.001), relative to their counterparts without CKD. Individuals in the groups with CKD were more likely to be prescribed antihypertensives (P<0.001), glucose‐lowering medications (P<0.001), lipid‐lowering medications (P<0.001), aspirin (P<0.001), and sodium glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors (P=0.031) compared with groups without CKD (Table 1).

There was a stepwise increase in the overall burden of concomitant hypertension, T2D, and dyslipidemia from the group with MASLD(−)/CKD(−) (hypertension: 24.5%, T2D: 6.6%, dyslipidemia: 37.5%), followed by the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(−) (hypertension: 47.0%, T2D: 20.9%, dyslipidemia: 55.6%; P<0.001), the group with MASLD(−)/CKD(+) (hypertension: 67.6%, T2D: 24.6%, dyslipidemia: 57.5%; P<0.001), and the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (hypertension: 77.5%, T2D: 49.7%, dyslipidemia: 67.0%; P<0.001) (Table 1).

In examining the social determinants of health, there were significant differences in the poverty‐to‐income ratio across groups (P<0.001). A greater proportion of individuals in the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(+)were in the low‐income category (24.1%) as compared with the other phenotypes. Conversely, the proportion of individuals in the high‐income category was highest in the group with MASLD(−)/CKD(−) (27.3%) and lowest in the group with MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (18.6%).

Coexistence of MASLD and CKD

Individuals with coexistent MASLD and CKD had the highest rates of multimorbidity such as obesity (77.6%, P<0.001), hypertension (77.5%, P<0.001), dyslipidemia (67.0%, P<0.001), T2D (49.7%, P<0.001), CHD (19.8%, P<0.001), heart failure (13.3%, P<0.001), and COPD (9.6%, P<0.001). Individuals with MASLD(+)/CKD(+) had the highest representation in low‐income groups (24.1%), followed by those with MASLD(−)/CKD(+) (23.1%). The proportion of men and women with the MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotype was similar, although those with obesity or T2D had a higher proportion of MASLD(+)/CKD(+) than their counterparts without obesity or T2D respectively (Figure 1). Figure 2 highlights that MASLD(+)/CKD(+) had the highest burden of metabolic diseases, with obesity and hypertension identified as the dominant risk factors.

Study Outcomes

The MASLD(−)/CKD(+) phenotype demonstrated the least favorable long‐term survival as compared with their counterparts without CKD (Table 2). The survival curve revealed the MASLD(−)/CKD(+) phenotype portended the least favorable prognosis followed by MASLD(+)/CKD(+), MASLD(+)/CKD(−), and MASLD(−)/CKD(−) (Figure 3). The Kaplan–Meier curves demonstrated consistent divergence in survival with the least favorable survival in MASLD(−)/CKD(+) and MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotypes, compared with MASLD(+)/CKD(−) and MASLD(−)/CKD(−) counterparts, across the various subgroups stratified by sex, obesity, and T2D status (Figure S6). The Cox regression model showed that the MASLD(+)/CKD(+) (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 3.28 [95% CI, 1.89–5.70], P<0.001) and MASLD(−)/CKD(+) (aHR, 2.18 [95% CI, 1.33–3.66], P=0.002) phenotypes were independent predictors of long‐term mortality, after adjusting for confounders (Table 3). Notably, the MASLD(+)/CKD(−) phenotype was not a predictor of all‐cause mortality (aHR, 1.48 [95% CI, 0.85–2.59], P=0.165). In the sensitivity analysis, stratified by hepatic fibrosis and CKD status, fibrosis(+)/CKD(+) (aHR, 2.97 [95% CI, 1.67–5.29], P<0.001) and fibrosis(−)/CKD(+) (aHR, 2.30 [95% CI, 1.65–3.21], P<0.001) remained significant predictors of all‐cause mortality (Table 3, Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

MASLD and CKD are epiphenomena underpinned by metabolic dysfunction, often occurring in tandem with other metabolic comorbidities. 22 Individuals with coexisting MASLD and CKD are at higher risk of CHD 17 and all‐cause mortality, compared with those with MASLD or CKD in silos. 12 Our study extends the literature by describing the distinct risk profiles, social determinants of health, cardiovascular morbidity, and survival across the 4 MASLD‐CKD phenotypes in a real‐world community‐based study. The pertinent findings were as follows. (1) The highest multimorbidity was observed in individuals of the MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotype, compared with those with isolated MASLD or CKD, with 77.6% of the MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotype having concomitant obesity, 77.5% with hypertension, 67.0% with dyslipidemia, and 49.7% with T2D. The MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotype also had the highest representation of individuals with low income. (2) Individuals with the MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotype had an increased risk of CHD and heart failure, but not stroke and cancer. (3) The MASLD(−)/CKD(+) and MASLD(+)/CKD(+) phenotypes demonstrated the least favorable long‐term survival compared with the other phenotypes, and these trends were consistent across sex, T2D, and obesity subgroups. (4) When the presence of hepatic fibrosis was examined instead of MASLD, risk stratification was enhanced across the fibrosis‐CKD phenotypes, with pronounced divergence in survival observed in individuals with concomitant fibrosis and CKD having the least favorable 10‐year prognosis compared with the other 3 phenotypes.

Our study cohort demonstrated the metabolic “double whammy” with the combined MASLD and CKD phenotype being an independent predictor of survival over 10 years. Based on our findings, the concerning mortality trends might be driven by underlying CKD to a larger extent, compared with the presence of MASLD, especially with the MASLD(−)/CKD(+) phenotype portending the poorest prognosis. This extends the existing knowledge derived from community‐based cohorts that demonstrated individuals with concurrent MASLD and CKD were susceptible to cardiovascular events but not all‐cause mortality, 21 whereas others have demonstrated higher risk of cardiovascular mortality only in the cohort with T2D. 19 Both CKD and MASLD perpetuate the vicious cycle of atherogenic dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, systemic inflammation, and oxidative stress, which can synergistically accelerate atherosclerotic and myopathic processes. This is exacerbated by CKD‐specific mediators that drive CHD and heart failure through the activation of the renin‐angiotensin system, increased afterload, uremia, abnormal iron metabolism, mineral bone disorders, and anemia. 48 This bidirectional relationship and interorgan crosstalk between MASLD and CKD has been postulated to be related to (1) hepatorenal syndrome in which cirrhosis can lead to renal dysfunction through increased splanchnic blood flow, systemic hypovolemia and renal perfusion, as well as (2) the early progression of subclinical portal hypertension in precirrhotic MASLD that could potentiate circulatory dysfunction and CKD progression. 50 This complex interplay of chronic metabolic conditions can accelerate CVD progression, and their interconnectedness underscores the concept of the cardiovascular‐kidney‐liver‐metabolic health, 16 emphasizing the need for an integrated approach in early detection and prevention of the comorbidities. Taken together, our findings as well as others 21 , 48 should be validated against future large‐scale prospective studies with systematic concomitant evaluation of MASLD and CKD, preferably by imaging modalities (short of biopsy descriptors), in better understanding the discriminatory capacity of the addition of concomitant MASLD and CKD to conventional cardiovascular risk stratification tools.

The current evidence highlights the key finding of the dose–response relationship between advanced liver disease, particularly the presence of hepatic fibrosis, and the incremental mortality risk. 19 , 21 Although the primary analysis revealed the limited additive prognostic value of MASLD in individuals with CKD, the presence of fibrosis significantly increased the risk of mortality in individuals with CKD. This is in line with current evidence that has shown the rise in fatal or nonfatal CVD events across the severity spectrum of MASLD. 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 Moreover, stepwise increments in CKD risk from 30%, 70%, to 150% among individuals with mild, moderate, and severe stages of MASLD, respectively, have been described in cohort studies, when compared with counterparts without MASLD. 48 This can have important clinical implications for contemporary cardiovascular risk stratification tools, such as the Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease Events calculator 55 , 56 that has taken CKD into account as a risk parameter, with the potential integration of biomarkers of hepatic fibrosis, rather than MASLD alone, to further enhance cardiovascular risk prediction. In correlation with the present study findings, serum‐based scoring systems for advanced fibrosis (ie, FIB‐4 score) could be prioritized over the mere measure of hepatic steatosis (ie, Fatty Liver Index), in the integration in risk prediction models. 16 , 57 The FIB‐4 uses clinical variables that are readily available in primary care and is the most validated screening tool that has outperformed other scoring systems in the screening for fibrosis. This is in line with cohort studies demonstrating that the high risk of liver fibrosis, as determined by FIB‐4 ≥2.67, is an independent predictor of increased cardiovascular mortality and morbidity in the population with MASLD. 58 , 59 More robust population‐based studies with the use of liver and kidney biomarkers are needed to evaluate the improvements in the discrimination value and net reclassification in novel cardiovascular risk assessment models, as well as their cost effectiveness in guiding primary prevention. 16 , 57 Examining longitudinal outcomes of CHD, heart failure, stroke, and cancer across the MASLD‐CKD phenotypes will be an important next step in enhancing contemporary cardiovascular risk stratification tools, that should encompass the screening of younger individuals, coupled with extended prognostication for up to 30‐year CVD and cancer risk. 56 Future work is also needed to explore the timeline of fibrosis and CKD diagnoses, unravel pathophysiological mechanisms, and provide cost‐effectiveness evaluation of renal screening within MASLD care pathways and vice versa for MASLD screening in renal care. 45 , 60 , 61 , 62

Our findings distinguish unique risk profiles across the MASLD‐CKD phenotypes, as individuals with CKD embodied higher cardiovascular risk compared with those with MASLD, though this is partly due to the higher proportion of older individuals in the group with CKD. 63 Moreover, metabolic profiles tended to differ across the MASLD‐CKD phenotypes with higher rates of obesity and dyslipidemia in MASLD, compared with the higher propensity toward hypertension and dyslipidemia in CKD. This is in keeping with our previous work that demonstrated more than two thirds of the global population with overweight living with MASLD and an even higher prevalence of MASLD in the population with obesity. 64 On the other hand, kidney dysfunction drives multifactorial processes involving the activation of the renin‐angiotensin system that can accelerate CKD‐specific complications including hypertension. 48 When social determinants of health were examined, individuals with coexistent MASLD and CKD were more likely from low socioeconomic status group, whereas those without MASLD and CKD had the highest representation in the high socioeconomic status group. Tackling social determinants of health disparities is critical in promoting cardiovascular‐liver‐metabolic health, as low socioeconomic status has been strongly associated with reduced access to health care, lower health literacy, limited access to more expensive healthy food, and higher exposure to environmental risk factors. These social barriers collectively contribute towards a poorer prognosis in the high‐risk population with concomitant MASLD and CKD. 65 , 66 Therefore, early identification of individuals with the twin burden of MASLD and CKD is crucial in combatting the rise in cardiometabolic comorbidities and CVD, which can consequently translate to improvements in global health care systems and service delivery in the future. 8 , 67

This study is not without its limitations. First, longitudinal cardiovascular outcomes were not available for the study. Future large‐scale studies are warranted to perform time‐to‐event analysis for cardiovascular end points across the 4 MASLD‐CKD phenotypes. Second, the gold‐standard liver biopsy or noninvasive imaging was not available for the determination of MASLD. Instead, externally validated serum‐based composite scores were adopted, enabling the pragmatic alternative for larger‐scale screening of MASLD in the diverse general population. 68 Third, the availability of data on alcohol use was not complete for all study participants. The self‐reported alcohol usage during health care visits was subjected to recall bias, presenting a common limitation within the current MASLD criterion. 45 Fourth, although NHANES was a population‐based survey, a subset of participants with complete data for the MASLD and CKD diagnoses was included in the study analysis, potentially contributing to selection bias. These individuals were more likely to have undergone broader clinical testing (including liver function tests) that might reflect a different baseline in health status compared with the general population. Additionally, the study definition of CKD based on contemporary clinical practice guidelines 69 may have captured a heterogeneous group of individuals with varying causes of kidney disease. Although diabetic and hypertensive nephropathy are the commonest causes of CKD, other syndromic causes (eg, nephrotic/nephritic syndromes or malignancy‐related CKD) might have been included in the study population. As the heterogeneity in the cause of CKD may skew risk profiles and mortality rates, the study outcomes should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, other potential confounders associated with CKD, such as advanced age, were considered and adjusted as covariates in the regression models. Last, the data did not provide insight into the chronology of MASLD, advanced fibrosis, and CKD. Thus, future longitudinal studies are necessary to ascertain the natural history of the MASLD‐CKD axis. 12

CONCLUSIONS

Community‐dwelling individuals with the double burden of MASLD and CKD are at increased risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity, as well as downstream sequelae of CHD and heart failure, compared with those with MASLD or CKD in silos. The coexistence of MASLD and CKD is an independent predictor of 10‐year mortality, with stepwise incremental mortality risk observed in high‐risk individuals with concomitant advanced hepatic fibrosis and CKD. There is a need for scalable and effective screening systems in facilitating risk prediction and interventional targets within the cardiovascular‐kidney‐liver‐metabolic disease spectrum.
