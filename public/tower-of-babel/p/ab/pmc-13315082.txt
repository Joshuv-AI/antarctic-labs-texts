Genetic spectrum and clinical features of PMP22 point mutations in Japanese Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease
Authors: Chikashi Yano, Masahiro Ando, Yujiro Higuchi, Takahiro Hobara, Jun-Hui Yuan, Akiko Yoshimura, Risa Nagatomo, Fumikazu Kojima, Yu Hiramatsu, Satoshi Nozuma, Tomonori Nakamura, Jun Mitsui, Shoji Tsuji, Hiroshi Takashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPMP22 duplications cause Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT), whereas PMP22 deletion results in hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP). Although PMP22 point mutations can cause a spectrum of neuropathies, including hereditary motor and sensory neuropathy (HMSN) I, congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy, HMSN III, and HNPP, they are rare, and their features in Japanese patients have not been well characterized. Therefore, we compared the genetic, clinical, and electrophysiological characteristics of patients with PMP22 point mutations.MethodsThis multicenter study enrolled 3352 Japanese patients clinically suspected of having inherited peripheral neuropathies/CMT who underwent fluorescence in situ hybridization, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification, gene panel sequencing, or whole-exome analysis between 1995 and 2024. Clinical and electrophysiological data were obtained from patients’ medical records.ResultsThe study enrolled 30 patients with PMP22 point mutations and 57 patients with PMP22 duplications. Among the point mutation cases, 24 distinct variants were detected, including four novel variants (p.T23K, p.R95Sfs*16, p.M111R, and p.P144R). Two splice-site variants (c.78 + 3G > T and c.79-2A > G) were associated with HNPP, whereas the others were associated with polyneuropathy. Compared with patients with PMP22 duplications, those with point mutations experienced earlier disease onset (0 vs. 35.0 years), were less likely to have a family history (14.3% vs. 61.7%), and were less frequently ambulatory (12/28 vs. 50/54). Upper limb compound muscle action potentials were more frequently undetectable in patients with point mutations (18/25 vs. 1/50).ConclusionsPMP22 point mutations-associated polyneuropathies were characterized by earlier disease onset and more severe effects than duplications, highlighting the broad clinical spectrum of PMP22-related neuropathies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00415-026-13946-3.Keywords: Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, PMP22, Demyelinating neuropathy, Hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies

Introduction

Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease (CMT) is the most common inherited neuromuscular disorder and typically presents as a length-dependent motor and sensory neuropathy. CMT is clinically and genetically heterogeneous and is characterized by progressive motor and sensory deficits, foot deformities, and reduced tendon reflexes. Among the causative genes, duplications of the peripheral myelin protein 22 gene (PMP22) are by far the most frequent cause and result in demyelinating neuropathy [1]. In addition to duplications, PMP22 deletions are associated with hereditary neuropathy with liability to pressure palsies (HNPP), whereas PMP22 point mutations have been reported in a broad spectrum of neuropathies, including HMSN I, congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy (CHN), HMSN III, and HNPP, depending on their effects on gene and protein expression [2, 3].

Within this spectrum, point mutations are relatively rare, with reported frequencies ranging from 0.4% to 4.6% globally [2–13]. In this study, we examined the clinical, electrophysiological, and genetic features of PMP22 point mutation-related neuropathy, in comparison with PMP22 duplication.

Methods

Data collection and ethical approval

Clinical and electrophysiological data were collected by reviewing patients’ records and assessments from the medical facilities involved. Ambulatory status was defined as the ability to walk independently or with assistive devices. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Kagoshima University, and informed consent was obtained from all patients and their family members before study participation.

Patients and study design

We included Japanese patients who participated in a nationwide genetic study of inherited peripheral neuropathies (IPNs) and CMT. Patients clinically diagnosed with IPNs/CMT were referred to our laboratory for genetic testing from other institutions participating in the nationwide genetic study, and underwent detailed clinical evaluation and genetic analysis. All patients were diagnosed by at least two experienced neurologists or pediatric neurologists.

The PMP22 point mutation group included patients who underwent genetic testing for clinically diagnosed IPNs/CMT between 2007 and 2024 through this nationwide study. The PMP22 duplication group included patients diagnosed at our institution since 1995. Among patients referred through the same nationwide genetic study, PMP22 duplications were identified using fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and, since 2007, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA). In Japan, PMP22 duplication/deletion testing is routinely performed in patients with demyelinating neuropathy, particularly those with motor nerve conduction velocities below 38 m/s. In addition, patients who underwent targeted panel sequencing or whole-exome sequencing were evaluated for PMP22 copy number variants using CNV detection algorithms, with confirmation by MLPA when appropriate.

The primary objective of this study was to characterize the clinical and electrophysiological features of neuropathy associated with PMP22 point mutations. Patients with PMP22 duplications were included as a reference group for contextual comparison rather than as a primary comparator group.

Genetic analysis

Point mutations

Between 2007 and 2024, genetic screening was conducted using in-house panel-based sequencing and whole-exome sequencing. These panels targeted known IPNs/CMT-related genes, including PMP22. The assays were conducted using one of the following platforms: DNA microarrays (2007–2012; Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA), Illumina MiSeq platform (2012–2014; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), or Ion Proton platform (2014–2024; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sequencing data were aligned to the human reference genome (NCBI37/hg19), and detected variants were annotated using CLC Genomics Workbench software (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and in-house scripts. All potentially disease-causing variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing. Variants were described according to RefSeq transcript NM_000304.4 and protein NP_000295.1. When DNA samples from family members were available, segregation analysis was performed by Sanger sequencing.

We evaluated all previously reported pathogenic variants with reference to the Human Gene Mutation Database Professional (HGMD: https://portal.biobase-international.com/hgmd/pro; accessed on February 4, 2026). All detected variants were compared against population databases, including the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD: https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/) and Japanese Multi-Omics Reference Panel (jMorp: https://jmorp.megabank.tohoku.ac.jp), and further checked against our in-house whole-exome sequencing database of individuals without IPNs. In silico analyses were performed using Polyphen2, FATHMM, CADD, REVEL, BayesDel, and SpliceAI. All candidate variants were evaluated according to the guidelines of the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG–AMP) and the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (https://clinicalgenome.org/working-groups/sequence-variant-interpretation/) [14–20]. These methods have been described previously [21].

Duplications

PMP22 duplications were detected by FISH or MLPA. FISH was performed using a commercially available test (LSI Medience Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). MLPA was performed using a commercial kit (SALSA P405 CMT1 MLPA® MRC-Holland, Amsterdam, The Netherlands) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Three-dimensional (3D) structure modeling of the PMP22 domain

To investigate the structural context of the identified PMP22 variants, we generated a 3D model of the human PMP22 protein using AlphaFold2 via the Google Colaboratory platform. Specifically, the ColabFold: AlphaFold2_mmseqs2 notebook was utilized for the prediction with default parameters [22, 23]. The amino acid residues corresponding to the variant sites identified in this study were subsequently highlighted within the predicted 3D model.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for quantitative variables, and the Fisher’s exact test for qualitative variables. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (R version 4.3.2).

Results

Patient selection and data availability

Among the 3352 patients who underwent genetic testing for inherited neuropathies at our institution, we identified 92 patients with PMP22 variants (Fig. 1). After excluding two patients without available clinical data, one patient with a concomitant MFN2 mutation, and two patients in whom the PMP22 variant was classified as a variant of uncertain significance (p.Q40K, p.F83L), 30 patients with PMP22 sequence variants and 57 patients with PMP22 duplications were included in the present analysis. Nerve conduction study (NCS) data were available in 27 of 30 patients with PMP22 sequence variants and 50 of 57 patients with PMP22 duplications (Supplemental file 1).

Genetic findings

We identified 24 distinct PMP22 sequence variants, including 20 single-nucleotide variants and four small deletions, in 30 patients. Some variants were observed in more than one patient. The identified variants consisted predominantly of missense variants (n = 17), with frameshift variants (n = 3), splice-site variants (n = 3), and in-frame deletion (n = 1). Among 24 variants, four novel variants (c.68C > A; p.T23K, c.285delG; p.R95Sfs*16, c.332 T > G; p.M111R, c.431C > G; p.P144R) were classified as likely pathogenic based on ACMG guidelines (Table 1). All four novel variants were observed in sporadic cases. Segregation analyses were performed in two of these cases (c.332 T > G; p.M111R and c.431C > G; p.P144R), revealing de novo occurrence in both (Fig. 2).

Three splice-site variants were identified in three patients (c.78 + 3G > T, c.79-2A > G, and c.179-1G > A). In silico analysis using SpliceAI predicted splicing alterations for all three variants, with delta scores of 0.55, 0.84, and 0.99, respectively. Two variants were located in the TM1 region, whereas one variant was located in the intracellular domain. The two variants located in TM1 (c.78 + 3G > T and c.79-2A > G) were both associated with a clinical and electrophysiological phenotype consistent with HNPP. In contrast, the intracellular splice-site variant (c.179-1G > A) presented with a polyneuropathy.

Three frameshift variants were identified in three patients, and they were located in different domains, namely the intracellular domain (c.285delG; p.R95Sfs*16), TM3 (c.310del; p.I104Ffs*7), and the extracellular domain (c.434del; p.L145Rfs*10). All three patients exhibited a clinical phenotype consistent with HMSN III. One patient carried an in-frame deletion variant (c.251-253del; p.F84del). The patient developed symptoms at birth and had never achieved independent walking at the time of diagnosis at 4 years of age. NCS revealed absent compound muscle action potential (CMAP) in the upper limbs at the time of diagnosis. The patient was classified as having congenital hypomyelinating neuropathy (CHN).

Patients were classified as CHN (n = 13), HMSN III (n = 9), HMSN I (n = 3), HNPP (n = 2), or unclassified phenotype (n = 3). Detailed clinical characteristics are summarized in Supplemental file 2.

Among the 23 patients without a family history (family history unknown in one patient), parental genetic testing data were available in eight patients, all of whom carried de novo mutations. Among the remaining 15 patients, all had disease onset at 10 years of age or younger, raising the possibility of de novo mutations in these cases. Compared with the 23 patients without a family history, the four patients with a positive family history (two fathers, one son, and one sibling) had significantly later disease onset (median [interquartile range], 17.5 [9.0–27.8] years vs. 0 [0–1.5] years), and they were all ambulatory (100% vs 34.8%).

Clinically, objective distal leg weakness was commonly observed in both patients with non-HNPP point mutations and duplications (95.7% vs. 89.6%). In contrast, proximal leg weakness (66.7% vs. 26.7%) and upper-limb weakness were more common in patients with PMP22 point mutations. The proportion of ambulatory patients was lower in the point mutation group than in the duplication group (12/28 [42.9%] vs. 50/54 [92.6%]). Among the patients with PMP22 point mutations, non-ambulatory patients (n = 15) had significantly earlier disease onset than ambulatory patients (n = 12; median [interquartile range], 0.0 [0.0–0.5] years vs. 6.5 [0.75–11.0] years, p = 0.0044), whereas there were no significant differences in current age (17 [2–53.5] years vs. 26 [11–36.5] years, p = 0.605) or disease duration (median [interquartile range], 17 [2–49] years vs. 10.5 [8.0–22.3] years, p = 0.993) between the two groups.

In addition, cranial nerve involvement was more frequently observed in patients with PMP22 point mutation (10/28 vs. 4/57), including ocular involvement such as ophthalmoplegia (n = 4), mydriasis (n = 2), nystagmus (n = 1).

Electrophysiological findings

Electrophysiologically, reduced tibial nerve CMAP amplitudes were commonly observed in both the point mutation and duplication groups (14/19 [73.7%] vs. 20/47 [42.6%]). By contrast, undetectable upper-limb CMAPs (median nerve when available; otherwise, the ulnar nerve) were markedly more frequent in patients with PMP22 point mutations than in those with PMP22 duplications (18/25 [72.0%] vs. 1/50 [2.0%]; Fig. 4). Among the nine patients with PMP22 point mutations in whom upper-limb CMAPs were detectable, two patients (c.78 + 3G > T and c.79-2A > G) displayed electrophysiological findings characteristic of HNPP, including conduction slowing and conduction block at entrapment sites. Both patients presented with unilateral foot drop, and their variants have been previously reported to cause HNPP. The remaining seven patients exhibited motor nerve conduction velocities (MCVs) lower than 38 m/s, consistent with a demyelinating pattern.

When restricted to patients with distal CMAP amplitudes of ≥ 1 mV, five patients with PMP22 point mutation-related polyneuropathy were eligible for analysis. The median upper-limb MCV was 8.6 m/s. Compared with the findings in patients with PMP22 duplications, those with PMP22 point mutations exhibited significantly lower CMAP amplitudes in both the upper (median [interquartile range], 0.0 [0.0–0.6] mV vs. 2.5 [1.1–4.0] mV, p < 0.001) and lower limbs (median [interquartile range], 0.0 [0.0–0.1] mV vs. 0.17 [0.0–0.45], p = 0.037), whereas no significant differences were observed in MCV (median [interquartile range], 8.6 [7.7–21.2] m/s vs. 21.5 [17.5–24.2] m/s, p = 0.130). Sensory nerves were severely affected in both groups (Supplemental file 3).

In patients with PMP22 duplications, all patients, excluding one with undetectable CMAPs, were classified as having a demyelinating pattern, with a median MCV of 21.5 m/s. The patient with undetectable CMAPs was 63 years old and wheelchair-dependent, suggesting severe neuropathy.

Discussion

This study presents a comprehensive clinical, genetic, and electrophysiological characterization of PMP22 point mutation–associated neuropathy in 30 patients among the 3352 individuals (0.9%) with suspected IPNs. We identified 24 distinct PMP22 sequence variants, including four novel variants. Compared with patients with PMP22 duplications, those with PMP22 point mutation-associated polyneuropathies exhibited more severe neuropathy, characterized by earlier disease onset (0.0 vs 35.0 years, p < 0.001), greater motor disability, and more frequent cranial nerve involvement, particularly ocular manifestations (10/30 vs 4/57). These findings highlight the distinct and often more severe clinical phenotype associated with PMP22 point mutations.

From a genetic perspective, among the 30 patients with PMP22 point mutations, four variants were novel and had not been previously reported. All the four variants were classified as pathogenic or likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines. Among 30 patients, we detected 24 distinct variants, including 17 with missense variants, 3 with splice-site variants, 3 with frameshift variants, and one with in-frame deletion.

Of the 17 missense variants (23 patients), all pathogenic variants identified in this study were located within the transmembrane domain, consistent with previous finding [24]. Such transmembrane domain changes are thought to lead to structural instability and protein misfolding, resulting in a toxic gain-of-function effect. By contrast, PMP22 duplication does not alter the primary structure of the protein; instead, it causes a quantitative increase in PMP22 expression [24–26]. These distinct molecular mechanisms may underlie the differences in clinical severity observed between PMP22 point mutations and duplications.

In addition to the overall enrichment of variants within transmembrane domains, a genotype–phenotype correlation was observed according to the position of the variants within the transmembrane domains. Variants located toward the intracellular side of the transmembrane helices were predominantly associated with non-ambulatory status, whereas those located toward the extracellular side were more frequently observed in ambulatory patients. Similarly, previous reports suggested that variants located deeper within the transmembrane regions are associated with more severe disease phenotypes, whereas variants located closer to the membrane surface tend to be associated with milder phenotypes. Our findings further support the concept that the intramembranous localization of PMP22 variants influences clinical severity [24]. In addition, non-ambulatory patients had significantly earlier disease onset than ambulatory patients (0 [0.0–0.5] vs 6.5 [0.75–11.0] years, p = 0.004), whereas no significant differences in disease duration were observed between these groups. These findings suggest that ambulatory status is associated with genotype rather than disease duration.

Among the three splice-site variants identified in this study (c.78 + 3G > T, c.79-2A > G, and c.179-1G > A), c.78 + 3G > T has been shown to cause exon 2 skipping, whereas c.79-2A > G is predicted to result in exon 3 skipping, leading to a frameshift and a premature termination codon that may trigger nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), consistent with PMP22 haploinsufficiency and the HNPP phenotype [27–29]. Consistent with these molecular mechanisms, both variants were associated with HNPP in our cohort. In contrast, for c.179-1G > A, although RNA analysis was not available in our case, a variant affecting the same splice acceptor site (c.179–2) has been reported to cause exon 4 skipping with an in-frame deletion and a stable transcript [30]. These findings suggest that c.179-1G > A may similarly result in exon skipping, potentially escaping NMD and producing a shortened PMP22 protein, which could explain the CMT phenotype [31].

Of the three frameshift variants (c.285delG; R95Sfs*16, c.310del; I104Ffs*7, and c.434delT; L145Rfs*10), the premature termination codons are located within the final coding exon, suggesting escape from NMD and the production of truncated PMP22 proteins that may explain the CMT phenotype observed in these patients [15].

From a clinical standpoint, patients with PMP22 point mutations-related neuropathy exhibited earlier disease onset (0 vs 35.0 years), a lower rate of ambulatory status (42.9% vs 92.6%), and more frequent proximal upper-limb weakness (42.9% vs 11.1%), indicating a more severe disease course than observed in patients with PMP22 duplications. These findings are consistent with previous reports that PMP22 point mutations can cause severe neuropathies, including CHN or Dejerine–Sottas syndrome [2, 26, 32]. Furthermore, cranial nerve involvement was observed in 10 of the 30 patients with PMP22 point mutations, including ocular involvement in seven patients (four with ophthalmoplegia, two with mydriasis, and one with nystagmus). Cranial nerve involvements such as hearing loss, ocular motor disorders, and nystagmus have been reported in CMT, although they are considered uncommon [2, 11, 33–36]. In contrast, hearing loss was identified in only one patient, which was less frequent than reported in previous studies [2, 37]. In addition, sporadic cases accounted for a high proportion of PMP22 point mutations in this study (86%). Among the patients for whom parental genetic testing data were available, all were confirmed to have de novo mutations. Among the remaining 15 patients without parental testing, all had disease onset at ≤ 10 years of age, strongly suggesting a de novo origin. PMP22 point mutation–associated neuropathies, including CHN, HMSN I, HMSN III and HNPP, have frequently been reported to present as sporadic disorders caused by de novo mutations, with many patients lacking an apparent family history [2, 32]. Our findings further support this observation and highlight the substantial contribution of de novo variants in PMP22-related neuropathy. These findings emphasize the well-known importance of considering genetic testing for PMP22 point mutations in patients with clinically severe demyelinating neuropathy, even in the absence of a family history.

Electrophysiological examinations in our study further demonstrated more severe neuropathy in patients with PMP22 point mutations-associated polyneuropathy compared to those with PMP22 duplications. Among 27 patients with PMP22 point mutations for whom NCS data were available, 25 patients (excluding two patients with HNPP) showed a high proportion of undetectable upper-limb CMAPs, compared to patients with duplications (18/25 vs 1/50). CMAP amplitudes were significantly lower in both upper and lower limbs, and SNAPs were absent in all cases, indicating more severe neuropathy in the point mutation group than in the duplication group. In contrast, MCVs did not differ significantly between the two groups, although both groups exhibited demyelinating patterns [38]. These findings suggest that PMP22 point mutations are associated with more severe electrophysiological abnormalities, consistent with the clinical observations. The two patients with HNPP presented clinically with foot drop, and their variants have been reported to cause HNPP, consistent with their clinical manifestations [28, 29].

This study has several limitations. First, clinical data were collected from multiple medical institutions, resulting in some heterogeneity in data availability and completeness. Furthermore, because this was a retrospective study, RNA samples were not available, precluding experimental validation of the predicted splicing effects. Although in silico analyses predicted splicing alterations for all three splice-site variants, their molecular consequences could not be experimentally confirmed. Additionally, differences in electrophysiological procedures across institutions may have introduced technical variability, potentially affecting diagnostic accuracy and interpretation of the findings. Second, in Japan, PMP22 duplications are frequently screened using commercially available FISH assays. Consequently, many patients with PMP22 duplications may have been excluded prior to referral for comprehensive genetic testing. Therefore, the distribution of PMP22 duplications in the present study does not reflect the true prevalence of CMT1A in the general population.

In conclusion, PMP22 point mutation–associated polyneuropathy in Japanese patients is characterized by greater clinical severity, more pronounced electrophysiological abnormalities, and a higher frequency of additional manifestations such as ophthalmoplegia, mydriasis, and nystagmus compared with PMP22 duplications. As many patients lack family history, PMP22 point mutations, although rare, should be considered and screened for in patients with severe demyelinating neuropathy.
