A Low LDL-C Level but not Statin Use is Associated with In-hospital Mortality in Spontaneous Intracerebral Hemorrhage; The J-ICH Registry
Authors: Kazo Kanazawa, Nobukazu Miyamoto, Hideaki Ueno, Satoshi Tsutsumi, Rikizo Saito, Joji Tokugawa, Yuji Ueno, Kazuo Yamashiro, Munetaka Yamamoto, Yukimasa Yasumoto, Makoto Hishii, Takuji Yamamoto, Chikashi Maruki, Takao Urabe, Akihide Kondo, Hajime Arai, Nobutaka Hattori, Ryota Tanaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim: A reduction in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is beneficial for vascular diseases; however, lower LDL-C levels may be associated with an increased risk of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH). The present study investigated the relationship between the LDL-C levels and in-hospital mortality after sICH using data from the Juntendo Registry of Spontaneous Intra-Cerebral Hemorrhage (J-ICH registry). Methods: Patients aged ≥ 20 years with non-traumatic sICH admitted to five Juntendo-affiliated hospitals between September 2016 and December 2019 were enrolled in this study. The relationships between the LDL-C levels and in-hospital mortality, statin therapy, and antithrombotic therapy were analyzed. Results: Among the 1,017 patients with sICH, lower LDL-C levels were associated with older age, a lower BMI, a larger hematoma volume, more severe neurological deficits, and a higher in-hospital mortality. A logistic regression analysis confirmed that LDL-C ＜100 mg/dL independently increased the risk of in-hospital death, along with age, the NIHSS score, hematoma volume, and intraventricular hemorrhage. Subgroup analyses showed that the association between low LDL-C levels and mortality was particularly evident in patients with deep/infratentorial intracerebral hemorrhage, those managed without surgery, and those without prior statin use. Prior statin use was associated with a potential protective effect against in-hospital mortality and hematoma volume. Conclusion: This study demonstrated that lower LDL-C levels were associated with a higher in-hospital mortality after sICH, whereas prior statin use was not associated with poorer outcomes and may instead offer a protective effect.

Introduction

Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) is a well-established risk factor for ischemic cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, and an intensive LDL-C reduction is known to reduce atherosclerotic events 1) . In contrast, low cholesterol levels may weaken the endothelium, making arteries more vulnerable, causing bleeding, or delaying repair after minor bleeding 2) . A meta-analysis of studies from the Asia-Pacific region showed that high blood pressure (BP), smoking, low cholesterol, and diabetes mellitus (DM) were associated with an increased risk of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH) 3) .

Several studies have investigated the relationship between the LDL-C levels and outcomes after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). For example, a single-center study conducted in Spain reported that patients with LDL-C ＜100 mg/dL had a significantly higher 90-day mortality rate than those with higher LDL-C levels (hazard ratio [HR] = 3.07; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.04–9.02; P = 0.042) 4) . Similarly, an observational study conducted in China examined the association between the rate of BP reduction and survival time in patients with acute ICH and demonstrated that lower LDL-C levels were significantly associated with 14-day mortality (adjusted HR = 1.47; 95% CI, 1.07–2.01; P＜0.001) 5) . More recently, a Chinese nationwide registry analysis also demonstrated that lower LDL-C levels (＜70 mg/dL) were independently associated with an increased risk of hematoma expansion or in-hospital mortality (HR =1.14; 95% CI, 1.02–1.27; P = 0.043) 6) .

Although these studies collectively indicated that lower LDL-C levels may adversely affect the short-term outcomes after ICH, many lacked detailed characterization of hematoma location or underlying etiology, and often provided limited information on concomitant medication use, such as statins or antithrombotics. Importantly, data from Japanese populations are scarce, despite Japan being one of the countries with the highest incidence of ICH worldwide 7) . Previous Japanese studies have primarily focused on the LDL-C levels as a risk factor for ICH rather than prognostic implications after onset 8 , 9) . Moreover, the potential modifying effects of prior statin use or hematoma characteristics such as deep/infratentorial versus lobar hemorrhage, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA)-related hemorrhage, or the role of surgical intervention remain insufficiently understood. Given the potential differences in the baseline lipid profiles, body composition, and statin prescription patterns between East Asian and Western populations 10) , clarifying these associations in a large, contemporary, multicenter Japanese cohort is therefore considered to be of clinical relevance.

Aim

The aim of this study was to investigate the impact of the LDL-C level on the short-term prognosis after sICH and to evaluate the effect of prior statin use based on the characteristics of sICH, such as hematoma location, CAA, and with or without surgical intervention. Therefore, we performed a sub-analysis using data from the Juntendo registry of spontaneous IntraCerebral Hemorrhage (J-ICH registry) 11) , an observational, multicenter, prospective registry of patients with sICH.

Methods

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The present study enrolled patients aged ≥ 20 years with non-traumatic ICH admitted to five stroke centers affiliated with the Juntendo University School of Medicine between September 1, 2016, and December 31, 2019 (J-ICH registry 11) ). We excluded cases in which more than eight days had passed since the onset of symptoms and cases of ICH due to cerebral arteriovenous malformation, aneurysm, brain tumor, blood disease, severe liver disease, hemophilia, thrombocytopenia, or vasculitis. A total of 1,102 cases met the criteria, and 1,017 were included in the analysis, following the exclusion of 85 cases with missing data ( Fig.1 ) .

Background Data and Risk Factors

We retrieved the following information from the medical records of each patient to examine the relationship between cerebral hemorrhage and the LDL-C level.

1. Demographic and baseline characteristics: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), pre-onset functional status (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score ≥ 3), current smoking, and regular alcohol consumption.

2. Previous medical history included hypertension (HT defined as systolic BP ＞140 mmHg, diastolic BP ＞90 mmHg, or drug treatment for HT), dyslipidemia (DL; defined as an LDL-C level ＞140 mg/dL, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level ＜40 mg/dL, triglyceride level ≥ 150 mg/dL, or drug treatment for DL), DM (defined as a glycated hemoglobin level; HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or drug treatment for DM), atrial fibrillation, ischemic cerebral infarction, ICH, ischemic heart disease (IHD), and hemodialysis.

3. Pre-stroke medications: antiplatelets, oral anticoagulants, and statins.

4. Onset conditions: systolic BP, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, hematoma location (deep/infratentorial, lobar ICH, probable CAA, probable + possible CAA), hematoma volume, intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and hematoma growth.

5. Treatment interventions: any surgery and neutralization/reversal therapy.

6. Clinical outcomes and complications: complications, mRS 5–6 at discharge, in-hospital mortality, and death within 24 h

Patients with lobar ICH involving the cerebral cortex and underlying white matter with strictly lobar cerebral microbleeds or cortical superficial siderosis were defined as having Probable CAA or Possible CAA according to the modified Boston criteria 12) . Possible CAA was excluded because this category has a lower sensitivity and specificity for identifying CAA 13) . The hemorrhage volume was assessed using the ABC/2 method, and hemorrhage expansion was defined as an increase of ＞33% or ＞6 ml from the baseline hematoma volume within 72 h 11) .

Ethical Considerations and Statistical Analysis

The study protocol was approved by the Human Ethics Review Committee of Juntendo University School of Medicine. Data were analyzed using SPSS 29.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and expressed as mean±standard deviation values or means (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were performed using the χ2 test for categorical variables, the t-test for parametric analyses, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-parametric analyses. Variables with a P-value ＜0.05 in the univariate analysis were entered into a binomial logistic regression analysis using the stepwise method. Statistical significance was set at p＜0.05.

Results

Baseline Characteristics by Each Level of the LDL-C Value in Patients with sICH

A total of 1,017 patients with ICH were enrolled in this study ( Fig.1 ) . Patients were categorized into three groups based on their LDL-C levels: LDL-C ＜70 mg/dL (n = 106), LDL-C 70–99 mg/dL (n = 276), and LDL-C ≥ 100 mg/dL (n = 635). A univariate analysis was performed to assess patient background, medical history, pre-medication history, vital signs and symptoms at onset, radiological findings, treatments, and discharge outcomes ( Table 1 ) . Among the patients with lower LDL-C levels, older age, male sex, lower BMI, and pre-onset functional status (mRS ≥ 3) were more prevalent. Additionally, there was a higher incidence of hemorrhagic stroke and a history of IHD and hemodialysis, as well as a higher percentage of patients taking antiplatelet drugs and statins (p＜0.05). Furthermore, these patients had a larger hematoma volume at onset, a higher rate of IVH, more severe stroke symptoms (as measured by NIHSS), a greater likelihood of poor prognosis at discharge, and higher in-hospital mortality rates (p＜0.05).

Association between the LDL-C Value or Prior Statin Use and In-hospital Mortality after sICH

To further clarify the relationships between the LDL-C levels, statin use, and in-hospital mortality, a binomial logistic regression analysis was conducted for variables with a p-value ＜0.05 in the univariate analysis. Three models were constructed: Model 1 examined the direct relationship between the LDL-C levels and statin use (due to a decrease in the LDL-C levels with statin use); Model 2 incorporated patient background factors; and Model 3 included previous medical history and current medications at the onset of ICH ( Table 2 ) . Using the LDL-C ≥ 100 mg/dL group as a reference, all models showed an increased odds ratio for in-hospital mortality in both the LDL-C 70–99 mg/dL and LDL-C ＜70 mg/dL groups, suggesting that lower LDL-C levels were associated with a higher in-hospital mortality.

Risk of In-hospital Mortality after sICH

We then identified the factors relevant to in-hospital mortality and determined whether there was a true relationship between in-hospital mortality and the LDL-C levels. To achieve this, we initially conducted a univariate analysis. The results indicated that LDL-C levels ＜70 mg/dL and those between 70 and 99 mg/dL carried a similar risk of in-hospital mortality. Therefore, for evaluation purposes, low LDL-C levels were categorized as ＜100 mg/dL (Table 3) . Our analysis (similar to Table 1 ) showed that in-hospital mortality (n = 71) was significantly more common in patients of advanced age, those with a history of alcohol consumption, DM, IHD, or hemodialysis, and in patients receiving antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy (p＜0.05), in addition to a low LDL-C level (p＜0.001). As expected, in-hospital mortality was also higher in cases with severe symptoms, a large hemorrhage volume, intraventricular hematoma, hematoma expansion, or complications such as pulmonary or urinary infections and congestive heart failure (p＜0.001). We then performed a binomial logistic regression analysis of the associated factors. The results identified age, severe symptoms (NIHSS), LDL-C level ＜100 mg/dL, a large hemorrhage volume, and intraventricular hematoma as independent and significant risk factors for in-hospital mortality (p＜0.05).

Association of Prior Statin Use with In-hospital Mortality and the sICH Characteristics

We also investigated the relationships between prior statin use and in-hospital mortality, hematoma volume, and IVH in each LDL-C group. No significant relationship was found between prior statin use and in-hospital mortality or the hematoma volume across LDL-C categories ( Fig.2A, B ) . However, the frequency of IVH among statin users tended to be higher in the LDL-C 70–99 mg/dL group ( Fig.2C ) . To further evaluate the outcomes in relation to statin use, separate multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed for each outcome variable (in-hospital mortality, hematoma volume, and IVH), with prior statin use included as the primary independent variable. Variables that showed a univariate association (p＜0.05) were entered as covariates in each model. As shown in Fig.2D , prior statin use was associated with a potential protective effect on in-hospital mortality (p = 0.002) and the hematoma volume (p = 0.039), whereas no significant association was observed with IVH (p = 0.778). These findings indicate that when analyzed from the standpoint of statin use, prior statin therapy might be a possible protective trend for in-hospital mortality and the hematoma volume, but not for IVH.

Association between the LDL-C Levels and In-hospital Mortality in Different Patient Groups with sICH

As we demonstrated that low LDL-C levels were associated with poor outcomes at discharge in the overall population of patients with sICH included in the J-ICH registry, we further investigated which subtypes of cerebral hemorrhage were more strongly associated with low LDL-C levels and unfavorable outcomes. To this end, we stratified the study population into the following groups: deep/infratentorial ICH (n = 786), lobar ICH (n = 231), probable CAA (n = 53), surgery (n = 258), non-surgery (n = 759), statin (n = 150), and non-statin (n = 867). Univariate analyses were then performed by dividing LDL-C into three categories, as shown in Table 1 , and in-hospital mortality is summarized ( Table 4 ) .

Association between Low LDL-C and In-hospital mortality with or without antithrombotic medication

Prior antithrombotic therapy is generally associated with a poor prognosis and short-term mortality; however, our current data did not show a significant association between prior antithrombotic medication use and in-hospital mortality. Therefore, we further examined the relationship between in-hospital mortality and prior antithrombotic therapy based on the LDL-C levels ( Fig.3 ) . The patients were categorized into five groups: no antithrombotic therapy, single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT), dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) therapy, and vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy ( Fig.3 ) . Among the patients who did not receive antithrombotic medication, the in-hospital mortality rate was significantly higher in those with low LDL-C levels (＜100 mg/dL). A similar but not statistically significant trend of higher in-hospital mortality was observed in patients who received DAPT, DOAC, or VKA with low LDL-C levels ( Fig.3 ) . We compared various clinical parameters among patients receiving antithrombotic therapy ( Supplemental Table 1 ) . Significantly higher prior statin use was found in patients with low LDL-C (＜100 mg/dL) who were receiving any type of antithrombotic medication, but not in those without these medications. Patients receiving no prior antithrombotic medication with low LDL-C levels were older, had a lower BMI, a lower functional status before hospitalization (mRS ＞3), presented with more severe symptoms (higher NIHSS scores), and had a larger hematoma volume (p＜0.05). In addition to prior statin use, older age, higher prevalence of IVH in the DAPT group, and older age and higher prevalence of complications in the DOAC group were correlated with low LDL-C levels.

In the non-antithrombotic drug (noATT) group, the mortality rate was significantly higher in the LDL-C ＜100 mg/dL group than in the LDL-C ≥ 100 mg/dL group. Discussion

This multicenter study enrolled 1,017 patients with sICH and showed a relationship between low LDL-C levels and a poor prognosis. The adjusted OR confirmed that low LDL-C levels were significantly associated with in-hospital mortality. In addition, prior statin use was not harmful and suggested a possible protective association between in-hospital mortality and the hematoma volume. Subgroup analyses revealed that the association between low LDL-C levels and in-hospital mortality was particularly evident in patients with deep/infratentorial ICH who did not receive prior statin treatment or acute surgical evacuation. The association between a low LDL-C level and in-hospital mortality was preserved among patients who had not used antithrombotic medication before onset, whereas this association was not evident in those who had been taking antithrombotic therapy prior to the event.

The incidence rate of ICH differs geographically, being estimated at 8–15% of all strokes in the USA, UK, and Australia, but higher in East Asian countries such as Japan, China, and Korea, where it ranges from 18 to 24% 14 , 15) . Despite this regional difference in incidence, several studies from both Western and Asian populations have consistently reported that lower LDL-C levels are associated with a poorer prognosis after ICH 4 , 16 , 17) . Similarly, a large-scale community-based cohort study in Japan demonstrated that low LDL-C levels were associated with death due to ICH during a median follow-up of 10.3 years, and LDL-C ＜80 mg/dL was inversely associated with mortality compared to LDL-C ≥ 80 mg/dL 9) . These findings, together with the results of the present study, suggest that although the frequency of ICH varies across regions and ethnicities, the adverse prognostic impact of low LDL-C levels appears to be consistent across populations. Moreover, recent reports from Western and East Asian cohorts, including those from the United States 18) , Finland 19) , Denmark 20) , China 21) , Taiwan 22) , and Japan 9) —did not demonstrate substantial racial or ethnic differences in the association between lipid levels, statin use, and the ICH outcomes. Taken together, these data indicate that the association between a low LDL-C level and a worse ICH prognosis, as well as the effect of statin use, is likely a universal phenomenon rather than an ethnicity-specific finding.

The present study suggests that prior statin use is associated with a lower in-hospital mortality and smaller hematoma volume. However, findings regarding the effects of statins on cerebral hemorrhage remain inconsistent. Some clinical studies have found no relationship between statin use and baseline total cholesterol levels, initial severity 23) , or 3-month mortality 24) , while others have reported different results 25) . Accordingly, meta-analyses have provided limited evidence of an association between ICH and statin use 26) . In contrast, a more recent meta-analysis, which included 30 clinical studies 27) , reported that statin use, both prior to and after ICH, was associated with reduced mortality after cerebral hemorrhage. Additionally, this meta-analysis showed that statin use increased the likelihood of patients achieving independent ambulation at discharge and 90 days post-discharge. Based on these findings, it can be inferred that statin use prior to the onset of cerebral hemorrhage is unlikely to exert harmful effects, and it may even confer short-term clinical benefits. Nevertheless, the current body of evidence is insufficient to establish any definitive conclusions. Further large-scale, well-designed, prospective studies are warranted to clarify the causal relationship between statin therapy and the outcomes in patients with cerebral hemorrhage.

We found a correlation between the LDL-C level and in-hospital mortality in the deep/infratentorial ICH group, but no significant difference was observed in the lobar ICH or probable CAA group. Previous investigations into the plasma lipid levels among patients with CAA-associated ICH reported reduced LDL-C values when compared to controls and patients with Alzheimer’s disease 28) . Another report showed that among patients with cerebral hemorrhage presumed to be related to CAA, those with follow-up LDL-C levels below 1.8 mmol/L (approximately 69 mg/dL) had an increased risk of recurrent hemorrhage 29) . Several studies have reported that decreased total cholesterol and elevated HDL cholesterol levels are associated with lobar cerebral microbleeds, a hallmark of CAA 30) . In this study, 13 of 231 patients with lobar ICH died, whereas only one of 53 patients with probable CAA died. Although the difference was not statistically significant, lower LDL-C levels were associated with higher in-hospital mortality in patients with probable CAA. The limited number of CAA cases may have influenced the statistical power, and validation in a larger cohort is required to draw any definitive conclusions.

Although the present results demonstrated that patients with low LDL-C levels had a trend toward a lower BMI, a direct relationship was not observed between a low BMI and in-hospital mortality. Previous studies have shown an increased risk of ICH severity when the BMI is extremely low or high 31 - 33) . Chen et al. reported that patients with a low BMI had a poorer prognosis. BMI ＜22.0 kg/m2 was associated with a 1.8-fold increase in ICH severity and a nearly 4-fold increase in the 3-month mortality rate 34) . In a Japanese study, a low body weight was associated with unfavorable functional outcomes and increased bleeding volume but not with in-hospital mortality 35) . In general, there is a certain relationship between the BMI and LDL-C levels 36) and the relationship between the BMI and LDL-C levels is reported to be an inverted U-shaped curve in men and nearly straight line in women 37) . However, we could not find that a low LDL-C level and a low BMI were directly related to in-hospital mortality in this study. Further confirmation is needed to determine the relationship between the short-term prognosis of patients with ICH and a low BMI.

The mechanisms by which low LDL-C levels increase the ICH risk remain unclear. Possible mechanisms include increased red blood cell fragility due to reduced cholesterol in the red blood cell membrane 38) , LDL-C-associated platelet activation and tissue factor expression 39) , and coagulation disorders 39 , 40) . Low LDL-C levels have been shown to promote the necrosis of smooth muscle cells in the arterial media, reducing resistance to vascular wall rupture 41) and may lead to the development of microaneurysms in the presence of HT 42) . Another possible mechanism is that low cholesterol levels may reduce platelet aggregation through changes in platelet-activating factors 43) . These mechanisms may explain why low LDL-C levels are associated with an increased risk of hematoma growth 17) . In contrast, hematoma growth has been identified as a factor contributing to mortality and a poor prognosis after ICH 44) . Furthermore, as a parameter related to nutrition, excessively low LDL-C levels were found to correlate with frailty in the elderly and an increase in all-cause mortality 45 , 46) . Based on these findings, low LDL-C levels may negatively influence the short-term prognosis of cerebral hemorrhage.

This multicenter registry study has several limitations that need to be addressed. Although stroke specialists at each hospital followed the stroke guidelines, treatment decisions were left to the discretion of the attending physicians, which may have affected the prognosis of the patients. Furthermore, the assessment of prognosis three months after discharge was difficult due to the large amount of missing data; therefore, we only evaluated the short-term prognosis at discharge. Moreover, the cause of death was not recorded in detail, which made it impossible to collect detailed statistics. Another limitation is the relatively small number of patients with LDL-C ＜70 mg/dL, which limited the statistical power to fully assess the outcomes in this subgroup. Nutritional assessment was also constrained because the serum albumin values were unavailable and total protein measurements were frequently missing, leaving the BMI as the only feasible surrogate marker of the nutritional status. Furthermore, the number of patients with lobar ICH or probable CAA was limited, making it difficult to draw any definitive conclusions regarding the association between low LDL-C levels and poor short-term outcomes or mortality in these subgroups, as well as the potential impact of statin use. The influence of different statins on these outcomes remains to be clarified in future studies. Therefore, further prospective studies are needed to address questions such as the long-term effects of LDL-C levels in patients with ICH and whether statins should be discontinued in these patients.

In conclusion, this multicenter study revealed a correlation between low LDL-C levels and in-hospital mortality after ICH. Notably, prior statin use was not linked to worse outcomes. Instead, it showed a potentially protective association with both mortality and the hematoma burden. These findings highlight the clinical importance of balanced lipid management in patients at risk for both ischemic and hemorrhagic events. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to determine the optimal LDL-C range that minimizes the risk of ischemic disease while avoiding adverse outcomes in ICH patients.
