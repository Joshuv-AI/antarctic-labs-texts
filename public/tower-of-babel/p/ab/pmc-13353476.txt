Mitochondria-related pathogenic genes in acute and chronic kidney disease: a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Jia Wang, TongLing Yuan, Qian Wei, Kangping Xie, Jiong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to utilize summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) to explore the potential associations linking mitochondrial-related genes with acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease CKD. This study utilized mitochondrial-related genes from the MitoCarta3.0 database, alongside DNA methylation (mQTLs), gene expression (eQTLs), and protein expression (pQTLs) quantitative trait loci data. We obtained summary statistics from genome-wide association studies for AKI and CKD from the United States Million Veteran Program, with subsequent validation in the UK Biobank. The SMR method, complemented by colocalization analysis, was used to assess associations. We further performed validation using kidney cortex-specific eQTL data from Genotype-Tissue Expression project and single-cell transcriptome data from the KPMP database. In the discovery datasets, SMR analysis identified 174 mQTLs, 64 eQTLs, and 20 pQTL associated with AKI risk, and 253 mQTLs, 80 eQTLs, and 23 pQTLs associated with CKD risk. A total of 32 mQTLs, 16 eQTLs and 9 pQTLs were identified as common signals associated with both AKI and CKD risks. Specifically, FASN, DELE1, SARS2, NDUFB2, and ATP23 were significantly associated with both AKI and CKD risks, while ATAD3B and GCDH were CKD-specific signals. Notably, the shared gene ATP23 was identified as a risk factor for both AKI and CKD in kidney cortex tissue. Furthermore, ATAD3B was validated as a potentially protective factor for CKD, although the effect size was modest (OR 0.95, 95% CI 0.90–1.00). This study utilized the SMR method to identify potential risk and protective genes for AKI and CKD, revealing a molecular link between mitochondrial-related genes and renal failure.

Introduction

Renal failure includes acute kidney injury (AKI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD). AKI is a clinical syndrome marked by a rapid decline in renal excretory function, leading to the buildup of metabolic waste products like creatinine and urea [1]. Mortality rates correlate with the severity of AKI, as reflected by rising serum creatinine levels [2]. Approximately 1.9% of hospitalized patients experience AKI, which is especially common among critically ill individuals [3]. CKD specifically refers to the progressive decline in kidney function, which can arise from various causes, including infections and immune-related injuries, often resulting in irreversible kidney damage [4]. CKD is a significant clinical challenge and is projected to become the 13th leading cause of death worldwide by 2030 [5]. Understanding the risk factors for kidney failure is crucial for improving prevention and treatment strategies.

Mitochondria perform a diverse array of interconnected functions, including the generation of ATP and numerous biosynthetic intermediates, as well as facilitating cellular stress responses such as autophagy and apoptosis[6]. Mitochondria are considered pivotal in AKI, where mitochondrial swelling and dysfunction leading to the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore is a key event in the progression of AKI. This process induces renal cell apoptosis through the release of pro-apoptotic mediators [7]. Defective mitochondrial dynamics plays a significant role in CKD. In patients with kidney disease [8], tubular cells and podocytes exhibit increased mitochondrial fragmentation[9]. Defective mitochondrial biogenesis also plays a crucial role in CKD [10]. Furthermore, research indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts the crosstalk between mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to tubular inflammation and fibrosis, as well as the transition from AKI to CKD [11]. Given the importance of mitochondrial function in the pathogenesis of renal failure, this study aimed to explore the causal relationships between mitochondrial dysfunction and renal failure risk and how to precisely target this process for therapeutic purposes in clinical practice.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a powerful statistical technique used to infer causality in observational data by leveraging genetic variants as instrumental variables [12]. Summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR) extends this approach by integrating summary statistics from multiple omics datasets to explore causal relationships between genetic variants and complex traits or diseases [13]. This method integrates summary statistics of DNA methylation quantitative trait loci (mQTLs), gene-related expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs), and protein expression quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) to provide a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying various diseases. This methodology is particularly useful when investigating the molecular underpinnings of kidney disease [14]. Therefore, this study aims to utilize SMR to identify potential associations linking genetically predicted mitochondrial-related genes with AKI and CKD, thereby providing insights into the underlying molecular mechanisms. Key findings will be validated using external single-cell transcriptomic data, providing valuable targets for future research.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study adheres to the STROBE-MR reporting guidelines and employs SMR analysis to explore the potential relationships between mitochondrial-related genes with acute and chronic kidney disease. The methodology involves a comprehensive analysis of genetic data, utilizing multi-omics SMR and HEIDI tests to evaluate the associations between genes and disease, as well as colocalization analysis to identify shared genetic determinants. Signals associated with AKI and CKD risks were subsequently integrated and validated at the tissue eQTL level and single-cell level using external datasets. The research workflow and analysis methods are shown in Figure 1. This study exclusively utilized publicly available summary statistics from previously published studies that had already obtained all necessary ethical approvals and patient consents. Consequently, no separate ethical approval was needed for this analysis.

Data source

We utilized GWAS summary statistics for renal failure from multiple large-scale consortia. For the discovery phase, we obtained data for AKI from the Million Veteran Program (MVP) under PheCode 585.1, which included 43,017 cases and 388,904 controls [15]. Similarly, the discovery dataset for CKD was sourced from the MVP under PheCode 585.3, comprising 64,595 cases and 374,886 controls [15]. All data used in this study were derived exclusively from individuals of European ancestry; notably, the MVP data were confirmed to be of European ancestry based on the harmonized ancestry and race/ethnicity (HARE) standard.

For replication, we used datasets from the UK Biobank (UKB) PheWeb, which were restricted to White British participants. The UKB PheCode 585.1 cohort (4533 cases and 395,835 controls) served as the validation cohort for AKI, and the UKB PheCode 585.3 cohort (2649 cases and 395,832 controls) served as the validation cohort for CKD (Supplemental Table S1). The consistency of PheCode definitions across both discovery (MVP) and replication (UKB) cohorts ensures a rigorous and comparable case-ascertainment strategy. Replication was defined as concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 in the UK Biobank.

The case definitions for these PheCodes are based on both the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10). Specifically, AKI (PheCode 585.1) was defined by ICD-9 codes (584.5–584.9) and ICD-10 codes (N17, N17.0-N17.2, N17.8-N17.9). CKD (PheCode 585.3) was defined by ICD-9 codes (585, 585.0) and ICD-10 codes (N18, N18.9). All GWAS summary statistics as provided by the original studies were already adjusted for principal components to account for any subtle population structure, minimizing potential confounding from population stratification.

We downloaded 1,136 mitochondrial genes from the MitoCarta3.0 database. Blood eQTL data for mitochondrial genes were obtained from eQTLGen [16], including genetic data for blood gene expression from 31,684 individuals. Blood mQTL summary data were obtained from a meta-analysis of two European cohorts [17]: the Brisbane Systems Genetics Study (n = 614) and the Lothian Birth Cohorts (n = 1366). Blood pQTL summary data were obtained from Pietzner et al. [18], Benjamin et al. [19], and Ferkingstad et al. [20], which contained 10,708, 54,219 and 35,559 individuals of European ancestry, respectively.

To further evaluate the tissue-specific expression of target genes and explore the potential causal impact of mitochondrial mechanism genes on renal failure, we used tissue-specific expression eQTL data from the Genotype-Tissue Expression project (GTEx) database (https://gtexportal.org/home/). The GTEx v8 dataset includes 838 donors and 17,382 samples from 52 tissues and two cell lines. In the analysis of renal failure diseases, we used the Kidney_Cortex eQTL data. In addition, we utilized the Kidney Precision Medicine Project (KPMP) database to visualize the expression of key genes in renal tissues at the single cell level [21]. The KPMP dataset includes data from biopsy samples from 79 participants, comprising 28 healthy references, 14 with AKI and 37 with CKD. For quantitative classification of gene expression patterns in the KPMP single-cell data, “diffuse” expression was defined as detection in ≥80% of cell types with mean log2(TPM + 1) < 1.5 [22]. “Low” expression was defined as below the median expression level of all genes detected in the respective cell type [23,24]. These thresholds were applied to ensure reproducibility of expression pattern descriptions.

SMR analysis

To evaluate the relationship between mitochondrial gene methylation, expression, and protein abundance with kidney failure, we used the SMR software (version 1.3.1) for SMR and HEIDI tests. Compared to traditional Mendelian Randomization (MR), SMR offers enhanced statistical power, especially when analyzing top-associated cis-QTLs within a ± 1000 kb window and setting a stringent P-value threshold of 5.0 × 10−8. We excluded SNPs with allele frequency differences greater than 0.2, using –diff-freq-prop 1 to set the maximum allowable difference.

In this study, we applied SMR to test for molecular cascades from methylation to gene expression to protein abundance. Specifically, we evaluated whether a methylation QTL (mQTL) is associated with gene expression (eQTL), and whether an eQTL is associated with protein abundance (pQTL), by assessing the consistency of genetic effect estimates across shared variants. This approach is a standard application of SMR for molecular mediation analysis rather than a traditional exposure-outcome framework [25]. The HEIDI test evaluates heterogeneity across cis-SNPs; p-HEIDI > 0.05 supports absence of horizontal pleiotropy, indicating a shared causal variant rather than linkage disequilibrium. For eQTL and mQTL colocalization, we used results with p-SMR < 0.05, and p-HEIDI > 0.05 [26]. To control for multiple testing, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately for mQTL, eQTL, and pQTL analyses. An FDR < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Colocalization analysis

We used the R package coloc for colocalization analysis to identify shared causal variants between mitochondrial gene-associated cis-QTLs (eQTLs, mQTLs, pQTLs) and kidney failure. This analysis helps infer whether GWAS signals colocalize with QTLs, suggesting that variants may influence the phenotype by altering gene-related biological processes. The analysis reports five posterior probabilities (H0 to H4), with H4 indicating a shared causal variant and H3 indicating two distinct causal variants. For this analysis, the colocalization region was delineated as a window centered on the gene, spanning ±500 [27], ±1000 [28], ±1000 kb [29] for mQTL, eQTL and pQTL level respectively. To allow for colocalization with QTLs that have weaker p-values, a QTL signal and a GWAS signal were considered to colocalize if they met both of the following criteria: (1) a high posterior probability for H4 (PP.H4) > 0.5 when the prior probability p12 was set to 5 × 10−5, and (2) a low posterior probability for H3 (PP.H3) < 0.5 when p12 was set to 1 × 10−5 [30]. These relatively permissive thresholds were chosen based on prior exploratory multi-omics studies and are appropriate for hypothesis-generating analyses. Sensitivity analyses using a more stringent threshold (PP.H4 > 0.7) were performed, and the main conclusions remained consistent.

Other statistical analysis details

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R (v4.3.0). The R package ggplot2 was used for creating Manhattan plots, and forestplot was used for generating forest plots. The code for SMRLocusPlot and SMREffectPlot was adapted from Zhu et al. [13].

Results

Integrating AKI and CKD GWAS and mitochondria-related blood mQTL data

After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (FDR < 0.05) applied separately for mQTL analysis, our study identified 174 methylation sites (corresponding to 93 genes) associated with AKI risk (p-SMR < 0.05 & p-HEIDI > 0.05) (detailed FDR-adjusted P-values are provided in Supplemental Table S2; Figure S1A). Among the identified signals, 44 methylation loci (22 genes) were found to colocalize with AKI risk (Supplemental Figure S2A). For instance, cg22110888 and cg25639749 (located near FASN) showed diverse effect estimate directions, with cg22110888 methylation positively associated with AKI risk (OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 1.01–1.05), while cg25639749 methylation exhibiting the opposite (OR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.83–0.95). Finally, the methylation level of 7 genes (FAHD1, FASN, GLOD4, OXR1, SLC25A44, SPHKAP and UQCR10) were replicated in the UK Biobank with concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 (Supplemental Table S3).

After FDR correction, a total of 253 methylation loci (122 genes) were associated with CKD risk (p-SMR < 0.05 & p-HEIDI > 0.05) (detailed FDR-adjusted P-values are provided in Supplemental Table S4; Figure S1B). Then 52 methylation loci (33 genes) were found to colocalize with CKD risk (Supplemental Figure S2B), where cg25639749 (FASN) methylation was negatively associated with CKD risk (OR = 0.89, 95% CI = 0.84–0.95). Finally, 30 methylation loci corresponding to 7 genes (MRPS15, PNPT1, DMGDH, COQ3, MTFR1, ACSF3, PDK2, SLC25A42, ECH1, TXNRD2 and COMT) were replicated in the UK Biobank with concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 (Supplemental Table S5). Notably, a total of 32 CpG sites (corresponding to 20 genes) were identified as being associated with both AKI and CKD risks (Supplemental Table S6; Figure S3A).

Integrating AKI and CKD GWAS and mitochondria-related blood eQTL data

After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (FDR < 0.05) applied separately for eQTL analysis, our study identified 64 genes associated with AKI risk (p-SMR < 0.05 & p-HEIDI > 0.05) (detailed FDR-adjusted P-values are provided in Supplemental Table S7; Figure 2(A)). Among them, 17 genes were found to colocalize with AKI risk (Supplemental Figure S2C). For instance, the expression of DELE1 was negatively associated with AKI risk (OR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.87–1.00). Finally, the expression level of 6 genes (BCL2L2, HAGH, MRPL48, NDUFB6, PLPBP and PRXL2A) were replicated in the UK Biobank with concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 (Supplemental Table S8).

After FDR correction, a total of 80 genes were found to be associated with CKD risk (p-SMR < 0.05 & p-HEIDI > 0.05) (detailed FDR-adjusted P-values are provided in Supplemental Table S9; Figure 2(B)). 15 genes were colocalized with CKD risk, where DELE1 expression was negatively associated with CKD risk (OR = 0.87, 95% CI = 0.82–0.92) (Supplemental Figure S2D). Finally, the expression level of 4 genes (ATAD3B, CPT1B, ECH1 and PNPT1) were replicated in the UK Biobank with concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 (Supplemental Table S10). Integrating AKI and CKD GWAS and mitochondria-related blood pQTL data

After Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (FDR < 0.05) applied separately for pQTL analysis, our study identified 20 proteins associated with AKI risk (p-SMR < 0.05 & p-HEIDI > 0.05) (detailed FDR-adjusted P-values are provided in Supplemental Table S12; Figure 2(C)). Among them, 6 proteins were found to colocalize with AKI risk (Supplemental Figure S2E). For instance, the level of ALDH2 was positively associated with AKI risk (OR = 1.32, 95% CI = 1.20–1.45). Finally, the level of 2 proteins (HAGH and NUDT5) were replicated in the UK Biobank with concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 (Supplemental Table S13). After FDR correction, a total of 23 proteins were associated with CKD risk (p-SMR < 0.05 & p-HEIDI > 0.05) (detailed FDR-adjusted P-values are provided in Supplemental Table S14; Figure 2(D)). 8 proteins were colocalized with CKD risk, where the GCDH level was negatively associated with CKD risk (OR = 0.86, 95% CI = 0.78–0.94) (Supplemental Figure S2F). Finally, the level of 3 proteins (COMT, NUDT5 and ECHS1) were replicated in the UK Biobank with concordant direction of effect and nominal p < 0.05 (Supplemental Table S15). Notably, a total of 9 proteins were identified as being associated with both AKI and CKD risks (Supplemental Table S16; Figure S3C).

A key finding was the consistent localization of the shared risk gene, DELE1. It was found to be highly expressed in microvascular endothelial cells in both AKI and CKD renal tissues, providing cellular-level evidence for mitochondrial stress as a common pathological event linking the two conditions.

In contrast, other genes displayed distinct patterns. The core respiratory chain component, NDUFB2, was broadly and highly expressed across various renal tubular and collecting duct epithelial cells in both AKI and CKD, confirming its fundamental role in kidney energy metabolism. Interestingly, the shared risk gene ATP23 and the CKD-specific protective gene, ATAD3B, showed a diffuse but low-level expression pattern in CKD tissues, consistent with its potential role as a baseline homeostatic factor. The expression pattern of FASN was particularly informative. While it showed notable expression in specific cell types in AKI such as cycling epithelial cells and ascending thin limb cells, its expression was markedly low in CKD tissues.

Discussion

Our study applied the SMR method to investigate the relationship between mitochondrial genes and renal failure diseases using data from large-scale datasets. We observed shared genes with AKI and CKD (FASN, DELE1, SARS2, NDUFB2, and ATP23) as associated with disease risks. In addition, ATAD3B and GCDH were revealed as CKD-specific genes associated with its risk. It is worth highlighting that the tissue-specific expression of ATP23 and ATAD3B has been supported by kidney cortex eQTL data.

A key finding of our study is the identification of ATAD3B as a potentially protective factor for CKD, though the effect size was borderline. To our knowledge, a direct link between ATAD3B and kidney disease has not been previously established. Our SMR results suggest a potential protective effect against CKD at both the blood and the kidney cortex eQTL levels. ATAD3B encodes a mitochondrial mitophagy receptor that promotes oxidative stress-induced clearance of damaged mitochondrial DNA, may play a role in mitochondrial quality control [31]. The kidney, particularly the renal tubules, is a highly metabolic organ and is exceptionally vulnerable to oxidative stress and subsequent mtDNA damage, which are known hallmarks of CKD [32]. Therefore, ATAD3B-mediated mitophagy pathway would be critical for maintaining mitochondrial quality control and cellular homeostasis in the kidney. Our finding that genetically predicted lower expression of ATAD3B increases CKD risk suggests that impaired clearance of damaged mitochondria may be a key mechanism driving CKD progression.

ATP23 (ATP23 metallopeptidase) is a mitochondrial inner membrane metalloprotease that processes and assembles ATP synthase subunit 6 during oxidative phosphorylation [33]. It plays dual roles in proteolytic maturation and facilitating ATP synthase complex assembly, which is crucial for maintaining mitochondrial ATP production in energy-demanding tissues such as the kidney. While its role in mitochondrial OXPHOS assembly is well-established, direct clinical evidence linking ATP23 dysfunction or mutations to AKI or CKD remains limited. In this study, we suggested that the expression of ATP23 was positively associated with AKI and CKD risks across both the blood and tissue level, implicating ATP23 as a shared pathogenic factor in both conditions. Moreover, persistent mitochondrial dysfunction during AKI can progress to CKD and end-stage renal disease through sustained inflammation and defective metabolic reprogramming [34], where impaired mitochondrial ATP production played a critical role. Therefore, our identification of ATP23 as a shared pathogenic factor represents a contribution in understanding the molecular links between AKI and CKD. Future studies elucidating the mechanistic details of ATP23 in patients with AKI or CKD could clarify whether ATP23 dysfunction contributes to these kidney disorders.

DELE1 is a mitochondrial stress–responsive protein that communicates perturbations in mitochondrial import and processing to the cytosol, activating components of the integrated stress response (ISR) and influencing transcriptional programs via the ATF4/CHOP pathways [35]. While direct renal-specific demonstrations are still being established, the broader literature on mitochondrial stress signaling supports a plausible role for DELE1 in renal injury contexts. Mechanistically, DELE1 could engage in pathways that can potentially lead to ATF4/CHOP-driven transcriptional responses and modulate translation-related and proteostatic processes under stress, thus affecting AKI and CKD risks in tissues with high mitochondrial demand. On the other hand, FASN encodes fatty acid synthase, the principal cytosolic enzyme catalyzing the de novo synthesis of long-chain fatty acids from acetyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA [36]. Chronic kidney diseases commonly feature dyslipidemia and impaired lipid handling, contributing to lipotoxic injury, inflammation, and fibrosis [37]. Consequently, FASN represents a plausible regulator of lipid-related renal injury. This pathogenic role is supported by mounting evidence; studies have shown that increased FASN expression promotes renal tubular lipid accumulation and extracellular matrix deposition in models of metabolic syndrome [38], while its downregulation is associated with protection against fibrosis in obstructive nephropathy [39]. FASN has also been implicated in AKI. For example, one clinical study reported that FASN levels were decreased in the urine of patients with established sepsis-induced AKI [40]. Our SMR results build upon this by providing the genetic evidence for a contribution of FASN to AKI susceptibility. In this study, our findings revealed that the expressions of DELE1 and FASN serve as shared risk factors for AKI and CKD. Moreover, single-cell transcriptome analysis indicated high expression of DELE1 in the endothelial cells of microvessels in tissues from AKI and CKD patients. These findings suggest that, during the pathological process of kidney diseases, the renal microvascular system undergoes intense mitochondrial stress, with DELE1 acting as a potential signaling molecule. This stress in endothelial cells disrupts their function, promotes barrier leakage, and triggers inflammatory cascades, thereby exacerbating renal tissue injury. Further studies are required to elucidate the involvement of DELE1 and FASN in both conditions and the detailed mechanism by which both genes contribute to disease risks.

The integrated SMR analyses identified shared genes, NDUFB2 and SARS2 as negatively associated with both AKI and CKD. NDUFB2 encodes a subunit of mitochondrial Complex I, which is part of the respiratory chain that transfers electrons from NADH to ubiquinone. Although considered an accessory subunit, it is crucial for the assembly and stability of Complex I, thereby supporting efficient electron transport and ATP production [41]. SARS2 encodes seryl-tRNA synthetase 2, mitochondrial, an enzyme found in both mitochondria and cytosol that attaches serine to its corresponding tRNA during protein synthesis. In this study, we demonstrated that NDUFB2 expression was negatively associated with the risks of AKI and CKD, with its methylation level negatively regulating its expression. SARS2 expression was found to be negatively associated with AKI risk, positively correlated with its protein level, and its protein level was negatively associated with AKI and CKD risks. While direct evidence regarding these genes and AKI and CKD are rather limited, the broader implication of mitochondrial dysfunction in AKI/CKD suggests disturbances in mitochondrial energy production, quality control, and signaling can exacerbate tubular injury, promote maladaptive repair, and foster fibrotic remodeling. On the other hand, GCDH encodes glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase, a mitochondrial enzyme required for the catabolism of lysine, hydroxylysine, and tryptophan. Deficiency causes glutaric aciduria type I (GA1), a metabolic disorder that can involve renal manifestations such as acute renal failure in some cases, likely through systemic metabolic derangements and mitochondrial dysfunction [42]. Our findings indicate that GCDH serves as a CKD-specific signal, with its expression and protein level negatively associated with CKD risk, while also showing a positive correlation between its expression and protein level. Future research are required to understand the detailed mechanisms by which GCDH served as a protective role against kidney diseases.

This study leverages SMR analysis, using genetic variants as instrumental variables to mitigate confounding factors. This approach provides a more precise evaluation of relationships between mitochondrial gene methylation, expression, and protein abundance with kidney failure. The SMR findings were further validated by tissue-specific eQTL and single-cell transcriptome analyses, confirming their relevance to AKI and CKD risks. We compared our findings with previous genetic studies on both AKI and CKD. While traditional GWAS have identified risk loci, such as the DISP1-TLR5 region for AKI [43], and recent SMR studies have identified new candidate genes for CKD, such as GATM [44], our findings revealed novel candidate genes potentially implicated in AKI and CKD, such as ATAD3B and ATP23, supported by tissue-level eQTL validation. Despite significant findings, several limitations must be acknowledged. Reliance on summary-level data may limit the capacity to uncover nuanced interactions between genetic and epigenetic factors, potentially obscuring complex relationships within the data. Additionally, the overlap between significant eQTL and pQTL signals was limited, with successful eQTL and pQTL regulatory linkage identified for a key subset of genes such as SARS2 and GCDH but not for all candidates. This highlights the ongoing need for more comprehensive proteomic QTL datasets. Moreover, our primary definitions, based on ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, may lack the precision of detailed clinical criteria for AKI and CKD. Thus, our findings reflect associations with these broad clinical entities, not specific etiologies, subtypes, or disease severities. Future studies using individual-level data with uniform, in-depth phenotyping are needed to explore these nuances. Furthermore, the data used in this study were derived exclusively from individuals of European ancestry, which restricts the generalizability of the conclusions to other populations. Future research should include diverse genetic backgrounds to validate these findings across different ethnic groups. Finally, it’s worth noting that our study relies on summary-level data, which precludes us from conducting a detailed analysis of how individual-level demographic and clinical characteristics may influence our results.

Conclusion

In summary, our study has identified shared genetic factors between AKI and CKD, including FASN, DELE1, SARS2, NDUFB2, and ATP23, as well as CKD-specific genes such as ATAD3B and GCDH. Notably, the tissue-specific expression of ATP23 and ATAD3B has been validated in tissue eQTL datasets. These findings provide valuable insights into the shared and distinct genetic underpinnings of AKI and CKD, and may pave the way for future research into these renal disorders.
