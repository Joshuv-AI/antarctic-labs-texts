The effect of empathic communication-based training on women’s positive birth perception, awareness, and birth experience: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Emine Aksüt Akçay, Sultan Alan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmpathic communication during childbirth can improve women’s birth perception and reduce fear and trauma, yet evidence on its effects across diverse populations is limited. This study addresses this gap by examining its impact on primiparous women’s psychological outcomes, providing evidence to support holistic maternity care.Thus, this randomized controlled trial examined the effect of empathic communication-based training on women’s awareness, positive perception of birth, and overall birth experience.MethodsBetween June 2023 and September 2024, 72 pregnant women attending the obstetrics and gynecology outpatient clinics of Kahramanmaraş Necip Fazıl City Hospital, Turkey, were randomly assigned to intervention or control groups. The intervention, an empathic communication-based training, was developed and delivered by the researchers. The study included three stages: development of the Positive Birth Perception and Awareness Scale (PBPAS), creation of the Empathic Communication-Based Positive Birth Guide, and assessment of group differences. Data were collected using the Prenatal Demographic Form, PBPAS, and the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire-A (WDEQ-A). Statistical analyses included chi-square tests and independent and paired t-tests, with results reported alongside 95% confidence intervals.ResultsThe PBPAS demonstrated high reliability. In the intervention group, PBPAS scores increased significantly from 68.24 ± 7.12 to 85.36 ± 6.48 (p < 0.001), while the control group showed no significant change (69.12 ± 8.03 to 71.45 ± 7.89, p = 0.08). WDEQ-A scores decreased significantly in the intervention group (80.45 ± 9.32 to 65.88 ± 10.12, p < 0.001) and increased in the control group (74.16 ± 8.54 to 79.88 ± 12.24, p = 0.02).ConclusionEmpathic communication improves primiparous women’s psychological outcomes and birth experiences. Future studies should assess its long-term effects, and healthcare systems should integrate empathic communication training to support holistic maternity care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08239-y.Keywords: Empathy, Awareness, Perception of birth, Midwifery, Birth experience

Introduction

Maternal mortality and perinatal mental health remain global concerns, with 10–13% of women experiencing perinatal mental disorders, highlighting the need for integrated maternal care and evidence-based interventions; continuous midwifery support, empathic communication, and structured programs targeting birth perception and psychological readiness have been shown to enhance maternal well-being, particularly amid the medicalization of childbirth [1–5]. This study aims to evaluate the effect of a structured empathic communication-based intervention on primiparous women’s birth perception, awareness, and psychological readiness, providing evidence to inform holistic maternity care practices.

The literature highlights a gap in understanding the impact of individualized interventions on maternal birth experiences across diverse populations. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the effect of a structured intervention on primiparous women’s birth self-efficacy and fear of childbirth, providing evidence on how such approaches can enhance maternal outcomes and inform holistic maternity care practices [5, 6]. Additionally, childbirth carries social and cultural meanings shaped by traditions, beliefs, and societal values [6]. Supporting mothers in early reintegration into daily life, and fostering positive behavioral patterns, such as secure mother–infant attachment, is essential [6, 7]. This study contributes to the literature by exploring how structured support interventions can improve both psychological well-being and birth experiences, addressing a critical gap in current maternity care research [1–7].

Although empathic communication in midwifery has been associated with improved birth experiences and maternal outcomes [1, 8–10], evidence regarding its effectiveness across diverse populations and healthcare settings remains limited [14, 15]. Previous studies have demonstrated that positive, respectful, and informative interactions by healthcare personnel can enhance mothers’ confidence, birth perception, and mother-infant bonding [11–13]. However, inconsistencies exist in the reported effects of training interventions on women’s awareness, birth self-efficacy, and fear of childbirth, indicating that current educational approaches may not uniformly translate into improved outcomes [12, 14].

These gaps highlight the need for structured interventions that specifically target empathic communication skills among maternity care providers and assess their impact on women’s birth experiences [13, 15]. By addressing these underexplored areas, the present study investigates the effect of a structured empathic communication-based intervention on women’s birth perception, awareness, and psychological readiness, aiming to provide evidence that informs targeted educational strategies and enhances overall maternal care quality [13, 15].

This study aims to develop a scale to assess women’s perception and awareness regarding positive birth experiences, to create a training guide based on empathic communication, and to evaluate the impact of this training on women’s birth perception, awareness, and overall birth experience. By addressing gaps in previous research on structured interventions and empathic communication, the study seeks to provide evidence-based strategies to enhance maternal psychological readiness and improve the quality of maternity care.

Method

Research design

The study was conducted in three stages. In the first stage, a methodological approach was used to develop the Positive Birth Perception and Awareness Scale. In the second stage, a guide for Empathic Communication Based Positive Childbirth Training was developed. In the third and final stage, a randomized controlled study was conducted to assess the effect of interactive, face-to-face guided training on birth experience and positive birth perception and awareness. This study is according to research plan (Fig. 1) and consort guideline (Fig. 2).

Sampling method

A randomized controlled intervention study was designed to evaluate the effects of empathetic communication–based education on women’s positive birth perception, awareness, and overall birth experience. The research was conducted in three stages.

Stage 1

A methodological study was undertaken to develop the Positive Birth Perception and Awareness Scale. The study population consisted of pregnant women attending the obstetrics outpatient clinics of Kahramanmaraş Necip Fazıl City Hospital. A total of 800 participants were recruited using a simple random sampling method. In addition, 12 midwife-academics provided expert opinions for content validation. The overall process of scale development is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Scale Development Diagram

Stage 2

A guide for Empathic Communication–Based Positive Childbirth Training was developed. For content validation, a total of 13 experts were consulted, selected through a purposive sampling method. These experts included 6 midwife-academics, 1 obstetrician, 1 psychologist, 1 social worker, 1 education specialist, and 3 practicing midwives. The development process of the training guide is presented in Fig. 4.

Training Guide Development Diagram

Stage 3

A randomized controlled experimental study was conducted to assess the effectiveness of interactive, face-to-face guided training on women’s birth experiences, positive birth perception, and awareness. The study population consisted of pregnant women enrolled in the Pregnancy Preparation School at Kahramanmaraş Necip Fazıl City Hospital. A total of 80 participants were randomly assigned to the intervention (n = 40) and control (n = 40) groups using a simple randomization method. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Population and sample

The study was conducted between 1 June 2023 and 30 September 2024. The first stage of the study was conducted in the obstetrics outpatient clinic of a state hospital in southern Turkiye. The final, third stage of the study was conducted at the childbirth preparation class and the labor and delivery clinic of the same hospital. The study sample included women who met the inclusion criteria—being pregnant, aged 18 years or older, having no high-risk pregnancy complications, and being able to communicate in Turkish—and did not meet any of the exclusion criteria, which included having a high-risk pregnancy, a history of psychiatric disorders, or any condition preventing participation in the intervention; all participants voluntarily agreed to take part and attended the childbirth preparation class.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the obstetrics and gynecology outpatient clinics of xxxxxxxxxxxx Hospital, Turkey. This hospital was selected due to its high patient volume, diverse patient population, and comprehensive maternal care services, which provided a representative sample of pregnant women in the region. Additionally, the hospital’s outpatient clinics routinely offer prenatal education programs, making it an ideal setting to implement and evaluate an empathic communication-based intervention in a real-world clinical environment. The availability of trained midwives and supportive infrastructure ensured the feasibility and consistency of the intervention delivery. The hospital also offers routine prenatal education programs, facilitating the implementation of the intervention.

Sampling method and randomization

The study included pregnant women aged 18 years and over, able to speak and understand Turkish, literate, without a risky pregnancy, and between 32- and 40-weeks’ gestation. Pregnant women eligible for participation were assigned to groups using the Random Integer Generator method under the “Numbers” section of the random.org website to ensure completely random and equal probability assignment. Therefore, considering the potential for case loss (10%), a total of 80 pregnant women (40 experimental, 40 control) were planned to be included in the study. During the randomization process, random numbers between 1 and 80 were generated, and pregnant women were randomized according to their registration numbers using the numbers 1 and 2. At the beginning of the study, a draw was conducted to assign 1 to the experimental group and 2 to the control group.

In the third stage of the study, the required number of participants was determined. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 program [16], considering Cohen’s (1988) recommendation for a medium effect size and the findings of Khwepeya et al. (2022) on fear of childbirth [17, 18]. As the primary objective was to evaluate the effectiveness of the training, the minimum sample size was based on the significance of between-group differences. The effect size was set at d = 0.67, and a two-tailed hypothesis with a 95% confidence interval and 5% significance level was applied, indicating that 72 participants (36 experimental, 36 control) were required. During the study, pregnant women who chose to withdraw, could not be contacted, did not have a vaginal delivery, or underwent cesarean section were excluded from the sample. Accordingly, one participant from the experimental group and two from the control group withdrew, while three participants (two experimental, one control) were excluded due to their mode of delivery. Overall, eight participants were excluded, resulting in the final sample of 72 pregnant women. A post hoc power analysis indicated that the study achieved 80% power (1-β = 0.80; critical t = 1.99). The consort guideline will be presented as Fig. 2.

Intervention and data collection

After the pregnant women in the study were randomized into groups, both groups received a pretest. The intervention group then received two 45-minute, interactive, 90-minute face-to-face training sessions based on empathic communication and a positive birth perception guide. The training sessions were conducted by a midwife and researcher who is an expert in the field. Following the pretest, the pregnant women in the intervention group were introduced to the educational materials during their first encounter, and the first session focused on empathic communication. The second training session was scheduled approximately one month after the first, and focused on positive birth perception and empathic communication. The phone numbers of the pregnant women who completed the training were obtained, and they were scheduled to meet at the birth. The study concluded with E. A. A. accompanying the pregnant women in the intervention group to the birth, completing the questionnaires in person during the first four hours postpartum and by phone on the 41 st day postpartum. Completing the data collection forms took approximately 15–30 min. Pregnant women in the control group received no intervention. In line with the equality principle of the research, after the post-test data were collected by the researcher through face-to-face interviews, the “Empathic Communication Based Positive Birth Perception Training” material was given to the pregnant women in the control group for training purposes, either in person or online.

Data collection tools

The data were collected using a Prenatal Demographic Form, the draft version of the Positive Birth Perception and Awareness Scale (PBPAS) developed by the researchers, and the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ-A).

The Prenatal Demographic Form was developed by the researchers in accordance with the relevant literature. The form consists of 24 questions about sociodemographic information, general health and psychosocial characteristics, obstetric history, and details about the newborn in the postpartum period.

The Positive Birth Perception and Awareness Scale (PBPAS) consists of 28 items across six sub-dimensions measuring perception and awareness of positive birth. Participants rate their responses on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Undecided, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly Agree). Higher scores indicate a more positive perception and awareness of birth. Scale scores range from a minimum of 0 to a maximum of 140, and the total score increases as the scale scores increase. For the scale’s internal consistency, Cronbach’s alpha was measured as 0.878. The researcher conducted the validity and reliability study of the scale.

Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ-A) was developed by Klaas and Barbro Wijma to assess the fear of childbirth experienced by women [19]. The scale consists of 33 items. The questionnaire was adapted into Turkish by Körükçü et al. [20]. Participants rate their responses on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 5 (“extremely”). The scale ranges from a minimum score of 0 to a maximum score of 165. Higher scores indicate a more serious fear of childbirth. Items 2, 3, 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, 15, 19, 20, 24, 25, 27, and 31 are reverse scored. Körükçü et al. (2012) assessed the reliability and validity of the scale and reported a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 and a split-half reliability of 0.91. The W-DEQ-A scores are categorized into four groups to assess the level of childbirth fear [20]. Mild Fear (W-DEQ-A score ≤ 37), Moderate Fear (W-DEQ-A score between 38 and 65), Severe Fear (W-DEQ-A score between 66 and 84), and Clinical Fear (W-DEQ-A score ≥ 85) [20].

Psychometric analysis and validation of PBPAS

In the first phase of the study, validity and reliability analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 22 and AMOS V25. In the exploratory factor analysis, sample adequacy was assessed using the KMO value and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. To explain the factor structure, principal component analysis was used as the factor extraction method, and the Varimax method, which best explains variance, was chosen as the rotation method. The factor structures were illustrated using a scree plot. The rotated component matrix was used to determine the factor to which each item was most highly correlated. For internal consistency assessment, correlation-based item analysis was conducted for the total scale and subscales. Hotelling’s T² analysis was performed (P < 0.05 means there is a difference between the scale items on average). The the split half (Guttman) reliability results were obtained (the closer the result is to 1, the higher the internal consistency). Furthermore, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were calculated. Cronbach’s alpha values between 0.81 < α < 1.00 were considered high; between 0.61 < α < 0.80, were considered medium; between 0.41 < α < 0.60, were considered low; and between 0.00 < α < 0.40, were considered unreliable. The test-retest method was used to assess the stability of the scale over time (The questions with a correlation value below + 0.3 in the retest are considered incompatible). The test-retest reliability of the overall scale score was evaluated by comparing the correlations of the subscale and total scores from the baseline and post-intervention assessments. To assess construct validity, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to verify the fit of factors obtained through exploratory factor analysis. The results were evaluated based on the fit indices. Confirmatory factor analysis employed a fit index. In the evaluation, CFI (Comparative Fit Index): ≥ 0.90 is acceptable; ≥ 0.95 is good fit. NFI (Normed Fit Index): ≥ 0.90 is acceptable. RMSEA (Root Mean Square Error of Approximation): ≤ 0.08 is acceptable; ≤ 0.05 is good fit. The χ² value was not found to be significant. GFI (Goodness of Fit Index): ≥ 0.90 is acceptable; ≥ 0.95 is good fit [21–23].

Data analysis

During the third and final stage, the data obtained were analyzed using the SPSS 22.0 software package. The analyses were reported using descriptive statistical measures (mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values, and percentage). Since the assumptions for parametric tests were met, the independent samples t-test was used to determine differences between the means of two independent groups; and the Chi-Square test was applied to assess differences between categorical variables (the Bonferroni-corrected Z test was applied to identify which groups contributed to significant differences). The Paired Sample t-test was used to evaluate the the pre- and post-education average scores in the experimental and control groups. The significance level was set at 0.05. To assess normality of the data, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test statistic and p-value were examined, and skewness and kurtosis coefficients were calculated. According to Tabachnick and Fidell’s (2013) recommendation, data distribution is considered to be within normal limits if the p-value is greater than 0.05 in the normality test or if the skewness and kurtosis coefficients fall within the ± 2 range [24].

Discussion

This study aimed to develop a scale and training guide based on empathic communication and evaluate their impact on women’s birth perception, awareness, and overall experience [17–25], with participants’ mean age (29.1 years) falling within the national peak fertility range of 20–30 years [28, 29], and no significant differences between experimental and control groups in sociodemographic, obstetric, or delivery-related factors, supporting sample representativeness and homogeneous distribution. To evaluate the effectiveness of the Empathic Communication-Based Positive Birth Guide, PBPAS total scores and subscale scores were compared between experimental and control groups. While the control group showed non-significant increases across all subscales, the experimental group demonstrated significant improvements in the cognitive support and perceptual support subscales, referred to as subdomains, which assess labor interventions, birth practices, pain management, quality of care, and postpartum newborn care. Although childbirth is a natural process, unnecessary interventions—such as restricted mobility, fundal pressure, and institutional protocols—can disrupt labor and negatively impact women’s birth perception and overall experience.

Interventions such as oxytocin administration, enema application, and movement restrictions during labor are associated with negative perceptions and mood disturbances [30], whereas a supportive, well-designed birth environment combined with empathic communication and a holistic approach—including appropriate delivery room settings, hospital-specific clothing, and individualized attention—reduces stress and anxiety and promotes a positive, individualized birth experience [25, 31]. Disrespectful treatment by healthcare professionals has been linked to higher perceptions of traumatic birth [32]. The observed improvement in cognitive subscale scores in our study aligns with existing literature and is likely due to the empathic communication-based training, midwifery interventions provided twice during pregnancy, and the researcher midwife’s involvement during birth. Primiparous women are more likely to perceive childbirth negatively and experience elevated anxiety and fear [33]; however, the lack of significant differences in obstetric characteristics, including parity, reflects the balanced distribution of pregnancies and overall comparability of study groups.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has some strengths and limitations. One of its key strengths is that no other study has been conducted with a positive birth perception and awareness scale and an empathic communication-based positive birth training guide. The Positive Birth Perception and Awareness Scale is the first tool developed to assess pregnant women’s perception and awareness of positive birth experiences and was first used in this study.

The findings are limited to the study population. In terms of limitations, the findings are restricted to the study population and may differ in community-based studies involving pregnant women from diverse regions. During the development process, the scale and guide were administered only to pregnant and puerperal women who were over 18 years of age, Turkish-speaking, without mental distress, and risky pregnancy status.

Conclusion and recommendations

The 28-item, six-factor PBPAS was confirmed as a valid and reliable measure of women’s awareness of positive birth perception. Additionally, empathic communication-based interactive training effectively enhanced both positive birth perception and overall birth experience.Empathic Communication-Based Positive Birth training significantly improved the PBPAS dimensions of cognitive and perceptual support. The results indicate that women who received the training perceived healthcare communication more positively and reported a more favorable birth experience. This demonstrates the effectiveness of empathic communication-based interventions in enhancing birth perception and experience.Creating supportive environments that enable midwives to practice effectively can enhance women’s birth perception and experience through continuous professional support. Implementing diverse care models and collaborating within the healthcare team can identify factors influencing childbirth and promote positive birth experiences and maternal satisfaction.
