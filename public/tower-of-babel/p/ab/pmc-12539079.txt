Clinical analysis of the efficacy of bronchial artery embolization with gelatin sponge in our hospital for cryptogenic hemoptysis: a single-center, retrospective, observational study
Authors: Manabu Suzuki, Satoki Hatano, Chie Morita, Akinari Tsukada, Akane Ishida, Yusaku Kusaba, Masao Hashimoto, Go Naka, Jin Takasaki, Hiroshi Nokihara, Shinyu Izumi, Yuichiro Takeda, Masayuki Hojo, Haruhito Sugiyama
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveHemoptysis is a life-threatening condition requiring urgent treatment. Bronchial artery embolization (BAE), established as the primary intervention, is an effective treatment for hemoptysis. Common etiologies include bronchiectasis, tumors, tuberculosis, aspergilloma, and cystic fibrosis. Approximately 20% of hemoptysis cases are categorized as cryptogenic. We conducted a retrospective observational study to understand the clinical characteristics of patients with cryptogenic hemoptysis (CH).MethodsThe clinical characteristics, angiographic findings, and immediate and long-term efficacies of BAE were retrospectively analyzed in 36 consecutive patients between January 2010 and December 2022. Superselective BAE was successfully performed in all patients by using a gelatin sponge (GS).ResultsThe median age was 68 years, and 21 patients (58.3%) were male. Among them, 15 patients (41.7%) presented with massive hemoptysis, and 7 (19.4%) required intubation. Angiographic evaluation revealed the treatment of total 57 vessels, comprising 51 (89.5%) bronchial arteries, 5 (8.8%) intercostal arteries, and 1 (1.8%) inferior transverse artery. The recurrence-free rates were 100% at 1 month, 94.4% at 3 months, 94.4% at 12 months, and 86.1% at more than 12 months. Only one patient required a second BAE for hemoptysis, while others were managed for recurrences with oral hemostatic agents. No serious complications, such as spinal cord infarction, were observed during the study period.ConclusionBAE treatment with GS for CH proved to be a suitable therapeutic modality, demonstrating high efficacy in halting bleeding in both the short and long terms, with the absence of severe complications, including spinal cord infarction.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered for the UMIN Clinical Trial Registry (UMIN000042050) on October 8th, 2020.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-025-03948-8.Keywords: Angiography, Bronchial arterial embolization, Emergency treatment, Treatment outcome

Summary at a glance

This study reported that BAE treatment with a gelatin sponge (GS) for cryptogenic hemoptysis (CH) resulted in a favorable course without major complications.

Although evidence of metal coils for hemoptysis has been reported in recent years, BAE with GS can keep the recurrence rate low.

Introduction

Hemoptysis is a common symptom of respiratory diseases, including lung cancer, tuberculosis, and aspergillosis. It is occasionally life-threatening because of the large amount of blood expectoration [1]. Additionally, cryptogenic hemoptysis (CH) has been reported as another cause. It is the expectoration of blood from the lower respiratory tract without a known cause. It can range from minor chronic bleeding with a benign course to a life-threatening condition [2]. Massive CH is a challenging condition, and its management often involves a multidisciplinary approach, including medical management, bronchial artery embolization (BAE), and, rarely, surgical treatment. Bronchial artery embolization is an effective and safe procedure for controlling hemoptysis with a relatively low complication rate [3]. The technique of BAE has improved significantly, especially with the emergence of "super selective" BAE, which has improved the immediate bleeding control rate. In addition, a recently published systematic review and meta-analysis by Fan et al. demonstrated that BAE was more effective than conservative treatment alone in controlling hemoptysis, reducing recurrence, and decreasing hemoptysis-related mortality, with an almost negligible risk of major complications [4]. Therefore, BAE is considered an effective method for treating massive or recurrent hemoptysis and has been shown to be effective in the management of hemoptysis caused by both benign [5] and malignant etiologies [1, 4].

The prevalence of CH among all hemoptysis patients is reported to be in the range of 7% to 25%, according to previous studies [2, 5, 6]. Notably, the incidence of this condition is relatively high in our country, comprising 35% of BAE cases in a large-scale epidemiological survey in Japan [7]. In another study, CH, in which diagnostic evaluation with thoracic imaging and fiberoptic bronchoscopy does not result in a diagnosis, has been reported as the etiology of hemoptysis in 5–50% of cases [8].

CH is a disease in which hemoptysis occurs suddenly, sometimes in large amounts, and requires intubation and management despite the absence of significant findings in the lung fields. However, there have been few reports on its characteristics. Therefore, we conducted this study aiming to characterize the clinical spectrum and subsequent hemostatic efficacy of BAE in patients with CH.

Methods

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board for Clinical Research of the National Center for Global Health and Medicine (NCMG-G-003639–00) and was registered in the UMIN Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000042050). An opt-out method was used to obtain consent for this study; therefore, patients were not required to provide informed consent.

Study population

We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of 186 consecutive patients who underwent BAE for hemoptysis between January 2010 and December 2022 at the National Center for Global Health and Medicine, Department of Respiratory Medicine, and identified patients with CH. We collected detailed data from medical records, including age, sex, BMI, smoking history, amount of hemoptysis at the Emergency department visit, comorbid condition, respiratory condition, use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant medications, imaging studies and analysis, timing of BAE, angiography and embolization situation, duration of hospitalization, and hemoptysis-free rate.

Adult patients who underwent Computed Tomography (CT) for diagnostic evaluation of hemoptysis and subsequently underwent BAE treatment were enrolled in this study. Patients with obvious lung field lesions that could cause hemoptysis on CT images, such as lung cancer, tuberculosis, non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), aspergillosis, or other diseases that could cause hemoptysis, were excluded from the study. Moreover, CH was defined as: (1) no history of obvious respiratory comorbidities causing hemoptysis; and (2) no evidence of an etiologic cause based on laboratory and radiologic findings. Whether these inclusion criteria were met was independently confirmed by two pulmonary physicians, including one experienced pulmonary physician and one diagnostic radiologist.

All patients underwent BAE during hospitalization and received standard therapies such as oxygen administration, intubation, mechanical ventilation when necessary, and intravenous hemostatic agents. The degree of hemoptysis was defined as follows: massive, > 200 mL/24 h; moderate, 100–200 mL/24 h; and mild, 10–100 mL/24 h [9].

Imaging studies and analysis

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT of the chest before BAE to determine the area of pulmonary hemorrhage, the anatomic location and diameter of the bronchial artery origin on the lesion side, and the presence or absence of bronchial artery dilatation and aneurysm. The bleeding site was identified by the greatest degree of diffuse ground-glass opacity and consolidation on CT and/or coagulation on bronchoscopy. Thin-slice data were obtained in all cases, and coronal and sagittal views were created using multiplanar reconstruction. Imaging studies were independently reviewed by at least one diagnostic radiologist and a pulmonary physician. The details are provided in the Supplementary Appendix 1 and Figure S1, S2.

Angiography and embolization

All the BAE procedures were performed by respiratory physicians or in collaboration with radiologists. We performed BAE via the right or left femoral arterial access with a 4- or 5-Fr long vascular sheath (25 cm). All possibly responsible arteries were selectively embolized using 4- or 5-Fr guiding catheters and 1.9/2.8-Fr microcatheter systems with microguidewires during the session. The microcatheter was co-axially introduced and advanced as distally as possible to avoid the reflux of embolic agents into the spinal arteries or the aorta (4). Embolization was performed on the responsible arteries with a gelatin sponge (GS) or a combination of GS and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) particles. Embolic materials were injected into the responsible arteries with care for ectopic embolization due to backflow until the proliferation of peripheral blood vessels disappeared on angiography [10]. Bronchial aneurysm was defined as localized arterial dilation ≥ 1.3-fold larger than distal vessels on angiography [5]. The details are provided in the Supplementary Appendix 2. The angiography findings were retrospectively reviewed by at least one radiologist and a well-experienced pulmonary physician.

Evaluation of therapeutic outcomes

Immediate effectiveness

Immediate efficacy was determined based on technical success and the immediate control of bleeding. Technical success was defined as the complete embolization of the target vessel. Immediate bleeding control was defined as no hemoptysis within 24 h of the BAE procedure. If the patient had recurrent bleeding in the respiratory tract or fresh blood in the sputum within 24 h of BAE, a failed case of BAE was considered. However, this procedure was considered successful if the patient had brown sputum or a small amount of blood in the sputum. Successful patients were discharged immediately after treatment of the bleeding and, if applicable, the underlying disease (e.g., pneumonia).

Long-term outcomes

The long-term outcomes of BAE were determined by the cumulative hemoptysis non-recurrence rates at 1, 3, 12, and over 12 months after BAE treatment.

Recurrence was defined as the need for a new intervention for hemoptysis, such as intravenous administration of hemostatic agents, bronchoscopy, or repeated BAE. The presence of mild hemoptysis and oral hemostatic agents was not defined as an apparent recurrence.

Early recurrence was defined as recurrence occurring within one month after BAE, and late recurrence was defined as occurring after one month following the BAE procedure.

Complications, such as additional treatment, permanent or significant disability, or death, were retrospectively reviewed.

Comparison of the intubated and non-intubated groups

The patients were divided into two groups according to their respiratory status, intubated and non-intubated, to compare the differences in clinical background and course of hemoptysis treatment. Clinical successes for the short- and long-term recurrence-free rates were also compared between the two groups.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians and ranges. Statistical comparisons between the two groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Fisher’s exact test for ordinal or nominal scales.

The bleeding recurrence-free rate was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the unstratified log-rank test. Statistical analyses were performed using EZR software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan).

Angiographic findings and efficacy of BAE

The angiographic findings are summarized in Table 2. The treatment sides were right, left, and bilateral in 23 (63.9%), 8 (22.2%), and 5 (13.9%) patients, respectively. All 36 patients exhibited vascular tortuosity, peripheral hypervascularity, and bronchial arterial hypertrophy on angiography (Fig. 2B, D). Ten (27.8%) patients had bronchial artery microaneurysm formation (Fig. 2E) and 15 (41.7%) patients experienced systemic pulmonary shunting (Fig. 2F). The total number of vessels treated was 57, of which 51 (89.5%) were bronchial arteries, 5 (8.8%) were intercostal arteries, and 1 (1.8%) was inferior transverse artery. A total of 51 bronchial arteries were treated, one of which was the common left trunk and the other was the right trunk. The intercostobronchial trunk (ICBT) was found in 22 patients (61.1%), all on the right side. In these patients, BAE was performed on the distal side to the branch of the intercostal arteries (Figure S3).

Recurrence-free rate

Immediate efficacy was achieved in all patients after BAE.

Thirty-five patients were followed up for more than one month, with an average follow-up period of 30.6 ± 24.3 months (range 0–95 months). The recurrence-free rates were 100% at 1 month, 94.4% at 3 months, 94.4% at 12 months, and 86.1% at 12 months or more (Fig. 3A). Although five cases of bleeding were observed during the observation period, three of them had a minimal amount of bleeding and were resolved with an oral prescription of a hemostatic agent for 90 mg carbazochrome sodium sulphonate hydrate daily and 750 mg tranexamic acid daily at an outpatient clinic; therefore, they were not considered apparent recurrences. Two patients required hospitalization; one underwent bronchoscopic hemostasis at 13 months, and the other was treated with intravenous hemostatic agents due to continued hospitalization after one month, and the hemoptysis resolved. Only one patient required a second BAE owing to re-bleeding. No patient experienced severe complications, such as spinal cord ischemia, that resulted in prolonged hospitalization for additional treatment, permanent or significant disability, or death.

Discussion

This study aimed to characterize the clinical spectrum and hemostatic efficacy of BAE in patients with CH. The recurrence-free rates were 100% at 1 month, 94.4% at 3 months, 94.4% at 12 months, and 86.1% at 12 months or more. Furthermore, the recurrence-free rate without requiring hospitalization was 94.4% at 12 months or more, indicating a favorable outcome. Although 41.7% of patients had massive hemoptysis (> 200 ml) and 19.4% of patients required ventilator management due to massive hemoptysis, no deaths occurred after BAE treatment, and there was no significant difference in the recurrence-free rate between the intubated and non-intubated groups.

In our study, 20% of BAE procedures were for idiopathic hemoptysis, but a large epidemiological survey of BAE procedures for hemoptysis in Japan reported that CH was the most common form of hemoptysis at 35%, suggesting that CH is a more common condition than expected in the real world [7]. On the other hand, a study of hemoptysis patients without suspected malignancy in Denmark reported that CH was present in 83.5% of patients. Although this means that the frequency may vary depending on what the list of hemoptysis is based on and the country being studied, CH appears to be a more common disease in daily clinical practice than previously reported [8].

Moreover, CH is a disease in which hemoptysis occurs suddenly, sometimes in large amounts, and requires intubation and management, despite the absence of significant findings in the lung fields. However, there have been few reports on its characteristics. Therefore, we conducted this study.

Previous reports have shown that the rate of severe hemoptysis is generally 20–50%, although the range is variable [11]. However, the definition of the volume of severe hemoptysis varies, ranging from 100 ml or more to 200 ml or more, and up to 400 ml or more [12]. Therefore, our data cannot be easily compared, but it is important to recognize that CH is a condition in which hemoptysis occurs unexpectedly, and intubation is required even in the absence of lung lesions. It has a good prognosis and sometimes resolves with hemostatic agents alone. However, Ando et al. reported that 28.3% of patients had recurrent hemoptysis during the natural course of the disease [6]. Therefore, it is necessary to consider BAE during initial hemoptysis. Similar to other diseases, BAE is the first choice of treatment, with a good outcome. With appropriate treatment such as BAE, the clinical course of the patient was good, and there were no deaths in our study.

In adults, normal bronchial arteries measure less than 1.5 mm in diameter at their origin and 0.5 mm at their entry point into a bronchopulmonary segment. According to Yoon et al., a bronchial artery larger than 2 mm on CT is most likely to be abnormal [13]. On contrast-enhanced CT, the hypertrophic bronchial arteries are easily visualized as enhancing nodular or tubular structures within the mediastinum and around the central airway [13]. Therefore, the abnormal bronchial artery findings during hemoptysis may be embolized. However, in our study, hemoptysis occurred even in patients with bronchial arteries less than 2 mm in diameter, and the proportion of such cases was 36.0%. In a similar report, 37.1% of patients with CH had bronchial arteries less than 2 mm in diameter on angiography findings, similar to our study [6]. Therefore, we need to acknowledge once again that even bronchial arteries with a diameter of less than 2 mm can be responsible for hemoptysis.

The characteristic angiographic findings of the hemoptysis-related artery were also well known: hypervascularity, hypertrophy of the bronchial artery, and systemic pulmonary shunts [2, 6, 14–17]. The prevalence of systemic pulmonary shunts in our study was 41.7%. Previous reports have reported variable rates (15% to 80%). Generally, the presence of an intrapulmonary shunt increases the risk of hemoptysis [18, 19]. When such findings are present, we believe that subsequent hemoptysis can be prevented by performing a thorough embolization. In addition, microaneurysm formation was observed at the periphery of the lung lesion even when there was nothing in the lung field. This particular condition has been reported as a possible cause of hemoptysis [6]. In our study, 10 patients (27.8%) were found to have microaneurysms, and these findings were similar to previous reports.

In our hospital, we use GS as the embolization material when performing hemoptysis treatment. The hemostatic effect was satisfactory not only in the short term, but also in the long term. GS has long been a widely used transcatheter arterial embolization material worldwide, and a national database study in Japan showed that GS was used in 79% of BAE for causative hemoptysis [7]. The advantages of GS include its low cost, wide availability, and accumulated years of clinical use, indicating its safety [20, 21]. In addition, as it is absorbed within 2–6 weeks after embolization [22], embolization with GS is useful for transient unexpected bleeding, such as trauma [23, 24]. The main goal is to temporarily interrupt the blood flow. One concern of BAE with GS is recanalization, which leads to another episode of hemoptysis. However, a second BAE procedure may coincidentally target the same artery during rebleeding.

Our results showed that BAE with GS was sufficient and effective for the treatment of CH. In patients followed for up to 8 years, the recurrence rate remained high (86.1%), and no life-threatening hemoptysis occurred at recurrence. Previous studies on CH have also reported favorable prognoses after BAE [6, 14–27]. These studies used detachable coils [6], N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) [14], or PVA [16] as permanent embolic materials. Outcomes with coil embolization showed that a hemoptysis-free survival rate of 97.0% for 20 months [6]. Additionally, outcomes with NBCA embolization showed a hemoptysis-free survival rate of 96% for 5 years [16] and outcomes with PVA embolization showed that 88.5% of patients had no recurrence during long-term follow-up [1, 4].

The common complications associated with BAE include embolic fever and local chest pain. Among such complications, the most severe is spinal cord ischemia caused by occlusion of the spinal artery [1, 7]. Spinal cord infarction, a serious complication, is mainly caused by mis-embolization of the anterior spinal artery, and is associated with paraplegia and rectal bladder dysfunction. In the present study, no spinal cord ischemia was observed. To avoid spinal cord ischemia, it is necessary to carefully observe the presence of the anterior spinal artery during angiography and selectively embolize the arteries responsible for hemoptysis. A more distal, super-selective BAE decreased the occurrence of spinal cord ischemia. In previous studies, the prevalence of spinal cord infarction ranged from 0.0% to 6.0% [7]. A recent report from a large-scale epidemiological survey in Japan reported that the prevalence of spinal cord ischemia is as low as 0.19% [7]. The incidence of complications varies depending on the embolic material, with the prevalence of spinal cord infarction being 0.06% in the coils group, 0.18% in the GS particles group, and 0.71% in the NBCA group [7].

Fortunately, in our study, we did not observe any serious complications, such as spinal cord infarction. According to a previous study, 180 patients underwent 254 angiographic procedures for hemoptysis, with no direct spinal arterial supply from the bronchial artery [26]. Moreover, it is well known that the great anterior radiculomedullary artery (the artery of Adamkiewicz [AKA]) is the dominant feeder of the spinal cord. Uotani et al. [27] reported that the AKA originates from the intercostal or lumbar artery at the Th7–12 or L1–3 levels. Based on these reports, we believe that embolization targeting the bronchial artery may be safer than targeting other vessels.

This study had several limitations. First, this was a single-arm retrospective analysis conducted at a single hospital. As this was not a prospective study, some patients were lost to follow-up over a short period of time. In addition, the composition of the diseases for which BAE was performed varied between the institutions, which may have introduced bias. Second, the definition of CH varied slightly between reports, which may explain the differences between our results and those of previous studies.

In previous studies, CH has been defined as the absence of signs of an underlying disease on clinical examination. Finally, different BAE operators may have affected the treatment time, the amount of contrast medium used, and the subsequent hemostasis rate. Fortunately, there were no cases of spinal cord infarction as a serious complication in our study, but due to the small sample size of 36 cases, we were unable to evaluate the safety of using GS for the occurrence of spinal cord infarction. A future prospective multi-institutional study concerning the effectiveness of GS for CH is needed. Consideration as to the most appropriate embolic material for BAE in relation to this disease is also required.

Conclusion

CH sometimes leads to massive hemoptysis requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation. BAE therapy is an effective treatment owing to its high hemostatic efficacy and low recurrence rates. Our data showed that the results of embolization using GS in CH were comparable to those of other known reports. BAE treatment with GS for CH is appropriate because it is highly effective in stopping bleeding not only in the short term but also in the long term. Additionally, no serious complications, such as spinal cord infarction, were observed.
