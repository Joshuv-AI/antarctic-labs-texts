‘Being empathetic to my patients and ourselves’: a qualitative process evaluation of READ-Y psychological first aid training for healthcare workers working in routine care
Authors: Ling Wang, Xizhao Li, Cailiang Qiu, Jianjian Wang, Lina Zeng, Ting Liu, Ziqing Zhong, Yeqing Wu, Lin Xu, Yuping Liu, Mary Leamy, Muli Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction: Healthcare workers (HCWs) are routinely exposed to distressing and potentially traumatic events during frontline care, yet receive limited structure training to manage such exposure. Psychological first aid (PFA) training has been widely disseminated among emergency responders and is increasingly being adapted for HCWs; however, its acceptability, implementation, and use in routine care remain underexplored. This study aimed to explore how an adapted PFA training programme was perceived, implemented, and used by HCWs in routine care, and to identify the mechanisms and contextual factors that influenced its impact.Method: A qualitative process evaluation was conducted alongside the delivery of the READ-Y (Rapport, Evaluation, Aid, Disposition, Yourself) PFA training programme in a Chinese tertiary hospital. Data collection included 3 months of observations of training delivery and 19 semi-structured interviews with HCW trainees, ward managers, and trainers/facilitators. Data were analysed using framework analysis.Results: Training was logistically feasible, consistently delivered, and characterized by high engagement in simulation and debriefing. Key fidelity elements of PFA training were maintained. Five key themes emerged: (1) positive reception of the adapted, blended-format training; (2) PFA was viewed as a pragmatic, stepwise tool for strengthening basic psychosocial support in routine care; (3) reported improvement in empathy, communication, and normalization of self-care among staff; (4) targeted skills development and peer learning as key mechanisms of reported change, fostering a shared language and support; and (5) multi-level contextual enablers (e.g. leadership endorsement, protected time) were identified and structural barriers (e.g. workload, burnout) existed that may hinder uptake.Conclusion: The adapted PFA training programme was perceived as feasible, relevant, and acceptable for HCWs, facilitating the integration of psychosocial support into routine care while promoting their own well-being. System-level integration and longitudinal evaluation of PFA training are recommended to assess sustained impact on patient and staff outcomes.KEYWORDS: Psychological first aid, psychosocial support, workforce well-being, process evaluation, qualitative research, China

Introduction

Exposure to distress and potentially traumatic events – such as physical injury, interpersonal violence, medical error, and public emergencies – is prevalent and inherent in healthcare. Working in frontline care, healthcare workers (HCWs) are often the first to respond to patients’ psychosocial suffering while carrying their own emotional burden, yet they receive limited structured training to prepare them for managing such exposure. In the absence of timely, routine response, substantial evidence documents a double burden: patients frequently experience unmet psychosocial support needs (Bergerot et al., 2024; Springer et al., 2024), while HCWs face cumulative occupational risks including anxiety, depression, compassion fatigue, and moral injury (Bakhamis et al., 2019; Li et al., 2024), with downstream consequences for quality of care and patient safety.

Psychological first aid (PFA), originally developed as a form of acute psychological crisis intervention, aims to reduce acute distress and promote adaptive functioning in the aftermath of disaster and emergencies, with emerging evidence supporting its benefits (Wang, Norman, Edleston, et al., 2024). A key strength of PFA is its scalability: it can be rapidly taught to non-mental health specialists who then can provide basic psychosocial support to individuals in acute crisis (Brymer et al., 2006; Everly & Lating, 2017). On this basis, PFA has been disseminated globally in emergencies as a capacity-building strategy to strengthen preparedness, resilience, and self-efficacy among first responders and disaster volunteers (Wang et al., 2021). Beyond disaster and large-scale emergencies, PFA has increasingly been extended to routine settings – including schools, police services, and law enforcement agencies – where it has been associated with improved mental health literacy, reduced stigma, and strengthened resilience (Geoffrion et al., 2023; Kamel et al., 2025; Kouvatsou et al., 2022; Rantho et al., 2025).

More recently, PFA training has been advocated as an alternative educational approach for HCWs in routine hospital care, where burnout and vicarious traumatization approach the level of a ‘silent epidemic’ (Everly, 2020), and as a means of strengthening trauma informed practice (Asaoka et al., 2023). A growing number of studies have evaluated PFA training for medical students and HCWs to improve disaster response and preparedness (Bayageldi & Kaloğlu Binici, 2024; Kılıç & Şimşek, 2019; J. Zhang et al., 2022). During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, PFA training was adapted for digital delivery to support HCWs in coping with crisis situations (Blake et al., 2021; Chandler et al., 2023; Schoultz et al., 2022). Nevertheless, uptake within national training initiatives – for example, for HCWs in UK care homes – remains limited (Schoultz et al., 2022). It is less well known how PFA is delivered, accepted, and used by HCWs in real-world settings, and the potential of PFA to support HCWs has not yet been realized.

Preparing Me project

To address these gaps, the ‘Preparing Me’ project adapted and evaluated PFA as a professional development strategy for frontline health workers in China (Wang et al., 2022). This programme was designed to proactively strengthen HCWs’ competencies in managing trauma exposure – by building skills for self-care or peer support, as well as providing initial psychosocial support to patients in both emergencies and routine care. This is particularly crucial in the Chinese healthcare context, where HCWs routinely face high patient volumes, workplace violence, and responding to public health emergencies, which have been exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic (Cai et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2020; Dong et al., 2021).

Central to this initiative was addressing the broader implementation challenges inherent in PFA by systematically adapting the training to the frontline care setting. The programme was developed through a participatory approach, grounded in an implementation science perspective and paying close attention to Chinese cultural norms and clinical realities. It particularly considered the dual roles of Chinese HCWs in providing psychosocial care during both emergencies and routine patient care. The resulting training intervention, known as the READ-Y (Rapport, Evaluation, Aid, Disposition, Yourself) PFA model and programme, builds upon the Johns Hopkins Guide to Psychological First Aid, while incorporating culturally relevant scenarios, terminology, and delivery methods to enhance feasibility and acceptability within Chinese hospital care settings (Wang et al., 2025). The training programme is being evaluated in an ongoing trial, with preliminary quantitative trial findings indicating improvements in psychosocial competencies, resilience, and reductions in burnout (Wang, Norman, Xiao, et al., 2024).

To deepen understanding beyond the question of ‘does it work?’, an embedded qualitative process evaluation was conducted alongside the trial. Guided by UK Medical Research Council process evaluation framework for structuring the process evaluation components (implementation, mechanisms of impact, and context) (Moore et al., 2015; Skivington et al., 2021), the study aimed to examine how the training intervention was implemented, participants’ experiences and engagement, as well as contextual and organizational facilitators and barriers shaping outcomes. Specifically, the study explored: (1) how HCWs perceived the training; (2) how PFA principles were applied and the perceived relevance of PFA in routine care; (3) the mechanisms of change activated by the programme; and (4) contextual factors that facilitated or hindered implementation.

Methods

Study design

This process evaluation study employed a qualitative methodology using observations and semi-structured interviews to understand participants’ experience of receiving the READ-Y PFA training programme and use in a routine care setting.

Process evaluation framework

Guided by the UK Medical Research Council guidance on complex intervention research (Moore et al., 2015; Skivington et al., 2021), this study examined three core domains: (1) implementation (perceived feasibility, acceptability, and delivery of the training); (2) mechanisms of change (how the training was understood and used); and (3) contextual factors influencing delivery. During analysis and interpretation, these domains were considered alongside key implementation outcomes, as articulated by Proctor et al. (2011) (e.g. acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, and perceived sustainability), which served as sensitizing concepts rather than a prescriptive coding frame. This structured, stepwise model is designed to guide HCWs in delivering basic psychosocial support within high-pressure clinical environments and caring for themselves. A one-day, face-to-face training workshop (approximately 6 h) comprising six structured sessions that cover each step of the READ-Y model. Setting and participants

The study took place in a large tertiary hospital in China between December 2021 and April 2022. The hospital leadership endorsed the READ-Y PFA training as a continuing professional development opportunity for frontline staff, and participation was voluntary.

Participant recruitment followed a purposive, maximum variation sampling strategy aimed at capturing diverse perspectives across clinical roles and departments. Eligible participants were identified and invited, as follows:

Frontline HCW trainees: All staff members who completed the READ-Y PFA training were invited to take part in the qualitative interviews. An invitation letter explaining the study’s purpose, confidentiality safeguards, and voluntary nature of participation was disseminated via WeChat (the hospital's internal communication platform). HCWs who expressed an interest were contacted by the research team to arrange a suitable interview time.

Frontline HCW trainees: All staff members who completed the READ-Y PFA training were invited to take part in the qualitative interviews. An invitation letter explaining the study’s purpose, confidentiality safeguards, and voluntary nature of participation was disseminated via WeChat (the hospital's internal communication platform). HCWs who expressed an interest were contacted by the research team to arrange a suitable interview time.

Ward managers: Managers responsible for enabling staff attendance and observing training integration in practice were identified in consultation with the hospital's nursing education department. Invitations were sent directly by the research team, and participation was voluntary.

Trainers and facilitators: Members of the multidisciplinary team involved in training delivery were invited by the principal investigator to provide reflective input on implementation and delivery feasibility.

Trainers and facilitators: Members of the multidisciplinary team involved in training delivery were invited by the principal investigator to provide reflective input on implementation and delivery feasibility.

Sampling sought variation by department, seniority, and role to ensure broad coverage across clinical specialities and implementation positions (learner, supervisory, and delivery levels).

Data collection

Observations

All 12 PFA training sessions and four online group discussions were observed by the first author. A structured checklist informed by Spradley's framework – a practical guide for moving from unstructured field notes to a structured, insightful analysis (Spradley, 2016) – guided field notes on training content, engagement, and group dynamics (see Supplementary Table 1).

Semi-structured, qualitative interviews

Following the completion of training, all trainees receiving PFA were invited for interviews via a WeChat-linked QR code. Sampling continued until thematic saturation was reached. To ensure triangulation of perspectives, interviews were also conducted with ward managers (who oversaw staff participation) and programme trainers/facilitators. Maximum variation was sought in terms of department, professional role, and seniority.

Interview followed a semi-structured topic guide, which was informed by the study objectives and the process evaluations described in Section 2.2. The guide was piloted with trainees and refined based on feedback, with core domains including perceived outcomes, mechanisms, acceptability/feasibility, barriers/facilitators, and suggestions for improvement (see Supplementary Table 2).

All interviews were conducted face to face in hospital office settings or online via secure platforms, depending on participant preferences. Informed consent was obtained verbally and in writing. Interviews were conducted in Mandarin, audio-recorded, anonymized, and transcribed verbatim. Interviews were led by the first author with support from a second researcher; these two female PhD nursing researchers were trained in mixed-method research and implementation science. They also documented field notes and reflexive memos. Neither researcher had any prior clinical, supervisory, or administrative relationship with the participants (i.e. no relationship was established prior to study commencement). At the outset, participants were explicitly informed that the researchers were independent evaluators focused on assessing the feasibility and fidelity of the training and seeking suggestions for improvement, to establish a non-judgemental space.

Data analysis

Qualitative data – including interview, observation notes, and field documentation – were analysed using framework analysis (Gale et al., 2013), which supports both deductive and inductive exploration. The analytical process followed an established five-stage procedure (Gale et al., 2013; Ritchie & Lewis, 2003):

Familiarization: The lead author read all transcripts and notes multiple times to identify initial patterns.

The coding framework was iteratively refined through discussions with co-authors, who provided clinical and methodological oversight. Triangulation across multiple data sources (interviews and field observations, notes, and documentation) and participant groups (trainees, ward managers, facilitators) strengthened the credibility and comprehensiveness of findings (Lauri, 2011). To enhance rigour, a subset of transcripts was independently coded by the second researcher, with discrepancies resolved through research team consensus. Member checking was conducted with selected participants to validate key themes, and regular supervisory review ensured the credibility of the analytical process.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from two institutions. All participants provided informed written or oral consent prior to their involvement. Confidentiality and anonymity were rigorously maintained throughout the study, with all identifiable information deidentified during transcription and analysis to protect the participants’ privacy.

Most participants were female nurses, along with one male trainer, and they represented multiple clinical specialities, providing heterogeneous perspectives.

Across the interviews and field notes, five overarching themes emerged: (1) Experience of receiving READ-Y PFA training; (2) PFA’s relevance to clinical care; (3) Valued outcomes – being empathetic to my patients and ourselves; (4) Mechanism of change – skills acquisition and peer learning; and (5) Contextual factors influencing implementation and future modification (see Supplementary Table 3).

Theme 1: Experience of receiving READ-Y PFA training

Blended, interactive design enhanced learning

HCWs consistently described the READ-Y PFA training as acceptable and engaging, compared to traditional didactic formats. The innovative blend of face-to-face teaching, simulation-based activities with standardized patients, and follow-up group sessions was widely perceived as particularly valuable: ‘Quite novel … better than one-day lectures … we truly engaged and benefited from follow-up sessions’ (PFA Trainee 01).

Culturally tailored, practically oriented content increased relevance

Trainees and ward managers reflected that these culturally and contextually relevant case scenarios closely mirrored their clinical practice reality, including both patient-facing situations and interactions with colleagues during COVID-19. This enhanced perceived relevance and appropriateness:

The situational cases are closely aligned with our clinical practice setting, for example, apart from the case of supporting patients in [the] emergency room, the scenario selected for helping my colleague during COVID-19 in the hospital … resonated [strongly], help[ing] me to practise by playing. (PFA Trainee 06)

The READY framework itself was valued as a succinct stepwise and memorable guide: ‘The catchy READY tool … integrated into a succinct structure together … really impressed’ (PFA Trainee 03).

Challenges: cognitive intensity and digital fatigue

Several trainees highlighted a limitation that they experienced the one-day workshop as being cognitively intense: ‘The training was really jam-packed … if you wandered a little bit, you’[d] easily miss the point’ (PFA Trainee 07). Some trainees struggled to remain engaged in online follow-up sessions owing to technical difficulties and competing demands: ‘[The] on-line session always disconnected … especially when you’re at home, or having a child around … I don't really have a sense of engagement or relate to [it]’ (PFA Trainee 01).

Theme 2: PFA’s relevance to clinical care

A pragmatic stepwise tool to guide psychosocial support

The trainees widely viewed READ-Y PFA as a pragmatic stepwise tool to guide psychosocial support into concrete, evidence-based practice:

In my mind, READ-Y tool is a set of actions when providing psychological care … . help to guide our existing practice into [a] more structured way with evidence that [we] should incorporate psychological support into our everyday work … . (PFA Trainee 09)

Some noted that elements of PFA (e.g. communication) were not entirely new, but that the framework provided structure and improved clarity: ‘Some PFA activities we [were] familiarized with, like communication techniques, we used to do in previous practice’ (PFA Trainee 02). They described PFA as providing a tangible roadmap to bridge the gap between biomedical routine and psychosocial care:

We often mention the biopsychosocial model in medicine, but we don't know how to do it, especially psychosocial aspect[s]. This tool gives a step-by-step guide, like our nursing procedures. (PFA Trainee 11)

Ward managers similarly described READY steps as aligning with existing workflows and offering a structured way to monitor emotional well-being and plan referrals:

READY connects the daily work we used to do into steps … rapport to keep a good relationship with patients … trust each other … . assess and monitor whether they need anything anytime … . comfort them … provide necessary information … and ask a doctor or other professionals for help and then help them to share with peer patients … connect them to their family or social support. (Clinical Ward Manager 02)

In practice, HCWs reflected that they frequently used three components – Rapport, Evaluation, and Aid – when responding to patient’s anxiety, distress, or agitation; for example, when de-escalating an overwhelmed caregiver:

Three core PFA steps really worked … rapport with a father (caregiver who agitated due to his son who had bipolar and [was] hospitalized experiencing tongue bite) [who was] emotionally overwhelmed … I approached and patted him, calmed down and listened to him [to help] me assess his concern, and found he just needed reassurance that his son was safe. After that, he calmed down completely. (PFA Trainee 05)

Fragmented yet continuous care processes

HCW trainees emphasized that emotional support was often delivered in fragmented, time-limited interactions, yet remains an ongoing process rather than a single, linear sequence, sometimes with only a few steps being delivered:

Our work is fragmented … no time to follow all steps in one go, but every short encounter helps recovery. (PFA Trainee 01)

Building rapport and providing reassurance often unfolded over multiple short contacts. For some, establishing empathy was seen as a sufficient and realistic endpoint: ‘After all, once empathy is established, [the] patient [is] relieved immediately and there is nothing else to do, it ends there’ (PFA Trainee 04)

Barriers to integrating PFA in clinical care

Assessment and triage were perceived as the most challenging PFA components. HCWs described uncertainty about their remit, fear of ‘overstepping’ without clear tools, and limited supervision: ‘Assessment and triage are complex; we need clearer tools or scales to guide triage decisions’ (PFA Trainee 07).

Without reinforcement, several participants reverted to old habits: ‘After training, I wanted to use it … but after some failed attempts, I lost confidence and went back to my usual practice’ (PFA Trainee 11).

Structural barriers – including heavy workloads, documentation requirements, and staff shortages – repeatedly constrained the delivery of psychosocial care, which tended to be prioritized only once core clinical tasks had been completed:

Most of us struggled to deal with [the] documents, all focused on finishing clinical tasks … only if I [had] finished all the tasks and [was] emotionally OK [could] I … pay attention to the care [of] my patients. (PFA Trainee 03)

Theme 3: Valued outcomes – being empathetic to my patients and ourselves

Greater empathy and patient-centredness in clinical encounters

HCWs frequently described an increase in empathy and relational engagement with patients, shifting from a task-oriented, efficiency-driven practice to one that prioritized listening and understanding:

The greatest change is I can stand in their shoes … I remind myself to do at least what I can, such as get along well with the patients and be more empathetic to my patients who are emotionally stressed. (PFA Trainee 06)

Some reported consciously slowing down, when possible, to listen and validate distress: ‘I tend to now take more time, if I can, to show my compassion and empathy’ (PFA Trainee 09).

Trainees felt more competent in using basic PFA skills – active listening, emotional validation, and referral, enabling them to provide a simple yet robust structure for minimal psychosocial support:

It's a very basic tool – we’re not doing therapy … but we can comfort, provide practical assistance, and that shift motivated me. (PFA Trainee 03)

These strategies were applied in high-pressure environments, for example, using relaxation techniques with ICU patients who were fearful of extubation and initiating referrals where needed:

I now use relaxation exercises for ICU patients afraid to be taken off ventilators … and refer more confidently when needed. (PFA Trainee 07)

Being empathetic to oneself and reduced stigma around vulnerability

Another notable outcome was that HCWs perceived increased self-empathy and a willingness to seek support. Trainees described being more attentive to their own feelings and less ashamed of seeking help: ‘I start[ed] to care more about my own feeling[s] … relieved not humiliated [when] asking for help and [it is] easier to handle my life and work balance now’ (PFA Trainee 06).

Some reported reduced internalized stigma around showing vulnerability, compared to a prevailing norm of feeling guilt when requesting leave: ‘For all clinical workers, every one of us was stressed and suffering; however, I feel that we cannot show vulnerability and [we] feel guilty if we request leave … now I don't feel blamed and [I am] open to sharing that vulnerability … .’ (PFA Trainee 09).

Ward managers noted that a shift towards self-care improved staff coping and resilience among ‘second-victim’ HCWs, which was useful in managing adverse events:

In my team, the training helped some HCWs, and [a trainee] could take the onus of caring for herself, or have that perception to prioritize her own well-being. (Clinical Ward Manager 02)

Improved communication and interprofessional teamwork

At a team level, participants and ward managers reported improved communication, and de-escalated conflict and aggression. One trainee participant reflected, ‘Since I learned [PFA], I am more capable of managing conflicts, proactive communicating; that helped my interprofessional teamwork, it worked, and I can again enjoy my job’ (PFA Trainee 01). Ward managers also described observable changes in how staff handle difficult interactions, and an improved team climate:

She used to post complaints in the group chat with brutal honesty … now, she speaks with us in a more relaxed way … she can handle more complex interpersonal relationships. (Clinical Ward Manager 03)

Theme 4: Mechanism of change – skills acquisition and peer learning

Knowledge and skills development: from uncertainty to action

A central mechanism underpinning reported changes was the acquisition of concrete knowledge to understand trauma and stress, and appropriate skills to provide psychosocial responses. HCWs reported feeling more confident in recognizing distress, normalizing reactions, and intervening within their role appropriately:

As the first contact with patients, I know emotional care is another aspect … not confused like before, now very clear what we can do and cannot do. (PFA Trainee 08)

Skills such as active listening, relaxation techniques, and structured referral pathways were cited as being particularly useful, transforming previous feelings of helplessness into more purposeful action:

Of all in READY, I use active listening, relaxation techniques a lot, and sometimes referral … previously I didn't know how to deal with them, and now I use relaxation exercises to calm them down and encourage them, while for some serious cases I get them referred to a psychiatrist for [a] consultation. (PFA Trainee 07)

Ward managers highlighted the importance of training in such competencies, particularly for junior staff who are not experienced in providing essential psychosocial support when confronted with patients’ emotional needs.

Group peer learning: shared language and safe reflection

The group-based format, and especially the small follow-up groups, was identified as a key enabling process. Peer discussions helped to create a shared language for psychosocial care, reinforced new skills, and supported the clarification of professional boundaries:

When we shared and got feedback on problems I’d struggled with, it suddenly made sense … and helped me understand the boundaries. (PFA Trainee 06)

A non-judgemental atmosphere further facilitated open reflection on difficult cases and personal reactions: ‘[the] small group enabled … a greater exchange [of] knowledge and practice together, without judging the ways’ (PFA Trainee 07).

Theme 5: Contextual factors influencing implementation

Enablers across organizational, delivery, and cultural levels

The success of training implementation was driven by key enablers at multiple levels. At an organizational level, visible endorsement by hospital leadership legitimized psychosocial care as a professional development priority. Positioning the programme as a free continuing educational opportunity supported recruitment, while ward managers arranged protected time – sometimes up to 3 days – for staff to attend:

We’re happy to support them to attend this training for improving competencies, with a hope to improve clinical practice of the patient's outcome. (Clinical Ward Manager 03)

At the delivery level, participants praised skilled facilitation, attention to group dynamics, and seemingly small but meaningful elements (e.g. structured breaks and refreshments), which helped to maintain engagement. The research component was perceived as adding credibility: ‘The research component made this programme more robust and convinced professional[s]’ (Psychiatry Head, HCW Trainer 01).

Culturally adapted content – using contextually relevant case scenarios and language – further enhanced acceptability, while ongoing supervision and the progressive structure of sessions were valued: ‘Every session is connected … to provide systematic knowledge of psychosocial support’ (Clinical Psychologist Trainer 02).

Concerns about scalability and sustainability

Despite strong local support, participants questioned the scalability and sustainability of the current intensive format. Ward managers noted that future organizational investment would depend heavily on demonstrating visible outcomes – such as improvements in patient satisfaction, communication, or clinical indicators: ‘Without visible results to justify the value of this training, the organization and even our staff will not be too willing to take on additional things outside of work’ (Clinical Ward Manager 02).

Suggested future modification

To address these barriers, participants consistently recommended several modifications. A ‘train-the-trainer’ model and small-scale team-based training was suggested to widen the reach and tailor content to specific wards. Many advocated more flexible delivery (shorter, more frequent sessions) to facilitate gradual skills acquisition. Peer-support platforms such as WeChat groups were seen as promising for ongoing reflection and informal supervision: ‘The WeChat group could be built as an active forum … even a few people can be passionate facilitators’ (PFA Trainee 03). Participants also requested structured feedback, simple evaluation checklists, and refresher materials to reinforce use over time. There was strong consensus that the programme should be formally embedded as essential professional development to build a durable cultural norm: ‘Make it an essential professional skill and sustain to build a norm … use every department's monthly learning meeting’ (Clinical Ward Manager 04).

Discussion

This qualitative process evaluation offers an in-depth account of how an adapted READ-Y PFA training programme was implemented, accepted, and practised among HCWs in a highly demanding Chinese hospital environment. Interpreted through a wide implementation lens, the findings suggest that this adapted PFA training was highly acceptable and perceived as relevant to strengthening both patients’ psychosocial care and staff well-being, but that structural and organizational constraints limit its integration into routine practice. In a context where trauma exposure is prevalent and inherent, yet lacking structured preparedness, the positive findings of this contextually adapted PFA training are notable and respond to an important unmet need.

Frontline HCWs’ perception of the adapted PFA training

The adapted blended design – face-to-face simulation, culturally tailored cases, interactive exercises, and follow-up group sessions – was consistently described as engaging and useful. This is not surprising, since the design principle closely aligns with effective continuing professional development (experiential learning, situated relevance, peer exchange, reflective practice) (Everitt et al., 2022; Manley et al., 2018). The addition of structured follow-ups addressed the limitations and concerns of one-off PFA training for HCWs, such as those reported during the Ebola response, where minimal ongoing support and unclear boundaries compromised both fidelity and safety (Horn et al., 2019). In a healthcare system, where psychosocial competencies are underprioritized in workforce development (Liu et al., 2016), such design features appear critical to acceptability and perceived appropriateness.

Nonetheless, concerns about cognitive load, digital fatigue, and scheduling within already pressurized services highlight feasibility challenges and raise questions about the scalability of PFA training. The current format is resource intensive and was supported by strong local leadership and protected time – conditions that may not be easily replicated. Participants’ calls for shorter, recurring sessions integrated into supervision or mandatory education, and for the greater use of digital platforms, reflect a need to optimize the balance between depth and feasibility.

PFA’s relevance to routine clinical care

HCWs reported that READ-Y PFA provided a concrete, stepwise structure for strengthening psychosocial care, helping them to structure empathic communication, prioritize patient needs, and provide practical support. Compared with the predominantly disaster and emergency-focused PFA literature, these findings extend current knowledge by illustrating how PFA can function as a proactive psychosocial skillset within routine practice, rather than solely as a post-disaster intervention.

HCWs valued the way in which the READ-Y framework guided psychosocial support into concrete, evidence-based practice. Some elements of PFA were experienced as familiar – particularly aspects of communication and empathy, needs assessment, and provision of practical assistance – closely mirroring established nursing care values, such as the concept of compassion competence (Mannion, 2014). These are also closely linked to principles of trauma-informed care, including safety, trust, collaboration, and empowerment, which further demonstrates the previous finding that PFA training has been positively associated to strengthen compassionate, trauma-informed practice in general care settings (Levy-Carrick et al., 2019).

However, participants also highlight that PFA delivery in routine care is inherently iterative and fragmented, rather than a one-off interaction – often involving repeated cycles of rapport-building, assessment, and practical support in response to patients’ evolving distress or trauma. This deviates from the traditional view of PFA as a discrete crisis intervention delivered in the initial hours, days, or weeks after trauma exposure (Schnyder & Cloitre, 2022). While there is growing recognition that many elements of PFA remain helpful beyond the initial post-trauma period (Lewis & Bisson, 2022), our findings raise a further conceptual question: whether full delivery of all PFA components (and, by extension, Hobfoll's essential elements) is necessary in every context, or whether briefer, partial applications can still achieve meaningful early-response goals in busy clinical environments. This warrants further empirical and theoretical work.

Workforce well-being and culture change

Beyond patient care, the perceived benefits of this adapted PFA training extended to workforce well-being and team culture. HCWs described greater self-compassion, reduced stigma around vulnerability, and enhanced self-care – findings consistent with evidence from other high-risk professional groups where PFA and related peer-support initiatives have been associated with improved help-seeking and destigmatization (Geoffrion et al., 2023; Jonker et al., 2020). Such shifts matter in blame-prone environments that undermine psychological safety (Brborović et al., 2019).

Ward managers described improved communication, conflict management, and interprofessional collaboration, resonating with studies from UK psychiatric settings where brief psychosocial training strengthened staff ‘togetherness’ and engagement (Kantaris et al., 2020). Several ward managers also noted the utility of PFA training for ‘second-victim’ support after adverse events, indicating a pragmatic role for PFA in organizational responses to harm (Busch et al., 2021). Many participants described adopting self-care, setting clearer personal boundaries, and feeling more able to speak out – all notable in light of long-standing professional norms of stoicism that can inhibit help-seeking (Jones et al., 2020). These behavioural and cultural shifts provide plausible mechanisms for the quantitative signals of reduced burnout and increased compassion satisfaction reported in the accompanying trial (Wang, Norman, Xiao, et al., 2024), areas that remain underexamined in the PFA literature.

Participants’ accounts pointed to two interlinked mechanisms underpinning these changes: knowledge and skills development, which helped them to move from uncertainty to action; and group peer learning, which created a shared language and safe space for reflection. This dual focus explains our trial findings and align with recommendations that burnout-prevention strategies for HCWs should combine individual-level capacity building with organizational support and culture change (Aryankhesal et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). In this light, PFA training can be conceptualized not only as an early psychosocial intervention, but also as an integrated workforce well-being and quality-improvement strategy.

PFA training implementation challenges and structural constraints

Although training improved awareness and confidence, many HCWs still felt insufficiently supported when dealing with complex assessment and triage decisions, describing a tendency to revert to task-oriented practices when uncertainty arose. This mirrors wider concerns in the literature that brief, didactic PFA courses – often delivered with variable quality – may provide false reassurance without ensuring competency. As noted by Horn et al. (2019) in the context of the Ebola response, brief, one-off PFA training frequently led to diluted PFA delivery, with minimal opportunity for participants to internalize core principles or adapt skills to their context. Such gaps risk undermining the ‘do no harm’ principle – not through the content of PFA itself, but through insufficient training duration, lack of scope, and limited cultural sensitivity, which may leave providers unprepared to respond effectively to nuanced distress. Furthermore, our participants consistently emphasized the importance of culturally grounded content in enhancing training relevance. Scenarios reflecting everyday realities – such as supporting distressed colleagues during COVID-19 outbreaks – resonated more deeply and promoted engagement. Several participants also noted that the READ-Y model's structure mirrored existing clinical procedures (e.g. nursing protocols), making the PFA steps feel familiar and actionable. These findings suggest that when training is culturally and contextually attuned, healthcare workers are more likely to adopt and apply psychosocial support techniques. Thus, future efforts should explicitly embed cultural competence into PFA design to ensure contextual fit and meaningful application.

More broadly, difficulties integrating PFA into routine practice reflected structural barriers to delivering psychosocial support, including biomedical dominance, excessive workloads, and workforce burnout. These mirror the global challenges in improving psychosocial support and trauma-informed care within routine clinical services (Emsley et al., 2022; Purtle, 2020; Sweeney et al., 2016). Without systemic integration, clear organizational expectations, and visible leadership support, the feasibility and sustainability of PFA in routine practice will remain constrained, even when frontline staff recognize its value.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it was embedded within an ongoing randomized controlled trial of READ-Y PFA training intervention, and guided by established process-evaluation principles, enabling a nuanced understanding of how the intervention was implemented and experienced, and how context shaped emerging outcomes. Secondly, we triangulated data across multiple sources (observations, interviews, and field notes) and stakeholder groups (HCW trainees, ward managers, trainers, and implementation staff), strengthening the credibility and completeness of the findings. Thirdly, the analysis was informed by implementation-science perspectives, using implementation outcomes as sensitizing concepts to interpret patterns of acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, fidelity, and perceived sustainability.

However, limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the qualitative sample, although diverse in terms of roles and specialties, included only a small number of ward managers and trainers, and underrepresented other professional groups, such as doctors. This limits the breadth of multidisciplinary perspectives and may constrain transferability to broader clinical contexts. Secondly, the single-centre design, situated in a well-resourced tertiary hospital with strong leadership support, may not transfer to other settings or levels of care. Thirdly, the outcomes were based on self-reported perceptions and observed behavioural patterns, without direct linkage to patient-reported or clinical outcome data, which constrains generalizability and causal inference. Finally, the study captured relatively early implementation and immediate post-training experiences; the durability of skills application, integration into routine workflows, and long-term sustainability remain uncertain.

Implications for research, practice, and policy

For practice, these findings support the integration of culturally adapted PFA training into routine professional development as a feasible, low-intensity strategy to enhance healthcare workers’ psychosocial competencies, including empathy, communication, and self-care. Such training may contribute towards more compassionate care and workforce resilience in high-pressure environments.

For policy, embedding PFA within continuing education standards and quality improvement frameworks can strengthen the psychosocial dimension of healthcare and foster trauma-informed, psychologically safe workplaces.

For research, future work should directly address the limitations of this study – particularly the small sample size and underrepresentation of certain professional groups – by conducting multi-site evaluations across diverse clinical settings. Key priorities include testing scalable delivery models (such as train-the-trainer cascades), exploring digital platforms for reinforcement and sustained peer learning, and assessing long-term skills retention and integration into routine clinical workflows. It is also necessary to investigate the uptake and relevance of PFA training among underrepresented disciplines, such as physicians. Finally, rigorous evaluation of both staff- and patient-level outcomes, including cost-effectiveness, is essential to inform broader implementation. These findings, combined with the trial results, lay the groundwork for a definitive evaluation of PFA training at scale.

Conclusion

The adapted PFA training programme was perceived as feasible, relevant, and acceptable for HCWs, facilitating the integration of psychosocial support into routine care while promoting their own well-being. System-level integration and longitudinal evaluation of PFA training are recommended to assess the sustained impact on patient and staff outcomes.
