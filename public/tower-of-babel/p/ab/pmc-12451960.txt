Finerenone in non-diabetic CKD: an underexplored therapeutic opportunity
Authors: Yu Zhao, Wenyun Wang, Jiaxing Nie, Jiaying Zhou, Zhilong Dong
Published: 2025

Dear Editor

We were particularly intrigued by the article by Li et al., [1] which comprehensively evaluated the safety and efficacy of finerenone in adult patients with non-diabetic chronic kidney disease (CKD). Finerenone, as a novel selective non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist, has shown greater inhibition of inflammation and fibrosis as well as offers end-organ protection in cardiac and renal. The retrospective study of 78 patients with non-diabetic CKD (including of 51% IgAN) showed that finerenone effectively reduced proteinuria (1.71 ± 0.32 g/d at baseline, 0.64 ± 0.14 g/d at 3 months, and 0.55 ± 0.14 g/d at 6 months), while eGFR showed a transient decline (88.34 ± 4.48 mL/min/1.73 m2, 82.09 ± 4.69 mL/min/1.73 m2, and 87.05 ± 6.21 mL/min/1.73 m2 for baseline, 3 months and 6 months, respectively). However, we aim to conduct an in-depth investigation into several critical determinants worthy of exploration to optimize these research findings, thereby facilitating the drug’s broad clinical adoption.

Firstly, heterogeneity in follow-up timepoints. The original protocol [1] designated the 3-month and 6-month as key follow-up points, whereas based on pivotal clinical trials the 4-month and 9-month time point were chosen. The 4-month follow-up was strategically selected to evaluate early treatment response, in line with FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD [2], in which finerenone significantly reduced urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) at this time point (31%–32%, p < 0.001). The 9-month follow-up time evaluated treatment durability, aligning with the NefIgArd trial [3], which established this as the primary time point for assessing urinary protein reduction and supported the FDA approval of Nefecon for IgAN. The retrospective cohort study showed that 24h urinary protein decreased by 15.36% at 4 months and 29.03% at 9 months of finerenone treatment in IgAN patients; Meanwhile, the least squares mean change in eGFR slope was −0.02 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 4 months and stable eGFR levels with a mean change of 1.87 mL/min/1.73 m2 at 9 months also in finerenone group, all the data from the Chinese Registry of Prognostic Study of IgAN [4].

Secondly, methods used to measure albuminuria varied. Proteinuria is an independent predictor for IgAN. A study by Zhao et al. [5] including of 438 patients with IgAN were explored which was the best predictor of IgAN prognosis during UACR, protein-to-creatinine ratio and 24 h urinary protein. In univariate survival analysis, UACR consisitently had better performance than protein-to-creatinine ratio and 24 h urinary protein as represented by higher area under the curve using time-dependent survival analysis. Furthermore, Cox proportional hazards model showed that UACR was most significantly associated with the composite end point (UACR: HR 1.56, 95%CI 1.29 - 1.89, p < 0.001; protein-to-creatinine ratio: HR 1.35, 95%CI 1.10 - 1.66, p = 0.004; 24 h urinary protein: HR 1.19, 95%CI 0.97 - 1.45, p = 0.10). Bayesian meta-regressions across 41 randomized trials of CKD progression found that combining information from treatment effects on UACR change and GFR slope improved prediction of treatment effects on clinical endpoints [6]. The study [1] employed 24 h urinary protein – urine collection is a cumbersome procedure and subject to collection errors and lower.

Thirdly, potentially overlooked confounders. Anemia is common in patients with CKD. The post hoc analysis investigating the treatment effects of finerenone vs placebo by anemia status at baseline, the results suggest that finerenone reduced the risk of the cardiovascular composite outcome to a greater extent in patients with vs without anemia (HR 0.75, 95% CI 0.65 - 0.88 vs HR 0.93, 95% CI 0.82 - 1.05, p = 0.03), no heterogeneity was observed at the risk of the kidney composite outcome. Likewise, patients with anemia experienced a higher incidence of treatment-emergent hyperkalemia (21.2% for finerenone vs 11.6% for placebo) vs those without anemia (10.5% for finerenone vs 4.6% for placebo) [7]. The article [1] did not provide hemoglobin-related data. Patients with eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 were not included in the article, Li et al. [8] found that finerenone decreased the occurrence of primary composite renal outcome by 15% in the eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 group (HR 0.85, 95%CI 0.76 - 0.95, p < 0.05).

Finally, the study by Li et al. [1] laid the groundwork for the safe use of finerenone in non-diabetic CKD. Finerenone may offer renal benefits to a broader patient population in clinical practice. For future prospective cohort studies and real-world evidence initiatives should be strategically integrated the above-identified considerations.
