Microbial predictors of sustained clinical remission following the Crohn’s disease exclusion diet in adults and microbiome shifts toward healthy pediatric controls
Authors: Rotem Sigall-Boneh, Sarah Organ, Mohammed Ghiboub, Henit Yanai, Lee Abramas, Nicolette Wierdsma, Charlotte M Verburgt, Wouter J de Jonge, Joep P M Derikx, James D Lewis, Hong Gu, Joseph Bielawski, Nitsan Maharshak, Iris Dotan, Eytan Wine, Katherine Dunn, Johan Van Limbergen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Crohn’s disease (CD) exclusion diet (CDED) is an emerging dietary therapy for inducing remission in CD. However, data on its effects on gut microbiome in adults remain limited. This study investigated microbial responses to CDED in adults with mild-to-moderate CD and compared them with pediatric patients and healthy pediatric controls.MethodsMicrobiome data were analyzed from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) in adults (baseline, weeks 6, 12, 24) and a pediatric RCT (baseline, weeks 6, 12). Baseline microbial composition, diversity, and functional potential were compared between patients who achieved sustained clinical remission (SCR) at both weeks 12 and 24 and those who did-not. Functional profiling was performed using gene ortholog annotations, linear discriminant analysis, and metabolite inference.ResultsBaseline microbial and functional profiles differed between patients with and without SCR. SCR was associated with lower alpha diversity, higher relative abundances of Alistipes and Faecalibacterium, and increased flagellin gene expression. SCR was associated with enrichment of genes for redox balance, fatty acid metabolism, and DNA repair, while non-SCR showed elevated NAD biosynthesis, bacterial adhesion, and pro-inflammatory pathways. Haemophilus and Prevotella were negatively linked to SCR. Compositional and functional microbiome analyses revealed a microbiome shift during CDED-induced remission toward a profile more similar to healthy pediatric controls.ConclusionsBefore and during CDED, distinct baseline microbial and functional profiles were associated with SCR. These highlight the potential of the gut microbiome as a biomarker for identifying patients most likely to benefit from sustained effects of dietary therapy, supporting a more personalized approach to CD management.Keywords: diet, Crohn’s disease, microbiome

What is already known?

The Crohn’s disease exclusion diet (CDED) induces remission and modulates the gut microbiome in pediatric Crohn’s disease.

What is new here?

This study identifies a microbiome signature linked to sustained remission in adults and demonstrates a shared shift toward a healthy microbiome in both pediatric and adults treated with CDED.

How can this study help patient care?

Microbiome profiling may serve as a predictive biomarker to guide personalized use of CDED and identify patients most likely to achieve sustained remission through dietary therapy.

Introduction

The prevalence of Crohn’s disease (CD) has risen significantly in recent years, particularly in Western countries where exposure to a Western diet is more common.1,2 CD is driven by a multifactorial interplay involving genetics, the immune system, and the gut microbiota,3 with diet playing a central role in modulating both host and microbial functions.4,5 Exclusive enteral nutrition (EEN) has long been used to induce remission, especially in children, achieving high success rates and mucosal healing.6,7 While its mechanisms remain unclear, growing evidence suggests that the exclusion of specific pro-inflammatory dietary components may be critical to its efficacy.5,8

The Crohn’s disease exclusion diet (CDED) has emerged as an effective alternative to EEN with demonstrated efficacy in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and inclusion in clinical guidelines.8–13 The CDED is based on the exclusion of potentially proinflammatory ingredients while incorporating components that promote a healthier gut microbiome.14 Studies have shown reduced dysbiosis during CDED, with decreased Proteobacteria and increased Firmicutes.8,15 While these shifts bring the microbiome closer to a healthy pediatric control, 12 weeks of therapy may not be long enough to fully correct dysbiosis, even when clinical remission is achieved.15 In contrast to EEN, where reintroduction of a free diet after EEN often leads to a resurgence of inflammation and rebound dysbiosis, including increased Proteobacteria.8 Continued adherence to CDED following remission up to week 12 supports sustained clinical improvement and progressive normalization of microbiome composition and function.15 CDED has also been associated with significant shifts in the metabolome, including in tryptophan-related pathways.16,17 CDED has recently shown efficacy in adults with mild-to-moderate CD, maintaining remission for up to 24 weeks.9 However, its long-term effects on the microbiome in adults remain unexplored. While dietary therapy is well studied in pediatric populations, little is known about whether microbiome responses to CDED are similar between children and adults. To our knowledge, no prior studies have systematically compared these responses across age groups.

Moreover, it remains unclear whether baseline microbial composition and functional capacity can predict sustained remission, and whether dietary therapy promotes convergence toward microbiome profiles observed in healthy individuals. Addressing these gaps may help advance microbiome-guided precision nutrition in CD. This study aimed to evaluate whether baseline microbiome biomarkers can predict sustained clinical remission (SCR) in adults with mild-to-moderate CD treated with CDED. Additionally, we sought to identify similarities and differences in microbiome composition between 2 cohorts, pediatric, and adult patients, treated with CDED as part of 2 independent RCTs.8,9 Finally, we aimed to compare these findings with healthy pediatric controls to determine whether SCR may modify CD-related dysbiosis.

Methods

Study overview and structure

This study combines data from 3 sub-studies, as outlined in Figure 1. The first presents unpublished microbiome results from the CDED-AD (CDED-adult) study.9 The second compares the adult microbiome to the pediatric microbiome from the index RCT8. Lastly, the third part compares both patient groups to healthy pediatric controls. For details on study design and analyses within these studies please see the corresponding references.8,9

Overview of study design and comparative analyses across cohorts.

Study design and participants

In part 1, we analyzed microbiome data from an open-label randomized pilot trial in adults (CDED-AD study),9 with samples collected at baseline and weeks 6, 12, and 24. Briefly, the study included patients with biologic-naïve, mild-to-moderate CD, defined by a Harvey–Bradshaw Index (HBI) score 5-14 and disease duration <5 years and evidence of active inflammation confirmed by imaging and elevated CRP or calprotectin levels. Participants were randomized to either CDED Phase 1 with partial enteral nutrition (PEN) using 1 liter of modulen (Nestlé Health Science, Vevey, Switzerland) for 6 weeks, transitioning to Phase 2 with 600 mL PEN, and Phase 3 up to week 24 and group 2 who followed the 3 phases of CDED without PEN. Clinical remission (HBI <5) and SCR, defined as remission at both weeks 12 and 24 were assessed.

Microbiome analysis adult cohort

DNA was extracted from stool samples using the MO BIO PowerFecal DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) at the integrated microbiome resource (IMR) of Dalhousie University (imr.bio). Amplification and sequencing of the 16S ribosomal RNA gene (V4/V5 regions) was performed at the IMR using primers 515FB/926R. Sequenced reads were processed using QIIME2 with the SILVA database. Sequences were processed and analyzed following Comeau et al.18 Microbial diversity and compositional changes at baseline and different weeks of treatment were assessed using QIIME2 (https://qiime2.org). To further investigate differences between patients who achieved SCR and those who did not, alpha diversity and Poisson principal component analysis (PPCA)19 was used to assess the differences in the microbiome of these 2 groups. PPCA was also used to assess the differences in the microbiome in relation to baseline HBI score, treatment type (CDED or CDED + PEN), and calprotectin levels.

Microbiome analysis adult cohort-shotgun metagenome

Extracted DNA was also used for shotgun metagenomic sequencing using Nextera XT library preparation and pair-end 150-bp Nextseq sequencing. Sequences were processed using the kneaddata pipeline, (Trimmomatic20 and bowtie221 to remove low-quality reads [<50 base pairs and quality score <Q20]) and contaminants (human and PhiX174). Using parallel22 paired forward and reverse sequence reads were concatenated and HUMAnN223 was run to functionally annotate reads into enzyme commission (EC) numbers which can be converted to substrate product pairs using BiomeNet scripts.24 Taxonomic assignment of shotgun sequences were determined from concatenated reads using Metaphlan225 which uses marker genes to assign taxonomic identity.

Comparison of microbiome responses to CDED in pediatric and adult cohorts

Microbiome responses were compared with data from the pediatric index RCT evaluating CDED + PEN versus EEN in children with mild-to-moderate CD.8 The study randomized patients to CDED with 50% PEN using Modulen or EEN for 6 weeks, followed by CDED Phase 2 with 25% PEN. Remission was defined as Pediatric Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (PCDAI) ≤10 at week 6 (intention to treat), and sustained remission as remission at both weeks 6 and 12. Both 16S rRNA (V4V5) and shotgun metagenomic data were analyzed.8

To allow cross-cohort comparison, a shared analysis pipeline was applied. Alpha diversity and Wilcoxon tests assessed microbial diversity and taxonomic changes across timepoints and remission status. PPCA19 was applied to examine associations between microbiome structure and clinical variables, including calprotectin and disease severity. T-tests and analysis of variance ANOVA were applied to PPCA scores to identify significant microbiome relationships with clinical variables. Additionally, PERMANOVA was applied to the PPCA scores to assess the significance of differences in microbial community clustering from the PPCA scores among the cohorts and remission/non-remission groups. Wilcoxon tests assessed significant changes in microbial abundances at different weeks and between remission/non-remission groups. Logistic regression and Kaplan–Meier curves were used to assess the relationship between the abundance of taxa and clinical outcomes, focusing on those taxa found to be significantly associated with clinical variables. All analyses performed at the genus or phylum level.

To predict and visualize metabolomic pathways affected when comparing remission versus sustained remission in response to CDED therapy, we performed pathway analysis based on the differential functional abundance of KEGG orthologs (KOs). For this purpose, we used the MicrobiomeAnalyst platform26 for functional profiling, and conducted KEGG pathway reconstruction using KEGG Mapper—Reconstruct (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/mapper/reconstruct.html). This approach allowed us to identify metabolic pathways potentially altered at baseline between the remission and sustained remission subgroups, providing mechanistic insights into differences in microbial functional capacity.

Statistical analysis

False discovery rate (FDR) correction using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied selectively in analyses involving extensive multiple hypothesis testing, specifically in taxa-level comparisons. Meanwhile, analyses such as PERMANOVA, alpha diversity, and PPCA were hypothesis-driven or global in nature, involving a limited number of comparisons. For these, we used raw (uncorrected) P-value. This decision was made to maximize the detection of potential associations, particularly in contexts where there was strong biological plausibility and prior evidence supporting the relevance of the microbial taxa and pathways, such as findings from our previous work in the pediatric cohort.15,16 Additionally, the small sample size limited statistical power, and the use of raw P-value allowed us to prioritize identifying meaningful patterns while reducing the risk of false positives due to existing knowledge and hypothesis-driven study design. In cases where raw P-value were used, they are reported as nominal P-value, reflecting their exploratory nature and acknowledging the absence of multiple testing correction.

Microbiome comparison between pediatric, adult, and healthy control cohorts

Lastly, we included metagenomic data from 26 publicly available healthy pediatric controls27,28 with no history of IBD, celiac disease, bowel surgery, or recent antibiotic use. Ethics approval was obtained in both source studies. Beta diversity was used to compare microbiome profiles at baseline and week 6 in both cohorts against healthy pediatric controls.

Unsupervised Bayesian analysis of community metabolism

Unsupervised Bayesian analysis of community metabolism (BiomeNet)24 was performed to identify metabotypes on pediatric, and adult CDED treated samples along with healthy pediatric controls. EC numbers for identified genes were converted to reaction substrate-product pairs to create metabolic network files. The Bayesian model was run with K = 3 metabotypes and L = 50 subsystems. The first 50 iterations were discarded as “burn-in” after which 2000 iterations were performed retaining samples every 10 iterations.

Results

Study participants

Adult cohort

In the adult CDED-AD study, stool samples were available from 36 of 40 patients; however, only the 24 patients who provided samples at all-time points were included in the microbiome analysis. Among them, remission rates were 18/24 (75%) at week 6, 17/24 (70%) at week 12, and 16/24 (66.6%) at week 24. Among the 24 fully sampled patients, 79.2% (n = 19) achieved SCR, while 20.8% (n = 5) did not.

Pediatric cohort

Data were derived from the index RCT in pediatric patients receiving CDED + PEN. Twenty-five patients with samples available at all timepoints (baseline, week 6, and week 12) were included in the microbiome analysis (out of 36 patients in the CDED group). Remission rates were 88% at week 6 and 84% at week 12.

Although both cohorts had mild-to-moderate disease, pediatric patients had significantly higher baseline disease activity, reflected by elevated CRP and fecal calprotectin levels, and more frequent ileocolonic involvement. In contrast, adults more commonly had isolated ileal disease. Patients’ characteristics are shown in Table 1; inclusion/exclusion criteria are listed in Table S1.

Part 1: microbiome analysis of adults with CD in the CDED-AD study

To identify baseline microbiome features associated with SCR, we compared microbiome composition between patients who achieved SCR and those who did not.

Baseline microbiome distinguishes sustained responders to CDED

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering between patients with SCR and those with non-SCR (Figure 2A) (PERMANOVA P-value: .0009). Specifically, at baseline, alpha diversity was significantly lower in patients with SCR (P = .0067) (Figure 2B).

To further characterize microbiome structure associated with clinical outcomes, we examined baseline principal components and their relationship with remission and disease activity.

Principal components of baseline microbiome link to clinical activity, remission, and treatment response

Baseline PCA identified 5 components associated with clinical outcomes (Figure S1a-d, Table S2). Cumulatively, PCs 1-5 explained approximately 60% of the total variance. PC1 (variance explained 35.2%), enriched in Firmicutes and Bacteroidota, was associated with SCR (a). PC2 (variance explained 7.4%), characterized by Bacteroides and Akkermansia muciniphila, was associated with baseline HBI (c) and predicted calprotectin levels at week 12 (d). PC3 (variance explained 6.5%), including Fusicatenibacter, Bilophila, and Oscillibacter, was associated with SCR (a) and treatment type (b) (with or without formula). PC4 (variance explained 5.9%) (Bacteroides, Akkermansia, and Ruminococcus) was associated with baseline HBI (c). PC5 (variance explained 5.1%) including Alistipes, Eubacterium, and Prevotella was also associated with SCR (a) and treatment type (b). The associations are shown in Figure S1a-d, with contributing taxa presented in Figure S2 and Table S2. Overall, the bacterial signatures identified across principal components reflected sustained remission status and baseline disease severity.

To identify specific taxa associated with SCR, we compared microbial abundance between SCR and non-SCR groups at baseline and during follow-up.

Microbial signatures associated with SCR across timepoints

Baseline Haemophilus abundance was significantly associated with reduced likelihood of achieving SCR (P = .0096) (Figure 3A). By week 6, patients with SCR showed increased abundance of Alistipes (obesi, shahii), Bacteroides, Barnesiella, Colidextribacter, and Oscillibacter (Figure S3a). At week 12, Bacteroides clarus and Oscillibacter increased, with a reduction in Granulicatella (Figure S3b), and by week 24, Alistipes and Bacteroides remained elevated (Figure S3c). In contrast, in patients without SCR, an uncultured Oscillospiraceae genus (g_UCG-005) at baseline was also linked to lower SCR rates (P = .0062) and was elevated at week 6 together with Anaerotruncus (P = .027) (Figure 3B). The taxa showed in Figure 3A and B were identified while controlling for the FDR at level q = 0.05 to account for multiple comparisons.29

To determine whether microbial functional potential at baseline was associated with remission outcomes, we compared gene abundance between SCR and non-SCR groups.

Baseline microbial functional signatures associate with SCR

Baseline gene abundance differed between patients with and without SCR, with specific genes showing strong associations with the latter group (Figure 4A). KO02477, representing bacterial flagellin expression was associated with SCR, suggesting that increased motility or shifts in the gut microbial profile may play a role in maintaining SCR. KO00561, representing dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), was associated with non-SCR. This may indicate that alterations in folate metabolism and nucleotide synthesis could be linked to the less favorable clinical outcomes observed in these patients. Together, these findings suggest that baseline microbial functional signatures are associated with sustained remission status and may reflect underlying microbial traits linked to favorable or unfavorable clinical trajectories.

To further identify functional genes discriminating between outcome groups, we applied linear discriminant analysis (LDA).

LDA identifies functional gene signatures distinguishing patients with and without SCR

LDA revealed modest but significant differences in KOs between groups (Figure 4B), with more associated with non-SCR. Key genes include K08600 (srtB; Sortase B), which is involved in bacterial surface protein modification and may influence microbial-host interactions in IBD30; K00767 (nadC), responsible for NAD biosynthesis and relevant to immune activation in IBD31,32; and K03390 (hdrC2), a heterodisulfide reductase subunit involved in redox regulation and the metabolism of methanogenic archaea, potentially contributing to dysbiosis and inflammation in IBD.33,34 Conversely, genes associated with SCR include K00245 (frdB), a succinate dehydrogenase subunit linked to microbial dysbiosis and intestinal inflammation35; K03572 (mutL), a DNA mismatch repair protein connected to genome integrity and autoimmune responses36; and K03603 (fadR), which regulates fatty acid metabolism in Escherichia coli and may influence microbial balance, contributing to gut dysbiosis and inflammation.37,38 These genes underscore key pathways related to inflammation, immune function, and microbial interactions that are strongly associated with either SCR or non-SCR, suggesting potential roles in IBD pathogenesis (Figure 4B).

To evaluate functional metabolic pathways associated with sustained remission, we performed pathway-level analysis based on gene abundance differences.

Microbial pathway analysis highlights functional and metabolic signatures associated with SCR

Pathway analysis revealed significant baseline differences in functional gene abundances of ∼159 genes between patients with SCR and non-SCR. Affected pathways include carbohydrate metabolism, cellular processes, genetic information processing, and human disease, with notable impacts on ascorbate, pyruvate, purine, and succinate metabolism. Key pathways such as ABC transporters, sulfur metabolism, bacterial chemotaxis, and flagellar assembly exhibited strong effects despite the small sample size. These findings align with earlier studies39 (Figure S4).

Based on the observed changes in functional gene abundance (KOs), we predicted the impacted metabolic pathways. We predicted alterations in metabolites involved in nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism, the citrate cycle, pyruvate metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and propanoate metabolism (Figure 4C, Figure S5a-d).

We observed significant changes in KO numbers related to carbohydrate metabolism, environmental information processing, and cellular processes, with marked impacts on metabolic pathways (eg, cysteine and methionine, ascorbate and aldarate, pyruvate, C5-branched dibasic acid, carbon fixation, and methane metabolism) as well as non-metabolic pathways (ABC transporters and flagellar assembly). These differences showed an association with remission status (Figure S6a-c). In addition, succinate synthesis was significantly associated with SCR (Figure S7).

To identify shared and cohort-specific microbial predictors of sustained remission, we compared microbiome features associated with SCR in both adult and pediatric cohorts.

Shared and distinct microbiome signatures associated with SCR in pediatric and adult cohorts

Pediatric patients exhibited higher baseline alpha diversity, which significantly increased by week 12 compared to the adult cohort, who showed a similar, but nonsignificant trend (Figure 5C). Analysis of microbiome composition confirmed adult findings, showing that baseline Haemophilus abundance was significantly associated with a reduced probability of SCR in the pediatric cohort (Figure 5D). Comparing the cohorts, we observed more pronounced microbiome changes in the pediatric CDED group between baseline and week 6. Additionally, Ruminococcus significantly increased in non-remission patients at week 6 in both cohorts (Figure S8).

At week 12, more microbial taxa were linked to patients with SCR in the pediatric cohort, while in adults, more taxa were associated with patients without SCR. Notably, Prevotella was associated with patients without SCR in both cohorts, while Akkermansia, Oscillibacter, UCG-005, Alistipes, Bacteroides, and Eubacterium_coprostanoligenes_group were associated with patients without SCR only in the adult cohort (Figure S9). At baseline, Prevotella was associated with patients who did not achieve SCR in both groups, with no other shared predictors. In the pediatric cohort, Oscillibacter was associated with patients with SCR, whereas in the adult cohort, Alistipes was associated with patients with SCR and Bacteroides with those without SCR (Figure S10).

Importantly, despite differences in baseline diversity and specific taxa, several consistent patterns associated with SCR were observed across both cohorts. In particular, Haemophilus abundance was associated with reduced likelihood of SCR in both adults and children, and several taxa linked to remission or non-remission showed similar directional trends. These shared signatures suggest common microbiome mechanisms underlying response to dietary therapy across age groups.

Part 3: microbiome comparison of adults and children with CD to healthy pediatric controls

To determine how the microbiome in CD differs from health and whether remission is associated with convergence toward healthy profiles, we compared CD cohorts with healthy pediatric controls.

Functional microbiome shifts differentiate pediatric and adult CD from healthy pediatric controls

Lastly, we compared baseline data from patients in the adult and pediatric cohorts with publicly available data of 26 pediatric healthy pediatric controls. Using BioMeNet with L = 100 subsystems, we estimated the contribution of various metabosystems to each sample, identifying 19 major reaction subnetworks across 3 metabosystems (definitions related to BioMeNet appear in the Supplementary Material). Thirteen subsystems showed notable variation, and 1 subsystem (Subsystem 19; Figure 6A, Table S3), involving sugar modification by phosphotransferases, differed significantly across metabotypes. The simplex plot (Figure 6A) shows that healthy pediatric controls (green dots) cluster tightly with high membership in metabosystem 2, while pediatric and adult cohorts are more dispersed, with some pediatric samples exhibiting higher membership in metabosystem 1. We next assessed microbiome structure following remission at week 24 in adults and remission at week 12 in children, compared with healthy pediatric controls. We used the last point of follow up at each cohort to maximize the longest remission we could detect based on the study design. BioMeNet analysis of samples of patients in SCR from both pediatric and adult cohorts revealed convergence and clustering toward the metabosystem 2 functional profile, as seen in simplex plots (Figure 6B). Metabosystem 2 was characterized by a significant reduction in subsystem 20 and an increase in subsystems 43 and 45. Subsystem 20, includes pathways linked to pro-inflammatory microbial activity such as lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis40 and bacterial motility41 (Table S5). In contrast, Subsystem 43 is enriched in amino acid biosynthesis and microbial stress responses,42,43 and Subsystem 45 includes folate-mediated 1-carbon43,44 metabolism and glycine cleavage supporting redox balance, methylation, and nucleotide biosynthesis. These functional shifts suggest a transition toward a more health-associated, anti-inflammatory microbial phenotype following SCR (Tables S6 and S7).

In contrast, adults showed increased butyrate-producing bacteria, such as s_Eubacterium_siraeum and g_Roseburia, while healthy pediatric controls also exhibited notable levels of butyrate producers, including s_Eubacterium_rectale and s_Ruminococcus_bromii.

We then evaluated SCR by comparing patients from the adult cohort at week 24 and the pediatric cohort at week 12 to healthy pediatric controls, using the same LDA cutoff of 2 (Figure 7B). Adults with SCR exhibited a higher abundance of butyrate-producing bacteria (eg, g_Roseburia_intestinalis and s_Eubacterium_hallii) compared to both pediatric cohort and healthy pediatric controls. Overall, the microbiome of patients after dietary therapy became more similar to that of healthy pediatric controls in both cohorts (Figure 7B). Notably, this convergence toward healthy-associated microbiome profiles was observed in both adult and pediatric cohorts, supporting the presence of shared microbiome restructuring during sustained remission. These findings suggest that dietary therapy may promote common functional and compositional shifts regardless of age.

To assess overall microbiome structure relative to healthy pediatric controls, we evaluated beta diversity across cohorts and time points.

Lastly, at baseline, beta diversity showed distinct clustering of the adult and pediatric cohorts, and healthy pediatric controls, reflecting differing microbial compositions (PERMANOVA P-value: .00099). After 6 weeks of CDED, patients in remission from both cohorts exhibited profiles more similar to healthy pediatric controls (PERMANOVA P-value: .49), (Figure 7C and D). After 12 weeks, beta diversity of CDED patients in remission did not exhibit differences from healthy pediatric controls (PERMANOVA P-value: .23) (Figure S11).

Taken together, both adult and pediatric cohorts demonstrated consistent microbiome features associated with sustained remission, including reduced abundance of pro-inflammatory taxa and convergence toward healthy-associated functional and compositional profiles. These similarities across independent cohorts strengthen the evidence for shared microbiome mechanisms underlying response to the CDED.

Discussion

This study examined the microbiome response to CDED in adults with mild-to-moderate CD, comparing outcomes with a pediatric cohort and healthy pediatric controls. We identified distinct baseline microbial profiles in patients who achieved SCR and those who did not, suggesting the microbiome’s potential as a predictive biomarker for long-term dietary response.

Specifically, patients who achieved SCR showed enrichment of taxa and functional pathways associated with anti-inflammatory activity, metabolic adaptability, and redox balance, whereas patients without SCR exhibited microbial signatures linked to dysbiosis and inflammation. In addition, patients who achieved SCR demonstrated functional convergence toward microbiome profiles observed in healthy pediatric controls. As some patients prefer to avoid pharmacologic therapy, identifying those likely to maintain remission could enable more personalized care and reduce unnecessary dietary restrictions. Prior studies have shown that early remission and adherence are strong predictors of sustained outcomes to dietary interventions, such as the Mediterranean diet10,45,46; however, both features clearly required patients to agree to dietary treatment first.

Identifying baseline biomarkers of response is key to personalizing dietary therapy in CD. The association between specific microbial profiles and SCR suggests that baseline composition may predict long-term response.47 Genera such as Faecalibacterium, Alistipes, and Bacteroides, known for anti-inflammatory properties48 may indicate a microbiome more responsive to CDED. Similarly, recent findings by Godny et al. support this concept, identifying a “beneficial” microbial cluster enriched in short-chain fatty acid producers like Faecalibacterium that positively correlated with adherence to a Mediterranean diet, while a “pathogenic” cluster enriched with E. coli and Ruminococcus gnavus was inversely correlated.46 In contrast, enrichment of g_UCG-005 (Oscillospiraceae) in patients with non-SCR also found in CD risk models like GEM49 may reflect a more dysbiotic or resistant community. Notably, the link between lower alpha diversity and SCR challenges the assumption that higher diversity is always beneficial50 and could refer to the importance of abundance in certain specific bacteria rather than diversity in general.

Such approaches are already being explored in the context of medical therapy, including thiopurines and biologics.51–53 The Leuven IBD group recently identified microbiome signatures and networks associated with response to biologics,53,54 whereas predictive microbiome signatures for dietary therapy have yet to be validated.55,56

Microbiome shifts over time differed between patients with and without SCR, indicating that CDED promotes changes linked to remission. Increases in Alistipes, Bacteroides, and Barnesiella SCFA producers associated with mucosal healing, align with prior IBD dietary studies, including EEN.57,58 In contrast, Anaerotruncus and g_UCG-005 (Oscillospiraceae), enriched in non-SCR, are linked to dysbiosis and inflammation in CD.50,59

Beyond microbial composition, functional profiling distinguished patients with and without SCR: Flagellin gene expression (KO02477) was higher at baseline in patients with SCR, suggesting a potential protective role for microbial motility and host–microbe interactions, possibly through mucosal stimulation via TLR5 pathways.60,61 In contrast, KO00561, encoding microbial DHFR, was enriched in patients who did not achieve SCR. DHFR is the target of methotrexate (MTX); its microbial expression may interfere with drug efficacy or reflect altered folate metabolism and increased microbial proliferation, potentially contributing to ongoing inflammation.62,63 These findings underscore the potential of baseline microbial functions such as flagellin and DHFR expression not only as indicators of disease trajectory, but also as candidate biomarkers for stratifying patients and guiding personalized therapeutic strategies in CD.

Our functional analysis showed that patients with SCR harbored microbiomes enriched in genes linked to metabolic adaptability, redox balance, and immune regulation specifically frdB (K00245), fadR (K03603), and mutL (K03572) supporting mucosal healing and homeostasis. These functions align with prior evidence connecting redox control and DNA repair to reduced inflammation and improved barrier integrity in IBD.64 In contrast, patients without SCR showed higher abundance of virulence- and host interaction-related genes (srtB, nadC, and hdrC2), associated with dysbiosis and immune activation.39 The enrichment of succinate metabolism, particularly via frdB, in patients with SCR adds complexity to the interpretation of the role of this metabolite. Succinate has been widely described as pro-inflammatory and linked to intestinal inflammation, and Ghiboub et al.17 reported reduced fecal succinate in patients achieving remission with CDED. However, in our study, succinate metabolism genes were enriched at baseline in patients who later achieved SCR. This finding is notable, as elevated succinate levels have been linked to gut dysbiosis and increased virulence of E. coli, a key factor in CD pathogenesis.65,66 This may suggest that succinate pathways reflect complex adaptations between microbial and host metabolism66 and redox balance, rather than simply indicating inflammation. As highlighted by Ghiboub et al., the role of succinate may be context-dependent and should be interpreted within the broader microbial and dietary environment.17

Expanding on the functional findings, our analysis revealed 2 complementary insights. First, patients with SCR showed enrichment in microbial pathways related to redox balance, energy metabolism, and DNA repair. Second, based on KO–metabolite mapping, we predicted potential shifts in metabolites such as nicotinamide, pyruvate, and intermediates of the citrate cycle. These findings suggest that combining predicted functional capacity with direct metabolomic measurement could offer more precise insights into treatment mechanisms.

Comparison of adult and pediatric cohorts may reveal how age, disease severity and disease phenotype may influence microbiome responses to CDED. Despite clinical and baseline microbial differences, remission by week 6 was associated with shared shifts across age groups. Alpha diversity increased in children only, supporting greater microbial adaptability in younger patients,67 potentially making them more responsive to dietary therapy. Haemophilus abundance at baseline was linked to reduced remission in both cohorts, reinforcing its potential as an age-independent marker of poor response.59

Microbiome changes between baseline and week 6 appeared more distinct in pediatric patients, which may reflect a higher degree of microbial adaptability in this group compared to adults. Ruminococcus increased in non-remitters in both cohorts, potentially reflecting a marker of poor response. Similarly, Prevotella was consistently associated with non-SCR, in line with its pro-inflammatory role in IBD,59 though context-dependent effects have been reported.68 In contrast, Alistipes and Oscillibacter, enriched in patients with SCR, have shown both protective and pathogenic roles depending on host context.69,70

Other dietary interventions have shown similar patterns, further supporting a potential role for the microbiome in modulating treatment response. As previously noted, adherence to the MD was associated with favorable clinical and microbial outcomes in newly diagnosed CD, including enrichment of Faecalibacterium and other SCFA producers, and a reduction in E. coli and R. gnavus.46 In contrast, studies of the low FODMAP diet in irritable bowel syndrome have not consistently identified baseline microbial predictors of clinical response,71 suggesting that such associations may be intervention specific.

Comparison of both cohorts to healthy pediatric controls revealed distinct functional differences using BioMeNet analysis, with healthy individuals showing tight clustering in metabosystem 2, indicating a stable metabolic profile. In contrast, pediatric and adult patients with CD were more dispersed across metabosystems, reflecting functional instability and disease-associated disruption. Notably, Subsystem 19, related to sugar modification by phosphotransferases, was significantly altered in CD, suggesting shifts in carbohydrate metabolism consistent with inflammation-driven microbial changes.72 Interestingly, in patients who achieved SCR, BiomeNet analysis revealed a shift in functional configuration toward the healthy pediatric control profile. Remission samples, both pediatric and adult, showed reduced dispersion and greater alignment with the metabotype 2, indicating a more stable and coordinated metabolic state. This transition was characterized by a decrease in Subsystem 20 linked to pro-inflammatory pathways and an increase in Subsystems 43 and 45, which include amino acid biosynthesis, redox regulation, and folate-mediated 1-carbon metabolism. Notably, pathways involved in folate biosynthesis, identified within Subsystem 43 and strongly reinforced by KEGG enrichment analysis, were consistently elevated during remission. These changes suggest a functional reprogramming of the microbiome during remission, reflecting a shift away from inflammation-associated activity and toward metabolic stability and repair.

These findings support functional profiling as a valuable complement to taxonomic analysis in identifying treatment-responsive individuals. When using LEfSe analysis, we further compared microbiome composition at baseline across adults, pediatrics patients, and healthy pediatric controls. Pediatric patients exhibited higher levels of Proteobacteria, consistent with dysbiosis and inflammation, while adults and healthy pediatric controls showed greater abundance of butyrate-producing genera like Roseburia and Eubacterium in line with previous pediatric CDED studies.15,16 Following dietary therapy, both cohorts shifted toward a healthier microbial profile, with adults with SCR showing increased butyrate producer levels compared to pediatric and healthy pediatric controls. Beta diversity results further support that CDED may shift the microbiome toward a healthier composition, as patients achieving remission showed a microbial profile more closely resembling that of healthy pediatric controls. These findings support CDED’s potential to promote beneficial microbial changes, though the degree of restoration may be influenced by treatment duration and other host factors.

The study has several strengths. Specifically, the use of 2 well-characterized RCT cohorts and the first direct comparison of microbiome responses to CDED in adult and pediatric CD patients; the inclusion of healthy pediatric controls enabling assessment of microbiome normalization; and advanced analyses PCA, functional predictions, and metabosystem modeling allowing detailed tracking of microbiome dynamics alongside clinical outcomes.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. The sample size was limited to patients with complete longitudinal microbiome data, which may reduce generalizability. Adults received the CDED with or without PEN, whereas all pediatric patients received CDED + PEN, introducing variability in dietary exposure between cohorts. The absence of metabolomic data in adults limited the ability to validate predicted microbial functions. Although we cannot determine the direction of change, our results suggested that certain metabolites might be affected; however, these predictions remain unverified, as the metabolites were not directly measured. Additional limitations include significant baseline differences in disease activity and phenotype between adult and pediatric cohorts, which may act as confounders influencing microbiome responses to the intervention. Moreover, due to the small sample size particularly the limited number of non-remission patients in the adult group we did not perform LDA on microbial abundance data, as was done for KO analysis. Nonetheless, PPCA analysis provided sufficient evidence of group differences between remission and non-remission patients. Finally, the lack of dietary data for the healthy control group limits contextual interpretation of their microbiome profiles.

In conclusion, our study provides new insights into the microbiome response to CDED in adults, alongside a direct comparison with pediatric patients. Despite baseline differences particularly greater dysbiosis in children, both groups showed a shift toward a healthier microbiome, including enrichment of butyrate-producing taxa. These findings support the role of microbiome profiling in identifying patients most likely to achieve sustained remission with dietary therapy, whether as a standalone approach or in combination with pharmacologic therapy. As we move toward more individualized care in CD, microbiome-based biomarkers could help identify those most likely to respond and sustain long-term remission, enabling more personalized and preference-sensitive treatment strategies. Integrating microbiome-informed tools into clinical decision-making could ultimately pave the way for precision nutrition designed to optimize both clinical outcomes and microbial health.
