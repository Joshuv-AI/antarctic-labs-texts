New modalities for ultra-high dose rate irradiation and FLASH experiments
Authors: Gabriel Adrian, Raphaël Moeckli, Elke Beyreuther, Ludvig P Muren, Brita Singers Sørensen
Published: 2025

In vitro radiobiological experiments using ultra-high dose rate (UHDR) irradiation were performed already during the 1960s to 1980s, with several promising studies demonstrating normal tissue sparing [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5]]. In the last decade, UHDR irradiation has again gained significant momentum within the radiotherapy community. UHDR irradiation has demonstrated increased normal tissue tolerance across organ systems and endpoints, using diverse delivery techniques. Intriguingly, the sparing effect appears to preferentially benefit normal tissue, suggesting a widening of the therapeutic window − a phenomenon now widely referred to as the FLASH effect [[6], [7], [8], [9]]. Furthermore, dose rate-dependent biological responses have reignited academic interest in fundamental radiobiological mechanisms that remain largely unexplained. Numerous questions are to be investigated, including the differential effects on tumour vs. healthy tissues, the impact of fractionation, the precise role of oxygenation, the optimal delivery techniques to achieve conformal dose coverage in deep-seated tumours, and potential differences across the various modalities used in UHDR studies.

The biological mechanisms underlying the FLASH effect should continue to attract attention. Yet, the primary goal in the FLASH community remains clear, to effectively treat human patients. While a full understanding of all underlying mechanisms might not be required for a successful clinical translation to humans, robust preclinical evidence is essential to guide the careful selection of clinical scenarios. To date, a few feasibility trials in humans have been conducted [10,11], with others ongoing (Table 1). Meanwhile, FLASH-related publications continue to emerge, although studies with biological/clinical endpoints are so far not dominating as there is an abundance of articles on technical development, delivery techniques, dosimetry, radiochemistry, treatment planning studies, in silico modelling, as well as numerous review articles and editorials.

In the present journal, three recent publications have further explored the landscape of UHDR irradiation, providing new insights and broadening the experimental possibilities [[12], [13], [14]]. Dal Bello et al. reported the successful conversion of a clinical linear accelerator for electron UHDR delivery, with a focus on future experiments using Drosophila melanogaster (fruit flies) [12]. Dose rates reached up to 7000 Gy/s in the most extreme position and 200 Gy/s to 700 Gy/s in more practical positions for multiple simultaneous samples. Interestingly, photon irradiation at 40 Gy/s was also proposed. While this is at the lower end of the UHDR spectrum, it may still offer a viable platform for experiments. In line with another recent publication [15], it demonstrates potential of UHDR irradiation with photons from clinical linear accelerators. Previously, conversions of clinical linear accelerators have enabled electron UHDR irradiation [16,17], along with commercially available adoptions of intra-operative radiotherapy devices for UHDR electrons [18,19]. The authors estimated that up to 500 fruit fly samples could be irradiated weekly. Given the short lifespan of fruit flies, this model offers rapid turnaround for outcome data, and statistical sample size limitations are unlikely to be a concern.

In a study from the Heidelberg Ion Beam Therapy Centre (HIT), Karle et al. presented a novel application of high linear energy transfer (LET) oxygen ions for UHDR delivery [13]. Dose rates exceeding 100 Gy/s were achieved across three experimental set-ups: oxygen consumption measurements, in vitro clonogenic assays, and in vivo tumour growth studies using a pancreatic cancer model. The authors reported no difference in tumour cell responses for UHDR compared with conventional dose rate irradiation. The study did not include normal tissue effects but opens for the possibility of UHDR irradiation with oxygen ions to be further explored as a FLASH modality.

In the third paper, scientists from the Institut Curie Proton Therapy Cancer explored the clinical potential of very high energy electrons (VHEE) for UHDR irradiation, in a comparison with pencil beam scanning (PBS) proton therapy [14]. In contrast to other electron UHDR devices useful for skin and more superficial tissues, the very high energy electron beam can reach deep-seated tumours. In this study, the authors were focusing on the dose and dose rate effect comparison between these two modalities using a new FLASH index. While the proposed index might be too simple to fully describe the FLASH effect, the authors present comparisons of treatment plans for four tumour locations (brain metastases, lung, liver and prostate lesions), indicating that PBS VHEE beams could be an attractive alternative to PBS shoot-through proton beams for FLASH radiotherapy.

The community has assembled an impressive array of possibilities to explore UHDR irradiation. With regards to treatment delivery, UHDR beams are now available using a range of modalities and technologies, including electrons from linear accelerators [16,17] or dedicated UHDR devices [18,20], very-high energy electrons (VHEE) [21], protons [22,23], photons [15,24], carbon ions [25], helium ions [26], and now also oxygen ions [13]. Each modality offers unique advantages and presents distinct challenges. Regarding the biological model systems used to explore the FLASH effect, they range from 2D in vitro cell cultures [27], to spheroids and organoids [28,29], to in vivo models of fruit flies [12], zebra fish [30], mice and rats [31,32], as well as larger animals like cats, dogs and pigs [33,34]. Testing each irradiation device across all biological model systems quickly multiplies into an unmanageable number of combinations. Moreover, some studies may be under-powered, potentially contributing to an overwhelming volume of data without clear direction.

Even in ideal scenarios with state-of-the-art beam conformity, the question remains of how generally applied radiobiological principles/factors influences the FLASH effect, including fractionation and volume effects and the ⍺/β-ratios of the normal tissues. This also applies to clinically applied combination therapies. In addition, it is yet to be established if it can generally be assumed that the tumour response to UHDR irradiation will be unaltered across all tumour entities and biological conditions, such as tumour hypoxia and intrinsic radiosensitivity.

A deeper understanding of the underlying mechanism for the FLASH effect could enable accurate predictions of UHDR responses across beam types and model systems, ultimately identifying the most suitable human clinical scenarios for UHDR irradiation. In the meantime, well-designed, adequately powered studies, with clearly reported experimental conditions (beam parameters, dosimetry [35], model system) that yield robust, quantitative insights are essential.

While a full mechanistic understanding may not be a prerequisite for clinical translation, the strength of preclinical evidence must be sufficient to guide strategic prioritization. This will be key to successfully translate FLASH from bench to bedside, alongside assuring patient safety aspects [36]. Given that the current surge of interest in UHDR irradiation may represent a “second wave” and acknowledging the continuous stream of promising work from research groups worldwide, we must proceed with scientific rigor. As we move forward, it will be impossible to test every UHDR irradiation device across every available model system. Instead, we must aim for a reasonable number of well-powered pre-clinical studies that assess both the normal tissue sparing and tumour control, with high enough precision to resolve response differences to the same degree in normal tissue as in tumours. Only then can we be confident that UHDR irradiation truly widens the therapeutic window. From that foundation, clinical translation stands a strong change of success.
