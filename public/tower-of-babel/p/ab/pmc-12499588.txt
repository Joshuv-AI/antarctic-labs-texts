Intracranial granuloma: a rare long-term complication of retained shunt catheter. Illustrative case
Authors: Rothaina Saeedi, Khaled Alfaramawy, Ibtihal Zabermawi, Salwa Bakhsh, Maher Kurdi, Saleh Baeesa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDVentriculoperitoneal shunt placement is the most commonly used treatment for hydrocephalus. However, its malfunction frequently presents a challenge in clinical practice, necessitating shunt revision. In certain instances, intracranial catheter revision may pose difficulties due to severe intraluminal adhesions, necessitating the surgeon to leave the catheter in situ. However, retained catheters can lead to severe complications.OBSERVATIONSA 17-year-old male presented with headache and unsteady gait for several weeks. He had a cystoperitoneal shunt for hydrocephalus associated with Dandy-Walker malformation during infancy. However, at the age of 12 years, the shunt was found to be nonfunctioning and was removed, except for the intradural catheter, due to severe adhesions. Brain MRI revealed a large mass encasing the catheter. Craniotomy and gross-total resection of the mass, including the catheter, revealed a granuloma, but no organisms were isolated from the specimen. The postoperative recovery was uneventful, and there was no recurrence for 10 years.LESSONSThis case highlights a potential complication associated with retained intracranial catheters and their neurosurgical management. Our literature review identified a limited number of comparable cases of retained catheter-related granuloma, and the pathophysiology and treatment were elucidated within the review. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25557

Intracranial shunt placement (including ventricular, subdural, or cyst), primarily directed toward the peritoneal cavity, remains the predominant treatment approach for hydrocephalus, providing lifesaving advantages. Nevertheless, the procedure inevitably entails complications such as obstruction, infection, and disconnection, resulting in shunt failure and necessitating surgical revision of the shunt.1 These complications can arise throughout a patient’s lifetime, necessitating one or more revisions in approximately 85% of patients over a 15-year follow-up period.2 Occasionally, complicated shunt revisions impede the complete removal of the nonfunctional component of the shunt system. Notably, portions of the intracranial catheter and other components may be intentionally retained. The incidence and consequences of retained nonfunctional shunt systems, either intracranial or peritoneal, have not been adequately assessed in the literature.3,4

Occasionally, revision of the intracranial malfunctioning catheters presents challenges due to intraluminal adhesions to the choroid plexus or brain tissue. Manipulation of adherent catheters carries the risk of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), which may be identified intraoperatively or visualized on early postoperative CT.5,6 This complication is estimated to occur in approximately 25%–30% of revisions and rarely leads to catastrophic outcomes. However, it may be associated with an increased rate of subsequent revisions. In contrast, the practical implementation of the intraluminal catheter coagulation technique effectively enhanced the probability of dislodging adherent catheters and reduced the likelihood of IVH and subsequent neurological complications.7 Nevertheless, the retention of an intracranial catheter or its fragments can be unavoidable in complex revisions and may be associated with long-term complications.

In this report, we present a rare case of a granuloma developing around a retained intracranial shunt catheter 5 years after revision. The patient underwent complete resection, resulting in favorable long-term outcomes. Furthermore, a comprehensive literature review was conducted to elucidate the pathophysiology, predictive factors, and management of this rare complication.

Illustrative Case

A 17-year-old male patient presented to the emergency department in May 2010 with a 3-month history of progressive occipital headaches and dizziness. The patient had a 5-year history of removal of a nonfunctional cystoperitoneal shunt (CPS). However, the proximal catheter was left in situ because of intradural adhesions.

His shunt history dates back to infancy when he presented with symptomatic communicating hydrocephalus associated with Dandy-Walker malformation (DWM), which required the insertion of a right-sided CPS at the age of 1 month. Since then, he has not encountered any complications associated with the shunt and has not required any revisions. However, at the age of 12 years, elective revision of the peritoneal catheter was recommended due to the detection of a short peritoneal catheter on shunt radiographic series surveillance. During revision surgery, when the proximal catheter was disconnected from the shunt valve, there was no spontaneous or attempted flushing of the CSF flow from the posterior fossa cyst. As a result, the shunt was identified as nonfunctional, and after intraoperative consultation with the parents, shunt removal was planned. The shunt valve and peritoneal catheter were promptly removed after the release of the surrounding scar tissue. However, the proximal catheter was extremely adherent and could not be removed despite several attempts at stylet intraluminal coagulation; therefore, the catheter was left in situ. The patient remained asymptomatic during the 5-year follow-up period, and serial brain CT scans were unremarkable (Fig. 1A). At presentation, his vital signs and general physical examination were unremarkable, and he was afebrile. There were no signs of swelling, discharge, or tenderness, particularly at the site of the previous shunt surgery. Neurological examination was unremarkable, except for mild gait ataxia, and there was no evidence of papilledema. Laboratory tests, including complete blood count, electrolyte levels, and renal and coagulation profiles, were within the normal range. A CT scan of the brain revealed a 3-cm hyperdense posterior fossa mass, at its maximum diameter, encircling the retained catheter at the level of the previous burr hole (Fig. 1B). There was no surrounding edema, and the size of the ventricular system remained unchanged compared with that in the previous study. MRI revealed a 30-mm × 20-mm right paramedian posterior fossa mass encircling the retained catheter. The lesion was isointense on T1-weighted, T2-weighted, and FLAIR sequences (Fig. 2A–C). The mass exhibited dura-based characteristics and infiltrated the right cerebellar hemisphere without distinct boundaries. Following intravenous gadolinium contrast administration, MRI revealed a dura-based mass characterized by homogeneous vivid enhancement, while the dura exhibited abnormal thickening and enhancement (Fig. 2D–F). Notably, there was no extracranial subcutaneous extension or enhancement, and no other lesions were detected in the supratentorial region of the brain or the spine. CSF analysis obtained from lumbar puncture yielded no abnormal parameters indicative of an infection. The initial diagnoses were either a tumor or an infection, and the parents consented to the surgery. A posterior fossa craniectomy was performed using a midline incision. During surgery, a mildly vascular and firm lesion adhering to the dura mater and cerebellar cortex was observed. Gross-total resection (GTR) was achieved, encompassing a 1-cm cuff surrounding the abnormally appearing lesion attachment, including the retained catheter. The dural defect was repaired using a synthetic dura substitute, and the incision was closed in several layers. A postoperative CT scan revealed an uncomplicated surgery and confirmed GTR of the lesion. The patient had a smooth postoperative course and was discharged in good health.

Histopathological examination of the specimen revealed fibrocollagenous tissue with clusters of perivascular inflammatory cell infiltration, including lymphocytes, histiocytes, plasma cells, multinucleated giant cells, and hemosiderin-laden macrophages (Fig. 3A and B). Multiple cystic areas filled with macrophages indicated an area of infarction surrounded by mild gliosis (Fig. 3B and D). The tissue was stained with anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and smooth muscle actin immunolabeling markers (Fig. 3B). No evidence of microbial organisms or malignancies was detected. These findings are consistent with those of granulomas. Microbiological examination of the tissue and retained catheter did not reveal any bacterial or fungal growth. Discussion

Observations

Retained intracranial shunt catheters are not uncommon in clinical practice, particularly in patients with a history of prolonged ventricular shunt placement and multiple complex revisions.

In such circumstances, complete catheter removal may not be feasible, and the surgeon may decide to leave the catheter in situ. This decision is frequently made because of the significant risk of severe bleeding and subsequent cerebral tissue damage, which is commonly attributed to ventricular plexus and brain adhesions.5,6 The incidence and consequences of retained nonfunctional, complete, or partial shunt systems have been underreported in the literature. Retained intracranial shunt catheters are typically asymptomatic and indolent; however, they can rarely present with delayed catheter-related complications, such as persistent CSF infection, dystrophic calcification, or the development of tumors, such as meningiomas.4,8–10

We present the case of a patient with a retained intracranial catheter for 5 years who developed headache and unsteady gait. These symptoms were attributed to a large posterior fossa–enhancing mass encasing the retained catheter. The radiological appearance of these lesions resembles that of tumors, necessitating surgical intervention. A craniectomy and GTR of the mass, which was consistent with a granuloma, was performed, and the patient remained recurrence free for 10 years. In addition to reporting this rare case, we sought to ascertain the prevalence of intracranial granulomas associated with retained shunt catheters in the literature. Furthermore, we investigated the potential mechanisms, pathophysiology, and predictive factors that contribute to this complication and outlined the management strategies.

A comprehensive search was conducted in the MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Google Scholar databases for articles published from inception to June 2025, utilizing the following search terms: “retained intracranial shunt catheter” AND “intracranial granuloma.” Intracranial procedures include subdural, ventricular, and cystic catheters. Studies involving retained intracranial catheters used for intraventricular chemotherapy and catheter-associated complications, such as meningitis, brain abscesses, and tumors such as CSF tumor seeding or meningioma, were excluded. The initial search yielded 890 reports that were screened. After conducting a meticulous search that excluded irrelevant and duplicate reports and applying the inclusion/exclusion criteria, comprehensive texts of 9 articles were retrieved.11–19 This process identified 11 cases of intracranial granulomas attributable to retained intracranial shunt catheters. The 12 patients, comprising 10 males (83%), had a mean age at presentation of 24.7 ± 17 years (range 3.5–57 years). Shunt procedures were performed for hydrocephalus of various etiologies in 10 patients and in 2 patients with bilateral subdural effusions. Six patients (50%) received a ventriculoperitoneal shunt (VPS), 2 had bilateral a subdural peritoneal shunt (SPS), and 1 had a CPS. In the remaining 3 patients, Torkildsen shunts were inserted to address obstructive hydrocephalus caused by third ventricular tumors. However, the shunts proved ineffective in 2 patients, necessitating the addition of a VPS and ventriculoatrial shunt (VAS).14 Shunt materials were underreported, except for 2 studies. One report documented a patient receiving a silicone rubber Pudenz shunt, while the other reported the use of 3 red rubber Robinson catheters for Torkildsen shunts in 3 patients.11,14 A Medtronic silicone rubber catheter shunt was used in our patient. On reviewing the CT scans in the full texts, silicone rubber catheters were observed to be used in the remaining 9 patients.

The mean time of presentation with granuloma from last shunt surgery, reported in 10 of 12 patients, was 9.15 ± 6.12 years (range 3.5–20 years). Prior shunt infection was reported in 4 patients (33%).12–14,19 Three of them experienced recurrent meningitis necessitating shunt removal; however, intracranial catheters remained in situ, and 1 patient with a Torkildsen shunt was treated with antibiotics only. The clinical presentation of granulomas depends on their location and mass effect. Most patients presented with hydrocephalus or granuloma mass effect with headache and focal neurological deficits, and occasionally with decreased consciousness.11,12,14–18 Additionally, 2 patients with convexity granulomas presented with focal seizures.13,19

From our review of the reported studies, on CT imaging the lesion was characteristically a circumscribed hyperdense mass that exhibited homogeneous enhancement following intravenous contrast administration. Occasionally, dural involvement, surrounding edema, and cyst formation occur in granulomas located in the convexity. On MRI, the granuloma exhibited an isointense signal on T1-weighted images and a hypointense signal on T2-weighted images, with variable postgadolinium enhancements. The retained catheter is typically completely enclosed within the granulomatous mass.

Ten patients (83%) underwent craniotomy and GTR of the lesion, including the retained catheter.11–14,16,18,19 A stereotactic biopsy was performed on a patient with a deeply located granuloma in the basal ganglia, in addition to catheter removal and intravenous antibiotic administration. The patient experienced a favorable outcome with no recurrence during the short-term follow-up period.15 One patient underwent craniotomy and GTR for a parasellar granuloma, which resulted in partial catheter removal due to adhesion to the dura surrounding the cavernous sinus. Subsequently, the patient experienced recurrence after 2 years, necessitating reoperation and GTR, as well as removal of the remaining catheter.17 Follow-up was reported in 4 patients for approximately 3.4 years (range 6 months–10 years) with no recurrence.

Histological examination was performed in all patients with granulomas, and one report described it as an inflammatory pseudotumor.11–19 They reported a mass consisting of diffuse nodular, centrally necrotic aggregates of inflammatory cells, partially covered by the meninges and the choroid plexus. The inflammatory cells were composed of small lymphocytes and reactive xanthomatous macrophages organized in small nodular, centrally necrotic aggregates. Occasionally, microcysts were identified in the inflammatory walls.11,12,14 No organisms were detected in the specimen, except in 2 patients, one of whom had Staphylococcus epidermidis; the other patient’s organism was not specified.13,15

The pathophysiological basis of granuloma formation around retained catheters remains unclear. Initially investigated by Del Bigio, this study directly pertains to shunt complications and tissue reactions to implants.20 He observed that various biological reactions occur between the shunt catheter and the nervous or other tissues in contact with it. Repeated obstruction may be attributed to glia, choroid plexus, fibrous tissue, immunological and clotting factors, and other cellular debris. Subsequent research by Sarkiss et al. elucidated the various types of biological reactions that occur between a ventricular catheter and the brain parenchyma, as well as the temporal sequence of these reactions.21 They proposed a three-step pathophysiological model for this process. First, the wound healing theory posits that mechanical trauma during intracranial catheter insertion initiates an inflammatory process similar to wound healing. This inflammatory response results in the recruitment of microglial cells and the formation of scar tissue in the brain. Consequently, this leads to the early onset of catheter obstruction, typically within 6 months. Second, the pro-inflammatory mediator theory suggests that a prolonged foreign body reaction or delayed immune response can lead to chronic inflammation and astroglial scar formation. This glial scar can initiate a foreign body reaction and potentially alter the biological properties of the silicone shunt tubing, contributing to late shunt obstructions (6 months–3 years after surgery). Activated macrophages cannot digest foreign bodies, resulting in the formation of multinucleated giant cells that release pro-inflammatory mediators. Finally, the late malfunction theory suggests that the late effects of the foreign body response are manifested by the mineralization of the shunt tube and its susceptibility to fractures or degradation, which can lead to late malfunction of the shunt catheter beyond 3 years.

The predictive factors for the development of granulomas in patients with retained intracranial catheters have not yet been fully elucidated. In addition, our comprehensive review suggests the following potential factors: First, an inflammatory lesion surrounding the shunt may be attributed to recurrent nonspecific chronic infections that subsequently heal, resulting in the formation of granulation tissue. Four patients (33%) reported in Table 1 had one or more shunt infections that necessitated shunt removal. Owing to technical constraints, the proximal shunt remained in situ and subsequently became the site of granuloma formation. Second, numerous inflammatory pseudotumors may be associated with IgG4-related disease, in which there is an increase in IgG4-positive plasma cells and lymphocytes infiltrating the exocrine gland and other organs.22 These mediators stimulate fibroblast proliferation, neutrophil extravasation, and activation of procoagulant activity in vascular endothelium. Thrombus formation can obstruct the small capillaries of the brain parenchyma, leading to inflammation and infarction. Although none of the reported cases investigated a potential association between IgG4 and granulomas associated with retained catheters, exploring this possibility is imperative. Third, granuloma formation may be associated with the catheter material. Silicone elastomers are generally considered biocompatible and have low biological reactivity. Nevertheless, acute and delayed hypersensitivity reactions to elastomers have been investigated previously. The immunoreactivity of polydimethylsiloxane elastomers leads to high IgG-Fab and immune-mediated inflammatory reactions to shunt catheters, which could lead to early shunt malfunction, late recurrent inflammation, or granuloma formation.23–25 Lastly, surgical factors, such as the inoculation of foreign bodies from cottonoids, nonabsorbable sutures, hair, or talc granules, during shunt surgery, may provoke subcutaneous reactions that retrogradely migrate through/around the catheter toward the tip and develop granuloma.

To eliminate or mitigate the occurrence of this rare complication, it is generally recommended to avoid the retention of shunt catheters during all revisions. In the event of adhesions between the catheter tip and the brain parenchyma or choroid plexus, specific maneuvers are used to avoid such hemorrhagic complications. These include gentle traction on the catheter, rotation of the catheter, placement of a hemostatic clip on the protruding catheter, and allowing the weight of the clip to provide traction. Intraluminal coagulation of the choroid plexus was then performed.7 Additionally, endoscopy facilitates visualization during traction maneuvers, allowing the identification of adhesions that are directly coagulated to facilitate untethering of the catheter. In addition, endoscopy can be conveniently used for the elective removal of ventricular catheters that have been inadvertently left or misplaced in the ventricular system.26 Nevertheless, these attempts may be unsuccessful in intricate revisions, leading to catheter retention and prolonged clinical and radiological monitoring periods.

Our case is unique in that it demonstrates a de novo granuloma that developed 5 years later due to a retained intracranial catheter in a DWM posterior fossa cyst. This is the first reported case of a granuloma in an infratentorial retained catheter. The only predictive factors postulated in our case were the use of silicone-based shunts and ligation of the intracranial catheter with a silk suture, both of which are known to induce local tissue reactions and granulomas. We also emphasize GTR with the retained catheter as the best treatment option to prevent recurrence, as shown in our case with the longest reported follow-up in the reviewed cases.

Lessons

The natural history of retained intracranial shunt catheters remains unclear in the literature. Surgeons typically consider removing intracranial catheters during revisions when feasible and safe. In complex revisions, each case should undergo a comprehensive evaluation, and the risk-benefit ratio should be meticulously calculated between catheter removal and the potential occurrence of hemorrhagic complications or catheter retention, which may pose long-term risks. While retaining the intracranial portion of a shunt catheter may be justified in certain circumstances, surgeons should ensure clear documentation and obtain informed consent. During the follow-up period, a history of recurrent local or CSF infections and meningitis served as important predictive factors for the development of granulomas and the need for evaluation and retained catheter removal. The development of new neurological symptoms in patients with retained catheters indicates urgent MRI, and if present, GTR and removal of the retained catheter is the preferred approach. Given the limited number of reported cases in the literature, further research is imperative to elucidate the incidence and natural history of retained shunts in general and, specifically, the incidence of granulomas associated with retained shunt catheters. Furthermore, it is imperative to investigate the predictive factors and pathophysiology to assess patients at risk of developing this complication and determine the most effective treatment modalities.
