Effect of stressors during COVID-19 lockdown on malnutrition and health-risk behaviors among Jordanian college students: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Hana Alkhalidy, Islam Al-Shami, Khadeejah Alnaser, Ana’am Alkharabsheh, Hala Nawaiseh, Dongmin Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A stressful condition such as the emergence of the coronavirus and its related lockdown measures might trigger alterations in college students’ behaviors. This cross-sectional study aimed to identify the changes in college students’ dietary and lifestyle behaviors during the lockdown and the effect of lockdown-related stressors on health-risk behaviors. A web-based survey was conducted among undergraduate college students in Jordan. Weight and height were reported by the students. The students’ dietary and lifestyle behaviors and their changes during 1 month of the countrywide lockdown were assessed. The Perceived Stress Scale-4 was utilized to assess stress levels. Results indicated that 77.2% of the students reported weight changes. Notably, 45.9% increased their intake of unhealthy food, while and 38% opted for healthier food choices. Most students experienced alterations in physical activity (80.0%), screen time (86.2%), smoking, and sleep patterns (85.4%), with a higher tendency toward adopting health-risk behaviors. Perceptions of curfew-related stress were associated with distance learning-related stress (OR = 2.73, CI: 1.08–6.90, P = .034), and a greater change in physical activity (OR = 3.59, CI: 1.36–9.48, P = .010). Additionally, weight changes were associated with perception of other types of stressors (OR = 3.39, CI: 1.39–8.28, P = .007). Overall, there was a considerable increase in malnutrition and health-risk behaviors among students during the lockdown. Understanding students’ responses to these stressful conditions and the role of stress in driving behavioral changes are crucial for developing interventions that enhance college students’ adaptation to such changes and maintaining healthy dietary and lifestyle behaviors in the future.

1. Introduction

On March 2, 2020, the Jordanian Ministry of Health confirmed Jordan first coronavirus-19 (COVID-19) case.[1] Two weeks later, the Jordanian government implemented the first lockdown and a complete curfew to prevent the spread of COVID-19 throughout the country. Most of the governmental and private sectors were closed, the schools and universities continued through online platforms, and residents’ movement was limited.[2] Jordan was designated as having a “cluster of cases” transmission for the virus based on the World Health Organization (WHO) Situation Report 83 released on April 12, 2020.[3] Therefore, a curfew with restrictive measures was implemented at the beginning of May. The government emphasized strict safety instructions, social distancing, and evening curfews and adjusted lockdown over time.[4,5] At the time of this study, the confirmed COVID-19 cases ranged from 389 cases in early April 2020 to 1132 cases at the end of June,[6] and 7 deaths had been reported by April 12, 2020.[3]

Although lockdown, curfew, or their measures were not globally prevalent before the COVID-19 pandemic, their adoption was a strategy for disease control across several countries.[7–10] These measures, including physical and social isolation, and several institute closures, including educational institutes, introduced stress, anxiety, fears, and difficulties in maintaining a healthy diet and lifestyle.[11–16] The fear of the disease showed to be related to other health consequences as well.[17] Collectively, these measures were specific to each country’s situation which highlights a complex interplay between the health crises and community dynamics.

The pandemic’s effect on college students, in particular, warrant close examination. College students are prone to adopting health-risk behaviors such as decreased intake of fruits and vegetables, smoking, inadequate sleep, and insufficient physical activity (PA).[18] The pandemic measures may exacerbates these behaviors as during the SARS outbreak in 2003 college students experienced high levels of perceived stress and a fear of the unknown.[19] The severity, duration, and types of stressors may associate with altered eating pattern and obesity.[20,21] Additionally, the shift to digital learning may bring about new stressors and challenges, including feelings of isolation and loneliness, as well as restricted opportunities for language and cultural exchange.[22]

Despite the previously documented prevalence of risk-health behaviors among college students in Jordan,[23] no studies have specifically investigated how the early stage of the pandemic influenced their dietary and lifestyle behaviors. This study aimed to examine the impact of the pandemic and its control measure on the Jordanian college students’ dietary and lifestyle behaviors. Additionally, it seeks to identify the stressors introduced by the pandemic and their relationship with students’ behaviors. Addressing stressors accompanying lockdown and curfew measures can help mitigate their adverse effects and support the well-being of students during such situations.[24]

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

A cross-sectional study was conducted between April and June 2020. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Jordan University of Science and Technology (64/128/2019, 28/11/2019). The measured outcomes included dietary and lifestyle behavior alterations during COVID-19, the prevalence of malnutrition, perceived stress (PS) during the pandemic, and its association with these alterations among college students in Jordan. The study employed an online questionnaire using Google Forms, which was distributed through e-learning platforms and social media channels to students across all academic years and specializations. A description of the study and its purpose was posted with the form link, and participation was voluntary. Recruitment and data collection took place during April, May, and June, with participants asked to document changes only during 1 month of the curfew/lockdown. The questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic data (age, sex, academic level, study major, living status, parents’ education, and family income), self-reported anthropometric measurements, dietary behaviors, and lifestyle (health-risk behaviors such as PA, screen time (ST), smoking, and sleep patterns). The study included college students aged 18 years and older from 3 of the largest public universities in Jordan to ensure a diverse and representative sample. The questionnaire was developed based on a thorough literature review and evaluated for clarity by an experienced research team and professionals in the nutrition field. To test the reliability and clarity of the questionnaire, a pilot study was conducted on 20 students of both sexes from different age groups, majors, and academic levels. Due to lockdown restrictions, the students were invited to participate in a brief phone conversation to ensure that their concerns and recommendations for improving the clarity of the questionnaire were adequately addressed. The questionnaire was edited and revised by the research team before being available online for the students. The informed consent was obtained from the study participants. The participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time, and withdrawals were not registered. A sample of 211 responses was obtained; 6 students responded twice and the second response for these students was excluded, and the final sample consisted of 205 participants.

2.2. Anthropometric data and weight change

The students reported their height (cm) and current body weight (kg), which were used to calculate the students’ current body mass index (BMI) using Quetelet index: BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m2)[25] and the BMI was classified according to the WHO criteria to underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (≥25.0 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2) categories.[26] The students were asked to report body weight change during the previous month, and the usual BMI which was considered as the baseline was calculated accordingly.

2.3. Dietary data and lifestyle data

Questions about students’ dietary behavior, such as meal patterns, food intake, eating behavior, and changes in dietary behavior during the curfew/lockdown were developed based on a taxonomy of diet and dietary behavior[27] and included in the questionnaire. Food was classified as unhealthy when it is high in calories, added sugars, sodium, and/or harmful fats including saturated and trans fats.[28]

The questionnaire also contained questions about lifestyle behaviors, including PA, ST, smoking behavior, and sleep patterns. The daily activities, including moderate and high-level activities, were assessed. The questionnaire included questions related to changes in PA during the curfew/lockdown and the reasons associated with these changes. The students reported their ST, and their eating habits while using screens, and the changes in ST during the curfew/lockdown. The students answered questions related to cigarette and hookah smoking, the changes in smoking behavior during the curfew/lockdown, and the factors associated with these changes. The students reported their sleeping hours and any changes in sleeping hours during the curfew.

2.4. Stress and perceived stress

PS was assessed using the Perceived Stress Scale-4 (PSS-4), a brief version of the original PSS. The PSS-4 measured the level of stress individuals experienced over the past month. PSS-4 is considered a helpful instrument for measuring PS in situations in which a short scale is preferred.[29] PSS-4 consists of 4 items, 2 positive and 2 negative items, with a five-point Likert scale. The negative items are usually scored as 0 = never, 1 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = fairly often, and 4 = very often, while the positive items are scored in the reverse as 4 = never, 3 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 1 = fairly often, and 0 = very often. The total PSS-4 from the negative and positive scores ranged from 0 to 16, with higher scores indicating a higher PS. The internal consistency of the scale was 0.59, as measured by Cronbach alpha, and the inter-item correlation was 0.27. Additional questions were used by the research team to assess reported stress, stress triggers, stress coping strategies, and the effects of distance learning on stress.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences software (SPSS, version 20, Chicago, Inc.). Means and standard deviations (mean ± SD) were calculated to describe the continuous variables, while numbers and percentages (N (%)) were used to describe the categorical variables. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the stress between the 2 tested groups (males vs females) for continuous variables, and Chi-Square test was used to test the difference between males and females. The multinomial logistic regression was used to identify the association between stress and its causes and the changes in weight status, lifestyle behaviors, and dietary behaviors. The significance level was established at P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 205 undergraduate students from 3 universities in Jordan were recruited in this study; 81.5% were female, with a mean age of 20.7 (1.72) years (Table 1). A higher proportion had a medium family income (350–800 Jordanian dinar) (50.7%) and lived in a family home (90.7%). Students’ mothers (65.4%) and fathers (64.9%) had earned a 4-year college or higher degree. There were no significant differences between males and females regarding demographic characteristics, except for students’ employment and study major, whereas more males were employed than were females (21.1% vs 3.0%, P < .001), and more females were studying health sciences than males (67.7% vs 44.7%, P = .014).

3.2. Weight status changes during the curfew/lockdown

As presented in Table 2, more than a third of participants exhibited malnutrition before curfew, with 11.8% being underweight, 16.3% being overweight, and 6.9% being obese. The mean usual BMI before curfew which was considered as the baseline was lower for females to males (P = .032), with more females being underweight (13.3% vs 5.4%) and more males being overweight (29.7% vs 13.3%) or obese (10.8% vs 6.0%) (P = .037). During a month of curfew/lockdown, 10.8% of the students were underweight, 16.7% were overweight, and 8.9% were obese. The weight status, including the current mean BMI during the study, and BMI categories, did not differ significantly between male and female students.

3.3. The students’ dietary behavior and the changes during the curfew/lockdown

The changes in meal pattern and food intake during the curfew/lockdown are presented in Table 3. Mealtime commitment changed among 76.1% of the students, with the majority having a decreased commitment (75.6%). The students exhibited changes in daily meals (66.8%), the majority of which were an increase in the number of daily meals (59.9%). A daily food intake that either increased (60.5%) or decreased (39.5%) was reported by the majority of the students (71.7%). Reasons for increased healthy food intake by some students (38.0%) were to maintain health and boost the immune system (58.0%), fear of COVID-19 infection (17.3%), or the limited availability of fast food (42.0%). On the other hand, 45.9% of students had increased intake of unhealthy food due to the long-lasting curfew (35.1%), higher availability of unhealthy food (12.7%), or personal preferences regarding taste and other convenience factors (12.7%). The changes in meal pattern and food intake during the curfew/lockdown did not differ significantly between male and female students.

3.4. Students’ physical activity and screen time and the changes during the curfew/lockdown

Students were considered to meet PA requirements if they performed 150 minutes per week of moderate-intensity PA, 75 minutes per week of vigorous-intensity PA, or an equivalent combination of both.[30] About two-thirds of the students did not meet the PA requirements during the study (Table 4). The majority of the students (80.0%) reported changes in PA behavior due to curfew/lockdown conditions; 81.1% reported decreased PA, and 18.9% reported increased PA. However, the differences were not statistically significant between males and females (P = .070, 0.792, and 0.787, respectively). Although there were no significant differences between males and females, increased leisure time (45.2%) was considered the most reported reason for increased PA, while laziness and sleeping (51.9%) were the main reasons for decreased PA.

While there are no current recommendations for maximum daily ST for adults, studies have referenced the guidelines of no more than 2 hours per day for children and adolescents.[31,32] Until guidelines are developed for adults,[31] the guideline can still be useful given its documented associations with increased risk of obesity and CVD,[32,33] and poor mental health in adults.[34] Most students in our sample exceeded the ST recommendations (93.1%): 86.2% reported changes in screen use during the curfew, 94.4% of whom showed an increased ST. Male and female students exhibited similar ST behavior, and changes in ST during the curfew/lockdown were also similar among both males and females. Three-quarters of the students consumed snacks during ST, primarily salted snacks (71.2%) and desserts (67.3%) with no significant differences between males and females (66.7% vs 72.4%, P = .653 and 56.7% vs 69.9%, P = .195, respectively) except for fruit and vegetable consumption. 3.5. Students’ smoking behavior and changes during the curfew/lockdown

About one-quarter (21.0%) of our participants were smokers, with more males being smokers than females (47.4% vs 25.0%, P < .001) (Table 5). Cigarette smoking behavior changed among 72.2% of the students, with 61.5% reporting an increase in their cigarette smoking. Hookah smoking behavior changed in 80.0% of the students, with 58.3% increasing their smoking during the curfew/lockdown. However, the changes in cigarette and hookah smoking behavior were similar between males and females (64.3% vs 100%, P = .278, and 71.4% vs 82.6%, P = .603, respectively). The major reasons reported for increases in smoking behavior were primarily boredom and increased leisure time (87.5%), while decreases were due to reduced availability (40.0%), fear of COVID-19 infection (40.0%), or a desire to maintain immune system health (40.0%) (Table 5).

3.6. Students’ sleeping duration and changes during the curfew/lockdown

The reported sleeping hours were categorized as below, meeting, and above the recommended number of hours according to the National Sleep Foundation.[35] As presented in Table 5, the current study showed that 19.5% of the students, both females and males, slept fewer hours than recommended, and 43.9% had more sleeping hours than recommended during the curfew/lockdown; however, there were no significant differences between males and females (26.3% vs 18.0%, and 28.9% vs 47.3%, P = .115, respectively). Changes in the sleeping patterns were found among the majority of the students (85.4%), with 77.7% having increased sleeping hours and 22.3% having decreased sleeping hours. The distribution of meeting sleeping hour recommendations before the curfew differed significantly compared to its distribution during the curfew/lockdown in the whole population (P < .001) and in females (P < .001), but not in males (P = .179) (Data Not Shown). About two-thirds of the students were meeting the recommended sleeping hours before the curfew/lockdown, and only a third met the recommendation during the curfew/lockdown. More students had less than the recommended sleeping hours before the curfew (23.4% vs 19.5%), while more students had more than the recommended sleeping hours during the curfew (43.9% vs 9.3%) without sex differences in sleeping hours before the curfew/lockdown (26.3% vs 22.8%, and 2.6% vs 10.8%, P = .0.289, respectively).

3.7. Stress, perceived stress, and coping strategies during the curfew/lockdown

Table 6 shows the mean PSS-4 of the participants (7.5 (3.12)), with no significant difference between males and females (6.87 (2.86) vs 7.65 (3.16), P = .165). The study found that 79.0% of the students with stress experience had a significantly higher mean score on the PSS-4 compared to those who did not report any stressors in their lives (8.0 (2.96) vs 5.67 (3.05), P < .001) (data not shown). The most frequently reported stressors were academic pressures (80.9%), followed by coronavirus/curfew conditions (49.4%) (Table 6). The mean PSS-4 did not differ significantly between the individuals who ascribed their stress to the presence of COVID-19, curfew/lockdown conditions, and other stressors (8.2 (2.89) vs 7.8 (3.02), P = .459) (data not shown). Distance learning during curfew/lockdown was reported as a source of stress by 78.5% of the students, and reports were significantly higher in females than males (81.4% vs 65.8%, P = .047). Males were more likely to smoke (42.1% vs 3.0%, P < .001) or exercise (23.7% vs 8.5%, P = .019) while females were more likely to sleep (56.1% vs 34.2%, P = .019) during stressful events (Table 6).

3.8. The associations using regression analysis

The different stress categories, including curfew- and coronavirus-related stress, other sources of stress, nonstressed individuals, and their association with PS, distant learning, and weight changes are presented in Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/N762. 4. Discussion

Human behaviors change in response to an event or a trigger.[36] The pandemic’s emergence and its consequences prompted an alteration in dietary and lifestyle behaviors, such as increased health-risk behaviors among adults and youth, including college students.[37] Previous studies underlined some health-risk behaviors in college students before the pandemic,[38] and their increased prevalence during and after the COVID-19 pandemic was noticed.[39] As a result of the pandemic and stay-at-home orders, adults may have exhibited anxiety, depression, and stress eating.[40] Several behaviors emerged during the lockdown, such as increased food intake during and between meals, increased snacking and junk food intake, night eating, and binge eating, that might contribute to WG.[41] The increased prevalence of health-risk behaviors during the pandemic might be associated with an increased risk of noncommunicable diseases among college students.[42]

Our data showed changes in the prevalence of malnutrition. The measurements based on self-reported weight and height correlated significantly with measured parameters in young adults.[43] The results show a decreased prevalence of underweight and an increased prevalence of overweight and obesity. In line with the present findings, in Italy, the alteration in PA and unhealthy nutritional behaviors were associated with WG and increased BMI during the lockdown among obese individuals.[44] Similarly, 33.6% of Polish women gained weight in response to these alterations, 18.1% reduced their body weight, and 48.3% maintained their weight, which was a higher percentage than those in this study who maintained their weight (n = 52, 25.4%).[45] About half of those in our study who gained weight gained 1 or more BMI units within 1 month of the curfew/lockdown, as in agreement with a new cohort study in England that showed that each BMI unit increment might be associated with increased COVID-19 severity. Increasing BMI was a greater risk factor for increased COVID-19 severity in young adults (20–39 years) than in older adults.[46]

The present findings showed that more students reported an increased number of meals or portion sizes, reflected an overall rise in food intake, compared to those who reported a decrease. The current results are consistent with a study from India in which 75% of the participants reported that the number of main meals and snacks and the portion size of meals and snacks were either increased or similar to their intake preceding the pandemic.[47] Greater food intake during the pandemic might be associated with WG among adults under these conditions.[45]

Although more than one-third of the study participants had increased attention to healthy food choices, a higher percentage of participants reported increased unhealthy food intake. The results agreed with adults’ behavior change found in Scotland, in which 40.9% of the participants had a less healthy diet, whereas 25.1% had a more healthy diet.[48] In comparison, more adults in Poland improved their diet during the confinement than tended toward a less healthy diet (27.6% vs 19.4%).[49] We observed that maintaining health and boosting immunity were the main reasons for following a healthy diet. Previous studies showed that college students paid attention to a balanced diet and avoided eating outside as a strategy to prevent COVID-19 infection.[50] About half of the participants increased their intake of immunity-boosting foods in India and increased their attention to healthy eating in response to COVID-19 presence.[47] However, our study showed that a long-lasting curfew was a reason for increased unhealthy food intake for some students. A study in Spain showed that health-risk behaviors, including the low consumption of fruits and vegetables, decreased within the first 3 weeks of the lockdown.[51]

We observed that more students increased their fruit and vegetable intake during the lockdown. In contrast, college students reduced their fruit and vegetable intake during the quarantine, as seen in Canada.[39] Also, 17.6% of adults in Brazil had low produce consumption during the quarantine.[52] In Poland, more participants reported a decreased vegetable (19.4% vs 18.5%) and fruit (20.1% vs 15.2%) intake than those having an increased consumption; however, the highest proportion did not have changes in their vegetable (62.1%) and fruit (64.7%) intake.[49]

The students’ intake of desserts and salted snacks changed during the curfew/lockdown, with a higher percentage of increased consumption. Our results are in accordance with adults’ intake in Poland, as more participants had an increased intake of salted snacks (19.7% vs 18.1%) and confectionery (18.8% vs 32.5%) than those having a decreased intake. In comparison, 36.6% of the participants showed decreased fast-food intake compared to 8.1% who increased consumption. In France, 28.4% of the participants increased their caloric/salty food intake, with 3.5% of the participants losing control over their intake.[53] The students’ intake changes in the current study presented an increase in caffeinated drinks but a decrease in soft drinks and energy drinks. In agreement with our results, more adults in Poland declared having a decreased intake of sweetened beverages (8.4% vs 5.7%) and energy drinks (5.0% vs 1.6%) compared to those reporting an increased intake.[49]

The students’ PA changes in our study were predominantly toward a decreased PA level. The results were consistent with a survey among Canadian college students, as more students failed to meet the PA guidelines with the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic.[39] In another study, about half of Scottish adults also exhibited lower PA due to pandemic-related measures.[48] On the contrary, during the lockdown, the PA of college students in Spain was higher than that in the pre-lockdown period, and the PA increase was significant and more evident in female students.[54] For this reason, public health strategies should involve the development and implementation of initiatives that promote safe physical exercise and discourage sedentary behavior, should future lockdowns be necessary.

The majority of our participants indicated an increase in ST, as was the case among college students in Spain[54] and Canada.[39] About half of the participants in a French study declared that they had an increase in ST during the pandemic, and 15.4% of them could not control this increased use.[53] The increased use of screens during the pandemic might be associated with boredom, work, entertainment, or distance learning.[49]

Though a higher percentage of our participants reported increased smoking behavior during the lockdown/curfew, a considerable ratio also decreased their smoking behavior. In contrast, the closure of universities and the pandemic-related changes were associated with a reduced frequency of smoking among US students.[55] One-quarter of French adults reported increased tobacco use, with 8.9% demonstrating an uncontrolled increase, while 21.1% reduced or stopped smoking.[53] Smokers in our study referred to boredom and increased leisure time as the main reasons for increased smoking. Smoking cessation, either temporary or permanent, might be associated with living at home and anxiety, while increased smoking might be related to increased exposure to COVID-19-related news.[55]

The lockdown/confinement conditions were associated with more students not meeting sleep recommendations. The majority of students in the current study had a change in their sleeping hours, with more students reporting increased hours of sleep than those reporting a reduction. Similarly, about half of US college students had either an increase (27.5%) or a decrease (21.2%) in sleeping hours.[56] The increase in sleeping hours among college students might be associated with increased stress, anxiety, and bedtime screen use during the pandemic.[57]

Nearly half of our participants referred to the curfew condition as a source of stress in their lives. The emergence of the pandemic and its related measures might be associated with a higher level of PS, as seen among college students in Egypt, where the fear of COVID-19 infection was positively related to increased stress levels.[58] The measured stress was positively correlated with the reported stress among the students, with curfew and pandemic being the highest predictors of PS. Similarly, in India, 82.7% of the students had moderate stress, and 13.4% had a high stress level under lockdown conditions.[59]

Weight change in this study was predicted by having stressors other than curfew/pandemic. A study from Italy showed that reported anxiety and depression during the lockdown were associated with WG among obese adults.[44] The present study found that feeling stressed due to the pandemic and its related measures predicted a greater alteration in PA. Low levels of PS among US college students were associated with increased PA and sleep duration, while high levels were associated with an unhealthy lifestyle.[56] Unhealthy diet and lifestyle behaviors were also associated with more negative moods during the pandemic.[48] Hence, whether the changes in dietary and lifestyle behaviors were triggered by stress or were stress-causing factors during the pandemic needs more investigation.

The present study has several limitations, which include the self-reported questionnaire and small sample size that may limit the generalizability of the results. The use of an online survey system depends on voluntary sampling; thus, the results might have been distorted by selection or social desirability biases. Self-reported questionnaires might result in underestimation or overestimation of these changes, so the results of the current study should be treated with caution. However, our research is valuable in understanding college students’ responses under these stressful conditions and investigating the role of stress in stimulating these changes, especially among Jordanian students, where the increased prevalence of health-risk behaviors before the pandemic had previously been documented.[23]

In conclusion, the emergence of the COVID-19 pandemic and its related lockdown measures induced a change in college student behaviors. To address these changes, it is necessary to implement behavioral and nutritional interventions that educate the students, empower them to make healthier dietary and lifestyle choices, and help them adapt to changes. Additionally, educational institutions may increase mental health resources to promote the students’ coping strategies with stressors in the future. Moreover, the challenges in controlling the spread of the virus around the world point to the possibility of future lockdown periods as well as the importance of investigating how public health restrictions affect general population behavior and urges for the need of comprehensive strategies that address the health and well-being of individuals. Therefore, developing suitable dietary and lifestyle guidelines is essential for creating effective interventions that mitigate the adverse effects of stressors on student’s behaviors.[60]
