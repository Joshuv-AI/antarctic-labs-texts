Fatigue-resistant and tough double network granular elastomers
Authors: Eva Baur, John Kolinski, Esther Amstad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Elastomers can be engineered as stretchable, compliant materials capable of bearing load, making them attractive for soft robotics, wearable electronics, and biomedical devices. Unfortunately, single-network elastomers are constrained by a stiffness-toughness compromise. While multinetwork elastomers can mitigate this limitation, tough multinetwork elastomers typically exhibit a limited fatigue resistance. Here, we demonstrate that double-network granular elastomers (DNGEs) composed of stiff elastomer microparticles connected through a softer second network combine toughness and fatigue resistance. Their locally varying composition and structure enable DNGEs to efficiently deconcentrate stress and repetitively dissipate energy if stretched to moderate strains, imparting them a good fatigue resistance. Leveraging the three-dimensional printability of DNGEs, we spatially vary the composition to combine stiffness, toughness, and fatigue resistance within one elastomer. We anticipate that DNGEs will unlock opportunities in soft robotics, wearables, and biomedicine, where current soft materials remain limited by the stiffness-toughness-fatigue resistance trade-off.

INTRODUCTION

Elastomers can bear substantial load while exhibiting a high strain at break, making them ideal candidates for applications that require compliant yet robust materials, for example, in soft robotics, smart wearables, soft electronics, or biomedical devices (1–4). Elastomers are hydrophobic polymeric networks whose mechanical properties are primarily defined by their structure and cross-link density. In conventional elastomers, an increase in stiffness is paired with a reduced extensibility and a lower fracture toughness, which is limiting their use in many applications (5–10). The stiffness-toughness trade-off can, at least in part, be addressed if elastomers are formulated as double-network (DN) elastomers where the cross-link density in the first network is higher than in the second network. The first network imparts stiffness to DNs and serves as sacrificial bonds that dissipate energy, thereby increasing the fracture toughness of the DN. The second network imparts DNs stretchability and ensures good shape retention (11–13). Yet, DN elastomers suffer from a toughness-fatigue threshold trade-off. The fatigue threshold of a material is its ability to resist crack propagation when exposed to numerous loading-unloading cycles (14–18). The fatigue resistance of elastomers is enhanced when localized stress is distributed over a larger area, such that less irreversible damage occurs (19). Because DN elastomers dissipate energy by irreversibly breaking sacrificial bonds within the first network, their toughness comes at the expense of their fatigue resistance (20). The fatigue resistance of elastomers increases if the first network contains reversible links that can recover upon strain release. Yet, such systems exhibit lower stiffnesses due to the weaker strength of dynamic and reversible bonds compared with stable covalent ones (21). The stiffness and toughness can be simultaneously increased if elastomers are functionalized with appropriate fillers, such as carbon black or silica nanoparticles (14, 22–25). However, these additives do not contribute to the fatigue resistance of the composite unless strongly bound to the elastomer matrix and can lead to stress softening (26–28). Moreover, many fillers compromise the transparency of the materials, make the processing of the composites more tedious, and add safety concerns. Hydrogels, which are swollen polymer networks that encompass a large fraction of water, can be reinforced with hydrogel microparticles, so-called microgels (29–32). These nearly transparent particles not only mechanically reinforce hydrogels but also facilitate their processing. Elastomers do not contain any solvent such that, in the undeformed state, their chains are typically much less extended than chains present in hydrogels, and, hence, they experience more viscoelastic dissipation through chain friction (24, 33–35). Because of this compositional and structural difference, mechanical properties of hydrogels differ markedly from those of elastomers (36, 37). Despite this important difference in polymer structure, we translated the concept of microparticle-reinforced hydrogels to elastomers by formulating DN granular elastomers (DNGEs). DNGEs are composed of jammed elastomer microparticles, which are interconnected by a percolating second elastomer network (38). If appropriately designed, DNGEs can combine stiffness, a high work of fracture, and three-dimensional (3D) printability, in stark contrast to bulk DN elastomers. However, how the microstructure of DNGEs affects their fatigue behavior remains to be investigated.

Here, we demonstrate that DNGEs combine stiffness, toughness, and fatigue resistance. Optimized DNGEs exhibit a fatigue threshold up to 72 J m−2, a fracture toughness up to 13500 J m−2, and a Young’s modulus up to 1.1 MPa even if prepared under ambient conditions in the presence of oxygen. The fatigue threshold of DNGEs is 3-fold higher than that of bulk DN counterparts and 15-fold higher than that of single-network (SN) elastomers. Similarly, the fracture toughness of DNGEs is 15-fold higher than that of DN counterparts and SN elastomers. We assign the enhanced fatigue resistance and toughness of DNGEs to their granular architecture, which allows stress to be redistributed more effectively, with localized damage in microparticles and repetitive energy dissipation through chain sliding and rearrangement in the interstitial spaces. The granular structure adds another benefit: Because of the differences in stiffness within the microparticles and the interstitial spaces, cracks primarily propagate in the interstitial spaces. Propagating cracks, therefore, attain a serpentine-like shape, prolonging their critical length where catastrophic fracture occurs, which further increases the toughness and fatigue resistance of the material (22, 26, 39, 40). The enhanced resistance to crack propagation of DNGEs under monotonic and cyclic loading conditions, combined with their ease of processing, offers opportunities for their use in structures that are subjected to dynamic loadings, for example, in soft robotics, wearable electronics, and biomedical and protective applications (2, 3).

RESULTS

Synthesis and characterization of elastomeric systems

DNGEs are composed of jammed elastomer microparticles that are firmly connected by a softer second elastomer network. Rather stiff elastomer microparticles are made from oil-in-water emulsions. The oil phase is based on ethyl acetate containing butyl acrylate (BA) used as a monomer, 1,4-butanediol diacrylate (BDA) as a cross-linker, and 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropiophenone (HMP) as a photoinitiator; the chemical structures of the monomer and cross-linker are shown in Fig. 1C. BA is used as monomer due to its lower volatility compared with lower–molecular weight acrylates such as ethyl acrylate. The reduced volatility of BA decreases the polymerization-induced shrinking below the threshold that we can quantify, minimizing the polymerization-induced stress within these materials and increasing their shape fidelity. The aqueous phase contains poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) used as a surfactant. The two liquids are mixed at an oil:water volume ratio of 1:3 before being vortexed for emulsification. The emulsions are converted into microparticles through an ultraviolet (UV)–initiated free radical polymerization of the reagents contained in the drops. The resulting elastomer microparticles are washed to remove unreacted reagents and surfactants before being swollen in excess precursor solution for the second network composed of BA, BDA, and photoinitiator, as shown in Fig. 1A and fig. S1. The reagent-loaded microparticles are jammed to form an ink that can be extruded or direct-ink written, as schematically shown in the top row in Fig. 1A. The granular structure is solidified by initiating the free radical polymerization reaction of the precursors for the second network through UV light exposure. The composition and structure of the resulting DNGEs vary locally: Within the microparticles, they are made of DNs, whereas their interstitial spaces are composed of an SN. To assess the influence of the microstructure of DNGEs on their mechanical properties, we fabricate bulk DNs with identical chemical composition but lacking any microstructure. The composition of the first network of bulk DNs is identical to that of the elastomer microparticles, whereas its second network composition is the same as that of the second network of DNGEs, as schematically shown in in Fig. 1B.

Microparticles are swollen in the precursor solution for the second network, jammed into an injectable ink and 3D printed. The resulting granular structure is rigidified by exposing it to UV light to initiate the formation of the second elastomer network that interpenetrates and covalently cross-links the microparticles. To assess the influence of the microparticles on the mechanical properties of elastomers, we prepare microparticle-free SN elastomers with compositions corresponding to those of the second networks of DNGEs, as illustrated in Fig. 1D. To avoid oxygen-induced inhibition of the free radical polymerization reaction, the samples are all prepared in a nitrogen (N2) atmosphere in the glove box, unless stated otherwise. Note that, for DN and DNGE systems, the cross-linker concentration in the first network is always higher than that in the second network because the first network contains sacrificial bonds that must break before the second network is loaded to increase the toughness of the system, as summarized in table S1 (11, 32, 41–44).

The strain at break of DNGEs depends on the covalent cross-linking density of the second network (32, 38, 45, 46). To maximize the strain at break of DNGEs, we minimize the cross-linker concentration of the second network and fix it at 0.05 mol %. Because we aim at increasing the toughness and fatigue resistance of our materials, we produce microparticles that swell in the precursor solution of the second network. The diameter of microparticles containing 1.5 mol % cross-linker increases by ~4.5-fold if dispersed in the precursor solution for the second network. Microparticles with higher cross-linker concentrations swell less, such that they take up less precursors for the second network, as summarized in fig. S1. To obtain a high weight fraction of the second network within DNGEs, we therefore fix the cross-linker concentration within the first network for DNs and DNGEs to 1.5 mol %, unless stated otherwise. Upon polymerization of the second network, the microparticles shrink by 5%, as shown in fig. S1.

To quantify the fraction of interstitial spaces in DNGEs, microparticles are fluorescently labeled and imaged by confocal microscopy. DNGEs composed of microparticles containing 1.5 mol % cross-linker contain an interstitial volume of 52%, as shown in fig. S1. The interstitial volume decreases to 40% if DNGEs contain stiffer microparticles containing 5 mol % cross-linker, as shown in fig. S1.

Influence of the microstructure on the stiffness and work of fracture of DN elastomers

Bulk DN elastomers can combine high stiffness with a high toughness if appropriately formulated (11, 12). To assess whether this is also the case for DNGEs, we perform tensile tests on them. The Young’s modulus of SNs is 0.17 MPa, whereas it increases to 0.46 MPa if a first network is added to SNs to transform them into DNs, as shown in Fig. 2C. The increase in Young’s modulus is much less pronounced if the first network is formulated as microparticles, resulting in DNGEs: The incorporation of soft microparticles into SNs increases it slightly to 0.23 MPa, as shown in Fig. 2C. We assign the less pronounced stiffening observed in DNGEs compared with that seen in DNs to the discontinuous nature of the first network in DNGEs (38). Yet, the discontinuous nature of the first network imparts DNGEs a higher strain at break than conventional DNs have, as shown in Fig. 2B. Consequently, we expect DNGEs to exhibit a higher work of fracture than DNs. To assess this expectation, we quantify the work of fracture as the area under the stress-strain curve until rupture. The addition of microparticles to SNs increases their work of fracture fourfold to 2.9 MJ m−3, as shown in Fig. 2D. DNs exhibit a much lower work of fracture of 0.3 MJ m−3 than DNGEs. Note that the work of fracture of these DNs is even lower than that of SN counterparts. These results suggest that the high work of fracture of DNGEs is mainly due to their interstitial spaces exclusively composed of the second network. They hint at an efficient stress transfer between the first and second networks within DNGEs: If DNGEs are stretched up to moderate strains, the load that is mainly carried by the stiffer first network present within the microparticles is transferred to the second network located within the interstitial spaces. Because the second network is much softer than the first counterpart, we expect it to efficiently dissipate energy primarily through chain slippage and rearrangement, enabled through the presence of entanglements within the interstitial spaces. These dissipative processes are repeatable under cyclic loading and occur without substantial irreversible damage. This behavior is in stark contrast to that of DNs in which a large fraction of the energy dissipated already at low strains arises from irreversible covalent bond breakage localized within the stiff first network, acting as sacrificial bonds (20, 27, 47). Note that, if DNGEs are subjected to higher strains, bond scissions also occur in these systems mainly within the much stiffer microparticles, eventually leading to the failure of the entire DNGE.

(A) Composition of the different elastomer systems: SNs (top, red triangles), DNs (middle, blue circles), and DNGEs (bottom, green squares if cured in air in the presence of O2 or orange squares if cured in nitrogen). The second network contains 0.05 mol % cross-linker. The first network of DNs and DNGEs contains 1.5 mol % cross-linker. (B) Stress-strain curves for SNs, DNs, and DNGEs. (C) Young’s modulus (symbols) and work of fracture (bars) extracted from the measurements in (B).

Our results hint at the importance of the soft interstitial spaces for the work of fracture of DNGEs. The strain at break of elastomers depends on the network quality: Elastomers with a higher density of dangling bonds typically exhibit a higher strain at break (5, 8). To further increase the extensibility of DNGEs, we produce them in air. We expect oxygen to terminate the free radical polymerization of some of the precursors, thereby increasing the density of dangling bonds. DNGEs produced in air exhibit a strain at break up to 1335%, as shown in Fig. 2 and detailed in fig. S2. However, they show a reduced work of fracture of 1.6 MJ m−3 as more network defects are present. By contrast, DNs fabricated in air are too brittle to perform reliable tensile tests on them, such that we fabricate DNs and SNs in the glove box to minimize oxygen-induced termination of free radical polymerizations. These results illustrate that DNGEs are much less sensitive to air than DNs. We attribute this finding to the drop-induced confinement of the free radical polymerization of the first network of DNGEs, which improves network homogeneity even under ambient conditions. Nevertheless, for consistent comparisons of mechanical properties with SN and DN systems, DNGEs are produced in the glove box.

Our results hint at efficient and repeatable energy dissipation mechanisms within interstitial spaces. Elastomers can dissipate energy through viscoelastic mechanisms such as chain rearrangement and slippage if they are operated above their glass transition temperature, Tg (37). To assess whether our formulations are in the rubbery regime, we quantify their glass transition temperature with differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). All our formulations exhibit similar glass transition temperatures (Tg’s) around −50°C, as shown in fig. S3. Hence, at room temperature, they are in the rubbery regime and can dissipate energy through viscoelastic mechanisms.

DNGEs are viscoelastic such that their mechanical properties are temperature and strain rate dependent. To assess the influence of strain rate on the mechanical response of DNGEs, we vary the strain rate from 10 to 200 mm min−1. At strain rates below 50 mm min−1, the Young’s modulus and dissipated energy increase with strain rate. However, these properties plateau at strain rates exceeding 50 mm min−1, as shown in fig. S4. Based on these results, we use a strain rate of 100 mm min−1 for the remaining experiments to minimize strain rate-dependent effects.

Influence of the microstructure on the fracture toughness of DN elastomers

Our results suggest that the locally varying composition of DNGEs increases their work of fracture. The work of fracture is defined as the energy required to mechanically deform a unit volume of a virgin sample, calculated as the area below a tensile stress-strain curve. It is often used to approximate the toughness of a material, although differences between these two properties can emerge because of defects and the formation and propagation of cracks (6). The fracture toughness of a material is its resistance to crack propagation, measured as the critical strain energy release rate required to extend a crack by a unit area until failure (48–50). To assess whether DNGEs are tougher than DNs, we perform pure-shear tensile tests on virgin and notched samples, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 3A (7, 8).

Fracture toughness of SN, DN, and DNGE systems.

(A) Schematic illustration of the test used to measure the fracture toughness. The area under the stress-strain curve of the virgin (unnotched) sample, up to the strain at break of the corresponding notched samples, is integrated to calculate the fracture toughness. (B) Fracture toughness for SNs (red), DNs (blue), and DNGEs (orange) with their first network containing 1.5 mol % cross-linker and their second network containing 0.05 mol % cross-linker.

To quantify the fracture toughness Γ, the width-to-height ratio must be sufficiently large to create a pure shear region (14, 49). To ensure that the measured result is independent of the initial crack length c, the crack must exceed a threshold value c>h/2, as schematically shown in Fig. 3A (37). To fulfill this requirement and minimize the influence of strained regions between the notch and the grips, the precut was set to half the width of the sample, c=w/2, with w∼8h. From the measured stress-strain curves, we quantify the critical stretch εc of the precut samples where the crack starts to propagate. We use this value to calculate the energy density of the virgin sample as the area under the stress-strain curve between 0 and εc, as illustrated in Fig. 3A.

The fracture toughness Γ is the product of the initial distance between the clamps h and the energy density of the virgin sample. The fracture toughness of our SN elastomer is 460 J m−2, as shown in Fig. 3B. This value is in good agreement with fracture toughness values reported for other SN elastomers (8, 9, 14, 51). The fracture toughness increases by 200% for DNs, a value that is within the range of reported fracture toughness of DN elastomers (11–13, 20). We attribute the measured increase in Γ of DNs compared with that of SNs to the introduction of sacrificial bonds present within the first network and the higher cross-link density of the first network, which spatially delocalizes damage, resulting in a more diffuse damage zone (6, 41, 48, 52). DNGEs exhibit an almost 15-fold higher fracture toughness than DN counterparts, as shown in Fig. 3B. This result corroborates our previous results confirming that DNGEs can more efficiently dissipate energy than DNs. As a result of the efficient energy dissipation, these DNGEs undergo ductile fracture, as shown in Fig. 2B and visualized in fig. S5.

We assign the high fracture toughness of DNGEs to their microstructure: DNGEs are composed of DNs within the microparticles and a soft SN within the interstitial spaces. If sufficiently strained, sacrificial covalent bond scission occurs within the microparticles, as evidenced by the fractured microparticles observed after crack propagation in fig. S5. This energy dissipation mechanism is similar to that occurring in bulk DNs. In bulk DNs, this toughening effect is more pronounced if the difference in stiffnesses between the first and second networks is higher (4, 11, 17, 41, 42, 53). To assess whether this is also the case for DNGEs, we fabricate DNGEs with stiffer microparticles containing 5 mol % cross-linker. The fracture toughness of these DNGEs is threefold lower than that of DNGEs with softer microparticles as shown in fig. S6. We assign this result to the reduced degree of swelling of stiffer microparticles, which limits the amount of precursors for the second network that can be stored in them. As a result, the volume of interstitial spaces of DNGEs decreases with increasing microparticle stiffness, leading to an embrittlement, as shown in fig. S1.

Our results suggest that energy dissipation and failure mechanisms of DNGEs are distinct from these of DNs. The granular nature of DNGEs enables the stress to be transferred to the second network already at low strains where the first network remains intact. Within the much softer interstitial spaces, elastomer chains and entanglements have a higher mobility than in the much stiffer microparticles. The enhanced chain and entanglement mobility within interstitial spaces of DNGEs enables repeatable energy dissipation for example through chain rearrangement, entanglement sliding, and microparticle rearrangement. Moreover, because the interstitial spaces are considerably softer than the microparticles, cracks preferentially propagate within them, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the fracture surfaces shown in fig. S5 when compared to SEM images of pristine DNGEs shown in fig. S1. The associated crack deviation caused by the microparticles promotes crack tip blunting and branching and prolongs the critical crack size before catastrophic failure occurs, as has previously been observed in filler-reinforced elastomers (39, 40, 49, 54). To further test this hypothesis, we produce DNGEs with stiffer second networks, containing 0.3 mol % cross-linker. These DNGEs exhibit a much lower fracture toughness of 352 J m−2, as shown in fig. S6. We assign this reduction in fracture toughness to a reduced mobility of chains within the interstitial spaces that limit the energy that can be repetitively dissipated. Note that the fracture toughness decreases even more, to 285 J m−2, if DNGEs are made of stiff microparticles connected through a stiff second network, as shown in fig. S6. We assign this decrease in fracture toughness to a reduction in interstitial volume of these samples as shown in fig. S1. These DNGEs even exhibit a brittle fracture in stark contrast to all other tested DNGEs, as visualized in fig. S5. These results indicate that DNGEs only exhibit a high fracture toughness if the volume fraction of the interstitial space is sufficiently high and the elastomer contained in it is soft. Together, our results suggest that sacrificial bond scission within the microparticles, viscoelastic dissipation within the interstitial spaces, and crack deviation act synergistically to enhance the fracture toughness of optimized DNGEs.

Fatigue of DNGEs

Most elastomers operate under cyclic loading conditions making fatigue resistance critical for their performance. The fatigue resistance is defined as the resistance to crack propagation under cyclic load, whereas the fracture toughness is the resistance against crack propagation under monotonic load. Hence, fatigue threshold and toughness differ by orders of magnitude. Most polymeric systems, including SN and DN elastomers, suffer from a stiffness-fatigue resistance trade-off: Stiffness is inversely proportional to the chain length between cross-links n, whereas fatigue resistance scales with the square root of n (7, 19, 48). Fillers can increase the stiffness of elastomers (39, 55, 56). Yet, if not strongly connected to the elastomer, they typically do not improve the fatigue resistance (16, 19, 26). Unlike conventional filler-reinforced elastomers where weak interfaces guide crack propagation, the DNGEs exhibit strong microparticle-second elastomer network interfaces because the second network interpenetrates the microparticles such that we expect these systems to be more fatigue resistant than SN or DN elastomers. To test our expectation, we quantify the fatigue threshold of DNGEs. We subject notched samples to cyclic tensile loading and compare them to fatigue thresholds of corresponding DNs and SNs, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 4A.

The crack is monitored with a camera to extract the crack growth rate per cycle for each G. Here, we define the fatigue threshold as the stress intensity below which the crack growth rate is below 1 Å/cycle. (B) Crack growth per cycle as a function of applied stress intensity for SNs (red triangle), DNs (blue circle), and DNGEs (orange square) with their first network containing 1.5 mol % cross-linker and their second network containing 0.05 mol % cross-linker. Measurements with crack growth rates below the resolution limit of the imaging setup, 1 μm/cycle, are excluded from the regression line. The fatigue threshold is defined as the energy release rate below which a crack does not propagate under cyclic loading (15, 16). It is quantified by monitoring the crack growth as a function of the applied strain. The corresponding strain-dependent energy release rates are measured from virgin not notched samples subjected to the same cyclic tensile loading conditions by multiplying the initial height of the sample by the area below the strain-stress curve. The area below the stress-strain curve is taken after 500 cycles when the sample has entered the stabilized regime. A crack-containing sample is cyclically loaded up to the same strain. We monitor the growth rate and plot it as a function of the applied energy release rate, as illustrated in Fig. 4A and shown in Fig. 4B. Our setup does not allow monitoring crack growth rates below 1 μm/cycle. We therefore linearly extrapolate the measured values to a critical growth rate of 1 Å/cycle, which is taken as a fatigue threshold (10, 20, 57) using the Paris law (10, 20, 57). While deviations from Paris-law behavior may occur near the threshold regime (23), this approach provides a consistent basis for comparing fatigue resistance (8, 16, 20, 58–60). The values for SNs are slightly lower than the range of reported values for acrylate-based elastomers (5, 20, 47, 61). This deviation can be explained with the buckling that we observe during strain-controlled cyclic loading; previous studies observed that buckling leads to underestimation of the fatigue threshold (60). By contrast, the fatigue threshold values of DNs are higher than previously reported values for unfilled rubbers and elastomers, as summarized in Fig. 4. The fatigue threshold of DNGEs is threefold higher than that of DNs with the same overall composition, as shown in Fig. 4. This result suggests that the granular structure enhances fatigue resistance.

The interstitial spaces of DNGEs are exclusively composed of the second network, a feature that is distinct from DNs. Our results hint at the importance of interstitial spaces for the fatigue resistance of DNGEs. To characterize the fatigue behavior of the interstitial spaces of DNGEs, we quantify the fatigue threshold of SNs of the same composition as the interstitial spaces of DNGEs to be 4.5 J m−2, this value is one order of magnitude lower than previously reported values for acrylate elastomers. We assign at least part of this discrepancy to the underestimation of the fatigue threshold that arises from buckling (9, 25). The fatigue threshold increases fivefold if a first network is introduced, resulting in DNs. We attribute the increase in fatigue threshold to an enhanced sacrificial bond scission that primarily occurs within the first network and stress delocalization, similar to what we and others observed for monotonic loadings (20, 27). The fatigue threshold increases much more, to 65.3 J m−2, if elastomers are formulated as DNGEs, as shown in Fig. 4 (B and C). According to the Lake-Thomas model, the fatigue threshold is governed by the elastic energy required to break polymer chains at the crack tip and scales with the length of chains between cross-links (10, 16, 25, 62). This model assumes an ideal network with no interchain friction and, therefore, provides a lower-bound estimate. In DNGEs, the interstitial spaces consist solely of the second network, which contains longer strands between cross-links than those present within microparticles and DNs. Within the Lake-Thomas framework, these longer polymer strands between cross-links can contribute to the higher fatigue threshold of DNGEs compared with DNs.

The enhanced fatigue resistance of DN and DNGE compared with that of SN systems cannot be explained by the Lake-Thomas model. The higher fatigue resistance of DNs relative to SNs has been attributed to stress delocalization enabled by the stiff first network (19, 20, 27). Similarly, in DNGEs, stiff microparticles can redistribute stress near the crack tip. Comparable mechanisms have been reported in phase-separated polymers, where the hard phase promotes stress deconcentration resulting in higher fatigue resistance (63). In DNGEs, stress is redistributed into the softer interstitial spaces, as visually observed with the crack that propagates within the interstitial spaces in fig. S5. In the interstitial spaces, the SN elastomer accommodates deformation through chains slippage and rearrangement. Such multiscale stress delocalization has also been reported for multimodal networks and elastomers containing strongly-bonded fillers, where fillers enhanced fatigue resistance beyond Lake-Thomas predictions (26, 64). Note that, in DNs, damage delocalization occurs mainly through the first network. If subjected to multiple loading cycles, stress will accumulate within the first network, eventually leading to its irreversible failure. Our results suggest that the enhanced fatigue resistance of DNGEs compared with that of DNs originates from multiscale stress delocalization supported by efficient dissipative mechanisms within the interstitial regions that can be repeatedly activated over many loading cycles.

Energy dissipated during cyclic loading

To verify that the higher fatigue threshold measured for DNGEs compared with DNs translates into a higher resistance to failure during cyclic loadings, we strain samples to 50% for up to 500 cycles and record the corresponding stress-strain curves, as illustrated in Fig. 5A. SN elastomers dissipate 10 kJ m−3, as shown in Fig. 5B and detailed in fig. S7. The same sample only dissipates 4.5 kJ m−3 during the 500th loading and unloading cycle. The 50% decrease in dissipated energy between the first and 500th cycle indicates that a substantial fraction of the energy is dissipated through the irreversible breakage of covalent bonds, albeit reversible displacements of entanglements likely also contribute to the energy dissipation. This mechanism is supported by a continuous softening of SNs with increasing stress-strain cycles that they have been subjected to, as shown in Fig. 5C.

(C) Maximum stress attained during the first (solid bar), second (lightly dashed bar), and last (heavily dashed bar) cycles for SNs, DNs, and DNGEs. (D) Ashby plot of elastomer systems with their respective fracture toughness and fatigue thresholds. The other elastomer systems are elastomers reinforced with stiff inorganic particles (filled elastomers) (10, 23, 24), natural rubber (NR) (14, 25, 49, 62), butadiene rubber (BR) (65), poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) (65), and poly(dimethyl siloxane) (PDMS) (51).

Optimized DNs are tougher than SNs. Hence, DNs dissipate more energy during the first stress-strain cycle than SNs do, 13.1 kJ m−3, as shown in Fig. 5B. Yet, the energy dissipated during the second cycle is only 7 kJ m−3 and it decreases to 4.5 kJ m−3 after the 500th cycle, a value similar to that of SN counterparts. The strong and continuous decrease in dissipated energy with increasing strain cycles suggests that DNs dissipate most of the energy through irreversible breakage of covalent bonds, mainly localized within the first network, in agreement with what has previously been reported for DN elastomers (12, 13). This suggestion is supported by a strong softening of DNs with increasing stress-strain cycles, as shown in Fig. 5C.

DNGEs are softer than the corresponding DN, yet the energy dissipated during the first stress-strain cycle is with 12.7 kJ m−3 similar to that of the corresponding DN. By analogy to DNs, also in DNGEs, some covalent bonds are irreversibly broken during the first loading cycles, as indicated by the decrease in stress, shown in Fig. 5. However, the dissipated energy only decreases by 30% during the second cycle and remains nearly constant thereafter such that these DNGEs still dissipate 8.8 kJ m−3 per cycle after the 500th cycle. The substantially higher amount of energy dissipated by DNGEs during the 500th cycle compared with that dissipated by DNs further corroborates our previous results, suggesting that DNGEs dissipate energy predominantly through repeatable mechanisms such as chain sliding and rearrangement, rather than through irreversible bond scission. Such repeatable mechanisms reduce local stress magnification without damaging the load-bearing network, resulting in enhanced fatigue resistance of DNGEs compared with that of DNs. It should be noted that high energy dissipation under cyclic loading can increase the temperature in the material, which is undesirable in certain engineering applications. The experiments reported here are conducted at temperatures well above the glass transition temperature of the systems, such that the influence of moderate temperature rises on the mechanical response is expected to be limited.

To quantify the percentage of dissipated input energy, we measure the stress-strain hysteresis, defined as the ratio of the dissipated mechanical energy to the total mechanical work done to the material under cyclic load (8). Systems containing higher densities of entanglements display higher hysteresis, as shown in fig. S8, consistent with the observed slopes in Fig. 4.

The granular structure of DNGEs imparts them a combination of fracture toughness and fatigue resistance that could not be achieved by previously reported nonfilled elastomer-based formulations, as summarized in Fig. 5D (10, 14, 23–25, 49, 51, 62, 65). Note that the fatigue thresholds summarized in Fig. 5D have been quantified with different experimental methods, threshold criterions, and test conditions such that a direct comparison should be made with caution. Nevertheless, the much higher fracture toughness of our DNGEs compared with previously reported elastomer formulations, combined with the high fatigue threshold, hints at the potential of DNGEs for load-bearing applications that subject stretchable materials to dynamic loading conditions.

DNGE composite

Air curing of DNGEs enables their 3D printing into complex structures. To assess the influence of oxygen present during the free radical polymerization on the mechanical properties of the resulting DNGEs, we perform tensile tests on systems produced in air, as summarized in tables S2 and S3. The stiffness and strength of SNs decrease when produced in air, as shown in fig. S9. We assign the softening to an increased density of dangling bonds, which is supported by the larger rate dependence of the storage modulus of samples prepared in air compared with counterparts formed in an inert atmosphere, as shown in fig. S10 (5, 8). Similarly, DNGEs prepared in air are softer, with a Young’s modulus of 0.13 MPa compared with analogs prepared in the glove box, whose Young’s modulus is 0.23 MPa, due to a lower polymerization efficiency, as shown in fig. S2. DNGEs achieve similar fracture toughness of 13 kJ m−2 and fatigue threshold of 72 J m−2 as those produced in an inert atmosphere, as shown in figs. S6 and S11. Because of the facilitated processing of DNGEs in air and their attractive mechanical properties, we produce them in air for the remainder of this study.

Our results reveal that DNGEs can combine toughness with fatigue resistance albeit at the expense of their stiffness. To combine stiffness, toughness, and fatigue resistance, we leverage the granular nature of the DNGE precursor to direct-ink write it into DNGE-based composites with locally varying compositions. We vary the stiffness of DNGEs by varying cross-linker concentrations within the microparticles and the second network, as detailed in table S1. All our DNGE precursors are shear thinning and have a low yield stress, characterized as the stress where the storage G′ and loss G″ moduli are identical. Moreover, they quickly recover their solid-like properties upon removal of the strain, as indicated in fig. S12 and observed in Fig. 6B during 3D printing.

Multimaterial 3D printing of DNGEs.

(A) Schematic illustration of the printed DNGE composite made of stiff fibers composed of DNGEs with stiff microparticles (5 mol % BDA) embedded in a matrix of soft microparticles (1.5 mol % BDA) and a core-shell structure composed of DNGEs encompassing soft microparticles in the core and stiff counterparts in the shell. (B and C) Photograph of the 3D printing process of the (B) soft DNGE component of the fiber-based composite and the (C) hard DNGE of the core-shell structure. (D and E) Mechanical properties of DNGEs made exclusively from soft microparticles (light green), stiff microparticles (dark green), and the composite (light gray, soft matrix/hard fiber; and dark gray, soft core/hard shell): (D) Young’s modulus and fracture toughness and (E) fatigue threshold.

We increase the stiffness of DNGEs by fabricating microparticles containing 5 mol % cross-linker; these particles are referred to as stiff microparticles. The stiff microparticles are interconnected with a second network containing 0.05 mol %, resulting in a DNGE stiffness of 0.3 MPa, as summarized in fig. S2. These stiff DNGEs have a lower fracture toughness of 4.2 kJ m−2 and fatigue threshold of 22.2 J m−2.

To combine stiffness and fatigue resistance, we 3D print samples comprising 40 vol % fibers made of stiff DNGEs. These fibers are separated by soft DNGEs made of soft microparticles and a soft second network, as shown in Fig. 6 (A and B). The composite exhibits a Young’s modulus of 0.53 MPa, as determined with tensile tests and shown in Fig. 6D. This value is fourfold higher than that of soft DNGEs and only twofold lower than stiff DNGEs. The fracture toughness of the composite is almost sixfold higher than that of stiff DNGEs, as shown in Fig. 6D. Moreover, its fatigue threshold under cyclic loading, is sixfold higher than that of stiff DNGEs, as observed in Fig. 6E and fig. S13. Note that the composites display a ductile fracture, in stark contrast to stiff DNGEs that catastrophically fail, as shown in fig. S13.

We expect the cohesion between different DNGEs to be strong because all particles are connected through the same second network that interpenetrates and covalently cross-links them. To test our expectation, we change the direction of the fibers by 90° such that the sample is loaded perpendicularly to the fiber direction. This DNGE composite fails within the soft DNGE and not at the material interface, as shown in fig. S14. These results illustrate the strength of the material interface.

Nature frequently fabricates core-shell structures to combine stiffness with toughness. A prominent example is the mussel byssus fiber (66–69). Inspired by the mussel byssus fiber, we 3D print a DNGE core made of soft DNGEs that is surrounded by a stiff DNGE shell, as shown in Fig. 6 (A and C). The resulting composite exhibits a Young’s Modulus almost identical to that of the stiff DNGE, around 1 MPa. Yet, the fracture toughness of the core-shell DNGE is almost threefold higher than that of stiff DNGEs, shown in Fig. 6D. The soft core increases not only the fracture toughness of stiff DNGEs but also their fatigue threshold almost fivefold. This unique combination of stiffness, fracture toughness, and fatigue resistance highlights the potential of our material to unite mechanical properties that are typically mutually exclusive.

DISCUSSION

We present DNGEs that unify toughness and fatigue resistance, properties that have this far been mutually exclusive in additive-free elastomers. Optimized DNGEs exhibit a 15-fold higher toughness and 3-fold higher fatigue resistance than bulk DN elastomers of identical composition owing to their ability to efficiently delocalize stress and dissipate large amounts of energy within the interstitial regions composed of a soft SN elastomer in a repetitive manner. By designing DNGE composites that integrate soft, fatigue-resistant DNGEs with stiff DNGEs, fatigue resistance and toughness can to a certain extent be combined with stiffness. Further work on the optimization of the composition of the interstitial spaces might further improve the stiffness of DNGEs. We anticipate that this material will enable the development of more durable elastomers for applications where soft components experience cyclic loading, such as in soft robotics and biomedical devices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

The objective of this study is to characterize the toughness and fatigue resistance of DNGEs and compare these properties to those of conventional bulk SN and DN elastomers.

Materials and methods

BA (Sigma-Aldrich, 234923), BDA (Sigma-Aldrich, 411744), HMP (Sigma-Aldrich, 405655), ethyl acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, 270989), PVA (Sigma-Aldrich, 363170), and Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich, P1379) were all used as received.

Preparation of bulk SN elastomers

SNs with the same composition as the second network were produced. A precursor solution composed of BA containing 0.05 or 0.3 mol % BDA and 0.1 mol % HMP was prepared. The solution was cast into dogbone (narrow area: w = 8 mm, t = 2 mm) or slab-shaped (w = 12 mm, t = 2 mm) Teflon molds and cross-linked under UV light (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 25 mW cm−2) for 3 hours in a glove box under nitrogen.

Preparation of bulk DN elastomers

The bulk DN elastomers were prepared following a previously reported protocol (11). The SN elastomer whose composition is identical to that of the microparticles was prepared from an ethyl acetate-based solution containing 50 wt % monomer (BA), 1.5 or 5 mol % BDA, and 1 mol % HMP. The solutions were cast into dogbone (narrow area: w = 8 mm, t = 2 mm) or slab-shaped (w = 12 mm, t = 2 mm) Teflon molds and rigidified by exposing them to UV light (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 25 mW cm−2) for 3 hours in a glove box under nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting first network elastomers were soaked overnight in an elastomeric precursor solution composed of BA containing 0.05 or 0.3 mol % BDA and 0.1 mol % HMP. The samples were cured under UV light (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 25 mW cm−2) for 3 hours in a glove box under a nitrogen atmosphere.

Preparation of elastomeric microparticles

The preparation method described below is adapted previously reported protocols (38). Elastomer microparticles were made from an ethyl acetate-based solution containing 50 wt % monomers (BA), 1.5 or 5 mol % of BDA, and 1 mol % of HMP. An aqueous solution containing 5 wt % of PVA, 13 to 23 kDa, and 87 to 89% hydrolyzed was prepared. Both solutions were degassed with argon for 60 s before being mixed and vortexed at 3000 rpm for 15 s. The resulting emulsions were converted into elastomer microparticles by stirring it at 400 rpm under a UV lamp (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 50 mW cm−2) for 3 hours to initiate the free radical polymerization reaction within drops. If stated “under N2,” the polymerization was done in a glove box under nitrogen. The microparticles were dispersed in an aqueous solution containing 2 wt % Tween 20 and centrifuged at 4000g for 15 min (MegaStar 1.6 R, VWR) before the supernatant was removed. This process was repeated five times, followed by five washing steps with isopropanol. Clean microparticles were dried in a fume hood.

Preparation of jammed elastomeric ink

An elastomeric precursor solution for the second network composed of BA, 0.05 or 0.3 mol % BDA, and 0.1 mol % HMP was prepared and degassed with argon for 60 s. The dried microparticles were soaked in this solution overnight. The dispersion was centrifuged at 4000g for 15 min before the supernatant was removed to convert it into an ink composed of jammed elastomer microparticles. In the jammed state, soft microparticles accounted for ~17 wt % of the overall paste. Stiff microparticles accounted for 23 wt %. All reagent ratios used to form SNs, DNs, and DNGEs are summarized in table S1.

Rheological measurement of jammed elastomeric ink

The ink was loaded onto an 8-mm-diameter parallel plate steel geometry in a DHR-3 TA instrument at 25°C and with a 1-mm gap. Amplitude sweep measurements were performed at 10 rad s−1 and subjected to strains from 0.01 to 300%. Oscillation frequency measurements were performed at 1% strain, from 0.1 to 50 Hz. Self-healing measurements were performed at 10 rad s−1, alternating between 0.1 and 100% strains for 60 s each, five times.

Printing of elastomeric ink into DNGE composite

The inks were loaded inside 3-ml syringes and centrifuged for 1 min at 2000 rpm to remove trapped air. 3D printing was performed with a commercial 3D printer (Bio X, Cellink). The inks were extruded through a 20-gauge nozzle (603-μm diameter) using a pressure-driven piston (15 to 30 kPa) with a speed of 10 mm s−1. Printed structures were cured with UV light (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 25 mW cm−2) for 3 hours.

Preparation of molded DNGEs

The ink was cast in dogbone (narrow area: w = 8 mm, t = 2 mm) or slab-shaped (w = 12 mm, t = 2 mm) Teflon molds. The samples were cured for 3 hours with UV light (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 25 mW cm−2) to initiate the free radical polymerization reaction of the precursors contained within the microparticles. If stated “under N2,” the polymerization was done in a glove box under nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting DNGEs were washed and stored in an aqueous solution containing 2 wt % Tween 20.

To ensure homogeneous polymerization, which is key to obtain uniform and reproducible mechanical properties, we limited the sample thickness to 5 mm. This constraint in terms of sample dimensions arises because of the limited penetration depth of UV light, needed to initiate the free radical polymerization of the second network, in the granular precursor. This limitation can be overcome by forming the second network through a different polymerization strategy.

Interstitial spaces quantification

To quantify the volume fraction of interstitial spaces within DNGEs, confocal microscopy was performed on DNGEs composed of fluorescently labeled microparticles containing 1.5 and mol % BDA. To produce fluorescent microparticles, 0.05 wt % of fluorescein o-acrylate was added to the elastomeric precursor solution before emulsification. The ink composed of jammed microparticles was pressed between two glass plates separated by 1-mm-thick spacers (t = 1 mm) and cured under UV light (UWAVE UV-365-100, 365 nm, 25 mW cm−2) to result in 1-mm-thick films. Tensile measurements of SN, DN, and DNGE systems

To characterize the mechanical properties of the elastomers, tensile tests were carried out with a commercial machine (ProLine 5 kN, 50-N load cell, Zwick Roell). The dogbone samples were mounted with pneumatic clamps (2 bars) and stretched at a constant velocity of 100 mm min−1. The Young’s modulus was calculated as the slope of the linear elastic region between 5 and 15% strain. The work of fracture was calculated as the area below the stress-strain curve until fracture and was expressed as energy per unit volume (joules per cubic meter).

For fracture toughness measurements, virgin samples and precut slabs were mounted with pneumatic clamps (2 bars) with an initial grip-to-grip separation h of 2 mm and stretched at a constant velocity of 100 mm min−1. For precut samples, a cut with a length of 6 mm (w/2) was made in the width direction of the slab using a razor blade. The strain at break of the precut sample εbreakcut was determined from stress-strain curves. The area below the stress-strain curve of the virgin samples until εbreakcut was calculated. This value was multiplied by the initial grip-to-grip separation h, to calculate the fracture toughness Г expressed as energy per unit area (joules per square meter), as summarized in Eq. 2Γ=h∫0εbreakcutσvirgindε(2)

For fatigue threshold measurements, a cut with a length c of 6 mm (w/2) was made in the width-direction of the slab using a razor blade. The precut samples were mounted with pneumatic clamps with an initial grip-to-grip separation of 2 mm. Cyclic loads were applied at a constant velocity of 100 mm min−1 to different strains for 1000 cycles. The crack was monitored with a camera (Direct Show Zwick, VDX, 1.0 fps), and the crack growth per cycle (dc/dN) was calculated for each strain applied. For each strain applied, the strain energy release rate G was calculated from the area under the tensile portion of the stress-strain curve of the virgin samples up to the corresponding strain during unloading after 500 cycles. This value was multiplied by the initial grip to grip separation and expressed as energy per unit area (joules per square meter). Compressive stress contributions associated with specimen buckling were not included as they do not represent tensile work acting on the crack. Then, dc/dN was plotted as a function of the stress intensity G, and a regression line was measured. The camera used has a resolution of 100 μm such that, after 1000 cycles, a crack growth of 1 mm/ for 1000 cycles: 1 μm/cycle can be realistically measured with our set up. For this reason, the measurements calculated with a growth lower than 1 μm/cycle were not taking into consideration for the regression line. Fatigue threshold was calculated as the intersection between the regression line and a crack growth of 1 Å/cycle and was expressed as energy per unit area (joules per square meter), following ASTM standard E647.

Cyclic loads are applied at a constant velocity of 100 mm min−1 to 50% strain for 1000 cycles. Rheological measurements of SN, DN, and DNGE systems

To compare the amount the entanglements present in each system, the storage modulus as a function of the angular frequency was measured. For this experiment, samples were cast in cylindrical Teflon molds (d = 8 mm, h = 5 mm). The samples were sheared with a DHR-3 TA Instrument with an 8-mm-diameter parallel plate steel geometry, under frequencies from 0.1 to 20 Hz.

Thermal measurements

DSC was carried out to characterize the thermal behaviors of SN, DN, and DNGE samples, by using a differential scanning calorimeter (Mettler Toledo). Aluminum pans were used for the reference and samples. Samples (3 to 8 mg) were heated at a rate of 10°C min−1 to 150°C and cooled at 10°C min−1 to −90°C. An isotherm was kept at −90°C for 30 min. The same heating protocol was repeated and used to determine the glass transition temperature Tg of the samples.

Fracture plane imaging

SEM was used to visualize the fracture plane of DNGE samples that have undergone fracture toughness tests. Samples were imaged with a field-emission SEM with Gemini II column, using an Inlens detector (HE-SE2, 1 kV, 70 pA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed on at least three independent samples (n ≥ 3) unless otherwise stated. Data are presented as mean values with standard deviations. Statistical analyses were conducted using Origin. Regression for fatigue threshold determination were performed by fitting crack growth rate (dc/dN) as a function of the applied energy release rate (G), and thresholds were calculated as the intersection between the fitted regression line and the critical growth criterion.
