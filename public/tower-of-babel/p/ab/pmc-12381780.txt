Clinical Validation of Manual Measurement of Cochlea Length With Post‐Operative Electrode Insertion Depth: A Pilot Study
Authors: Fei Wang, Dharmikkumar Anaghan, Anandhan Dhanasingh, Lian Hui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo clinically validate manual measurement of cochlear length from pre‐operative image of cochlea with post‐operative image of cochlear implant (CI) electrode.MethodsTemporal bone computer tomography (CT) scans of 23 ears were available for this pilot study. The inner ear was three‐dimensionally (3D) segmented, and cochlea length was manually measured for preferred angular depth depending on inner ear anatomical types for CI electrode length selection. Post‐operative CT scans were made available for electrode reconstruction to measure electrode length inside the cochlea to validate pre‐operative measurement of cochlear length.ResultsNormal anatomy (NA) inner ear was identified in 11 ears, enlarged vestibular aqueduct syndrome (EVAS) in 4 ears, incomplete partition (IP) type I in 2 ears, cochlear hypoplasia (CH) in 5 ears, and otosclerosis in 1 ear. Cochlear length was measured for 650° of angular depth in NA ears, 540° in EVAS and otosclerosis, 360° in IP type I, and for available angular depth depending on the development of the cochlea in CH type. Based on the cochlea length measured, a closely matching electrode length was chosen for implantation from 1 CI manufacturer. Partial insertion was seen in 2 NA ears, 1 otosclerosis ear, and 1 CH ear, whereas full insertion of the chosen electrode was achieved in the remaining 19 ears.ConclusionManual measurement of cochlea length from 3D image of cochlea is a practical way of choosing electrode length for preferred angular insertion depth depending on the cochlea anatomy.Level of EvidenceBetween level 2 and 3.

Introduction

Around 5% of the world's population, which is approximately 430 million people, are suffering with some degree of hearing loss requiring rehabilitation to restore it [1]. Hearing aid is a solution for mild to moderate hearing loss conditions, whereas cochlea implant (CI) is the solution for sensorineural hearing loss conditions [2]. A CI has two components, namely the externally worn sound processor and the implantable component. The microphone in the sound processor receives sound signals and breaks them down to digital signals, which are then transferred to implant electronics through the induction link coil. The implant electronics convert digital signals to electrical impulses, which are carried by electrode leads and delivered inside the length of the cochlea by the intracochlear electrode array to cover most of the sound frequencies [3].

Optimal placement of an electrode inside the cochlea lumen is one of the key factors toward a successful CI treatment [4]. The length of the cochlea lumen varies among the human population, with normal inner ear anatomy demanding that electrode arrays be available in different lengths [5]. Hearing outcomes with CI are affected by several factors, as reported in the literature, and one of the key factors that positively affects hearing outcomes is the angular depth of the electrode covering beyond the basal turn of the cochlea [6]. Estimation of cochlea duct length (CDL) with the aim of choosing the appropriate length of CI electrode, is well‐studied basic science research in the CI field [7, 8, 9].

Escude et al. in 2007 was the very first study to propose a mathematical equation to estimate the CDL along the outer wall with a single input value of A‐value, the cochlea basal turn diameter [10]. In 2014, Alexiades et al. formulated a mathematical equation to estimate the CDL along the organ of Corti (OC) as the position of the straight electrode is right under the basilar membrane; this could help better in choosing electrode length and to map the patient‐specific cochlea frequencies [11]. Again, this method involves only the A‐value as the input. In 2017, Schurzig et al. fine‐tuned this equation and increased the accuracy by bringing in a 2nd input value of cochlea basal turn width, the B‐value [12]. In 2020, Agrawal et al. applied high‐resolution synchrotron images to perform cochlea measurements to formulate yet another mathematical equation to estimate the CDL along the OC [13]. All these studies focused on estimating CDL in patent cochlea with 2.5 turns of cochlear lumen on average.

To the best of our knowledge, none of these methods have undergone clinical validation to check the accuracy of the estimated CDL by measuring the cochlea length from the post‐operative images following the electrode. As reported in the literature, approximately 10%–20% of congenital deaf subjects have some degree of inner ear abnormalities in whom the CDL equations validated for normal anatomy cochlea cannot be applied [14]. Moreover, some inner ear malformation types have a cystic apical portion where the placement of the electrode should be avoided to minimize electrode placement complications like tip fold over [15].

Therefore, we hypothesize that manual measurement of cochlea length for a safe angular depth on a three‐dimensional (3D) segmented model of the inner ear from pre‐operative images is a clinically accepted method for choosing an electrode length for any possible inner ear anatomy, as reported in the literature. A pilot study on clinical validation was performed by measuring the electrode length inside the cochlea from the post‐operative computer tomography (CT) images and compared it with the pre‐operative measurements.

Materials and Methods

Twenty consecutive CI patients (23 ears) from our clinic between February 2021 and March 2022 were considered for this study. From the pre‐operative computer tomography (CT) images, a 3D model of the inner ear was created in slicer freeware (www.slicer.org) as previously described elsewhere [16]. For an experienced user of slicer software, 3–5 min is needed to 3D reconstruct the inner ear and make a manual measurement of cochlear length for a certain insertion depth depending on the inner ear anatomical type. Manual measurement of CDL was performed on the 3D model using the markup toolbar function in slicer freeware, as shown in Figure 1A. From the post‐op CT, the electrode was 3D segmented and the length of the electrode inside the cochlea was measured manually using the markup toolbar function, as shown in Figure 1B. The study protocol was approved by the ethics review committee of the first hospital of China Medical University (IRB: 2024‐898).

Manual measurement of cochlear length for an angular insertion depth of 650° in pre‐operative image of a normal anatomy inner ear (A). Manual measurement of electrode length inside the cochlea from post‐operative image (B).

Electrode Length Selection

For the normal anatomy (NA) cochleae, electrode insertion depth was pre‐planned for 650° of angular depth because spiral ganglion cell bodies are reported to be distributed to that angular depth [17, 18]. An electrode length matching that insertion depth was chosen for implantation. For enlarged vestibular aqueduct syndrome (EVAS) and incomplete partition (IP) type I, electrode insertion depth was pre‐planned for 540° and 360° of angular insertion depth, respectively, because after which the apical portion appears cystic in those anatomical types, and the corresponding electrode length was chosen for implantation [19]. For cochlea hypoplasia (CH), electrode length selection was made individually to the extent of cochlea lumen availability. All 20 patients were implanted with various electrode lengths from MED‐EL GmbH, Innsbruck, Austria.

Discussion

Cochlea implant (CI) is mainly to provide electrical stimulation by the placement of an electrode inside the cochlea of any anatomy [20]. Speech/sound perception is then dependent on how the brain reads the electrical stimulation and makes hearing sense out of it [21]. We strongly believe that the optimal placement of the electrode inside the cochlea is the base for a successful CI treatment. This was the motivation for us to carefully choose the electrode length that matches the cochlear size and anatomy and to check if the pre‐planned electrode insertion depth/length is reflected in post‐op images.

There are prior works reported in the literature on the estimation of CDL and choosing electrode length accordingly [7, 8]. To our limited understanding, those solutions were validated only for normal anatomy inner ear and not for abnormal anatomies. Therefore, we decided to manually measure the cochlea length to desired angular depth depending on the inner ear anatomy and chose a matching electrode length. Out of 23 ears taken for analyses, full insertion of the chosen electrode length was achieved in 19 ears, making a success rate of 82.6%. Partial insertion of the chosen electrode in 4 ears (1× hypoplasia, 1× otosclerosis, and 2× normal anatomy) was due to the felt insertion resistance, and we stopped further advancing the electrode. Other than the partial insertion of the electrode seen in 4 ears, no other electrode insertion complications were observed, as seen from the post‐op images shown in Figure 2. Within this small group of patients, 6 different electrode variants from 1 CI manufacturer were used to match with the cochlea anatomies. All these subjects had improved hearing outcomes with CI.

Achieving full insertion of a chosen electrode length was the primary aim during the CI surgery. We realized that full insertion is not just dependent on the cochlear size measured pre‐operatively but also on the surgical space available for maneuvering the electrode. While our method might appear primitive, it gives a clear picture of the actual cochlea size and anatomy along with the electrode insertion path. This pilot study is encouraging us to recommend manual measurement of cochlea length as a clinically valuable method in choosing an electrode for any inner ear anatomy. Alshalan et al. reported recently on a method of estimating basal turn coverage in several inner ear malformation types [22]. Other than IP I, other malformation types have the cochlear portion developed more than basal turn (360°) and that motivated us to look for a practical method of measuring the cochlea length for desired insertion depth depending on the cochlea anatomy.

Our rationale for choosing different angular insertion depths for electrode insertion in different inner ear anatomies is as follows: In a NA inner ear, the spiral ganglion cell bodies are distributed up to an angular insertion depth of 650°–680°; therefore, we measured the CDL for 650° of angular depth in all NA cochleae. For IP type I inner ear, which is known to have a cystic cochlear portion, a 360° coverage for electrode placement was planned to minimize the electrode running over itself when placed beyond 360° of angular depth. EVAS is known to have a normally developed cochlear lumen for an angular depth of 540°; after which the cystic apex starts, and therefore, we measured the CDL for 540° for electrode length selection. Four ears diagnosed with CH type anatomy varied a lot in the development of the cochlear portion, and that made us manually measure the cochlear length for various angular depths accordingly.

Post‐operative CT scan is routinely taken in our clinic as a standardized procedure helping us to validate our pre‐operative measurements. One of the limitations of this study is the small population in which we validated our method; as well, the audiological outcomes are not fully available for all the patients. However, the primary aim was to choose an electrode length that minimizes electrode insertion complications in malformed inner ear anatomies, which was successful from this pilot study, observing no insertional complications other than partial insertion seen in 4 ears.

Conclusion

Our study shows that manual measurement of cochlea length for desired insertion depth on a 3D model of the inner ear of any anatomy is a clinically useful method in choosing an effective electrode length. Achieved full insertion of the electrode chosen in most of the ears is encouraging us to prospectively apply our method in our future patients. Also, this will be an educational exercise for the junior surgeons to understand the cochlea anatomy before implanting with a CI electrode.
