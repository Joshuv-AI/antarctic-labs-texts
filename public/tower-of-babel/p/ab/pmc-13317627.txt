Highly Functionalized and Precisely Defined Multipodal Silsesquioxanes with 16 Phosphonate Anchors: Hybrid Nanoscale Consolidants, Tailored for Cultural Heritage Preservation Applications
Authors: Stefania Liakaki-Stavropoulou, Konstantinos D. Demadis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tangible cultural heritage artifacts (e.g., archeological sites, historical buildings, monuments, and statues) can suffer severe deterioration upon exposure to external stresses (human-, or environment-derived) through the centuries. Hence, the implementation of effective conservation and consolidation strategies is necessary. A distinctive part of these is the design, preparation and application of functional chemical consolidants. In this paper we report on the synthesis and characterization of a multipodal polyhedral oligomeric silsesquioxane (POSS) with 16 protruding phosphonate groups (PHOS–POSS) that are grafted to the Si8O12 cage-like silsesquioxane core. A diphosphonate (PAPTS) containing a polymerizable silanetriol unit has been synthesized, as a small molecule partial analog of PHOS–POSS. PHOS–POSS is a potent consolidant for gypsum stones, as proven by drilling resistance measurements on artificial model gypsum specimens. This mineral was chosen because it has been used since the ancient times as a structural material (e.g., in the Minoan Palace of Knossos, Crete, Greece). It achieved more than a 1000-fold increase in drilling resistance, while exhibiting high penetration depth (>40 mm). PAPTS, on the other hand, is a much weaker consolidant (enhancing drilling resistance by a factor of 2), most likely due to the low number of phosphonate groups (two). Based on structural correlations with several calcium phosphonate networks and structure solution of the compound Ca-methylamino-di­(methylenephosphonate) (Ca–C1D), it is proposed that the high consolidation efficiency of PHOS–POSS is ascribed to its high affinity for Ca sites on gypsum (ability to substitute the surface Ca2+-bound water molecules), and to the random substitution of sulfate groups in the structure of gypsum.

Introduction

The preservation of cultural heritage (CH) is one of the top priorities of nations that wish to bequeath tangible artifacts and intangible traditions to future generations. CH is a vital component of human identity across generations. Not only is it a link to the distant past, but it also nurtures a sense of cohesion and belonging, while fostering cultural diversity and persistency in a world under constant change. Hence, the preservation of CH empowers the citizens of societies at the local, national and global level to contemplate their ancestry and contribute to a more peaceful, inclusive and amicable global society.

According to UNESCO, CH is divided into two principal categories: intangible and tangible. The latter is additionally classified into movable (e.g., paintings, furniture, sculptures, etc.) and immovable (e.g., archeological sites, including historical buildings and monuments). Research and development of diverse treatments is ongoing for the protection and conservation of tangible CH assets. The protection of CH is critical because of the following reasons: (a) it protects our past, (b) it acts as a linkage to our roots, (c) it contributes to the shaping of our future, (d) it enhances our understanding of our origins, (e) it accompanies us in our voyage from the past to the present times, (f) it helps us carry forward our values and traditions. Therefore, by protecting and conserving the elements of tangible and intangible CH, we strengthen a sense of fellowship and identity within our communities, while promoting intercultural interactions, and bestowing a more spirited and divergent world for future generations to come.

Consolidants are crucial materials for CH preservation. A consolidant is broadly defined as a substance or material that is able to strengthen and stabilize artifacts, structures, or artworks that have suffered, or are undergoing constant deterioration. The consolidant functions by boosting the cohesion of loose or friable artifact components, and by preventing or decelerating further damage. Consolidant application is a routine approach for restoring stone-made monuments and other stone-based artworks. Furthermore, consolidant use contributes to establishing (or re-establishing) effective cohesion on weathered stones. It additionally sustains strong adhesion between healthy and affected stone locales. In the end, it reinforces and stabilizes artifacts and prevents further damage from environmental factors or mechanical stresses. Notably, a consolidant does not simply fill the voids in the stone interior, but binds together deteriorated particles, thus ensuring stone survival for future generations. Importantly, a consolidant must not provoke any aesthetic or other physicochemical changes. A great variety of consolidants have been reported that are categorized into the following broad families: inorganic, organic, hybrid, or synergistic blends of suitable and compatible chemical species.

The attributes of an ideal consolidant include: (a) chemical compatibility with the stone, (b) high penetration depth, (c) improvement of the stone’s mechanical properties, (d) durability, (e) minimization of the possibility of further degradation, (f) cost-effectiveness, (g) low or no toxicity, (i) practicality and ease of application, and (j) maintenance of the aesthetic features of the stone, and (i) durability and resistance to further degradation.

In this paper we report on the synthesis and characterization of a polyhedral oligomeric silsesquioxane (POSS) that possesses 16 phosphonate groups (coined PHOS–POSS) that have been introduced to the Si8O12 cage-like silsesquioxane core, see Figure .

Energy-minimized calculated structure of PHOS–POSS showing the Si8O12 cubic core and the eight pendant propylamino-diphosphonate moieties. H atoms on the C atoms have been omitted for clarity. Color code: Si green, O red, P orange, C black, N blue, H white.

The eight pendant di­(phosphonate) moieties were grafted on the Si8O12 core by the phospha-Mannich reaction, starting from the preformed precursor, octa-aminopropyl-terminated POSS (OAP–POSS). Although the chemistry of silsesquioxanes is well developed, no such material exists, “decorated” with phosphonate groups. To the best of our knowledge, no polyphosphonate exists that gathers this high number of phosphonate groups (with a well-recognized high affinity for metal ions and mineral surfaces), positioned in such a precise and symmetrical manner, achieving a high density of phosphonates on the same core.

The initial design of PHOS–POSS was inspired by the need for a molecule that possesses the following attractive features: (a) a high density of functional phosphonate groups, (b) a molecular, but not polymeric structure, (c) a symmetric distribution of the phosphonate moieties (T 8 structure), (d) an appropriate molecular size, and (e) satisfactory stability and water solubility. Each of the above attributes are necessary for the application sought, i.e., the use of PHOS–POSS as a nanoscale chemical consolidant for the reinforcement and consolidation of structural materials that exist in CH, such as gypsum stones, used in this case as a model material. Mineral gypsum (selenite) stones were used extensively by ancient Cretans in the Minoan Palace of Knossos (Crete, Greece; the second most recognizable archeological site in Greece after the Acropolis), mostly for building and ornamental purposes. Interestingly, the mineral gypsum building and ornamental elements catalogue of the palatial complex of Knossos consists of 2185 entries. The quantification of the consolidation efficiency was based on drilling resistance measurements (DRMS) that showed that PHOS–POSS can enhance the gypsum resistance by a factor of 1500 (compared to unconsolidated, “control” gypsum specimens). DRMS is the technique of choice in characterizing degraded and consolidated stone specimens in CH. −

Synthetic Protocols

Synthesis of Octa­(3-aminopropyl)­octasilsesquioxane (OAP–POSS)

OAP–POSS was prepared according to procedures described in the literature, and was isolated as the octakis hydrochloride salt. All characterization data obtained (1H, 13C, 29Si NMR (solution), 29Si MAS solid state NMR, ATR–IR, elemental analyses) are in excellent agreement with those reported in the literature and are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S1–S3).

Synthesis of the Hexadecaphosphonate-Functionalized Sesquioxane PHOS–POSS

The synthetic procedure is schematically shown in Figure . Phosphonomethylation was carried out via a phospha-Mannich reaction. Specifically, 2.5 mL deionized water, 3.66 g (0.003 mol) OAP–POSS, 4.1 g (0.050 mol) phosphorus acid and 2.5 mL hydrochloric acid (37%) were introduced in a two-necked round-bottom flask fitted with a magnetic stir bar and a reflux condenser and heated to reflux. After 1 h, 7.5 mL aqueous formaldehyde solution was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was kept at reflux for 24 h. The flask was then opened to the air, and the volatiles were allowed to evaporate, leaving behind a viscous yellowish liquid, to which methanol (50 mL) was added. The mixture was stirred at ambient temperature overnight, during which the oil was transformed to a white solid. It was isolated by filtration and washed with methanol and diethyl ether. Yield: ∼62% (based on OAP–POSS). The product PHOS–POSS is very hygroscopic and upon exposure to laboratory humidity turns back into an oily residue. It can be preserved indefinitely in a glass flask in methanol. 1H, 13C, 31P, 29Si NMR data are consistent with the structure shown in Figure and are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S4–S11). Elemental analysis by EDS, calculated Si/P ratio 1:2, found 1:2.11.

Synthetic scheme for the synthesis of PHOS–POSS, which is shown in its zwitterionic form.

Synthesis of the Silyl-Diphosphonate PAPTS [(((3-(Trihydroxysilyl)­propyl)­azanediyl)­bis­(methylene))­diphospho-nic Acid]

The synthetic procedure is shown in Figure and was based on a phospha-Mannich reaction. Specifically, 5.0 mL deionized water, 5.534 g (0.025 mol) APTES, 4.1 g (0.050 mol) phosphorus acid and 5.0 mL hydrochloric acid (37%) were introduced in a two-necked round-bottom flask fitted with a magnetic stir bar and a reflux condenser and heated to reflux. After 1 h, 7.5 mL aqueous formaldehyde solution was added dropwise. The reaction mixture was kept at reflux for 3 h. The flask was then opened to the air, and the volatiles were allowed to evaporate, until a viscous yellowish liquid was obtained, to which methanol (50 mL) was added. The mixture was stirred at ambient temperature overnight, during which the oil was transformed to a white solid. It was isolated by filtration and washed with methanol and diethyl ether. Yield: 59% (based on APTES). The product is very hygroscopic and upon exposure to laboratory humidity turns back into a viscous oil. It can be preserved indefinitely in a sealed glass flask in methanol. 1H, 13C, 31P, 29Si NMR data are consistent with the structure shown in Figure and are provided in the Supporting Information (Figures S12–S14). Elemental analysis by EDS, calculated Si/P ratio 1:2, found 1:1.93.

Synthetic scheme for the synthesis of PAPTS, which is shown in its zwitterionic form.

Synthesis of Metal–PHOS–POSS Compounds

A detailed procedure is given for Ca–PHOS–POSS. Identical procedures were followed for the Sr- and Ba–PHOS–POSS compounds. In a Teflon lined reactor, a quantity of 0.119 g (0.05 mmol) PHOS–POSS was dissolved in 2.0 mL deionized water and the pH was adjusted to 2.0. Then, 0.029 g (0.2 mmol) CaCl2·2H2O were added under stirring and the pH was readjusted to ∼1.0. The reactor was sealed and placed in an oven at 120 °C for 3 days. After that, it was removed and allowed to reach ambient temperature before it was opened. The product was isolated by filtration as a white precipitate, washed with deionized water and left to air-dry. Yield: ∼50%. For the Sr- and Ba–PHOS–POSS compounds the appropriate amounts of salts were used (0.053 g, 0.2 mmol SrCl2·6H2O, and 0.049 g, 0.2 mmol BaCl2·2H2O) with similar yields.

Synthesis of Metal-PAPTS Compounds

A detailed procedure is given for Ca-PAPTS. Identical procedures were followed for the Sr- and Ba-PAPTS compounds. For the Sr- and Ba-PAPTS compounds the appropriate amounts of salts were used (0.024 g, 0.1 mmol SrCl2·6H2O, and 0.027 g, 0.1 mmol BaCl2·2H2O). Yields were 40–60% (based on the metal source). In a Teflon lined reactor, a quantity of PAPTS (0.130 g, 0.2 mmol) and CaCl2·2H2O (0.015 g, 0.1 mmol) were dissolved in 3 mL deionized water under stirring. The pH was adjusted to 3.0. The reactor was sealed and placed in an oven at 120 °C for 3 days. After that, it was removed and allowed to reach ambient temperature before it was opened. The product was isolated by filtration as a white precipitate, washed with deionized water and left to air-dry.

Synthesis of Ca-Methylamino-Di­(methylenephosphonate) (Ca–C1D)

The synthesis was performed under hydrothermal conditions. The ligand C1D (44 mg, 0.20 mmol) was dissolved in deionized water (2 mL). The pH was adjusted to 4.5, and then solid CaCl2·2H2O (29 mg, 0.20 mmol) was added. The mixture was stirred until it was homogeneous, placed in a Teflon-lined autoclave, and finally heated at 120 °C for 3 days. After it was cooled to ambient temperature, a white crystalline solid was formed, which was isolated by filtration, washed with deionized water and air-dried. Yield: ∼25%. The Ca/P atom ratio was found ∼1:2 by EDS.

Preparation of Artificial Gypsum Specimens

Given the limited quantity (if any) of the original mineral material (selenite) from the archeological site of Knossos and keeping in mind that the final objective of this approach is related mostly to the chemical bonding (surface coordination chemistry) of the consolidating compounds with the surface of gypsum, artificial samples were prepared and used in the testing. Calcium sulfate hemihydrate (CaSO4·1/2H2O; mineralogical name bassanite, a.k.a. “plaster of Paris”) was used as the precursor in the synthesis of gypsum. Hence, 1 kg of hemihydrate was slowly and gradually added to 600 mL of deionized water, and the mixture was gently mixed with a plastic spatula to avoid formation of bubbles until it became a homogeneous greyish thick/viscous fluid. The freshly prepared material was poured into silicone molds of cubic shape (dimensions 5 × 5 × 5 cm3) within 10 min, where it was left to settle and solidify at ambient conditions. After 2 h, the semidry specimens were removed from the molds and were left exposed to air for further drying for at least 1 week. At the end of this process all calcium sulfate hemihydrate had been transformed to calcium sulfate dihydrate (CaSO4·2H2O, gypsum). Complete transformation was ensured by powder XRD, which showed peaks corresponding to gypsum exclusively. Figure shows the morphology of a representative gypsum specimen, geometrical description, and identity of the mineral.

Consolidant Application on the Gypsum Specimens and Drilling Resistance Measurement (DRMS) Protocol

For the study of the performance of the new phosphonates (PHOS–POSS and PAPTS) and their metal derivatives as consolidant materials, artificial gypsum specimens were prepared in the laboratory, as described above. All gypsum samples of external dimensions ∼5 cm3, were immersed in Petri dishes (9 cm base diameter) containing 30 mL consolidant solution (up to ∼1 cm height) of different concentrations (3.33 mM and 10.00 mM) and pH values (from 0.5 to 7.0), Figure .

Artificial gypsum cubes partly immersed into consolidant solution showing the gradual consolidant absorption (upper) and a gypsum cube mounted onto the DRMS drill (lower).

The time required for the entire volume of the solution to be absorbed by the gypsum specimens varied between 30 and 60 s. Stock solutions of H2SO4 (25% v/v) were used for pH adjustment in the cases of very acidic solutions. The final pH of the consolidant solution was ∼1. HCl was avoided to prevent the deterioration of gypsum specimens due to formation of the water-soluble CaCl2 salt. Prior to each DRMS measurement, the specimens were allowed to dry for 5 days under ambient conditions, as it was noted that partially wet specimens cause unreliable measurements. DRMS measurements were performed on each individual gypsum specimen prior to consolidant application (so that each gypsum specimen has its own “control” measurement). Drillings were made on the “bottom” side of the cube (the one previously immersed in the liquid) and after three maturation times, i.e., 5, 10, and 15 days. Forn each drilling test, three sets of measurements were performed with a ±3% standard deviation.

Results

Synthesis and Characterization of the Consolidants PHOS–POSS and PAPTS

According to the Mannich-type (a.k.a. Moedritzer-Irani or phospha-Mannich) reaction strategy, a primary alkylamine can be smoothly converted to a di­(methylenephosphonate) moiety. Published work includes various amines (giving phosphonates with variable number of –CH2PO3H2 moieties), for example, monoamines (diphosphonates), diamines (tetraphosphonates), triamines (pentaphosphonates), oligoamines, and even polymeric amines. − The phospha-Mannich reaction works well for primary and secondary amines, but not for tertiary amines. Sterically hindered secondary amines are more difficult to be functionalized by this methodology. In the case of OAP–POSS the reaction (Figure ) proceeded smoothly and in good yields, in spite of the high number of n-propylamine moieties on the same Si8O12 core. This is ascribed to the low steric hindrance of each amine group connected to a fairly long n-propyl group. The energy-minimized structure of the hexadecyl phosphonate PHOS–POSS is shown in Figure . PHOS–POSS adopts a nearly spherical structure with mean diameter of ∼21 Å (calculated phosphonate O···O distances). PHOS–POSS was characterized by several techniques in both the solid state and in solution. The 1H NMR spectrum of PHOS–POSS (in D2O, pH < 2, Figure S4, Supporting Information) shows the characteristic peaks of the protons of the n-propyl group with chemical shifts at δ 0.634 (t, 2H, α-position to Si), 1.751 (m, 2H, β-position to Si), 3.450 (m, 2H, γ-position to Si). The protons of the methylene phosphonate moiety overlap with the ones at the γ-position (hence the splitting of the peak). The ratio of the peaks is equal to 1:1:3 and agrees well with the expected ratio. In the 13C NMR spectrum (in D2O, pH < 2, Figure S5, Supporting Information) the carbon atoms of the n-propyl group exhibit chemical shifts with δ 10.65 (α-position to Si), 18.87 (β-position to Si), 59.03 (γ-position to Si), while the peaks at 53.12 and 54.96 ppm correspond to the –CH2– carbon atoms next to the phosphonate groups. This differentiation is due to the partial deprotonation of one of the phosphonate moieties on each propyl group. The 31P NMR spectrum (in D2O, pH < 2, Figure S6, Supporting Information) shows a single peak at 17.27 ppm, proving the presence of one type of P. The symmetry of the core is corroborated by a single peak in the 29Si MAS solid state NMR spectrum (Figure S7, Supporting Information) and the solution 29Si NMR spectrum (Figures S8 and S9, Supporting Information).

The synthesis of PAPTS (Figure ) proceeds smoothly as well, with satisfactory yields. Here, phosphonomethylation occurs directly on APTES, with concomitant hydrolysis of the ethoxy groups to give a silanetriol [–Si­(OH)3]. Based on the NMR characterization of the product, the hydrolysis of the Si–OCH2CH3 groups was complete. The characteristic peaks of the protons in PAPTS with chemical shifts at δ 0.622 (t, 2H, Si–CH2–), 1.736 (m, 2H, Si–CH2–CH2–), 3394 (m, 6H, Si–CH2–CH2–CH2–), see Figure S12 in the Supporting Information. The ratio of the peaks is equal to 1:1:3. In the 13C NMR spectrum (Figure S13, Supporting Information) the carbons of the propyl chain show shifts δ 8.90 (Si–CH2–), 17.60 (Si–CH2–CH2–), and 58.43 (Si–CH2–CH2–CH2–), while the peaks at 50.06 and 51.91 ppm correspond to the –CH2– carbons next to the phosphonate groups. The 31P NMR spectrum (Figure S14, Supporting Information) shows a single peak at 9.36 ppm. Peaks at 3.2 ppm in the 1H NMR spectra and at 48.62 ppm in the 13C NMR spectra correspond to methanol used to wash the solid. The ATR–IR spectra of PHOS–POSS and PAPTS are shown in Figure S15 in the Supporting Information. The characteristic bands due to the Si–O–Si core linkages appear as an intense band at ∼1100 cm–1. The N–H+ bond of the amino-diphosphonate moiety gives rise to a weak band at 1500 cm–1. The region 845–1105 cm–1 includes bands due to the P–OH groups, while the 1104–1257 cm–1 region shows bands assigned to the PO stretch. The bending vibration of the –PO3 group appears in the 400–510 cm–1 range. Other salient features of the spectrum include bands at 700 cm–1 (–CΡΟ3), 820 cm–1 (C–Ν), and 2890–3000 cm–1 (–CH2–). The spectrum of PAPTS shares several features with that of PHOS–POSS, but the “fingerprint” region exhibits differences due to the absence of the Si8O12 core. EDS on the pure solids showed Si/P atom ratios of 1:2.11 (PHOS–POSS) and 1:1.93 (PAPTS) (Figure S16 in the Supporting Information), close to the theoretical 1:2 value.

Stability of the Consolidants PHOS–POSS and PAPTS

TG analysis measurements were performed for OAP–POSS and PHOS–POSS under nitrogen flow (Figure S17, Supporting Information). The first “step” of mass loss (approximately 40% for OAP–POSS and 15% for PHOS–POSS) is due to the presence of methanol in the samples (they need to be kept in methanol to avoid severe hydration). The decomposition of both compounds begins at about 340 °C and the mass loss is approximately 30% for OAP–POSS and 20% for PHOS–POSS. The thermal decomposition results point to the fact that PHOS–POSS is stable at ambient temperature in the solid state and can be stored safely.

In order to evaluate the stability of PHOS–POSS and PAPTS in water, aqueous solutions were prepared at different pH values and then monitored by 31P NMR and 29Si NMR spectroscopies. Establishing the condition for solution stability is important because consolidants are applied as solutions. 31P NMR spectra of PHOS–POSS at pH = 1 showed identical peaks for the two samples taken, first at t = 0 and then t = 5 days (Figure S10, Supporting Information), suggesting satisfactory stability. However, when pH was raised to 2, the 31P NMR spectrum of the sample taken at t = 1 day showed the existence of an additional shift at 7.64 ppm which indicated the decomposition of the Si8O12 core (Figure S11, Supporting Information). The 29Si NMR spectrum of the sample of PHOS–POSS at pH = 2 showed that the characteristic peak at −68.65 ppm for the symmetrical silsesquioxanes was shifted to −40 ppm, which confirms that PHOS–POSS is not stable in aqueous solutions with pH ≥ 2 and decomposes generating PAPTS. An identical approach was followed for aqueous solutions of PAPTS. The study showed that PAPTS is stable in water, regardless of the solution pH.

Synthesis and Characterization of Metal Derivatives (Ca, Sr, and Ba) of PHOS–POSS

The organic portion of PHOS–POSS resembles the family of diphosphonic acids of the general formula [(HO)2(O)­PCH2]2N–(CH2) x CH3, where x = 0, 1, 3, 5, 7, and 11, reported by us. The coordination chemistry of these alkylamino-diphosphonate ligands is being explored in our group with diverse results with a multitude of multivalent metal ions. In light of the application being sought in this work, i.e., stone consolidants, emphasis was given to the coordination chemistry of PHOS–POSS with the colorless alkaline-earth metal ions (Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+) that are cognate to the gypsum substrate.

All metal derivatives of PHOS–POSS are amorphous (by powder XRD, see Figure S18 in the Supporting Information). Their ATR–IR spectra were identical (see Figure S19 in the Supporting Information). Upon comparison of the spectrum of pristine PHOS–POSS with the spectra of its metal derivatives, slight shifts of the characteristic peaks of the phosphonic groups are noted. Specifically, the peaks 400–510 cm–1 due to the bending vibration of the –PO3 group are shifted to lower frequencies. Likewise, the peak at 700 cm–1 due to bending vibration of –CPO3 group and the peaks at 845–1105 cm–1 of the P–OH bond have shifted as well.

Due to the amorphous nature of these metal derivatives, other techniques were sought. For example, EDS can give valuable information about the Si/P/metal atom ratios that will suggest the number of metal ions bound to the phosphonate units. The results showed that the Si/P/metal atom ratios are close to 1:2:1 (see Figures S20–S22 in the Supporting Information), and with acceptable variability (1.61–1.83 for P and 0.92–1.18 for the metals). These results prove that Si8O12 core remains intact and that one metal ion is bound to each amino-diphosphonate unit.

The metal derivatives of PAPTS are amorphous as well (by powder XRD, see Figure S23 in the Supporting Information). Their ATR–IR spectra were identical (see Figure S24 in the Supporting Information). Upon comparison of the spectrum of pristine PAPTS with the spectra of its metal derivatives, slight shifts of the characteristic peaks of the phosphonic groups are observed. The H-bonded Si–OH stretch appears as a very broad band in the 3200–3600 cm–1 region. A medium intensity band at ∼950 cm–1 is assigned to the Si–O stretches, while the Si–C vibration appears as a shoulder at ∼1200 cm–1. The region 850–1200 cm–1 is dominated by bands associated with the phosphonate and silicate groups. Metal–O stretching vibrations appear in the 400–500 cm–1 region.

EDS results (see Figures S25–S27 in the Supporting Information) reveal that the Si/P/metal atom ratios are close to 1:2:1 with acceptable variability (1.69–2.05 for P and 0.91–1.16 for the metals). These results strongly suggest that one metal ion is bound to the amino-diphosphonate unit.

Consolidation Performance PHOS–POSS PAPTS, and Their Metal Derivatives

The effect of PHOS–POSS and PAPTS, as well as their metal derivatives (where metal = Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+), on gypsum resistance to drilling was evaluated by DRMS. The specimens were partially immersed in aqueous solutions of each consolidant material of different concentrations, pH values and exposure times. Stock solutions of H2SO4 were used for pH adjustment due to sulfate anion being present in both the gypsum substrate and the acid. HCl was avoided because it was found to cause deterioration of the gypsum specimens. Initially, DRMS measurements were performed on all specimens in the absence of consolidant, which were used as “control” experiments. After immersion of the specimens and prior to each DRMS measurement, the specimens were allowed to dry in air for at least 5 days. In each test, three sets of drilling measurements were performed on the same gypsum cube for each time interval. The average value was calculated with an experimental error ±3%.

The comparative raw drilling measurement data OAP–POSS, PHOS–POSS, and PAPTS (all at 3.33 mM concentration) are given in Figure . The performance of OAP–POSS (containing terminal –NH2 groups, red circles) is identical to the control (black rhombs), thus it offers no consolidation to gypsum. PAPTS (green squares) nearly doubles the resistance, which is consistently above the control values, but still with relatively low resistant values (with a maximum of ∼1 N at 2 mm depth). PHOS–POSS (blue triangles) clearly demonstrates superior performance, achieving a maximum resistance (10.5 N at 8.5 mm depth and 14.6 N at 44.8 mm depth). It is important to emphasize the fact that the values of drilling resistance are consistently well above the control even at high depth values. It demonstrates the high penetration ability of this consolidant and fulfills one of the most significant requirements in consolidation.

Comparative consolidation performance for OAP–POSS, PHOS–POSS, and PAPTS. All consolidants were at 3.33 mM concentration and the measurements were performed after 5 days.

Two additional variables were evaluated, the maturation time (5, 10, and 15 days) and the consolidant concentration (3.33 mM and 10.00 mM). Their influence is shown in Figure , where average drilling resistance values were used. The strong influence of PHOS–POSS is clearly demonstrated by dramatically enhancing the resistance: 5.76 N (∼+1000% at 5 days), 6.97 N (∼+1300% at 10 days) and 7.64 N (∼+1500% at 15 days). Tripling the consolidant concentration causes minor increases in the resistance values for OAP–POSS, PHOS–POSS, and PAPTS, not exceeding 14%. Notably, for the 10.00 mM concentration of PHOS–POSS, a small decrease in resistance is observed (∼22% at 15 days).

Comparative consolidation performance at different maturation times for OAP–POSS, PHOS–POSS, and PAPTS at 3.33 mM (upper) and 10.00 mM (lower) concentrations.

Alkaline-earth metal containing species have been used in the past for consolidation purposes. Both PHOS–POSS and PAPTS contain phosphonate groups, which have a high affinity for metal ions in general (both in solution, and on mineral surfaces). Hence, each of the cations Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+ were used in consolidating solutions, together with PHOS–POSS or PAPTS to assess whether the presence of these cations might exhibit synergistic effects. The comparative drilling measurements are shown in Figure .

Comparative consolidation performance for PAPTS (upper) and PHOS–POSS (lower), in the presence of externally added metal ions (Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+). Consolidants were at 3.33 mM concentration and the measurements were performed after 10 days.

For PAPTS, the presence of all three Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+ cations enhances its consolidation performance (see Figure upper), by 41%, 58% and 32%, respectively, compared to “free” PAPTS, and roughly by double these numbers compared to the control. Resistance values of 0.8–1.5 N are observed at low depth values (<5 mm), possibly due to either fast metal-PAPTS precipitate formation, and/or polycondensation of the silanetriol moiety at the early stages of metal ion/PAPTS adsorption. The possible enhancing role of the cations is discussed below (in Discussion).

The beneficial effects of the Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+ cations are reversed for PHOS–POSS (see Figure lower). Its consolidation performance is compromised by −21%, −4% and −48%, respectively, compared to “free” PHOS–POSS. The possible role of the cations is discussed below (in Discussion).

The effect of multiple immersion cycles was studied for PAPTS and PHOS–POSS, but only insignificant differences were noted. Interestingly, in the case of PHOS–POSS the consolidant solution was not absorbed by the gypsum specimen during the second immersion.

The excellent performance of PHOS–POSS is somewhat limited by the fact that it requires a low pH (<2) for its application to avoid its decomposition to PAPTS. Based on the results presented in Figure , PHOS–POSS shows substantial consolidation performance at 5 days (∼12-fold increase in drilling resistance). Due to its rapid infusion into the stone, the pH in the gypsum stone interior may remain sufficiently low to avoid decomposition. Even if PHOS–POSS decomposes to PAPTS within the stone, it can still demonstrate some consolidation activity. The generation of 8 mol of PAPTS from 1 mol of PHOS–POSS, however, is not expected to increase consolidation efficiency because, as shown in Figure , PAPTS concentration increase does not translate into enhanced performance. This highlights the fact that consolidation performance is not directly proportional to consolidant concentration (i.e., number of phosphonate groups on a “weight” basis) but it is dependent on the structural architecture of the consolidant and the density of the phosphonate groups attached to the same molecule.

It is understandable that all results presented herein were collected from artificial gypsum specimens. Obviously, these consolidants will show different consolidation efficiency on mineral gypsum (selenite). The latter is macrocrystalline, with several heterogeneities and void spaces. Additionally, the drilling resistance values of mineral gypsum are at least 10 times higher than those of artificial gypsum. Based on our experience, the scatter of these values across a specimen is very wide (from 5 to 15 N) due to the heterogeneous nature of the stone. However, the consolidation efficiency results on artificial gypsum do have value, at least from a comparative consolidation performance point of view. Further consolidation experiments on mineral gypsum are needed to fully evaluate consolidant efficacy in a more realistic mineral environment.

Crystal Structure of Ca–C1D

Both POSS–PHOS and PAPTS feature the amino-di­(methylenephosphonate) moiety. In view of their gypsum consolidation performance presented above, structural details of the coordination of this moiety to Ca2+ ions may reveal details on how it may interact with gypsum surfaces in ways that enhance its resistance. For this, the model compound Ca-methylamino-di­(methylenephosphonate) was structurally characterized and its structural details were studied (see Figure ). Unfortunately, single crystals of metal containing PHOS–POSS or PAPTS materials could not be obtained, as all products were grossly amorphous (Figures S18 and S23 in the Supporting Information).

Structural features of the compound Ca–C1D. (A) Coordination environment of the Ca2+ center with Ca–O bond lengths, showing the 4- and 8-membered chelating rings and the monocapped octahedron of the Ca2+ center. (B) Coordination of the Ca–C1D ligand. (C) Interlayer H-bonds between the phosphonate groups and lattice water. (D) Packing of the 2D structure along the b-axis (four layers shown). (E) A single layer of the Ca–C1D structure along the a-axis. Lattice waters are shown in yellow.

The crystallographically unique Ca2+ center is located in a monocapped octahedral environment, see Figure A, (confirmed by the SHAPE software), The Ca2+ is coordinated by seven O atoms from phosphonate moieties. There are no Ca-bound waters. There are two types of chelating rings (Figure A), one 4- and one 8-membered. The former is formed by two oxygens (O1 and O3) originating from the same phosphonate group (P1). The latter includes the amino-di­(phosphonate) unit and is formed by two oxygens (O1 and O4) originating from the two phosphonate moieties (P1 and P2, respectively). In spite of the inherent instability of an 8-membered chelating ring, this structural motif is not uncommon in metal phosphonate chemistry. Notably, the Ca-bound O4(P2) oxygen is protonated, a rare phenomenon in metal phosphonate structural chemistry. The coordination sphere of the Ca2+ is completed by three P–O– moieties, coming from three different C1D ligands. The Ca–O bond distances show a wide variation and are found in the range 2.315–2.672 Å, which are expected and noted for other Ca–phosphonate compounds. The Ca/C1D molar ratio in the structure is 1:1, with each C1D ligand binding five Ca2+ cations (Figure B), with one phosphonate (P2) acting as a bridge (through O4 and O5) and the second (P1) being both bridging and chelating. The P1 phosphonate group is doubly (fully) deprotonated, the P1 phosphonate is singly deprotonated, with a proton residing on Ca-bound O4 and the N is protonated due to its high basicity (a common feature in metal phosphonate chemistry). This renders the C1D ligand with an overall charge of “–2”. The structure of Ca–C1D is 2D layered with shortest interlayer Ca···Ca distances at 12.939 Å (Figure D). The 2D polymeric structure is propagated within the layer by (a) the bridging Ca···O4–P2–O5···Ca and Ca···O1–P1–O2···Ca phosphonate groups running along the c-axis, and (b) the bridging/chelating Ca···O1–P1–O3···Ca phosphonate group running along the b-axis. The –N­(H)­CH3 aliphatic group extends in the interlayer region, along with the phosphoryl P2O6 moiety. The latter (one from each neighboring layer) participates in a hydrogen bonding interaction with the lattice water (O···O distance of 2.728 Å, Figure C).

Discussion

Structural Insights from Calcium Phosphonate Chemistry and Correlations with Consolidation Efficiency

The interactions between phosphonates and gypsum surfaces have been at the epicenter of interest of several studies. , Relevant reports come from the industrially important field of mineral scale inhibition. Aminomethylene (poly)­phosphonates (just like C1D) are used to combat gypsum scale in process industrial waters. In the context of this research, it is important to know which specific interactions of a phosphonate (or an amino-dimethylenephosphonate) moiety with a gypsum surface can induce substantial alterations. Such modifications may, in turn, modify and enhance the resistance of gypsum to deterioration. The crystal structure of gypsum (Figure A) reveals two important features: (a) all sulfate oxygens are bound to Ca2+ forming 4-membered chelating rings (Figure B), and (b) two water molecules (in a cis configuration) are bound to Ca2+, protruding into the interlayer space (Figure C). Hence, the questions posed are (a) can a phosphonate group form a 4-membered chelating ring with Ca2+ (like the one shown in Figure D), essentially replacing one sulfate group, and (b) can an amino-di­(methylenephosphonate) group form a 8-membered chelating ring with Ca2+ (like the one shown in Figure E) via the substitution of the Ca-bound water molecules? If these interactions occur, then the “anchoring” of the consolidant molecules on the gypsum surface can be envisioned that can explain their consolidating activity.

Calcium phosphonate structural chemistry can offer useful tools that allow relevant comparisons to be made between gypsum and the phosphonate fragment. Below, we are attempting a comparative structural analysis of key important features of several calcium phosphonate compounds, including the metric features (CaA–OA, CaB–OA, CaB–OB, P–OA, and P–OB bond distances and CaA–OA–CaB, OA–CaB–OB, OA–P–OB angles) of the moieties shown in Figure B,C. Despite the large number of structurally characterized Ca-phosphonate compounds, only published data were used of those compounds whose structures exhibit these specific binding modes, for a direct comparison with gypsum. Emphasis is given to the deviations of these metric features from those of gypsum (used as reference).

The data used are compiled in Tables S1 and S2 in the Supporting Information. Figure displays the deviations in the bond distances CaA–OA, CaB–OA, CaB–OB, P–OA, and P–OB from the corresponding metrics of gypsum (refer to Figure as a guide; the P–OA, and P–OB bond lengths are compared to the corresponding S–OA, and S–OB ones of the sulfate anion in gypsum, used as reference). The numbering of the Ca-phosphonate compounds refers to Tables S1 and S2 in the Supporting Information. Most of the CaA–OA bond distances (blue circles) are shorter than those in gypsum. The maximum deviation is −0.170 Å (compound 8). There are two exceptions of bond lengthening (compounds 1 and 5), with most pronounced elongation at +0.133 Å in compound 1). The bond distances CaB–OA, CaB–OB show some variations in Ca-phosphonate compounds (Table S1) with the largest at 0.326 Å, which is not uncommon in these compounds. Compared to the corresponding bond distances in gypsum, the CaB–OA distances (orange triangles) appear shorter (with one exception, compound 8, which shows an elongation of +0.311 Å), while the CaB–OB distances (green squares) are shorter for most cases (except compound 4 and 10, with the maximum deviation at −0.048 Å). The P–OA (red squares) and P–OB (yellow rhombs) bond lengths are invariably (and expectedly) longer than the corresponding S–OA, and S–OB ones in gypsum due to the differences in atomic radii of P and S (P has a larger atomic radius than S), differences in P–O and S–O bond order (higher in sulfate) and differences in electronegativity (S is more electronegative than P).

Figure displays the deviations of the CaA–OA–CaB, OA–CaB–OB, OA–P–OB angles from the corresponding metrics of gypsum (refer to Figure as a guide; the OA–P–OB angles are compared to the corresponding OA–S–OB ones of the sulfate anion in gypsum). The deviations of the CaA–OA–CaB angle are all in the “strained” domain, i.e., they are negative and range from −0.62° to −14.91°. The fact that the CaA–OA–CaB angles become more acute in Ca-phosphonates is consistent with the shortening of the CaA···CaB distance (from 4.058 Å in gypsum to ∼3.9 Å, on average, in Ca-phosphonates). The OA–CaB–OB angle deviations are all in the “relaxed” domain but show relatively small values (+1.35° to +4.85°). Lastly, the OA–P–OB angle deviations are also in the “relaxed” domain (except compounds 5 and 12, with deviation values −2.58° and −1.53°, respectively) and show relatively small values (+1.67° to +4.66°). Compound 8 is an exception, showing a deviation of +18.91°. Based on the data shown for compound 8 in Figures and , large deviation values are noted for this Ca–K-etidronate compound. This may be explained by the presence of the large sized and easily polarizable K+ cation (Ca/K ratio 1:1) that may distort several metric features in the structure and cause these high deviations.

CaA–OA–CaB, OA–CaB–OB, OA–P–OB angle deviations from gypsum in several Ca-phosphonate compounds. Figure must be used as a guide. The OA–P–OB angles are compared to the corresponding OA–S–OB ones of the sulfate anion in gypsum.

The above analysis strongly suggests that the amino-di­(methylenephosphonate) is an important structural feature in Ca-phosphonate chemistry that allows not only a plethora of binding motifs with Ca2+ cations but also allows specific coordination to Ca2+ that resembles that of the sulfate anion in gypsum. The similarities between the metric features (CaA–OA, CaB–OA, CaB–OB, P–OA, and P–OB bond distances and CaA–OA–CaB, OA–CaB–OB, OA–P–OB angles) of relevant Ca-phosphonate compounds with those of gypsum, as exemplified by the calculated deviations, point to the scenario that the amino-di­(methylenephosphonate) can “fit” onto the gypsum surface. This can be accomplished in two modes of operation: (a) a phosphonate group can form a 4-membered chelating ring with Ca2+ (Figure D), essentially replacing one sulfate group, and (b) an amino-di­(methylenephosphonate) group can form an 8-membered chelating ring with Ca2+ (Figure E) via the substitution of the Ca-bound water molecules. Notably, it has been reported based on geometrical arguments from molecular dynamics simulations, that substitution of sulfate by phosphonate in ettringite, a sulfate mineral with the formula Ca6Al2(SO4)3(OH)12 · 26H2O, is possible. Similar arguments were put forth for barium sulfate (Barite) , and calcium carbonate.

Although it is highly challenging to prove these binding modes on gypsum experimentally, it seems reasonable to assume that they could be operative in the presence of PHOS–POSS or PAPTS. Note that PHOS–POSS contains eight amino-di­(methylenephosphonate) groups, compared to only one in PAPTS. This is undoubtedly related to the superior consolidating activity of the former (∼×7 after 5 days and ∼×8 after 10 days, Figure ). PAPTS contains a reactive silanetriol functionality that may augment its consolidation activity through polycondensation to generate a “silicone” type product with an extensive Si–O–Si network within the gypsum pores. This mechanism of action has been proven for tetraethyl orthosilicate, 3-(trihydroxysilyl)­propyl methylphosphonate, and alkoxysilane-based polymers. The multifuctionality of PHOS–POSS is related to the great number of phosphonate groups (16 in total) that display proven affinity for gypsum surfaces.

The high affinity of the phosphonate group for metal ions and surfaces, especially when several of them are attached to the same molecule, has been extensively studied. This is why phosphonates are used in so many industrial and household applications, and, in particular, in pharmaceutical applications as antiosteolytic medications. The latter take advantage of the high affinity of bisphosphonates for bone. Hence, the superior consolidating action of PHOS–POSS can be correlated to the following features: (a) the presence of 16 phosphonate groups that anchor the molecule to the gypsum surface, (b) its large size (mean diameter of ∼21 Å) that can fill the nanopores in the gypsum interior, and (c) its flexible structure due to the aliphatic propyl groups. Notably, PHOS–POSS is not just a “filler”. The consolidation performance of OAP–POSS (lacking phosphonate groups), see Figures and , proves this point. OAP–POSS is of a similar size to PHOS–POSS and has –NH2 terminal groups that have no affinity for gypsum surfaces.

The intercalation of PHOS–POSS or PAPTS within the gypsum layers seems unlikely. This is based on the following arguments: (a) the powder XRD patterns of a pristine gypsum specimen and one that has undergone consolidation, are identical, meaning that no structural changes have been induced on gypsum. (b) The size of PHOS–POSS and PAPTS is too large to fit in the interlayer space of gypsum (shortest interlayer Ca···Ca distance = 5.903 Å). Hence, the consolidation scenarios discussed above (substitution of Ca-bound waters and/or replacement of a sulfate group) are solely surface phenomena that do not alter the gypsum bulk structure.

It is emphasized that the possible modes of action of these phosphonate consolidants (substitution of Ca-bound waters and/or substitution of a sulfate anion) is proposed and is based mainly on performance data and structural analogies. Additional and more specialized techniques are required to shed further light to its validity. These may include the use of radiolabeled compounds (consolidants or gypsum prepared in D2O, solid state NMR methodologies for 31P) or XPS and Raman or IR surface spectroscopies.

As noted in the Results section the consolidation behavior of PHOS–POSS and PAPTS changes in the presence of alkaline-earth metal ions, albeit in an opposite way. An enhancement is noted for PAPTS, but PHOS–POSS is compromised. Some possible explanations follow. Although these compounds share some structural similarities (e.g., PHOS–POSS can be envisioned as an “octamer” of PAPTS) their behavior as consolidants follows distinctly different pathways. PHOS–POSS is a “bulky” molecule and does not undergo any skeletal changes during consolidation, but it uses its phosphonate groups for surface coordination. PAPTS, on the contrary, is a “small” molecule and contains a polymerizable silanetriol unit. When PAPTS polymerizes within the stone pores, its phosphonate groups are available for surface complexation to gypsum, and for coordination of the added cations. Hence, a complex siloxane network forms due to Si–O–Si bonds and M–O­(P) bonds (M = Ca, Sr, Ba). On the other hand, PHOS–POSS does not possess any polymerizable moieties, so no polymeric network forms. The decline in consolidation performance of PHOS–POSS in the presence of Ca2+, Sr2+, or Ba2+ cations may be ascribed to the preference of the phosphonate groups to coordinate to the solvated cations that are abundant in solution, rather than interact with the gypsum surface. If such a scenario occurs, then some of the phosphonate groups are bound to the metal cations and are unable to act in a consolidating fashion.

It is worth mentioning that the consolidating performance of PHOS–POSS is dramatically superior (by a factor of several thousands) to commercially available consolidants, such as the well-known TEOS-based RC-70 (tetraethyl-orthosilicate), and the RC-90 silicone-type polymer blend (TEOS + methylphenyl silicone resin).

Drawing Parallels to Antiosteolytic and Mineral Scale Inhibition Action of Phosphonates

The antiresorptive activity of bisphosphonates (a.k.a. gem-phosphonates, or “dronates”) has been established for several decades based on chemical, clinical, and computational studies. Bisphosphonates are organic analogs of the naturally abundant pyrophosphate, with the general formula R-C­(OH)­(PO3H2)2 (R = organic fragment) and constitute a wide family of drugs for calcium related disorders (osteoporosis being a prominent case). Their principal structural feature is the positioning of two phosphonate and one –OH groups on a central C atom, on which the fourth substituent R is a functional organic moiety (R = methyl, aminoalkyl, pyridine, imidazole and others). Their mode of action is the strong anchoring of the bisphosphonate moiety onto the surface of hydroxyapatite (the major inorganic constituent of bone), with the –OH group playing an augmenting role.

On the other hand, phosphonates are extensively used as threshold crystal growth inhibitors for mineral scales, such as calcium carbonate, gypsum, strontium, and barium sulfates, and several metal sulfides. Relevant computational studies have also been reported. One study in particular invokes strong Ca2+ chelation on gypsum by polymeric surfactant additive molecules (not phosphonates). Here, as well, their mode of action is based on the interaction of the inhibitor molecule with the mineral scale surface through the strong binding of the phosphonate groups onto specific sites of the growing crystal. It is important to note that the phosphonate moieties act in a “promiscuous” fashion, as they can interact with a variety of mineral surfaces.

Given the profound differences in the crystal structures of these minerals (either natural or industrial) and their distinct surface chemistry, the essentially universal action of phosphonates is surprising. This could be related to some important structural and functional aspects of phosphonates: (a) the high affinity of the anionic phosphonate (–PO3 2–, or –PO3H–) group for the metal sites on the mineral surface, (b) the multitude of binding modes of the (nearly) tetrahedral phosphonate moiety (one –PO3 2– can bind up to nine metal ions in solution), (c) the synergistic action of several phosphonate groups on the same molecule backbone, and (d) the enhancing effect of other functional groups on the molecule. Based on the experimental data presented above, it appears that PHOS–POSS possesses all these attributes that make it a potentially ideal consolidant for archeological gypsum stones.

Conclusions

The above-described results and their interpretation lead to the following conclusions:

A highly functionalized, precisely defined multipodal silsesquioxane, PHOS–POSS, containing 16 phosphonate anchoring groups has been successfully synthesized and characterized (Figures and ).

A diphosphonate, PAPTS, containing a polymerizable silanetriol unit has been synthesized (Figure ). PAPTS is the main decomposition product of PHOS–POSS upon exposure of the latter at pH > ∼2.

PHOS–POSS is a potent consolidant for gypsum stones, as proven by drilling resistance measurements on artificial gypsum specimens. It achieved more than a 1000-fold increase in drilling resistance, while exhibiting high penetration depth (>40 mm) (Figures and ).

PAPTS, on the other hand, is a much weaker consolidant (enhancing drilling resistance by a factor of 2), most likely due to the low number of phosphonate groups (two). Notably, the presence of the autocondensable silanetriol moiety to generate Si–O–Si bonds in situ, offers little advantage (Figures and ).

Based on structural correlations between gypsum and a plethora of calcium phosphonate networks [including the compound Ca-methylamino-di­(methylenephosphonate), Ca–C1D (Figure )], it is proposed that the high consolidation efficiency of PHOS–POSS is ascribed to its multifunctional ability to either substitute the Ca-bound water molecules, or to randomly replace a sulfate group in the structure of gypsum (Figures and ), or to a combination of both.

The excellent consolidation performance of PHOS–POSS notwithstanding, there are several challenges in real-world implementation. So, these considerations must be taken into account by a professional conservator. Presently, it can be efficiently synthesized in gram quantities. This limits its application in rather small surfaces and not large building elements. Scalability is another issue related to engineering aspects of the synthetic methodology. The production cost is substantial, which may drop upon successful scale-up. In our work the immersion technique was followed, which is unsuitable for large objects and structures. For the latter, surface treatment approaches must be followed. Whether these are successful is an open question to be investigated. Last but equally important, is the long-term durability of the consolidant. This can be addressed either by field trials, or by accelerated aging experiments, simulating environmental conditions.

The quest for an ideal chemical consolidant is ongoing. Herein, we demonstrated that the careful design of a hexadeca-phosphonate molecular consolidant can offer attractive advantages in conservation science. PHOS–POSS could potentially be used in applications that require some kind of interaction with metal ions or mineral surfaces, such as metal ion sequestration for toxic or radioactive metals, dispersion and stabilization of solid slurries, just to mention a few. Such endeavors are underway in our laboratory.
