Machine learning for sudden cardiac death prediction among older adults using community-based electronic health records
Authors: Yue Li, Zihan Mei, Guolei Sun, Zhengkun Liu, Ji Li, Marcus Eng Hock Ong, Haojun Fan, Chunxia Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMachine learning (ML) models have shown good performance in predicting cardiovascular disease risk. However, the usefulness of ML models has yet to be fully elucidated for sudden cardiac death (SCD) risk using long-term follow-up electronic health records (EHRs). This study aimed to develop and validate ML models for SCD risk prediction among community-dwelling older adults using 6-year primary-care EHRs data.MethodsData were obtained from the Jinnan Study, an EHR-based cohort of older adults. A total of 30,535 individuals aged ≥ 65 years from three towns were included. The primary outcome was SCD. Candidate predictors included gender, age, heart rate, body mass index (BMI), waist, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), physical activity, current smoking status, diabetes, hypertension, fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), QTc prolongation and ST wave abnormality. We employed the data of one town as the training dataset (16,049 individuals), and the data of other 2 towns as the test dataset (14,486 individuals). The two feature selection strategies used were Cox proportional hazards model (Cox model) and random survival forests model (RSF model). Cox model, Fine-gray model, RSF model, lasso Cox regression model and boosted Cox regression model were fitted, optimized through 5-fold cross-validation using the training dataset, and externally validated on the test dataset. Model discrimination and calibration were assessed.ResultsWith a median follow-up of 5.67 years, 224 SCD events occurred. RSF outperformed other models, with a C-index of 0.820 in the training set and 0.757 in the test dataset (P < 0.001). Calibration plots indicated that most SCD events occurred in individuals within the highest deciles of predicted risk. Summary plots using the “survex” package were generated to enhance interpretability of the RSF model.ConclusionML models can significantly enhance the prediction of SCD risk using community-based EHRs. Our proposed risk model may enable the identification of high-risk individuals among older adults, facilitating targeted interventions and personalized care strategies. Future research should focus on integrating this model into routine primary care workflows and evaluating its effectiveness in real-world settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24724-7.

Introduction

Sudden cardiac death (SCD) is defined as an abrupt and unexpected loss of cardiovascular function, leading to circulatory collapse and sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) [1]. As a major cause of mortality and healthcare burden, SCD is responsible for over 4 million deaths annually worldwide [2, 3]. In China, approximately 1.03 million out-of-hospital cardiac arrests occur each year [4]. The risk of SCD increases exponentially with age, placing older adults at the highest risk [5].

Despite public health initiatives such as widespread cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) training and the deployment of automated external defibrillators, the incidence of SCD remains high, and survival rates among older adults are particularly low [6]. Notably, SCA is the first clinical manifestation of cardiovascular disease in up to half of SCD cases [7]. Although the incidence of SCD is relatively low at the individual level in the general population, the absolute number of cases is substantial among older adults [8]. Therefore, identifying individuals at elevated risk of SCD is crucial to guide primary prevention strategies in this vulnerable population [2].

Currently, the primary diagnostic tool for estimating the SCD risk among older adults with known heart disease is left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) [9]. However, LVEF assessment is impractical in primary care settings. To support evidence-based, risk-stratified prevention at the community level, there is a need for innovative, scalable tools that rely on easily obtainable patient information [10].

Previous studies, such as those by Brittany M. Bogle et al. and Rajat Deo et al., have developed community-based SCD risk prediction scores using Cox proportional hazards models in White and Black populations [11, 12]. However, it should be noted that the above studies were based on traditional cohort studies using traditional statistical models. With advances in computer science, machine learning (ML) has shown promising results in predicting cardiovascular outcomes based on electronic health records (EHRs) data, compared to traditional methods [13]. Nonetheless, most ML-based SCD risk models have been developed in disease-specific cohorts with short-term outcomes [13]. It remains uncertain whether ML models can make accurate SCD risk predictions using community-dwelling EHRs over the long term.

To address this gap, we developed and externally validated ML models for predicting SCD risk among community-dwelling older adults using EHRs with long-term follow-up. Our goal was to identify high-risk individuals to support targeted interventions and improve outcomes in this growing population.

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the updated guidance for reporting clinical prediction models that use regression or machine learning methods (TRIPOD + AI) [14].

Study population

Data for this study were derived from the Data Library of Jinnan study. The Jinnan study was designed as a retrospective, population-based cohort study using EHRs. This study used anonymized and encrypted EHRs of three towns (town a, town b and town c) including physical examination and cause-of-death surveillance data provided by the Tianjin Jinnan District Health Commission. These three towns were located in Jinnan District, Tianjin (117.41 N, 38.92 E). Detailed information about the data has been previously published [15].

According to the Tianjin Code for Basic Public Health Services, the national physical examination was recorded by township health centers based on the Community health service information system. This was done by primary medical and health institutions. Over 68% of older adults (≥ 65 years old) were included in this physical examination dataset every year. To obtain accurate outcomes for all older adults, the study linked physical examination data to cause-of-death surveillance data from the China national cause-of-death surveillance system. All deaths in the national cause-of-death surveillance system were reported online through the cause-of-death registration and reporting information system of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, which reviewed and verified the data reported by provinces and corrected any errors found.

A total of 34,939 older adults (≥ 65 years old) who underwent national physical examinations in 3 towns between January 2017 and December 2017 were included in this study. 4,404 older adults were excluded for missing key previous records of laboratory tests or electrocardiogram (ECG). After exclusion, 30,535 older adults were enrolled in the study. We used the data of town a as the training dataset, and the data of town b and c as the test dataset.

Sample size calculations

The annual incidence of SCD in China was estimated at 1.13/1000 in the general population [4, 16]. Assuming 18 candidate predictor variables and a conservative 15% of the maximal Cox-Snell R2, we estimated that the minimum sample size for fitting the regression models was 14,638 [17, 18]. There was no standard method for estimating the minimum sample size for ML models. The previous study suggest that some ML models may require much more data, compared with regression models [19].

Outcome definitions

Based on the criteria for SCD in Framingham study, SCD was defined as death caused by coronary heart disease (definite myocardial infarction, coronary insufficiency, or angina pectoris) within one hour of the onset of symptoms with no other probable cause of death indicated from cause-of-death surveillance data [11]. Suspected SCD events were adjudicated by a panel of three trained physicians who applied the SCD criteria, using cause-of-death surveillance data. The outcome used SCD events that occurred after the baseline (1 January 2017) and before 31 December 2022. Interrater reliability values were calculated using Cohen’s Kappa. The Cohen’s Kappa was above 0.7.

Candidate predictor parameters

We pre-identified four common categories of cardiovascular risk factors with 18 markers in total as the pool of candidate variables. The candidate variables were enrolled in this study based on demographics (including gender, age), risk factors (including heart rate, body mass index (BMI), waist, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), physical activity, current smoking status, diabetes and hypertension), serum measures (including fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), serum creatinine (Scr) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)), and Electrocardiograph (ECG) (including QTc prolongation and ST wave abnormality). The candidate predictor measurement is listed in the supplementary materials.

Missing data

Missing values in both the training and test datasets were imputed using the missForest data imputation algorithm (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Modeling strategy

Figure 1 shows the study flowchart. Two different feature selection strategies were used for model development: (1) The variables with P values less than 0.1 in univariate analysis of the Cox model were included in model development. (2) Feature selection was performed based on ranking possible predictors by their predictive importance using a random survival forests (RSF) model.

A flowchart describing the general framework of the study. Cox proportional hazards model (Cox model), random survival forests model (RSF model), lasso Cox regression model (Lasso Cox model), boosted Cox regression model (Boosted Cox model)

The risk of SCD in community elderly was assessed using regression models and ML models with two feature selection strategies. The regression models included Cox model and Fine-gray model using the training dataset. The ML models were constructed by RSF, Lasso cox regression (Lasso cox) and Boosted cox regression (Boosted cox). These models were widely used machine learning approaches for survival analysis [20, 21]. A brief description of each method is provided in the Supplemental Materials. SCD risk prediction models were developed from a training dataset. The final ML model was optimized using 5-fold cross-validation. The best hyperparameters of the ML models in this study were summarized in Supplementary Table 1. SCD risk prediction models were subsequently externally validated in a test dataset.

Model performances were evaluated from two perspectives: discrimination and calibration. The predictive accuracy and discriminative ability of models were determined by Harrell’s C-statistic and cumulative/dynamic time-dependent receiver operating characteristic curves (AUC) [22]. Each decile of participants was divided according to the mean predicted probability of SCD, and the mean predicted probability was compared with the observed Kaplan-Meier estimate of SCD for each decile. Additionally, given the variable nature of the data, we selected a community-based risk prediction model for American SCD as a reference (there are missing data of drug use in our dataset) [18]. In this study, two models were compared to determine the fairness: (i) Compared with the original model, the variables and coefficients were maintained; (ii) Compared with the recalibrated model, the recalibrated model was developed by replacing all the coefficients in the original model using a training dataset but preserving variables.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or the median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical variables were sorted by frequencies (percentages). The Student t test, the Mann-Whitney U test, or the χ2 test were applied to baseline characteristics comparison, as appropriate. Correlations between each feature were assessed by Spearman ρ coefficients. If the coefficient between 2 variables > 0.7, only one variable was included in the model. Cox model and Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed via the survival package. RSF model was conducted with the randomForestSRC package. Lasso Cox was implemented via the glmnet package. Boosted cox was performed via coxBoost package. The C-indexes were generated via the rms package. The cumulative/dynamic time-dependent AUC were generated via the timeROC package. Comparisons of C-indexes between different models were performed using the “compareC” package. The “survex” method was used to explain survival models via the survex package. The survex package focuses on the field of survival analysis and provides tools for interpreting survival analysis models. Unlike general-purpose interpretability methods, such as SHAP and LIME, the survex package offers specifically tailored explanations that incorporate the time dimension inherent in the survival models’ predictions [23]. All analyses were conducted by R version 4.3.2. A 2-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The training and test datasets consisted of 16,049 older adults from town a, and 14,486 participants from towns b and c, respectively. There were no significant differences in age or gender between the training and test datasets (P = 0.533 and P = 0.853, respectively). A significant difference was found between the training dataset and the test dataset in regard to risk factors, serum measurements, and ECG.

Feature selection

Two feature selection methods were applied: univariate Cox model and RSF model. Univariate Cox regression identified 11 statistically significant predictors: gender, age, heart rate, physical activity, smoke, diabetes, hypertension, FBG, Scr, QTc prolongation and ST wave change (Supplementary Table 2). Compared to non-SCD cases, individuals who experienced SCD were older, had a higher proportion of males, faster heart rates, lower levels of physical activity, more frequent smoking, greater prevalence of diabetes and hypertension, higher FBG, and more frequent QTc prolongation and ST-wave abnormalities. The RSF-based feature importance ranking identified the following 11 variables as the most predictive: age, diabetes, BMI, heart rate, Scr, BUN, FBG, TC, ST wave change, DBP and physical activity (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Derivation of prediction models

The performance of the models with 2 feature selection methods in training is summarized in Table 2. When the feature selection method was the Cox model, the C-index (indicating discrimination) for the Cox model, Fine-gray model, RSF, Lasso Cox and Boosted cox were 0.795, 0.795, 0.820, 0.769 and 0.769 in training dataset respectively, indicating excellent discrimination. Among the above approaches, RSF performed best (P<0.001). This was followed by the Cox model and Fine-gray model. When the feature selection method was the RSF, the C-indexes of the training were 0.783 for the Cox model, 0.784 for the Fine-gray model, 0.818 for RSF, 0.769 for the Lasso Cox and 0.769 for the Boosted cox. The predictive performance of the RSF was superior to other models in the training dataset (P<0.001). The time-dependent AUCs exceeded 0.7. Specifically, the RSF model (mean AUC = 0.807 and 0.848) outperformed Cox model (mean AUC = 0.772 and 0.760), Fine-gray model (mean AUC = 0.807 and 0.848), Lasso Cox (mean AUC = 0.759 and 0.759) and Boosted cox model (mean AUC = 0.746 and 0.746) at all time points (Fig. 2).

External validation of prediction models

Table 2 shows the performance of models with two different feature selection methods in the test dataset. When the feature selection method was the Cox model, the C-indexes of the training were 0.747 for the Cox model, 0.753 for the Fine-gray model, 0.755 for RSF, 0.724 for the Lasso Cox and 0.732 for the Boosted cox. The predictive performance of the RSF and Fine-gray models was superior to other models (P<0.001). When the Feature Selection method was the RSF, the C-indexes in the test dataset with the Fine-gray and RSF model were 0.745 and 0.757, respectively, outperforming the C-index in the test dataset. The time-dependent AUCs were close to or slightly above 0.7, indicating that these models had dependable abilities at predicting long-term and short-term SCD risk. In external validation, the RSF model (mean AUC = 0.805 and 0.790) outperformed Cox model (mean AUC = 0.787 and 0.770), Fine-gray model (mean AUC = 0.787 and 0.768), Lasso Cox (mean AUC = 0.763 and 0.763) and Boosted cox model (mean AUC = 0.699 and 0.699) at all time points (Fig. 2). Supplementary Fig. 5f-j and p-t demonstrate the calibration of models in the training dataset. There was a poor correlation between observed and predicted average risk for the Fine-Gray model, indicating poor calibration.

C-indices for the original community-based SCD risk prediction model was 0.692 in our dataset. The C-indexes for RSF with 2 feature selection methods were superior to the original community-based risk prediction model for SCD (ref) (P<0.001). The C-index of the recalibrated community-based risk prediction model for SCD was 0.781 in the training dataset and 0.744 in test dataset. There was no statistical difference in the C-index between the recalibrated community-based risk prediction model for SCD and RSF (P = 0.690), and the C-index for RSF was superior in test dataset (P<0.001), implying the superior performance of the RSF model.

Model explanation

Since clinicians were reluctant to accept a prediction model that was not directly explainable and interpretable, the “survex” method was utilized to interpret the output of the final model by calculating the contribution of each variable to the prediction. Global explanation described the overall functionality of the model. The time-dependent feature importance of the top 7 feature importance values was shown in Fig. 3. Physical activity was more critical in the early follow-up period. Diabetes and age became more relevant in later follow-up. When we utilized the RSF model to assess variable importance, our analysis also identified diabetes and age as the two most influential predictors of SCD risk (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Discussion

Key findings

This study demonstrates the feasibility and utility of developing a CD prediction model for older adults using routinely collected community-based EHRs. By identifying a set of relevant predictive features and evaluating 10 models, we found that the RSF-based model provided superior discrimination and calibration performance. These findings represent a significant step toward practical SCD risk stratification in older adults.

Utilization of electronic health records

EHRs have emerged as a valuable resource for risk prediction, offering a low-cost, timely, and scalable alternative to traditional cohort studies. They provide rich longitudinal data, capturing a wide range of individuals’ characteristics and clinical events that are essential for developing and validating predictive models [24]. In many countries, EHR systems are widely adopted in primary care, enabling continuous data collection across large, representative populations [25, 26]. To our knowledge, this study has the largest sample size among studies predicting SCD risk (N = 30,535), highlighting the potential of large-scale, community-based EHRs data in advancing cardiovascular risk prediction [27].

Research opportunities are provided by dynamic health information contained in structured EHRs, which can be used to construct and improve risk prediction models. Compared to classical risk models developed from cohort studies, EHR-based approaches offer several key advantages. These include larger sample sizes, more frequent data points, and the inclusion of diverse health parameters captured during routine care [28]. Moreover, EHR-based models are more readily implementable in workflows in community-based primary care settings, avoiding the gap between research and practice often seen with traditional models. Embedding the SCD risk assessment model of this study into the EHR system of primary medical institutions may allow for automated and cost-effective screening, which can facilitate timely decision-making and personalized care for the health risks of the elderly [29].

Predictors in sudden cardiac death

Effective prediction of SCD requires comprehensive input variables, including demographic characteristics, risk factors, laboratory biomarkers, and ECG features. Previous studies have demonstrated the utility of combining such variables to estimate SCD risk in community-based populations [27]. Our model incorporated the most significant predictors of SCD risk with 2 feature selection methods, which included gender, age, heart rate, physical activity, smoking, BMI, DBP, diabetes, hypertension, FBG, TC, Scr, BUN, QTc prolongation and ST wave change. The incidence of SCD in males was two to three times higher than in females [30]. The steepest SCD increase occurred in the elderly population [5]. Increased levels of physical activity, not smoking and appropriate BMI were associated with reduced cardiovascular disease risk [31, 32]. Blood pressure, blood sugar and blood lipid, and related disease history were recognized as important independent prognostic factors for SCD [11, 33]. Scr and BUN, as kidney function indexes, were associated with SCD risk [34, 35]. ECG was recognized as an important independent biomarker for SCD in the general population. ECG markers have been evaluated extensively since ventricular fibrillation (VF)/ventricular tachycardia (VT) is a potentially treatable and identifiable manifestation of SCD. Higher heart rate was associated with increased SCD risk, reflecting cardiac autonomic abnormality [36]. QTc prolongation and ST wave change reflect abnormal ventricular repolarization.

Sudden cardiac death risk prediction and utilization of machine learning

In this study, RSF model demonstrated superior performance in both discrimination and calibration compared to other ML models. Previous studies also have found that the RSF model performs best [20]. Among the models in this study, RSF demonstrated superior discrimination performance. This is likely due to its ability to model non-linear effects and complex interactions without relying on proportional hazards assumptions [37].

Implications in primary care practices

Predictions made by the ML algorithms are largely unexplainable, making it a “blackbox”. Community health service providers may be reluctant to use it as a result since they are hesitant to make medical decisions based on opaque information. This brings up another advantage of this study: we utilized the “survex” method to explain the ML models [23]. The “survex” method could explain time-dependent feature importance in survival analysis models.

Furthermore, our prediction model is designed for practical application. Moreover, this prediction model can be used on the web and embedded in existing EHRs systems. This enables real-time risk assessment and supports routine use in community-based primary healthcare settings.

Future research should focus on integrating the model into primary care EHR systems for routine screening of older adults. Risk-stratified interventions based on model outputs could improve prevention efficiency. Prospective studies are needed to evaluate the model’s real-world effectiveness in reducing SCD incidence.

Limitations

We should acknowledge several limitations of this study. First, our study is based on regional data, which may not fully represent the diversity of the Chinese population nationwide. Second, this study did not include other SCD prediction parameters, such as echocardiography. We plan to collect LVEF data to compare the predictive performance of the current model. Third, while discrimination was still excellent when validated, we observed less acceptable calibration using the original coefficients. In the validation population, recalibration achieved excellent results. As Harrell notes, developing a model with strong discrimination is more important than one with strong calibration, as models can usually be recalibrated for various populations [38]. Forth, in future research, we plan to incorporate an independent test dataset or multi-center data to further enhance the robustness and generalizability of our findings. Finally, the identification of SCD case is uncertain, as autopsy data are often not available in community population cohorts and there are no uniform criteria for determining SCD across epidemiological studies. In order to identify cases as accurately as possible, the definition of SCD for this study was based on the Framingham study. Moreover, suspected SCD events were adjudicated by three trained physicians.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and validated models for SCD risk prediction among community-dwelling older adults based on EHRs in China. The RSF model displayed excellent discrimination and calibration. The proposed model may be used to identify SCD high-risk in the older adults of community, which may inform efforts to optimize community health services. The future research should focus on integrating this model into routine primary care workflows and evaluating its effectiveness in real-world settings.
