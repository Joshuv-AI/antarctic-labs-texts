Civilian family members’ deaths and perceived causes in war-torn Tigray, Ethiopia: modified poisson regression

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCivilians suffer greatly from mortality as a result of armed conflicts.The magnitude of family members’ deaths and their perceived causes in households in war-affected settings of Tigray region of Ethiopia has not been investigated.MethodsThis study was part of a large integrated cross-sectional survey conducted in selected 13,915 households from 19 districts.The data analysis was done using STATA 17 versions software packages.We have fitted all the possible count regression models to discover the best fitted model. Log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criteria (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC) were also used to compare various candidate models. We found that the robust Poisson regression model better fits the mortality data. Adjusted Incidence Rate Ratio (AIRR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) at P < 0.05 was used to establish the statistical significance in multivariable robust Poisson regression to assess the association between independent variables and the out come variable.ResultsIn this study, from the total 13,915 participants, 5% (n = 699) of them experienced civilian family members’ war-associated death.Households with a disabled member (AIRR = 2.46; 95%CI: 1.80, 3.39), displaced from home (AIRR = 0.60; 95%CI: 0.50, 0.74,five and more family size (AIRR = 1.09; 95%CI: 1.04–1.14) and widowed marital status (AIRR = 3.72; 95%CI: 2.89–4.81),, and semi-urban place of residence (AIRR = 0.49; 95CI: 0.30, 0.82) were associated with the outcome variable .ConclusionCivilian family members’ death report in this study is high. Large family size, widowed marital status, not displacing from home, having a disabled member of the family, and rural residences were the identified factors associated with civilian’s death. In the context of the cessation of hostilities following the Pretoria Peace Agreement, sustained efforts from the government, humanitarian organizations, and the international community are essential .

Introduction

Armed conflict is a major driver of excess civilian mortality through both direct violence and indirect pathways, including displacement, food insecurity, collapse of health services, and disruption of essential infrastructure [1, 2].

Over the past decade, mortality in complex humanitarian emergencies has become an increasing focus of global public health research [1]. According to the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP), the number of armed conflicts worldwide has risen substantially in recent years, reaching 59 state based armed conflicts in 2023 and 61 conflicts in 2024, the highest levels recorded since systematic data collection began in 1946. A disproportionate share of these conflicts occurs in Sub-Saharan Africa, which currently hosts the largest number of active conflicts globally [3].

Africa continues to experience the highest burden of conflict-related morbidity and mortality compared to other regions [4]. Armed conflicts have affected nearly two dozen Sub-Saharan African countries over recent decades, contributing significantly to preventable deaths and long-term social instability [2, 4].

Empirical evidence from Syria, Israel–Gaza, Yemen, and northeast Nigeria demonstrates considerable variation in civilian mortality magnitude across settings, reflecting differences in conflict intensity, displacement patterns, and health system resilience [5–7].

In November 2020, armed conflict erupted in the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia, leading to a widespread humanitarian crisis. Emerging evidence has documented elevated maternal and neonatal mortality during the war period [8, 9].

However, previous studies have largely focused on specific population groups or facility-based outcomes and have not examined household-level civilian mortality patterns or perceived causes of death across the broader community. Importantly, there remains limited empirical evidence documenting the magnitude of civilian family member deaths and their perceived causes at the household level in Tigray.

In conflict settings, civil registration and vital statistics systems often collapse, resulting in substantial under reporting of deaths and absence of medically certified causes. In such contexts, household-reported or perceived causes of death provide critical community-level mortality intelligence. Unlike medically certified causes, which rely on clinical verification, perceived causes reflect household attribution based on lived experience and contextual understanding of the circumstances surrounding death. While subject to recall and reporting limitations, such data are essential in data-constrained environments and have been widely used in complex emergencies [10].

Household level analysis is particularly appropriate in armed conflict settings because the household represents the primary unit through which exposure to violence, displacement, economic hardship, and health system disruption is experienced. Examining mortality at this level allows identification of structural vulnerabilities and social determinants influencing civilian survival during war.

Despite the scale of the humanitarian crisis in Tigray, there is a paucity of rigorous, community-based evidence examining civilian family member deaths and their perceived causes. Generating such evidence is essential not only to address academic knowledge gaps but also to inform humanitarian prioritization, protection strategies, and post-conflict health system reconstruction.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the magnitude of civilian family members’ deaths and their perceived causes in war-affected Tigray, Ethiopia, and to identify associated household-level factors using robust Poisson regression modelling. Modified Poisson regression was employed because the outcome was not rare and the method provides directly interpretable incidence rate ratios, avoiding overestimation that may occur with logistic regression in cross-sectional count data [11].

Methods

Study area and period

This study was conducted in the Tigray region of northern Ethiopia as part of a large scale integrated health survey [12]. The integrated household survey was conducted between March and June 2023 following the cessation of active hostilities in Tigray. Respondents were asked to report deaths of household members occurring from November 2020, when the armed conflict began, until the date of the survey. The study area covered 19 districts (woredas) across multiple zones of Tigray and included both host community households and internally displaced persons (IDPs) residing in accessible settings.

Study design and population

The parent survey employed a community based cross sectional design. The source population comprised all households residing in selected districts during the study period. Eligible households included those with usual residents present at the time of the survey .

Sample size and sampling procedure

A total of 13,915 households were selected using a multistage probability sampling technique to ensure regional representativeness. In the first stage, districts were selected based on accessibility and security considerations. In the second stage, enumeration areas (or kebeles) were randomly selected within districts. Finally, households were selected using systematic random sampling from updated household lists.

Both host communities and IDP households were included to capture the heterogeneous effects of conflict exposure. However, households located in highly insecure and inaccessible areas particularly in the Western Zone of Tigray were excluded due to safety and logistical constraints. As a result, the findings may slightly underestimate the true magnitude of war associated civilian mortality in the region.

Data collection and analytical sample

Since this study was based on secondary analysis of data from the integrated health survey, Enumerators collected data on household deaths using a structured questionnaire, including the date, perceived cause, and circumstances of each death. Mortality measurement followed established methods for conflict settings, ensuring accuracy and comparability with other humanitarian surveys [13, 14].

Enumerators were trained to minimize recall bias by using household rosters and prompts for key events, and participants could pause or stop interviews to reduce emotional distress. To safeguard participants’ well being, the original survey protocol allowed respondents to pause or discontinue the interview at any time. In addition, referral mechanisms for psychological support were established for participants who experienced emotional distress during the interview process.

Following data collection, data entry, and cleaning, variables relevant to war associated civilian death status and its potential determinants were extracted from the parent dataset. The analytical sample for this study consisted of all households with complete information on mortality outcomes and key explanatory variables.

Study variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was defined as the number of civilian family members who died in each household due to conflict related causes from November 2020 until the survey date in 2023 .

Predictor variables

Although the parent survey was not originally designed around a single theoretical model, the selection of variables for this sub analysis was informed by established literature on conflict related mortality and social determinants of health. Variables were chosen based on prior evidence linking demographic characteristics, displacement, and service disruption to increased mortality risk in conflict settings.

Sociodemographic variables (Age, marital status, education levels, Family size and Residence ) ,health related (transportation to healthcare, nearby health post functionality, nearby health center/hospital functionality, and distances to healthcare facilities and centers (health posts and health centers/hospitals), Food related ( household food security, food accessibility in the last 24 h and household wealth index (income), participant type (host community and IDP), and disability status in a given household and war impact (exposure to combat situation). Causes of death were categorized as direct (e.g., gunshots, mass killings) and indirect (e.g., chronic illnesses, infectious diseases, starvation), following widely used conflict-mortality frameworks [15, 16]. Civilian war-associated death was defined as any death attributable directly or indirectly to the armed conflict during the study period.

Data management and analysis

The data analysis was done using, STATA 17 versions of software packages after being collected using ODK. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables and then the information was presented using tables and figures. Mean (λ) and variance were used to describe the count data. The likelihood ratio test was used to test the null hypothesis of no over-dispersion against the alternative hypothesis.

Although the mortality data showed a slightly higher variability than expected (variance to mean ratio = 1.12) and a large proportion of households with zero deaths (95.32%), we tested several statistical models that are commonly used for count data to identify the most appropriate one.

We compared different models, including standard Poisson, negative binomial, zero-inflated, and hurdle models. To determine which model performed best, we used statistical criteria such as the log-likelihood, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). In simple terms, the best model is the one with the highest log-likelihood and the lowest AIC and BIC values, as this indicates better fit with less unnecessary complexity.

After comparison, the robust Poisson regression model provided the best overall fit for our data (Table 1). Although the data showed many zero values, more complex zero-inflated or hurdle models did not improve the model performance. Additionally, the robust Poisson model allows direct estimation of Incidence Rate Ratios (IRRs), which are easier to interpret than the parameters produced by zero-inflated models.

The multi-stage model selection procedure

Since the outcome variable was the count of deaths per household, we included the natural logarithm of household size as an offset term in the regression model. This allowed us to estimate mortality rates per household member, accounting for differences in exposure due to household size.

To further ensure that the model was appropriate, we examined the residuals (the differences between observed and predicted values). The residuals were centered around zero and followed an approximately straight line in the Q–Q plot, indicating that the model assumptions were reasonably satisfied .

Univariable analysis

Variables with p < 0.25 in bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model to avoid omitting potentially important predictors, following standard methodological recommendations [17–19].

In the final multivariable analysis, we used Adjusted Incidence Rate Ratios (AIRR) with 95% confidence intervals to measure the association between predictor variables and the occurrence of death. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

The study examines various sociolect-demographic and healthcare characteristics to understand their impact on mortality in the war-affected region of Tigray. The analysis of household characteristics and their association with various factors in a war-affected region reveals significant insights. A total of 13,915 individuals participated in this study. Regarding the cause of death in each zone, the highest and lowest number of reported deaths were 192 and 56 in North West and Mekelle zones respectively.

The majority of the participants in this survey were adults (56.5%), with a small youth age group (2.7%). Early marriage before the age of 18 is 72.8%, and a low literacy rate, as 57.9% are unable to read or write. Educational attainment is limited, with only 5% having a college degree or higher. Most participants were married (72%), while 11.7% are divorced and 11.2% were widowed. Family sizes are mostly large, with 52.1% having five or more members. Additionally, 69.8% of the participants live in rural areas (Table 2).

Socio-economic and infrastructure related characteristics

According to this survey, a large portion of the population (65.9%) has to walk more than an hour to reach the nearest health center, and 99.7% face the same issue for health posts. While 93.4% have access to functional health centers, only 56.7% have a functional health post, and 58.9% lack public health transport. Regarding food security, households are split nearly evenly between secure (49.7%) and insecure (50.3%) food status, though most (98.4%) ate in the last 24 h. Income inequality is evident, with 63.7% classified as poor, and 56.1% of families having displaced members. Additionally, 8.4% of households report a member with a chronic disease, and 4.3% have a disabled member (Table 3).

Six hundred ninety-nine (5%) of the study participants experienced death of civilian family members. In the households, a family member’s civilian death was reported from 612 households with a maximum of four and a minimum of one death. Regarding zonal based death report, North West and Mekelle zones reported the highest and lowest deaths, with 192 and 56 deaths, accounting for 29.5% and 8.6% of the total deaths respectively. Causes of death

Direct war-related deaths included gunshot injuries and mass killings. Gunshot wounds were the leading cause of death, accounting for 226 deaths (30.8%), followed by mass killings, which contributed to 66 deaths (10.1%). Together, direct violent causes represented a substantial proportion of overall mortality during the conflict period.

Indirect war-related deaths included chronic illnesses, infectious diseases, starvation, and pregnancy and labor related complications, reflecting the broader disruption of health systems, food supply, and essential services. Chronic illnesses accounted for 136 deaths (19.8%), infectious diseases for 86 deaths (13.2%), and starvation for 38 deaths (5.3%). Additionally, 31 deaths (4.8%) were attributed to pregnancy- and labor-related complications. Other causes accounted for 110 deaths (16.9%) (Fig. 2).

Determinants of War-Associated Civilian Mortality Tigray region

Poisson regression was used to model the count of civilian deaths per household, an approach suitable for rare event count data in epidemiology [20, 21]. Crude IRRs were first estimated in bivariable analyses, and variables with p < 0.25 were included in the multivariable model. Adjusted IRRs with 95% confidence intervals were reported, controlling for potential confounders and the variables like: age, marital status, educational status, family size, residence, perceived food security, occupation, residence, electric supply, public transport access, perceived household income, and chronic disease in households or for the participants who gave the response were the candidate variables for the robust Poisson count regressions at p < 0.25 using Wald chi-square value.

The results of the robust Poisson regression analysis on the provided data set highlight several significant predictors of mortality of some selected variables in the war-affected Tigray region. Accordingly, in the final multivariable model, number of household members, displacement status, disability within the household, marital status, and place of residence were significant predictors of civilian family member deaths.

Households with five or more members had a significantly higher incidence of reported civilian deaths compared with those with fewer than five members (IRR = 1.09; 95% CI: 1.04–1.14), indicating a 9% higher incidence of death, after adjusting for other variables.

Marital status was also strongly associated with mortality. The expected number of deaths among households where the respondent was widowed was 3.72 times higher than among married respondents (IRR = 3.72; 95% CI: 2.89–4.81), suggesting a 272% higher incidence of reported deaths, controlling for other covariates.

Regarding displacement status, households that experienced internal displacement had a significantly lower incidence of reported deaths compared with non-displaced households (IRR = 0.60; 95% CI: 0.50–0.74), corresponding to a 40% reduction in the incidence of civilian deaths.

Similarly, households with at least one disabled family member had a markedly higher incidence of reported deaths compared to households without a disabled member (IRR = 2.46; 95% CI: 1.80–3.39), reflecting a 146% higher incidence of mortality, after adjustment.

Place of residence was also significant. Households residing in semi-urban areas had a significantly lower incidence of civilian deaths compared to rural households (IRR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.30–0.82), indicating a 51% reduction in mortality incidence (Table 5).

Discussion

Magnitude of Civilian Deaths and Causes of Mortality

We conducted an investigation into the perceived causes of civilians family member’s death report among households in the war-affected region of Tigray, Ethiopia. The 5% household reported civilian death in this study highlights the serious impact of the Tigray conflict. Although lower than some previous estimates, such as a diaspora survey reporting over 102,000 conflict-attributable deaths in Tigray [22], and higher mortality in other conflicts like Syria, Yemen, and northeast Nigeria [23–25], our finding still reflects a substantial humanitarian burden. Differences likely arise from study design, recall periods, and household versus community-level reporting. These results underscore the need for continued humanitarian assistance and health system recovery in the region.Gunshot, pregnancy and labor related, chronic disease and mass killing were the most mentioned perceived causes of mortality.

Our findings revealed that several factors, including a number of household members, displacement during war, existence of disabled family member, marital states, and semi urban residence were the major predictors.

The recent armed conflict in Tigray was characterized by widespread disruption of health services, destruction of infrastructure, mass displacement, and severe food insecurity, creating conditions that intensified both direct and indirect mortality risks. In addition, cultural and social structures, including patriarchal household systems and limited social protection mechanisms, exacerbated the vulnerability of widowed and disabled households.

Household vulnerability

Those households with greater than or equal to 5 family size were more likely to report family members’ death compared to households having less than 5 family size. It is similar with previous studies large family size was associated with mortality [26]. The possible reason might be a very specific context of harsh economic conditions and limited access to health facilities. In addition, households with more members are less likely to have access to sanitary sewage systems and clean water facilitates. Furthermore, in the setting of an armed conflict, large families may find it difficult to flee the front lines of battle without losing every member.Household size was measured at the time of the survey. We acknowledge that this may not perfectly reflect the population at risk during the mortality recall period, as household composition may have changed due to deaths, displacement, or migration.

Similarly, this study examined how marital experiences might condition the effects on individual mortality, expected number of deaths in family members who were widowed marital states increased family mortality compared to those married. The loss of a spouse, often the primary income earner and household protector in conventional or patriarchal societies, can leave the family economically insecure and physically unprotected. This increases exposure to hunger, disease, and unsafe environments, all of which elevate mortality risk. Widowed households may also face social isolation, stigma, and limited access to humanitarian aid, as relief programs sometimes prioritize male-headed or larger families. In contrast, married households benefit from shared decision-making, pooled resources, and broader support networks, buffering them against the direct and indirect impacts of conflict. Together, these factors help explain the substantially higher mortality observed among widowed families in this post-conflict setting [31–32].

Disabled individuals often encounter mobility limitations could indeed have hindered individuals with disabilities from leaving conflict-affected areas. Research from multiple conflict zones shows that people with physical or sensory impairments often face serious barriers to evacuation during violence, including inaccessible terrain, lack of assistive devices, and destruction of support systems. These barriers can prevent timely escape and increase the risk of harm and entrapment during mass displacement or attack [35].

During armed conflict, disruptions to health services, transportation, and humanitarian aid can disproportionately affect disabled household members, increasing their risk of malnutrition, untreated illness, and mortality. Additionally, households caring for disabled members may face greater economic strain, limiting their ability to secure food, medicine, or safe shelter, which contributes further to elevated mortality in this group.

Displacement dynamics

Interestingly, our analysis showed that displaced households had a lower incidence of reported civilian deaths compared with non-displaced households, which appears counterintuitive. Our finding is different from other studies of excess mortality of internally displaced persons [42–44]. This finding may reflect selective survival, as households able to move away from high-risk areas could have avoided direct attacks, whereas non-displaced households remained exposed to greater violence. Additionally, under reporting among displaced populations may have contributed, since household members could have been separated or lost contact, leading to incomplete reporting of deaths. Displaced households may also have benefited from humanitarian assistance, such as food, medical care, or protective services in camps or settlements, which could have temporarily reduced mortality from indirect causes such as starvation or disease. Finally, timing and sampling effects may play a role, as the survey could have reached displaced households after the peak period of conflict, while non-displaced households experienced higher mortality earlier, resulting in lower observed deaths among the displaced at the time of data collection.

These findings have important policy and humanitarian implications. Households that are widowed, have disabled members, or are located in rural areas are disproportionately affected, highlighting the need for targeted protection of vulnerable civilians in conflict affected settings.Ensuring equitable access to humanitarian aid, including food, healthcare, and psychosocial support, is critical for mitigating indirect mortality among high-risk households. Furthermore, the results underscore the urgency of restoring and strengthening local health systems, particularly maternal and child health services, nutrition programs, and emergency care, to reduce preventable deaths in post-conflict communities.Tailored interventions that address both the direct and indirect consequences of armed conflict are essential for rebuilding resilience and safeguarding .

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study used advanced methods of analysis by comparing different models to identify the best-fitted model among which Robust Poison regression was identified. Furthermore, as of our best knowledge, this is the first study to report perceived causes of mortality in the study setting which is critically important to design interventions for health promotion and disease prevention.

Limitations of the study

However, as we did not conduct a census in the region, this result does not generalize the mortality magnitude of the region during the mentioned period. Furthermore, due to security reason, we excluded areas including the Western zone of the region where many more civilian massacred is reported which may underestimated the magnitude of civilian’s death report. Therefore, the interpretation of this result should take this limitation into account. the study relied on retrospective self-reported household mortality, which may be subject to recall bias, as respondents might not accurately remember the timing or circumstances of deaths during the conflict period. However, efforts were made to minimize this by using household rosters and key event calendars during data collection.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the severe impact of the conflict in Tigray, with 5% of households experiencing war-associated civilian deaths. Key household-level predictors of higher mortality included larger family size, widowed marital status, and presence of disabled members, while semi-urban residence was protective. Displacement was unexpectedly associated with lower reported deaths, highlighting a critical, policy-relevant finding that mobility and access to humanitarian support may mitigate mortality risks. These findings underscore the uneven effects of conflict on civilian households and provide new evidence linking household characteristics to mortality in a post-conflict setting. They can inform post-conflict recovery strategies, targeted humanitarian interventions, protection of vulnerable populations, and restoration of health services, as well as guide ongoing monitoring to reduce preventable deaths.
