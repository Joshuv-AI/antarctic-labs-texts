Association between frailty and gout in middle-aged and older adults: A nationwide cross-sectional and Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yuan Sun, Wenli Liu, Delun Li, Zhuo Cen, Yaohua Shen, Tingxiu Zhao, Zhaoyu Lu, Xusheng Liu, Xi Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
With the aging population, frailty is becoming increasingly prevalent among older adults, yet its association with gout, a common form of arthritis affecting middle-aged and older adults, remains unclear. This study investigated the frailty-gout relationship through a nationwide cross-sectional analysis combined with Mendelian randomization (MR). Data from 22,324 adults aged 40 and above in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 2007–2018) were analyzed utilizing survey-weighted logistic regression models. Genome-wide association study data from UK Biobank, TwinGen, and FinnGen were utilized to explore genetic correlations and causality. The cross-sectional study indicated a significant relationship between pre-frail (odds ratio [OR] = 2.33, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.76–3.08, P < .0001) and frail (OR = 4.63, 95% CI = 3.67–5.84, P < .0001) individuals and gout. This association persisted after comprehensive covariate adjustment (pre-frail OR = 2.13, 95% CI = 1.59–2.85, P < .0001; frail OR = 4.11, 95% CI = 3.16–5.35, P < .0001). The interaction effect between frailty and gout was significantly influenced by race/ethnicity (P interaction = .0046) and gender (P interaction = 6.72 × 10−6). Linkage disequilibrium score regression analysis demonstrated a moderate yet significant genetic correlation between the frailty index and gout (rg = 0.319, standard error (SE) = 0.042, P = 2.19 × 10−14). MR analysis confirmed a causal link between frailty index and gout (OR = 2.29, 95% CI = 1.45–3.62, P < .001), while reverse MR analysis indicated no causality. These findings elucidated frailty as a risk factor for gout, underscoring the necessity for uric acid monitoring to mitigate gout risk in frail individuals, potentially informing sustained healthcare initiatives for gout in middle-aged and older adults.

1. Introduction

With the global population aging, the proportion of individuals aged 60 and over is projected to increase from 12 to 22% between 2015 and 2050, leading to a rise in age-related diseases.[1,2] To mobilize various sectors to promote longer and healthier lives, the United Nations has designated 2021 to 2030 as the “Decade of Healthy Ageing.”[2,3] As an aging-related syndrome, frailty is marked by diminished functional reserves and increased vulnerability to stressors with subsequent adverse outcomes including higher healthcare needs, disability, and even mortality,[4] affecting approximately 12% of older adults according to a comprehensive review of data from 62 countries.[5] The widespread presence and severity of frailty among older adults impose a significant burden on families and the socio-economy. Its etiology is multifaceted, involving declines in various physiological systems, such as endocrine, immune, and musculoskeletal functions.[6,7] Although significant advancements in epidemiological and biological research that has improved our comprehension of the negative consequences of frailty, effective strategies for its prevention and long-term care are still insufficient.[8] Therefore, insights into the interconnections between frailty and its adverse outcomes are crucial for tailored management and early intervention for older individuals at risk.

As one of the most prevalent inflammatory arthritis conditions, gout is characterized by urate crystal deposition in joints and soft tissues, leading to intense pain and inflammation. Accumulating evidence suggested a possible link between frailty and gout, potentially through mediators like persistent inflammation and renal impairment.[9–11] However, the detailed association between frailty and gout has not been thoroughly investigated.

In this study, we initially investigated the relationship between frailty and gout via a cross-sectional analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) dataset. Subsequently, we utilized linkage disequilibrium (LD) score regression (LDSC) to assess the genetic correlation and Mendelian randomization (MR) to explore the causal link between frailty index and gout. The NHANES is a critical cross-sectional study that collects health and nutritional information from the United States population. Its data is extensively utilized, propelling advancements in epidemiological research and the development of public health policies. Nevertheless, NHANES is a cross-sectional study, causality cannot be inferred. Drawing on data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), methods such as LDSC and MR analyses are commonly utilized in the realm of medical research. LDSC assesses genetic correlations without the confounding impact of sample overlap.[12] Furthermore, MR analysis leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables to ascertain causal links between traits, while addressing confounders and reverse causality.[13]

2. Materials and methods

The study comprised 2 phases as shown in Figure 1. Initially, a cross-sectional analysis was performed to evaluate the association between frailty and gout in adults aged 40 years and above, drawing upon a comprehensive dataset from NHANES. Subsequently, we investigated the genetic correlation and causal link between frailty and gout through LDSC and bidirectional MR analyses, respectively.

2.1. Cross-sectional analysis

2.1.1. Data source and study population

Our cross-sectional investigation leveraged data from NHANES, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey of the noninstitutionalized civilian population in the United States. With the objective of comprehensively assessing the health and nutritional status of Americans, NHANES employs a multi-stage, stratified, and cluster sampling methodology to select a sample that reflects the national demographic and collecting data by personal interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Thorough information about the NHANES methodology can be available in the relevant documentation.[14] The Ethical Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics has approved the NHANES survey protocols.[14] Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to participation.

We extracted data for participants aged 40 and above from NHANES survey cycles incorporating 2007 to 2008, 2009 to 2010, 2011 to 2012, 2013 to 2014, 2015 to 2016, and 2017 to 2018. Then we used the Multivariate Imputation by Chained Equations package (version 3.15.0) to perform multiple imputation for addressing the issue of missing data. The analysis was conducted using R Studio (version 4.3.1), developed by Posit PBC (Boston).

2.1.2. Frailty index and gout

Following the protocol established by Searle et al,[15] we constructed a frailty index based on criteria that should be included: health deficits that accumulate with age, prevalence not approaching 0% or 100%, coverage across multi-system, and documentation in no <80% of the study participants to minimize bias from missing data. A severity score between 0 and 1 was assigned to each deficit, allowing for the accommodating of both categorical and continuous variables. We referred to the research conducted previously to define the variables in the frailty index and their corresponding scores.[16,17] The comprehensive index involved 49 deficits spanning multiple systems, comprising cognitive function, dependency, mood disorders, comorbidities, healthcare utilization, physical performance and anthropometry, and laboratory findings (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q586).[16] The frailty index value is determined by the ratio of the individual’s deficits to the total possible deficits. In the study, gout was identified through self-reported physician diagnosis in the NHANES questionnaire, wherein participants were asked, “Doctor ever told you that you had gout?” This methodology aligned with previous epidemiological studies utilizing NHANES data.[18] Validation studies suggested that self-reported physician-diagnosed gout was a reliable tool for epidemiological research, demonstrating 84% sensitivity and good agreement with gout-specific medications and hospital discharge diagnoses.[19]

2.1.3. Covariates

The sociodemographic data encompassed age, gender, race/ethnicity, family poverty income ratio (FPIR), education level, and marital status. FPIR was computed by comparing the annual family income with the poverty threshold, serving as an income indicator. Race/ethnicity categories included Mexican American, non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, Other Hispanic, and Other Races (including Multiracial). Educational level was categorized as follows: below ninth grade, 9th to 11th grade (including 12th graders without diplomas), high school graduate/GED or equivalent, some college or AA degree, and college graduate or above. Marital status was classified as living with partner, divorced, married, never married (single), separated, and widowed.

Besides, the additional covariates considered in this study included alcohol consumption and smoking. In term of alcohol consumption, individuals were categorized as never (fewer than 12 drinks lifetime), former (at least 12 drinks in the past years but none in the last year), mild (at least 1 drink daily for women or at least 2 drinks daily for men), moderate (at least 2 drinks daily for women, at least 3 drinks daily for men, or binge drinking at least 2 days monthly), and heavy (at least 3 drinks daily for women or at least 4 drinks daily for men, or binge drinking on at least 5 days monthly). We classified smokers into 3 groups: now (those who have smoked 100 or more cigarettes and continue to smoke), former (those who have smoked 100 or more cigarettes but have quit), or never (those who have smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes).[20,21]

2.1.4. Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into 3 groups based on their frailty index scores, referring to previous studies: frail (>0.21), pre-frail (0.10–0.21), and non-frail (≤0.10).[22] For the data analysis based on frailty status, continuous variables were reported as mean values with corresponding 95% CIs, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies, accompanied by proportions within the entire population. To evaluate continuous and categorical data, we employed a survey-weighted one-way analysis of variance and a survey-weighted χ2 test, respectively.

To investigate the association between frailty and gout, we utilized survey-weighted logistic regression with stepwise adjustments. Initially, the model was unadjusted. Subsequent adjustments considered age, gender, and race/ethnicity, whereas the final model further included marital status, education, income, as well as smoking and alcohol consumption patterns. We also performed secondary interaction analysis to discern whether this association varied by age, gender, and race/ethnicity. Hierarchical survey-weighted logistic regression models were applied when significant interaction effects emerged. Multicollinearity was assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIFs), with adjusted GVIF values (corrected for degrees of freedom) below 2.5 indicating acceptable collinearity among predictors.

Our study utilized the complex, multi-level sampling framework of NHANES, incorporating the appropriate primary sampling units, stratification, and sampling weights. We performed two-tailed statistical analyses, considering results significant if the P-value was <.05.

2.2. GWAS based analysis

2.2.1. Data source

In this study, we identified frailty-associated single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) via a comprehensive GWAS meta-analysis carried out by Atkins et al[23] The analysis encompassed data of 164,610 European participants from the UK Biobank (Stockport, United Kingdom), and 10,616 individuals in the TwinGene (Swedish Twin Registry, Stockholm, Sweden) project. The frailty index, capturing the cumulative effect of 49 health deficits across a lifespan, serves as a validated measure of frailty widely used in clinical research and practice.[23]

Summary statistics for gout from the FinnGen consortium (Release R10), comprising 9568 cases and 262,844 controls.[24] Gout diagnoses followed the criteria of International Classification of Diseases, 10th Edition (ICD-10), with the code M10. Ethical approval for our study was waived, as the original studies had already acquired approval for each GWAS dataset.

2.2.2. Instrumental variable selection

Initially, we filtered SNPs for significant genome-wide associations with exposures (P < 5 × 10−8). Subsequently, these SNPs underwent LDlinkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping utilizing PLINK v1.9, setting a stringent LD threshold (R2 < 0.001) across a 10,000 kb window.[25,26] To address biases from weak instrumental variables, we evaluated the F-statistics of the SNPs and excluded those possessing an F-statistic under 10. SNPs directly related to the outcome with a P-value less than 5 × 10−5 were also eliminated. We further applied MR Steiger filtering to preclude potential reverse causality.[27] For exposure-related SNPs absent in the outcome dataset, we identified suitable proxy SNPs with an LD R2 of at least 0.8 to serve as proxies if applicable.[28]

2.2.3. Statistical analysis

LDSC approach facilitates the estimation of SNP-based heritability and the genetic correlation between 2 traits. It was utilized to assess the genetic correlation (rg) between frailty and gout.

Our MR analysis utilized bidirectional MR to assess frailty both as a risk factor for gout development and as a consequence in gout patients, aiming to clarify the causal link between frailty index levels and gout. The MR analysis primarily utilized the fixed-effect Inverse variance weighted (IVW) method.[29] We then assessed the heterogeneity using Cochran Q statistic and significant heterogeneity was indicated by a P-value <.05. In the presence of heterogeneity, estimation of the effects was re-performed using the IVW method with a multiplicative random-effects model. To assess potential biases due to horizontal pleiotropy, we implemented several well-established sensitive methods, including the weighted median, MR-Egger, weighted mode, simple mode, and MR-Pleiotropy Residual Sum and Outlier (MR-PRESSO). Furthermore, we applied MR-Egger intercept analysis to detect horizontal pleiotropy, where a nonzero intercept and P-value < .05 indicate pleiotropy.[30] To assess the robustness of our findings, a leave-one-out (LOO) sensitivity analysis was conducted to check if any single SNP drove the overall effect.[28] In the MR study, the results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) for gout outcomes and as beta coefficients (β) for frailty index outcomes. To calculated statistical significance, two-tailed tests were utilized with an alpha level of 0.05. The statistical analyses were conducted employing R software (version 4.2.2; Posit PBC). Finally, post hoc statistical power was calculated using an online tool for MR studies with binary outcomes (https://sb452.shinyapps.io/power/). The analysis included the following parameters: exposure GWAS sample size (N = 175,226; UK Biobank and TwinGene), outcome GWAS data (9568 gout cases and 262,844 controls from FinnGen; case–control ratio 1:27), SNP-based heritability of the frailty index (R2 = 0.11),[23] and a significance level of α = 0.05.

3. Results

3.1. Association between frailty and gout

Our analysis encompassed data from 22,324 NHANES participants, which included 10,831 males and 11,493 females. Relative to the non-frail group, individuals with higher frailty levels were predominantly older, more likely female, and had lower levels of educational attainment and income; furthermore, they had a higher propensity for smoking (Table 1). Participants exhibiting higher frailty levels demonstrated a significant association with gout compared to non-frail individuals. The association was apparent in pre-frail (OR = 2.33, 95% CI = 1.76–3.08, P < .0001) and frail (OR = 4.63, 95% CI = 3.67–5.84, P < .0001) individuals, persisting after controlling for age, gender, and race/ethnicity (pre-frail OR = 2.15, 95% CI = 1.61–2.86, P < .0001; frail OR = 4.05, 95% CI = 3.17–5.19, P < .0001). The association remained significant after comprehensive adjustment (pre-frail OR = 2.13, 95% CI = 1.59–2.85, P < .0001; frail OR = 4.11, 95% CI = 3.16–5.35, P < .0001) (Table 2). GVIF analysis confirmed no evidence of problematic multicollinearity. In the fully adjusted model, all adjusted GVIF values were well below the threshold of 2.5 (frailty level: 1.25; age: 1.47; gender: 1.73; race/ethnicity: 1.28; education level: 1.27; marital status: 1.27; poverty income ratio: 1.59; smoking: 1.45; alcohol consumption: 1.32), supporting the adequacy of the model specification and the reliability of effect estimates.

Age did not significantly impact the frailty-gout association (P interaction = .412).

3.2. Genetic correlation

The LDSC study indicated a moderate yet significant genetic correlation between frailty and gout, with rg values of 0.319 (standard error [SE] = 0.042, P = 2.19 × 10−14). This genetic correlation suggested that approximately 32% of the genetic factors influencing frailty overlap with those affecting gout risk, indicating shared biological pathways between these conditions.

3.3. Causal relationship between frailty and gout

The MR-Egger regression intercept exhibited no substantial evidence of directional pleiotropy (P = .906), and the genetic variants showed no significant heterogeneity (Cochran Q = 15.77, P = .202). Using the fixed-effects IVW model, we identified that a genetically predicted increase in frailty was linked to a significantly elevated risk of gout (OR = 2.29, 95% CI = 1.45–3.62, P < .001). This association was replicated by the weighted median method (OR = 2.31, 95% CI = 1.26–4.23, P = .007) and the MR-PRESSO analysis (OR = 2.29, 95% CI = 1.45–3.62, P = .004) (Fig. 2; Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q586).

Among the 13 SNPs analyzed, 5 (rs12739243, rs2071207, rs2396766, rs583514, rs9275160) demonstrated a significant association with an increased risk of gout. Furthermore, the LOO analysis indicated that the overall effect was not driven by single SNP (Figs. S1 and S2, Table S5, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q586). Post hoc power calculation confirmed that our MR analysis had 100% statistical power to detect the observed causal association between frailty and gout risk, highlighting that the study was adequately powered for the primary analysis, due to the large-scale consortium data and substantial effect size.

3.4. Causal relationship between gout and frailty

The MR-Egger regression intercept did not indicate significant directional pleiotropy (P = .575). However, substantial heterogeneity was found among the genetic variants (Cochran Q = 25.49, P = .008) (Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q586). Therefore, we used the multiplicative random-effects IVW model, which demonstrated no significant causal relationship between genetically predicted gout and increased frailty risk (β = 0.005; 95% CI = −0.013 to 0.023; P = .590) (Fig. 2). Furthermore, none of the SNPs demonstrated a significant association with an increased risk of frailty (Figs. S3 and S4, Table S6, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q586).

4. Discussion

In the present study, we systemically explored the association and causality between frailty and risk of gout, utilizing both nationwide cross-sectional data from the NHANES, combined with genetic correlation and MR analysis. This study revealed several key findings. Firstly, the cross-sectional study demonstrated a significant correlation between frailty and gout. Individuals classified as pre-frail or frail exhibited a significantly higher odds of gout, even after adjustments. Second, race/ethnicity and gender significantly influenced the association between frailty and gout. Additionally, the LDSC analysis indicated a genetic correlation between the frailty index and gout. The MR study identified a causal relationship between a higher frailty index and elevated risk of gout, while no causality between gout and increased frailty.

The association between frailty and the risk of gout can be explained by several interrelated factors, with abnormal serum uric acid (UA) levels being a primary consideration. Frailty fundamentally involves multi-system decline, with renal function deterioration being a key component. Reduced glomerular filtration rate and tubular dysfunction in frail individuals directly impair UA excretion through decreased activity of renal urate transporters, leading to hyperuricemia and subsequent gout risk.[31] This mechanism is particularly relevant given that approximately 70% of UA elimination occurs via renal excretion. Besides, multimorbidity, present in 99.8% of frail older adults, further compounds gout risk.[32] Conditions like hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease, along with their treatments, disrupt UA metabolism through diverse mechanisms: kidney disease impairs UA excretion; hypertension and diabetes contribute via insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, increased purine production, reduced excretion.[33–35]

The progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, a hallmark of frailty, increases cellular turnover and purine nucleotide degradation.[36] Muscle atrophy releases intracellular purines, which are metabolized to UA via xanthine oxidoreductase. Simultaneously, reduced physical activity in frail individuals further compounds this effect by decreasing UA utilization and promoting its accumulation. Elevated serum UA levels lead to urate crystal deposition, triggering acute inflammation and gout attacks.[37] The frailty-gout link is also significantly influenced by inflammation. Frail and pre-frail older adults exhibited elevated levels of inflammatory markers, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, factor VIII, and increased leukocyte counts.[38,39] This inflammatory milieu primes the NLRP3 inflammasome, creating a state of heightened sensitivity to urate crystal deposition. When hyperuricemia occurs in this pre-inflammatory context, the threshold for gout attacks is substantially lowered, explaining the increased gout risk in frail individuals.[40] Additionally, frail individuals often take multiple medications, and long-term use of medications, particularly diuretics and low-dose aspirin, can also raise serum UA levels by enhancing reabsorption or reducing excretion, while polypharmacy itself increases frailty risk.[41] Lastly, nutritional decline, marked by reduced appetite and gastrointestinal dysfunction, leads to protein-energy malnutrition and muscle loss, all of which further elevate UA levels.[42]

Genetic correlation analysis using LDSC revealed a moderate but significant association between the frailty index and gout, underscoring shared biological underpinnings. This overlap is largely attributable to variants in inflammation- and aging-related pathways, particularly those involving the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, which regulates age-associated inflammation and frailty.[43,44] Moreover, genetic variation in renal urate transporters impaired urate excretion while contributing to metabolic dysfunction linked to frailty.[31] Additional shared loci implicated in purine metabolism, mitochondrial function, and immune regulation may concurrently influence muscle decline in frailty and urate handling in gout.[9] Collectively, these findings highlighted a common genetic architecture through inflammatory, metabolic, and aging pathways that mechanistically link frailty and gout.

Bidirectional MR analysis identified a unidirectional causal relationship, indicating that the shared genetic architecture contributes to gout primarily through frailty-related processes. Post hoc power analysis indicated that our study had > 80% power to detect clinically meaningful effects in the reverse direction, suggesting that the nonsignificant reverse MR finding is unlikely to be merely due to limited statistical power. Instead, it more plausibly reflects the absence of a substantial causal effect of gout on frailty. This may be explained by the episodic and treatable nature of gout, which lacks the sustained systemic stress required to drive frailty, a progressive multi-system aging syndrome. Frailty instead reflects fundamental biological processes such as immune senescence and chronic inflammation, whereas gout primarily affects joints. In addition, heterogeneity in gout presentation (attack frequency, severity, treatment response) may have attenuated causal signal detection, further supporting that frailty more plausibly predisposes to gout rather than the reverse.

These findings underscored the importance of incorporating frailty assessment into clinical risk warning systems for gout. For middle-aged and older adults with frailty, especially those with metabolic conditions, early targeted interventions, such as nutritional support, inflammation control, medication optimization, and UA monitoring, offer a scientific basis for ongoing preventive care to reduce gout risk. Future research on frailty-gout comorbidity should focus on inflammatory regulation or pathways related to UA metabolism to support more effective prevention and treatment strategies.

Our findings indicated a more pronounced correlation between frailty and gout among non-Hispanic Black, non-Hispanic White, and Other Hispanic individuals. This may reflect the interplay of genetics, lifestyle, socioeconomic status, and healthcare access.[45] Although UA-related loci show similar effect sizes and shared structures across ethnic groups, population-specific genetic variants still exist.[46,47] Lifestyle factors – such as diet, alcohol use, and adherence to healthful eating patterns – also vary by race.[48,49] Furthermore, disparities in healthcare access contribute to unequal use of frailty- and gout-related treatments and services, reinforcing these differences.

Our study found that frail females had a higher risk of developing gout, suggesting that the frailty-gout link could potentially influence by complex biological and environmental factors. Biologically, sex hormones play a pivotal role: androgens increase serum UA production, whereas estrogens reduce serum UA by enhancing transporters like ATP binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2) and solute carrier family 2 member 9 (SLC2A9), and mitigate gout risk factors like renal decline and hypertension.[50] Consequently, gout in females often emerges postmenopause, aligning its incidence in older females more closely with that in males.[51] In postmenopausal females, increased markers of fibrin turnover and fibrinolysis have been reported,[52] signaling heightened inflammation, and studies have suggested a more significant frailty-inflammation link in females than males.[53] Genetically, ABCG2 and PDZ domain containing 1 (PDZK1) polymorphisms are linked to hyperuricemia risk in males, and genome-wide studies revealed more gout-associated loci in males (247 loci in males vs 14 in females).[54] Environmentally, females may have higher serum UA due to thiazide diuretics for kidney disease, whereas men are more prone to gout from higher intake of red meat, seafood, and alcohol.[55,56] These multidimensional differences may elucidate why gout risk is more pronounced in males among adolescents and young adults but becomes increasingly significant in frail middle-aged and older females. This underscores the necessity to tailor frailty care strategies based on population-specific factors, including race and sex, with important implications for addressing public health inequalities.

To reduce gout risk in frail middle-aged and older adults, evidence-based interventions are essential. Firstly, early identification and diagnosis of frailty are crucial. Research indicated that the Kihon Checklist (KC) and the Reported Edmonton Frail Scale (REFS) are among the most promising tools for widespread use in general practice, aiding in the personalized care of frail older adults.[57] Additionally, some tools have shown efficacy in the early detection of frailty factors. Studies have demonstrated that integrating electronic medical records with kinematic data from wearable devices using artificial intelligence can effectively identify and diagnose frailty in both clinical and nonclinical settings.[58] Secondly, emphasis should be on balanced nutrition and gout prevention. Research indicated that a low-purine diet – limiting red meat, seafood, legumes, and alcohol while prioritizing vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and low-fat dairy – significantly lowers serum UA levels and reduces gout attacks.[59] Besides, high-quality proteins from low-fat dairy, eggs, and soy support muscle recovery while minimizing purine intake. Moreover, regular medication reviews are necessary to adjust drugs like diuretics which elevate UA levels.[41]

This study possesses several noteworthy strengths. Firstly, we employed an expansive 49-item frailty index for a detailed evaluation of individuals aged 40 and above, offering a more nuanced insight into the association between frailty and gout risk. Additionally, our study design encompassed a national-wide cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data, LDSC, and MR techniques, systemically constructing robust evidence between frailty and gout. These rigorous analyses contributed significantly to advancing our understanding of the interplay between frailty and gout.

5. Limitations

Despite the strengths highlighted in our study, there are some limitations in our study. The gout phenotype was self-reported, and individuals may not accurately recall or report their diagnoses, potentially leading to bias of certain conditions. Inconsistent data on gout-specific medications (allopurinol, febuxostat, colchicine) across NHANES cycles (2007–2018) limited the use of medication proxies to refine case definitions or perform sensitivity analyses using alternative gout definitions. Certain covariates, such as detailed dietary data and medication use, were also inconsistently available across NHANES cycles and could not be included. Additionally, to reduce bias caused by population stratification, we utilized GWAS data from European cohorts, based on the available data. The genetic correlation and causal estimates derived from our European-ancestry GWAS data may not directly translate to other populations due to differences in LD patterns, allele frequencies, and population-specific environmental interactions. Future studies with medical record access, clinical validation using established criteria, biochemical confirmation, and more ethnically diverse cohorts would strengthen these findings.

6. Conclusions

Our study provided evidence for a connection between frailty and increased risk of gout. These results underscored the significant of monitoring UA levels in frail individuals. Targeting prolonged care solutions for middle-aged and older adults in frail and pre-frail can enhance their quality of life and help prevent gout. Further research is necessary to explore the frailty-gout mediating factors in more detail to guide prevention and treatment strategies.
