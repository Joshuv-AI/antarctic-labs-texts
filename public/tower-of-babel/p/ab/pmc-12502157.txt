Prevalence of drug resistance mutations and their association with time to virological failure in people living with HIV in Uganda
Authors: Collins Ankunda, Jude Emunyu, Sharon Namasambi, Conrad Sserunjogi, Brendah Kyomuhangi, Iving Mumbere, Jane Nakaweesi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDespite advancements in antiretroviral therapy (ART), virological failure and drug resistance mutations (DRMs) continue to pose major challenges, especially in resource-limited settings. This study examined DRM prevalence and their association with time to virological failure, informing strategies to optimize HIV treatment outcomes.MethodsThis cohort study retrospectively reviewed medical records of 235 People living with HIV (PLHIV) who underwent HIV drug resistance testing at the Mildmay-Uganda Hospital from 2018 to 2023. Data on demographics, ART, and DRMs were analyzed. Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests were used to compare median time to virological failure. Cox proportional hazards models assessed associations factors associated with time to virological failure, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsParticipants had a mean age of 28.0 years, 11.2 years on ART, and DRMs detected in 59.6%. Mutation prevalence was NNRTIs (52.8%), NRTIs (50.2%), PI major (25.5%), and INSTI (≤ 0.9%). Key drug class mutations included, NNRTIs (K103KN 25.1%, G190AS 14.0%, Y181CF/V/F 12.3%), NRTIs (M184V 45.0%, T215FY 30.2%, M41L 18.3%), PIs (M46I 15.7%, V82VA 12.3%, I54LV 9.4%), and INSTIs (E138K, T66A, G118R, E92EV all at 0.4%). DRMs were significantly associated with longer time (months) to virological failure: NRTIs (129.3 vs. 88.2, p = 0.008), NNRTIs (127.4 vs. 84.2, p = 0.003), and PIs (170.9 vs. 88.7, p = 0.000). In cox regression analysis, time to virological failure was significantly associated with age (HR: 0.987; CI: 0.976–0.998; p = 0.019, aHR: 0.976; CI: 0.959–0.993; p = 0.007), ART duration (HR: 0.828; CI: 0.800–0.856; p = 0.000, aHR: 0.827; CI: 0.792–0.863; p = 0.000), drug mutations; NRTI (HR: 0.659; CI: 0.484–0.898; p = 0.008), NNRTI (HR: 0.628; CI: 0.463–0.851; p = 0.003), and PI (HR: 0.257; CI: 0.146–0.453; p = 0.000, aHR: 0.337; CI: 0.137–0.831; p = 0.018).ConclusionDrug resistance is common among PLHIV failing ART, dominated by NNRTI and NRTI mutations. DRMs were present among PLHIV who took a longer time to virological failure. Delayed failure was independently associated with older age, longer ART duration, and PI mutations. Findings support early DRM testing and individualized ART, especially as newer ART options emerge.Keywords: HIV, HIV drug resistance mutations, Virological failure, Antiretroviral therapy

Introduction

Antiretroviral therapy (ART) has improved HIV outcomes and reduced transmission [1], but lifelong treatment poses challenges, including adherence issues, drug resistance, mental health, and socioeconomic difficulties [2–5]. A meta-analysis showed a 29% annual rise in drug resistance among ART-naive patients in East Africa. In Uganda, non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NNRTIs) resistance now exceeds the World Health Organisation(WHO)’s 10% threshold, raising concern [6].

In resource-limited settings, ART regimen selection adopts WHO guidelines, utilizing a public health approach to optimize treatment access and outcomes. Currently, first-line ART usually consists of nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors (NRTIs) combined with Integrase strand inhibitors (INSTIs)-dolutegravir (DTG), while second-line therapy includes NRTIs paired with either Protease inhibitors (PIs) or DTG, if not previously used in the first-line regimen. Uganda’s ART treatment guidelines recommend switching regimens after confirming treatment failure is due to DRMs and not non-adherence [3, 7].

HIV drug resistance testing optimizes ART by identifying DRMs for tailored treatment [8]. Previously due to cost, genotype testing was limited to specific groups like children or patients failing second- or third-line ART [7]. In the era of DTG as an anchor drug with a high genetic barrier to resistance, genotype testing is recommended for PLHIV switching from one regimen to a superior regimen after repeated non-suppressed VL despite Intensive Adherence Counselling(IAC) [9]. This approach accelerates patient outcomes, helps monitor emerging HIV variants, guiding public health interventions and policy development [10].

Amidst the limited availability of resistance testing and ART regimen switching options, data on the prevalence of DRMs among People living with HIV (PLHIV) with virological failure in Uganda remains scarce. This gap is especially pronounced in resource-limited settings, where the interplay between DRMs and treatment outcomes over time is under-researched. Therefore, this study assessed the prevalence of DRMs and their association with time to virological failure among PLHIV, leveraging real-world clinic-based data to provide contextually relevant insights. These findings contribute to HIV surveillance programs and informing future policies to enhance HIV management and optimize treatment outcomes.

Methods and materials

Study design

This cohort study retrospectively reviewed medical records of People living with HIV-1 who underwent HIV drug resistance testing at the Mildmay Uganda Hospital (MUgH) from 2018 to 2023.

Study site and population

This study was conducted at MUgH, a centre of excellence for HIV care and treatment in peri-urban Wakiso District, serving ~ 15,000 PLHIV. Of these, 65% are female, 95% are adults aged 18 years and above, and virological suppression stood at 98% as of 2023 [11]. In line with the HIV programmatic approach, PLHIV on ART who experienced virological failure were requested to undergo HIV drug resistance (HIVDR) testing. The study population comprised participants who had HIV drug resistance tests requested, with results uploaded to the Uganda HIVDR database [12] between 2018 and 2023. We did not identify any participants within this cohort whose results were not included in the HIVDR database.

Data collection and management

Data were collected from April to June 2024, including HIV DRM results, socio-demographic, and medical history from ART registers, patient cards, Electronic Medical Records (EMR), and the National HIVDR database [12]. Data were entered in Excel, exported to Stata, and verified against hard copies. Manual chart reviews addressed EMR gaps [13].

A flowchart illustrates the data collection process.

Data analysis

The analysis was conducted using Stata version 14. Descriptive statistics summarized as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables, means and standard deviations for continuous variables. Prevalence of DRMs was reported as the proportion of participants with specific mutations. Virological failure was defined as having an HIV viral load greater than 200 copies/ml. Kaplan-Meier survival estimates were employed to estimate time to virological failure across different mutation categories, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival functions. Survival time was defined as the duration from ART initiation to the occurrence of virological failure, based on annual viral load monitoring. Virological failure was identified at the first viral load result exceeding 200 copies/ml. Cox Proportional Hazards models adjusted for covariates like age, BMI, were run with interaction and confounding also being tested. A manual stepwise elimination with a P value cutoff of 0.05 was applied during the test for confounding. Final model results are reported as crude Hazards (HRs) and adjusted Hazard ratios (aHR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Most were male (123, 52.3%), underweight (81, 43.3%) and had a last viral load between 10,000 and 100,000 copies before DRM testing (108, 47.6%). Most PLHIV were initiated on NNRTIs as the anchor drug (126, 54.8%), whereas the most initiated backbone was AZT(Zidovudine)-based (93, 40.8%). Most DRM testing followed second-line failure (189, 80.4%), mainly on NRTI + PI (78, 40.6%).

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Prevalence of drug resistance mutations across ART regimen drug classes

DRMs were detected in 140 of 235 participants (59.6%). NNRTI mutations were most prevalent at 52.8% (124/235; 95% CI: 46.4–59.2), followed by NRTI mutations at 50.2% (118/235; 95% CI: 43.8–56.6). PI major and accessory mutations were less frequent, 25.5% (60/235; 95% CI: 20.0–31.1) and 4.3% (10/235; 95% CI: 1.7–6.8), respectively. INSTI mutations were rare: 0.9% major (2/235; 95% CI: 0.3–2.0) and 0.4% accessory (1/235; 95% CI: 0.4–1.3) as shown in Table 2 below.

Prevalence of specific mutations in four different ART classes

Figure 1 summarises the prevalence of mutations in four different ART classes as shown below. The most frequent NNRTI mutations were K103KN (25.1%), G190AS (14.0%), and Y181CF/V/F (12.3%). Among NRTI mutations, M184V (45.0%) was the most common, followed by T215FY (30.2%) and M41L (18.3%). Among INSTI major mutations, E138K, T66A, G118R, and E92EV each occurred at 0.4%, with S153SF (0.4%) being the only INSTI accessory mutation detected.

Discussion

This study offers important insights into the role of genotypically guided antiretroviral therapy in enhancing HIV-1treatment outcomes, especially in resource limited settings like Uganda. We evaluated the prevalence of DRMs, and the time taken to virological failure among PLHIV who subsequently presented with DRMs.

In this cohort, the mean age was 28 years with an average ART duration of over a decade, which represents a predominantly young population with long-term ART exposure. A slight male predominance was also observed which aligns with national HIV trends in sub-Saharan Africa among PLHIV failing on ART [14]. NRTI-based regimens, particularly AZT- and TDF-based combinations, were the most common initial therapies, reflecting historical treatment guidelines in resource-limited settings [15]. These findings align with previous studies showing widespread use of NNRTI- and NRTI-based regimens, which have been the cornerstone of first-line ART for over two decades [6, 16]. This explains the increased resistance to these regimens, particularly in high HIV burden countries like Uganda. DRM testing for resistance to ART regimens was predominantly done for PLHIV failing on second-line regimens. These regimens mainly consisted of PI-based combinations suggesting potential treatment exhaustion and delayed virological failure detection [7].

The study revealed that over half of the participants harboured DRMs, with NNRTI and NRTI mutations emerging as the most prevalent. This finding is consistent with earlier research highlighting the low genetic barrier of NNRTIs and the historical reliance on thymidine analogues like AZT, which are linked to characteristic resistance mutations [17]. These mutations compromise drug efficacy and can persist even after regimen changes [18]. A notable proportion of PI-associated mutations was also observed, though less frequent than NNRTI and NRTI mutations. This is likely due to the higher genetic barrier of protease inhibitors and their more restricted use in second- and third-line ART [8, 10]. Major and accessory INSTI mutations were rare (< 1%), reflecting limited exposure to this drug class. Nevertheless, their presence, even at low levels, raises concern given the increasing global use of dolutegravir as a first line anchor drug [15].

This study provided a detailed characterization of the prevalence of specific DRMs across major ART drug classes: PIs, INSTIs, NRTIs, and NNRTIs. Among NNRTI mutations, K103N/S/KN were the most prevalent. This mutation is particularly concerning as it confers high-level resistance to first-line NNRTIs such as nevirapine and efavirenz, limiting HIV treatment options. The dominance of K103N/S/KN in this cohort is consistent with previous studies indicating a high prevalence of NNRTI resistance, which exceeds the WHO resistance threshold and emphasises the need for alternative regimens, such as those incorporating INSTIs or PIs [10, 19, 20]. In the NRTI category, M184V/MV/MIV was most prevalent, highlighting the widespread resistance to lamivudine and emtricitabine, which are present in almost all ART regimens prescribed in Uganda. This mutation not only reduces the effectiveness of these drugs but paradoxically increases susceptibility to other NRTIs, like TDF and AZT, potentially allowing for continued viral suppression when used in combination with other drugs [21, 22]. However, the high prevalence of this mutation in the study emphasizes the need for caution when prescribing regimens containing these NRTIs. Among PI mutations, M46I, I54LV, and V82VA were the most common, conferring high level resistance to protease inhibitors like atazanavir and lopinavir and low level resistance to darunavir, which are often used in second and third line ART regimens [18, 23]. The high prevalence of these mutations suggests that the efficacy of PI-based second line and to lesser extent third line treatment may be compromised. This stresses the need for careful monitoring and consideration of alternative options for patients experiencing treatment failure. In contrast, INSTI mutations were found at a much lower prevalence, likely reflecting the recent introduction of INSTIs such as dolutegravir (DTG) in ART regimens. The major INSTI mutations that occurred were; T66A, G118R, and E138K which could potentially affect the efficacy of INSTIs, particularly in cases of virological failure consistent with findings from a study done Mozambique [24].

The analysis of time to virological failure demonstrated a statistically significant association with the subsequent emergence of DRMs. PLHIV who developed DRMs experienced a longer median time to virological failure, possibly reflecting better adherence in this group. Previous studies from low- and middle income countries report that the median time to first-line ART failure ranges from 50 days to over 31 months, depending on factors such as ART regimen, adherence, baseline CD4 count, and prior ART exposure [25, 26]. While many PLHIV experience early treatment failure, prolonged viral suppression allows for the gradual accumulation of drug resistance mutations (DRMs). Our findings suggest that by 10 years on ART, PLHIV are likely to develop DRMs and subsequent virological failure, supporting our recommendation that individuals with a decade of ART undergo resistance testing to enable early detection of DRMs and timely optimization of therapy. These findings further suggest that DRMs tend to emerge after extended ART use, highlighting the potential of time to virological failure as a predictor for the occurrence of DRMs and a tool for informing optimized treatment planning. Also, a longer time to virological failure may reflect sustained ART effectiveness despite the presence of emerging mutations, a situation further influenced by the practice of aligning HIV drug resistance testing with confirmed virological failure. Therefore, the occurrence of resistance mutations may as well be linked to delayed DRMs detection rather than sole influence of a longer time to virological failure. Enhanced care strategies such as enhanced adherence counselling may as well contribute to delayed virological failure [27]. Additionally, non-canonical mutations outside traditional drug target regions may restore viral fitness or modulate drug susceptibility, potentially delaying treatment failure [28]. The predominant presence of M184V mutations, which have been cited to reduce viral fitness and delay virological failure augment the explanation. This mutation not only confers resistance to specific drugs but also increases susceptibility to other NRTIs like TDF and AZT, supporting the delayed failure phenomenon [21, 22]. It is also important to note that, prior to the implementation of current HIV treatment guidelines, limited access to genotypic testing likely delayed the timely detection of DRMs [7]. The INSTI mutations did not yield statistically significant results for time to virological failure analysis possibly due to the small sample size of INSTI-treated PLHIV. Overall, these findings emphasize the importance of regular genotype testing, especially for patients with persistent non-suppressed viral loads despite adherence efforts and support the implementation of personalized ART regimens to optimize treatment outcomes [9, 24].

The analysis of factors associated with virological failure demonstrated that age and ART duration were protective against virological failure. Older individuals and those on ART for longer duration experienced significantly reduced hazards for shorter time to virological failure. This likely reflects the benefits of sustained treatment and adherence, as long-term ART users often adapt their lifestyles for viral suppression and may experience fewer side effects, reducing the risk of discontinuation [29]. Older patients may also benefit from stronger support systems and greater ART literacy, contributing to better outcomes [30]. These findings contrast with a meta-analysis linking prolonged ART duration to higher failure risk [31], suggesting variability across contexts. Younger individuals, often facing stigma, limited support, and behavioural challenges, and were more vulnerable to failure [30]. These findings highlight the importance of age-related considerations in managing HIV treatment and suggest that younger individuals may benefit from tailored interventions that address the unique barriers they face.

Having an underweight or obese BMI were associated with a reduced hazard of virological failure. However, this finding was not significant in the multivariable model indicating high influence of confounders. Non the less, this may be partly explained by the metabolic effects of certain ART regimens, some of which are linked to muscle wasting regardless of their virological effectiveness, while others are associated with weight gain. These complex dynamics are better explained by studies with pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic models to explain causality. Contrary, some studies have reported wasting as a marker of advanced HIV disease and virological failure [32], suggesting that further research with detailed pharmacological and clinical assessments is needed to fully understand this phenomenon [33].

Limitations

While this study provides important insights, several limitations warrant caution. First, the relatively small sample size, particularly for patients with INSTI mutations, may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, potential confounders such as genetic variation, HIV clade, contextual factors including treatment interruptions, and possible selection bias may have influenced the observed associations. The study’s retrospective, observational design restricts its ability to establish clear cause-and-effect relationships, partly because some important variables needed to assess causality were missing. Larger, prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings. However, this study benefits from real-world, clinic-based data, capturing PLHIV who were clinically diagnosed with ART failure offering contextually relevant insights into treatment dynamics. Moreover, the population characteristics in this cohort reflect expected patterns in similar high-risk groups, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to HIV resistance testing remains limited.

Conclusion

Drug resistance remains a substantial challenge among PLHIV failing on ART, with NNRTI and NRTI mutations dominating the resistance landscape. While DRMs were present among PLHIV who took a longer time of virological failure, the findings emphasize the need for early DRM testing, individualized treatment strategies and proactive regimen switching. The study further emphasises significant role of patient-specific factors, such as age and ART duration, in influencing time to virological failure. Future studies should explore longitudinal outcomes with newer agents, including long-acting injectables and second-generation INSTIs, to inform the next phase of ART optimization in high-burden settings.

These findings carry several programmatic implications. First, the high prevalence of DRMs, highlights the importance of routine viral load monitoring and timely DRM testing to guide treatment modifications. Second, the potential for paradoxical protective effects of certain mutations invites further investigation into functional resistance, viral fitness, and regimen interactions. Lastly, as integrase inhibitors become more widely used, robust surveillance is needed to monitor emerging resistance, especially in resource-limited settings where second- and third-line options remain constrained.
