Trends in preservation rhinoplasty publications: A bibliometric analysis of presented data originality
Authors: Rakan Saadoun, W Katherine Kao, Tang Ho
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent literature on dorsal preservation rhinoplasty (DPR) has seen a resurgence. This study performs a bibliometric analysis of publications and co-authorships within this research area, considering factors such as gender, country of origin, international collaboration, and the originality of the presented data.MethodsThe PubMed-indexed literature on DPR from 2018 to 2024 was extracted and reviewed, limited to English publications. Descriptive statistics were performed regarding the contributors by gender, country, publishing journal, authorship rank, and department of affiliation. Association tests were used to determine the association between the originality of the data presented and the affiliation’s country and department.ResultsOne hundred seventy-five (n=174) articles were co-authored by (n=503) collaborators were included. Of those (75/503, 14.9%) were female physicians. The annual publishing rate on DPR rose from 5 papers in 2019 to 39 in 2024. The most common country in which the first author’s affiliation was the United States (136/503, 27.0%), followed by Turkey (102/503, 20.2%). The most frequent department affiliation of the collaborators was Otolaryngology (270/503, 53.7%), followed by Plastic Surgery (178/503, 35.4%). There was a significant association between presenting original data and the country where the research originated (p <0.0001), the department’s affiliation (p =0.005), and publishing journal (p <0.0001).ConclusionsThe resurgence in publications on DPR does not correspond to a major increase in the presentation of original data, which is essential for determining the evidence of this technique. The findings highlight areas for improvement, such as addressing gender disparities and promoting original data presentation, particularly among Otolaryngologists.Keywords: Rhinoplasty, Dorsal preservation rhinoplasty, Bibliometric analysis, Septorhinoplasty

Introduction

Rhinoplasty is one of the most common procedures that patients seek for functional or aesthetic reasons. It is widely regarded as the most challenging plastic surgery procedure due to all the variables the surgeon must account for to achieve consistent, pleasing results.1, 2, 3, 4

Many techniques for performing rhinoplasty have been described over the years. Joseph's structural rhinoplasty was first described in Germany in the early 1990s.5,6 Most surgeons worldwide adopted modifications of this technique, which included opening the middle vault and reconstructing it using several grafting techniques.5, 6, 7 Another technique is the dorsal preservation rhinoplasty (DPR) technique, which was described even earlier than the structural one, more than 100 years ago.8, 9, 10, 11 The first documented DPR was described back in 1898 by Goodale.8 This technique has gained attention over the last few years, translating to increased academic output.7,10,12, 13, 14 This interest could be attributed partially to the assumed advantage of (DPR) over structural rhinoplasty, which includes less resection and reductive surgical maneuvers.7,12,15,16 Of note, published literature has shown comparable results to component dorsal hump removal regarding aesthetic and functional patients’ reported outcomes, though.9,17,18

DPR includes preserving the integrity of the middle third of the nose. It can be performed using a closed endonasal or an open transcullumellar approach and has two main categories, the push-down, and the let-down, as well as various modifications. However, further description of those classes is beyond the scope of this work.16,19, 20, 21, 22 DPR has been used in hump reduction and crooked nose correction.23,24

Over time, the development of (DPR) has evolved in a paradigm shift in nasal surgery toward the current preservation rhinoplasty (PR) practice. This philosophy has experienced a resurge over the last few years. It represents a for aesthetic and functional purposes.10,13,25,26 It is based on three different pillars:

(1) DPR, which mandates maintaining the continuity of the osteocartilaginous middle vault and is widely considered the essential PR component. (2) The conservation of nasal ligaments by choosing dissection planes that spare soft tissue or reconstructing them at the end of the surgery and (3) the preservation of the alar cartilage, which is achieved by preferring tensioning techniques to resectional ones.1,16,22, 23, 24,27, 28, 29

In his 2018 editorial, Rollin Daniel called for an essential change in performing rhinoplasty toward PR. He raised questions about the more destructive surgical approaches used in structural rhinoplasty.30 Since then, there has been a notable surge in publications about PR.7,19,30

This study aims to explore the authors of published work on PR by gender, specialty, and country of the first reported affiliation. It also analyses the publications the originality of the data presented.

Methods

The PubMed-indexed literature on DPR from 2018 to 2024 was extracted and reviewed. All Articles that address DPR’s variabilities were included. The search was limited to publications in English. Data regarding the author's gender, specialty, rank of authorship, and country of affiliation were included. Assumptions about the authors' gender were made based on their first names using the Genderize.io Application Programming Interface (API) used for this goal in previous published work.31, 32, 33, 34

Only the first reported one was noted in the case of authors with multiple affiliations.

Moreover, data related to each published work were analyzed, such as the year of publication, number of authors per paper, originality of the data presented, country of origin, and presence of international collaboration. International collaboration was defined as the presence of authors from two different countries in the manuscript. The country of origin of a paper was determined to be the one affiliated with the manuscript's first author.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the median and interquartile range (IQR) of continuous variables with skewed data, while frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables.

All analyses assumed a type 1 error rate of 0.05 with no adjustments made for multiplicity (the threshold for statistical significance was P < 0.05). Statistical analysis was performed using the JMP (18.1) student package.

Discussion

This is the first study to examine authorship trends and the originality of data related to DPR. Key findings reveal an essential gender gap among collaborators, with a low representation of female physicians. The United States, Turkey, and Portugal emerged as the top countries for first-author affiliations. The number of publications has steadily increased recently, with a notable peak in 2021. Plastic surgeons published significantly more original data than otolaryngologists.

The growing number of publications since 2018 reflects a rising academic interest and output in recent years (Figure 1).

The gender gap in collaborator numbers could be attributed to the fact that specialties such as otolaryngology and plastic surgery have historically been primarily male-dominated. However, a recent, pleasant, steady increase in women’s representation could establish a trajectory to narrow this gap in the foreseeable future.31,32,35, 36, 37

The United States emerged as the leading nation for author affiliations, accounting for 27.0% (136/503) of total contributors. This aligns with the fact that the first reports on DPR originated in the USA.8,10, 11, 12,25 However, a global survey by Patel et al.7 indicates that the current use of DPR techniques is among the lowest worldwide. It remains unclear why the PR technique has not gained popularity among American surgeons. One reason could be the significant visual advantages offered by the structured approach.12 Another factor might be the limited incorporation of PR techniques into the North American fellowship curriculum, where most rhinoplasty surgeons in the US receive their training. Patel et al. discovered in a questionnaire for the American Academy of Facial Plastic Surgery that only 11 out of 145 respondents (7.5%) had exposure to formal training.12

Regarding the collaborators' countries, Turkey (20.2%, 102/503) and Portugal (10.9%, 55/503) ranked second and third, respectively. This distribution highlights the significant influence of North America and Europe on academic contributions to the discipline.

Although the publications were primarily domestic contributions, a notable portion, 31.6% (55/174), identified international teams from various countries. This highlights the global knowledge-sharing that is popularizing PR academically.

The varied departmental affiliations of otolaryngologists, plastic surgeons, and maxillofacial surgeons illustrate the multidisciplinary nature of the research field.

Nearly half of the included publications presented original data (49.7%). Among these, Turkey contributed the largest share (47.8%), followed by the United States (14.5%) and Italy (8.7%). This suggests that original data on PR is still not widely available, and a substantial portion of the published literature on this technique remains anecdotal. Turkey's prominent role in providing original data may be due to its surgeons' familiarity with PR. Patel et al.,7 in a global survey study, found that Turkish surgeons have the highest familiarity with PR, with nearly 12% considering themselves experts in this technique.

Another significant finding is that plastic surgeons are more likely to publish original data than otolaryngologists. This should encourage otolaryngologists who use PR to share their outcomes.

Conclusion

The resurgence in publications on DPR does not correspond to a significant increase in the presentation of original data, which is essential for improving the evidence status of this technique. This analysis provides valuable insights into the dynamics of collaboration and publication related to PR. The findings highlight areas for improvement, such as addressing gender disparities, encouraging international collaborations, and promoting original data presentation, particularly among otolaryngologists.

Limitations

This retrospective descriptive study has strengths and limitations. For example, assumptions about gender were based on available names instead of individual self-identification. Only PubMed-indexed English publications were considered, excluding works indexed in other databases and those written in different languages. This potentially contributed to the underrepresentation of specific countries and collaborators. The election of the first reported affiliation may have further enhanced this underestimation. Additionally, the lack of prior data on this topic made comparisons challenging.
