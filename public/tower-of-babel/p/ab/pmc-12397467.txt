CD44 overexpression as a mediator of drug resistance in oral cancer: a meta-analysis unveiling molecular underpinnings and therapeutic implications
Authors: Shushu Miao, Caiqiong Wang, Anqi Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTherapeutic resistance in oral cancer, with cancer stem cells (CSCs) playing a pivotal role, remains a major clinical challenge. CD44, a key CSC marker, has been implicated in multidrug resistance mechanisms through drug efflux, DNA repair, and anti-apoptotic pathways. This meta-analysis aims to systematically evaluate the association between CD44 expression and clinicopathological characteristics in oral cancer patients through analysis of controlled clinical studies.MethodsWe systematically searched PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, VIP, WanFang, and China Biomedical Literature Database from inception to September 2022. Controlled clinical studies examining CD44 overexpression in relation to clinicopathological characteristics and survival outcomes in oral cancer patients were included. Quality assessment was performed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Data were analyzed using RevMan 5.3 software.ResultsTen high-quality studies comprising 846 oral cancer patients were included. CD44 overexpression was significantly associated with advanced local tumor invasion (T category: OR: 1.38; 95% CI 1.14–1.66; P < 0.001; I²=25%). No significant associations were found with N category, histological grade, or vascular invasion. Pooled survival analysis was limited by heterogeneous reporting methods across studies.ConclusionCD44 overexpression serves as a molecular marker of therapeutic resistance in oral cancer, particularly associated with local tumor progression. These findings highlight CD44-targeted therapies as promising strategies to overcome treatment resistance. Future large-scale, multicenter trials are needed to validate CD44 isoforms as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03436-9.

Introduction

Oral cancer represents a significant global health burden, with an estimated 377,713 new cases and 177,757 deaths annually according to GLOBOCAN 2020 data [1]. The five-year survival rate remains poor, particularly for advanced-stage disease, at approximately 50% [2]. Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounts for approximately 85–95% of all oral cancers [3]. Despite advances in treatment modalities, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, the prognosis for oral cancer patients has shown limited improvement over the past decades.

The identification of cancer stem cells (CSCs) has revolutionized our understanding of cancer biology and therapeutic resistance. CD44, a glycoprotein cell adhesion molecule and cell surface receptor for hyaluronic acid, has emerged as a key cancer stem cell marker [4]. CD44 exists in multiple isoforms generated through alternative splicing. The standard isoform (CD44s) contains the constant exons and is widely expressed, while variant isoforms (CD44v1-v10) include variable exons and exhibit tissue-specific expression patterns. Among these, CD44v6 has been particularly implicated in tumor invasion and metastasis through its enhanced binding to growth factors and extracellular matrix components [5]. The CD44v3 and CD44v8-10 isoforms have also shown associations with cancer progression, though their specific roles in oral cancer require further elucidation.

The role of CD44 in oral cancer progression has been the subject of numerous investigations. Studies have reported varying associations between CD44 expression and clinicopathological features such as tumor depth, lymph node metastasis, and patient survival. However, results have been inconsistent, partly due to differences in study populations, CD44 detection methods, and cutoff criteria for positivity.

Previous individual studies have shown conflicting results regarding the prognostic value of CD44 in oral cancer. While some studies report strong associations with poor prognosis [6], others show limited or no correlation [7]. This inconsistency necessitates a comprehensive meta-analysis to provide more definitive evidence.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

This meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [8].

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search of multiple databases including PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI, China Biology Medicine, VIP, and WanFang from inception to September 2022. The search strategy employed a combination of MeSH terms and keywords including: (“CD44” OR “cluster of differentiation 44”) AND (“oral cancer” OR “oral carcinoma” OR “oral squamous cell carcinoma” OR “OSCC”) AND (“overexpression” OR “expression” OR “immunohistochemistry”). Additionally, we searched gray literature sources including conference proceedings from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), and relevant dental/oral oncology conferences from 2019 to 2022. Clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP) were also searched for unpublished studies. Reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually searched for additional eligible studies [9–15].

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data using standardized forms. Extracted information included: first author, publication year, country, study design, sample size, CD44 detection methods, cutoff criteria, clinicopathological variables, and survival data.

Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort studies. Studies scoring ≥ 6 were considered high quality. Detailed quality assessment scores for each study are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan 5.3 software supplemented with R software (version 4.2.0) for meta-regression analyses. While RevMan 5.3 is a previous version, it was chosen for its robust handling of dichotomous outcomes and compatibility with our institutional analysis protocols. All analyses were verified using updated statistical packages in R. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for categorical variables. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I² statistic and Chi-square test. Values of I² ≤25%, 26–49%, 50–74%, and ≥ 75% were considered to represent low, moderate, substantial, and considerable heterogeneity, respectively.

Fixed-effects models were used when I² <50%; otherwise, random-effects models were applied. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to explore sources of heterogeneity, examining the effects of: (1) study year, (2) sample size, (3) geographic region, (4) CD44 detection method, and (5) cutoff criteria for CD44 positivity. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially removing individual studies. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots and Egger’s test.

Subgroup analyses were planned based on: (1) geographic region, (2) CD44 detection method, and (3) CD44 cutoff criteria.

Results

Literature search results

The initial search yielded 358 articles. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 24 articles underwent full-text evaluation. Fourteen studies were excluded due to insufficient data (n = 12) or data inconsistencies (n = 2). Finally, 10 studies [16–25] were included in this meta-analysis (Fig. 1).

T category

Eight studies reported data on T category. Meta-analysis revealed that CD44 overexpression was significantly associated with higher T category (OR: 1.38; 95% CI 1.14–1.66; P < 0.001; I²=25%) (Fig. 2). The funnel plot demonstrated relative symmetry (Fig. 3), and sensitivity analysis confirmed result stability (Fig. 4). Egger’s test showed no significant publication bias (P = 0.940). Meta-regression analysis revealed no significant contribution of study year (P = 0.32), sample size (P = 0.48), or geographic region (P = 0.61) to the observed heterogeneity.

N category

Four studies examined the relationship between CD44 expression and N category. No significant association was found (OR: 1.29; 95% CI 0.92–1.81; P = 0.139; I²=38%) (Fig. 5). Results remained consistent across sensitivity analyses (Figs. 6 and 7). Egger’s test showed no significant publication bias (P = 0.670).

Histological grade

Eight studies provided data on histological grade. Meta-analysis showed no significant association between CD44 overexpression and histological grade (OR: 1.13; 95% CI 0.96–1.34; P = 0.152; I²=52%) (Fig. 8). The substantial heterogeneity (I²=52%) prompted further investigation through meta-regression, which identified CD44 detection cutoff criteria as a potential source of heterogeneity (P = 0.04). Studies using > 10% positivity as cutoff showed stronger associations than those using > 25% cutoff. Considerable heterogeneity warranted random-effects modeling (Fig. 9).

Blood vessel invasion

Four studies provided blood vessel invasion data. No significant association was found (OR: 0.85; 95% CI 0.31–2.31; P = 0.751; I²=67%) (Figs. 14 and 15). The considerable heterogeneity (I²=67%) was explored through meta-regression, revealing that geographic differences in diagnostic criteria may contribute to this variability (Asian vs. Western studies, P = 0.03).

Overall survival

Three studies reported survival data. Due to heterogeneous reporting methods and different follow-up periods, quantitative synthesis through meta-analysis was not feasible. However, narrative synthesis revealed consistent trends: Rodrigues et al. found a significant association between increased CD44 expression and disease-related mortality (P = 0.02). Moraes et al. reported a trend toward worse 5-year cancer-specific survival in CD44-positive patients (74% vs. 38%, P = 0.052). Kokko et al. demonstrated a significant correlation between CD44 overexpression and poor 5-year survival in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. These findings suggest a potential prognostic role for CD44, though standardized survival reporting in future studies is needed for definitive meta-analysis.

Discussion

This meta-analysis provides important insights into the relationship between CD44 overexpression and clinicopathological characteristics in oral cancer. Our findings demonstrate a significant association between CD44 overexpression and higher T category, suggesting its involvement in local tumor progression. However, CD44 expression was not significantly associated with other clinicopathological parameters.

Biological mechanisms

From a molecular perspective, our preclinical understanding reveals that CD44 plays crucial roles in cell-cell adhesion, cell migration, and cancer stem cell maintenance. The standard form (CD44s) and variant forms (particularly CD44v6) have distinct functions in cancer progression. CD44 interaction with hyaluronic acid activates multiple signaling pathways, including PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and Wnt/β-catenin, promoting cell proliferation, invasion, and therapeutic resistance [11].

Clinical observations from our meta-analysis support these preclinical findings. The association between CD44 overexpression and higher T category may reflect its role in facilitating local tumor invasion through enhanced cell motility and matrix degradation. CD44v6, in particular, has been implicated in epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tumor invasion [26].

Clinical implications

The relationship between CD44 expression and T category suggests its potential as a prognostic marker for local disease progression. However, the lack of association with lymph node metastasis (N category) indicates that CD44 expression alone may be insufficient for predicting distant spread. This finding aligns with the concept that different molecular mechanisms may govern local invasion versus distant metastasis. In clinical practice, CD44 assessment could complement traditional staging to identify patients at higher risk for local progression who might benefit from more aggressive local therapy or closer surveillance.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, considerable heterogeneity was observed in several analyses, particularly for histological grade and blood vessel invasion. Our meta-regression analyses identified CD44 detection cutoff criteria and geographic differences as sources of this heterogeneity, but residual unexplained variance remains. Second, the relatively small number of included studies and patients limits the statistical power for some analyses. Third, most studies were retrospective, introducing potential selection bias. Fourth, we were unable to perform meta-analysis on survival outcomes due to limited data availability and heterogeneous reporting methods. Fifth, our analysis could not differentiate between CD44 isoforms, which may have distinct prognostic implications.

Future directions

Future research should focus on standardizing CD44 detection methods and cutoff criteria to enable more consistent comparisons across studies. Investigation of CD44 isoforms, particularly CD44v6, may provide more specific prognostic information. Additionally, combination of CD44 with other cancer stem cell markers may enhance prognostic accuracy. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized protocols for CD44 isoform detection and long-term follow-up are essential to validate these findings.

Conclusion

CD44 overexpression is significantly associated with higher T category in oral cancer patients, suggesting its role in local tumor progression. However, its association with other clinicopathological features and survival outcomes requires further investigation. These findings support the potential of CD44 as a biomarker for local disease progression, though additional high-quality studies are needed to establish its clinical utility. Future large-scale, multicenter trials should focus on validating specific CD44 isoforms as prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Development of standardized detection protocols and investigation of CD44-targeted therapeutic strategies in combination with conventional treatments represent critical next steps in translating these findings into clinical practice.
