Performance assessment of ISO 23036-1:2021 for parasite detection in the seafood industry
Authors: H Rodríguez, A Ramilo, E Abollo, AF González, S Pascual
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA verification plan was carried out to assess the reliability of seafood companies (operators) in complying with the standard ISO 23036-1:2021, an international voluntary benchmark method specifically designed for detecting zoonotic Anisakidae larvae in fish musculature (edible part). This plan involved the intensive inspection of 19,465 individual fish across various sample batches (three consecutive one year projects), fish species, NE Atlantic fishing grounds and seasons. A verification process was carried out on 5600 fish pressed portions, which were primarily inspected by operators at their own facilities, followed by independent quality control at the IIM-CSIC, an experienced research lab acting as verifier. Sample and data traceability were made possible by the Biobank Technical Unit (UTB-IIM-CSIC), which previously trained the staff of operators and provided complete technical assistance and mentoring on critical control points along the verification process. Performance evaluation was based on the level of agreement between operator findings and those of the verifier, both in parasite detection and counting. While numerical discrepancies (i.e. different number of anisakid larvae detected by the operator and the verifier) reached up to 22.8%, critical false positives and negatives were relatively low at 2.5% and 1.7%, respectively. Two main sources of error were identified: a) faults related to deviations from the ISO procedure, and b) misinterpretations of fluorescence signals, stemming from limitations in technical skill. Both error types were traced back to staff competence and the need for more continuous robust training. Overall, the verification plan yielded promising results, demonstrating that ISO 23036-1: 2021, if properly used, is a reliable tool that should be incorporated in HACCP for controlling fish parasite hazards at the seafood industry.Keywords: Standard ISO 23036-1:2021, Anisakidae, Parasite detection, HACCP

Introduction

Since the mid-20th century, scientific evidence has increasingly reported a high and rising number of anisakid larvae in fish species of commercial interest worldwide, with a significant 283-fold increase in Anisakis spp. abundance for the period 1978–2015 (Fiorenza et al., 2020). Anisakidae are at the forefront of a “dirty list” of parasites found in seafood during veterinary inspections, with a growing number of records in the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed (Bouwknegt et al., 2018; Rodrigues Caldeira et al., 2021). In addition to this increasing awareness in Europe of anisakids as a re-emergent risk (Fiorenza et al., 2020), the importance of the fish infection lies in the repercussion its impact on seafood safety and quality. Two zoonotic genera, Anisakis and Phocanema (Bao et al., 2023), are of greatest public health concern (EFSA, 2024), and also influence consumer perception due to the visual impact of these worms (both alive and dead) on the marketability of fish (Bao et al., 2017, Bao et al., 2018). As a result, fish product consumption is declining in some European countries, such as Spain, where this issue has been recognized by the fishing industry as socially unacceptable and “too big to ignore”. In 2020 this led to the creation of an Expert Working Group on Anisakis, coordinated by the Ministry of Fisheries.

Current EU legislation (Regulation EC No 853/2004 of the European Hygiene Package) (European Union, 2004) requires a visual inspection of the entire fish abdominal cavity and gut of fish to control the risk of visible parasites by fish operators. However, this visual inspection scheme is controversial due to its lack of standardization and poor performance characteristics, which means it cannot reliably prevent contaminated fish from reaching consumers (Celano et al., 2013; Llarena-Reino et al., 2012; Levsen et al., 2005). Specifically, fish operators have long demanded a rapid, simple (i.e., not requiring highly skilled personnel) and accurate method that can efficiently implemented in industrial settings. In response to these needs, Gómez-Morales et al. (2018) conducted a preliminary β-testing of the UV-Press method with the fish industry, reporting a successful performance with 97% of accuracy, 94.4% of sensitivity, and 100% of specificity. These results encouraged the ISO/TC 34/SC9/WG 6 Committee to develop the ISO 23036-1:2021 standard (Microbiology of the food chain - Methods for the detection of Anisakidae L3 larvae in fish and fishery products - Part 1: UV-press method). The rationale behind the development of the ISO 23036-1:2021 was precisely to reduce the number of rejections and customer complaints under the existing visual inspection scheme. This method quantifies parasitic infections in fish fillets, and it is applicable to both fresh and/or frozen fish and lightly processed fish products (e.g., marinated, salted, cold smoked). Results obtained with this method can be integrated in self-control processes (HACCPs), allowing efficient classification of fish lots through risk-ranking tools (e.g., FPR rating) (Rodríguez et al., 2018; Smaldone et al., 2020).

From 2018 to 2022, as a part of several collaborative projects between CSIC and the Spanish seafood industry, a verification plan was designed to assess the technical capacity of economic operators to comply with the ISO 23036-1:2021 standard. Data obtained with this inspection method would be used in management decisions (through FPR standard tool, as described in Rodríguez et al., 2018). The objective was to determine whether the data obtained using this methodology by fishing industry operators would be good enough (in other words, that the collected data never exceeds a permissible and previously stablished error percentage), to be used in fish parasite risk management, and then could be used as a standard procedure for fish lot inspection.

Material and methods

Fish inspection

Five seafood companies (operators) at the fish industry were selected to represent the most commercially relevant target fish species and fishing grounds for the domestic fresh fish market. Between 2018 and 2022, those operators participated in three consecutive ISO 23036-1:2021 training sessions (namely Anichain, Aniblock and Aniseg) designed to guide and support them in inspecting fish parasites in the respective sample batches. Prior to fish inspection, the operators received training in the method at the Biobank Technical Unit Service from Institute of Marine Research-Spanish National Research Council (UTB-IIM-CSIC, https://www.iim.csic.es/en/about-iim/organization/biobank-technical-unit), which also provided mentoring, advising and technical assistance throughout the whole inspection process. The UTB was additionally responsible to ensure sample and data traceability full course of the verification process (González et al., 2018). In accordance with ISO 23036-1:2021, the operators assessed the presence of zoonotic Anisakidae family (Anisakis and Phocanema) in more than 13 t of fish, totalling 19,465 specimens from the following species: European hake (Merluccius merluccius), European anchovy (Engraulis encrasicolus), megrim (Lepidorhombus spp.), monkfish (Lophius budegassa and Lophius piscatorius), blue whiting (Micromesistius poutassou) and Atlantic mackerel (Scomber scombrus). Fish specimens were caught in different seasons from different European fishing grounds (NE Atlantic, ICES IVa, VIa, VIIb, VIIc, VIIg, VIIh, VIIj, VIIIa, VIIIc, VIIId and IXa), aiming to create diverse combinations (i.e., subsamples) of fish species, fishing ground and season. As an example, in the Anichain sample batch (2018–2019), 36 subsamples were examined: combinations of one species (M. merluccius), 10 NE Atlantic fishing grounds (ICES IVa, VIa, VIIb, VIIc, VIIh, VIIj, VIIIa, VIIIc, VIIId and IXa) and four seasons (spring, summer, autumn and winter), although in some fishing grounds it was impossible to obtain subsamples for every season.

From any fish specimen, different pressed portions were obtained to be processed by the UV-Press method. The number and size of those portions depended on the size of the fish. For example, anchovies (E. encrasicolus) were pressed in only one portion (complete body) and blue whiting (M. poutassou) in left and right fillets. The biggest fishes (for example, some European hakes, M. merluccius, or monkfish, Lophius spp.) were sliced in multiple portions: upper and lower belly flaps (BF), upper and lower loans and a variable number of tail portions. In any case, Anisakis spp. or Phocanema spp. larvae location on musculature were always indicated. The total count of anisakid larvae (L3) was recorded for every fish pressed portion, applying a threshold counting limit of 31 parasites. This threshold was considered a trade-off value by the verifier, balancing expected parasite loads, fluorescent reading performance, and a reasonable inspection effort by the operators.

Verification process

All inspected specimens within each subsample were numbered by operators, and at least five numbers were randomly selected. All the pressed portions from the selected specimens were then sent to the verifier. Thus, a total of 5600 pressed portions of fish (from 691 fish specimens), were re-examined in UTB-IIM-CSIC facilities, under the ISO 23036-1:2021 standard by trained laboratory personnel (i.e., with more than 10 years of experience in parasite detection and UV-Press method). The total count of anisakid larvae (L3) was recorded for all fish pressed portions, applying again a threshold counting limit of 31 parasites. For each fish portion, all the fluorescence images were digitised. After examination with ISO 23036-1:2021 method, fish pressed portions were examined by candling. Any anomalous finding were analysed microscopically through morpho-anatomical studies, and anisakid and other parasites were recovered, to be biobanked.

Result evaluation

After the verification process described above was completed, the results obtained by the verifier were compared with those recorded by the operators on the UTB-platform. Particular attention was given to any fish portion in which different results were obtained by operator and verifier (by the analysis of digitised images), with the aim of understanding the source of discrepancy. In the case of discrepancy between the parasite counts reported by the operator and the verifier, the difference was classified as either numerical (single minor difference between counting data) or dichotomous (presence-absence of parasites). Dichotomous discrepancies were further expressed numerically as false negatives (when the operator recorded no parasites but at least one was detected by the verifier) and false positives (when the operator recorded the presence of Anisakidae that were not confirmed by the verifier). Minor numerical discrepancies were considered less critical than dichotomous ones, based on the criteria of prevailing EC legislation. Those data obtained by operators for any subsample (any combination: fish species/fishing ground/season), were considered as compromised for an accurate risk analysis when percentage of significant errors (false negatives or positives) are above 5% of inspected fish pressed portions. When the percentages of false negatives, positives, and genus misidentifications exceeded that value, the operators were contacted to inform them and to implement corrective actions, such as additional information or training sessions.

The performance characteristic of the process (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity) was determined following the guidelines to the OIE (2012). The term accuracy was defined as the closeness of the verifier results to that obtained by the operator. Sensitivity referred to the proportion of pieces yielding a positive result when analysed by both operator and verifier (being the results obtained in the verification process the reference standard). Specificity represented the proportion of samples reported as negative by the operator that were also confirmed as negative during verification.

Internal quality control

As our results were considered as the reference standard, a validation test was conducted as part of internal quality control during the verification process. This test involved 120 fish pieces (from belly flaps and epaxial-tail muscles of European hake), which were processed and sequentially analysed using the UV-Press (in accordance with ISO 23036-1:2021), candling and Artificial Peptic Digestion (APD, according to ISO 23036-2:2021) methods. The performance characteristic of this process was also determined as described in OIE (2012), by comparing the results obtained by each of those methods, with the total anisakid number in each pressed piece achieved by combining the three methods results, as well as the analysis of the differences.

Statistical analyses

A generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with binomial error distribution and logit link was fitted to evaluate the probability of discrepancy as a function of fish species, fishing ground, and operator. Batch was included as a random intercept to account for non-independence among samples. Categorical predictors were re-leveled to use M. merluccius, IVa, and OPERATOR 1 as reference categories, selected based on sample size and biological representativeness. Model fitting was performed in R version 4.4.5 (R Core Team, 2023) using the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015). Model convergence was ensured by applying the bobyqa optimizer with extended iteration limits. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were extracted using the broom.mixed package (Bolker and Robinson, 2018), and significant predictors (p < 0.05) were visualized in a forest plot generated with ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016). To facilitate interpretation and highlight the most influential predictors, the forest plot was restricted to the ten variables showing the strongest statistical support in the GLMM, selected based on the smallest p-values and limited to predictors whose 95% CI did not cross the null value (OR = 1). Predicted probabilities of discrepancy for all possible combinations of species, fishing ground, and operator were obtained from the fitted GLMM using marginal predictions with random effects set to zero.

Results

Discrepancies

Discrepancies were related to differences in the interpretation (operator vs. verifier) of the brightly fluorescent signals revealing Anisakidae larvae in pressed fish fillets under UV-light (Fig. 1a and b). Overall, the mean parasite counting values for operator and verifier were very similar (7.45 ± 0.15 vs. 7.32 ± 0.15), with a high degree of correlation between both readings (Pearson correlation r = 0.985; p < 0.001). Numerically, discrepancies in the mean number of parasites averaged less than one per sample (0.57 ± 0.02), though the range extended from 0 to 30. In fact, single-larva discrepancy represents the most frequent group (11.1%), and the differences in parasite counting rarely (< 1%) were found above 4. Discrepancies related to uninfected fish pieces accounted for 0.8% and those involving heavily infected pieces (i.e. ≥31 parasites) accounted for 0.2%.

Differences in readings between operators and the verifier were detected in 1278 pieces (22.8% of total 5600 examined). Most of them (65.5% of the 1278) reflected numerical discrepancies (Nd), while dichotomous discrepancies (No, overall discrepancies) were found in 238 pieces. This dichotomous discrepancies, considered as significant errors, comprised 142 false positives (N+, 2.5%) and 96 false negatives (N-, 1.7%), together constituting the 4.3% of all the 5600 examined pieces (Table 1).

The UTB-IIM-CSIC also found discrepancies which were likely due to operator's difficulty in distinguish (or even recognise) L3 of Phocanema spp. These discrepancies were referred as genus “misidentifications”, (Ng, Table 1) and was not considered as relevant as dichotomous errors (false positives or negatives), as both anisakid genera have the same relevance (i.e., zoonotic) for risk analysis. However, in some cases the lack of expertise/training to detect the L3 Phocanema spp. larvae by operators produced a “not parasitized” record, in pieces with Phocanema spp. larvae presence (false negatives, eight pieces out of 5600: 0.1%). Genus misidentifications were also found associated to false positives, where presence of Phocanema spp. were recorded in pieces without Anisakis spp., and none of these genera could be detected in the verification analysis at UTB-IIM-CSIC (four pieces out of 5600: 0.1%).

Notably, the distribution of numerical discrepancies (Nd) varied significantly across fish species, with the highest rate (30.9%) observed in Lophius spp. (Table 1). In this fish species, an important source of discrepancies was mainly attributed to genus misidentifications (Ng, 11.4%). The significant errors (false positives N+, false negatives N-, as well as the overall value of dichotomous discrepancies No), remained below 5% for all species, except for S. scombrus, which presented 7.4% overall dichotomous discrepancies (No, Table 1). Discrepancies also varied across the three sample batches (Table 2), though significant errors (No, overall values of false negatives and positives) remained mostly below 5%. Slightly higher rates were observed (Table 2) in the Anichain batch for overall dichotomous errors (6.3%) and in Aniblock for genus misidentifications (5.6%). The former coincided with the first training session, while the latter included the introduction of Lophius spp. samples. Regarding discrepancies attributable to different operators (Table 3), significant differences in numerical discrepancies (Nd) and rates (ranging from 6.3 to 31.9%) were observed (H = 64.349; p < 0.001). Overall, most significant dichotomous errors remained below 5%, with the exception of operator 4, which exceeded that 5% with false negatives (N-, 8.8%) in the first training session, Anichain (Table 3). Operator 1 also surpassed the 5% threshold of discrepancies with genus misidentifications (Ng, 8.1%).

Additionally, evolution on discrepancies observed in the four seasonal sub-samplings through a single sample batch (Anichain) provided insights into the learning curve of fish operators during the one-year verification process, as no species bias was present (Table 4). After each seasonal subsampling and its corresponding verification process, operators who showed values above 5% for significant errors, were contacted, with the aim of reducing that percentage. As an example, Table 4 shows that Operator 4 showed a very high percentage of false negatives in the first seasonal inspection. They were contacted and, at the operator's request, a document with additional training was prepared, illustrating some real cases in which they had not detected the anisakid larvae, highlighting the fluorescent larvae. This information reached Operator 4 after the second seasonal subsampling had been completed, so its effect was not noticeable, and the false negative percentage remained very similar in both seasons. However, the effect of the additional training information on false negatives could be seen in the third subsampling, reaching values below 5% in the fourth one. Notably, a clear decrease in the percentage of false negatives/positives was observed throughout the four seasonal inspection rounds. The learning curve appeared to peak at the end of this first sample batch (Anichain), and remained stable across subsequent batches, Aniblock and Aniseg. Mean numerical discrepancies declined from 0.72 in the first training session (Anichain) to 0.55 in the second one (Aniblock), and 0.33 in the third one (Aniseg). The overall percentage of dichotomous discrepancies also showed a declining trend from the first batch (Anichain) to the last one (Aniseg) (Table 2).

The GLMM analysis revealed several significant predictors influencing the probability of discrepancy, with clear biological and operational patterns emerging. Using M. merluccius as the reference species, higher odds of discrepancy were observed for Lophius spp., whereas Lepidorhombus spp. showed a reduced discrepancy risk (OR < 1). These patterns suggest that species-specific morphological traits or handling characteristics may influence the likelihood of inconsistent identification or reporting. When IVa was used as the reference fishing ground, several grounds (VIIc, IXa, VIIh, VIa, VIIIc, and VIIId) showed significantly lower odds of discrepancy, indicating spatial heterogeneity in data quality that may reflect differences in operational practices across areas. Regarding operators, Operator 1 served as the reference category, and Operator 2 exhibited significantly higher discrepancy risk (OR > 1), highlighting the influence of operator-specific procedures or expertise (Fig. 3). The GLMM-derived probability table (Supplementary Table S1) showed clear variation in discrepancy risk across the full set of species, fishing grounds, and operator combinations, with predicted values ranging from low to high according to the modelled effects.

Causes for discrepancies

Overall, most discrepancies were of unknown origin, accounting for 66.9% of false negatives and 36.5% of false positives. A second major source of error was the incorrect technical application of ISO 230366-1 standard, which explained 12.0% of false negatives. These includes issues such as incorrect pressing that resulted in excessively thick fish pieces (Fig. 1c), failure to inspect both sides of the pressed bag under UV-light, as specified by norm instructions, and input errors in the biobank database. In other cases, discrepancies were due to misinterpretation of results, often caused by reading errors linked to melanisation of anisakids (e.g., particularly in monkfish and hake samples, Fig. 1d), or fluorescence interferences from host tissues. Finally, genus misidentification (Fig. 2a and b) also contributed to several false negatives (Fig. 2b).

Regarding the false positives, 26.0% of cases were associated with misleading fluorescent signals in the pressed pieces. These were often due to host structures (e.g., scales, nerves, spines, and even fatty muscle bundles), or even external fibres introduced during fish processing (Fig. 1d and e). Sample deterioration also played a role, contributing up to 28.1% of false positives. Degraded tissue in the pressed bags interfered with UV readings by emitting fluorescence (likely due to bacterial proliferation) and simultaneously reducing the auto fluorescence of anisakids (Fig. 2c-d). In some cases, fluorescent emissions were attributed to non-Anisakidae parasites or involved genus misidentification.

In the internal quality control or validation test, 75 off the 120 fish pressed portions gave exactly the same result for anisakid presence and number, when examined with the three different methods. Number of positives increased from the 76 positives detected by candling to the 94 detected by UV-Press (87 positives by APD). No false negatives were detected when using UV-Press method (we mean that in none of the 120 fish pressed pieces, a larva detected by candling or by APD was not detected by UV-Press), and no false positives across any of the three methods (no cases in which the object identified as an Anisakis spp. larva was not so once isolated). So, values of accuracy, sensitivity and specificity for ISO 23036-1:2021 reach the maximum (Table 6). We have also obtained a 100% specificity (no false positives) in the other two detection methods, ISO 23036-2: 2021 (APD) and candling. Accuracy and sensitivity were above 90% for the APD method, and above 80% for candling. The candling method contributed the highest proportion of false negatives (15%), while 5.8% of false negatives were observed with APD, mainly due to broken or damaged Anisakis, which seemed to be unable to resist the digestion process.

Discussion

The main purpose of the verification process was to validate the technical and staff capacities of the seafood industry to ensure compliance with the ISO 23036-1:2021 standard. Considering the complete dataset, discrepancies between fish operator and verifier were observed in 22.8% of cases. However, the proportion of critical errors (i.e., false negatives and false positives) remained consistently below 5%, despite the large number of inspected samples. This outcome should be interpreted as a highly satisfactory and aligns with the findings of the previous β-testing evaluation of the UV-Press method (Gómez-Morales et al., 2018). Accuracy, sensitivity and specificity values associated to the application of the ISO standard were all above 95%, far surpassing the performance reported for current visual inspection schemes (Levsen et al., 2005; Mercken et al., 2022). Accuracy value obtained in Gómez-Morales et al. (2018) β-testing was slightly higher (97%) than the 95.75% found in our study and, as they could not detect any false positive, specificity reached 100%. However, it must be taken into account that operators who participated in that β-testing examined only 15 samples, consisting on ∼100 g of fish fillets from Anisakidae-free sea bass (Dicentrarchus labrax), inoculated with different numbers of Anisakis pegreffi L3 larvae. In our study, operator carried out the examination of complete fish specimens, where different fish elements, as well as external fibres or other pathological items, could lead to a false positive result. Even so, specificity in our study reached 96.20% and sensitivity values were very similar. Moreover, Gómez-Morales et al. (2018), found 11 discrepancies between the results obtained for the operator and the reference (controlled inoculum) in the 45 fish fillets analysed. This represented a 24.4% of discrepancies, compared with the similar 22.8% in our case.

ISO 20036-1:2021 also reach the maximum values of performance in our internal quality control, confirming itself as the most reliable method in terms of performance characteristics. The candling method, an improved version of the current legal method, showed the poorest performance results. This is consistent with Mercken et al. (2022), who reported that candling had a sensitivity of 31% in skinned fish portions, allowing the detection of merely 23% of Anisakis spp. larvae. Although our results exceeded by far those of Mercken et al. (2022), it must be taken into account that our “fish portion” are 2 mm pressed pieces, so they were not comparable with the skinned portions usually used in the studies about candling efficiency. Also, candling reading were carried out after UV-Press reading, and although there were not sequentially performed (this is, all the UV-Press readings were performed together before starting candling readings, to avoid successive UV and candling readings for the same fish piece), we cannot discard that the prior information improved the candling accuracy of the laboratory staff.

Our results suggest that continuous training for seafood industry personnel should be mandatory to assure reliable inspection outcomes. A clear learning curve was observed, with discrepancies decreasing across the four subsamples in the first sample batch Anichain), and remaining broadly stable throughout the verification process. As an example, operator 4 exceeded the 5% of false negatives in the first subsample. That problem of under-detection of Anisakis spp. was addressed with the operator, and some additional training was carried. That led to a clear diminution of false negatives during the whole training session (from 15.2% in the first subsample to 2.2% in the last one). Training seemed to be especially necessary when something changes in the inspection process, for example, the introduction of new fish species, mainly when changes in the parasite species present would be expected. For example, operator 1 also surpassed the 5% threshold of discrepancies with genus misidentifications, but that operator 1 analysed almost 80% of Lophius spp. samples, and encountered the highest prevalence and intensity of Phocanema infection.

While some false positives and false negatives could not be conclusively explained, it proved valuable to distinguish discrepancies arising from misinterpretation (i.e., reading error) from those caused by technical errors or limitations. Examples of the latter include errors in recording results on laboratory sheets or in data enter to the biobank platform as well as incorrect application of ISO instructions. An inherent limitation of the method is its inability to detect melanised anisakids, which do not emit fluorescence and are often found in fish species with strong immune responses, such as monkfish and hake (Pascual et al., 2018). Considering misinterpretations, different tissue elements from fish, other parasite entities or signs of autolytic spoilage in fish can interfere with observations. Furthermore, discrepancies linked to unskilled operators included failures to distinguish the fluorescence signals between two anisakid genera, Anisakis and Phocanema. At this stage, a routine review of the most relevant discrepancies could meaningfully improve compliance with the ISO standard. Such a strategy should support the development of a comprehensive framework for designing additional targeted training programs aimed at strengthening operator capabilities. Furthermore, technical innovations could contribute significantly to ISO 23036-1:2021 compliance. Specifically, the adoption of standardized automated devices integrated with optimized machine learning algorithms could facilitate real-time detection and identification of parasites by interpreting fluorescence signals with high precision. These systems would also reduce misidentification by less-skilled personnel and allow for the storage and reporting of fluorescence data in a more accessible and manageable format.

Conclusions

The ISO 23036-1:2021 standard is a reliable tool for routine analysis of several species of marine fish of commercial interest, concerning the presence of zoonotic parasites of socio-economic importance. This standard meets the specific needs and priorities demanding the industrial environment, and it is easily includable into self-control programs to better guarantee the safety and quality of seafood products. ISO training is clearly the bottleneck in this process to make sure that all processes are managed effectively.
