USH2A-Mutated Human Retinal Organoids Model Rod–Cone Dystrophy
Authors: Kristen E. Ashworth, Jiajie Zhang, Cassandra D'Amata, Elise Héon, Brian G. Ballios
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose USH2A mutations are the leading cause of autosomal recessive retinitis pigmentosa (RP), a progressive blinding disease marked by photoreceptor degeneration. Animal models fail to recapitulate the features of USH2A RP seen in humans, and its earliest pathogenic events remain unknown. Here, we established a human model of USH2A RP using retinal organoids derived from patient induced pluripotent stem cells and CRISPR-Cas9-engineered isogenic-USH2A−/− induced pluripotent stem cells.MethodsWe assessed organoids for cellular, molecular, and morphological defects using serial live imaging and whole organoid and fixed section analyses.ResultsBoth patient-derived and isogenic-USH2A−/− organoids showed preferential rod photoreceptor loss followed by widespread degeneration, consistent with the clinical phenotype. Additionally, isogenic-USH2A−/− organoids showed early defects in proliferation and structure.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that molecular changes precede overt photoreceptor loss in USH2A RP, and pathogenesis may begin before clinical symptoms emerge. By defining early and late disease features, we provide new insight on the developmental origins of USH2A RP to guide therapeutic strategies.

Inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) are genetically and clinically diverse neurodegenerative eye conditions in which progressive loss of rod and cone photoreceptors—the light-sensitive neurons of the retina—results in irreversible visual impairment.1–3 Approximately 5.5 million people worldwide face the reality of inevitable blindness from IRDs, and approximately 2.7 billion people are expected to be carriers of an IRD-causing mutation.4 Among the more than 300 genes associated with IRDs identified to date,5 mutations in the USH2A gene are the leading cause of autosomal recessive RP—one of the most common rod–cone dystrophies—as well as Usher syndrome type II, a prevalent form of syndromic RP.1,6,7

USH2A encodes the transmembrane protein usherin, localized at the photoreceptor connecting cilium at the root of the outer segment. It plays an essential role in stabilizing ciliary architecture and is required for long-term maintenance of photoreceptors.8 In addition, it is crucial in the normal development of cochlear hair cells necessary for hearing.8,9 USH2A mutations cause misfolded or truncated usherin, compromising ciliary structure and function, which leads to progressive rod, then cone, degeneration, resulting in peripheral and central vision loss.8,9 The developmental origins and progression of USH2A-associated RP remain poorly understood, and no cure exists—largely owing to the lack of suitable preclinical models that recapitulate the human-specific features of human USH2A disease.

Although USH2A-null mice as a disease model have elucidated the role of usherin in the maintenance of cochlear hair cells, they have failed to mimic the retinal degeneration seen in humans. Patients with USH2A-associated RP typically present symptomatically in childhood or early-adolescence with night blindness, and evidence of retinal dysfunction can be detected even in early childhood,10 making it challenging to estimate disease onset or determine whether there is a developmental origin of the disease. In contrast, USH2A-null mice show normal retinal features up to 10 months of age and exhibit only mild or late-onset retinal degeneration.8 USH2A variant mice engineered to express human pathogenic mutations show similar late-onset loss of retinal function and mild degeneration.9 Although zebrafish models of USH2A disease have shown more moderate, early-onset retinal degeneration,11 the strong regenerative capacity of even adult zebrafish retina makes it difficult to compare the disease progression with that seen in humans. Taken together, there is an obvious need for a human model of USH2A-associated RP to understand disease mechanisms—whether developmental or degenerative—to facilitate new therapeutic discoveries.

The use of human pluripotent stem cells to generate retinal organoids—three-dimensional (3D) self-organizing structures resembling the mature retina—was first described in a landmark paper in 2011.12 Through their growth and maturation in vitro, retinal organoids recapitulate many of the features of human retinal development in vivo, forming a laminated tissue comprised of the neural retinal cell types found in native retina.1,13 By using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs),14 retinal organoids can be derived from any patient genotype and used to study differences in retinal maturation between healthy and disease states. As such, retinal organoids provide a powerful preclinical model for studying IRDs in a human context. A growing body of work has shown their use in the discovery of new pathogenic IRD variants,15,16 and that cellular and subcellular retinal abnormalities can be observed in organoid models for a variety of clinical retinal disorders.17–20 However, only recently have investigators begun to generate retinal organoids for mutations relevant to USH2A-associated RP.21–23 Of these, only one group is using USH2A patient lines with confirmed pathogenic variants22; other groups21,23 have published work using a compound heterozygote patient-derived line in which one of the alleles (c.9127_9129delCCT) remains a clinical variant of uncertain significance. To date, no studies of USH2A-diseased retinal organoids have yet carefully explored disease progression over time, whether during retinogenesis or during photoreceptor maturation.

Here, we have developed a novel human retinal organoid model of USH2A-associated RP, derived from both USH2A patient and mutation-engineered iPSCs. We performed rigorous analysis of morphological, cellular, and genetic differences arising at early, mid, and late organoid differentiation stages to model disease progression across retinogenesis and retinal maturation. Our USH2A mutation-engineered organoids exhibit early defects in retinal proliferation and photoreceptor survival, suggesting a previously unrecognized role of USH2A in retinal development. Our USH2A organoids faithfully recapitulate key features of USH2A-associated RP, including progressive mid- to late-stage retinal degeneration characterized by preferential loss of rod photoreceptors, followed by widespread photoreceptor degeneration. We anticipate that our human USH2A organoids will provide a valuable, clinically relevant disease model that can be leveraged to investigate mechanisms of disease pathogenesis and identify new cellular and genetic therapies for USH2A-associated RP.

Methods

Experimental Model and Subject Details

Human iPSCs

Our USH2A patient-derived iPSC line harbors two common pathogenic mutations as a biallelic compound heterozygote: c.2299delG, and c.1256G>T, causing Usher syndrome in this male patient. Patient-derived iPSCs were reprogrammed by Sendai virus from peripheral blood samples at the Centre for Commercialization of Regenerative Medicine (Toronto, Ontario, Canada). Healthy donor iPSCs were obtained from the Personal Genome Project of Canada and were derived from a male participant (PGPC-17).24 Healthy iPSCs underwent whole genome sequencing, with no disease-causing retinal genotypes detected. Both iPSC lines underwent rigorous validation following reprogramming, including pluripotency marker screening, karyotyping, and trilineage differentiation assays, with no abnormalities detected (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S3).

Human WERI-Rb1 Cells

WERI-Rb1 cells (ATCC HTB-169), a human retinoblastoma-derived cell line, were cultured in suspension in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; 51536C) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Wisent, Saint-Jean-Baptiste, Quebec, Canada; 920-040). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. Media changes were performed every 2 to 3 days and cells were passaged approximately every 4 days. All cultures selected for RNA extraction tested negative for mycoplasma by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

Method Details

Maintenance of Human Pluripotent Stem Cells

All human iPSC lines were cultured in a feeder-free system on 6-well plates coated with Geltrex (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA; A1413201) and maintained with daily feeding using Essential 8 medium (Gibco, A1517001) at 37°C in a standard 5% CO2 incubator. Cells were thawed into medium with 10 µM of ROCK inhibitor (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 13624S). Cells were then passaged every 4 days (or upon reaching 60% to 80% confluence) using ReLeSR (STEMCELL Technologies, 100-0484) at 37°C for 4 minutes, then dissociated gently with a pipette and suspended in E8 and re-plated on new Geltrex-coated six-well plates. Cells were passaged at least three times before retinal organoid differentiation was initiated. Cultures were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination using the e-Myco VALiD mycoplasma detection kit (FroggaBio, Ronronti, Ontario, Canada; 25239) before beginning retinal organoid experiments; any affected cultures were discarded.

Characterization of Human iPSCs

To assess expression of pluripotency markers in patient iPSCs, flow cytometry was performed by the Centre for Commercialization of Regenerative Medicine (Toronto, Ontario, Canada) as part of their iPSC validation program. iPSCs were dissociated using Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (Stem Cell Technologies, 100-0485), counted via an NC200 cell counter and resuspended at 106 cells/mL in flow wash buffer. An equal number of cells per sample were incubated in PBS containing 1/500 Zombie Red viability dye (Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA; 423109) for 15 minutes on ice to identify nonviable cells. Heat-killed cells were included as a viability control in all flow cytometry runs. Samples were fixed with the eBioscience Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set fixation buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 00-5523-00) and stored at 4°C for up to 2 weeks or immediately processed for flow. We plated 2 × 105 cells on V-bottom 96-well plates and permeabilized for 5 minutes at room temperature with the eBiosciences FoxP3 permeabilization buffer, then incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature with antibodies dyes: TRA-1-60 AF647-conjugated antibody (Biolegend, 330605; 1:50), OCT3/4 PE-conjugated antibody (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA; 561556; 1:100), SOX2 PerCP-Cy5.5-conjugated antibody (BD Biosciences, 561506; 1:100), SSEA-4 V450-conjugated antibody (BD Biosciences, 561156; 1:100), and Zombie Red Fixable Viability Kit (Biolegend, 423109; 1:500). Samples were subsequently analyzed for flow cytometry. Samples were assessed using a Beckman Coulter Cytoflex S flow cytometer with CytExpert software (version 2.4.0.28). Compensation matrices were precalculated, and at least 10,000 events were captured per condition. Analysis of captured data was assessed using FlowJo software, with experimental samples normalized to unstained samples. At least 80% of cells are required to be pluripotency-marker positive to pass quality control.

For pluripotency markers immunostaining, iPSCs grown in 6-well cell culture plates at approximately 60% confluency were fixed in fresh, cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 minutes at room temperature and then washed 3 times with PBS. Fixed iPSCs were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes at room temperature and blocked with 10% normal donkey serum (NDS) for 1 hour at room temperature. Cells were incubated in primary antibodies (Supplementary Table S1) diluted in PBS with 1% NDS and 0.1% Triton-X overnight at 4°C or 1 hour at room temperature. Cells were then washed 3 times with 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS and incubated in secondary antibodies diluted 1:1000 in PBS with 1% NDS and 0.1% Triton X-100 for 1 hour at room temperature. Slides were then washed once with PBS and incubated in Hoechst or DAPI diluted 1:1000 in PBS for 11 minutes at room temperature. Cells were finally washed 3 times with PBS, stored at 4°C in PBS, and imaged using confocal microscopy within 2 days. Click-iT EdU cell proliferation kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10340) was used according to manufacturer's protocol. At approximately 50% to 60% confluency, iPSCs were labelled with 10 µM EdU in E8 media for 2 hours (pulse) and switched to E8 media without EdU for another 2 hours (chase) before fixation.

Sanger Sequencing

Genomic DNA from iPSCs was extracted using the Monarch genomic DNA purification kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA; T3010L). PCR primers were designed using Primer3Plus (www.primer3plus.com). Sequences of the PCR primers are listed in Supplementary Table S6. PCR was performed using DreamTaq PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, K1081), with 30 ng genomic DNA input for each 25-µL reaction. PCR products were purified using QIAquick PCR Purification kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany; 28106) and then sent for Sanger sequencing. Sanger sequencing, digital droplet PCR (ddPCR), and karyotyping were performed by The Centre for Applied Genomics at The Hospital for Sick Children (Toronto, Ontario, Canada).

ddPCR

Detection of edited USH2A was performed using the QX200 Droplet Digital PCR system (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) with TaqMan hydrolysis probe chemistry (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). A 40× custom primer and TaqMan probe mix was designed for the USH2A gene (part number 4332077; sequences listed in Supplementary Table S4). The 20-µL reaction mix consisted of 10 µL of 2× ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP) (Bio-Rad Laboratories, 186-3023), 0.5 µL of the 40× custom assay, 7.5 µL of water, and 2 µL of DNA diluted to approximately 50 ng/µL. The assays were validated using a temperature gradient to ensure optimal separation of reference and edited signals. A mixture of donor template and genomic DNA from iPSC cells was used as a control. Cycling conditions were as follows: 95°C for 10 minutes, followed by 45 cycles of 94°C for 30 seconds and 55°C for 1 minute, then 98°C for 10 minutes, and finally a 10°C hold on a Life Technologies Veriti thermal cycler. All ramp rates were set to 2°C per second. Data were analyzed using QuantaSoft v1.7.4 (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). A mixture of iPSC DNA plus donor template and a nontemplate control were included with each sample run. The ddPCR primers and probes are listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Retinal Organoid Differentiation

Retinal organoids were generated using previously established protocols for two-dimensional (2D) to 3D retinal organoid differentiation.25 Briefly, when human pluripotent stem cells reached 90% to 95% confluence, differentiation was initiated (differentiation day 1 [DD1]) by switching from Essential 8 Medium to Essential 6 Medium (Gibco, A1516401). Fresh E6 medium was provided daily on DD1 and DD2. At DD3, neural induction was initiated using proneural induction medium (PNIM) (Advanced DMEM/F12 [Gibco, 12634010] supplemented with 1% GlutaMax [Gibco, 35050061], 1% N2 supplement [Wisent, 305-016-IL], and 1% penicillin/streptomycin [Wisent, 450-201-EL]). Media was changed every 2 to 3 days. By approximately week 4 of 2D differentiation, when optic vesicle-like structures had formed, cultures were scraped in a checkerboard pattern26 from the adherent plate and transferred to 3D suspension on low-attachment plates coated with poly-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (poly-HEMA; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA; P3932-25G). Once in 3D suspension, half-volume media changes were performed every 2 to 3 days. PNIM was replaced with initial retinal maturation media (IRM) (3:1 DMEM + Glutamax [Gibco, 10569-010]:F12 + Glutamax [Gibco, 31765-035], supplemented with 2% B27 supplement without vitamin A [Gibco, 12587001], and 1% MEM Non-Essential Amino Acids [Gibco, 11140-050]). At week 6, organoids were manually dissected and isolated into individual wells of a 96-well poly-HEMA-coated plate for long-term maintenance. From week 6 to week 9, organoids were given retinal maturation media (MRMM) (IRM with the addition of 10% fetal bovine serum [Wisent, 920-040] and 100 µM taurine [Sigma, T0626]). From week 10 to week 12, 1 µM retinoic acid (RA) (Sigma, R2625) was added to MRMM (MRMM with RA). From week 12 onwards, final retinal maturation medium was fed to organoids (MRMM with 1% N2 supplement and 0.5 µM RA).

Fixation, Sectioning, and Immunohistochemical (IHC) Analysis

Organoids were washed with PBS (Gibco, 14190250), and then incubated in fresh, cold 4% PFA in PBS for 15 minutes. After PFA fixation, organoids were rinsed with PBS, then dehydrated in 30% sucrose in PBS at 4°C overnight. The next day, organoids were rinsed with PBS, then embedded in a cryomold with Neg-50 Frozen Section Medium (Epredia, Kalamazoo, MI, USA; 6502G) and slowly cooled in liquid nitrogen. Organoids were sectioned at 12 µm using a Leica CM3050S cryostat (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and Superfrost Plus slides were stored at −20°C after minimum 30 minutes of air drying.

For IHC staining, slides were rehydrated by rinsing with PBS 3 × 5 minutes. Slides were then permeabilized with 1% Triton X-100 (Sigma, X100-100ML) in PBS for 15 minutes, then washed 3 times with PBS, and blocked with 10% NDS (Cedarlane, Burlington, Ontario, Canada; 017-000-121) in PBS at room temperature for 1 hour. Subsequently, primary antibodies (diluted in 1% NDS + 0.1% Triton X in PBS) were applied, and slides were incubated at 4°C overnight (minimum 16 hours). The next day, primary antibodies were removed and slides were washed with 0.05% Triton X in PBS for 5 minutes, 3 times. Secondary antibodies were applied (diluted in 1% NDS + 0.1% Triton X in PBS) and slides were incubated in darkness at room temperature for 1 hour. After incubation, slides were washed with 0.05% Triton X in PBS for 5 minutes. Hoechst 33342 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4082S) diluted 1:1000 in PBS was applied on slides for 11 minutes, then washed in PBS 3 × 5 minutes and mounted in ProLong Gold Antifade mounting media (Invitrogen, P36930). Image acquisition was conducted using an inverted LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope with a 20× or 40× air objective, and two to four regions of interest (ROIs) were captured per organoid. Image analysis was conducted using HALO (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA; Version 3.5), Imaris (Oxford Instruments, Abingdon, UK; Version 10.11), and Fiji (ImageJ; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MS, USA). For nuclear markers, quantification was performed by calculating the ratio of marker-positive nuclei to the overall number of nuclei within the annotated ROI. For cytosolic markers and for usherin puncta, quantification was based on the marker-positive area (in pixels) relative to the total nuclei-positive area within the annotated ROI.

Details of all antibodies and dilutions used for IHC can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Real-time Quantitative PCR

RNA was extracted from retinal organoids and WERI-Rb1 cells using the NucleoSpin RNA II Kit (Macherey-Nagel, Duren, Germany; 740955.50) or Monarch Total RNA Miniprep Kit (New England Biolabs, T2010). cDNA was synthesized using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix with ezDNase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11766050), and cDNA was diluted in nuclease-free water at a 1:5 to 1:10 dilution, depending on the RT-qPCR experiment. All RT-qPCR reactions were performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System. Reactions were conducted in a 10 µL volume with TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4444557) and TaqMan probes specific to each target gene, according to manufacturer instructions. All genes were expressed relative to GAPDH, which was chosen as the housekeeping gene owing to its stable expression across our retinal organoid samples and its use in previously published retinal organoid and in vivo retinal developmental work.27,28 Data were quantified using the 2−∆∆Ct method. Specific TaqMan probe IDs used for each target gene are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Retinal organoids were fixed in 2% PFA and 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer for 2 hours. Subsequently, samples were rinsed in buffer, and post fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in buffer for 90 minutes. Samples were then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%, 20 minutes each step), followed by two propylene oxide changes for 30 minutes, and embedded in Quetol-Spurr resin. Blocks were cured overnight in the oven at 60°C. Sections 50-nm-thick were cut on a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome, and post stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 3% lead citrate for 20 minutes each and washed for 5 minutes after each staining. Sections were air dried at room temperature before viewing under a Hitachi HT7800 transmission electron microscope.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Retinal organoids were fixed in 2% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, rinsed in buffer, then dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%). The samples were critical point dried in a Bal-tec CPD030 critical point dryer, then mounted on aluminum stubs and gold coated in a Leica ACE200 sputter coater and examined in a Hitachi SU1000 scanning electron microscope. To quantify photoreceptor cell surface density, one 4800-µm2 ROI was selected from the most planar area of the SEM image for each organoid. Photoreceptor inner and outer segments (visually identified as the pedicle-like projections extending from the surface of the organoid) were manually counted by a blinded observer. Data are expressed as photoreceptors per ROI.

Brush Border Analysis

Live imaging of retinal organoid brush border projections was conducted at bi-weekly time points, from week 24 (onset of photoreceptor brush border growth) to week 42 (full maturation) on a Zeiss AxioObserver 7 inverted widefield microscope with phase-contrast at 20× magnification. Brush border projections were measured from live images with the Line Tool in Zen Blue Pro (Zeiss, Jena, Germany; Version 3.6). A measurement line was drawn perpendicular from the outer limiting membrane (OLM) of the organoid to the longest projection at that point, and these measurements were taken at approximately 10-µm intervals, with approximately 20 to 60 measurements taken per ROI, and averaged across 1 to 2 ROIs per organoid.

Whole-mount IHC

Whole-mount IHC of retinal organoids was performed as previously described26 with modifications. Briefly, organoids were fixed for 15 minutes in cold 4% PFA at room temperature, followed by 3 × 5 minutes washes with PBS, then incubated in blocking buffer A (10% NDS, 1% BSA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.02% sodium azide in PBS) for 48 hours at room temperature. After this, primary antibodies (1.5× standard IHC concentration) diluted in blocking buffer B (3% NDS, 1% BSA, 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.02% sodium azide in PBS) were applied for 48 hours at 4°C. After incubation, samples were washed 3 times for 1 hour each wash with PBS with 0.05% Triton X-100. Samples were then incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer B (1:500) for an additional 48 hours at 4°C, followed by a 1-hour wash in PBS + 0.05% Triton X-100. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:500) for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by wash 3 times for 1 hour each wash with PBS. To clear organoids, 50% CUBIC1 reagent diluted in dH2O was added to the immune-stained organoids and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. Then 50% CUBIC1 reagent was removed, and organoids were incubated in 100% CUBIC1 reagent at 37°C for 2 to 4 days. New 100% CUBIC1 reagent was added to the organoids each day. CUBIC1 reagent is composed of dH2O with 25 wt% urea (Sigma), 25 wt% Quadrol, and 15 wt% Triton X-100.29 Organoids were imaged on an inverted LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope with a 20× objective in chamber slides or 48-well cell culture plates. Z-stacks with 6-µm intervals throughout the whole organoids were taken. Whole-mount retinal organoid images were analyzed using Imaris version 10.2 and ImageJ2 version 2.14.0/1.54f.

TUNEL Assay

PFA-fixed retinal organoid sections for the week 42 cell death analysis were subjected to TUNEL assay using the Invitrogen Click-iT Plus TUNEL Assay Kits for In Situ Apoptosis Detection (Thermo Fisher Scientific, C10619) as described by the manufacturer. Sections were imaged using an LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope with a 40× air objective, and 2-4 ROIs were captured per section. TUNEL-positive cells were counted manually.

Neuroblastic Layer Thickness Measurements

Live images of retinal organoids at week 6, week 7, and week 8 were taken on a Zeiss AxioObserver 7 inverted widefield microscope with phase-contrast at 20× magnification; or, for immunofluorescent measurements of neuroblastic layer thickness at week 8, images were taken on an inverted LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope with a 20× air objective. For each retinal organoid, 5 to 10 measurements of neuroblastic layer thickness were taken around the whole organoid and an average was calculated as the designated neuroblastic layer thickness.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses and graphs were completed with GraphPad (Prism, version 10.0.0 [131]; La Jolla, CA, USA). For bar graphs, each dot represents an individual organoid. Normal distribution was determined by D'Agostino–Pearson omnibus normality test, Shapiro–Wilk test, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov test with Dallal–Wilkinson–Lillie correction. Student's t test was used to assess differences between two groups. One-way ANOVA was used to assess differences across more than two groups; if a main effect was present, Tukey's multiple comparisons test was applied. Significance was established at a P value of 0.05 or less. Statistical significance (P values) were only shown where differences were detected. Details on specific P values, values of N (number of independent experiments), values of n (number of organoids per group, per independent experiment), and how data are presented can be found in the figure legends.

Results

Generation of iPSC-derived Retinal Organoids From Healthy Human Donors and Patients With USH2A-associated RP

For this study, peripheral blood mononuclear cells were isolated from a male patient with Usher syndrome and reprogrammed into iPSCs using Sendai virus-based delivery of reprogramming factors. Fundus images reflect the clinical phenotype of the patient, including bilateral optic disc pallor and peripheral retinal bone spicule pigmentation (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Patient-derived iPSCs showed normal stem cell morphology (Supplementary Fig. S1B); robust expression of pluripotency markers SOX2, OCT4, SSEA4, and TRA-1-60 by flow cytometry (Supplementary Fig. S1C); and a normal karyotype (46, XY) with no abnormalities (Supplementary Fig. S1D). Sanger sequencing confirmed that patient-derived USH2A iPSCs carried compound heterozygous mutations in the USH2A gene: a missense mutation (c.1256G>T) and a frameshift mutation (c.2299delG). The c.2299delG mutation leads to a premature stop codon and is predicted to result in a truncated, nonfunctional usherin protein (Supplementary Fig. S1E). The c.2299delG mutation is the most common pathogenic USH2A variant associated with Usher syndrome type II, accounting for approximately 25% of disease alleles.30,31 The human control iPSC line used for this study was generated from a healthy male individual with no history of retinal disorders, obtained through the Personal Genome Project Canada,24 and showed a wild-type sequence at both loci (Supplementary Fig. S1F).

We induced retinal organoid differentiation in control and patient-derived USH2A iPSCs using a previously described 2D to 3D retinal organoid differentiation protocol25 (Fig. 1A). Consistent with previous reports,25 we could maintain these organoids for long periods in culture (>30 weeks). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the presence of typical photoreceptor brush border formation (composed of putative photoreceptor inner and outer segments) extending from the apical edge of the organoid (Fig. 1B, left) at a late stage of maturation (week 42) in control organoids. Ultrastructural components of mature photoreceptors were present in healthy week 42 organoids, including mitochondria-rich inner segments, connecting cilia, and outer segments with characteristic stacked membranous discs (Fig. 1B, middle and right).

Rod-specific Defects in USH2A Patient Retinal Organoids Coincide With Developmental Upregulation of USH2A Gene Expression During Retinogenesis and Persist to Maturation

To better understand when USH2A mutations start to impact retinal differentiation and maturation, we first generated a time course of USH2A gene expression from pluripotent stem cell stage (week 0) through retinal organoid development (Fig. 1C). Human retinoblastoma cells (WERI-Rb1) were used as a positive control for USH2A gene expression32 and the probe used detected both major transcript isoforms (A and B), reflecting total USH2A transcript expression. USH2A expression remained low during early development (weeks 0–12), then began to increase between weeks 16 and 20, coinciding with the first observed formation of photoreceptor brush border.33,34 At week 20, transcript levels of USH2A were similar between healthy and USH2A patient retinal organoids. By week 34, USH2A expression had markedly increased in healthy controls but remained significantly lower in USH2A patient organoids (Fig. 1C).

To confirm whether phenotypic differences between healthy and USH2A patient retinal organoids exist before the onset of USH2A gene expression, we analyzed protein and gene expression of early retina-specific markers at week 6 (Figs. 1D–G). We did not observe significant differences in early retinal markers CHX10, OTX2, or CRX by protein (Figs. 1D, 1G), or corresponding gene expression (Fig. 1E) between healthy and USH2A patient organoids at week 6. In addition, there were no significant differences in markers of proliferation, including width of the neuroblastic layer (NBL)—a transient proliferative zone in early retinogenesis that precedes outer nuclear layer (ONL) and inner nuclear layer (INL) formation (Fig. 1F)—or mRNA expression of the proliferation marker KI67 between healthy and USH2A patient organoids at week 6 (Fig. 1E).

During midstage retinal organoid development (week 20), gene- and protein-level differences between healthy and USH2A patient organoids began to emerge, with differences observed in rod markers. USH2A patient organoids showed significantly reduced labelling of the rod-specific photoreceptor marker NRL (Figs. 2A, 2B), with a corresponding decrease in NRL mRNA expression (Fig. 2C) compared with controls. Usherin expression was reduced in USH2A patient organoids compared with controls, although this reduction was not statistically significant (Fig. 2D). In addition, the apoptosis gene cleaved caspase-3 (CASP-3) was significantly upregulated in USH2A patient organoids (Fig. 2C); and, structurally, the ONL (composed of photoreceptor nuclei) was significantly thinner compared with healthy organoids (Fig. 2E). Together, these results support that molecular and structural defects emerge in midstage developing USH2A patient-derived organoids. Onset of these defects coincided with upregulation of USH2A expression at week 20, during the beginnings of photoreceptor inner and outer segment formation in our organoids (Fig. 2A, phase).

To assess disease progression at more mature stages of retinal organoid development, we examined USH2A patient organoids at week 34. By this time, USH2A transcript levels were significantly lower in USH2A patient organoids compared with controls, coinciding with further reductions in rod photoreceptor markers at both the gene (NRL; Fig. 2F) and protein levels (rhodopsin staining via whole-mount IHC; Fig. 2H). At week 34, USH2A patient organoids also showed significantly decreased mRNA expression of CRX and the cone-specific marker ARR3 (Fig. 2F), although at the protein level, there were no differences in labelling with the cone-specific marker L/M-opsin between groups via whole-mount IHC (Fig. 2H). Usherin expression was significantly decreased in week 34 USH2A patient organoids compared with controls (Fig. 2G). Structurally, the ONL was significantly thinner compared with healthy organoids, with no significant differences between groups in terms of INL thickness (Supplementary Fig. S2A–C). These findings demonstrate that by week 34, rod-specific structural loss is pronounced in patient-derived USH2A organoids, whereas cones appear relatively preserved—consistent with the early stages of clinical rod–cone dystrophy in USH2A RP. The absence of cone degeneration indicates that studying the advanced stages of pathogenesis for these organoids requires longer-term maturation beyond week 34, which was the focus of our next step.

Late-stage USH2A Patient Retinal Organoids Exhibit Rod–Cone Dystrophy and Pronounced Inner and Outer Segment Defects Reflective of Advanced RP

A consistent clinical feature of USH2A RP is the progressive loss of photoreceptor inner and outer segments, first in rods, then in cones, in advanced disease.35 At week 34, we identified that rod-specific defects persisted in USH2A patient organoids, consistent with early features of rod–cone dystrophy, but overt cone degeneration had not yet emerged. Therefore, we extended our cultures and analysis to week 42 to determine whether cone degeneration would manifest in our organoids at this late stage, recapitulating the advanced-stage rod–cone dystrophy seen clinically. At week 42, USH2A patient organoids had a marked decrease in expression of rhodopsin (Figs. 3A, 3B), L/M-opsin (Figs. 3C, 3D), and ATP synthase (Figs. 3E, 3F) compared with controls, demonstrating inner and outer segment degeneration in both rods and cones. In addition, USH2A patient organoids had a significantly thinner photoreceptor ONL, as detected by staining with CRX costained with NRL (Figs. 3G, 3H). Staining by apoptosis markers CASP-3 (Figs. 3I, 3J) and TUNEL (Supplementary Fig. S2D, E) did not reveal differences in the number of apoptotic cells in the ONL between control and USH2A patient organoids, suggesting that most photoreceptor degeneration has already occurred by week 42.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the retinal organoid brush border length can be indicative of disease in models of ciliopathy.36,37 To better understand how our organoids change as they mature from week 24 to week 42, we first tracked brush border outgrowth using live phase-contrast imaging (Fig. 4A). The brush border length was similar between groups from week 24 to week 38; however, by week 42, USH2A patient organoids exhibited a significant reduction in brush border length compared with controls (Fig. 4B). To reconcile why differences in brush border length did not match temporally with retinal gene and protein downregulation, we analyzed photoreceptor density using whole-mount imaging and SEM. Whole mount staining of recoverin (RCVN) in optically cleared organoids enabled the quantification of photoreceptors across the apical surface of the organoid, and revealed significantly decreased photoreceptor density in USH2A patient organoids at week 28 compared with controls (Figs. 4D, 4E), despite similar brush border lengths between groups at this stage. This finding suggests that early photoreceptor loss precedes the degeneration of their inner and outer segment structures. By week 42, USH2A patient organoids showed further significantly decreased photoreceptor density compared with controls, as detected by SEM (Figs. 4F, 4G), consistent with progressive structural degeneration over time in both rods and cones.

Together, these findings show that USH2A patient organoids have reduced photoreceptor density beginning at week 28, and remaining photoreceptors exhibit preserved brush border length at this stage. By week 42, however, significant structural degeneration of photoreceptor inner and outer segments is evident in both rods and cones.

Engineered, Isogenic USH2A(−/−) Retinal Organoids Show Structural, Proliferative, and Survival Deficits in Early Development

Because our USH2A patient line and healthy donor line were derived from allogenic individuals, it remains possible that our observed differences are due to the independent genetic background of each line. To address this limitation, we used CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing to generate isogenic USH2A-mutant iPSCs in the healthy control iPSC background to create engineered retinal organoids with either heterozygous or homozygous c.2299delG mutations. Since USH2A diseases are inherited in an autosomal recessive manner, and heterozygous carriers of pathogenic USH2A mutations have no clinical phenotype, heterozygous (iso-USH2A(+/−)) retinal organoids were used as an additional control to account for any unintended impacts of CRISPR editing, such as potential off-target effects. A single guide RNA was designed in the vicinity of the c.2299G locus, with a knockin donor template with 113bp/83bp homology arms flanking the delG mutation (for sequences, see Supplementary Table S3). A silent G>A mutation was introduced in the PAM site within the donor template to ensure irreversible editing and to discriminate edited from wild-type alleles. ddPCR assays were designed and validated to detect nonedited iPSCs and iPSCs with successful homology-directed repair events by the CRISPR donor template (Supplementary Table S4). Both Sanger sequencing and ddPCR confirmed the successful knockin of heterozygous and homozygous (iso-USH2A(−/−)) c.2299delG mutations (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Both iso-USH2A(+/−) and iso-USH2A(−/−) iPSCs showed healthy morphology and expression of pluripotency markers SOX2, OCT4, and NANOG (Supplementary Fig. S3B), and iso-USH2A(−/−) iPSCs had a normal karyotype (Supplementary Fig. S3C). No off-target edits were detected in these CRISPR-engineered mutant iPSCs by Sanger sequencing (Supplementary Table S5). In addition, EdU assay and phospho-histone H3 staining, S-phase and M-phase cell markers, respectively, showed no intrinsic difference in the proliferative capacities between control, iso-USH2A(+/−), and iso-USH2A(−/−) iPSCs after CRISPR editing (Supplementary Fig. S3D).

We first identified a structural difference in NBL width during early retinal organoid development. The iso-USH2A(−/−) retinal organoids displayed significantly reduced NBL thickness compared with both iso-USH2A(+/−) and healthy organoids from week 6 to week 8 of development when measured from live phase images (Figs. 5A–C). The reduction in week 8 iso-USH2A(−/−) NBL thickness was further confirmed in cryosections as marked by CHX10 and OTX2 costaining of retinal progenitor cells (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S4A). No differences were found between the healthy and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids.

To uncover what might account for these early NBL thickness differences, we investigated retinal progenitor cell proliferation and survival. Iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids showed reduced EdU labelling (Figs. 5D, 5E) and phospho-histone H3 staining (Supplementary Fig. S4B, C) compared with both iso-USH2A(+/−) and control organoids, indicating decreased proliferation. CASP-3 staining showed a significantly increased number of apoptotic cells in iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids compared with both iso-USH2A(+/−) and control organoids (Figs. 5F, 5G). Thus, iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids show early defects in retinal progenitor cell proliferation and survival, suggesting that USH2A may play a role in early retinal development even at the earliest stages of photoreceptor specification in retinal organoids.

Engineered Isogenic USH2A(−/−) Retinal Organoids Exhibit Mid-development Rod Photoreceptor Loss

We next examined the effect of the homozygous c.2299delG mutation during retinogenesis in midstage retinal organoids, when postmitotic photoreceptors are emerging but are not yet fully mature. Iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids showed significantly decreased expression of RCVN compared with control and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids at week 16 (Supplementary Fig. S4D, E), suggesting fewer photoreceptors and less elaboration of inner and outer segments. Costaining for CRX and NRL revealed that iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids showed a significantly lower proportion of rod photoreceptors (CRX+/NRL+) and a greater proportion of cone photoreceptors (CRX+/NRL−) than control and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids at this stage (Figs. 5H, 5I). No significant differences were found between the healthy and iso-USH2A(+/−) retinal organoids. Additionally, we found that iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids showed increased photoreceptor apoptosis marked by CASP-3 in the ONL compared with control and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids (Fig. 5J). By costaining CASP-3 with CRX and NRL, we found that although both rods and cones showed increased cell death in iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids, there were significantly more rods than cones undergoing apoptosis (Figs. 5K, 5L). These results suggest that the homozygous USH2A c.2299delG mutation impairs photoreceptor survival at week 16 of retinal organoid development, with rod photoreceptors preferentially affected. This finding is consistent with clinical observations in USH2A-RP, where rods are primarily affected by USH2A mutations, followed by subsequent cone degeneration.

Late-stage Photoreceptor Inner and Outer Segment Loss Occurs by Week 28 in Engineered Isogenic USH2A(−/−) Organoids

We next assessed the late pathogenic phenotype of iso-USH2A(−/−) retinal organoids at week 28. Our iso-USH2A(−/−) retinal organoids showed significantly decreased photoreceptor density by whole mount IHC for RCVN compared with iso-USH2A(+/−) and healthy organoids (Figs. 6A, 6B). Through fixed-section IHC, we observed that iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids had significantly decreased expression of usherin (Figs. 6C, 6D). In addition, iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids displayed a thinner ONL (marked by CRX) compared with healthy and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids (Figs. 6E, 6F). In particular, we found a significant reduction in the proportion of rods (CRX+ and NRL+) in the ONL of iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids, compared with healthy and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids (Fig. 6G). By staining for rod and cone inner and outer segment markers, we saw that iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids had significantly reduced expression of rhodopsin (Figs. 6H, 6I) and L/M-opsin (Figs. 6J, 6K) compared with iso-USH2A(+/−) and control organoids. ATP synthase expression in inner segments was also reduced, although not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. S4F). Notably, the integrity of the OLM—the structural barrier composed of junctional complexes between photoreceptors and Müller glial cells, and at the apical side of the photoreceptors ONL—was significantly disrupted in iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids (marked by zonula occludens-1), whereas healthy and iso-USH2A(+/−) organoids showed continuous OLMs (Supplementary Fig. S4G, H). It is possible that disruption of OLM integrity is secondary to the loss of photoreceptors in the iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids after maturation. There were no significant differences in the expression of late-stage bipolar cell marker CHX10 in the INL between groups at week 28 (Supplementary Fig. S4I, J). There were also no observable differences in the presence or organization of Müller glia cells (identified by glutamine synthetase) between groups at week 24 (Supplementary Fig. S4K).

Taken together, these results suggest that the introduction of the USH2A homozygous c.2299delG mutation into a healthy donor background induces the degeneration of mature photoreceptors and their inner and outer segment structures in late-stage retinal organoids, preferentially affecting rod photoreceptors. These results are consistent with the rod–cone degeneration pattern of USH2A-associated RP, as observed in our patient-derived USH2A disease organoids.

Discussion

In this study, we established novel human retinal organoid disease models of USH2A-associated RP derived from patient iPSCs with compound heterozygous USH2A mutations (c.1256G>T and c.2299delG), and from an isogenic CRISPR-Cas9-engineered homozygous mutant line (c.2299delG) generated on a healthy background. Both USH2A-diseased organoid models exhibit evidence of late-stage retinal degeneration and loss of photoreceptor density, mimicking the human clinical phenotype. Furthermore, we could discern preferential rod-specific defects, consistent with the rod–cone dystrophy nature of USH2A disease. Our data suggest that there is a developmental component to USH2A disease stemming from defects in progenitor cell proliferation and photoreceptor maturation and survival. In patient-derived USH2A-mutated retinal organoids, we observed structural and molecular defects during mid-developmental stages, when postmitotic photoreceptors are emerging but are still maturing their inner and outer segments and when USH2A gene upregulation is occurring (approximately week 20).

Our data indicate that USH2A organoids have fewer rod photoreceptors by mid-development (weeks 16–20), consistent with an early deficit in photoreceptor cell number. This is likely the result of a limited progenitor pool, as shown by decreased NBL thickness and reduced proliferation early in development (week 8). Importantly, however, despite these early defects, we show that USH2A organoids are still capable of generating rods and cones that mature with inner and outer segment–like structures. Indeed, brush border length remains comparable between USH2A organoids and controls until advanced maturity (after week 38). The presence of outer-segment-like structures (although limited) in USH2A organoids at weeks 28 to 30, followed by the progressive loss of these structures by week 42, supports the interpretation that some rods (and subsequently, cones), do mature with outer segment–like structures, but later undergo true degeneration. Therefore, the observed decrease in photoreceptor density in USH2A organoids at late stages may be attributable to a combination of both impaired development and subsequent degeneration. Whether opsin protein trafficking is impaired across the connecting cilium to prevent full maturation could be explored in more depth by SEM and whole-mount IHC during mid-development stages. In addition, the pronounced early developmental defects we demonstrate, including decreased NBL thickness, reduced retinal cell proliferation, and increased cell death as early as week 6 of development, highlight a potential role for USH2A in early retinal development that has not been previously explored. Importantly, this work suggests that an early treatment window may be needed to detect rescue effects when assessing therapeutic strategies for this disease.

Our findings provide the most comprehensive analysis of USH2A-diseased organoids to date, with observations across key developmental stages. Only a few previous studies have used human USH2A retinal organoids to find preliminary insight into the early21 and later22 phenotypes of disease progression. A recent paper by Sanjurjo-Soriano et al.22 described cone photoreceptor gene and protein upregulation in mature Usher patient-derived USH2A organoids homozygous for the c.2299delG mutation, relative to allogenic healthy controls. In contrast, both our patient-derived and our isogenic, engineered USH2A-mutant organoids exhibited rod-selective defects within general photoreceptor cell loss from mid to late development. This result is likely due in part to our use of whole-mount techniques, which enabled us to quantify broad regions more representative of the organoid, rather than independent cryosections. A known limitation of retinal organoid work is iPSC line-to-line variability in differentiation capacity,38 which could explain the phenotypic differences observed between our iso-USH2A(−/−) organoids and this group's patient-derived line, despite both being homozygous for the same pathogenic mutation. The fact that we were able to introduce clinically relevant disease effects into an otherwise healthy retinal organoid model provides strong evidence that our retinal defects are specific to the c.2299delG mutation. Importantly, our iso-USH2A(+/−) control organoids were essentially indistinguishable from the healthy parent line, supporting our conclusion that these retinal defects did not arise because of off-target effects from gene editing itself, and recapitulate the well-established autosomal-recessive nature of USH2A disease.

Our new observations of apical structure malformation in USH2A-diseased organoids could lead to further understanding of a developmental phenotype for USH2A disease. By applying techniques and methodologies not previously used in USH2A organoid modeling, including SEM and whole-mount IHC to analyze brush border density, we captured structural changes in organoids with greater resolution and in greater breadth than previously examined. SEM images of week 42 USH2A patient organoids confirmed a significant decrease in mature photoreceptor density compared with healthy controls, correlating with our observed reduction in photoreceptor protein expression in fixed-section IHC at this timepoint. Diseased organoids exhibited malformed putative outer segments and an abundance of microfilament structures. These findings were particularly striking, given that observations such as these have only been seen in vivo in mouse retinal dystrophy models; for example, in previous studies of SEM in CRX-null mice (Fig. 4 in Morrow et al., 200539) as well as rhodopsin- and peripherin-null mouse retinas (Fig. 6 in Morrow et al., 200539). These observations have not been made in human retinal organoids nor in native human retina from patients with disease. It has been hypothesized that these filaments may reflect the breakdown of outer segment membranes improperly formed, or could be the result of the breakdown of Müller cell microvilli.40

In parallel, we established a detailed time course of brush border growth and development across organoid maturation, extending to week 42—the longest time point reported for USH2A organoids (and most other IRD organoid models) to date. The use of various brush border quantification modalities allowed us to determine that loss of outer segment marker density actually precedes brush border length loss in our USH2A patient organoids. Although brush border lengths only significantly diverged between healthy and USH2A patient organoids after week 38 of development, our whole-mount IHC revealed decreased rod outer segment marker density a full 10 weeks earlier, at week 28 of development. The importance of analyzing whole-organoids to avoid selection bias has been stressed by other groups in the field. For instance, Capowski et al.34 performed OCT and whole-mount IHC of staged retinal organoids and were thereby able to uncover distinct anatomical features that were not otherwise apparent by light microscopy or in sectioned samples. Our multimodal approach has provided greater resolution and a more accurate timeline of USH2A disease progression in retinal organoids compared to previous reports, which will be useful when determining critical intervention windows for testing and assessing novel treatments.

By examining early- to mid-developmental defects in patient-derived and isogenic(−/−) organoids, we have significantly built upon the preliminary work of Guo et al.,21 who focused on the earliest stages of pathogenesis in USH2A disease (days 0–86) using a novel patient-derived organoid model. Our work shared some similar findings related to early retinal abnormalities, including reduced NBL width, decreased proliferation, and increased cell death by week 8. In their RNA-seq analysis, Guo et al. reported significant downregulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) genes, such as laminin, in USH2A-diseased organoids up to day 86 (approximately week 12), suggesting that cell adhesion defects could underlie poor retinal proliferation.21 These results should be interpreted cautiously, because the variant in their patient-derived iPSC line (c.9127_9129delTCC) has not been definitively classified as pathogenic. Still, ECM disruption remains a plausible explanation for the early retinal defects observed in our USH2A patient organoids. The usherin protein contains several highly conserved laminin EGF domain repeats, which supports a possible role in ECM function.41 Whether similar ECM changes occur in our USH2A disease models will be investigated in future studies.

It is important to point out that, in our models, the establishment of a developmental phenotype uncovers disease features that may not yet be fully appreciated in the clinical context. Many patients receive an USH2A RP diagnosis in early adulthood, only after visual symptoms, such as night blindness or peripheral vision loss, appear.42 However, the developmental defects we observed during organoid retinogenesis emerged well before or at the onset of photoreceptor maturation, corresponding to approximately mid-gestation through to early postnatal development in humans.13 This suggests that the cellular pathology of USH2A RP begins far earlier than symptom onset. Previous studies suggest that the timing of disease manifestation in retinal organoid models can provide some insight into disease progression in patients. For example, patient-derived and engineered organoid models of RP2-associated X-linked RP demonstrated pronounced rod-specific defects in organoids corresponding to approximately week 21.43 Additionally, patient-derived RPGR organoid models have shown early ciliary dysfunction and opsin mislocalization at week 22 of development, adding new insight to the traditional view of RPGR RP as childhood-onset disorder and suggesting it may have early developmental origins.44 In contrast, a recent organoid model of RDH12 autosomal dominant RP, known to be a later-onset disease, did not demonstrate photoreceptor defects until approximately week 44.45 Our retinal organoid models demonstrate the developmental origin of USH2A disease and highlight a critical window for early diagnosis and therapeutic intervention.

Limitations of the Study

A key limitation of our study is the differences in pathogenic genotypes of our patient-derived and mutation-engineered USH2A iPSC lines. Both lines harbor the c.2299delG mutation, which is the most common pathogenic variant among USH2A patients30,31; however, this mutation is compound heterozygous with the c.1256G>T mutation in our patient line, whereas our engineered disease line is homozygous for c.2299delG. An allelic hierarchy of USH2A pathogenic variants causing either syndromic (i.e., Usher syndrome) or nonsyndromic (i.e., USH2A-associated RP) disease46 has been proposed; however, why these distinct phenotypes arise from particular variants is not yet understood. Although both our patient-derived and engineered disease genotypes are associated with Usher syndrome, there may be subtle differences in the underlying molecular mechanisms of disease. The c.2299delG variant is a protein-truncating mutation, effectively causing USH2A loss-of-function. In contrast, the c.1256G>T variant is a missense mutation that is nontruncating and may result in translated but misfolded usherin protein.23 To interrogate this further, future studies could include an isogenic USH2A disease line engineered with the compound heterozygous c.1256G>T genotype (i.e., on the wild-type allele of our c.2299delG(+/−) control line), to enable direct comparisons between these genotypes. In parallel, a homozygous engineered c.1256G>T iPSC line would allow us to dissect the specific effect of this mutation in retinal development and maturation. More broadly, our engineered USH2A disease platform, alongside patient-derived diseased lines, will provide a future avenue to identify variant-specific differences in USH2A pathobiology in an isogenic human organoid model, and improving on previous attempts to determine genotype–phenotype correlations associated with syndromic and nonsyndromic USH2A-RP.

Conclusions

This study established the first comprehensive developmental model of USH2A-associated RP that captures previously uncharacterized structural and temporal features of the disease across all stages of retinal growth and maturation. By defining the underlying pathogenesis of USH2A RP during development, our findings underscore the importance of early intervention for future therapeutic strategies.
