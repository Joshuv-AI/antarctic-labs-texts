Case report of a rare clinical triad in mitral valve prolapse: when ventricular arrhythmias coexist with thromboembolic complications
Authors: Francesco Foschini, Valentina Mantegazza, Fabrizio Tundo, Claudio Tondo, Gianluca Pontone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitral valve prolapse (MVP) has traditionally been considered a benign condition, yet malignant ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) may occur. This case illustrates a rare overlap of complex VAs, thromboembolic stroke, and myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) in a patient with MVP, underscoring the value of multimodality imaging and electrophysiological assessment for arrhythmic risk stratification.Case summaryA 50-year-old woman with MVP was referred to our centre for arrhythmic risk assessment after documentation of frequent premature ventricular complexes and polymorphic non-sustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT) on Holter monitoring. Cardiac magnetic resonance revealed transmural late gadolinium enhancement (ischaemic pattern) in the mid-lateral left ventricular wall. Introduction

Arrhythmic mitral valve prolapse (A-MVP) is characterized by frequent and/or complex ventricular arrhythmias (VAs) in the absence of other arrhythmogenic substrates.1 Arrhythmic mitral valve prolapse has been associated with specific electrocardiography (ECG) and Holter findings, morphological features, and left ventricular (LV) late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR).1–3 While secondary-prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) is recommended in patients with documented ventricular fibrillation (VF) or haemodynamically not-tolerated ventricular tachycardia (VT), the role of primary-prevention ICD in MVP remains uncertain.1,2

We report the case of a female patient with mitral valve prolapse (MVP) and mild mitral regurgitation (MR), previous ischaemic stroke, and myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA), presenting with complex VAs. This case emphasizes the importance of multidisciplinary approach to MVP patients, allowing arrhythmic phenotyping and individualized management.1

Case presentation

A 50-year-old woman with hypertension and MVP was referred for arrhythmic risk stratification after complex VAs’ documentation at 24-h Holter monitoring and exercise testing.

As summarized in Figure 1, 8 months earlier, she had been admitted for dysarthria. Computed tomography of the supra-aortic trunks was normal. Brain magnetic resonance imaging revealed three subacute thromboembolic ischaemic lesions. Aspirin was started 100 mg daily and 24-h Holter monitoring recommended. Three days later, she was readmitted for chest pain, inferolateral subepicardial ischaemia at ECG, and elevated cardiac enzymes. Coronary angiography showed no stenosis; transoesophageal echocardiography excluded patent foramen ovale and left atrial (LA) thrombosis; thrombophilic screening was negative. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed MVP with mild-to-moderate MR and severe LA dilatation. The patient was discharged on statin, dual antiplatelet therapy, bisoprolol 1.25 mg daily, with a diagnosis of MINOCA and recommendations for ambulatory 24h-Holter and CMR. Holter monitoring documented a clinically significant VAs burden: 11 830 polymorphic premature ventricular complexes (PVCs), 171 pairs, 8 triplets, and 2 runs of non-sustained VT (NSVT) with torsades de pointes–like morphology (Figure 2A). Bisoprolol was replaced with metoprolol 75 mg daily. Three weeks later, 24-h Holter showed 7092 PVCs with 48 pairs and 1 triplet; an exercise test induced a 4-beat NSVT with left bundle-branch morphology and superior axis (Figure 2B).

At presentation to our centre, the patient reported occasional palpitations. Cardiac auscultation revealed a mid-systolic mitral click and grade 2/6 apical murmur. Electrocardiography showed sinus bradycardia without repolarization abnormalities. Transthoracic echocardiography confirmed bileaflet thickening and prolapse, mild MR, a biplane LA volume of 74 mL/m2 (which is markedly higher than the cut-off value indicated by guidelines for severe LA dilatation),4 and LV enlargement (end-diastolic volume = 76 mL/m2) with mid-inferolateral akinesia and preserved ejection fraction (EF) (see Supplementary material online, Videos S1–S4). Cardiac magnetic resonance confirmed non-significant MR, LV dilatation, focal akinesia and thinning of the mid-lateral LV wall, basal inferolateral curling without mitral annular disjunction (MAD), and a borderline LVEF. Transmural LGE of the mid-lateral LV wall and posteromedial papillary muscle, along with increased native T1 and extracellular volume values, suggested an ischaemic scar (Figure 3). This distribution of LGE and CMR-mapping abnormalities was atypical for MVP-related fibrosis and allowed exclusion of other potential myocardial diseases (e.g. Takotsubo syndrome or myocarditis). During electrophysiological study (EPS), programmed ventricular stimulation with a standard protocol (two extrastimuli after a 600 ms drive) induced a rapid 11-s self-terminating polymorphic NSVT, reproducible on repeated pacing (Figure 2C). A sustained episode of atrial fibrillation (AF) also occurred, requiring electrical cardioversion. Given the patient’s high-risk phenotypic features (redundant and thickened mitral valve leaflets, severe LA dilatation, CMR-documented fibrosis, frequent PVCs, polymorphic spontaneous and inducible NSVT), primary-prevention ICD was implanted. At 3-month follow-up, ICD interrogation detected asymptomatic paroxysmal AF. With a CHADS2VAsc2 score of 4, antiplatelet therapy was discontinued, and oral anticoagulant therapy initiated, associated with flecainide 100 mg daily and metoprolol. Six months later, Holter monitoring recorded 191 isolated PVCs of a predominant morphology, with brief episodes of bi- and trigeminy. Stress test documented isolated PVCs, occasionally bigeminal, disappearing at peak exercise. Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator interrogation revealed two further AF episodes lasting 1 h each; flecainide was increased to 200 mg daily and catheter ablation proposed. No sustained VAs were documented.

Discussion

This case illustrates overlap of high-risk VAs, ischaemic stroke, and MINOCA in MVP. Patients with MVP in sinus rhythm exhibit an increased risk of ischaemic stroke, independently predicted by age and leaflet thickening, and partly by LA enlargement.5 Recent studies in Barlow’s disease suggest that LA remodelling may be disproportionate to MR grade, indicating early atrial myocardial involvement in these patients.6 The concept of atrial cardiomyopathy has emerged as a potential cause of stroke, independent of AF documentation.7 A primary myopathic process may represent also the substrate for VAs. In MVP, VAs are thought to arise from mechanical stretching and fibrosis of the inferolateral LV wall and papillary muscles, especially in the presence of bileaflet prolapse, curling, and MAD.8 Such fibrosis typically appears as mid-wall or patchy LGE on CMR.

Given our patient’s phenotypic and arrhythmic risk features, ICD implantation could have been directly considered, particularly in view of the controversial prognostic value of EPS in MVP.1,2 However, in this case, transmural LGE in the mid-lateral LV wall suggested an ischaemic scar, consistent with previous MINOCA, rather than typical A-MVP fibrosis. In patients with ischaemic heart disease, preserved LVEF, and haemodynamically tolerated VTs—including polymorphic forms—EPS is a reasonable diagnostic step, as inducible monomorphic VTs could be suitable for catheter ablation to prevent future ICD interventions.9,10 In our patient, ICD implantation was primarily guided by clinical and imaging high-risk features1 and supported by reproducible induction of rapid polymorphic NSVTs using low-frequency burst pacing. Our decision was shared after a multidisciplinary discussion and was endorsed by the patient after she had been informed of the potential short- and long-term risks and benefits. A subcutaneous ICD was preferred to a transvenous system to prevent long-term potential lead’s complications, considering the patient’s age. This was supported by the non-inducibility of sustained VTs potentially requiring anti-tachycardia pacing. Premature ventricular complex ablation was not pursued since the patient was paucisymptomatic and ectopy was polymorphic, likely originating from the papillary muscles and thus suggesting poorer outcomes.11 Surgical mitral valve repair was also deferred, as MR was mild and current evidence does not support a reduction in arrhythmic risk after correction of MR and annular abnormalities.12

Subsequent detection of AF likely provided a unifying mechanism for previous ischaemic stroke and MINOCA, although it was not possible to definitively prove causality between AF and the embolic events. Despite the presence of ischaemic myocardial scar, we considered it reasonable to introduce a class IC antiarrhythmic agent to reduce atrial and ventricular arrhythmic burden, given the absence of coronary artery stenosis on cardiac CT, lack of inducible ischaemia on exercise testing, absence of spontaneous or inducible sustained VTs, and protection afforded by the ICD in the event of proarrhythmic effects of flecainide. The severely dilated LA likely favoured asymptomatic AF episodes, predisposing to both the cardioembolic stroke and coronary embolism, which in turn resulted in MINOCA (Summary figure).

The arrhythmic burden in this patient cannot be ascribed solely to A-MVP. Rather, it reflects the convergence of MVP-related structural abnormalities, ischaemic scar formation, and LA cardiomyopathy, underscoring the complexity of arrhythmic risk assessment in MVP. This case exemplifies how an integrated multimodality approach—including echocardiography, CMR, Holter monitoring, exercise testing, and EPS—is essential to define the arrhythmic substrate and guide individualized ICD decision-making.
