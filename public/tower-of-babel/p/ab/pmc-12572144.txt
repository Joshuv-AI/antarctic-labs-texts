Public perception of environmental sanitation regulations on solid waste management in Owo and Akure cities, Ondo State, Nigeria
Authors: Modupe Mulikat Ologunagba, Timothy Oyebamiji Ogunbode, Olumide Michael Owoeye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSanitation is critical for public health, environmental sustainability, and community well-being. Beyond maintaining clean surroundings, effective sanitation reduces stress, anxiety, and disease burdens. However, in Nigeria, particularly in urban centres like Akure and Owo, compliance with environmental sanitation laws remains inconsistent. Understanding residents’ perceptions of these laws is therefore essential for strengthening solid waste management systems. This study employed a quantitative research design using structured questionnaires administered to residents in Akure and Owo, Ondo State. The survey of 600 questionnaires with 350 and 250 from Akure and Owo respectively yielded 100% retrieval. Data were analysed using Factor Analysis with SPSS software to identify key factors influencing perceptions of environmental sanitation regulations. In Akure, five principal components explained 64.2% of the variance in perception. Community leadership was the most significant factor, accounting for 20.5% of the variance, followed by the belief that waste management is primarily a government responsibility (17.4%) and weak enforcement (14.4%). In Owo, seven principal components explained 60.5% of the variance. The dominant factor was gendered perception, with 15.3% of the variance attributed to the belief that women are primarily responsible for waste management. Other notable factors included poor monitoring by law enforcement (13%), efficacy of enforcement agents (10.9%), and governance quality (7.3%). While both cities shared concerns related to enforcement and governance, cultural context strongly shaped perceptions in Owo. The study highlights the complex socio-cultural and institutional drivers of compliance with sanitation laws in Nigerian urban areas. It concludes that culturally sensitive strategies are essential, particularly those that leverage community leadership in Akure and challenge restrictive gender norms in Owo. Strengthening law enforcement capacity, improving governance transparency, and promoting inclusive community participation are recommended to achieve sustainable compliance and effective solid waste management.Keywords: Public perception. environmental sanitation, Regulations, Waste managementSubject terms: Environmental studies, Geography, Geography

Introduction

Sanitation is a key factor in promoting public health and improving the overall well-being of communities1–3. It not only ensures clean and safe environments but also significantly influences mental health by reducing stress, anxiety, and depression4. In Southwest Nigeria, inadequate sanitation practices have led to serious health challenges, including the widespread occurrence of diseases such as malaria and waterborne illnesses5,6. Specifically, in Akure, the lack of proper sanitation infrastructure and limited community participation in waste management has worsened these health issues7.

Sanitation policies, as outlined by8,9, aim to enhance public health by addressing deficiencies in waste management, particularly in areas lacking proper infrastructure. Although international standards and research provide valuable guidance for improvements, implementing these policies effectively remains a challenge, especially in developing regions10–13. Factors such as negative public attitudes, weak governmental involvement, and limited civic participation often hinder effective solid waste management, contributing to significant public health risks, including measles, diarrhoea, and malaria14,15.

This study aims to explore the factors that shape residents’ perceptions of complying with environmental laws related to solid waste management in the Akure and Owo metropolitan areas. By examining these perceptions, the study seeks to offer insights that can assist policymakers in tackling waste management challenges, improving public health, and promoting sustainable urban development. The specific objectives of this study are: (i) to identify the factors influencing public perceptions of the regulations governing environmental sanitation programs in Akure and Owo, Ondo State; and (ii) to rank these factors in order of their significance or priority, to guide appropriate authorities in implementing strategies based on their relevance.

The importance of environmental sanitation, as highlighted by16–19, is vital, as it covers a broad range of services and infrastructure aimed at reducing health risks and promoting sustainability. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), environmental sanitation involves the safe disposal of human excreta, solid waste management, water supply systems, and the control of disease vectors. The primary goal is to minimize environmental health risks, enhance quality of life, and support environmental sustainability20. emphasized the need to integrate practical strategies for environmental sanitation to improve public welfare and ensure long-term ecosystem sustainability.

Environmental sanitation, as highlighted by21–23, plays a crucial role in public health, urban planning, and environmental management. In Nigeria, the National Environmental Sanitation Policy (NESP), as set forth by the National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency (NESREA), seeks to ensure clean, safe, and healthy environments. Despite the presence of these regulatory frameworks, the implementation of sanitation policies faces significant challenges, including inadequate funding, insufficient infrastructure, and limited public awareness24,25.

Given these challenges, a collaborative approach involving government agencies, private sector stakeholders, and local communities is crucial for effective sanitation management. This study addresses that gap by investigating the factors shaping residents’ perceptions of compliance with environmental laws related to solid waste management in Akure and Owo. Specifically, it seeks (i) to identify the factors influencing public perceptions of sanitation regulations in the two cities, and (ii) to rank these factors by significance to guide targeted interventions. By adopting a comparative approach, the study contributes to a more nuanced understanding of urban sanitation governance in Nigeria’s mid-sized cities, offering insights for policymakers on how to strengthen enforcement and promote sustainable urban development.

Theoretical framework

Stakeholder Theory, introduced by R. Edward Freeman in 1984, posits that institutions should consider the interests and influences of all relevant groups, beyond narrow organisational goals. Freeman argued that long-term success depends on effectively managing relationships with multiple stakeholders, including employees, customers, communities, and governments26. Applied to environmental sanitation, stakeholders include local governments, private waste management companies, community leaders, residents, and enforcement agents. The theory underscores that sustainable sanitation outcomes require shared responsibility, collaboration, and recognition of the diverse roles each group plays.

In this study, Stakeholder Theory provides a lens for analysing how different actors shape perceptions of, and compliance with, environmental sanitation regulations in Akure and Owo:

Community leaders act as intermediaries between government agencies and residents. They influence awareness, mobilisation, and cultural attitudes toward environmental laws.

By aligning with the research objectives—(i) to identify the factors shaping residents’ perceptions of sanitation regulations and (ii) to rank these factors in terms of significance—Stakeholder Theory provides a framework for examining how these groups interact, where gaps in participation or enforcement emerge, and how policy interventions can be better tailored to the realities of mid-sized Nigerian cities.

However, it should be noted that Stakeholder Theory reminds us that no institution can succeed by acting alone; it must recognise and balance the interests of all the groups that influence or are affected by its actions27. When applied to environmental sanitation, this means acknowledging the roles of residents, community leaders, enforcement agents, private waste contractors, and government authorities. Each of these actors has a stake in whether sanitation laws are respected and whether urban environments remain clean and healthy.

In this study, Stakeholder Theory did more than provide a broad lens — it shaped how the research tools were designed and how the findings were interpreted. When preparing the questionnaire, for example, questions were deliberately framed to reflect the different perspectives of the key stakeholders. Residents were asked about their awareness of sanitation laws and their willingness to comply; community leaders were considered in terms of how they mobilise participation and influence attitudes; and enforcement agents were reflected in questions about fairness, consistency, and effectiveness of monitoring. In this way, the voices of all the main actors were built directly into the data collection process.

The analysis also followed the same logic. Responses were grouped around stakeholder-related themes such as government enforcement, community participation, and individual behaviour. The factor ranking process then allowed the study to see not just which issues mattered most overall, but also how these issues reflected the interaction of the different groups. By grounding both the questionnaire and the analysis in Stakeholder Theory, the study stayed focused on its main objectives: to identify the drivers of residents’ perceptions of sanitation compliance and to prioritise them in ways that can help policymakers act more effectively in cities like Akure and Owo.

Study areas

Akure, like many other traditional Yoruba cities in Nigeria, predates British colonial rule and modern planning standards. It is the capital of Ondo State, located in the southwestern part of Nigeria. Geographically, Akure is bordered by Idanre Local Government to the south, Ifedore Local Government to the west, Owo Local Government to the east, and Akure North Local Government to the north. Akure, as shown in Fig. 1 (generated using QGIS software version 3.22 of 2025), is one of the country’s 36 states. It lies between latitudes 7° 5′ 1″ and 8° 0′ 01″ North of the Equator and longitudes 5° 45′ 1″ East and 6° 00′ 1″ East of the Greenwich Meridian, at an altitude of 370 m above sea level.

Location of Akure town in Ondo State, Nigeria.

As a medium-sized urban center, Akure became the provincial headquarters of the old Ondo Province in 1939 and later, in 1979, it became both the capital of Ondo State and a Local Government Headquarters. These dual political roles have contributed to an influx of people into the city28.

Situated approximately 700 km southwest of Abuja, the Federal Capital of Nigeria, and about 350 km from Lagos, the former capital, Akure is within Nigeria’s tropical rain forest region, characterized by high rainfall year-round. Since becoming the state capital in 1976, Akure’s political influence has significantly accelerated its growth and socio-economic activities. This rapid expansion is evident in its spatial growth from approximately 16 square kilometers in 1980 to about 30 square kilometers by 2000. The city’s population also increased from 38,852 in 1952 to 71,106 in 1963.

Akure, the administrative capital of Ondo State, is one of the most urbanized cities in the region, with a population exceeding 500,000. As the political and economic hub of Ondo State, it is home to numerous government agencies, educational institutions, and commercial centers. This rapid urbanization, as noted by29, has resulted in a significant increase in solid waste generation, surpassing the capacity of the existing waste management infrastructure.

Owo town

Owo, shown in Fig. 2 (also generated using QGIS software version 3.22 of 2025), is a town of significant historical and cultural importance in Ondo State, renowned for its rich heritage as an ancient Yoruba kingdom. With a population of over 300,000, Owo is one of the larger cities in the state, and, like Akure, it faces increasing challenges in managing its solid waste. The town’s waste management system is underdeveloped, with most residents relying on informal waste collection services or self-disposal methods. This has resulted in widespread environmental degradation, as waste is often dumped in unauthorized areas or burned in open spaces29.

Location of Owo town in Ondo State, Nigeria.

Owo’s waste management challenges are further complicated by its rural-urban mix, which results in diverse patterns of waste generation. In rural areas, agricultural waste, including crop residues and animal manure, contributes to the overall waste burden. The town’s informal waste collection system is overwhelmed by the volume of waste, especially in the urban center. This has led to the rise of illegal dumpsites in residential areas, markets, and along major roads. These dumpsites not only create unsightly conditions but also pose serious health risks to residents, attracting vermin and facilitating the spread of diseases30.

The government’s efforts to improve waste management in Owo have been minimal, and enforcement of waste disposal regulations is weak. While there are provisions for waste disposal in the state’s environmental laws, these laws are rarely enforced, and there are no designated waste disposal sites or landfills in the town. Residents are left to manage their waste as best they can, leading to widespread non-compliance with waste disposal regulations. The town’s lack of a formal waste management system means that much of the waste generated ends up in open dumps, waterways, or is burned, contributing to air and water pollution31.

In addition to its waste management challenges, Owo is also facing environmental degradation due to deforestation and erosion. The town’s growing population has led to increased demand for land for housing and agriculture, resulting in the clearing of forests and the degradation of the natural environment. This, combined with poor waste management practices, has contributed to the contamination of water sources and the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera and dysentery. The town’s waste management challenges are further exacerbated by its proximity to rural areas, where waste management services are virtually non-existent29] and [32.

Owo’s waste management challenges are indicative of the broader issues facing many cities in Ondo State. The town’s growing population and lack of waste management infrastructure have led to widespread environmental degradation and health risks. Addressing these challenges will require a coordinated effort from both the government and the private sector to develop a sustainable waste management system that can meet the needs of the town’s residents31.

Data collection

The data collection process was designed to ensure accuracy and representativeness, considering geographical location, time of distribution, and the convenience of the respondents. The study was carried out in Akure and Owo, the two study areas, in Ondo State. The area was divided into smaller units known as wards. Each ward represents a specific neighborhood or locality within the cities, and the distribution of questionnaires was carried out in these wards to ensure that the sample represents the diversity of the population in both cities.

Sampling technique

The study employed a multi-stage sampling technique. First, wards were purposively selected within Akure city and Owo town, Ondo State, based on their involvement in solid waste generation and collection. According to the National Population Commission (2023), Akure has an estimated population of 360,268, while Owo has about 222,262 residents. From the selected wards, 25 households were randomly sampled in each, and one respondent was surveyed per household. The sampling procedure is illustrated in Fig. 3.

Questionnaire distribution

A total of 600 questionnaires were distributed across the study areas, with 350 allocated to Akure and 250 to Owo. This distribution was based on the population size and level of urbanisation in the two cities. Akure, the state capital, is more urbanised and demographically diverse, with a projected 2025 population of about 803,00033. It comprises two local government areas (Akure North and Akure South) and hosts state administrative offices, higher institutions, federal agency branches, industries, and other major establishments, all of which contribute to its higher population density. Owo, by contrast, has a smaller projected population of about 422,000, consists of only one local government area, and retains semi-urban characteristics. Allocating 250 questionnaires to Owo ensured adequate representation without oversampling, while the larger sample in Akure captured the complexity of its urban environment. To maximise questionnaire retrieval, eight (8) field assistants—five in Akure and three in Owo—were engaged and closely supervised and motivated over a two-week period in July, 2025. This strategy resulted in a 100% retrieval rate.

The distribution of questionnaires was organised around the political wards in each study area. Akure comprises 23 wards (11 in Akure South and 12 in Akure North), while Owo has 11 wards. From these, 14 wards within the urban centre of Akure and 10 in Owo were selected for the purpose of the survey. In each selected ward, 25 households were randomly sampled, giving a total of 600 questionnaires—350 in Akure and 250 in Owo as shown in Table 1. Household heads were the preferred respondents, but where unavailable, another responsible adult was engaged.

Data analysis

Quantitative data obtained from the structured questionnaires were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistical methods. The analysis was conducted using the Statistical Products for the Service Solution (SPSS) version 27.

Descriptive statistics such as frequency distributions, percentages, and means, were used to summarize responses and identify trends in household waste generation, disposal practices, and public awareness of waste management policies. Inferential statistics, where applicable, factors analysis was used to examine relationships between variables such as demographic characteristics and perception of policy enforcement.

This approach enabled objective interpretation and facilitated the identification of significant patterns across the study areas34.

Limitations of the Study

Although this study offers useful insights into how people in Owo and Akure perceive environmental sanitation regulations on solid waste management, it is not without limitations. One major limitation is the reliance on self-reported data. Because respondents were asked to describe their own behaviours and perceptions, there is the risk of social desirability bias. In other words, some participants may have exaggerated their compliance or downplayed instances of non-compliance in order to appear more responsible, which could affect the accuracy of the findings.

Another limitation comes from the possibility of sampling bias. While efforts were made to include participants from different backgrounds, it is possible that some groups, such as migrants, informal settlement dwellers, or transient residents, were underrepresented. These groups often face unique waste management challenges, and their limited inclusion means the findings may not fully capture the diversity of experiences in the study areas.

The study is also geographically limited to Ondo State, focusing only on Owo and Akure. While these locations provide valuable case studies, the results cannot be assumed to reflect the situation across Nigeria. Differences in culture, governance, and socio-economic conditions in other regions may lead to very different perceptions of environmental sanitation. This makes the findings most relevant for Ondo State but less generalisable beyond it.

In terms of analysis, the use of Factor Analysis brings its own challenges. While FA is a useful statistical tool for identifying patterns, interpreting the rotated component matrix involves a level of subjectivity. The labels given to components—such as “leadership influence” or “poor governance”—reflect the researcher’s judgement. Different researchers, or even the same data analysed differently, could produce slightly different interpretations.

Finally, the study provides only a snapshot in time. Public perceptions of sanitation are not static—they change in response to government actions, cultural shifts, or economic pressures. Because this study used a cross-sectional design, it does not capture how perceptions might evolve. Similarly, only selected socio-cultural and governance factors were examined, while other influences such as religious beliefs, media narratives, or financial costs were not directly considered. This means the findings, though insightful, do not tell the whole story.

Sources of information on awareness of environmental sanitation in the study areas

Figure 8 revealed that in both Akure and Owo, radio programs are the primary source of information on environmental sanitation, with over 40% of respondents citing it as their main source (Akure: 40.8%; Owo: 45.2%). This highlights radio’s wide accessibility and affordability as a communication medium in these communities36–38. Personal contact also plays a significant role, with 31% of respondents in Akure and 30.2% in Owo relying on interpersonal communication through trusted social networks such as family, friends, and community leaders39,40. Television programs and community campaigns contribute smaller but notable shares (Akure TV: 12.4%, community campaigns: 15.8%; Owo TV: 12.3%, community campaigns: 12.3%). These channels complement radio and personal contact by offering visual content and fostering grassroots engagement, respectively37,41. The consistency of these patterns across both cities suggests that communication strategies for solid waste management in Ondo State should prioritize radio broadcasts and personal networks, while also incorporating television and community campaigns to reinforce key messages.

Equally important is the widespread perception that waste management is mainly a government responsibility. While understandable in contexts where public services are weak, this mindset risks discouraging individual or communal action44. warn that such passive attitudes reduce engagement and make sustainable waste management difficult to achieve. The implication is that government-led initiatives must not only deliver services but also reframe sanitation as a co-owned responsibility, otherwise compliance will remain fragile.

The concerns about weak enforcement and poor governance highlight deeper institutional credibility gaps. Respondents’ emphasis on insufficient monitoring, corruption, and inefficiency echoes45–47] and [48, all of whom note that ineffective enforcement erodes deterrence and weak governance weakens trust. The lesson is clear: technical improvements in enforcement must be coupled with transparent governance and visible accountability if compliance is to improve.

Another critical insight is that many residents see sanitation as a lower priority compared to pressing socio-economic challenges. This mirrors findings from49] and [50, who note that livelihood struggles often redirect attention away from environmental concerns. The implication is that compliance cannot be pursued in isolation from broader development needs. Linking waste management to visible benefits—such as job creation, health improvements, or community development—may encourage stronger participation.

Taken together, the Akure findings suggest that compliance cannot be secured through regulations alone. What matters are the enabling conditions: empowered community leaders, a shared sense of responsibility, credible and accountable institutions, and integration of environmental goals with socio-economic realities. This aligns with the broader perspectives of51,52] and [53, who stress that multi-layered strategies are essential to move environmental compliance from paper to practice.

Weak monitoring and lax enforcement also feature prominently. Residents perceive that inadequate oversight reduces the pressure to comply, which confirms the argument by53 that laws without credible enforcement lose their deterrent effect. This suggests that enforcement must move beyond symbolic presence toward consistent monitoring and meaningful penalties to strengthen compliance.

Closely related is the perceived competence of law enforcement officers. Respondents emphasised that enforcement depends not only on the existence of agencies but on the training, motivation, and integrity of personnel52. similarly argue that lack of capacity, poor resources, and corruption undermine enforcement effectiveness. The implication here is clear: building compliance requires investment in human capacity and professional standards within enforcement agencies, not merely more regulations.

At the governance level, respondents pointed to improvements in transparency and accountability as drivers of compliance. When communities see governance improving, they are more likely to cooperate47] and [48. reinforce this view, noting that governance reforms and greater accountability strengthen community trust, which is essential for regulatory compliance. The policy lesson is that credible governance is not peripheral but central to environmental law enforcement.

The role of community encouragement also emerges as significant. Lack of peer support and social reinforcement was seen as a barrier to compliance21. highlighted that community encouragement and positive reinforcement can enhance participation in sanitation programmes. This implies that compliance strategies should not only rely on enforcement but also build on collective motivation through community-driven initiatives.

Corruption within enforcement agencies further erodes compliance. When residents believe officials can be bribed or are acting for personal gain, trust in the system collapses47] and [48. both stress that corruption weakens legitimacy and fosters non-compliance. Tackling corruption is therefore indispensable for restoring faith in environmental regulation.

Finally, competing socio-economic priorities reduce compliance, as residents tend to prioritise survival and livelihoods over sanitation obligations56] and [57. note that environmental issues often receive less attention where pressing social and economic needs dominate. This suggests that environmental policies will be more effective when integrated with socio-economic development, such as linking sanitation efforts to income opportunities or community welfare.

Overall, the Owo analysis underscores that compliance cannot be achieved through legislation alone. Cultural expectations, enforcement capacity, governance credibility, and socio-economic pressures all shape outcomes. The “so what” is that interventions must be holistic—tackling gender bias, strengthening institutions, rooting out corruption, and aligning environmental initiatives with community realities—if compliance with waste management regulations is to become sustainable.

Both cities, however, shared concerns about poor monitoring by law enforcement agents. Yet Owo residents went further, pointing out not just inefficiency but also corruption among enforcement officers, while Akure residents did not raise this issue. Governance was also perceived differently: in Akure, people spoke of poor governance as a barrier, whereas in Owo, there was some optimism, with respondents suggesting that governance was improving.

Overall, the findings suggest that while weak enforcement is a common problem, Akure residents are more focused on structural and governmental roles, whereas Owo residents highlight cultural expectations, community support, and the credibility of law enforcement.

Policy implications

The findings of this study highlight the importance of context-specific policy approaches to improve environmental sanitation compliance in Akure and Owo. In Akure, where community leadership strongly influences compliance, government policies should deliberately harness the authority and influence of traditional chiefs, neighbourhood leaders, and youth associations. Formalising their roles through “Environmental Compliance Committees,” offering leadership training, and providing small grants or recognition awards for sanitation initiatives can strengthen their ability to mobilise residents and foster collective responsibility.

In Owo, where cultural perceptions frame sanitation as “women’s sole responsibility,” interventions must focus on shifting gendered norms and promoting inclusive participation. Public campaigns showcasing men’s active involvement, school and religious programmes emphasising shared responsibility, and incentive schemes that reward households rather than individuals can help reduce gender barriers and ensure balanced engagement across the community.

Conclusion

This study has shown that compliance with environmental sanitation regulations in Akure and Owo is shaped by a mix of socio-cultural and institutional factors. Community leadership emerged as the strongest influence in Akure, while gendered cultural perceptions were more pronounced in Owo. Both cities also face common challenges of weak enforcement, governance shortcomings, corruption, and socio-economic pressures. These findings confirm that compliance behaviour is context-specific and requires tailored approaches that respond to local realities rather than generic, one-size-fits-all policies.
