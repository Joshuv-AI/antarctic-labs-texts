Human promoter-driven AAV tools enable precision gene therapy targeting cochlear hair cells
Authors: Liyan Zhang, Yicheng Lu, Xianmin Wu, Jinyi Fan, Xinru Zhang, Yang Xu, Hao Wei, Qiuhan Sun, Lulu Jiang, Huawei Tuo, Nianci Li, Hua Jin, Wu Cao, Beibei Ye, Ziyu Zhang, Yunkai Zhang, Fangyi Chen, Ling Lu, Jieyu Qi, Renjie Chai, Fangzhi Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryApproximately 50% of hereditary deafness cases and most age-related hearing loss result from cochlear hair cell dysfunction. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated gene therapy offers a promising strategy for hearing restoration. However, the cochlea’s complex cellular composition poses a significant challenge for targeted gene delivery into hair cells. Here, we establish a cross-species screening platform for cochlear AAV tools, applicable from rodents to large animals. Using this platform, we identify human promoters that specifically target inner hair cells, outer hair cells, or all hair cells in mice. The hair cell-specific coProB2 promoter is further validated in Bama miniature pigs and cynomolgus monkeys, achieving specific transgene expression in hair cells without auditory toxicity. Importantly, coProB2-driven gene therapies in DFNB79 and DFNB9 mouse models restore auditory function to near wild-type levels. Thus, this study establishes a cross-species-compatible, hair cell-specific AAV system, providing a versatile toolbox for cochlear hair cell gene therapy.Keywords: deafness, AAV, human-derived specific promoter, cochlear hair cells, DFNB9, DFNB79, gene therapy, hearing loss

Introduction

Hearing impairment is a predominant sensory neurological disorder worldwide.1,2,3 According to the latest epidemiological data from the World Health Organization, more than 5% of the people worldwide experience varying degrees of hearing loss.1 Hearing loss is mainly caused by inner ear or auditory pathway defects, with hair cell (HC) dysfunction responsible for over half of the cases.4 Gene therapy has achieved remarkable effectiveness in different animal models of deafness,5,6 and clinical trials of autosomal recessive non-syndromic hearing loss 9 (DFNB9) have successfully restored hearing of the patients.7,8,9,10,11 However, the clinical translation remains constrained by two major challenges: the high cellular heterogeneity of the inner ear, which includes several distinct functional cell subtypes imposing stringent requirements for precise targeted delivery,12,13,14 and the variability in vector efficacy across species, leading to fluctuations in therapeutic outcomes. Therefore, the development of cross-species targeted gene therapy tools has become a critical scientific challenge.

Several adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors including Anc80L65,15 AAV2.7m8,16 and AAV-ie17 could efficiently transduce broad cochlear cell types without specificity. This non-specific transgene expression risks toxicity in off-target cells. In addition, promoter elements within AAV vectors regulate gene expression and can also significantly impact therapeutic safety.6,18,19,20 And the non-specific transgene expression driven by ubiquitous promoters often induces toxicity. For instance, exogenous gene delivery under the CAG promoter in adult mouse cochleae causes HC damage and hearing impairment.21 Similarly, our previous studies found that overexpression of Tprn or Gjb2 driven by the CAG promoter led to the death of HCs and elevated auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds in wild-type (WT) mice.20,22 In contrast, specific promoter mediated precise gene therapy efficiently rescued hearing in Gjb2-deficient mice and exhibited favorable biosafety profiles in WT mice, Bama miniature pigs, and cynomolgus monkeys.20 Thus, these findings underscore the urgent need to develop cell type-specific promoters for gene therapy.

In this study, we generated a library of AAVs, each with a candidate human promoter. We tested these AAVs in vivo and identified four HC-specific promoters. In mice, AAVs with coProA1, coProA2, coProB1, and coProB2 promoters showed specificity for targeting inner HCs (IHCs), outer HCs (OHCs), and HCs. To further confirm that these promoters are suitable for clinical translation, we validated the HC specificity and auditory safety of the coProB2 in both Bama miniature pigs and cynomolgus monkeys. Moreover, exogenous Tprn expression driven by coProB2 successfully restored auditory function in DFNB79 mice, with therapeutic effects stably maintained for at least 3 months. The coProB2-mediated exogenous Tprn expression did not affect auditory function in mice, but ubiquitous promoter CAG-driven Tprn overexpression induced hearing impairment in WT mice. In addition, coProB2 promoter can also mediate full-length human OTOF protein production via split intein-mediated protein trans-splicing, restoring hearing function in DFNB9 mouse models. This work establishes a promoter screening platform spanning rodents, artiodactyls, and primates, providing a molecular toolkit that combines cross-species compatibility with cell-type specificity for the precise gene therapy of hereditary hearing loss.

Results

Design and testing of human-derived cochlear HC-specific promoters in neonatal mice

To develop specific AAV tools for cochlear HC gene therapy, we first constructed a library of AAV plasmids, each containing a different human promoter sequence upstream of the green fluorescent protein mNeonGreen. The promoter sequences were constructed using two different strategies. The group cochlear promoter A (coProA) included sequences upstream of the start codon of selected human cochlear HC type-specific genes. Group cochlear promoter B (coProB) was generated by conserved DNA elements located in the region upstream of human cochlear HC type-specific genes. The coProB also contained a minimal promoter from human SHH gene23 (Figure 1A; Table S1).

These plasmids were packaged with the AAV-ie17; capsid and viral particles were delivered into the inner ear of P2 mice via microinjection. Cochlear tissues were harvested at P12 for immunofluorescence analysis (Figure 1A). The targeting efficiencies were assessed using Myo7a labeling of HCs, Sox2 labeling of supporting cells (SCs), and quantification of the fluorescence signals. Immunofluorescence results showed that the CAG promoter exhibited broad activity in the neonatal mouse inner ear, transducing various cell types such as HCs, SCs, and spiral ganglion neurons (SGNs) (Figures S1–S3). Most candidate promoters showed limited fluorescent signals, and some promoters were found to be cell specific (Figures S1 and S3). The coProA1 targeted almost 100% of OHCs (apex: 99.46 ± 0.54, middle: 99.44 ± 0.56, base: 97.27 ± 2.06), while the number of mNeonGreen-positive IHCs decreased from the apex to base (apex: 61.54 ± 13.07, middle: 21.96 ± 10.73, base: 0), with no detectable expression in SCs (Figure 1B). The coProA2 was found to be IHC specific, with targeting efficiency exceeding 94% (apex: 100 ± 0, middle: 95.99 ± 2.32, base: 94.07 ± 3.71) (Figure 1C). The coProB1 promoter also highlighted nearly 100% OHCs (apex: 99.40 ± 0.60, middle: 98.86 ± 0.66, base: 96.67 ± 2.65) (Figure 1D) with specificity. The AAV-coProB2 led to efficient transgene expression in both IHCs (apex, middle, and base: 100 ± 0) and OHCs (apex: 99.45 ± 0.56, middle: 98.31 ± 1.07, base: 97.78 ± 0.91) (Figure 1E).

Additionally, we analyzed the activities of different promoters in the cochlear vestibular tissues. In the tested AAVs, only AAV-coProB1 did not target vestibular tissues (Figure S4). As expected, the CAG promoter mediated strong fluorescent expression in both SCs and HCs of the mouse utricle and ampulla. The AAVs with coProA1, coProA2, and coProB2 promoters led to robust fluorescent expression in the HCs of both the central and peripheral regions of the utricle, as well as in the ampullar HCs. Similarly, all these promoters showed no activity in the SCs of the utricle and ampulla (Figure S4).

The promoters mediate specific and efficient transgene expression in HCs of adult mice

To investigate the characteristics of candidate promoters in the mature cochlea, we evaluated the targeting profile of these promoters in the adult murine cochlea. Since AAV2.7m8 exhibited higher transduction efficiency in the OHCs of adult mice than AAV-ie (Figure S5), we used AAV2.7m8 vectors to package these promoter-GFP reporter constructs. AAVs carrying the coProA1, coProA2, coProB1, and coProB2 were introduced into the cochlea of P30 mice (Figure 2A). In contrast to CAG and CMV promoters (Figures 2B and S5), coProA1, coProA2, coProB1, and coProB2 all led to specific transgene expression in HCs (Figures 2C–2F), consistent with the results observed in neonatal mice. Among them, coProA1 highlighted approximately 95% of OHCs in the cochlear apical and middle region (apex: 94.68 ± 0.92, middle: 95.90 ± 1.03, base: 61.65 ± 12.50) and about 65% of IHCs in the apical region (apex: 65.69 ± 5.56, middle: 37.55 ± 4.56, base: 42.28 ± 4.48) (Figure 2C). The coProA2 specifically targeted IHCs with high efficiency (apex: 100 ± 0, middle: 94.21 ± 1.95, base: 87.63 ± 5.26) (Figure 2D). The coProB1 produced transgene expression in most OHCs in the cochlear region (apex: 99.59 ± 0.41, middle:85.92 ± 2.60, base: 74.34 ± 3.72) (Figure 2E). The AAV-coProB2 was found to target nearly 100% of IHCs (apex and middle: 100 ± 0, base: 98.49 ± 1.52) and most OHCs (apex: 91.12 ± 2.27, middle: 85.31 ± 7.65, base: 74.01 ± 3.67), while no signal was detected in the other cells (Figures 2F and 2G). Similarly, we found that the coProA1, coProA2, and coProB2 promoters were all capable of mediating the targeting of HCs in the vestibular tissues of adult mice, with no detectable fluorescent protein expression of SCs. In contrast, the coProB1 promoter exhibited no transduction activity in either vestibular HCs or SCs (Figure S6).

Furthermore, we performed comparison between the coProB2 promoter and the previously reported mMyo15 promoter,6 which is known for its HC-specific activity and has been used in AAV-mediated OTOF gene delivery to successfully restore hearing in DFNB9 patients.7,8,9,10 At the same dose, the AAVs with mMyo15 and coProB2 promoters achieved similar targeting efficiencies in both IHCs and OHCs of the adult mouse cochlea (Figures 2F–2H). Quantitative analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in fluorescence intensity in IHCs and mid-turn OHCs (Figure 2I). Notably, the mMyo15 promoter mediated higher fluorescent protein expression in apical OHCs, whereas the coProB2 promoter conferred stronger expression activity in basal-turn OHCs.

The coProB2 promoter enables specific and safety targeting in Bama miniature pigs

To determine whether the promoter AAV tools could specifically target HCs in species other than mice, we selected and tested AAV-coProB2 in Bama miniature pig (Figure 3A). AAV vectors carrying either coProB2-EGFP or CMV-EGFP were administered into the cochlea. ABR measurements, which assess brainstem auditory pathway integrity by recording scalp potentials in response to sound,24 were performed 1 week before and 2 weeks after AAV injection. The results showed unchanged ABR thresholds post-injection, indicating preserved auditory function (Figures 3B and 3C). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed different EGFP expression patterns. EGFP expression driven by coProB2 promoter was restricted exclusively to the HC region (Figures 3D and 3E). In contrast, the CMV promoter drove widespread EGFP expression across multiple cochlear regions, including the lateral epithelial ridge, HCs, and greater epithelial ridge (Figures 3F and S7). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that the CMV and coProB2 promoters achieved similar transgene expression in IHCs, but coProB2 targeted much more OHCs, and no SCs (Figures 3G and 3H). The data demonstrated that coProB2 promoter could mediate transgene expression in the HCs of Bama miniature pig with high efficiency, specificity, and safety.

AAV-coProB2 enables specific and safe transgene expression in cynomolgus monkeys

To deeply assess AAV-coProB2 performance, we measured auditory safety and targeting efficiency in cynomolgus monkeys following inner ear administration of EGFP-expressing vectors driven by either coProB2 or CMV promoter. Auditory function was assessed 2 weeks post-injection, followed by long-term monitoring (Figure 4A). Two weeks post-injection, ABR measurements revealed no significant changes in hearing thresholds from baseline (Figures 4B and 4C). Long-term auditory function was assessed at 1, 2, 3, and 4 months post-injection. The coProB2 promoter showed no delayed auditory toxicity, as ABR thresholds at all tested frequencies were unchanged from pre-injection levels (Figures 4B and 4C). Consistent with the observation in Bama miniature pigs and our previous study,6 the CMV promoter drove widespread EGFP expression in various cochlear cell types (Figures 4D and 4F). In contrast, EGFP expression driven by the coProB2 promoter was strictly confined to HCs, with little fluorescent signal detected in non-HC regions (Figures 4E, 4G, and S8). Quantitative analysis showed that AAV-coProB2 and AAV-CMV achieved similar targeting efficiency in IHCs. But OHC targeting by AAV-coProB2 was markedly superior to that of CMV (Figure 4H).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the coProB2 promoter mediates efficient and specific transgene expression in the HCs of large animals without auditory dysfunction. These findings support the auditory safety and translational potential of coProB2-driven gene delivery.

The coProB2-driven gene therapy restores hearing in DFNB79 mice

DFNB79, a genetic deafness disorder, is characterized by progressive and severe sensorineural hearing loss resulting from TPRN gene mutation. Our previous study identified Tprn protein forming characteristic ring-like structures in cochlear HC stereocilia.22 Aberrant expression of Tprn (overexpression, knockdown, or knockout) disrupts this ring-like structure and leads to severe hearing loss in mice. Although gene delivery using a weak promoter, CMV173, partially restored hearing in Tprn−/− mice, the auditory function remained suboptimal compared to WT levels.22 To compare the therapeutic effect of different promoters, we delivered the Tprn gene into the cochlea of the knockout mice by AAVs with CAG, CMV173, or coProB2 promoter, followed by auditory testing (Figure 5A). As expected, the Tprn-HA driven by coProB2 was specifically expressed in HCs (Figure 5B). AAV-coProB2-Tprn injection recovered auditory thresholds, achieving levels close to WT controls at 1 month. In contrast, CAG-mediated Tprn expression could not rescue hearing and CMV173 could partially restore the auditory function (Figures 5C and 5D), which is consistent with our previous study.22 Furthermore, we statistically analyzed wave I latency and amplitude of the ABR at different frequencies and found no distinct differences between the treated mice and the WT mice at 1 month (Figure S9). Importantly, long-term hearing assessments revealed that the therapeutic effect of AAV-coProB2-Tprn-HA was sustained for at least 3 months (Figure 5E). Unlike humans, who are born with functional hearing, mice develop functional hearing around post-natal day (P) 14. AAV-coProB2-Tprn was also capable of restoring auditory function in Tprn−/− mice at P14 (Figures S10A and S10B), which suggests its potential utility in the context of human gene therapy. However, it failed to restore auditory function in P30 mice (Figure S10C).

To further evaluate auditory perceptual recovery, the acoustic startle response (ASR) and pre-pulse inhibition (PPI) were used to assess central nervous system processing of acoustic stimuli following AAV treatment. The ASR is mediated by the transmission of acoustic signals to the pontine reticular nucleus, which are then relayed to the spinal motor neurons. PPI involves higher-order central nervous system centers, such as the inferior colliculus and the lateral globus pallidus.25 The PPI rate was significantly reduced at 16, 24, and 32 kHz under a 70-dB SPL pre-pulse stimulation in knockout mice, but AAV treatment partially restored the PPI rates (Figure 5F), suggesting the recovery of higher-order auditory processing.

Next, we assessed the safety of AAV-coProB2-Tprn in the cochlea of WT mice. Mice injected with AAV-CAG-Tprn exhibited hearing loss (Figure 5G), indicating the lack of safety associated with using a ubiquitous promoter for DFNB79 gene therapy. In contrast, click and tone-burst (TB)-ABR thresholds in mice injected with AAV-coProB2-Tprn were comparable to those in controls. Furthermore, we confirmed that Tprn forms a ring-like structure in WT mice and coProB2-directed Tprn-HA successfully reconstructed the characteristic ring-like structures in Tprn−/− mice (Figures 5H–5K). Thus, these data indicated that HC-specific promoter coProB2 could enable precise and effective Tprn expression and restore both ring-like structures and auditory function in DFNB79 mice.

Human OTOF gene expression driven by coProB2 restores hearing in DFNB9 mice

coProB2 is only 521 bp in size, making it suitable for delivering large deafness genes. DFNB9 is a form of moderate to severe hereditary deafness caused by mutations in the OTOF gene,26 and OTOF gene (6 kb) exceeds the packaging limit of a single AAV (∼4.7 kb). We investigated the recovery of auditory function in Otof-deficient mice (Otof939X/939X mice) using protein trans-splicing and the coProB2 promoter.

Through systematic analysis of the coding region of the human OTOF gene, we identified 30 pairs of candidate splicing sites that meet the intein splicing requirements27 (Table. S2). In vitro western blotting experiments showed that 18 of these sites successfully facilitated the recombination of full-length OTOF protein (Figure S11). Among these, we selected recombination site 13, which has high recombination efficiency and enables the split dual AAVs to have similar sizes, to construct the dual AAV-hOTOF vector with the coProB2 promoter. Next, we delivered the AAV-coProB2-hOTOF virus into P30 Otof-deficient mice (Figure 6A). One week after injection, ABR tests showed that the hearing threshold of the mice had recovered to near-WT levels, with this effect being stably maintained for at least 5 months (Figures 6B–6G). Nevertheless, sustained evaluation of its long-term therapeutic efficacy remains essential for advancing this technology toward clinical translation.

We also performed the ASR and PPI to assess higher-order auditory processing following gene therapy. As expected, Otof-deficient mice exhibited severe deficits in ASR and impaired PPI. Following treatment, both the ASR and PPI rates were restored to levels comparable to those of WT controls across all three tested frequencies (Figure 6H), indicating successful restoration of auditory perception.

Furthermore, to confirm whether OTOF protein was successfully expressed in the Otof-deficient mice, we performed immunofluorescence at 1 month post-treatment. Confocal microscopy results showed effective expression of OTOF protein in the mice’s IHCs (Figure 6I). These results indicate that the cochlear HC-specific promoter coProB2 can drive OTOF protein expression in IHCs and restore hearing of DFNB9 mice.

Discussion

HC dysfunction represents a major causative factor for hereditary hearing loss, accounting for approximately 50% of all cases.4 Age-related hearing loss is among the most common sensory deficits in older adults, and most of the cases are also caused by HC damage. Thus, developing HC gene therapy with high specificity and safety holds significant scientific and commercial value. Here, we identified four HC-specific AAV tools (Figures 1 and 2), which theoretically enable gene therapy for all deafness genes expressed in HCs, as well as the delivery of therapeutic genes for treating age-related hearing loss. Among these promoters, coProB2 showed high targeting efficiency for both OHCs and IHCs in mice and large animals. Crucially, Tprn expression driven by coProB2 fully restored the hearing of DFNB79 mice, which was unachievable by using the CAG promoter (Figure 5). Moreover, coProB2 exhibited no adverse effects in WT mice (Figure 5G). In addition, the coProB2 promoter-mediated OTOF expression successfully restored auditory function in DFNB9 mice (Figure 6).

In DFNB79 mice, however, only a transient hearing recovery was observed following neonatal treatment (Figure 5). This may be attributed to an improper dosage of TPRN expression in HCs. One possible explanation is ototoxicity caused by supraphysiological levels of TPRN in some HCs, which aligns with the finding that TPRN overexpression impairs hearing in WT mice (Figure 5G). Moreover, and more importantly, the underlying degenerative pathology may not have been fully corrected, as evidenced by the lack of complete hearing recovery within the first month after treatment (Figure 5D). This suggests that progressive structural deterioration may continue even after the initial intervention.

Although AAV-coProB2 efficiently transduced HCs in P30 WT mice (Figure 2), therapeutic delivery of AAV-coProB2-Tprn at the same age failed to restore hearing in DFNB79 mice, whereas treatment at P14 successfully rescued hearing (Figure S10). This discrepancy likely reflects the advanced pathological state of the adult DFNB79 cochlea, which already exhibits disorganized and lost stereocilia by P30. These structural deficits may be irreversible through TPRN restoration alone. Therefore, efficient viral transduction does not ensure functional recovery when treatment is initiated after significant damage has been established, highlighting the critical need for early intervention before irreversible cochlear degeneration occurs.

In addition to tissue-specific promoters, the AAV capsid is critical for the efficient expression of AAV-delivered transgenes in target cells. Specifically, AAV2.7m8 achieved a significantly higher transduction rate in OHCs than AAV-ie in adult mice (Figure S5). Using the same dose, we compared AAV-ie and AAV2.7m8 capsids carrying mNeonGreen driven by CMV promoter in adult mice. While both AAVs efficiently transduced IHCs, AAV2.7m8 exhibited significantly higher transgene expression in OHCs compared to AAV-ie. This capsid-dependent tropism was maintained when carrying coProB1 and coProB2. All these suggested that both the AAV capsid and the promoter are critical determinants on tropism.

The safety and precision of AAV therapy critically depend on the capsid and promoter. Commonly used AAV capsids in cochlea (e.g., AAV-ie, Anc80L65, and PHP.eB) exhibit limited specificity, and ubiquitous promoters (CAG and CMV) frequently cause off-target toxicity, impairing hearing in mice21 and inducing neuro-inflammation in primates.28 Here, we also observed that CAG promoter-mediated Tprn overexpression failed to restore hearing in DFNB79 mice and conversely, induced hearing loss in WT mice (Figure 5). Although not attributable to the CAG promoter itself, the toxicity underscores the potential risks associated with inappropriate transgene expression levels.22 This stringent safety profile positions coProB2 as a vital component for clinical translation.

High specificity in large animal models proves critical for clinical translation. Previous studies using ubiquitous promoters (CBA, CMV, and CB7) in pigs,29 and non-human primates30,31,32,33 consistently showed transgene expression across diverse cochlear cell types. In contrast, the HC-specific promoter like mMyo15 successfully restricted transgene expression to HCs in macaques5,6 and has been used in AAV-mediated OTOF gene delivery to successfully restore hearing in DFNB9 patients.7,8,9,10 coProB2 showed superior HC-targeting specificity compared to CMV in both miniature pigs and macaques (Figures 3 and 4), offering another clinically viable strategy.

In summary, this study identified promoters conferring specificity to cochlear IHCs and OHCs. The coProB2 promoter was validated across species, demonstrating high efficiency, precise HC specificity, and auditory safety in mice, Bama miniature pigs, and cynomolgus monkeys. Moreover, its therapeutic efficacy was confirmed in two mouse models. Collectively, these results provide compelling experimental evidence to support the clinical translation of precise gene therapies for hearing loss.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be noted. First, the therapeutic efficacy in DFNB79 model mice was monitored for only 3 months post-treatment, with functional recovery showing signs of decline by the third month compared to the 1-month time point, leaving the long-term outcome uncertain. In the DFNB9 model, observations were limited to 5 months, whereas existing studies in this model have reported follow-up exceeding 1 year. Longer-term monitoring (e.g., 1 year or more) of treated animals is, therefore, a valuable and necessary future direction to assess durability. Second, the comparison between the coProB2 and mMyo15 promoters was conducted only in adult mouse cochleae and not validated in large-animal models. Consequently, whether coProB2 offers a clinical advantage over mMyo15 remains unclear and requires further confirmation. Third, although auditory safety of the coProB2 promoter was evaluated over 4 months in cynomolgus monkeys (Figure 4), longer-term assessments of safety will be essential to support future clinical translation. Finally, safety and specificity evaluations for coProB2 in this study were only performed in male large animals, and the results cannot completely exclude the possibility of sex differences.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and culture conditions

HEK-293T cells were cultured in 15-cm dish (Jet Biofil, Cat#TCD050150) and maintained in a high-glucose formulation of DMEM (Vivacell, Cat#C3113-0500). Fetal bovine serum (10%, VivaCell, Cat#C04001-500) and penicillin-streptomycin (1%, Gibco, Cat#15140122) were added to the DMEM. To ensure consistent cell growth and prevent over-confluence, the HEK-293T cells were passaged every 48 h. The passaging process involved detaching the cells from the culture vessel using a 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Cat#25200072) solution. HEK-293T cells used in this study were tested to ensure no mycoplasma contamination.

Animals

This study utilized both sex 2-3-day-old Tprn−/− mice (Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc.), 1 month Otof939X/939X mice (Cyagen) and age-matched C57BL/6 mice (Cyagen). The Tprn−/− & Otof939X/939X mouse line, including its establishment and genotyping protocol, was described in detail in our earlier publication.22 For genotyping, genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tail biopsies or ear punches using gDNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme Biotech, Cat#DC112). The animals were placed in an environment with stable temperature and sufficient food (the temperature was controlled at 22 ± 1°C), with 12 h of light exposure per day, and they were allowed to move freely. To ensure the welfare of the mice used in the experiment, the use of the mice was all approved by the Animal Experiment Ethical Committee of Southeast University. Ethics No.2024072601. Four-month-old male Bama miniature pigs and 5–8-year-old male cynomolgus monkeys were used in this study. All large animals were housed individually in stainless steel cages under controlled conditions (20°C–26°C, 40–70% humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to water and scheduled feeding twice daily. After surgery, the incision site was disinfected daily with iodophor for 7 days. Ceftizoxime sodium (10 mg/kg, i.m.) was administered for 7 days and meloxicam (0.1 mg/kg, i.m.) for 5 days to prevent infection and provide analgesia. The use of all large animals was examined and approved by ClinBridge Biotech Co. Ltd.'s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Ethics No. (AP-C220505-20240516, AP-C220505-20240313, AP-C231016).

Method details

Plasmid construction and viral packaging

For OTOF protein cleavage sites design: A dual trans-splicing intein vector system was used to segment the human otoferlin isoform 5. In this system, the first vector was constructed to encode the N-OTOF sequence (the N-terminal portion of hOTOF) followed by the splicing donor sequence Rma-intein N, whereas the second vector was designed to contain the corresponding splicing acceptor sequence Rma-intein C, immediately upstream of the C-OTOF coding sequence (the C-terminal portion of hOTOF) with a C-terminal HA tag. To generate AAV vectors for gene delivery, the target gene sequences were first amplified using PCR and subsequently cloned into AAV expression vectors. For AAV production, HEK-293T cells were transfected with a standard triple-plasmid system, which included the AAV vector plasmid, the helper plasmid, and a targeted plasmid. AAV-ie, AAV2.7m8, and Anc80L65 serotypes were utilized in this study. Culture supernatants were captured 48 h after transfection, and both cells and medium were harvested at 96 h for further processing. The AAV vectors were purified using established protocols,17 and the viral genome titer was determined by qPCR. The primers targeting the AAV genome’s ITR region enabled precise viral genome quantification. The primers listed at Table.S3.

AAV delivery in neonatal mice

Neonatal mice were placed on ice for 2 min to induce hypothermia. A retroauricular incision was made using scissors, followed by gentle tissue retraction with dilator to expose the RWM. AAVs were microinjected through the RWM. Post-injection, tissues were repositioned and the incision sealed with surgical adhesive (3M Vetbond, Cat#1469SB) to prevent rupture. Mice were returned to home cages upon regaining ambulation.

Adult mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of tribromoethanol (500 mg/kg, EasyCheck, Cat#M2920). A post-auricular incision was performed to expose the semicircular canal. A small fenestration was made in the post-semicircular canal, and recombinant AAV was injected using a glass micropipette. The fenestration was sealed with autologous fat tissue, and the incision was closed with absorbable tissue adhesive. Postoperative recovery was performed on a 37°C heating pad until full recovery.

ABR measurements in mice

ABR measurements in adult mice were conducted using a Tucker-Davis Technologies (TDT, RZ6) system. After the mouse was fully anesthetized and its condition stabilized, it was placed on a suitably elevated platform. Needle electrodes were inserted subcutaneously beneath both cheeks and the scalp. A speaker was positioned facing the mouse’s ear to deliver acoustic stimuli, and ABR thresholds were recorded to assess neural activity in response to sound. The experimental setup utilized Click stimuli and short tone bursts at different frequencies, with intensity levels ranging from 10 to 90 dB SPL. All procedures were carried out in a sound-attenuated chamber. The recorded signals were amplified 10,000-fold, with band-pass filtering set between 0.1 and 2 kHz, and averaged over 512 to 1024 sweeps. The BioSigRZ software application was used for data acquisition and analysis.

AAV injection in large animal models

Animals were intubated and maintained under continuous general anesthesia throughout the surgical procedure. After anesthesia of Bama miniature pigs and cynomolgus monkeys, the RWM was exposed via the mastoid facial recess route as reported in the previous article.6 Using a dedicated injection pump, 30 mL of AAV vectors were slowly injected into the inner ear of each animal at a rate of 50 nanoL per second. During the surgical procedure, the respiratory rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation of the experimental animals were continuously monitored to ensure their physiological stability. Animals were monitored daily after surgery for activity level, food intake, wound healing, and stool to ensure normal recovery.

ABR measurements in large animal models

The ABR tests on Bama miniature pigs and cynomolgus monkeys were conducted using the Neuro-Audio system (Neurosoft). The large animals were subjected to the examination under anesthesia, with their normal vital signs maintained. The detection electrodes were placed subcutaneously at the central forehead and the ipsilateral mastoid, with the ground electrode positioned subcutaneously on the opposite side. The closed-field pure-tone ABR test frequencies and Click ABR were measured. Within the range of sound pressure levels from 10 decibeLs to 90 decibeLs (sound pressure level unit), the ABR waveforms induced by clicking sounds and pure tone pulses were recorded. The audiometric data were processed using Neuro-Audio software to determine the ABR thresholds. Throughout the entire recording process, the basic vital signs such as blood pressure and body temperature remained within the stable physiological range.

Immunofluorescence and confocal imaging

After being carefully removed from the temporal skeleton, the cochleae were put in PBS (Multicell, Cat#311-011-CL) that had been cooled beforehand. Depending on age, tissues were kept for one to two hours at room temperature, in 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime, Cat#P0099-500). Following fixation, the samples were blocked for one to two hours at room temperature in PBS including donkey serum (10%, Solarbio, Cat#SL050) and Triton X-100 (0.5%, Solarbio, Cat#T8200-500). The primary antibody was then incubated with them overnight at 4°C. Anti-TPRN (rabbit, Merck, Cat#HPA020899), anti-Myo7a (rabbit, Proteus Bioscience, Cat#25–6790), anti-Sox2 (goat, R&D systems, Cat#AF2018), Anti-Otoferlin (C-terminal, rabbit, Thermo Scientific, Cat#PA552935), Anti-Otoferlin (N-terminal, mouse, Abcam, Cat#ab53233), and anti-HA (rat, Roche, Cat#11867423001) were among the primary antibodies. Fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied the next day, along with DAPI (Solarbio, Cat#C0060) for nuclear counterstaining. After washing the sample with PBS, put it on a slide and cover it with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting media (Dako, Cat#S3023). A confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss, LSM900) was utilized to perform fluorescence imaging, and its filter set was specifically configured for each type of fluorescent substance.

Western blotting experiments

HEK-293T cells were collected in centrifuge tubes 48 h post plasmid transfection, centrifuged to remove the supernatant, and lysed with lysis buffer (Thermo Scientific, Cat#89900). Protein samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer (GenScript, Cat#MB01015) at the appropriate ratio, heated at 75°C for 15 min, centrifuged, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to electrophoresis. SDS–PAGE was performed using 4–15% gradient gels (Beyotime, Cat#P0520S) under a constant voltage of 100 V for 1 h. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto membranes at a constant current of 300 mA for 1 h using rapid transfer buffer (Beyotime, Cat#P0572). The membranes were then blocked with rapid blocking buffer (Beyotime, Cat#P0252) for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies: anti-HA (mouse, CST, Cat#2367S) and anti-β-actin (mouse, CST, Cat#3700S).

After primary antibody incubation, membranes were washed in PBST for 10 min and subsequently incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (mouse, Proteintech, 1:5000, Cat#SA00001-2) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, membranes were washed three times with PBST (10 min each), and protein signals were detected using chemiluminescence.

ASR measurements in mice

The complete PPI procedure was conducted in a sound-attenuating chamber. Mice were placed individually in a wire-mesh cage, which was mounted on a customized platform connected to a pressure sensor. Acoustic stimuli were delivered through a high-frequency loudspeaker positioned 15 cm above the wire-mesh cage. The entire system was controlled by a data acquisition card (USB-6365, National Instruments, USA), which was used to generate different PPI stimulus paradigms and to synchronously record startle responses transmitted from the pressure sensor.

Sound stimuli were calibrated before each PPI testing session using a high-frequency microphone (PCB-378C01, PCB Piezotronics, USA). Prior to testing, mice were habituated to the wire-mesh cage inside the sound-attenuating chamber for 15 min, after which the PPI test was initiated immediately.

Each testing session consisted of 40 trials, including 10 startle-only trials and 30 pre-pulse + startle trials. In the startle-only trials, the startle stimulus was a 20 ms broadband noise at an intensity of 110 dB SPL. In the pre-pulse + startle trials, the startle stimulus was identical to that used in the startle-only trials. The pre-pulse stimulus was a 50 ms narrowband noise presented at three different center frequencies (16 kHz, 24 kHz, and 32 kHz; 10 trials per frequency) with an intensity of 70 dB SPL and was delivered 100 ms prior to the startle stimulus. All trials were presented in a randomized order, with an inter-trial interval of 7–15 s.

STED imaging

Under a 100 oil immersion (HC PL APO CS2, Leica), images were obtained using an STED 3× super-resolution microscope fitted with a pulsed laser (WLL2) and STED deplete lasers. For both confocal and STED acquisition modes, laser power, imaging velocity, and frame averaging were carefully adjusted to obtain high-quality images with a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels. Image deconvolution was carried out using the Lightning deconvolution module (Leica), to enhance image quality.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The initial acquisition software exported the images in TIFF format, and Fiji software was used for additional processing. All the photographs’ visibility and brightness were systematically changed. When quantifying the fluorescence intensity of infected green fluorescent IHCs and OHCs, one group is normalized to 1. GraphPad 10.3.2 software was used to conduct statistical analysis. All experimental values are reported as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance between groups was evaluated via one-way ANOVA or two-tailed unpaired Student’s t test, with significance defined as a p-value <0.05. ∗: p < 0.05, ∗∗: p < 0.01, ∗∗∗: p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗: p < 0.0001, n.s.: no significance. And, the statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends and results.
