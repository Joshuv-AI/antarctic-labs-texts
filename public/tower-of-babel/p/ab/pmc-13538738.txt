Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), which remains the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in this population [1]. Despite advances in multifactorial risk management targeting glycaemia, blood pressure, and lipids, substantial residual cardiovascular risk remains [2, 3]. Importantly, cardiovascular risk in T2D is heterogeneous, and traditional clinical risk scores, derived from population‐level demographic and metabolic factors, may not accurately reflect an individual's underlying coronary artery disease (CAD) burden [4, 5].

Computed tomography coronary angiography (CTCA) enables direct visualisation and quantification of coronary atherosclerosis, providing incremental risk stratification beyond conventional risk assessment [3, 6]. Prior studies have demonstrated that a proportion of individuals with T2D have minimal or no detectable CAD, while others exhibit significant subclinical or obstructive disease despite similar clinical risk profiles [7, 8]. These findings highlight the limitations of population‐based risk stratification and underscore the potential value of imaging‐based phenotyping in T2D.

In parallel, advances in human genomics have led to the development of polygenic risk scores (PRS) that quantify inherited susceptibility to CAD by aggregating the effects of numerous common genetic variants [9]. PRS capture lifelong genetic susceptibility and provide information that is largely independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors [10]. Emerging evidence suggests that genetic risk stratification may help identify individuals at increased risk of CAD who are not readily recognised using clinical measures alone [11, 12]. Importantly, elevated polygenic risk is not deterministic. Prior studies suggest that, even among individuals at high genetic risk, favourable lifestyle behaviours and lipid‐lowering therapy are associated with substantial reductions in CAD risk [13, 14]. However, the relationship between genetic risk and anatomical CAD, particularly in people with T2D, remains poorly defined.

To date, limited evidence has jointly evaluated coronary anatomical disease burden and polygenic risk specifically in adults with T2D [15]. Understanding the distribution and concordance between imaging‐based and genetic risk at baseline is essential for informing personalised approaches to cardiovascular risk assessment and prevention. The Evaluation of polygenic scores and CT imaging in risk factor modification in patients with Type 2 diabetes (VOLTAIRE) study was designed to address this gap.

This cross‐sectional analysis describes the baseline clinical characteristics, CTCA findings, and polygenic risk profiles of participants enrolled in the VOLTAIRE study. It aims to characterise the heterogeneity in clinically‐derived, anatomically determined, and genetic cardiovascular risk in adults with T2D prior to intervention and to explore concordance and discordance between these complementary risk assessment modalities.

Methods

Study Design and Population

VOLTAIRE is a single‐centre, prospective, three‐arm, parallel‐group, randomised controlled trial conducted at a tertiary heart hospital in Melbourne, Australia. The rationale and design of the parent VOLTAIRE study have been described previously [16]. The trial evaluates the impact of CTCA and PRS on cardiovascular risk factor modification in adults with T2D, combined with nurse‐led health coaching over 12 months. Adults aged 40 years or older with a diagnosis of T2D and no known clinically manifest atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) were eligible. Participants underwent baseline clinical assessment, CTCA, and PRS testing prior to randomisation.

The report is a cross‐sectional analysis of baseline data from participants who completed both CTCA and PRS assessment. Participants with obstructive CAD (≥ 50% stenosis in the left main coronary artery or ≥ 70% stenosis in any other epicardial coronary artery) were excluded from randomisation but were retained in an observational registry and included in this descriptive analysis. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (see Supporting Information S1) [17].

Baseline Clinical Assessment

At baseline, demographic characteristics, duration of diabetes, cardiovascular risk factors, medication use, and lifestyle behaviours were collected using standardised case report forms. Physical measurements included body mass index (BMI) and blood pressure (BP). Laboratory assessments included lipid profile, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hsCRP), and lipoprotein(a). Cardiovascular risk was estimated using the New Zealand Society for the Study of Diabetes 5‐year CVD risk calculator, a diabetes‐specific model developed for adults with T2D without previous CVD and externally validated in people with T2D.

Computed Tomography Coronary Angiography

All participants underwent baseline CTCA as part of the VOLTAIRE study. Coronary atherosclerotic burden was characterised using coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores, CTCA‐reported maximal luminal stenosis severity, and subjective plaque burden assessed using the Coronary Artery Disease‐Reporting and Data System (CAD‐RADS) 2.0 plaque burden categories (P1–P4) [18]. CTCA findings were obtained from clinical reports based on consensus reads between two CTCA‐certified reporting clinicians in the same session. For descriptive analyses, stenosis severity was categorised as no detectable disease, minimal disease (1%–20% stenosis), mild disease (21%–49%), moderate disease (50%–69%), or severe disease (> 70%), in accordance with CAD‐RADS 2.0 reporting criteria.

Polygenic Risk Score Assessment

At baseline, venous blood samples were collected for genomic analysis. Genotyping was performed using the Confluence array (Illumina, CA, USA), and PRS calculated at The Broad Institute. Polygenic risk for CAD was assessed using a previously described genome‐wide PRS for CAD [10]. The primary analysis used the ancestry‐adjusted weighted PRS. For descriptive analyses, PRS values were categorised into tertiles within the study cohort: low (≤ 33rd percentile), intermediate (34th–66th percentile), and high (≥ 67th percentile).

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables are presented as mean ±standard deviation or median with interquartile range, as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. CTCA findings, PRS categories, and clinical risk measures were described across the cohort. Exploratory subgroup comparisons using cross‐tabulations were performed to examine concordance and discordance between anatomical (CTCA), genetic (PRS), and clinical risk measures. Agreement beyond chance between binary high‐risk classifications was assessed using Cohen's kappa for pairwise comparisons and Fleiss' kappa for the three‐domain classification. All analyses were cross‐sectional and descriptive in nature and were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29). Missing data were minimal and analyses were performed using complete‐case data; no imputation procedures were applied. RC had full access to all the data in the study and takes responsibility for the integrity of the data and the accuracy of the data analysis.

Results

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 126 participants with T2D were included in this baseline analysis, all of whom completed CTCA and PRS assessment prior to randomisation. The mean age of the cohort was 57.5 ± 8.7 years, and 62.7% were male. Participants were ethnically diverse, with 52.4% identifying as White and 38.1% as Asian. The mean duration of diabetes was 11.1 ± 8.3 years, and 52 participants (41%) were on insulin.

The mean BMI was 31.0 ± 7.2 kg/m2. Mean systolic and diastolic BP were 125.2 ± 14.0 mmHg and 78.2 ± 9.4 mmHg, respectively, with one‐third of participants recording a baseline systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg. With respect to lifestyle factors, almost 20% were current smokers, 45.2% met physical activity recommendations (≥ 150 min/week), and 29.4% reported consuming at least five daily servings of fruits and vegetables. Overall, 72 participants (57.1%) were classified as high clinical risk (≥ 15%). Baseline demographic, clinical, and lifestyle characteristics of the study population are summarised in Table 1.

Cardiometabolic and Laboratory Measures

Baseline cardiometabolic and laboratory measures are summarised in Table 1. Glycaemic control was heterogeneous, with a mean HbA1c of 8.0% ± 2.1%. Lipid measures indicated residual cardiometabolic risk, with a mean LDL cholesterol of 2.6 ± 0.9 mmol/L and HDL cholesterol of 1.3 ± 0.3 mmol/L. Triglycerides, lipoprotein(a), and hs‐CRP showed skewed distributions.

Baseline CTCA Findings

Baseline CTCA findings are summarised in Table 1. CAC burden was highly variable, with a median of 19 (IQR 0–150). Over one‐third of participants had a CAC score of 0 (44, 34.9%), while 25 (19.8%) had significant calcification (CAC ≥ 400). On CTCA assessment of luminal disease, 20 (15.9%) participants had no detectable stenosis, whereas the remainder demonstrated at least minimal disease (106, 84.1%). Overall, 51 (40.5%) had moderate‐to‐severe stenosis (≥ 50%). For CAD‐RADS 2.0 plaque burden modifiers, P1 was the most common category (57, 45.2%), while P4 plaque burden was present in 13 participants (10.3%).

Among participants with CAC = 0 (n = 44), CTCA identified plaque or stenosis in 24 participants (54.5%), including 15 (34.1%) with minimal disease, 3 (6.8%) with mild disease, and 6 (13.6%) with moderate‐to‐severe stenosis. Non‐calcified plaque was explicitly noted in CTCA reports in 20 participants (45.5%). High PRS and high clinical cardiovascular risk were present in 10 (22.7%) and 13 (29.5%) participants, respectively (Table S1).

Polygenic Risk Score Distribution

Baseline PRS distributions are summarised in Table 1. Using cohort‐derived tertile thresholds, 47 participants (37.3%) were classified as low genetic risk (PRS ≤ 33), 45 (35.7%) as intermediate risk (PRS 34–66), and 34 (27.0%) as high risk (PRS ≥ 67).

Participants With Obstructive CAD at Baseline

Among those with obstructive CAD on baseline CTCA (n = 43), CAC scores within this group were widely distributed, with a median of 294 (IQR 44–954) and values ranging from 0 to 4082. Notably, obstructive CAD was observed across the spectrum of CAC burden, including 13 participants (30.2%) with CAC < 100. Polygenic risk profiles were heterogeneous, with 15 participants (34.9%) classified as high genetic risk.

Overlap and Agreement Between Anatomical, Genetic, and Clinical Risk Measures

Integration of anatomical (CTCA), genetic (PRS), and clinical risk measures revealed heterogeneous risk classification among adults with T2D. Cross‐tabulation of risk categories demonstrated limited overlap between high‐risk classifications across domains (Table 2A). Only 11 participants (8.7%) met the predefined criteria for high risk across all three domains, defined by moderate‐to‐severe coronary stenosis (≥ 50%), high polygenic risk (PRS ≥ 67), and high clinical cardiovascular risk (5‐year CVD risk ≥ 15%). The expected three‐way overlap under an independence assumption was 6.2%, or approximately eight participants, only modestly lower than the observed overlap of 8.7%.

CTCA stenosis severity varied across polygenic risk categories (Table 2B). Among participants with high polygenic risk (n = 34), coronary anatomy ranged from no detectable disease in 7 (20.6%) to moderate‐to‐severe stenosis in 17 (50.0%). Conversely, among those with low polygenic risk (n = 47), 12 (25.5%) nonetheless had moderate‐to‐severe stenosis. Among participants with moderate‐to‐severe stenosis (n = 51), genetic risk profiles were widely distributed, with 17 classified as high genetic risk and 12 as low genetic risk. Within this subgroup, diabetes duration tended to be longer in those with high polygenic risk compared with those with lower genetic risk (mean 17.0 vs. 12.8 years; p = 0.09).

Anatomical findings also diverged from clinical cardiovascular risk categories (Table 2C). Of the 72 participants classified as high clinical risk, 40 (55.6%) had moderate‐to‐severe stenosis. Among those classified as low clinical risk, 11 (20.4%) also had moderate‐to‐severe stenosis. High clinical cardiovascular risk was observed across all polygenic risk categories (Table 2D), including 20 (58.8%) of those with high PRS, 28 (62.2%) with intermediate PRS, and 24 (51.1%) with low PRS.

Agreement beyond chance between binary high‐risk classifications was limited (Table 2E). Cohen's kappa was highest for CTCA‐defined anatomical risk versus high clinical risk (κ = 0.34), and lower for CTCA‐defined anatomical risk versus high PRS (κ = 0.11) and high PRS versus high clinical risk (κ = 0.02). Fleiss' kappa across the three binary risk domains was 0.13, consistent with limited overall agreement.

Ethnicity‐stratified distributions of PRS category, CTCA stenosis severity, and clinical risk are presented in Table S2. High PRS was observed in 19 of 66 White participants (28.8%), 13 of 48 Asian participants (27.1%), and 2 of 12 participants from other ethnic groups (16.7%). Moderate‐to‐severe CTCA stenosis was present in 29 White participants (43.9%), 19 Asian participants (39.6%), and 3 participants from other ethnic groups (25.0%). Given the modest subgroup sizes, these analyses were descriptive and were not intended to assess PRS calibration or ancestry‐specific predictive performance.

Discussion

This baseline cross‐sectional analysis of adults with T2D demonstrates considerable heterogeneity and limited overlap across anatomical, genetic, and clinical cardiovascular risk domains. Obstructive CAD was observed across all strata of genetic and clinical risk, highlighting the limitations of single‐domain risk assessment. These findings underscore that anatomical disease burden, inherited susceptibility, and clinically estimated risk represent distinct but complementary dimensions of cardiovascular risk. This may reflect the different biological and temporal aspects of atherosclerotic risk captured by each risk measure.

Heterogeneity of CAD Anatomy in T2D

CTCA revealed substantial heterogeneity in coronary atherosclerotic burden among adults with T2D, despite their generally similar clinical risk profiles [7, 8]. In our cohort, CAC burden ranged from absent in over one‐third of participants to severe calcification in nearly one‐fifth. Clinically significant luminal stenosis was also identified in a substantial proportion of participants, including approximately one‐fifth of those classified as low clinical cardiovascular risk. This degree of anatomical variability reinforces that T2D does not uniformly confer coronary risk and challenges the concept of diabetes as a homogeneous coronary disease equivalent. These findings further highlight the potential for under‐recognition of disease when cardiovascular risk assessment relies predominantly on population‐based algorithms alone [7, 8].

These observations are consistent with prior CT‐based studies in T2D, which have demonstrated substantial variability in coronary anatomy, including subgroups with minimal or absent disease alongside others with extensive, often subclinical, atherosclerosis [19, 20]. Previous cohorts have reported a high prevalence of zero CAC scores in people with T2D, while also identifying non‐calcified plaque and obstructive disease in some individuals with low or absent calcification, underscoring the limitations of calcium scoring when used in isolation [21, 22]. Our CAC = 0 subgroup analysis further supports this point. Among participants with CAC = 0, CTCA identified plaque or stenosis in more than half, and non‐calcified plaque was explicitly noted in 45.5%. This aligns with Yoon et al., who reported that adults with diabetes and CAC = 0 may still have a higher prevalence of non‐calcified plaque (16.7% vs. 11.6% in those without diabetes) and subsequent revascularisation risk [21]. Accordingly, CAC = 0 should not be interpreted as excluding coronary atherosclerosis in adults with T2D, and these data support the additional value of CTCA for characterising non‐calcified disease. CTCA‐based screening studies have also shown that a proportion of asymptomatic individuals with T2D harbour obstructive coronary disease despite only low‐to‐moderate estimated clinical risk [23, 24].

These findings suggest that conventional factor‐based clinical risk scores, which lack direct assessment of coronary anatomy, may underestimate clinically relevant atherosclerotic burden in some individuals with T2D [25]. Obstructive CAD among individuals with low clinical or genetic risk further implies that cumulative metabolic exposure, inflammatory processes, and other non‐traditional mechanisms may contribute to plaque development [24, 26].

Polygenic Risk Stratification and CAD in T2D

PRS varied across participants but showed limited discrimination of CTCA‐defined disease severity. High polygenic risk was not consistently associated with advanced coronary atherosclerosis, and obstructive CAD was also observed in some individuals with low genetic risk. These observations suggest that PRS captures a dimension of risk related to, yet distinct from, the current anatomical burden of disease [27].

Population‐based studies show that PRS are strongly associated with incident coronary events and long‐term cardiovascular risk, largely independent of traditional risk factors [10]. However, the clinical utility of PRS for improving conventional risk prediction remains uncertain. An analysis of the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study and the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis found that a CAD PRS was associated with incident coronary heart disease, but did not improve discrimination, reclassification, or calibration when added to the Pooled Cohort Equations [28]. Our study instead examines baseline relationships between PRS, diabetes‐specific clinical risk, and CTCA‐defined anatomical disease in adults with T2D. Thus, our findings support the complementary nature of genetic risk information, but do not show that the T2D context changes the incremental predictive utility of PRS.

Previous imaging‐based analyses show that polygenic risk is associated with overall atherosclerotic burden, including CAC and angiographic CAD severity, but not specific plaque characteristics or lesion‐level severity on cross‐sectional imaging [29, 30]. In 6021 patients undergoing coronary angiography, higher PRS was associated with greater CAD severity [30]. In our smaller CTCA‐based T2D cohort, moderate‐to‐severe stenosis was present in 50.0% with high PRS versus 25.5% with low PRS. The finding that one‐quarter of low‐PRS participants nonetheless had obstructive stenosis highlights the contribution of non‐genetic and diabetes‐related factors to anatomical CAD burden. These findings suggest that PRS primarily reflect lifelong susceptibility rather than current anatomical disease expression, particularly at a single time point [27, 31].

Accordingly, individuals with high polygenic risk but limited anatomical disease may represent an earlier stage along the atherosclerotic trajectory, in whom early and sustained preventive strategies may yield the greatest long‐term benefit [32]. Conversely, advanced CAD in individuals with low polygenic risk underscores the influence of non‐genetic factors, such as duration of diabetes, cumulative metabolic exposure, and environmental or behavioural determinants, in driving plaque development [2]. Within T2D, PRS therefore function as complementary risk markers that provide forward‐looking information not captured by imaging or clinical assessment alone [9].

Clinical Implications

In our cohort, integration of anatomical, genetic, and clinical risk measures revealed limited overlap across domains, highlighting the multidimensional nature of cardiovascular risk in T2D [2]. This pattern is clinically informative because each modality captures a different aspect of risk: CTCA characterises current coronary anatomy and plaque burden [6], PRS estimates lifelong inherited susceptibility [9], and clinical calculators predict near‐term event risk based on population‐level associations [4]. These findings support a multimodal risk‐assessment framework that integrates clinical, anatomical, and genetic information, rather than relying on any single modality.

These domains are also differentially influenced by treatment exposure. PRS is fixed from conception, whereas clinical risk estimates and CTCA‐derived coronary phenotypes may be modified by prior and ongoing preventive therapies. In this cohort, baseline use of statins (62.7%), SGLT2 inhibitors (38.1%), and GLP‐1 receptor agonists (34.1%) was common. The limited overalp observed may therefore partly reflect the interaction between lifelong genetic susceptibility and treatment‐modified cardiometabolic and anatomical phenotypes.

Patterns of multimodal risk classification across domains may inform tailored preventive strategies. For example, a high atherosclerotic plaque burden may support more intensive risk factor management irrespective of genetic risk, whereas high genetic susceptibility in the absence of advanced anatomical disease may identify individuals who would benefit from earlier or more sustained preventive interventions. These hypotheses require prospective validation.

Clinically, this integrated approach may enable more personalised prevention, including tailored lipid‐lowering and blood pressure management, while making cardiovascular risk more tangible for patients. Longitudinal follow‐up is required to determine whether baseline risk classification patterns predicts future cardiovascular events or differential response to interventions. Equitable precision prevention will also require validation and calibration of PRS and imaging‐derived thresholds across diverse ethnic populations to ensure broad generalisability, particularly in the Asia‐Pacific region, where current PRS may perform less well in non‐European groups [27, 33].

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it integrates anatomical imaging, genetic risk, and clinical cardiovascular risk assessment within the same cohort of adults with T2D, enabling direct comparison of overlap and agreement across risk domains. Second, the use of CTCA allowed detailed characterisation of coronary atherosclerosis, including obstructive CAD that may not be captured by traditional risk scores or CAC assessment alone. Third, PRS were incorporated alongside contemporary clinical risk calculators, providing insight into the complementary and non‐redundant information contributed by genetic susceptibility.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional baseline design precludes causal inference and cannot determine the prognostic implications of the observed multimodal risk classifications. These questions will be addressed in the parent VOLTAIRE trial, in which plaque progression will be assessed using serial CTCA imaging. The moderate sample size may limit statistical power for subgroup analyses and the detection of modest associations. PRS analysis was based on a single CAD PRS construction derived predominantly from European‐ancestry populations, which may limit portability and generalisability to other ethnic groups in our cohort, including 38.1% Asian participants. Although an ancestry‐adjusted weighted PRS was used, findings from a single PRS may vary across populations and PRS models [11, 27, 33]. Future analyses should assess robustness using alternative CAD PRS constructions in larger and more ethnically diverse T2D cohorts. Furthermore, PRS categories were derived using cohort‐specific percentile thresholds, which may have reduced discrimination across groups and limited comparability with external populations. As this cohort had a mean diabetes duration of more than 11 years, it is uncertain whether similar findings would be observed in individuals with shorter‐duration T2D. Baseline coronary atherosclerotic burden and clinical risk estimates may have been influenced by prior and ongoing preventive therapies, including lipid‐lowering therapy, SGLT2 inhibitor therapy, and GLP‐1 receptor agonist therapy. As PRS is fixed from conception, this uneven exposure to treatment across risk domains may have affected the observed relationships between anatomical, genetic, and clinical risk measures. Finally, clinical outcomes were not assessed. Longitudinal follow‐up will be required to determine whether baseline multimodal risk classifications are associated with subsequent cardiovascular events or differential treatment response. This will enable evaluation of the health economic implications of integrating imaging and genetic testing into CVD risk assessment.

Conclusion

In this cohort of adults with T2D, overlap between anatomical, genetic, and clinical cardiovascular risk measures was limited, underscoring the multidimensional nature of cardiovascular risk in this population. Obstructive CAD occurred across genetic and clinical risk strata, including among individuals with low risk by conventional measures. These findings support a multimodal approach to cardiovascular risk assessment that integrates coronary imaging, genetic risk, and clinical evaluation.
