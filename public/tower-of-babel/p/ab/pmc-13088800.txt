A toothache gone wrong: an unexpected case of perioperative lactation ketoacidosis
Authors: Charlotte Bassuel, Maxime Michot, Sandrine Dabernat, Marie-Lise Bats
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLactation ketoacidosis is a rare metabolic complication of breastfeeding, characterised by high anion gap metabolic acidosis due to elevated levels of ketone bodies in the blood or urine. Common symptoms include nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and general malaise. The typical triggers for lactation ketoacidosis are intensive exercise, a ketogenic diet, reduced food intake or fasting, often associated with an attempt to lose weight.Case presentationWe report the case of a 31-year-old woman who had been breastfeeding continuously for two years, including throughout her most recent pregnancy, and had been breastfeeding both children for the past six months, without following any special diet. In November 2023, she was admitted to the emergency department with severe toothache, fever, dysphagia, and odynophagia, leading to the diagnosis of dental cellulitis requiring urgent surgery. In the postoperative period, she developed metabolic acidosis with a high-anion gap, normal lactate, and elevated ketones but without hyperglycaemia, consistent with lactation ketoacidosis. This exceptional presentation was likely triggered by a combination of recent fasting due to dental pain, acute infection, and perioperative metabolic stress. Prompt treatment with bicarbonate, glucose infusion, and enteral hydration led to rapid clinical and biological improvement.ConclusionsThis case underscores the necessity of a careful pre-anaesthetic history in lactating women, including dietary information, breastfeeding habits and potential stressors such as intercurrent illness. To mitigate the risk of ketoacidosis, preoperative fasting times should be minimised, as recommended. In breastfeeding patients at high risk of metabolic complications, perioperative administration of glucose might be considered. Furthermore, it is crucial to pay close attention to the dental health of pregnant and lactating women to reduce the need for surgical procedures that may lead to such severe complications.Keywords: Breastfeeding, Dental surgery, Lactation ketoacidosis, Metabolic acidosis, Oral intake, Perioperative stress, Preoperative fasting

Background

Lactation ketoacidosis is a rare but clinically significant condition, characterised by a high-anion gap metabolic acidosis due to the accumulation of ketone bodies. This metabolic complication, which presents with non-specific symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and abdominal pain, represents an important differential diagnosis to consider in lactating women. Although uncommon, lactation ketoacidosis typically arises in the context of a prolonged caloric deficit, compensated by accelerated lipolysis and ketogenesis, leading to metabolic acidosis [1]. Initially documented in extensive-lactating cows, where the energy demands of milk production exceed the metabolic capacity of the animal, lactation ketoacidosis remains a rare condition in humans. As of 2020, fewer than 20 cases of ketoacidosis of breastfeeding mothers have been documented, mainly triggered by reduced food intake and/or intensive exercise, often associated with attempts to lose weight [2–4]. To date, no cases of lactation ketoacidosis have been described in the perioperative setting, probably due to existing recommendations that limit preoperative fasting and encourage early resumption of feeding and breastfeeding. Nevertheless, these guidelines do not usually recommend consideration of glucose-containing fluids or monitoring for lactation ketoacidosis [5]. The main objective of this article is to highlight the importance of actively screening breastfeeding women to enable prompt and appropriate medical care.

Case presentation

We report here an atypical case of lactation ketoacidosis in a perioperative context. The patient was a 31-year-old Ukrainian woman who had resided in France for one year in a refugee reception centre. It was noted that she did not speak French, which is the initial language of the medical staff, and no interpreter was called for help. In November 2023, she was admitted to the emergency department of Bordeaux University Hospital with complaints of a severe toothache that had persisted for five days. The condition was further complicated by dysphagia, right lateral cervical swelling and fever for two days. The patient was treated with paracetamol. Following admission, the clinical examination revealed right perimandibular edema, trismus with a one-finger mouth opening, pain on axial pressure of teeth 47 and 48, and right jugulocarotid reactive lymphadenopathy. The patient exhibited normal blood pressure and a soft abdomen on palpation. Her heart rate was 89 beats per minute. The laboratory evaluation was suggestive of an inflammatory syndrome with high C-reactive protein (CRP) level and hyperleukocytosis (Table 1), in concordance with her clinical symptoms. The blood analysis also indicated a subnormal bicarbonate concentration combined with a slight increase in the anion gap.

The patient had a recent history of dental drilling of tooth 47, three weeks prior to hospitalisation. She had no relevant past medical or surgical history, apart from an obstetric status of G4P4. Her two eldest children were aged 11 and 9 years, while her two youngest children were aged 2 years and 6 months, respectively. The patient reported continuous breastfeeding for two years, including throughout her most recent pregnancy. At the time of hospitalisation, she had been breastfeeding both of her two youngest children for six months. She was exclusively breastfeeding and did not use a breast pump. The patient had a body mass index (BMI) of 30 kg/m², with a height of 1.65 m and a weight of 82 kg, and was not following a special diet. She did not practise exercise regularly. The patient reported reduced oral intake for one week prior to hospitalization secondary to dental pain. The surgical examination revealed the presence of two dental abscesses with cellulitis, which required immediate surgery. Antibiotic treatment by amoxicillin and clavulanic acid were started a few hours before surgery. During the intraoperative period, the patient received 1,5 L of Plasmalyte ® (Baxter International). She was not supplemented with glucose. She underwent total intravenous anaesthesia with remifentanil and propofol. In the immediate postoperative period, due to the severity of the upper airway oedema secondary to oral infection, continuous sedation with propofol and sufentanil was required, in preparation for transfer to the surgical intensive care unit (ICU). The edema required a short course of corticosteroid therapy to allow subsequent consideration of extubation. At this time, as she remained sedated, it was not possible to assess whether the patient’s clinical status deteriorated. However, routine laboratory investigations performed at ICU admission revealed a severe metabolic acidosis with markedly elevated anion gap. The presence of high-anion gap can be attributed to four common causes: renal failure, the abnormal presence of toxins (methanol, ethanol, salicylate), lactic acid or ketones. In the case of the patient, plasma creatinine exhibited a slight increase during the postoperative period, which was likely due to dehydration. The possibility of lactic acidosis was excluded as plasma lactic acid was within the normal range. Furthermore, there was no documented history of exposure to exogenous acids. Nevertheless, high levels of ketones were found in both the urinary sample and capillary blood, thereby confirming the diagnosis of ketoacidosis. Diabetic ketoacidosis was rapidly excluded, as blood analysis did not evidence hyperglycaemia, nor pre-existing diabetes (Table 1). Given the prolonged and intense breastfeeding experience, the diagnosis of lactation ketoacidosis was established. The acute episode was managed with bicarbonate 4.2% (500 mL over one hour), intravenous glucose 10% (500 mL over 6 h), and continuous intravenous glucose 5% (1500 mL per day), combined by enteral hydration using water at 20 mL per hour. This treatment led to rapid resolution of the symptoms and metabolic abnormalities within 24 h.

In the ICU, the patient was extubated and fully awakened after 24 h. Because the patient experienced nausea, oral intake was not immediately resumed. Continuous intravenous glucose 5% 1500 mL per day was maintained. Capillary blood glucose monitoring performed during this period showed no abnormalities.

The patient was discharged from the ICU three days after surgery, and resumed cold liquid oral intake on postoperative fourth day. Postoperative lactation was supported in the ICU through the use of a breast pump, with an immediate postoperative milk expression of 380 mL. Access to a breast pump was provided, allowing continued milk expression throughout hospitalisation. The medical staff actively encouraged this practice and subsequently recommended resumption of direct breastfeeding in accordance with the patient’s usual habits at hospital discharge. Direct breastfeeding of her two youngest children was resumed seven days after hospital admission. No lactation-related complications, including mastitis, reduced milk supply, early weaning, or other adverse outcomes, were observed. Given the patient’s lactation status, no contraindicated analgesic medications were prescribed.

Discussion and conclusions

The originality of this report lies in its focus on metabolic needs for a breastfeeding mother, disrupted by the combination of intensive lactation, low oral intake, acute infection, and perisurgical systemic stressors.

Breastfeeding is a physiological process that requires significant high-energy input, particularly carbohydrates, which may predispose individuals to ketogenesis. The perisurgical context enhances the metabolic challenges for lactating women [6]. Preoperative fasting, commonly used to reduce the risk of aspiration during anaesthesia, can induce a starvation state and may lead to severe ketoacidosis in rare cases, especially under physiologic stress or high energy demand. This was reported in vulnerable populations such as pregnant women, or infants, and patients undergoing prolonged fasting [6–9]. To avoid this situation, guidelines on anaesthesia and sedation in breastfeeding women recommend minimising preoperative fasting times to avoid dehydration and supporting early resumption of oral intake and breastfeeding as soon as the patient is alert and able to feed. This is easily implemented in routine practice when surgery is planned [5].

In our scenario, minimal preoperative fasting instructions were not a contributing factor, as she was taken urgently to surgery. Anaesthesia and surgery are therefore unlikely to be the primary cause of ketoacidosis. However, she had been suffering from a dental cavity for several weeks, a common buccal affliction, with increased incidence during pregnancy [10]. Although the exact timing of her last oral or carbohydrate intake could not be determined during the hospital stay due to a language barrier, information obtained retrospectively through an interpreter confirmed that the severe dental pain and reduced jaw mobility had led to markedly decreased oral intake during the week preceding admission. This likely exacerbated the carbohydrate deficiency associated with intensive breastfeeding, resulting in lipolysis and the production of ketone bodies.

Moreover, patients who present in an inflammatory state with poor oral intake, as for the present case, may experience additional catecholamine and cortisol release in response to surgical intervention, further promoting catabolic pathways [11, 12].

Thus, as summarized by Al Alawi et al. [3], the patient presented several risk factors of ketoacidosis, including breastfeeding two children (comparable to twin lactation), a likely insufficient overall calorie intake, and an intercurrent illness.

Among key recommendations, we suggest that anaesthesiologists should obtain detailed lactation history, including the number of children and the duration-, nutritional habits -including fasting or ketogenic diets-, exercise routines with an emphasis on intensive activities. The history should also include potential stressors, such as infections, coexisting acute or chronic diseases, and concomitant treatments. In some cases, language barriers may contribute to incomplete clinical assessment in acute care settings, underscoring the importance of timely access to interpretation assistance.

Increased vigilance may be warranted in perioperative patients requiring urgent surgical management who present with infection, reduced oral intake, and increased metabolic demand. To mitigate the onset of ketosis if such risk factors are present, preoperative carbohydrate supplementation might be considered, such as oral glucose loading, particularly in elective surgical procedures [13]. In this particular case, the metabolic challenges would have required preoperative glucose-containing fluid administration.

Intraoperative management may also focus on limiting metabolic stress. Anaesthetic agents and surgical practices influence metabolic pathways. To this end, management of diabetic ketoacidosis may be informative since this condition shares similar metabolic anomalies. For example, the use of locoregional anaesthesia in diabetic patients can help mitigate hypotension [14]. Furthermore, it offers additional benefits by reducing postoperative opioid requirements, thereby limiting postoperative nausea and vomiting, which can in turn trigger ketoacidosis. Postoperative refeeding occurs earlier following regional rather than general anaesthesia [15]. Indeed, the prompt reintroduction of food is necessary for resuming glycogen storage and thus meeting the high energy requirements of lactation [5].

Close perioperative monitoring of blood gases, glucose, and electrolytes should be considered in selected high-risk cases or in breastfeeding patients at increased risk of complications, to allow early identification and management of metabolic disturbances and to reduce the risk of organ dysfunction and adverse outcomes.

This report also illustrates the necessity for anaesthesiologists to consider lactating women undergoing surgical procedures at risk for lactation ketoacidosis, especially given that prevalence and duration of breastfeeding have increased over time for its major benefits to newborn development [16]. The preoperative history of lactating women should include details of their breastfeeding habits, if applicable. Moreover, mothers should be informed of the potential metabolic risks during breastfeeding and the need for adequate nutrition. When breastfeeding intensively (i.e. feeding two children in this case), the additional energy expenditure must be taken into account to avoid recurrence of such situations.

Finally, careful monitoring of the dental health of pregnant and lactating women is essential to minimize the need for surgical interventions that could result in critical complications.

In summary, lactation ketoacidosis should be considered a preventable metabolic complication resulting from insufficient caloric intake in the setting of increased energy demands, rather than a contraindication to breastfeeding itself. This case highlights the importance of adequate nutritional intake, particularly during periods of illness or fasting, and underscores the need for targeted education of both lactating women and healthcare professionals.
