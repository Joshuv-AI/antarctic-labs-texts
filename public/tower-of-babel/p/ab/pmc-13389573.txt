Protective role of Luteolin against salinity-induced oxidative damage in Medicago sativa
Authors: Laiane Vieira da Silva, Antonia Adeublena de Araújo Monteiro, Reginaldo Rodrigues Souza, Luiz Marivando Barros, Elizangela Beneval Bento, Jean Paul Kamdem, Carlos Alonso Leite dos Santos, May Bin-Jumah, Ahmed A Allam, Mostafa R Abukhadra, Mohammad Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSalinity is among the major barriers to vegetative growth which disrupts plant development, nutrient uptake and metabolic homeostasis. The overabundance of ions (sodium, chloride, etc.) has an impact on the important plant processes, and the death of the plants is frequent. Medicago sativa L. has moderate tolerance to salt with a reduction in development being experienced when high levels of saline stress caused by the addition of NaCl are absorbed. Antioxidant flavonoids have therefore been of focus with regard to their protective properties; one of the such antioxidant flavonoid is luteolin (LTON) which has been explored as a possible mitigating flavonoid against the damages caused by environmental pressures. This experiment compared the protective action of LTON (250 and 500 µg/mL) alone and in the presence of NaCl (50 mM) against morphological, physiological and biochemical parameters of M. sativa. The seeds were incubated for a period of 10 days and germination, root and shoot length, relative water content, oxidative biomarkers and in silico molecular docking were evaluated. The findings revealed that saline stress significantly inhibited the germination, root, and leaf growth, and enhanced the oxidative stress. The isolated LTON spurred germination and growth and when it was coupled with NaCl, it reduced the ill-effect of salt. These results imply that LTON has a great protective effect on saline stress in M. sativa and this may be linked to the antioxidant potential of LTON in promoting plant resilience.Keywords: Antioxidant, Flavonoids, Oxidative stress, Molecular DockingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Salinization of soil is one of the global issues impacting the environmental services and socioeconomic relations and is a crucial factor in land degradation that makes land cultivation impossible and results in the loss of biodiversity1,2. Salinity is an abiotic stress factor that restricts the agricultural productivity in various parts of the world to a great extent which is mainly enhanced by poor agricultural practices3. First, it can be described as a surplus of minerals in plants which can be sodium (Na+) and or chloride (Cl−) leading to saline stress4,5. This kind of stress inhibits the rate of photosynthesis and lowers the capacity of the plants in transforming energy into biomass6,7.

Such a situation limits the absorption of CO₂ and causes the reduction of chlorophyll8. Also, this ionic toxicity has the ability to stimulate oxidative stress leading to the build-up of reactive oxygen species (ROS)9, which causes the redox imbalance, forcing the plant to respond by activating its defense mechanisms to overcome cellular damage10,11.

Alfalfa (Medicago sativa L.), a perennial leguminous forage, is widely cultivated due to its desirable traits, including its high nutritional value12. This perennial plant is of tremendous agronomic significance as it has the ability to fix N₂13. Nevertheless, salinity exceeding 2.0 dS m⁻¹ in M. sativa inhibit nutrient absorption, cell membrane stability and structure, enzymatic activities, rate of photosynthesis, and water potential14,15.

In order to reverse the damages mentioned above, flavonoids become the basic strategies. The phenolic compounds are also prevalent among the vegetative species and have been known to be anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory in nature, and as such, they provide a range of positive impacts on the vegetation16. Besides flavonoids, plants can also produce chalcones that perform secondary metabolism17.

Under this context, Chalcone Synthase (CHS) is the key to plant survival and is considered to be the gatekeeper enzyme that triggers the biosynthetic pathway of flavonoids and isoflavonoids18. In this context, Chalcone Synthase (CHS) stands out as a key enzyme for flavonoid regulation, playing a major role in plant signaling and resistance to saline stress19. One of the most active of such molecules is luteolin (LTON)-a 3’,4’,5,7 -tetrahydroxyflavone. The presence of the catechol group in the B-ring and a C2 = C3 double bond in the C-ring grants LTON a higher radical scavenging capacity than other flavonoids20. Furthermore, the economic viability justifies the choice of LTON, as it can be extracted from abundant agricultural by-products, such as peanut shells21.

The exogenous application of LTON emerges as a promising strategy due to the ability of flavonoids to restore redox homeostasis and directly scavenge free radicals, inducing membrane stabilization and protection against oxidative damage caused by osmotic and ionic stresses22. Flavonoids interact with plant regulatory proteins, and their application is a growing trend in sustainable agriculture to mitigate the effects resulting from salinity23. This potential is further linked to its capacity to support the plant’s phytochemical profile, thereby safeguarding overall metabolism24.

Nevertheless, the alleviating effect of the luteolin in particular on the M. sativa crops, in the pursuit of restoring the growth and development of the plant, remains to be thoroughly studied. While the antioxidant properties of LTON are recognized in other species, its specific interaction with the physiological pathways of alfalfa under saline conditions has not been fully established. Therefore, this study was undertaken to examine the possibility of using LTON as an antioxidant chemical on the seeds of M. sativa that were subjected to saline stress caused by NaCl. We aimed at examining the effectiveness of this flavonoid in reducing the destruction of biochemical, physiological and morphological parameters of the seedlings.

Materials and methods

Plant material

The seeds of Medicago sativa L. (creole alfalfa) used in this experiment were purchased through a reputed commercial supplier (Sao Paulo, Brazil) and kept in dry, refrigerated conditions until usage.

Exposure of seeds to the compound

A total of 900 M. sativa seeds were used, distributed across 18 plates (50 seeds per plate). The seeds were surface sterilized with 0.1% KMnO₄ for 10 min, followed by successive washes with distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then pre-soaked by full immersion in the treatment solutions for 2 h at room temperature. The experimental design included: a negative control [Control (-)] containing 0.1% (v/v) dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO); a positive control [Control (+)] with 50 mM NaCl; isolated luteolin (LTON) treatments at 250 µg/mL and 500 µg/mL; and combined treatments (LTON + 50 mM NaCl) in a 1:1 ratio. The choice of the 50 mM NaCl concentration was based on recent studies involving flavonoids and salt stress25. This dosage proved to be effective without compromising plant development. Additionally, the use of two LTON concentrations was determined based on screening protocols for antioxidant flavonoids, aiming to identify dose-dependent effects25. Each Petri dish, containing two layers of filter paper, was saturated with 5 mL of the respective solution. The plates were sealed and incubated in a Germination Chamber with Photoperiod (BOD) growth chamber at 26.0 °C ± 0.6 °C under a 12-hour light/dark photoperiod. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Germination and germination inhibition analysis

The germination index was assessed within 10 days and measured on a daily basis. It was observed after every 24 h following the administration of treatment. The 10-day experimental interval was adopted in accordance with the Rules for Seed Analysis26, which establish this period as the parameter for the final germination count in M. sativa. The root growth activity was relatively inhibited and the values were in form of percentages. To determine the reproducibility of the results, all the experiments were performed in triplicates.

Germination speed coefficient

Based on the approach outlined by Kotowski, the coefficient of the germination speed (GSC) was computed, with the changes suggested by27. M. sativa germination was observed in Petri dishes and the germinated seed count counted at 24 h and counted at 10 days intervals. According to the obtained data, the germination speed coefficient-a measure of the speed of the germination process-was identified. In the calculation, time in days of germination (T) was used and so was the number of germinated seeds (A).

Measurement of root and shoot length

Measurement of the length of the shoots (leaves) and roots was done at the end of experimental period of 10 days. A digital caliper was used to obtain measurements to provide a reliable analysis of length. The number of roots and the number of leaves of each treatment was also counted. Repeatedly, the experiment was done three times.

Determination of relative water content

Relative Water Content (RWC) was calculated according to the guidelines of28. An M. sativa leaf was cut in the form of a disc and weighed to get the fresh weight (FW). The discs were then left to undergo a period of hydration till the achievement of full turgor. The samples were then removed, blotted and weighed to get the turgid weight (TW). Lastly, the discs were dried in an oven at 24 h and reshaped to ascertain the dry weight (DW).

Electrolyte leakage

The measurement of electrolyte leakage (EL) was carried out according to the algorithm that was outlined by29,30. The weight of the fresh (FW) of M. sativa leaf discs was first established. These discs were placed in 25 mL of double-distilled water and allowed to incubate at the room temperature of 12 h. At this point, the first electrical conductivity (EC₁) was obtained through the conductivity meter. The samples were then incubated in a water bath at a temperature of 90.0 ± 0.5 °C at 30 min so that the cell membranes were completely broken. Once it was cooled to room temperature, electrical conductivity (EC₂) was measured.

Experimental treatments

Preparation of M. sativa homogenates

Biochemical tests of the seeds were done after morphological and germination evaluation of M. sativa seeds. Each of the experimental groups (control group, isolated LTON, and combined LTON and NaCl) was randomly chosen to select six plants. The tissue (whole seedling) was dried at 100 °C and weighed followed by homogenizing it in 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) in a ratio of 2 mg of tissue per 20 mL of buffer. The material was then centrifuged at 8,000 x g and 10 min to get the supernatant to be used in biochemical analysis.

Measurement of Protein Thiols (PSH) and Non-Protein Thiols (NPSH)

The oxidative alterations of the sulfhydryl groups (-SH) in the proteins and peptides in the supernatant were set using a modified methodology31 to identify the presence of sulfhydryl groups in the supernatant. In this way, the levels of protein thiol (PSH) and non-protein thiol (NPSH) were considered. The samples were incubated with Ellman reagent (DTNB) 30 min and the absorbance measured at 405 nm in a microplate reader (TP-Reader, Brazil). The results were expressed in nmol per gram of tissue, using a standard curve of the DTNB-GSH complex. Each of the assays was performed in sextuplicate (n = 6).

Determination of thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS)

The extent of oxidative stress of M. sativa tissues was assessed by quantifying thiobarbituric acid reactive substance (TBARS), which is a methodology founded on32. The protocol involved addition of thiobarbituric acid to phosphoric acid and incubation at 100 °C over a period of 60 min and then one measured the subsidies at 532 nm using microplate reader (TP-Reader, Brazil). The results were translated into malondialdehyde (MDA) in micromoles (Columnar units, µmol)/gram of tissue. The standard curve was prepared with MDA that was obtained by hydrolyzing 1,1,3,3-tetramethoxypropane (TMP). The experiment was conducted using sextuplicate (n = 6).

Determination of free Fe²⁺ content

The modified technique of33 was used to determine the levels of free iron (Fe²⁺). The values that were obtained were used in the form of nanomoles of (Fe²⁺) per gram of tissue. The mixture of saline, Tris-HCl, the sample and 1, 10-phenanthroline were incubated in a 1 h period at room temperature. The absorbance of 492 nm was applied to determine the amount of free (Fe²⁺) in the supernatant, where iron (II) sulfate was used as the standard curve and a microplate reader (TP-Reader, Brazil) was used. The experiment was done in sextuplicate (n = 6).

Evaluation of nitric oxide (NO) levels

The concentration of nitrite as an indirect measure of nitric oxide (NO) was assessed with the Griess reagent (sulfanilamide and N (1-naphthyl) ethylenediamine dihydrochloride in an acidic solution phosphoric acid; Sigma- Aldrich, USA). The reactions were performed at room temperature and in a microdilution plate under low light. 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), supernatant, and Griess reagent were added in triplet in the wells. No quantification was done against a standard curve constructed using various concentrations of sodium nitrite as a standard as identified by34.

In silico experimental treatments

Receptor preparation

The Protein Data Bank (PDB) was used to obtain the protein of interest and its ligand, the Chalcone Synthase (CHS) structure of M. sativa (PDB ID: 1BI5). It was chosen as it has a high crystallographic resolution of 1.56 Å. The PDB files provide the atomic coordinate information, metadata and polymer sequences. The receptor structure, the inhibitor and the water molecules were prepared using Biovia Discovery Studio 2021 CLIENT (https://www.3ds.com/products/biovia/discovery-studio).

Ligand preparation

The 3D structure had been modeled with the help of ACD/ChemSketch v2024 software (https://www.acdlabs.com/resources/free-chemistry-software-apps/chemsketch-freeware/ ) and the 2D model was obtained with ChemSpider (ChemSpider ID: 4444362). This interaction was not first minimised by energy, and it was directly entered into molecular docking analysis in AutoDock VINA v1.2.7 software (https://github.com/ccsb-scripps/AutoDock-Vina/releases), using a rigid proteinflexible ligand strategy. The conformation of the ligand and receptor was then assessed as the most stable one by using Discovery Studio.

Grid calculation and docking

AutoDock VINA v1.2.7 system of software used grid dimensions in X, Y, and Z axis, which was effective within 100 conformations. Moreover, the grid was put at 126 × 126 × 126 Å with a grid space of 0.375 Å. In the case of 1BI5 protein, the X, Y, and Z axis centered the docking box to 24.898, 54.241, and 45.801 Å respectively. The site of interaction was identified in accordance with co-crystallized ligands found in PDB structure. Biovia Discovery Studio 2021 was used to compute the free binding energy with the appropriate components of energy, such as Van der Waals forces, hydrogen bonds, and electrostatic interactions taken into account35.

Statistical analysis

Biomarker values have been given as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM). The statistical analysis was done through one way ANOVA to be followed by post hoc test of Bonferonni. P value of 0.05 was taken to be statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 8.1 (https://www.graphpad.com/) was used to estimate the IC₅₀ by performing linear regression.

Results

Germination analysis

According to the data shown in Fig. 1A, there was a significant decrease in germination of M. sativa seeds under the Control (+) (50 mM NaCl) compared to Control (-) (p < 0.05). While LTON alone did not significantly differ from the Control (-) (p > 0.05), its application at 250 µg/mL and 500 µg/mL combined with NaCl showed a phytoprotective effect by reducing the effects of salinity. The maximum germination rate under saline stress was achieved with 500 µg/mL of LTON (45.0%), which was significantly higher than the Control (+) group (#, p < 0.05). Concerning seed emergence inhibition, as depicted in Fig. 1B, Control (+) (50 mM NaCl) significantly augmented the inhibitory factor of M. sativa seed emergence compared to Control (-) (p < 0.05). Contrarily, the concentration of 500 µg/mL LTON + 50 mM NaCl was the most effective in reducing stress, resulting in only 55.0% inhibition, a value significantly lower than that of the NaCl-only group (#, p < 0.05). Statistical analysis was performed using One-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test, with the symbols * and # indicating significance towards Control (-) and Control (+), respectively. According to the linear interpolation of the data that were obtained on inhibition, LTON showed an estimated the median lethal concentration (LC₂₀) of around 80 µg/mL and LC₅₀ of around 200 µg/mL. Maximal biological effect is achieved at a lower concentration as indicated by an inhibition plateau between 250 and 500 µg/ mL.

Molecular docking analysis

The docking study of LTON with Chalcone Synthase (1BI5) revealed that the protein had a stable interaction with the amino acid residues (Fig. 11A), which contain LYS A:49 and LYS A:78 of the conventional hydrogen bonds (Fig. 11C). Such interactions are mostly between the hydroxyl groups (-OH) of LTON and the amino groups of the protein residues, which serve as significant stabilization forces of the LTON-protein association. The PRO A:81 residue showed π -alkyl interactions, which stabilize the packing of the molecules and prefer the insertion of the LTON into the protein cavity (Fig. 11E). Moreover, another potent acceptor of bonds with aromatic ring is LTON; the π–sulfur interactions with CYS A:84 can be realized (Fig. 11B). Interpolated charge and ionizability maps used in active site balance are pointed out by the interactions of the π -anion with GLU A:85 residues (Fig. 11D and F). Lastly, the Solvent Accessible Surface (SAS) map shows that LTON interacts with a poorly exposed site (Fig. 11G), which is blue and green, which is thermodynamically stable to maintain the ligand less vulnerable to the solvent.

Discussion

The findings point out that there is a reduction in the percentage of germination of M. sativa seeds when subjected to saline pressure to 23.0%. This decrease confirms the inhibitory effects of salt36 on plant growth, and development, and it is in line with the research indicating that NaCl causes osmotic stress, which is affecting the reinitiation of seed imbibition37. Salinity elevated the level of Na⁺ e Cl⁻ in the plant cells, potentially leading to metabolic lesion and nutritional imbalance38. On the other hand, the correlation between LTON and NaCl had good results as it overturned the salinity effects. The maximum concentration (500 µg/mL) was sufficient to counter the action of the salt and the germination rate was the greatest at 45.0%. These results reinforce the proposed ability of flavonoids, including LTON, to reduce saline stress11.

The findings reveal that saline stress caused a great deal of growth inhibition in M. sativa. It has been reported in the literature that plants in poor environment can experience a water shortage and oxidative stress, which is associated with the extent of inhibition experienced in the presence of NaCl37. In addition to that, the associations of LTON with NaCl displayed partial data of inhibitions. The finding suggestsis that LTON may act as an antistress agent in saline stress, since the substance has theoretical potential in regulating pro-oxidant enzymes and it has the added benefit of having low toxicity39,40.

The adverse effect of saline stress in M. sativa was notable within the 10 days. The Control (-) indicated a stronger progression curve than the NaCl group that had the lowest germination rate indicating a slowing of germination speed. This condition coincides with the earlier researches that indicated tremendous reduction in germinative vigor and seedling establishment in germination process by inhibition of water absorption by NaCl41,42. It is also in accord with the analysis of Atta et al.37, who point out that the delay of germination is caused by a reduction in the activity of alpha-amylase. Nevertheless, LTON, alone and in consortium with NaCl displayed germination better than the Control (-) and germination faster than the Control (+). This effect is worthy to note because LTON has the potential capability of cheating transition metals in ROS production and induction of oxidative injury through Fenton reaction43, which may facilitate embryonic metabolism restoration.

The Control (+) had the lowest leaf elongation as far as the rate of growth of the shoot was concerned. This observation shows that stress has the ability to slow down the growth of leaves and ultimately make the total development of the shoot to decrease36. The literature has ample evidence of this inhibition, that is, the accumulation of sodium chloride ions inhibits shoot development37. On the other hand, LTON in the presence of NaCl presented a better data of shoot growth than Control (-); this can be supported by the findings of the potential of LTON when it is used exogenously on crops like maize, where it enhances germination of the crop, and promotes seedlings growth in salty and non-salty media44.

With respect to root growth data, the NaCl group had limitations of the following parameters. Such decrease is verified by the studies which point out that salt results in a precipitated decline in root conductivity and this directly disrupts growth45. On the contrary, isolated and NaCl-associated LTON dosages exhibited significant root length values as compared to the (-) group of Control. Nevertheless, so far there is a paucity of research showing the potential of LTON as a root growth stimulant during saline stress. In this view, the analyses conducted in this paper indicate the capability of LTON to respond positively on root elongation, possibly via modulation of physiological processes, in line with the capacity of some Secondary Metabolites (SMs). As it has been mentioned46, , secondary metabolites play a very important role in making plants resilient. Moreover, the maximum dose (500 µg/mL LTON + NaCl) had the greatest growth of the roots. This action is consistent with the hypothesis that LTON is correlated with salt tolerance and the decrease in cell membrane damages47. This response pattern is consistent with the enhanced root growth observed in rice seedlings under salinity following pretreatment with apigenin, a flavone structurally analogous to LTON. Such effect stems from the ability of flavones to regulate ionic homeostasis, specifically by modulating transporter genes such as NHX1 and SOS1, which mitigate Na⁺ toxicity48.

There was a decrease in the leaf emission in NaCl group relative to Control (-). Such reduction in the number of leaves under salinity can be attributed to prior studies49–51, in which certain forms of saline stress have a considerable influence on the leaf number. Isolated LTON at the two concentrations (250 and 500 µg/mL) and in combination with NaCl at the same concentrations maintained leaf emission. The data are in line with the research suggesting that flavonoids such as LTON are vital metabolites in the salt response and have the potential to influence growth characteristics and leaf number52.

Apparently, the number of roots increased with the 50 mM NaCl group. The current situation can be viewed in the context of Stress-Induced Morphogenetic Response (SIMR), which is the ability of stress to affect root elongation and stimulate the growth of lateral roots as a survival measure53. Nevertheless, LTON treated, isolated or concomitant with NaCl did not exhibit more root numbers than NaCl treated, indicating that LTON does not appear to modulate this particular parameter under the tested conditions.

The water status in Abiotic stress was severely impaired relative to the Control (-). This observation supports the earlier studies that revealed that salt tolerant varieties of rice types had reduced relative water content related to membrane damage54. Surprisingly, a low amount of isolated LTON (250 µg/mL) minimized the RWC, suggesting that an isolated LTON concentration of 250 µg/mL may have a limiting impact on the water-holding capacity. Conversely, when the dose of isolated LTON was increased fivefold (500 µg/mL), it caused increased values of RWC as compared to Control (+) which is supported by the research indicating the capacity of flavonoids to potentially preserve membrane integrity and turgidity54.

Nevertheless, the LTON + NaCl treatments recorded a decrease in the hydration levels and this was contrary to the findings that were realized in isolated 500 µg/mL LTON. This discussion indicates that LTON, when combined with salt, showed limited protective effects on the aspect of water retention. A recent review indicates that nitric oxide modulation is a stimulant to the synthesis of proline to enhance the osmotic adjustment capacity of the plant55. According to our findings, it may be proposed that under these conditions, LTON interactions with NO might be involved in the maintenance of the dehydration state in alfalfa.

The fresh weight (FW) and turgid weight (TW) values were also considerably lowering in the group of 50 mM NaCl. This decline is in line with the studies which indicate a decline in alfalfa biomass in the presence of salinity56. As a result, the treatment of 500 µg/mL LTON + 50 mM NaCl yielded the lowest dry weight (DW). In view of this observation, when contrasted with the maintenance of root growth, it represents a growth-defense tradeoff, as metabolic energy is diverted from storage toward structural escape mechanisms57. NaCl group was observed to have increased electrolyte leakage when compared to Control (-), which highlights its role in membrane damage. This electrolyte loss is predictable and it was thoroughly reported in literature using this phenomenon as a stress-induced assay of plant tissues58–61. This leakage causes efflux of K⁺, an essential component of plant salt tolerance, and leads to ionic homeostasis loss62,63. Nevertheless, one of the most interesting findings is that the results of LTON and NaCl exhibited the maximum electrolyte leakage among all the groups that were studied. According to the literature, abiotic stress induces plants to undergo physiological performance adjustments to ensure survival, involving changes in membrane permeability64. In this sense, though the issue of electrolyte leakage is usually linked to the measurement of stress tolerance and plasma membrane damage58, our discussion proposes that the LTON + NaCl integration might also indicate active metabolic changes. Rather than representing irreversible damage, this transient increase in ion efflux, particularly the loss of active K⁺ is suggested to permit “energy-releasing” catabolic processes65. Based on performance principles, such leakage may reflect a physiological adjustment response to extreme abiotic stress64. The growth of both the protein and non-protein thiols in the Control (+) supports the protection against oxidative stress and is consistent with the literature in other plants, including aquatic macrophyte Ceratophyllum demersum66. LTON was associated with a reduction in PSH and NPSHs when combined with NaCl and compared to Control (+), suggesting that LTON may contribute to reinstating redox homeostasis and potentially attenuating reactive species. Although NaCl treatment caused the increase in the NPSH content, likely with the addition of GSH to balance the stress67, the use of the flavone appears to modulate this biochemical requirement. Unlike rutin-a common flavonoid25- which can also protect leaves by serving as an antioxidant to thiols in proteins, luteolin presents a profile that may suggest a possible mechanism of S-nitrosation through the action of NO55. The Control (+) showed increased lipid peroxidation, which is an indicator of stress indices in M. sativa. This finding is consistent with the findings of the earlier researches by56 who treated alfalfa with saline stress and observed that MDA levels increased significantly by 75.57%. The combined LTON/ saline stress treatments recorded a statistically significant decrease in MDA, implying a protective role of LTON on salinity. This ability is supported by studies mentioned by68 that established that a LTON-laden extract of parsley (Petroselinum crispum) exhibited the potential for lowering lipid peroxidation, acting as an effective protective agent. The ability of LTON to mitigate oxidative damage in M. sativa corroborates the findings of69, who demonstrated that analogous flavonoids, such as quercetin, optimize the glyoxalase system and ionic homeostasis in tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum) subjected to salinity. Such similarity reinforces the functionality of these polyphenols in stabilizing cellular membranes and reducing lipid peroxidation, which are essential mechanisms for maintaining physiological integrity under salt stress.

Isolated NaCl stimulated the free iron accumulation, potentially leading to oxidative damage to the M. sativa culture. The relevance of this situation is demonstrated by the high rise in the number of Fe²⁺ in roots and shoots of Arabidopsis treated with NaCl where studies by70 have highlighted the same. Such unfavorable conditions can result in binding of Fe²⁺ to the cellular components, which may interfere with the cellular functioning and induce stress in the plant71. Moreover, in the groups where LTON + NaCl was used, the level of free iron was considerably lower than in the Control (+); this finding suggests a potential indicates the preventive value of LTON in the mitigation of Fenton reaction25.

M. sativa seedling tissues had a higher nitrite level of Control (+). This finding supports observations37,72, which point out that NaCl induces ion accumulation, which is potentially capable of initiating enzymatic pathways of NO synthesis, like nitrate reductases. This reaction is often described as an effort by a plant in mediating saline stress tolerance. On the other hand, the interaction of LTON + NaCl with the highest concentration used had a significant impact in decreasing the levels of NO to the lowest levels than the Control (-) and Control (+) and the 250 µg/mL LTON + NaCl concentration.

Such results suggest that despite any researches regarding the antioxidant capacity of LTON that are conducted in cellular models73,74, there is limited evidence on the application of LTON with saline stress in the production of NO. It was proposed in study37 that NO may act as an antioxidant action realized by activating vital enzymes involved in the action. Hence, the findings of this article point toward a potential protective effect of LTON on the redox status of the seedlings; as suggested by56, the NO could be modulated, and thus, the compound may be effective through the recovery of the balance of the plant.

The molecular docking simulations between LTON and the M. sativa chalcone synthase (CHS) protein (1BI5) demonstrated stable interactions with key amino acid residues. The hydroxyl groups of LTON formed hydrogen bonds with the nitrogen of LYS A:49 and LYS A:78. These specific findings correlate with previous research identifying lysine residues as essential components for the active site stability75. Furthermore, such interactions support the role of CHS in condensation mechanisms and the flux of secondary metabolites under both normal and deleterious environments74. Correspondingly, the interactions between LTON and the PRO A:81 demonstrated significant thermodynamic stability π-alkyl interactions, suggesting that this residue may facilitate ligand fitting76. Furthermore, the communication with the CYS A:84 residue indicates a high affinity for the active site, corroborating research that LTON is capable of binding to cysteine amino acids of regulatory enzymes, potentially modulating the generation of ROS77.

These CHS active sites cysteine residues are strategic targets for inhibitors irreversible inhibition of the modulation biosynthetic pathway78. Since LTON interacts with CYS A:84 and is stabilized by PRO A:81, it is proposed that the flavonoid influences the redox microenvironment of the enzyme, thereby regulating CHS activity and the metabolic pathway74. Regarding TYR A:86 residue, the observed π-hydrogen or π-π interactions promote the stabilization of the ligand in the molecule and its activity. This interaction is pointed out as being central towards flavonoid affinity79. These findings confirm that LTON adapts to the catalytic site through aromatic geometries with face-to-edge contact, forming stable π-π stacking and π-anionic interactions75,80,81. Moreover, surface analyses indicate a high degree of affinity driven by LTON presents a high electrostatic charge distribution between the negative parts of LTON and the positive residues of the active site. Hydrophobic interactions and hydrophobic bridges involving LTON hydroxyl groups act as the primary stabilization mechanism81,82. Specifically, SAS analysis showed an accommodation of LTON in less accessible, protected areas while keeping polar groups exposed to the solvent. This positioning suggests that deep hydrophobic interactions and peripheral hydrogen bonds modulate the enzyme towards a less active state81,82.

Finally, the coupling of LTON to CHS is proposed as a chemical indicator to terminate or slow the rate of the biosynthetic pathway when adequate amounts of the product already exist19,24. This mechanism aligns with the control mechanism of M. sativa, where CHS isoforms serve as the central nucleophile for proper substrate positioning and enzymatic control19.

There is a number of studies indicating that CHS is inhibited non-competitively by flavonoid pathway products, such as naringenin and naringenin chalcone. Other flavonoids, including luteolin and apigenin, have also shown potential inhibitory ability in some species. It is suggested that flavonoids build up until they inhibit CHS, potentially preventing such metabolites from reaching levels that could be detrimental to the plant itself83. Moreover, the fact that nuclear localization signals (NLS) have been detected in CHS reveals the possibility of a translocation to the cell nucleus. This type of dynamics implies the direct mediation of chemical safety responses in the genomic compartment with the protein playing the lead role19. Thus, this binding may serve as an indicator that the plant maintains a metabolic equilibrium, mitigating the impact of exogenous forces and the regulation of its own defense products.

Conclusion

This experiment suggests that luteolin (LTON) can be used when relieving saline load the 50 mM NaCl particularly at 500 µg/ml of the mixture. Our results demonstrate that LTON treatment significantly improved germination percentages and root length, which are critical for seedling establishment in saline soils. Biochemical analyses elucidated the protective mechanism of LTON, which dramatically reduced lipid peroxidation (TBARS), free iron accumulation, and nitric oxide (NO) levels, effectively mitigating oxidative and nitrosative damage. Although a reduction in dry weight was observed at the highest dose, the overall enhancement in hydration (RWC) and membrane stability (reduced electrolyte leakage) suggests a prioritized resource allocation for survival and osmotic adjustment. Besides, the molecular docking tests demonstrate that plants may also survive in salinity as the compound was proved to have high affinity with Chalcone Synthase (CHS) binding itself to the active site and establishment of the required molecular interactions. Furthermore, this molecular docking revealed a high affinity between LTON and CHS, suggesting a role in modulating the phenylpropanoid pathway to bolster plant defense. In conclusion, these findings reveal the high antioxidant nature of luteolin and its potential as a biostimulant to improve M. sativa resilience against unfavorable conditions. These results imply that LTON could be developed into a practical, bio-based technology for enhancing crop productivity in salt-affected agricultural areas, though future field studies should optimize dosage to balance biomass accumulation and stress tolerance. The validity of these molecular mechanisms must be tested in future studies that will in turn aid to prove LTON as a practical biological technology in the modern-day agricultural practice in saline-infested soils.
