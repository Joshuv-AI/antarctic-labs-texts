Epidemiology of osteoarthritis overall and by body mass index category in the United States: A retrospective analysis of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey
Authors: Tammy Forrester, Juliana Meyers, Abby Hitchens, Magaly Perez-Nieves
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a known risk factor for both the incidence and progression of osteoarthritis (OA). This retrospective analysis describes the prevalence of OA and demographic, clinical, and prescription characteristics by body mass index (BMI) category.MethodsThis was a retrospective, cross-sectional, observational, descriptive epidemiologic study based on the secondary analysis of the 2015–2016 and 2017–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) databases. Adults ≥ 20 years with self-reported OA were evaluated. Measures were reported overall and by BMI category (i.e., underweight, normal weight, overweight, class I, II, and III obesity) and included OA prevalence, demographics, comorbidities, weight-related self-reports, time since arthritis onset, and prescription arthritis medications used in the prior 30 days. Analyses were descriptive and weighted to be representative of the United States (US) population.ResultsIn total, 31,936,699 adults (aged ≥ 20 years) in the US had OA, with a prevalence (per 1000 persons) of 135.0 (95% confidence interval, 122.9–147.0). Prevalence was highest in adults aged ≥ 65 years (315.7) and increased by BMI category from 72.4 in underweight individuals to 191.4 in class III obesity. Approximately 30.2% of people with OA had overweight, and 51.8% had obesity (i.e., 25.1%, 14.9%, and 11.8% in obesity classes I, II, and III, respectively). At the survey date, people had been living with OA for a mean (standard error) of 13.5 (0.7) years. Common self-reported comorbidities included hypertension (58.4%), cancer/malignancies (25.4%), and thyroid problems (24.5%). Among people with OA, 57.8% reported that a doctor told them they had overweight, and 39.2% reported they were currently controlling or losing weight. The most commonly prescribed OA-related medications were nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and opioids (used in 16.2% and 13.9% of the population, respectively.ConclusionThe prevalence of OA in people with obesity class III was more than twice the prevalence in people with normal weight.Keywords: Epidemiology, Obesity, Osteoarthritis, Overweight

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a progressive degenerative joint disease typically seen in individuals over age 50 [1]. It is estimated that approximately 32.5 million adults in the United States (US) are living with OA [2]. The prevalence of OA has been found to vary by affected site, with the knee (60.6% of cases and an estimated prevalence in 2019 of 364.6 cases per 100,000 persons), hand (23.7% of cases, 142.5 cases per 100,000 persons), other sites (10.2% of cases, 61.4 cases per 100,000 persons), and hip (5.5% of cases, 32.9 cases per 100,000 persons) the most common sites [3]. Previous studies have found that people with OA experience greater pain, fatigue, levels of disability, and activity limitations than people of a comparable age [4]. Furthermore, the overall economic burden associated with OA in the US is estimated to be more than $136 billion annually [2,5]. Correspondingly, OA has been estimated to account for 4.3% of all hospital costs and more than 1 million knee and hip replacements performed each year in the US [6].

The link between OA and obesity, particularly OA of the knee, is well recognized, with obesity being a known risk factor for both the incidence and progression of OA [7 8]. Weight loss is an established mechanism for improving the symptoms of OA; therefore, the American College of Rheumatology and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines recommend weight loss for people with OA of the knee having overweight or obesity [9,10]. With continuing development of new treatments for people with overweight or obesity, it is of interest to understand the prevalence of people with overweight or obesity and OA.

The primary objectives of this project were to estimate the prevalence of OA (regardless of site) in adults in the US and to estimate the prevalence of OA in adults in the US overall and stratified by body mass index (BMI) category (i.e., underweight, normal weight, overweight, obesity class I, obesity class II, obesity class III) and by payer type. Additionally, this study also describes demographics, clinical characteristics, obesity and weight loss characteristics, and treatments for OA, overall and by BMI category. Specifically, in light of the advent of new, highly effective obesity management medications and their potential application for patients with OA, information is needed on the overall patient population and the implication that these therapies could have for patients and the health care system.

Methods

This was a retrospective, cross-sectional, observational, descriptive epidemiologic study based on the secondary analysis of the 2015–2016 and 2017–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) databases [6]. The 2017–2018 survey represented the most recent available completed survey at the time the analysis was conducted (note: the 2019–2020 survey was disrupted due to the coronavirus disease pandemic). The NHANES database is a series of health examination surveys conducted annually by the US National Center for Health Statistics to assess the health and nutritional status of the US population. The survey combines in-home interviews and physical examinations. The interview portion consists of demographic, socioeconomic, dietary, and health-related questions. The examination component consists of medical, dental, and physiological measurements, as well as laboratory tests. The RTI International Institutional Review Board determined that this study was not research with human subjects.

The NHANES data are weighted to produce estimates of the entire resident noninstitutionalized US population. Specifically, each participant in the database is assigned a sampling weight that equals the number of people in the population represented by that participant. The sampling weight assigned to each participant considers the unequal probabilities of selection given that some demographic groups are oversampled. Adjustments are made for nonresponse answers in the survey. Poststratification adjustments are made to match estimates of the US civilian noninstitutionalized population available from the US Census Bureau. As this study combined the 2015–2016 and 2017–2018 NHANES databases, a 4-year–sample weight was calculated from the 2-year–sample weights, and the sum of the combined weights was reasonably close to an independent estimate of the US civilian noninstitutionalized population at the midpoint of the combined interval.

Using the medical conditions questionnaire in NHANES, individuals with OA were identified. Any respondent with an answer of “yes” to the question “Doctor ever said you had arthritis?” and who also answered the question “Which type of arthritis was it?” with a response of “Osteoarthritis or degenerative arthritis” was selected for inclusion in the study. The study sample was restricted to adults aged ≥ 20 years at the time of the survey and examination (note: 20 years was selected as the minimum age as all questions in the NHANES related to arthritis are asked only among participants at least 20 years of age). All results were reported overall and by BMI category, including underweight (i.e., BMI < 18.5), normal weight (i.e., BMI 18.5 to < 25 or 18.5 to < 23 for patients who are Asian), overweight (i.e., BMI 25 to < 30 or 23 to < 25 for patients who are Asian), obesity class I (i.e., BMI 30 to < 35 or 25 to < 27.5 for patients who are Asian), obesity class II (i.e., BMI 35 to < 40 or 27.5 to < 40 for patients who are Asian), and obesity class III (i.e., BMI ≥ 40) [11].

Study measures included the distribution of people by BMI category, the prevalence of OA, and demographic characteristics (i.e., age, sex, race/ethnicity, insurance payer type, income level, and education level). Clinical characteristics including the time from age of arthritis onset to current age (note: this question was asked for arthritis in general and was not specific to OA); the presence of comorbidities; and responses to questions regarding weight, exercise, and nutrition were reported. Additionally, usage of prescription OA-related medications in the 30 days before the interview was also reported.

The prevalence of OA per 1000 persons was calculated as the weighted number of adults with OA divided by the weighted number of adults in the US population, based on values in the NHANES database. The prevalence of OA per 1000 persons was reported overall and stratified by age group, payer type, and BMI category. Demographics, clinical characteristics, and prescription OA-related medication utilization were reported descriptively.

Results

In total, 31,936,699 (weighted; 1327 unweighted) people with OA were identified (of 318,661,882 individuals [weighted; 19,225 unweighted] in the NHANES; Fig. 1).

The majority of people with OA in the study population had overweight (30.2%) or obesity (51.8% total; 25.1%, 14.9%, and 11.8% were in obesity class I, obesity class II, and obesity class III, respectively; Fig. 2).

The estimated prevalence of OA in the US population was 135.0 per 1000 persons (95% confidence interval, 122.9–147.0; Table 1). Estimated prevalence increased with BMI category (i.e., lowest for persons in the underweight category [72.4 per 1000 persons] and highest for those in obesity class III [191.4 per 1000 persons]). The estimated prevalence of OA was highest in adults aged ≥ 65 years (315.7 per 1000 persons) and lowest in adults aged < 41 years (24.0 per 1000 persons). When considering payer type, the estimated prevalence of OA was highest among adults with Medigap insurance (387.4 per 1000 persons) and lowest among adults with Medicaid (122.7 per 1000 persons).

Overall, for people with OA, the mean (standard error [SE]) age was 62.38 (0.48) years (range, 54.52 [6.3] years in the underweight category to 63.78 [0.93] for those in obesity class II), and 76.2% of the population with OA was aged ≥ 55 years (Table 2). Approximately 90% of the population with OA obtained at least high school diploma or GED. One-third of the population with OA attended some college or obtained an associate degree, and 32.6% were college graduates and above. Among the 90% of people who provided a specific household income category, the median income category was $55,000 to $64,999.

The mean (SE) time from age of arthritis onset to the current age was 13.5 (0.7) years overall (range, 12.06 [5.65] underweight to 15.27 [1.78] obesity class III) (Table 3). Overall, approximately 73% of people had at least 1 comorbidity, and the comorbidities most commonly self-reported were hypertension (58.4%), cancer/malignancy (25.4%), thyroid problems (24.5%), diabetes (19.6%), asthma (18.7%), gallstones (12.3%), chronic bronchitis (12.2%), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (10.0%). Aside from hypertension (which was the most common comorbidity observed for all BMI categories), the distribution of the types of comorbidities observed varied by BMI category. The most commonly reported comorbidity (after hypertension) for the underweight category was angina/angina pectoris (25.1%); normal weight, thyroid problem (26.9%); overweight, cancer/malignancy (28.6%); obesity class I, cancer/malignancy (25.7%); obesity class II, diabetes (31.3%); and obesity class III, diabetes (35.2%). Furthermore, 42.2% of people with obesity class III had 3 or more comorbidities (versus 18.3% of people in the normal weight category).

Among individuals with overweight or obesity, more than two-thirds self-reported that a doctor had said they had overweight (range, 39.6% for people in the overweight category to 98.2% in obesity class III). Additionally, among individuals with overweight or obesity, 42.1% self-reported that they were controlling or losing weight (range, 39.9% for people in the overweight category to 43.5% in obesity class III), reducing fat in their diet (range, 34.4% in the overweight category to 44.8 in obesity class III), increasing exercise (range, 34.7% in the overweight category to 37.2% in obesity class I), and/or that their doctor had told them to exercise (range, 20.4% in the overweight category to 45.9% in obesity class III).

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) (16.2%) and opioids (13.9%) were the most commonly used self-reported prescription treatment types overall (Table 4). NSAIDs were the most common prescription treatment type received among all BMI categories, except for overweight and obesity class II, where opioids were more common. Discussion

This study identified approximately 32 million people with OA in the US (representing 13.6% of the adult US population), which is similar to other published estimates [2]. The prevalence of OA was 135.0 per 1000 persons and increased with increasing age and BMI. Similar to our present study, several other recent studies have also used the NHANES database to evaluate the prevalence of OA in the United States. These previous studies have estimated the prevalence of OA in the United States to be between 11.0% and 14.28%, depending on the years of data evaluated [[12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. However, none of these studies evaluated the relationship between prevalence of OA and BMI category. This is important as overweight and obesity are recognized risk factors for OA, particularly OA of the knee, which represents the most common site of OA [3,7,8]. This study observed that most people with OA in the study population had overweight or obesity (i.e., 82.0%), which is consistent with other recent published studies (e.g., Schapiro et al. [18] reported 91% of people with OA in their study had overweight and obesity). Further, the present analysis also found that more than 70% of people with OA had a BMI ≥ 27 and nearly three-quarters (i.e., 72.9%) of patients with OA had at least 1 additional comorbid condition.

Although the NHANES data did not include any information on sites of OA, previous studies have estimated that OA of the knee may be present in up to 69% of people with OA [19]. Data in this study may therefore be extrapolated to estimate the prevalence of OA of the knee as 93.1 per 1000 persons (from the overall prevalence estimate of OA of 135.0 per 1000 person-years). Additionally, as the prevalence of OA in this study was observed to increase with increasing BMI (range, 72.4 per 1000 people with underweight to 191.4 per 1000 people with obesity class III) and the relationship between OA of the knee and obesity is well recognized, the prevalence of OA of the knee could be estimated to be at least 132.1 per 1000 people with obesity class III. However, additional research would be needed to confirm these findings.

Demographics of the study population presented in this analysis are similar to those reported previously. Mohammed et al. [20] compared risk factors for OA and rheumatoid arthritis using NHANES data from 1999 to 2016. They found that approximately 64% of people with OA were female (vs. 66% observed in our study), three-quarters of people with OA in their study were White (vs. 78% in our analysis), and over three-quarters of people with OA had either overweight (32% in their analysis vs. 30.2% in the present study) or obesity (44.3% in their analysis vs. 51.8% in the present study).

Our study observed fairly low rates of prescription pharmaceutical treatment among people with OA. Current American College of Rheumatology guidelines recommend treatment with oral NSAIDs, acetaminophen, duloxetine, and tramadol as oral therapy for the treatment of OA [9]. However, we observed that only 8.7%, 16.2%, 3.7%, and 4.9% of people in our study reported a prescription for nonopioid analgesics, NSAIDs, duloxetine, and tramadol, respectively, in the 30 days before the survey. Also, in the 30 days before the survey, nearly 6% and 4% of people in our study reported taking either hydrocodone or oxycodone, respectively; 2.3% reported taking corticosteroids; and 2.0% reported taking muscle relaxers, none of which are recommended in current treatment guidelines. Of note, the percentages of people reporting receipt of nonopioid analgesics was highest among patients in obesity class III (i.e., 12.8%) and obesity class II (i.e., 13.2%), as were the percentages of people reporting receipt of opioids (17.1% for obesity class III and 18.3% for obesity class II).

Weight loss is an established mechanism for improving the symptoms of OA; therefore, the American College of Rheumatology and National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines recommend weight loss for people with OA of the knee having overweight or obesity [9,10]. Currently, health insurance coverage of obesity management medications in the US is often limited to patients having another condition with an FDA-approved indication in addition to obesity, such as type 2 diabetes, sleep apnea, established cardiovascular disease, or MASH [[21], [22], [23]]. Currently coverage for other conditions such as OA is not mandated under any current federal or state coverage requirements. Recent clinical trials for incretins have shown promising results in reduction of both body weight and OA knee pain [[24], [25], [26]]. Expanded coverage of obesity management medications could be possible if there were an FDA-approved indication for patients with obesity and OA.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. People were identified for inclusion based on their responses to survey questions. People may have incorrectly answered questions on whether they have been diagnosed with arthritis and OA, and it was not possible to confirm patient responses with medical records. This may have resulted in a misestimation of the prevalence of OA in the US. Although questions regarding the diagnosis of OA were included in NHANES, no further questions on sites of OA (e.g., knee, hip, wrist) or pain related to OA were asked, so no additional information on OA sites or severity was available. Questions regarding clinical characteristics were subject to patient responses to the best of their recollection (i.e., age of arthritis onset, self-reported comorbidities, and responses to questions on doctor diagnoses of overweight, weight loss, exercise). Receipt of medications included only those prescriptions the patient had taken within the most recent 30-day period, and no information was available on medications received previously. Therefore, it was not possible to evaluate longitudinal treatment patterns. Additionally, no information was available on topical medications or injections that may have been received in a doctor's office, and no information was available regarding over-the-counter medications. Therefore, rates of treatment may be underestimated in this analysis.

Conclusion

This study's findings are consistent with previously published literature identifying obesity as a risk factor for OA. In the present analysis of adults in the US, the prevalence of OA increased with higher age and BMI categories, with the prevalence in individuals in obesity class III more than double that observed among individuals with normal weight. In addition, in people with OA, the comorbidity burden was higher among individuals with higher BMI categories. The frequency of conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and multiple comorbidities increased with increasing BMI category. Given that weight loss is an effective strategy for relieving OA symptoms, weight loss medications may confer additional benefits in this population by alleviating symptoms and potentially reducing the incidence and thereby overall prevalence of OA. Furthermore, in light of the advent of new, highly effective obesity management medications, expanding access to these medications for patients with OA could have significant impacts on both patient outcomes and the healthcare system.
