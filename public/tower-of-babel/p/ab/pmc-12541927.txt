Objective

Leuconostoc mesenteroides is a Gram-positive, facultatively non-sporogenous bacterium [1]. This lactic acid bacterium is commonly found in fermented foods such as kimchi and is classified as Generally Regarded as Safe (GRAS) due to its proven safety. In South Korea, this species is widely utilized as a starter culture for kimchi fermentation [2, 3]. With increasing interest in the probiotic potential and efficacy of this species, recent studies have evaluated its ability to inhibit pathogenic bacteria, as well as the presence of antibiotic resistance genes and bacteriocins from a genomic perspective [1]. Recently, whole genome sequencing combined with in silico analysis has been employed to assess the probiotic potential of specific bacterial strains from a genomic standpoint [4, 5]. We have demonstrated that L. mesenteroides B22051, isolated from young ginseng, exhibits efficacy against pancreatic cancer [6]. The objective of this study is to report and analyze its complete genome and to confirm its probiotic potential through genome-based analysis.

Data description

Leuconostoc mesenteroides B22051 was isolated from Panax ginseng Meyer and cultured in de Man-Rogosa-Sharpe (MRS) medium at 37 °C for 24 h. Due to its effectiveness against pancreatic cancer demonstrated in our previous study [6], the strain was deposited in the Korean Collection for Type Cultures (KCTC); however, its genome has not been analyzed. For genome sequencing, genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted using the Wizard Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted gDNA was sequenced at Macrogen (Macrogen, Seoul, South Korea) using the Illumina HiSeq platform for paired-end sequencing (151 bp × 2) and the PacBio Sequel system (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) [7]. For de novo draft genome assembly, the PacBio SMRT analysis pipeline 8.0 with default options (http://www.pacb.com/devnet/) was used. Assembly error correction of the draft genome was performed using Pilon (v. 1.21) [8]. Gene prediction for the chromosome and plasmids was conducted using Prokka (v. 1.13) [9]. Gene Ontology IDs (GOIDs) were assigned to coding sequences (CDSs) based on functional annotation using the Gene Ontology database [10]. Gene ontology was applied to assess the presence of reductase and oxidase genes. The completeness of the genome assembly was assessed using BUSCO (v. 5.13) [11]. Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COG) analysis based on protein-coding sequences (CDSs) was performed using EggNOG (v. 5.0) [12]. Identification of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes was conducted using the CARD RGI (Comprehensive Antibiotic Research Database Resistance Gene Identifier) tool (https://card.mcmaster.ca/analyze/rgi) [13]. The presence of bacteriocins and RiPPs (Ribosomally Synthesized and Post-Translationally Modified Peptides) was determined using the BAGEL4 tool (http://bagel4.molgenrug.nl/) [13]. Comparative genomic analysis was performed to investigate the phylogenetic relationship between L. mesenteroides B22051 and related L. mesenteroides strains. Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) was computed using FastANI [14], and core gene alignments were generated with Roary [15]. Subsequently, hierarchical clustering and an ANI-based Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree were produced in R (version 4.5.0) [16]. Additionally, a core genome NJ phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA11 [17], with Leuconostoc pseudomesenteroides ATCC 12291 serving as an outgroup.

The complete genome of L. mesenteroides B22051 is 1,994,797 bp in size, with a guanine + cytosine (G + C) content of 37.7%. It consists of a single chromosome (1,943,350 bp) and two plasmids (37,364 bp and 14,083 bp), exhibiting similar genomic characteristics to other L. mesenteroides strains [18]. Genome annotation identified 71 transfer RNAs, 24 ribosomal RNAs, and 1,987 coding sequences (CDSs) (Table 1, Data file 2 [20]). Among the 1,987 CDSs, 98.09% were assigned to the Cluster of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification system, and 64.92% (1,290 genes) were assigned to the Gene Ontology classification system (Table 1, Data file 3 [21]). We prepared two main resources for this study. Data files 1–5 summarize different analyses and results, including genomic features, chromosomal organization, COG categorization, and phylogenetic and comparative genomic analyses among L. mesenteroides strains. Data set 1 specifically contains the complete genome sequence of L. mesenteroides B22051. A summary of these data sets is provided in Table 1 (Table 1 should be placed here). Among the genes with GO IDs, 26 contain the term reductase in their GO annotations, while 1 contains oxidase. Ambiguous terms such as redox or oxidoreductase were excluded from this count. Prediction of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes revealed that among 322,710 unique antimicrobial resistance gene (ARG) allele sequences [25], two partial sequences of vanT (32.61% identity) and vanY (34.36% identity) were detected, and although in vitro assays confirmed vancomycin resistance, the low sequence identity and absence of a complete van operon indicate that this resistance is intrinsic rather than acquired. This intrinsic vancomycin resistance has also been reported in other L. mesenteroides strains as well as in other Leuconostoc species [26]. Using BAGEL4, we analyzed nearly 500 RiPPs (including Bacteriocin Class IC), 230 Bacteriocin Class IIC, and 90 large (> 10 kDa) Bacteriocin Class IIIC [27]. The analysis confirmed the presence of the Entero_X_chain_beta gene, associated with Enterocin in Bacteriocin Class IIC (chromosomally encoded), in L. mesenteroides B22051. The same gene has been previously identified in other L. mesenteroides strains [18]. The ANI values among L. mesenteroides strains were all above 98.5%, confirming that they belong to the same species. L. mesenteroides B22051 showed the highest similarity (99.70%) with L. mesenteroides DRC1506. In contrast, L. pseudomesenteroides ATCC 12291 had an ANI value of approximately 80%, indicating that it is a distinct species. In the phylogenetic analysis based on the core genome (Table 1; Data file 5 [23]), L. mesenteroides B22051 clustered with L. mesenteroides DRC1506 but was positioned on a separate branch with a bootstrap support value of 92%, which is consistent with similar findings reported in previous studies [18].

These results suggest that although L. mesenteroides B22051 exhibits high genomic similarity to other L. mesenteroides strains, it harbors distinct functional traits, including bacteriocin-related and antioxidant-related genes. Based on its genomic characteristics and prior studies on related strains, L. mesenteroides B22051 may serve as a promising probiotic candidate for applications in both human health and animal husbandry, although further functional studies are necessary to confirm its efficacy (Figs 1, 2 and 3).

Limitations

This study provides insights into the genomic characteristics and probiotic potential of L. mesenteroides B22051, though several limitations exist. The predictions regarding bacteriocins and reductases are based solely on in silico analyses and remain hypothetical without experimental validation. Additionally, using a single strain limits the assessment of intraspecies diversity. Future in vitro and in vivo studies are needed to confirm these genomic predictions and validate its probiotic functionality.
