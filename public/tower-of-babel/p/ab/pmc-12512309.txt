ADSC-enriched adipose extract alleviates cartilage fibrosis in temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis by inhibiting chondrocyte senescence
Authors: Zerou Zhang, Dan Jin, Bingshuai Jing, Shanluo Zhou, Yi Sun, Jeroen Van Dessel, Rui Ren, Fuwei Liu, Mian Zhang, Yunpeng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) is a degenerative joint disease causing chronic pain and restricted mandibular movement. Its complex pathology and lack of effective therapies make it a clinical challenge. Recent studies have demonstrated that cartilage fibrosis correlates closely with TMJOA progression. Inflammatory microenvironments induce chondrocyte senescence, altering secretory phenotypes and driving fibrocartilage formation, characterized by hardened texture and poor mechanical properties, compromising joint biomechanics. Targeting cartilage fibrosis represents a key therapeutic strategy. Stem cell therapies show antifibrotic potential but face clinical limitations due to complex preparation and safety risks.MethodsSynovial lavage fluid from TMJOA patients was collected and analyzed for fibrosis marker ACTA2 and inflammatory factor IL-1β expression to confirm intra-articular fibrosis and explore contributing factors. Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (ADSC)-enriched adipose extract (ARDE) was prepared using mechanical emulsification with low-speed centrifugation. Its efficacy was assessed in a prospective clinical trial evaluating pain, mouth opening, and radiographic changes. ARDE’s modulation of inflammation and repair of fibrocartilage were histologically assessed in a rabbit TMJOA model. In vitro TMJOA chondrocyte models induced by inflammatory factors (IL-1β, TNF-α) were co-cultured with ARDE’s core component, ADSCs, to observe their impact on pathological chondrocytes. Transcriptome sequencing further explored their repair mechanisms.ResultsTMJOA patient synovial lavage fluid showed elevated fibrotic markers correlating significantly with disease stage and inflammation levels. Prospective clinical trial follow-up demonstrated that ARDE injection substantially improved pain, mouth opening limitation, and quality of life, with reduced joint effusion radiographically evident in some patients. In vivo, intra-articular ARDE promoted histological cartilage repair and extracellular matrix redeposition while downregulating inflammatory factors and fibrotic markers in articular cartilage. Mechanistically, inflammatory cytokine-stimulated chondrocyte models exhibited upregulated fibrotic and senescence-associated pathways; ADSC co-culture suppressed fibrotic marker expression and attenuated cellular senescence.ConclusionARDE effectively alleviates cartilage fibrosis by suppressing chondrocyte senescence, significantly restoring cartilage structure and alleviating disease symptoms. The preparation process meets clinical requirements, and its demonstrated safety and efficacy indicate promising clinical translation prospects.Trial registrationHuman studies were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University (Approval No: IRB-YJ-2022033) and were registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No: ChiCTR2300069677, registered 23 March 2023, https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=184860).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07108-8.Keywords: Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, Cartilage, Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells, Fibrosis, Cellular senescence

Introduction

Temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis (TMJOA) is a degenerative disease of the temporomandibular joint that is often caused by various factors, including trauma, inflammation, malocclusion, and psychological stress [1, 2]. Patients primarily experience pain, joint clicking, and progressive restriction of mouth opening, which severely impact their quality of life. The pathological characteristics of this disease are cartilage matrix degradation, subchondral bone remodeling, and chronic synovial inflammation [3–5]. Recent studies on large weight-bearing joints also suggest potential associations with abnormal deposition of type I/III collagen and chondrocyte fibrosis. There are no standardized therapeutic guidelines for TMJOA, and the currently employed treatment strategies are mainly used to provide symptomatic relief. Conservative therapies such as occlusal adjustment, splint therapy or intra-articular injection of hyaluronic acid (HA) alleviate symptoms by reducing the intra-articular pressure [6, 7]. For advanced TMJOA patients with irreducible disc displacement, perforation, or even condylar bone destruction, surgical interventions, including disc repositioning, cartilage repair, or joint replacement, are often needed [8, 9]. Traditional TMJOA treatments exhibit limitations such as limited efficacy, tissue damage, and poor regeneration of damaged structures [10–12]. Therefore, developing novel regenerative medicine strategies to promote joint self-repair effectively has become an urgent research priority.

With advancements in stem cell therapy research, its potential for osteoarthritis (OA) treatment has garnered widespread attention. Numerous preclinical studies have demonstrated that mesenchymal stem cells derived from various tissues induce protective, anti-inflammatory, and cartilage-regenerating effects in OA models [13–15]. The antifibrotic effects of stem cells have also been comprehensively validated, suggesting their potential value in reversing pathological fibrotic remodeling of cartilage [16]. However, the clinical translation of stem cell therapies still faces multiple obstacles, including dependence on specialized laboratory equipment and rigorous quality control systems, as well as uncontrollable risks during in vitro expansion, such as phenotypic alterations, proliferative dysregulation, and tumorigenic potential [17, 18]. Therefore, widespread clinical implementation of stem cell therapy requires overcoming not only technical barriers but also ethical, legal, and societal challenges. To circumvent these challenges, research has progressively shifted toward the use of stem cell derivatives as therapeutic alternatives to mitigate these risks [19].

In this study, an adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cell (ADSC)-enriched adipose extract (ARDE) was innovatively produced and used for TMJOA treatment, and its therapeutic effects on the pathological characteristics of TMJOA and its mechanisms were systematically analyzed. ARDE is prepared from natural adipose tissue through mechanical emulsification followed by low-speed centrifugation. It consists of liquid adipose along with the stromal vascular fraction (SVF), which is enriched with ADSCs. As a widely accessible type of adult stem cell, ADSCs have been extensively validated for their therapeutic potential in osteoarthritis treatment [20]. Furthermore, lipids within the liquid adipose exhibit excellent lubricating properties. They are not only used in the artificial tears and for lubricating medical implants [21, 22], but have also been shown to significantly enhance the lubrication of articular cartilage surfaces under osteoarthritic conditions, effectively reducing friction and thereby exerting an important protective effect on cartilage [23]. First, analysis of articular lavage fluid from TMJOA patients at different disease stages revealed that progressively elevated fibrotic marker expression correlated with disease progression, with a significant positive correlation with intra-articular inflammatory levels, suggesting that fibrotic processes are novel therapeutic targets. Subsequently, clinical studies were conducted to observe the therapeutic effects of ARDE injection on patients with TMJOA. Follow-up data revealed significant reductions in both disease symptoms and radiographic manifestations among patients, transforming those originally categorized as “requiring surgical intervention” into individuals achieving marked symptom relief through minimally invasive therapy. At the mechanistic level, treatment of TMJOA model animals confirmed that ARDE effectively mitigated pathological remodeling of the joint, reduced inflammatory factor levels, and alleviated condylar cartilage fibrosis. In vitro studies revealed that ADSCs exert antifibrotic effects by inhibiting inflammation-induced chondrocyte senescence processes. These results reveal the relationship between chondrocyte senescence and fibrosis in TMJOA and the potential mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of ARDE, potentially providing a new treatment strategy for this refractory disease and novel insights for future research .

Materials and methods

Clinical trial design

This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) for medical research involving human subjects. All human studies were conducted with informed consent from the participants and their families, were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Medical University (Approval No: IRB-YJ-2022033), and were registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No: ChiCTR2300069677). Patients were enrolled between 2022 and 2024. All participants received intra-articular ARDE injections and underwent radiographic evaluations with symptom quantification preoperation, at 1 month postoperation, and at 6 months postoperation.

Inclusion criteria: A diagnosis of disc displacement without reduction, with limited opening, made according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD); VAS (visual analog scale) score > 4; active maximum mouth opening (AMMO) < 3.0 cm; absence of systemic diseases (e.g., rheumatism); and compliance with evaluations and complete medical history documentation.

Exclusion criteria: History of maxillofacial infection or trauma; prior neurogenic headache disorders; connective tissue or autoimmune diseases; previous temporomandibular joint injections; history of maxillofacial surgery; significant condylar bone resorption observed on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); contraindications to lipoaspiration; and noncompliance or incomplete medical records.

Clinical trial procedures

Joint lavage fluid collection: Samples were collected from TMJOA patients via intraoperative joint lavage. The procedure was performed under sterile conditions with the patient under general anesthesia and in the supine position. The TMJ puncture site (anterior to the tragus and slightly inferior to the zygomatic arch root) was confirmed by direct palpation. After povidone iodine disinfection, 2 ml of 0.9% sterile saline was aspirated using a 5 ml syringe. The needle was carefully inserted into the upper joint compartment, followed by slow saline infusion and repeated aspiration to fully irrigate the joint space. Lavage fluid was collected into sterile syringes, transferred to sterile tubes, and stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Adipose harvesting: The abdominal adipose donor site was marked, and tumescent solution (500 ml 0.9% saline + 5 ml 5% sodium bicarbonate + 10 ml 2% lidocaine + 0.5 ml 0.1% epinephrine) was prepared. A 3 mm transverse incision was made in an inconspicuous abdominal area. A 20 ml syringe with a blunt cannula was used to inject the tumescent solution subcutaneously at a 2:1 ratio (tumescent volume: adipose harvest volume). After 5 min of vasoconstrictive action, lipoaspiration was performed using a suction cannula connected to a 20 ml syringe under negative pressure (Figure S1A–D).

ARDE processing: Harvested adipose tissue was centrifuged at 800 rpm for 3 min to remove residual tumescent fluid. The purified adipose tissue was washed repeatedly with saline. A total of 10 ml of adipose tissue was transferred into a 20 ml syringe, connected via a three-way valve to another empty syringe, and mechanically emulsified by pushing and pulling the syringe 60 times until a homogeneous semifluid consistency was achieved. Following centrifugation of the emulsified adipose tissue at 1000 rpm for 5 min, three distinct layers formed: the upper layer comprised semifluid adipose tissue admixed with adipose debris, the intermediate layer contained residual tumescent fluid, and the lower layer consisted of the SVF pellet. The upper layer components were filtered through a 0.5 mm sterile nylon mesh to remove adipose debris, yielding liquid adipose. The intermediate liquid layer was discarded, and 1 ml of the liquid adipose was combined with the SVF pellet to obtain the ARDE for clinical therapy (Figure S1E–I).

ARDE administration: The puncture site (1 cm anterior to the tragus, between the glenoid fossa and posterior condylar slope) was identified via mandibular motion palpation. After povidone iodine disinfection, a needle was inserted into the upper joint compartment under mouth opening. Following negative blood aspiration, 1.5 ml of 2% lidocaine was injected for lavage. After lavage, 0.8 ml of ARDE was administered. Postinjection compression was applied for 5–10 min, and the patients were instructed to avoid eating hard foods (Figure S2).

TMJOA patient outcome assessment

All patients underwent MRI at the Department of Radiology, School of Stomatology, Air Force Military Medical University before the operation, 1 month after the operation, and 6 months after the operation. MRI was used to evaluate disc position and joint effusion (hyperintense signals in the temporomandibular joint space on T2-weighted imaging). Imaging findings were interpreted by experienced radiologists to ensure accurate structural and pathological evaluation.

Clinical symptoms were quantified preoperatively and at 1-month/6-month postoperative intervals. The primary outcome was pain severity measured using a 10 cm VAS. The scale ranged from 0 (“painless”) to 10 (“intense pain”), with incremental labels: slight pain (2), mild pain (4), moderate pain (6), and severe pain (8). Patients marked a point on the line reflecting their perceived pain intensity, and the final scores ranged from 0 to 10 (higher values indicating greater pain).

The secondary outcomes included AMMO and PMMO (passive maximum mouth opening). AMMO was measured as the vertical distance between the incisal edges of the maxillary and mandibular central incisors at maximal unassisted opening. PMMO was recorded after examiners applied gentle downward pressure to manually achieve maximal assisted mouth opening.

ELISA

Actin alpha 2 (ACTA2) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β) levels in the TMJ lavage fluid of TMJOA patients were quantified using a Human ACTA2 Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA; ab240678) and a Human IL-1 beta ELISA Kit (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA; ab214025), respectively. Prior to detection, all the samples and reagents were equilibrated to room temperature. Serial dilutions of standards were prepared to generate a standard curve. The cell extraction buffer PTR, wash buffer PT, and antibody cocktail were prepared according to the manufacturers’ instructions. All standards and blank controls were assayed in duplicate, while samples were analyzed in triplicate to minimize experimental variability. Fifty microliters of each sample and standard (50 µl of 0.9% saline for blank control wells) were added to the designated wells of a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 50 µl of the prepared antibody cocktail. The plate was incubated on an orbital shaker for 1 h at room temperature. The wells were then washed three times with 350 µl of wash buffer PT, and the residual liquid was completely removed after each wash. Subsequently, 100 µl of 3, 3′, 5, 5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) development solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated on a shaker for 10 min until a yellow color developed. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µl of stop solution, followed by a 1-minute shaking incubation to ensure homogeneous mixing, resulting in the development of a blue color. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The average absorbance of the blank controls was subtracted from all standard and sample readings to obtain the final absorbance values for calculation. A standard curve was generated by plotting known standard concentrations against their corresponding absorbance values, followed by linear regression analysis. The concentration of the target protein in each sample (ng/ml) was determined by interpolating the sample absorbance values on the standard curve.

Animal source

New Zealand rabbits (2–2.5 kg, 4–6 months old) were selected for this study. To minimize interference from sex hormone fluctuations, male animals were exclusively used in this study. All rabbits were purchased from the Animal Experiment Center of Air Force Medical University. The animal experimental protocols were approved by the Ethics Committee for the Management of Laboratory Animals, School of Stomatology, Air Force Military Medical University (Approval No. kq-2022-082). Prior to selection, the animals were confirmed to be healthy and met the experimental requirements. Housing conditions were standardized as follows: ambient temperature 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity 50 ± 10%, and a 12-hour light‒dark cycle. The rabbits had ad libitum access to food and water. Throughout the acclimatization and experimental periods, no morbidity or mortality was observed.

Animal grouping and surgical procedures

Thirty male New Zealand rabbits were randomly assigned to 4-, 8-, and 12-week ARDE groups and the corresponding control groups via simple randomization using Excel-generated random numbers (5 animals/group/timepoint). Preoperative procedures included anesthesia, surgical site shaving, povidone iodine disinfection, and incision marking. A 3 cm longitudinal incision was surgically created in the scapular region of the animal, followed by careful dissection of the subcutaneous fascia to expose the adipose layer, from which approximately 5 ml of strip-shaped subcutaneous adipose tissue was harvested. The adipose tissue was emulsified by transferring it into a syringe connected to a three-way valve. Another empty syringe was then attached to the valve. Bidirectional pumping of the syringes was performed 60 times to achieve complete emulsification, ensuring that the adipose tissue passed smoothly through the valve and encountered no resistance during syringe manipulation. The parameters for low-speed centrifugation and the filtration method were identical to those used in the clinical preparation of ARDE. Subsequently, 1 ml of the liquid adipose was mixed with the SVF pellet to prepare the ARDE for animal experimental injections.

TMJOA animal models were established via TMJ disc perforation. In the ARDE group, a 1.5 cm transverse incision was made from approximately 1 cm inferolateral to the lateral canthus toward the external auditory meatus. Subcutaneous tissue, muscle, and fascia were bluntly dissected to expose the distal zygomatic arch. The zygomatic root bone was removed to access the TMJ disc, which was exteriorized and subjected to a full-thickness circular perforation (2 mm diameter) at its central region. The disc was then repositioned naturally. The upper joint cavity was exposed, and 0.5 ml of ARDE was injected into the joint cavity. The control animals underwent identical disc perforation procedures without ARDE injection. Both the dorsal and joint incisions were closed by suturing each layer of the subcutaneous tissue and skin.

Behavioral assessments

Prior to testing, the animals were placed in a dedicated restraint device. Rubber bands were looped around the maxillary and mandibular incisors and retracted to achieve maximum mouth opening capacity. The distance between the incisal edges of the upper and lower incisors was measured and recorded as passive mouth opening. A digital push‒pull force gauge with a flat probe was applied perpendicularly to the temporomandibular joint region, and gradually increasing force was applied until the animal exhibited pain-related avoidance behaviors (head withdrawal or evasion). The minimum applied force triggering this response was recorded as the head withdrawal threshold (HWT), with higher values indicating greater pain tolerance (milder pain symptoms). All behavioral data were collected and analyzed to compare differences in passive mouth opening and HWT between the ARDE and control groups. Measurements were conducted under blinded conditions, where the assessor remained unaware of group assignments, following predefined standardized evaluation protocols to eliminate observer bias.

Histological examination

Tissue sectioning: The bilateral temporomandibular joints were dissected for histological sectioning and analyzed via hematoxylin‒eosin (HE) and safranin O/fast green staining. Harvested joint tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. Fixed tissues were sequentially dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%), cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 5 μm-thick sectioned using a microtome and mounted on glass slides. The sections were dried in a 60 °C oven, deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through descending ethanol concentrations (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, and 70%), and then rinsed with distilled water for subsequent staining.

HE staining: HE staining was performed via a commercial HE staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, G1120). Following the manufacturer’s protocol, deparaffinized sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed with distilled water, differentiated in acid alcohol for 30 s, incubated in 50 °C warm water for 5 min, counterstained with eosin for 2 min, and then rinsed again. The sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin for microscopic observation.

Safranin O/Fast Green staining: A modified Safranin O/Fast Green staining kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, G1371) was used. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, deparaffinized sections were stained in a 1:1 mixture of Weigert’s 1 and 2 solutions for 3 min, differentiated in acid differentiation solution for 15 s, rinsed with distilled water for 5 min, stained with fast green solution for 5 min, briefly washed with weak acid solution for 10–15 s, and counterstained with Safranin O solution for 5 min. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral resin for observation via light microscopy.

Modified Mankin OA scoring: Evaluations were performed independently by five blinded evaluators who remained unaware of group assignments. Assessors applied a predefined standardized scoring system to evaluate histological outcomes comprehensively, ensuring objectivity and eliminating observer bias during the assessment process.

Immunofluorescence staining: After washing the paraffin sections with PBS, goat serum blocking solution (ZSGB-Biotech, Beijing, China, ZLI-9056) was applied and incubated for 30 min. Primary antibodies against MMP-13 (1:500, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA, NBP1-45723), IL-1β (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 66737-1-Ig), TNF-α (1:500, Novus Biologicals, Littleton, CO, USA, NBP2-34301), ACTA2 (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, ab7817), collagen I (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 66761-1-Ig), collagen III (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 68320-1-Ig) and p53 (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 60283-2-Ig) were diluted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and incubated overnight at 4℃. Following PBS washing, diluted fluorescently conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 4412 and 4408) was added and incubated for 1 h in the dark. Finally, the slides were mounted with an anti-quenching fluorescence mounting medium containing DAPI (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0131) to stain the nuclei. Visualization and image acquisition were performed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Isolation and culture of primary mandibular condylar chondrocytes

Primary chondrocytes were isolated from the mandibular condylar cartilage of New Zealand rabbits. Cartilage tissue was aseptically dissected from the condylar surface using sterile forceps. The harvested cartilage was immersed in PBS containing 1% penicillin‒streptomycin and washed three times. The tissue was minced into approximately 1 cm3 fragments with sterile ophthalmic scissors and digested with 0.5% type II collagenase (equal volume to the tissue mass) for 12 h at 37 °C. Undigested debris was removed by filtration through 70 μm nylon mesh (BD Falcon, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). The digestion was terminated by adding PBS (1:1 volume), followed by centrifugation at 1000 rpm for 5 min. Pelleted cells were resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and transferred to culture dishes. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator, with the medium replaced every 2–3 days. Subculturing was performed when the cells reached approximately 80% confluence.

Establishment of the TMJOA chondrocyte model

Primary mandibular condylar chondrocytes were cultured to passages 2–3 and allowed to reach the logarithmic growth phase prior to TMJOA model induction. Chondrocytes were stimulated with IL-1β (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 200-01B) or tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) (Biotechne, Minneapolis, MN, USA, 5670-TG) at a concentration of 10 ng/ml for 24 h to simulate pathological changes in chondrocytes under inflammatory conditions in the joint cavity.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, Shanghai, China; C0041). According to the manufacturer’s instructions, 2 × 103 cells/well were seeded in 96-well plates and divided into IL-1β-treated, TNF-α-treated, and untreated control groups, with triplicate wells per group to minimize variability. After 24 h of culture for cell adherence and entry into the logarithmic growth phase, the experimental groups were treated with medium containing 10 ng/ml IL-1β or TNF-α for 24 h, whereas the control group remained untreated. After treatment, the medium was replaced with cytokine-free medium for continued culture. The original medium was discarded, and 10 µl of CCK-8 reagent mixed with 90 µl of DMEM was added to each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 4 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The absorbance values were directly proportional to cell viability.

Transcriptome sequencing analysis

Condylar chondrocytes were seeded in culture dishes and divided into IL-1β-treated, TNF-α-treated, and untreated control groups. After 24 h of adhesion culture, the experimental groups were treated with medium containing 10 ng/mL IL-1β or TNF-α for 24 h, whereas the control group remained untreated. According to the manufacturer’s protocol, total RNA was extracted from chondrocytes (3 × 106 cells per sample) using TRIzol reagent (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan), with three biological replicates per group. The RNA libraries were sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq™ 6000 platform by LC Bio Technology CO., Ltd (Hangzhou, China). Data analysis was performed via LC-Bio Cloud platform (Hangzhou, China) focusing on KEGG pathway enrichment.

Cellular Immunofluorescence staining assay

Primary mandibular condylar chondrocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well plates precoated with slides of cells and cultured for 24 h until adherence and entry into the logarithmic growth phase. The cells were divided into five experimental groups: the control group, the IL-1β group, the IL-1β + ADSC coculture group, the TNF-α group, and the TNF-α + ADSC coculture group. The IL-1β group and the IL-1β + ADSC coculture group were treated with medium containing 10 ng/mL IL-1β, while the TNF-α stimulation group and the TNF-α + ADSC coculture group were treated with medium supplemented with 10 ng/ml TNF-α. Transwell inserts (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA, 140620, pore size: 0.4 μm) containing 2 × 103 ADSCs were placed into the coculture groups. After 24 h of culture, the original medium and Transwell inserts were removed. The cells were fixed with 1 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde per well for 15 min, permeabilized with 500 µl of PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0096) for 10 min, and blocked with 1 ml of 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Primary antibodies against ACTA2 (1:200, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, ab7817), collagen I (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 66761-1-Ig), and collagen III (1:500, Proteintech, Wuhan, China, 68320-1-Ig) were diluted in antibody dilution buffer, and 500 µl of diluted antibody solution was added to the designated wells and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following three washes with PBS, the cells were incubated with 500 µl of fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA; 4412 and 4408) protected from light for 1 h. Coverslips were mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fade fluorescence mounting medium (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, P0131) and visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

β-galactosidase staining

Cellular senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity was detected using a Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C0602). Primary mandibular condylar chondrocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h until adherence and entry into the logarithmic growth phase. The cells were divided into a control group, an IL-1β group, and an IL-1β + ADSC coculture group. The IL-1β group and the IL-1β + ADSC coculture group were treated with medium containing 10 ng/ml IL-1β, while Transwell inserts preseeded with 2 × 103 ADSCs were placed into the IL-1β + ADSC coculture group. After 24 h of culture, the original medium and Transwell inserts were removed. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, the cells were fixed with 1 ml of staining fixative per well at room temperature for 15 min and washed three times. A staining working solution was prepared according to the specified ratio, and 1 ml of the solution was added to each well. The plate was sealed with parafilm and incubated at 37 °C in a CO₂-free environment for 12 h. β-Galactosidase-positive cells were observed and imaged under a standard light microscope.

Cell cycle analysis

The cell cycle distribution was analyzed using a Cell Cycle and Apoptosis Analysis Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C1052). Primary mandibular condylar chondrocytes were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well in 24-well plates and cultured for 24 h until adherence and entry into the logarithmic growth phase. The cells were divided into a control group, an IL-1β group, and an IL-1β + ADSC coculture group. The IL-1β group and the IL-1β + ADSC coculture group were treated with medium containing 10 ng/ml IL-1β, while Transwell inserts preseeded with 2 × 103 ADSCs were placed into the coculture group. After 24 h of culture, the Transwell inserts were removed. In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, conditioned medium was collected in centrifuge tubes. The cells were digested with trypsin-EDTA, neutralized with the collected medium, and centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of ice-cold PBS, centrifuged again, and fixed in 1 ml of ice-cold 70% ethanol with gentle pipetting for 2 h at 4 °C. Fixed cells were centrifuged (1000 rpm, 5 min), washed with ice-cold PBS, and stained with 0.5 ml of propidium iodide staining solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Red fluorescence (excitation: 488 nm) and light scatter characteristics were analyzed using a flow cytometer (NovoExpress 1.5.6 software).

Adipogenic and chondrogenic differentiation induction of ADSCs

Adipogenic and chondrogenic differentiation experiments were performed using the Adipogenic Differentiation Kit for Adipose-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (Oricell®, Guangzhou, China, GUXMD-90031) and Chondrogenic Differentiation Kit (Oricell®, Guangzhou, China, GUXMD-90041), respectively. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, cells were seeded and differentiation-inducing reagents were prepared. Induction was maintained until observation of abundant, appropriately sized lipid droplets (adipogenesis) and formation of cartilage microspheres with 1.5–2 mm diameter (chondrogenesis), at which point samples were subjected to subsequent staining. Oil Red O staining was employed to visualize adipogenic differentiation, while Alcian Blue staining was used to assess chondrogenic differentiation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5; La Jolla, CA, USA). The normality of continuous variables was assessed by the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-tailed Student’s t test was used to analyze significant differences between two groups, whereas one-way ANOVA was applied for comparisons among three or more groups. Nonnormally distributed continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), with statistical significance determined by the Mann‒Whitney U test (two groups) or Kruskal‒Wallis test (multiple groups). A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with asterisks denoting significance levels: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. The sample size (n) and specific statistical method for each analysis are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results and discussion

Increased expression levels of fibrosis markers in joint lavage fluid from TMJOA patients

Recent studies on knee OA have revealed that cartilage fibrosis, which is characterized by the transdifferentiation of normal hyaline chondrocytes into chondro-myofibroblasts and the abnormal deposition of type I/III collagen, is as a critical pathological process that occurs during OA development [24, 25]. This newly formed fibrocartilage has an inferior load-bearing capacity and wear resistance, significantly impairing normal joint function [26]. Therefore, targeting cartilage fibrosis has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for treating OA. Emerging evidence has demonstrated that inhibiting the progression of fibrosis significantly improves symptoms and joint function in OA [27, 28]. However, the role of this pivotal pathomechanism in TMJOA progression, the correlations between fibrosis and clinical factors, and the development of novel therapeutic strategies targeting fibrotic remodeling have remained unexplored. To verify this phenomenon, this study included 10 patients who were diagnosed with TMJOA, and their basic information is provided in Table 1. The severity of the disease in each patient was assessed using the Wilkes staging system (Table S1). Among them, five patients were in Wilkes stage III, showing mild joint disc displacement deformities, and five patients were in stages IV-V, exhibiting severe deformities with bone destruction. The levels of the proinflammatory cytokines IL-1β and ACTA2 in the joint lavage fluid of the patients were measured using ELISA. IL-1β is a pivotal pro-inflammatory cytokine driving OA pathogenesis. Clinical evidence indicates significantly elevated serum IL-1β levels in OA patients compared with non-OA controls [29]. ACTA2 is a fibrosis marker that is secreted by OA chondrocytes and is a key protein that promotes the formation of fibrocartilage [25]. The results revealed that the ACTA2 concentration in the joint lavage fluid of patients with Wilkes stage III disease was 19.64 ± 10.38 pg/ml, which was significantly lower than that of patients with stage IV-V disease (105.3 ± 30.88 pg/ml) (Fig. 1A). Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between ACTA2 and IL-1β concentrations (Fig. 1B), suggesting that the severity of joint fibrosis increases as inflammation levels increase. This study is the first to detect fibrosis markers in human TMJ synovial fluid samples and explore related factors, with the results showing significantly elevated fibrosis marker levels in the joint cavities of TMJOA patients, which were significantly correlated with both inflammation severity and Wilkes stage. These findings provide clinical evidence for subsequent basic research on fibrosis as a core therapeutic approach for TMJOA.

ARDE improves clinical symptoms and mitigates imaging abnormalities in TMJOA patients

This study included 18 patients diagnosed with disc displacement without reduction, with limited mouth opening according to the Diagnostic Criteria for Temporomandibular Disorders (DC/TMD), all of whom received ARDE intra-articular injection. These patients had received conventional conservative therapy for at least 6 months but subsequently experienced disease recurrence or exhibited inadequate symptomatic relief. Therefore, minimally invasive injection therapy is an appropriate treatment option for these patients. In clinical practice, injectable ARDE was prepared from the abdominal adipose aspirates of patients through mechanical emulsification, low-speed centrifugation, and filtration. The detailed preparation protocol is illustrated in Figure S1. Efficacy was evaluated based on clinical indicators, and MRI was performed before the operation, 1 month postoperation, and 6 months postoperation. The VAS was used to quantify subjective pain intensity as the primary evaluation metric (Fig. 2A), and the AMMO and PMMO were used to quantify joint function as secondary evaluation metrics. The results revealed that at 1 month postoperation, the VAS score significantly decreased (1.8 ± 0.3 vs. 5.2 ± 0.7, ****p < 0.0001; Fig. 2D), and the AMMO and PMMO increased from the preoperative values of 2.2 ± 0.6 cm and 2.6 ± 0.8 cm to 3.3 ± 0.5 cm (****p < 0.0001; Fig. 2E) and 3.6 ± 0.5 cm (***p < 0.001; Fig. 2F), respectively. The therapeutic effect was enhanced at 6 months postoperation, with the VAS score further decreasing to 0.9 ± 0.8 and the AMMO, PMMO reaching 4.0 ± 0.3 cm and 4.2 ± 0.3 cdm, respectively (****p < 0.0001). Figure 2B shows the preoperative and postoperative mouth opening measurements in a representative patient. These results demonstrate that ARDE injection not only alleviates pain but also ameliorates mouth opening restriction in TMJOA patients, leading to the restoration of joint function. Studies have indicated that lipids can provide a “sustained, extreme lubrication” for cartilage, which helps maintain a favorable joint mechanical environment, thereby promoting tissue repair and alleviation of clinical symptoms [30]. Thus, their value in the treatment of osteoarthritis is significant. We propose that the long-term maintenance of the therapeutic efficacy following ARDE injection is not only attributable to the chondroreparative and regenerative functions of ADSCs but also to the lubricating properties of the liquid adipose. A significant restoration of mouth opening and pain relief were achieved in 88.9% of patients (16/18) at 6 months postoperatively, whereas two cases exhibited no significant symptomatic improvement (Table S2). In addition to symptom improvement, some patients with joint effusion in the joint cavity preoperation had complete resolution of joint effusion at 6 months postoperation according to the imaging results (Fig. 2C). No adverse events were reported during the follow-up period, and all patients tolerated the treatment well, with no patients lost to follow-up. The above results validate the clinical efficacy and safety of ARDE in patients with TMJOA.

ARDE promotes cartilage repair, suppresses inflammation, and inhibits fibrosis in TMJOA animal models

Prospective clinical trials have preliminarily demonstrated that the degree of joint fibrosis in TMJOA patients is associated with inflammatory mediator levels and that ARDE can significantly alleviate clinical symptoms. However, its underlying mechanisms remain unclear. In this study, the regulatory effects of ARDE on the pathological microenvironment of joint tissues were systematically investigated through validation using an animal model. Figure 3A shows the preparation of ARDE for the animal model experiment. The adipose tissue was mechanically emulsified (Fig. 3B), during which larger adipocytes were disrupted, releasing intracellular lipid droplets and generating substantial amounts of adipose debris. Following low-speed centrifugation of the emulsified adipose tissue, the upper layer consisted of semifluid adipose tissue admixed with adipose debris, whereas the lower layer comprised the SVF pellet. The SVF is a heterogeneous cell population with ADSCs as the core cellular component and possesses stem cell properties, including anti-inflammatory properties, immunomodulatory properties [31], and properties that promote cartilage repair [32]. Both preclinical studies and clinical trials in which SVF was used to treat patients with knee OA reported positive clinical outcomes [33, 34]. The upper layer was filtered to remove adipose debris, yielding liquid adipose. Subsequently, 1 ml of liquid adipose was combined with the SVF pellet to prepare the ARDE injectate (Fig. 3C, D). The SVF was isolated and expanded in culture to assess the multilineage differentiation potential of ADSCs. Owing to their adherent growth characteristics, ADSCs were selectively retained during serial passaging, whereas nonadherent cellular components were progressively removed during passaging until they were completely eliminated (Figure S3) [35]. Adipogenic and chondrogenic induction experiments demonstrated the robust multidirectional differentiation capability of ADSCs (Figure S4).

F–K ARDE treatment was administered according to the following steps: marking the surgical area, removing the bony root of the zygomatic arch, exposing the joint disc, creating a central perforation in the joint disc to model, exposing the joint cavity, and injecting ARDE. Next, a TMJOA animal model was established through joint disc perforation. Our preliminary research showed that this animal model successfully recapitulates the core clinical symptoms (joint pain, limited mouth opening) and typical pathological changes (condylar cartilage structural disruption, matrix degradation). Moreover, the continuous proliferation of the fibrous cartilage layer along with the upregulation of fibrosis markers and inflammatory factors was observed, providing a reliable methodological foundation for subsequent ARDE efficacy validation. As shown in Fig. 3E and 30 male New Zealand rabbits were randomly divided into 4-, 8-, and 12-week ARDE treatment groups (ARDE injection into the joint cavity at the time of joint disc perforation modeling) and a control group (modeling only), with 5 samples per group. The effects of ARDE on cartilage repair, inflammation suppression, and fibrosis inhibition were verified using behavioral and histological assays. The surgical procedure for the animals is shown in Fig. 3F-K.

Postoperative behavioral assessments demonstrated that, compared with control animals, ARDE-treated animals presented a significantly higher HWT (p < 0.05), indicating alleviation of joint mechanical hyperalgesia (Fig. 4A). PMMO was significantly increased in ARDE-treated animals versus control animals (p < 0.05), suggesting restored mandibular mobility (Fig. 4B). Histological analysis revealed typical OA pathological alterations in the condylar cartilage of the control group, including superficial delamination fissures, indistinct zonal boundaries, disordered cellular arrangement, localized cell loss, and abnormal cell clustering. The cartilage of the ARDE-treated groups exhibited restored structural integrity, continuous smooth surfaces, distinct cartilage layers, and uniform cellular morphology/arrangement (Fig. 4C). Safranin O staining revealed pale and regionally restricted matrix staining in the control group, and patchy focal staining was observed at 12 weeks. In contrast, the cartilage of the ARDE-treated groups exhibited more intense matrix staining with continuous ribbon-like safranin O retention in the hyaline layer, indicating mitigated cartilage matrix loss (Fig. 4D). The modified Mankin OA scoring criteria [36] (Table S3) revealed significantly lower degeneration scores in the joints of ARDE-treated animals than in control animals (4 weeks: 2.4 ± 0.5 vs. 6.6 ± 0.5; 8 weeks: 1.6 ± 0.9 vs. 6.8 ± 0.8; 12 weeks: 2.8 ± 0.8 vs. 8.2 ± 0.4; p < 0.05; Fig. 4E). In conclusion, ARDE ameliorated disease symptoms and effectively promoted cartilage repair, resulting in a near-normal histoarchitecture (Figure S5).

Histological examination revealed significant fibrocartilage hyperplasia in the superficial condylar cartilage of the control group, indicating cartilage fibrosis (areas marked by dashed red lines in Fig. 4C). Compared with the control group, the ARDE-treated groups presented significantly reduced fibrocartilage thickness at matched timepoints (4 weeks: 132.5 ± 15.3 μm vs. 189.7 ± 38.2 μm; 8 weeks: 156.8 ± 29.5 μm vs. 229.9 ± 31.6 μm; 12 weeks: 116.9 ± 12.4 μm vs. 163.9 ± 19.3 μm; p < 0.05; Fig. 4F). Immunofluorescence quantification of fibrosis markers (Fig. 4G) revealed diffuse ACTA2 expression throughout entire cartilage layers in control animals at 4 weeks, with ACTA2 distribution progressively decreasing over time. The cartilage of ARDE-treated animals displayed early-stage ACTA2 expression in superficial ribbon-like patterns, which nearly disappeared by 12 weeks (Fig. 4H). Both type I collagen (COL-I) and type III collagen (COL-III) presented significantly lower expression levels in the ARDE-treated groups than in the time-matched controls (Fig. 4I, J). These findings indicate that ARDE abrogates pathological fibrocartilage transformation by downregulating fibrotic protein expression, thereby decelerating disease progression. To validate that cartilage fibrosis is induced by modeling, we performed immunofluorescence staining for fibrosis markers on condylar cartilage from healthy New Zealand rabbits and observed minimal expression of these markers (Figure S6).

Additionally, ARDE exerts synergistic therapeutic effects by inhibiting cartilage matrix degradation and suppressing inflammation. Immunofluorescence revealed that only a limited number of cells in the condylar cartilage of healthy New Zealand rabbits exhibited positive immunoreactivity for IL-1β, TNF-α, and metalloproteinase-13 (MMP-13) (Figure S7). Control groups demonstrated marked positive immunoreactivity for these markers in the condylar cartilage (Fig. 4K). Following ARDE intervention, expression levels of all three markers were significantly suppressed compared with time-matched controls (Fig. 4L-N). These findings suggest that ARDE effectively improves the pathological cartilage microenvironment through modulation of matrix homeostasis and inflammatory activity, thereby facilitating cartilage repair.

ADSC coculture attenuates the fibrotic phenotype in an inflammatory factor-induced TMJOA chondrocyte model

The animal model experiments confirmed that ARDE significantly inhibits TMJOA fibrocartilage progression; however, the core functional components remain undefined. ADSCs promote tissue repair via multipotent differentiation and paracrine activity [37] and exhibit potent antifibrotic effects in the contexts of hepatic fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, and dermal scarring [38–40]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that ADSCs may mediate the antifibrotic effects of ARDE. To test this hypothesis, we established an in vitro inflammatory mediator induced-TMJOA chondrocyte model. Consistent with the characteristic IL-1β and TNF-α overexpression observed in the TMJOA animal model, primary condylar chondrocytes were stimulated with these inflammatory factors to recapitulate the pathological joint microenvironment (Fig. 5A). This modeling approach has been widely utilized in OA research to reliably induce chondrocyte degradation, providing a foundation for mechanistic investigations [41].

The results of the CCK-8 cell proliferation assay demonstrated that stimulation with inflammatory mediators significantly promoted chondrocyte proliferation. The IL-1β-induced group presented significantly increased proliferative activity on day 4, whereas the TNF-α-induced group presented similar increases on day 5 (p < 0.05), with sustained effects persisting through day 7 postintervention (Fig. 5B, C). This aberrant proliferative phenotype observed in vitro aligns with the increase in fibrocartilage thickness observed in the in vivo experiments, suggesting that inflammation-mediated compensatory proliferation of damaged chondrocytes may act as a driving factor in fibrocartilage progression.

To validate the fibrotic characteristics of the TMJOA cellular model, transcriptomic sequencing was performed on chondrocytes before and after inflammatory stimulation. Figure S8 summarizes the differential gene expression between inflammatory cytokine-induced chondrocytes and untreated chondrocytes. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes (Fig. 5D, E) revealed significant activation of profibrotic pathways, including the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway, in model cells (p < 0.05), with gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) confirming their upregulation (Figure S9). These pathways are established drivers of fibrosis across multiple organs and diseases [42, 43].

The regulatory effects of ADSCs on the fibrotic phenotypes of chondrocytes in an inflammatory microenvironment were investigated through quantitative immunofluorescence analysis. The results revealed that IL-1β stimulation upregulated ACTA2, COL-I, and COL-III expression in TMJOA model cells as evidenced by the significantly increased mean fluorescence intensities in the stimulated cells compared with those in the control cells. Moreover, a perinuclear diffuse distribution pattern was observed (Fig. 5F). ADSC coculture significantly decreased the expression of these fibrotic markers to levels similar to those of the controls (Fig. 5G-I). TNF-α stimulation induced the upregulation of ACTA2 and COL-III, while COL-I exhibited no significant changes. Following ADSC coculture, both ACTA2 and COL-III expression levels were markedly decreased (Fig. 5J-L). Notably, the differential regulatory effects of IL-1β and TNF-α on the expression of fibrotic markers (IL-1β-induced COL-I upregulation versus negligible TNF-α-mediated changes) suggest that single-factor stimulation inadequately recapitulates the complex inflammatory microenvironment of joint cavities in patients with TMJOA. These findings demonstrate that ADSCs alleviate pathological cartilage fibrosis by downregulating the expression of key fibrotic effector proteins, which is consistent with our hypothesis.

ADSC coculture alleviates inflammatory factor-induced senescence in a TMJOA chondrocyte model

We performed transcriptomic sequencing analyses to explore the potential mechanisms by which ADSCs alleviate cartilage fibrosis. The phenomenon of upregulated expression of senescence-associated genes in the inflammatory factor-induced chondrocytes drew our attention. (Figure S10). As shown in Fig. 5D, E, IL-1β and TNF-α stimulation resulted in the significant enrichment of senescence-related KEGG pathways, including cellular senescence, the AGE–RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, and the NF-kappa B signaling pathway (p < 0.05). Among them, the NF-kappa B signaling pathway has also been confirmed to be closely related to inflammation reaction activation, inducing joint pain and swelling [44]. GSEA confirmed the upregulation of these pathways (Fig. 6A, B). Cellular senescence constitutes an irreversible state of cell cycle arrest, characterized by concomitant mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress mediated by elevated intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS). Although this process functions as a normal physiological phenomenon, pathological accumulation of senescent cells beyond homeostatic levels acts as a driver of disease pathogenesis [45]. Although cellular senescence, a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest, occurs under physiological conditions, aberrant cell senescence drives disease progression [46]. A strong interplay exists between senescence and fibrosis: persistent accumulation of senescent cells leads to the induction of a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), promoting aberrant tissue repair and fibrogenesis. The molecular mechanisms linking senescence to tissue fibrosis are multifactorial and context dependent and form a positive feedback loop [47]. We hypothesized that ADSCs exert antifibrotic effects by delaying chondrocyte senescence. Flow cytometry analysis of the cell cycle (Fig. 6C, D) revealed that ADSC coculture restored the abnormal cell cycle distribution of IL-1β-treated chondrocytes (G1 phase: 35.19 ± 0.82% → 42.28 ± 1.57%; S phase: 49.81 ± 1.23% → 37.00 ± 1.89%; G2 phase: 15.00 ± 1.55% → 19.72 ± 1.82%), which resembled the cell cycle distribution of the control cells (G1: 42.99 ± 1.39%; S: 37.96 ± 0.63%; G2: 19.06 ± 1.40%). β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining revealed that ADSC intervention reduced the proportion of senescence-positive cells from 40.90 ± 5.44% (IL-1β-induced) to 28.26 ± 4.24% (p < 0.01), which was not significantly different from the proportion of senescence-positive cells in the control group (25.86 ± 5.42%) (Fig. 6E, F).

Mounting evidence in recent years confirms OA as an age-related multifactorial disorder. Chondrocyte senescence plays a pivotal role in OA progression by activating oxidative stress responses and activating signaling pathways such as the TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin, and NF-κB pathways, thereby promoting cartilage fibrosis and joint inflammation [48]. Compared with normal chondrocytes, OA-derived chondrocytes exhibit characteristic overexpression of senescence markers [49]. Consequently, targeted elimination of senescent chondrocytes or inhibition of these pathways has been demonstrated to effectively delay OA progression [50]. This study revealed that ADSCs significantly suppress senescence phenotypes in chondrocytes under inflammatory stimulation. To validate the consistency between the in vitro and in vivo findings, p53 immunofluorescence staining was performed to further confirm the anti-senescence effects of ARDE in the TMJOA models (Figure S11). The results revealed negligible p53 expression in the cartilage of both the ARDE-treated and control groups at 4 weeks postintervention. However, sustained p53 overexpression emerged in the control group at weeks 8–12, whereas the ARDE-treated groups maintained significantly lower expression levels than the control groups, further validating that ARDE effectively suppresses chondrocyte senescence even within the complex physiological microenvironment in vivo. Although the current study yielded satisfactory results, the specific active components of ARDE (such as cytokines, proteins, or cell-derived exosomes) and the potential signaling pathways and molecular mechanisms involved have not yet been thoroughly explored. These critical aspects will be explored in our future work.

Conclusions

This study is the first to validate the presence of fibrotic changes in TMJOA through clinical joint lavage fluid analysis and proposes a novel therapeutic strategy to slow TMJOA progression and improve clinical symptoms by inhibiting fibrosis. Prospective clinical studies demonstrated that intra-articular ARDE injection significantly alleviated symptoms in more than 80% of patients with TMJOA while mitigating radiographic manifestations such as joint effusion, confirming its clinical efficacy and safety. Animal experiments verified the capacity of ARDE to mitigate tissue deterioration in condylar cartilage, inhibit cartilage matrix loss, and inhibit pathological cartilage fibrosis in TMJOA model animals. Further in vitro studies established ADSCs as the core active components within ARDE; they significantly suppressed fibrotic marker secretion in inflammatory factor-stimulated chondrocytes, potentially by inhibiting cellular senescence. These findings not only introduce a clinically ready-to-use, low-risk therapeutic approach with validated efficacy for TMJOA but also provide a new research direction for future studies on the treatment of this disease through the targeting of chondrocyte senescence to mitigate cartilage fibrosis. In summary, ARDE integrates the dual therapeutic advantages of SVF and liquid adipose, leveraging the chondroreparative and regenerative properties of mesenchymal stem cells while utilizing the liquid adipose component to provide sustained joint lubrication as a replacement for traditional first-line HA injection therapy. It demonstrates significant therapeutic efficacy and possesses considerable potential for clinical translation.
