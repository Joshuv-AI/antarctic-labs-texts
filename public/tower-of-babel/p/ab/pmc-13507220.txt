Genotype-by-environment interactions and seed yield stability of narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties (Lupinus angustifolius L.) based on AMMI and GGE biplot analyses
Authors: Destaw Mullualem, Tesfaye Mengie, Desalew Fentie, Alemu Tsega, Walelgn Bitewlgn, Esubalew Sintie, Shiferaw Alemu Alayachew, Temesgen Assefa Gelaw, Yitayih Dessie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNarrow-Leaf lupin sweet varieties (Lupinus angustifolius L.) is a leguminous crop valued for its protein-rich seeds, which are used as both human food and livestock feed. Beyond its nutritional value, this plant is crucial for improving soil health because of its natural nitrogen-fixing ability. Despite its critical status as a valuable multipurpose crop, narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties remains significantly underutilized, with cultivation limited by insufficient data on its performance across diverse environments in Ethiopia. To overcome this limitation and unlock sweet lupine’s performance potential, this study evaluated seed yield of five varieties across three environments over two growing seasons. The analysis employed Genotype-by-Environment Interaction biplot and Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction methods to assess genotype performance, stability, and adaptability, as well as to identify environments ideal for distinguishing genotype responses. This study also employed parameters such as cultivar superiority, AMMI Stability Value, and Genotype Selection Index, which provide valuable insights into the performance of genotypes under varying environmental conditions. The results showed that G3 (Probor) and G2 (Vitabor) were the highest-yielding, stable, and best-adapted genotypes. These two narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties demonstrated greater resilience to environmental fluctuations and enhanced suitability for sustainable future, making them preferable choices for local farmers. They also contribute to increased resilience against climate variability, thereby supporting long-term agricultural sustainability. The AMMI model confirmed that genotype-by-environment interaction has a significant impact on seed yield. These findings offer important insights into the genetic potential of narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties, which can assist breeding programs in developing high-yielding, adaptable, and stable genotypes for the study area and other areas with similar agro-ecological conditions.Keywords: Adaptability, High yield, Multi-environment trials, Principal component analysis, Sustainable agricultureSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Legume plants play a vital role in global agriculture by fixing atmospheric nitrogen, enhancing soil fertility, and providing high-protein crops for food security and sustainable farming1. Among legumes, lupines are also major contributors to society due to their high-quality protein, suitability for eco-friendly farming, and potential health benefits2. With an annual yield of about 1.28 million tonnes, Australia is the world’s top producer of lupin. Approximately 3.53 million tonnes, or more than 67% of the total worldwide, are produced mainly from the narrow-leafed sweet lupin (Lupinus angustifolius)3. About 19,249 hectares are used for sweet lupin cultivation in Ethiopia. According to data from 2019/20, the nation maintains an average yield of 1.87 t/ha during the main cropping season, producing 36,046 tonnes. With 95–99% of the total area and output coming from the Amhara region (West Gojam and Awi administrative zones), production is highly centralized. The Benishangul-Gumuz region comes in second4.

A narrow-leaf lupin sweet variety (Lupinus angustifolius L.) is rising as an important crop in Ethiopia. It is a great source of nutrition and protein for people who can’t afford meat. As an affordable meat alternative with 30–40% protein content, it combats malnutrition through traditional dishes like shiro wot, cooked lupine (nifro), and roasted lupine (kolo), while delivering essential minerals such as iron, zinc, potassium, and sodium. Its adaptability to diverse soils and role as a sustainable organic fertilizer enhance food security and soil health in low-input farming systems5–7.

The genus Lupinus includes nearly 300 species. Recently, industrial and consumer interest in lupins has grown because of their many agricultural and health benefits and their important role in supporting sustainable farming. Only four species are commonly cultivated: Lupinus angustifolius L. (narrow-leaf lupin), Lupinus albus L. (white lupin), Lupinus mutabilis (tarwi or pearl lupine), and Lupinus luteus L. (yellow lupin). Among these, narrow-leaf lupin sweet variety (Lupinus angustifolius L.) is considered to have great potential as a protein source for both animals and humans8,9.

Despite its recognized nutritional value and ecological benefits, narrow-leaf lupin sweet variety (Lupinus angustifolius L.) remains significantly underutilized as a protein-rich crop in Ethiopia. Its wide-scale adoption in the study area and similar agro-ecology is hindered by a lack of comprehensive studies evaluating its performance across different environmental conditions. Current knowledge gaps exist regarding the crop’s seed yield stability and adaptability, making it difficult to recommend sweet lupine for large-scale cultivation in the study area.

The interaction between genetic makeup and environmental factors is crucial in determining adaptation and stability for optimizing yield. Multi-environment trials are conducted to assess yield stability, and adaptability of advanced breeding lines in order to select and recommend the most superior cultivars10,11. Among the various statistical approaches, Additive Main Effects and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI)12 and Genotype Main Effect plus Genotype-by-Environment Interaction (GGE)13 biplot analyses are widely used methods for addressing challenges in multi-environment trial data analysis. AMMI integrates analysis of variance for genotype and environment main effects with principal component analysis (PCA) to dissect genotype-by-environment interactions, making it effective for identifying genotype stability across environments. Conversely, GGE biplot focuses on genotype and interaction effects, using PCA on environment-centered data to visually represent genotype performance and stability, aiding in identifying superior genotypes and classifying mega-environments14,15. Both methods are used extensively in agricultural research for analyzing genotype-environment interactions, particularly to improve crop yield stability and adaptation across diverse climatic and soil conditions. They complement each other but GGE is often preferred for visual genotype evaluation and mega-environment identification.

The study aims to evaluate genotype-by-environment interactions and identify stable, high-yielding sweet lupin genotypes across multiple environments using AMMI and GGE biplot analyses. It seeks to enhance understanding of genotype adaptability, inform breeding programs for improved seed yield stability, and unlock the untapped potential of narrow-leaf lupin sweet variety for sustainable agriculture in the study area and related agro-ecologies.

Materials and methods

Experimental locations and planting material

The study was conducted at three sites in the Awi Administrative Zone, Amhara Region, Ethiopia, namely Asewa, Injibara University Campus Farm Center, and Shinkurta sites (Fig. 1). The Awi Administrative Zone lies between 10◦23′N and 10◦85′N latitudes and 36◦35′E and 36◦57′E longitudes, at altitudes ranging from 1800 to 3100 m above sea level. It receives mean annual rainfall of 1750 mm and experiences mean monthly temperatures between 17 °C and 27 °C. The predominant soil type, nitosol, is well-suited for crop production.

For this study, five varieties of narrow-leaf lupin sweet variety (Welela, Vitabor, Probor, Sanabor and Bora,) obtained from Holletta Agricultural Research Center and Gondar Agricultural Research Center was evaluated under rainfed conditions in 2022 and 2023(Table 1). Proper permission to obtain the seed materials were granted through official communication from the Research and Community Service Directorate of Injibara University, addressed to the Holetta Agricultural Research Center and from Gondar Agricultural Research Center under Amhara Regional Agricultural Research Institute (ARARI).

Experimental design and data collection

A 3 × 5 factorial design was implemented within each year, arranged in three blocks. For the statistical analysis, six combinations of year and field blocks were treated as blocks. Each plot measured 2.5 m in length and 1.8 m in width, totaling an area of 4.5 m2. Each experimental plot comprised six rows, each 2.5 m long, with 30 cm spacing between rows. The field was thoroughly prepared prior to sowing, and planting rows were marked manually using a hand-pulled row marker. Seeds were hand-planted using the drilling method, maintaining 10 cm spacing between plants within each row. The space between plots was 0.50 m, and 1 m gap separated the three blocks. Manual weeding was performed in all experimental fields at the three locations to manage weeds. No herbicides or fungicides were applied for weed or disease control.

Data were gathered by averaging measurements from five randomly selected plants per plot. These representative plants were carefully chosen from the central rows of each plot, excluding the two outermost border rows, and marked for subsequent observations. Data were collected both at the plot level and the plant level. The assessed response variables included the days to 50% flowering (DF), measured as the number of days from sowing until the first flower appears on at least 50% of the plants in each plot, and days to 90% maturity (DM), which were also recorded at the plot level. Other variables included Plant height (PH) in cm, number of branches per plant (NBP), number of seeds per pod (NSP), number of pods per plant (NPP), Above ground biomass (kg/plot), hundred seed weight (g/plot), and seed yield (kg/plot), as well as the harvest Index (HI). However, the primary focus of our study was seed yield that is why it was the only variable analyzed and interpreted in detail.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance with AMMI analysis

Multi-location trials are frequently employed in plant genetics to evaluate the yield performance of various genotypes across different environments. This model includes blocks nested within environments as random effects, the main effects of environment and genotype, and their interaction effects, creating a mixed-effects model17,18. AMMI analysis and GGE plot analysis were conducted using GenStat version 1819 and PBTools 1.4 software, an open-source tool developed by the International Rice Research Institute’s biometrics group20. GenStat version 18 was specifically used for the AMMI analysis of variance within the mixed-effects model, treating genotype as a fixed effect and environment as a random effect11.

Results and discussion

Additive main effect and multiplication interaction (AMMI) Analysis of Variance

Additive Main Effect and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) is a powerful statistical approach widely used in crop improvement programs to evaluate genotype adaptation and stability across different environments. For a crop like narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties, which can be grown in diverse agro-environments, AMMI analysis helps breeders understand how different genotypes perform under varying environmental conditions and how stable their yield or other agronomic traits are27,28. A combined analysis of variance was performed to describe the main effects and quantify the interactions both among and within the sources of variation. The Additive Main Effect and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) model was then applied to evaluate the effects of genotype, environment, and the genotype-by-environment interaction29,30.

The AMMI (Additive Main effects and Multiplicative Interaction) model integrates analysis of variance (ANOVA) with principal component analysis (PCA) to analyze genotype-environment interaction (GEI) effects in multi-environment trials. This approach helps identify genotypes that are both high-yielding and stable in different environments. Analysis of variance using the AMMI model for narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties evaluated across three locations demonstrated that the environment (E), genotype (G), and their interaction (G × E) had a significant impact on seed yield (Table 2), with all factors showing highly significant effects (p ≤ 0.001). Notably, the first axis accounted for 99.5% of the sum of squares associated with the G × E interaction (Table 2). The significant effects of environment (E), genotype (G), and genotype-environment interaction (G × E), as elucidated by the AMMI model, indicate that seed yield in narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties is shaped not only by genetic variations and environmental conditions in isolation but also by the differential performance of specific genotypes across various environments. This suggests that genotype performance is not uniform across all locations; certain genotypes may exhibit enhanced yields in particular environments as a result of interaction effects. The AMMI ANOVA results (Table 2) indicate that genotypic variation accounts for the largest portion of the observed differences in the traits under study, followed by the contribution of environmental factors and their interaction. This underscores that distinct genotypes exert substantially different influences on the trait being measured, highlighting the predominant role of genetic factors in trait variability. This study is supported by the result of11,31.The presence of genetic variability is fundamental to crop breeding programs focused on developing new varieties with enhanced yield potential and consistent performance under varying climatic conditions. This genetic diversity provides the essential raw material for selecting and improving traits to adapt crops to diverse environments and achieve stable productivity32. The substantial proportion of variance elucidated by the first axis of the AMMI model (99.5%) underscores its capacity to encapsulate much of the complexity inherent in the G × E interaction, thereby facilitating the identification of patterns regarding the stability and adaptability of genotypes. This insight is crucial for breeding programs focused on selecting genotypes that achieve both high yields and consistent performance across diverse environments. Ultimately, AMMI analysis enhances the understanding and visualization of these interactions, enabling more informed selection decisions.

The AMMI model is widely used to analyze genotype-by-environment interactions, particularly in assessing seed yield. This robust approach is especially valuable in multi-environment trials as it aids in identifying genotypes that are both stable and high-yielding across diverse conditions14,34. In contrast, the Y-coordinate reflects the interaction effects, specifically the scores of the first interaction principal component. GGE biplot visualization tool

Multi-environment trials in plant breeding are carried out to assess how genotypes perform across various environmental conditions. The GGE biplot serves as an effective graphical method to visualize and evaluate genotype performance across different environments38.

Which-won-where

The "Which-won-where" analysis is a method from GGE biplot analysis that identifies the top-performing genotype for each environment in multi-environment trials. This is represented as a polygon in the biplot, where the vertices correspond to genotypes that excel in specific sectors. Rays extend from the origin, creating sectors linked to the environments where each vertex genotype performs best39. This helps in selecting genotypes that excel in particular environments. This approach allows researchers and breeders to visually interpret and understand genotype by environment interactions clearly by showing which genotype “won” (i.e., yielded best) in which environmental context, improving genotype selection decisions for breeding or cultivation. The polygon is formed by connecting the points (genotypes) furthest from the origin, and the environments are placed in sectors delineated by rays’ perpendicular to polygon sides. The genotype at each vertex is best for the environments in its sector.

In this study, the application of GGE biplot analysis to dissect the genotype and genotype-by-environment interaction demonstrated that the first two principal components, IPCA1 and IPCA2, cumulatively explained 99.94% of the total variation in sweet lupine seed yield. Specifically, IPCA1 accounted for 86.63%, while IPCA2 contributed 13.31% of the observed variability (Fig. 3). The implication of the study is that the GGE biplot analysis effectively captured almost all the variation (99.94%) in sweet lupine seed yield through the first two principal components, IPCA1 and IPCA2, indicating a very strong representation of genotype and genotype-by-environment interactions. This means that the major sources of variability influencing seed yield are well explained by these components, signifying that genotype performance and its interaction with environment can be reliably assessed and visualized. Practically, this implies that breeders and researchers can confidently use these components to identify genotypes that perform best in specific environments, understand stability across environments, and make informed decisions on variety selection for breeding programs. The high percentage of explained variation also suggests that genotype evaluation and recommendation based on this model will be robust, allowing identification of genotypes with both high yield potential and stability over diverse environmental conditions.

In this study, the GGE biplot was segmented into five distinct sectors, each representing a mega-environment within the biplot. The genotypes located at the vertices of these sectors were identified as the superior, or "winning," genotypes for their respective mega-environments. In contrast, sweet lupine genotypes that fell into sectors devoid of any associated environments were deemed poorly adapted across all tested conditions and correspondingly exhibited lower yields40. Notably, vertex genotypes may represent the best or worst performers across some or all environments. Specifically, the vertex genotypes G2 and G3 emerged as the highest-yielding within all environments encompassed by their sectors, highlighting their superior adaptation to those conditions (Fig. 3). This finding underscores the critical role of genotype-by-environment (G × E) interaction in explaining how different narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties vary in adaptation and performance across diverse environments. The identification of vertex genotypes as winners in respective mega-environments aligns with previous studies of11,15.

Evaluation of the different test environments

Test environments in plant breeding are evaluated on their capacity to differentiate between genotypes and how well they represent the target population of environments41. In a GGE biplot, principal component analysis is used to graphically represent both genotypes and environments in the same plot, where the angle between environment vectors reveals the correlation between environments: an acute angle indicates a positive correlation meaning similar effects on genotype performance, a right angle indicates no correlation42 suggesting independent genotype responses, and an obtuse angle indicates a negative correlation implying opposite effects on genotype performance; this angular relationship facilitates visualization of how test environments relate in terms of genotype response, aiding interpretation of genotype by environment interaction and identification of mega-environments for targeted breeding.

An environment that creates a small angle between its vector and the average environment coordinate (AEC) axis in a GGE biplot is regarded as more representative of the overall set of test environments38,43. This indicates that its genotype performance closely matches the average environmental response, making it an effective proxy for the target mega-environment. Such representativeness allows the environment to reliably indicate genotype differences across other test sites, which is essential for selection and breeding decisions.

The vector view of the GGE biplot visually summarizes the relationships among different test environments and highlights which are most representative. In this study, Environment One (E1) and Environment Two (E2) were identified as the most representative environments because their vectors formed a small angle with each other (Fig. 4), indicating a strong positive correlation. This implies that these environments have similar effects on genotype performance and provide reliable information about genotype behavior. Such representative environments are critical in breeding trials as they effectively reflect the overall target environment, thereby guiding breeders in selecting genotypes that will perform well across similar conditions. This observation aligns with results reported in earlier research15.

Evaluating and positioning genotypes based on an ideal genotype benchmark

Genotype ranking is the process of evaluating and selecting the best crop varieties (genotypes) by comparing their performance and stability to a theoretical "ideal genotype." This is frequently done using GGE biplot analysis in multi-environment trials, which provides a quantitative and visual way for breeders to identify stable, high-yielding options38,44. The Average Environment Axis (AEA) in GGE biplot analysis is a line that extends from the origin of the biplot to the average coordinates of all environments, determined by their first and second principal component scores. This axis serves as a combined indicator of both mean performance and stability across environments, providing a benchmark for ranking environments in relation to an ideal one that excels in both discriminating ability and representativeness for genotype evaluation45. The ideal environment is situated near the center of concentric circles aligned with the AEA, and environments that are close to the AEA and form small angles are deemed more representative and effective for selecting stable, high-performing genotypes. The AEA plays a vital role in visualizing and ranking environments in multi-environment trials, helping breeders identify environments that best differentiate genotypes while accurately reflecting the desired growing conditions46. This reflects the relationship between genotype placement on the GGE biplot and mean seed yield performance, helping to identify genotypes with superior yield characteristics. Accordingly G3 (Probor) > G2 (Vitabor) > G4 (Sanabor) > G5 (Welela) > G1 (Bora). The results of this study (Fig. 5) reveal that genotype G3 (Probor) emerges as the most ideal, owing to its superior average seed yield combined with remarkable stability across diverse environments. Additionally, genotypes like G2 (Vitabor), situated near the ideal genotype, also display commendable yield performance and stability. Genotypes positioned closer to the origin of the average environment axis exhibit greater stability than those near the axis’s extremes; consequently, G1 was classified as a low-yielding genotype. These findings, derived from the AEA approach, carry substantial implications for narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties breeding, as focusing on genotypes that demonstrate both high productivity and stability can foster sustainable farming practices, enhance food security by improving protein crop sources, and bolster resilience against environmental stresses. Previous studies4,47 have reported similar conclusions, emphasizing that identifying an ideal genotype facilitates the selection of varieties that perform consistently across diverse environments, thereby enhancing sweet lupine yields. This approach also contributes to environmentally sustainable agricultural practices and supports future advancements in crop development.

Seed yield stability analysis in narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties

A primary aim of plant breeding programs is the identification and selection of genotypes that combine superior seed yield with robust stability, ensuring consistent performance across varied environments. Environmental factors frequently explain a larger proportion of yield variability in narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties than genetic differences alone, underscoring the imperative to select genotypes that exhibit both superior yield capacity and resilience across diverse conditions48,49. Stable, high-yielding genotypes mitigate farmers’ risk by ensuring more dependable seed production despite environmental fluctuations. Seed yield stability is shaped by a complex interplay among genotype-specific traits, environmental influences, and their interaction effects, which are pivotal when recommending cultivars that, deliver consistent performance and bolster food security. This integrative approach advances breeding programs focused on developing varieties that harmonize high yield potential with adaptability to variable environments, enhancing overall production reliability and sustainability. In the present study, yield stability was quantified using diverse stability metrics, including the AMMI Stability Value (ASV), Genotype Selection Index (GSI), and Cultivar Superiority Measure (Pi), as detailed in (Table 2). The analysis of AMMI stability values is a crucial technique in crop improvement programs for assessing the stability and adaptability of genotypes across various environments50. Genotype selection index analysis is a sophisticated statistical methodology employed in agricultural research to proficiently identify and select outstanding genotypes based on multiple traits concurrently51. This analysis is pivotal for ensuring both adaptation and stability in crops, as it amalgamates various agronomic and yield-related characteristics into a singular index that encapsulates the overall genetic potential of the genotypes. Lower values across these parameters signify greater stability among narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties, reflecting enhanced adaptability and consistent performance across varying environments. These indices collectively enable robust discrimination of genotypes that maintain yield stability, facilitating breeders’ efforts to identify cultivars with optimal yield persistence under environmental fluctuations. Moreover, the modern approaches like AMMI and GGE biplot models combine features to provide comprehensive stability indices, aiding in the identification of genotypes with both high yield and stability43,52. The ideal genotypes for selection criteria are those that combine both high mean yield and high stability. However, stability should not be the only parameter for selection53. Regarding mean seed yield, the genotypes were ranked as G3 > G2 > G4 > G5 > G1. When considering mean seed yield and cultivar superiority indices, genotypes G2 and G3 emerged as the most stable and superior performers compared to the others. The maximum seed yield of narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties in this study (0.87 kg/plot) was obtained from genotype G3, whereas the minimum yield (0.68 kg/plot) was from G1 (Table 3).

Conclusion

The study on genotype-by-environment interactions and seed yield stability of narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties using AMMI and GGE biplot analyses provides valuable insights into the crop’s adaptation and performance across various environments. It confirms that genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI) significantly affects seed yield, highlighting its important implications for the stability and adaptability of genotypes. The AMMI and GGE biplot analyses identified genotype G2 as the most stable and consistently high-yielding across the tested environments, making it ideal for broad adaptation. In contrast, genotype G3 demonstrated a high yield potential but with less stability across environments, suggesting its best use may be in more specific or favorable conditions. The cultivation of these stable and high-performing narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties has the potential to enhance agricultural sustainability and improve food security in the study area as well as in other similar environments within the region. However, the findings of this study are specific to the environmental conditions of the Awi administrative zone in the Amhara region and may not be directly applicable to other regions with different climatic or soil characteristics, which limits the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, socioeconomic factors that could influence the adoption of these genotypes were not considered. Future research should address these limitations and evaluate the broader applicability of the identified genotypes across diverse agricultural settings. These findings underscore the importance of selecting genotypes with both high yield and stability to optimize narrow-leaf lupin sweet varieties production as a vital legume crop for sustainable farming systems.
