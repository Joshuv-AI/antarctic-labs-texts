Aging-related skeletal muscle loss on the incidence of chronic kidney disease in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease
Authors: Aryoung Kim, Danbee Kang, Sung Chul Choi, Dong Hyun Sinn, Hye Ryoun Jang, Geum-Youn Gwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Sarcopenia independently increased the risk of CKD development in MASLD.•Progressive muscle loss over time was associated with a dose-dependent increase in CKD risk in MASLD.•The association between sarcopenia and incident CKD was the strongest in MASLD with advanced fibrosis.•This study is the first to demonstrate that both sarcopenia and progressive muscle loss are independently linked to new-onset CKD in MASLD, highlighting the importance of sarcopenia screening and muscle preservation.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease (CKD), Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), Sarcopenia

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is a chronic liver disease characterized by hepatic fat accumulation in the presence of metabolic dysfunction [1]. Historically, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) was defined as hepatic steatosis in the absence of significant alcohol consumption or other secondary causes [2]. However, this exclusion-based definition was criticized for insufficiently reflecting the central role of metabolic dysfunction in disease pathogenesis. In 2020, metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) was proposed as an inclusion-based definition requiring hepatic steatosis with metabolic risk factors, regardless of alcohol intake [3]. The terminology subsequently evolved to MASLD to address concerns regarding etiologic heterogeneity and the stigmatizing term “fatty,” while maintaining a focus on metabolic dysfunction and excluding significant alcohol consumption [1]. Clinically, MASLD is associated with hepatic and extrahepatic complications and contributes to substantial morbidity and mortality [[4], [5], [6]]. With its rising global prevalence, MASLD has today become a significant public health concern [[5], [6], [7]].

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is another major health issue owing to its high complication rates, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality [8,9]. Growing evidence indicates that MASLD increases the risk of CKD development [10,11]; however, the underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. Identifying aging-related and potentially modifiable determinants of CKD risk in individuals with MASLD is therefore essential for improved risk stratification and targeted preventive strategies.

Sarcopenia, characterized by the age-related loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and physical performance, is a key aging phenotype and an independent risk factor for adverse health outcomes, including frailty and mortality [[12], [13], [14]]. As the global population ages, sarcopenia has gained increasing attention in relation to hepatic outcomes, such as liver fibrosis and mortality in MASLD [15,16]. However, its association with extrahepatic conditions, particularly CKD, remains largely unknown.

To the best of our knowledge, no study till date has specifically examined whether sarcopenia increases the risk of CKD in patients with MASLD. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether sarcopenia increases the risk of CKD in patients with MASLD. Additionally, we aimed to determine whether a progressive decline in muscle mass is associated with an elevated CKD risk, providing insights into the role of age-related muscle loss in CKD development among individuals with MASLD.

Methods

Study population

We performed a retrospective cohort study involving individuals aged ≥18 years who underwent comprehensive health screening at the Samsung Medical Center Health Promotion Center in Seoul, South Korea, between January 1, 2002, and December 31, 2020 (Fig. 1). To investigate the long-term relationship between sarcopenia and the development of CKD in the context of MASLD, we focused on participants with MASLD who had completed at least two screening sessions, which included a renal function test, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), and abdominal ultrasound (US) (n = 24,453). We excluded 2,228 participants at baseline according to the following criteria: estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 mL/min/1.73 m² (n = 429), proteinuria (n = 107), history of chronic hepatitis B (n = 871), chronic hepatitis C (n = 129), liver cirrhosis (n = 93), and a history of cancer (n = 878). After applying these exclusion criteria, 22,225 participants remained eligible. Additionally, 2,358 participants were further excluded owing to missing data on alcohol consumption (n = 704), body mass index (BMI) (n = 2), waist circumference (WC) (n = 1,225), blood pressure (BP) (n = 3), lipid profile (n = 269), or HbA1c levels (n = 681). The final sample size analyzed comprised 19,867 participants. The Institutional Review Board of the Samsung Medical Center approved this study (Approval no. 2025-03-003) and waived the need for informed consent because we only used anonymized data routinely collected during health screening visits. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Flow chart of study participants.

MASLD definition and severity

Participants were diagnosed with MASLD based on the 2023 multi-society Delphi consensus statement [1]. MASLD was defined by the presence of hepatic steatosis observed on US in combination with at least one of the following five cardiometabolic risk factors: (1) BMI ≥23 kg/m2 or WC >94 or >80 cm for males and females, respectively; (2) fasting glucose levels ≥100 mg/dL or hemoglobin A1c ≥5.7% or type 2 diabetes or treatment for type 2 diabetes; (3) blood pressure ≥130/85 mmHg or specific antihypertensive drug treatment; (4) plasma triglycerides ≥150 mg/dL or specific lipid lowering treatment; (5) plasma high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ≤40 mg/dL for males and ≤50 mg/dL for females or specific lipid lowering treatment [1].

Significant alcohol intake was strictly excluded to ensure a refined MASLD population. Alcohol consumption was assessed using standardized, self-administered questionnaires, and increased intake was defined—in accordance with MASLD diagnostic criteria—as an average daily consumption of >20 g for females and >30 g for males [1].

The severity of MASLD was determined by calculating the fibrosis-4 (FIB-4) score as follows: age (years) × aspartate aminotransferase (AST; U/L) / (platelet count [109/L] × alanine aminotransferase [ALT; U/L]1/2). Participants were categorized into two groups based on the FIB-4 score: those with a low likelihood of liver fibrosis (FIB-4 < 1.3) and those with an intermediate-to-high likelihood of liver fibrosis (FIB-4 ≥ 1.3) [17].

Assessment of sarcopenia and changes in skeletal muscle mass

Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM), the sum of the muscle mass of both arms and legs, is a commonly used index to assess the skeletal muscle mass [18]. BIA was performed using a multifrequency bioelectrical impedance device according to the manufacturer’s instructions (InBody 720; InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) to determine ASM at each health screening visit. The measurements were taken after an overnight fast (including water) of at least 8 h to enhance accuracy [14].

Sarcopenia was assessed using the skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), calculated as the ASM divided by body weight (BW) (kg) and expressed as a percentage [ASM/weight (%)] [19]. Sarcopenia was defined as an SMI (%) <2 standard deviations below the sex-specific mean for healthy young adults in the Korean population: <29.0% in men and <22.9% in women [19]. For sensitivity analysis, an alternative definition from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Sarcopenia Project was applied [20]. The sarcopenia index (SI) was calculated as total ASM (kg) divided by BMI (kg/m2), with sarcopenia defined as SI < 0.789 in men and <0.521 in women [20].

The changes in skeletal muscle mass were evaluated by calculating the change in SMI and SI. The change in SMI was defined as △ASM/weight (%) (follow-up–baseline), and the change in SI was defined as △ASM/BMI (follow-up–baseline) [21].

Definition of CKD

CKD was defined as an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2, calculated using the CKD-Epidemiology Collaboration equations [22].

Clinical and biochemical evaluations

At each visit, participants completed standardized self-administered questionnaires to provide details regarding demography, smoking history, medical conditions, and medication usage. The Smoking status was categorized as either never smoker or ever smoker, and physical activity was classified as <3 times per week or ≥3 times per week.

Serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol, HDL-C, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were measured using enzymatic colorimetric methods, while glucose levels were quantified using the hexokinase/glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase technique. Liver enzyme levels, including AST, ALT, and gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), were determined using the International Federation of Clinical Chemistry guidelines. To ensure high-quality results, the Department of Laboratory Medicine and Genetics at the Samsung Medical Center participates in external proficiency testing programs from recognized organizations, such as the Korean Association of Quality Assurance for Clinical Laboratories, the Asian Network of Clinical Laboratory Standardization, and the College of American Pathologists.

Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, waist circumference, and sitting blood pressure, were conducted by trained nursing staff. Blood pressure was recorded once in a seated position following a 5-minute rest period, utilizing an attended automated measurement system, either the Eagle 1000 (GE Medical Systems IT Inc., Milwaukee, WI) or the Dinamap Procare 300 (GE Medical Systems IT Inc.). According to the MASLD diagnostic criteria established by the Delphi consensus, elevated blood pressure is characterized by a systolic blood pressure of ≥130 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure of ≥85 mmHg, or the current administration of specific antihypertensive medication [1].

Statistical analysis

Values are presented as median (interquartile range) or frequency (percentage). Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, as they did not follow a normal distribution based on the Shapiro–Wilk or Anderson–Darling normality tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

The primary endpoint of this study was the incidence of CKD. Participants were followed from the baseline visit until the date of CKD diagnosis or their last recorded visit.

For risk analysis, Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident CKD in relation to baseline sarcopenia and progressive skeletal muscle mass change in MASLD. The multivariable model was adjusted for age (≥50 or <50 years), sex, baseline eGFR (mL/min/1.73 m2), BW, smoking status (never, ever, or missing), physical activity (<3 times per week, ≥3 times per week, or missing), high BMI or WC, high glucose, high BP, high TG, and low HDL-C. In the FIB-4 analysis, models were not adjusted for age because age is included in the FIB-4 calculation.

Stratified analyses were conducted to assess whether the association between sarcopenia and incident CKD in MASLD varied across predefined subgroups based on age (<50 or ≥50 years), sex, smoking status (never or ever), physical activity (<3 or ≥3 times per week), high BMI or WC, high glucose, high BP, high TG, and low HDL-C. Since no CKD events occurred in the high BMI or WC group, this group was excluded from the subgroup analysis.

Additionally, penalized smoothing splines with an appropriate degree of freedom were used to flexibly model the dose-response relationship between changes in skeletal muscle mass and the incidence of CKD in patients with MASLD. The akaike information criterion was used to choose an optimal degree of freedom.

The association between longitudinal changes in skeletal muscle mass and CKD incidence was evaluated using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables. Specifically, CKD incidence was compared between participants with and without skeletal muscle loss, as defined by SMI and SI, and absolute changes in SMI (%) and SI were compared between participants with and without incident CKD.

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.3 (Vienna, Austria; http://www.R-project.org/). All reported p-values were two-tailed, with p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The mean (standard deviation) age of the study participants was 50.4 (9.0) years. The baseline prevalence of sarcopenia in MASLD was 7.8% (n = 1,549; Table 1). When sarcopenia was defined using the SI, the prevalence was 5.7% (Supplementary Table S1). Compared to participants without sarcopenia, those with sarcopenia in MASLD were more likely to be males, smokers, and metabolically unfavorable. Baseline eGFR was higher in participants with sarcopenia compared to those without sarcopenia (median 90.1 vs. 86.8 ml/min/1.73 m2, P < 0.001). Risk of CKD incidence by sarcopenia in MASLD

During 124,236 person-years of follow-up (median, 5.6; range, 2.8–9.4), 993 participants developed CKD, with an overall incidence rate of 8.0 cases per 1000 person-years. The incidence rate of CKD in participants with and without sarcopenia were 9.6 and 7.9 per 1000 person-years, respectively (Table 2). The cumulative incidence of CKD was consistently higher in participants with sarcopenia over the entire follow-up period compared to participants without sarcopenia. The adjusted HR (aHR) for incident CKD comparing participants with sarcopenia to those without sarcopenia was 1.41 (95% CI, 1.12–1.77). To confirm the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using an alternative definition of sarcopenia. Sarcopenia defined by SI also showed a significant association with CKD incidence in MASLD (Supplementary Table S2). In subgroup analyses, the positive association between sarcopenia and CKD incidence in patients with MASLD was consistent across all the analyzed subgroups (Fig. 2). Notably, sarcopenia was associated with an increased risk of CKD in both age groups (≤50 and >50 years), however, the magnitude of association appeared more pronounced in the older age group (aHR 1.55, 95% CI 1.18–2.04).

Risk of CKD incidence by skeletal muscle mass changes in MASLD

We further examined the longitudinal impact of skeletal muscle mass loss on CKD incidence in patients with MASLD. After adjusting for confounding factors, participants with loss of muscle mass showed an increased risk of incident CKD, indicating a dose-dependent relationship (Fig. 3). Compared to those with preserved or increased skeletal muscle mass, participants experiencing muscle mass loss had a significantly higher risk of incident CKD. The aHRs for CKD incidence were 1.13 (95% CI, 1.00–1.29) when skeletal muscle mass change was measured by both SMI (%) (△ASM/weight, %) and SI (△ASM/BMI) (Table 3). Furthermore, participants with decreased SMI (%) during follow-up showed a significantly higher incidence of CKD compared with those with preserved or increased SMI (%) (57.1% vs. 51.4%, P < 0.001). Additionally, the absolute change in SMI (%) (△ASM/BW, %) differed significantly between participants with and without incident CKD (−0.2 [−0.9 to 0.5] vs. −0.0 [−0.8 to 0.8], P < 0.001), consistent with the results using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Comparable results were obtained when analyses were performed using SI instead of SMI (%).

Risk of CKD incidence by sarcopenia and MASLD severity

Compared to MASLD without sarcopenia, the adjusted HRs for incident CKD in participants with MASLD and FIB-4 scores <1.3 and ≥1.3 were 1.16 (95% CI, 0.85–1.59) and 2.20 (95% CI, 1.57–2.94), respectively (Table 4). These results were consistent when sarcopenia was defined using the SI, with higher FIB-4 scores being associated with an increased risk of incident CKD.

Discussion

In this large-scale longitudinal cohort study, the risk of developing CKD was significantly higher in participants with sarcopenia than in those without sarcopenia in MASLD. This association remained consistent in all predefined subgroups and sensitivity analyses. Furthermore, the progressive loss of muscle over time was associated with an increased risk of CKD in patients with MASLD in a dose-dependent manner. The relationship between sarcopenia and incident CKD was more pronounced in participants with MASLD with advanced fibrosis. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate that both sarcopenia and progressive muscle loss are independently associated with new-onset CKD in MASLD, highlighting the critical need to screen for sarcopenia and prevent muscle loss in patients with MASLD.

MASLD is well-known to be closely associated with the development of CKD [[23], [24], [25]]. A recent meta-analysis of 13 observational longitudinal studies confirmed that NAFLD is significantly associated with a 1.45-fold increased long-term risk of incident Stage 3 CKD (defined as an eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2) [10]. Moreover, two longitudinal studies utilizing data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) have demonstrated that sarcopenia is linked to new-onset CKD [26] as well as a rapid decline in kidney function or progression to CKD [27]. However, despite the growing evidence linking sarcopenia to CKD in other populations, research specifically examining its role in CKD development in patients with MASLD is lacking. Our study addresses this gap and confirms the association between sarcopenia and CKD in patients with MASLD and extends these findings to subgroups, providing new insights into the relationship between sarcopenia and CKD in this context.

To more clearly evaluate the causal impact of muscle mass loss on CKD development, we assessed the risk of CKD incidence based on the changes in muscle mass over the follow-up period. Our findings showed that a decline in muscle mass was associated with an increased risk of CKD. This finding is consistent with that of a previous prospective cohort study including 1,272 patients with type 2 diabetes, which reported that individuals with a greater reduction in skeletal muscle mass over time had a significantly higher risk of CKD progression compared to those with minimal changes [28]. These results emphasize the critical role of muscle mass loss in CKD development, highlighting the importance of preventing the reduction of muscle mass in patients with MASLD to reduce the CKD risk.

Several plausible mechanisms could explain the association between sarcopenia and CKD incidence, including inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and vitamin D deficiency [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Given that the skeletal muscles play a crucial role in insulin-mediated glucose metabolism, their reduction may exacerbate insulin resistance, ultimately leading to glomerular dysfunction and CKD progression [31]. Additionally, insulin resistance, atherogenic dyslipidemia, inflammation, and vitamin D deficiency, all of which are influenced by altered liver–kidney interactions in MASLD, are believed to contribute to CKD development [[32], [33], [34]]. Although further research is needed to fully elucidate the precise mechanisms, these shared factors suggest that sarcopenia and MASLD may act synergistically in promoting CKD development.

The incidence of sarcopenia and progressive skeletal muscle loss increases with advancing age, partly through metabolic and inflammatory pathways, and age-related decline in skeletal muscle mass may contribute to increased susceptibility to CKD in older adults via similar mechanisms [[27], [28], [29], [30],35,36]. Consistent with this biological rationale, in our cohort, the association between sarcopenia and incident CKD was observed across all age groups, but the effect was more pronounced in participants aged >50 years, suggesting that aging may amplify the impact of low muscle mass on renal outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring and preserving muscle mass, particularly in older adults with MASLD, as a potential strategy to mitigate CKD risk.

Advanced fibrosis in MASLD, likely caused by changes in portal and splanchnic vasoregulation that trigger a hepatorenal reflex, with subclinical portal hypertension further exacerbating renal dysfunction, is a well-established risk factor for CKD development [37]. Additionally, sarcopenia is closely linked to advanced fibrosis in MASLD, suggesting that muscle loss may be both a consequence of and a contributor to disease progression [19,38]. Notably, a recent cohort study reported high all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in patients with coexisting MASLD, sarcopenia, and advanced fibrosis, further emphasizing the clinical significance of these overlapping conditions [39]. In the present study, the risk of CKD incidence was particularly elevated in patients experiencing sarcopenia and MASLD with advanced fibrosis. These findings suggest that assessing the severity of fibrosis in patients with MASLD and sarcopenia may help with CKD risk stratification and early intervention.

Our analysis has several strengths. First, our study is based on a large sample size and has a longitudinal design, which is less prone to bias compared with other observational studies. Second, we not only examined the presence of muscle loss but also investigated the causal impact of the changes in muscle mass over time on CKD incidence, offering a more comprehensive understanding of this relationship. Third, sensitivity analyses using various definitions and subgroup analyses further reinforced the robustness of our findings. Most importantly, this is the first study to explore the impact of sarcopenia on extrahepatic outcomes in patients with MASLD, extending our understanding beyond hepatic complications such as liver fibrosis and mortality.

However, some limitations need to be considered in the interpretation of our findings. First, we assessed only the skeletal muscle mass, although muscle loss does not always correlate with a decline in muscle function [40]. Further research incorporating muscle function assessments, such as the 6-min walk test, are needed. In addition, we measured the ASM using BIA. While BIA is non-invasive, affordable, and simple to perform, its accuracy is lower than that of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry. Next, hepatic steatosis was not diagnosed by analyzing liver biopsy specimens. Instead, we used US, which is widely recognized for its high accuracy and is recommended by the Delphi consensus for diagnosing SLD [1,41]. However, classification bias may still exist owing to potential measurement errors with US 41]. Fourth, CKD was defined based on creatinine-based eGFR. Because serum creatinine is influenced by muscle mass, the association between low muscle mass and renal function may be underestimated [42]. Additionally, CKD and sarcopenia are known to interact bidirectionally, with CKD potentially accelerating skeletal muscle loss [43,44]. While participants with CKD at baseline were excluded from the study population and longitudinal analyses were performed to examine the association between changes in skeletal muscle mass and the subsequent development of CKD, the potential effect of CKD progression on the development or worsening of sarcopenia might not be fully avoided, highlighting the need for future studies to evaluate these bidirectional interactions. Finally, given that our cohort is based on a Korean health screening population, caution is needed when generalizing these findings to other racial or ethnic groups and different healthcare settings.

In summary, sarcopenia was independently associated with the development of CKD in MASLD, with longitudinal muscle loss increasing the risk of CKD. This association was more pronounced in those with advanced fibrosis. These findings suggest that routine assessment of muscle mass and strategies aimed at preventing age-related muscle decline may help identify individuals with MASLD who are at increased risk of CKD and potentially delay disease onset.
