Epidemiology and prognosis of childhood lupus nephritis in the Afro-descendant population of the French Overseas Departments of America
Authors: Chloé Michau, Lindsay Osei, Narcisse Elenga, Frederique Delion, Christophe Deligny, Benoit Suzon, Abdelmajid Bourassi, Olivia Boyer, Theresa Kwon, Cedric Aglae, Marion Rabant, Gwenaelle Roussey, Arthur Felix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionLupus nephritis (LN) is a severe manifestation of pediatric systemic lupus erythematosus (pSLE). This study aims to describe the epidemiology, clinical, biological, histological features and prognosis of pediatric LN in the Afro-descendant (AD) population of the French Overseas Departments of America.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective and longitudinal multicenter study of pediatric LN patients from January 2000 to June 2024. Patients were identified from multiple sources including pediatricians’ registries, pathologist’s registries for kidney biopsies, national registry for rare diseases and referral centers. Data were collected from medical records. Diagnostic criteria for pSLE kidney impairment were based on international classifications.ResultsTwenty-seven patients with pediatric LN were included. The mean age at diagnosis was 11.3 years (range: 6–17) and mean follow-up duration was 7.5 years (range: 0.3–27). Kidney involvement (75% of ISN/RPS class III/IV) was present at pSLE diagnosis in 55% of patients. Kidney flares occurred more frequently in patients under 12 years of age at diagnosis, with an average of 1.7 flares per patient (p = 0.03). The mean number of background therapy per patient during follow up was 2.5 (1–5) with a majority (n = 15, 55%) receiving glucocorticoids and mycophenolate mofetil as first line. Six patients (22%) had an eGFR below 60 ml/min/1.73m2 at the last follow-up (2 patients on dialysis, 2 had kidney transplants).ConclusionThis study represents the largest cohort of pediatric LN in AD population. Younger patients exhibited more frequent kidney flares, particularly within the first two years of diagnosis. Overall outcomes in pediatric LN showed a higher rate of dialysis and kidney failure than in Caucasian series.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05058-x.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Pediatric lupus nephritis, Afro-Caribbean, Afro-descendant, Long-term prognosis

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematous (SLE) is an auto-immune disease characterized by antibodies directed against self-antigens at the origin of tissue damage in numerous organs. The prevalence of SLE is up to 43/100.000 people worldwide [1] (41/100.000 in France [2]). SLE and pediatric SLE (pSLE) are more prevalent in the Afro-Caribbean population (up to 126.7/100.000) [3] in the French Overseas Department of America (FODAs: Martinique, Guadeloupe, French Guiana, Saint-Martin, Saint-Barthelemy, combined population 300.000 children). This population of the FODAs is mostly (> 90%) of Afro-Descendant (AD) origin [4].

The SLE disease begins in childhood in 2–20% of cases, depending on the series [2, 5]. Childhood-onset forms are considered to be more severe than adult-onset disease, in particular due to a higher incidence of severe kidney, hematological and neuropsychiatric involvement and greater number of flares and infections [6, 7]. Lupus nephritis (LN) is a severe manifestation of SLE associated with morbidity and mortality [8]. Among children with pSLE 35 to 55% will develop lupus nephritis (LN) [9–12], with worse outcomes than in adult LN [13, 14]. LN tends to be more frequent and more aggressive in AD population than in Caucasian in European and North America studies [10, 15, 16]. However, there are inequities in the level and access to health in the North American populations where Afro-Americans may experience socioeconomic disparities that negatively impact access to care [17]. This does not apply to the AD population of the FODAs, as these territories benefit from the same free and universal healthcare system as mainland France [18] with three university hospitals and two regional competence centers for pediatric autoimmune diseases approved by the French Ministry of Health. Two recent studies in the FODAs have shown a similar prognosis for the overall outcomes of pSLE in childhood and adult-onset LN [3, 19, 20].

The aim of this study was to describe the epidemiology of pediatric LN in patients from the FODAs and to describe the clinical, biological and histological features and to study the long-term prognosis of this population.

Methods

This is a retrospective multicenter observational study including patients under 18 years of age at diagnosis with LN from 01/01/2000 to 01/06/2024. This is an ancillary study of the FODA pediatric lupus cohort [3]. Briefly, the identification of patients was obtained from various sources: patients followed by a referring pediatrician in each FODA, registry of adult patients with pediatric onset followed by a reference specialist in each FODA, patients identified through pathologist’s registries for kidney biopsies and the national registry for rare disease (BAMARA). Patients were included if they were raised, residents of the FODA or neighboring Caribbean islands (St Lucia and Dominique) and followed and treated in the FODA after diagnosis, while patients present for a short period and followed in another center were excluded from the analysis. If a patient was referred to a mainland France center for specific treatment (mostly dialysis), the data were still collected.

Data collection was completed by consulting follow-up and hospitalization reports available in the patients’ medical files. Patients with less than 6 months of follow-up were included in clinical, biological and histological analyses but were excluded from the follow-up analyses. Histological data were centralized in two referral centers for pathology in mainland France (Necker and Kremlin Bicêtre hospitals). Kidney remission was defined according to French and European recommendations [21].

pSLE diagnosis was established clinically by the treating physicians. The 2019 EULAR/ACR classification criteria were applied retrospectively for standardized research classification and not as diagnostic criteria [22]. Lupus-related kidney impairment was defined according to the international classification (ISN/RPS) [23], and biopsies performed after 2018 were described following the revised classification of ISN/RPS [24]. Kidney involvement was defined homogeneously within the cohort [25]. Kidney flare was define as kidney involvement with active disease requiring more intensive treatment (glucocorticoid > 0.5 mg/kg/day change in treatment regimen) [25]. Because of the retrospective design, minor biological fluctuations that did not lead to therapeutic escalation were not classified as kidney flares. Acute kidney injury (AKJ) and Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) stages were defined according to KDIGO’s recommendations [26, 27]. Estimated glomerular Filtration rate (eGFR) was estimated in pediatric patients by Schwartz formula, and in adult patients by MDRDs formula.

Quantitative data are described as mean ± standard deviation or median and range, as appropriate. Qualitative data are described as numbers and percentages. Proportions were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate. Statistical tests were performed with Stata software. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate the probability of survival without kidney failure or a GFR below 60 ml/min. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Institutional Review Board of the University Hospital of Martinique approved the study under the number 2021/116. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents or legal guardians of all participants.

Results

Twenty-seven patients with LN and 46 LN flares were included in the final analysis. The flowchart is shown in the supplementary Fig. 1. The description of the clinical and biological characteristics of the population is shown in Table 1. The mean age at diagnosis of pSLE was 11.3 years (range: 6–17 years), and the mean age at the first occurrence of kidney involvement was 12.0 years (range: 6–17 years). The mean follow-up duration was 7.5 years (range: 0.3–27 years), with an average age at final follow-up of 18.5 years (range: 9–41). Two patients were excluded from the follow-up analyses because they were followed for less than 6 months. Follow-up reports were available for 13 patients (48%) beyond the age of 18 years old. All patients had positive ANA (> 1/320).

There were 15 patients (55%) with kidney involvement at pSLE diagnosis. For patients without an initial kidney involvement, the median time from diagnosis to the first kidney involvement was 1.8 years (range: 0.3–3.5 years). The clinical and biological descriptions of lupus nephritis and subsequent flares are shown in Table 2; no significant differences were observed, except for a higher rate of AKI during the initial kidney involvement. There were significantly more LN flares among those diagnosed with pSLE before the age of 12 years old (Table 3) (p = 0.03), with a flare rate of 30.1 per 100-Patients-years versus 14.5 per 100 Patients-years for those diagnosed after the age of 12 years old (p = 0.02). No significant differences were observed between patients with > 2 LN flares and those with ≤ 2 LN flares according to sex, other organ involvement, autoimmune specificity, treatment of the first kidney flare (meaning diagnosis of lupus nephritis), and class of the first kidney flare, except for age at onset (p = 0.02) (Table 3). The follow-up duration did not differ between patients with > 2 LN flares and those with ≤ 2 LN flares. Antiphospholipid antibody positivity was not statistically associated with having > 2 renal flares. Moreover, no thrombosis occurred in this group, whereas three thrombotic events, including one renal artery thrombosis, occurred in the ≤ 2 flare.

At the first LN flare, 24/27 patients (89%) had a kidney biopsy showing proliferative lesions in 18 patients (75%) (INS/RPS: III, IV, III + V, IV + V). Overall, 48 kidney biopsies were performed at pediatric age during the study (Fig. 1). Immunofluorescence data were available for 34 biopsies (80%); 25 (73%) showed a lupus full house nephropathy. In patients who underwent repeat biopsy within 2 years (41%: 11 patients), 55% had a change in LN histological class compared to the initial diagnosis of LN (Suppl Fig. 2). There was no significant difference in terms of age, sex, treatment or number of flares between patients with or without a class change.

Histological class of the patients following ISN RPS classification. 48 biopsies were performed. For three of these, the ISN/RPS class could not be determined for technical reasons (lack of glomerulus)

The different induction treatments for the first LN flare are summarized in Suppl Fig. 3. The mean number of maintenance therapy lines for LN disease was 2 (range: 1–3, Suppl Fig. 4). The mean number of background therapy lines among patients under the age of 12 at onset was not significantly higher (2.5 vs. 1.9 for the > 12 years old group; p = 0.14). Among the patients aged < 12 years at diagnosis who were treated with MMF and glucocorticoids following the initial flare (n = 8), 75% (n = 6) experienced a second flare, compared with 20% (n = 1) of 5 patients aged 12 years or older (p = 0.1) during follow up. In comparison, among patients aged under 12 years at diagnosis, only one patient (20%) had a subsequent flare following treatment with intravenous cyclophosphamide and glucocorticoids after the initial flare (n = 5).

The probability of survival without kidney failure or a GFR below 60 ml/min in our population is shown in Fig. 2. At the last follow-up, the mean eGFR in our cohort was 105 ml/min/1.73m2. During follow-up six patients had a CKD with eGFR below 60 ml/min/1.73m2 (Fig. 2). Among these 6 patients with poor kidney outcome, 4 were born in low-income countries (St Lucia and Dominique) and 2 were born in Western French Guiana. Four patients (15%) underwent dialysis for kidney failure. Despite this unfavorable outcome, there was no difference in the number of flares (p = 0.28), number of background therapies (p = 0.08), age at onset (p = 0.4), or sex (p = 0.14) between them and the rest of the cohort.

Discussion

This retrospective study included 27 patients with pediatric lupus nephritis between 2000 and June 2024. To our knowledge, this is the largest cohort of pediatric LN among Afro-Caribbean and AD children treated in a high-income country. Despite having the smallest pediatric population among the FODAs, most of these patients were followed in Martinique [18]. This may be attributed to the higher incidence of both pSLE and adult SLE in the Martinique population [2, 3].

The mean age at diagnosis of pSLE was 11.3 years; this is slightly younger than North American (CARRA) and UK cohorts (UK JSLE) but similar to South Africa’s cohort (PULSE), and South East Asian cohorts (SEA) [10, 28–30]. One reason might be that AD patients tend to have an earlier onset of pSLE than Caucasians [29]. Younger patients are also known to have more organ failure than others and poorer kidney outcomes [3]. In the present cohort, the patients under 12 years at diagnosis had more kidney flares than the others but did not have overall poorer outcomes. 75% of them who were treated with MMF as first-line therapy had a second kidney flare against 20% with IV cyclophosphamide. Subsequent flares were frequently observed in younger patients after initial MMF-based therapy. In contrast only 20% of patient above 12 years at diagnosis and treated with MMF as first line therapy experienced a second kidney flare. However, given the small sample size and non-randomised treatment allocation, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and considered as a hypothesis to be investigated further. A multi-target therapy approach for the treatment of LN nephritis can be discussed as a first option for these patients [31].

Among FODAs patients with pSLE, 55% had kidney involvement at diagnosis. In patients without initial kidney involvement, the median time from diagnosis to first kidney involvement was 1.8 years. This finding was consistent with previous studies; most patients with kidney involvement show the first signs of LN in the first three years after diagnosis of pSLE [13]. Therefore, clinicians must closely monitor clinical and biological signs of LN in the Afro-Caribbean population during the first three years of pSLE, with a quarterly assessment of proteinuria using the protein-to-creatinine ratio. Additional testing should be performed in the event of a systemic flare-up or the appearance of new symptoms, and prompt kidney biopsies should be performed when necessary.

The overall distribution of the histological findings was consistent with those reported in the literature, with the majority of LN lesions being proliferative in children [10, 32, 33]. Our proportion of proliferative glomerulonephritis at first kidney involvement was higher than in patients from CARRA and UK JSLE [10, 32] and similar to the PULSE, SEA and Mexico cohorts [28, 30, 33]. However, comparisons should be interpreted with caution as a substantial proportion of patients in the SEA cohort did not undergo kidney biopsy, reaching 58.2% in some countries [30] and 44% in the Mexican cohort [33]. This may have introduced selection bias based on biopsy and limited the comparability of histological class distributions across studies. None of the patients in our cohort had class VI LN at the time of initial kidney involvement, whereas class VI LN represented 7% of cases in the PULSE cohort [28]. This difference may be related to variations in biopsy access and timing, and in disease duration, between countries [28, 30, 33].

The overall prognosis for this pSLE cohort was identical to Western countries [3], but the patients showed a propensity for more adverse kidney outcomes than those observed in Caucasian series, as evidenced by the higher percentage of CKD stage 5 (n = 4; 15%) requiring dialysis during the follow-up period. This is in contrast to the results for other organ involvement in the pSLE cohort of FODAs, where we showed activity criteria and a symptomatology that was more florid at diagnosis but reversible with appropriate treatment [3]. Patients with poorer outcomes were from low-income countries (St Lucia, Dominica) where access to specialised care is limited. The percentage of patients receiving dialysis was higher than in previous cohorts from Western countries: 1% for CARRA, UK JSLE, and TORONTO, between 3.14 and 6.6 in Southeast Asian cohort but similar to low- or middle-income countries, with 15% for the PULSE cohort [9, 28–30]. This higher-than-expected percentage may also be due to our longer follow-up data than other cohorts with older patients at the end of follow-up [10, 28–30, 33]. In our cohort, 6 patients (22%) had an eGFR below 60 ml/min/1.73m2 at the last follow-up (2 on dialysis, 2 had kidney transplants, 2 had no kidney replacement therapy). Four of them came from low-income countries, and two came from Western French Guiana (on the border between French Guiana and Suriname, which cares for the isolated population of the Maroni River, which has difficulty accessing health care and a high level of illegal immigration from Surinam) [34]. The severity of the kidney damage observed in these patients may be partly explained by difficult access to initial care, a delay between the first signs and diagnosis, low-income, and precarious conditions as shown in previous studies [17, 18].

Our study has several strengths. The main strength of this study is that it is the first study to describe the clinical, biological phenotype and outcomes of paediatric LN in an Afro-Caribbean and AD population of the FODAs. Our study was multicentre with participation from all centres in the FODAs. The use of multiple sources to identify and enrol our patients ensured exhaustive identification of patients. The health organisation in the FODAs with tertiary paediatric centres in Martinique, Guadeloupe and French Guiana was likely to include all patients with pSLE and LN and ensure their follow-up. Therefore, we believe that the loss of patients and information in this study was minimal (the number of cases was also within the range predicted by epidemiology). We also had long follow-up data, with a mean follow-up of 7.5 years, with information available after the age of 18 years for 48% of them. In addition, the French healthcare system is universal and free, so the bias related to socioeconomic status and access to healthcare or therapeutics for AD populations should be less significant in our cohort. However, some health inequalities persist in French Guiana [35].

This study also had several limitations. It was retrospective and included a small sample size (27 patients and 48 episodes of LN); however, our cohort is the largest focused on pediatric LN in the Afro-Caribbean and AD population. Pediatric LN is a rare disease; the largest published cohorts are from CARRA, which had 216 LN patients from across North America, and a previous French cohort found 81 patients from 89 pediatric centers at French university hospitals [32]. Another limitation of our cohort was that the definition of a flare was based on a marked degree of severity, as opposed to a slight worsening of symptoms leading to a minor therapeutic adjustment. This may have reduced the number of flares per patient. As the histological classification system evolved during the study period and central biopsy review was not feasible, the activity and chronicity indices were not compared in order to minimise any bias related to the classification system. In addition, the socio-economic strength of the French healthcare system in the FODAs may limit the extrapolation of the data to other AD populations, especially in low-income countries or for those who face barriers to health care. Also, the incidence and epidemiological data in our study may have been underestimated due to the bias of retrospective studies of rare diseases over a long period of time. Finally, during our study time international LN classification was revisited, because of the multicentric characteristic of this study we could not perform a re-evaluation of the biopsies made before 2018. However, by not comparing the activity and chronicity indices, which represent the primary differences between the two classifications, we minimize biases when assessing biopsies performed before and after 2018.

Conclusion

This retrospective cohort of patients with LN followed in the FODAs is the largest cohort of Afro-Caribbean children and Afro-descendant children treated in a high-income country. Long-term kidney outcomes were characterised by a significant proportion of patients with CKD stages 3–5. Severe outcomes were mainly observed among children referred from neighbouring low-resource settings or remote areas. This raises the hypothesis that delayed access to specialised care may contribute to prognosis, but this should be confirmed in larger studies specifically designed to assess determinants of healthcare access.
