Physiological effects of putrescine and modified atmosphere packaging treatments on quality, phenolic compound and biochemical stability of cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.) fruit by suppressing respiration rate
Authors: Tansel Kaygısız
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the effects of putrescine (Put), modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), and their combination (Put + MAP) on the physicochemical and biochemical quality of fruits during postharvest storage. The fruits were stored for 30 days, and changes in weight loss, decay rate, respiration rate, organic acids, vitamin C, sugars, and phenolic compounds were examined throughout the storage period.ResultsSignificant quality losses were observed in the control group as the storage period progressed; by the end of the 30th day, weight loss and decay rate reached 7.91% and 6.69%. Conversely, the Put + MAP treatment was able to effectively suppress weight loss and decay, hence minimizing the deterioration of overall quality. Although respiration rate was high at the end of harvest, it significantly decreased towards the end of storage in the Put + MAP treatment, hence showing a substantial reduction in metabolic rates. Organic acids generally tended to decrease in concentration throughout storage; however, their levels were better maintained in the Put + MAP treatment than in the control. Citric acid content decreased to 544.74 mg 100 g⁻¹ in the control group at the end of 30 days, while it was maintained at 617.26 mg 100 g⁻¹ in the Put + MAP application. Similarly, vitamin C content decreased to 20.28 mg 100 g⁻¹ in the control group, while it was determined to be 30.83 mg 100 g⁻¹ in the Put + MAP group. Phenolic compounds were also affected by the treatments. Although phenolic acids tended to decrease in concentration throughout storage in the control treatment, their retention was significantly higher in fruits treated with putrescine, either alone or in combination with MAP. The combined Put + MAP treatment was therefore more effective in maintaining biochemical quality attributes and in slowing down the rate of postharvest senescence than the control.ConclusionAnalysis of variance showed that storage time and the “treatment × storage time” interaction were statistically significant for many parameters (mostly p ≤ 0.001). PCA, heat map, and correlation analyses revealed that Put + MAP treatment maintained the most balanced physiological profile and best preserved quality related components. In conclusion, the Put + MAP combination emerged as an effective preservation strategy that delays post-harvest quality loss.Keywords: Physalis peruviana L.; Putrescine; Packaging; Storage; Physiological profile; Bioactive components, Postharvest quality

Background

The cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.), a member of the Solanaceae family and native to South America, is an exotic fruit that is yellow-orange in color, round, 20–25 mm in diameter, and weighs approximately 4–5 g. Resembling a tomato but with a rich aroma profile that is sweet and sour, the fruit contains 150–300 small seeds [1–4]. During the ripening process, the papery calyx structure completely envelops the fruit, protecting it from insects, birds, and adverse weather conditions, thereby supporting its healthy development [5–7]. Today, the plant, which is cultivated across a wide geographical area from America to Australia, is known by different names such as “cape gooseberry,” “uchuva,” and “goldenberry” [8]. Unlike other species that grow naturally in Turkey, P. peruviana, which is produced mainly in the Söke Plain, has become an important variety due to its economic value and increasing popularity [9]. The chemical composition, nutritional, and functional properties of the increasingly popular cape gooseberry have been examined in numerous studies.

A tropical fruit rich in vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds, cape gooseberry has a wide range of potential uses in the food, baking, and beverage industries in forms such as fresh consumption, drying, and fruit juice [10]. Its approximately 80% water content and acidic pH value in the range of 3.6–4.1 make the fruit more susceptible to fungal spoilage than bacterial spoilage [5, 11, 12]. Containing an average of 53 kcal of energy per 100 g, the fruit is rich in carbohydrates and exhibits diabetic properties due to its fructose content [12, 13]. 90% of the fruit’s 2% fat content is stored in the seeds [14], and more than 84% of this fat consists of unsaturated fatty acids, primarily linoleic acid, which is critical for cardiovascular health and hypertension control [3, 5]. In addition, thanks to phytosterols such as campesterol and Δ5-avenasterol, it provides total and LDL cholesterol-lowering, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial effects [5, 13].

Cape gooseberry, which is a 4.9% source of fiber thanks to its gut-regulating pectin [3], is a powerful antioxidant source with a vitamin C content of 46 mg 100 g− 1, close to the values of oranges and strawberries [5, 10, 15]. Vitamin E (mainly γ- and α-tocopherol), which protects the cell membrane against lipid peroxidation [5] and vitamin K1, which is necessary for blood clotting, are found in much higher amounts in the pulp and peel oil of the fruit compared to seed oil [13, 14]. When examining the mineral profile, it is rich in potassium (347 mg 100 g− 1), phosphorus, magnesium, and iron, but poor in calcium [16, 17] and also provides protection against oxidative damage with its zinc content [18]. The fruit, which contains approximately 40 mg 100 g− 1 of phenolic compounds [5], is also an important source of carotenoids (total 2.52 mg 100 g− 1), which are known to reduce cancer risk and increase with ripening [3, 4]. In addition, 17 types of withanolides and physalin compounds with antimicrobial, liver-protective and immune-modulating effects contribute to the fruit’s bioactive value [19, 20]. The phytochemical content of Physalis peruviana L. fruit varies according to ecological conditions; high acidity, vitamin C, and gallic acid values were detected in Mersin [21], Antalya [4], and Atacama Desert [22] locations, while in Tokat, plant growth regulators have been reported to modify quality parameters [23]. With the ripening process, the sugar/acid ratio, rutin, and especially β-carotene content in the fruit show a marked increase [8, 11]. In post-harvest processing, pectolytic enzymes increase fruit juice yield to 83% but cause nutrient loss [24]; on the other hand, 1-MCP applications slow down respiration and extend shelf life up to 14 days by preserving antioxidant content [25, 26]. Recent studies have revealed that the fruit has a high gallic acid content [27] and that extracts and nanoformulations possess antiproliferative, hepatoprotective, and “superfruit” bioactive properties [28–30]. Furthermore, immersion and coating techniques have been reported as effective methods for extending the storage life of cape gooseberry [31].

Putrescine (Put) is the most common form of polyamines, amino acid derivatives that regulate vital processes such as cell division and stress response, in plants. It has been determined that exogenous Put applications delay fruit ripening and softening by suppressing ethylene production; they also significantly improve yield, firmness, and postharvest quality parameters [32, 33]. Studies on peaches [34] and plums [33] have shown that Put application extends shelf life by reducing weight loss and decay, preserves phytochemical content and fruit firmness, and also increases fruit size and delays ripening. Furthermore, it has been reported that putrescine is an effective method for controlling physiological deterioration caused by chilling injury during low-temperature storage of tropical and subtropical products [35].

While losses ranging from 2% (processing) to 20% (production) occur at different stages of the supply chain in Europe, fruits and vegetables account for 32.9% of total food losses in the US. Due to cold chain and infrastructure deficiencies, rates reach 35–45% in the Middle East, North Africa, and India, while the highest global loss of 55% is seen in North Africa and West-Central Asian countries [36]. Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), which limits physiological deterioration by slowing down respiration rates, stands out as an effective method for reducing these losses. MAP, which preserves quality with the appropriate gas composition, contributes to the marketability of products by extending their shelf life. Continued respiration and ethylene production after harvest lead to quality losses in fruits and vegetables. MAP technology, which slows this process by reducing the amount of O2 and increasing the amount of CO2 in the environment, extends the shelf life of products by reducing respiration rate, reducing water loss, and inhibiting microbial growth [37]. Studies conducted on fruits such as strawberries, apricots, peaches, and nectarines [38–41] have shown that MAP application alone or in combination with applications such as 1-MCP, KMnO4, and melatonin preserves fruit flesh firmness, color, and aroma. This study aims to investigate the quality parameters and biochemical properties of MAP and putrescine combination on cape gooseberry.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, treatments, and storage

In this study, cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.) 33AL genotypes grown in Mersin, Turkye were used as the experimental material; the fruits were harvested at the commercial maturity stage, and those uniform in color, size, and physiological integrity were selected, while mechanically damaged, diseased, or defective fruits were excluded from the experiment. After harvest, the fruits were rapidly transported to the laboratory and subjected to the treatments following surface cleaning. To reduce postharvest quality losses, a portion of the fruits was treated with putrescine (1,4-butanediamine) at a concentration of 1 mM by dipping for 5 min, after which the fruits were allowed to air-dry at room temperature until surface moisture was removed. Putrescine application was made by immersing the fruits in Tween-20 (2%) added solutions. The fruits were left to stand at room temperature for a while, and then they were placed under storage conditions. The study was designed with 3 replicates, 10 fruits were used in each replicate, and a total of 120 fruits were used for each treatment. The reason for choosing a putrescine dose of 1 mM is that the study by Alan et al. [42], which showed that a 1 mM putrescine dose used in the preservation of tomato fruit preserved the quality characteristics and biochemical stability of the fruit, was taken as a reference. The modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) was chosen to hold 1 kg of strawberry fruit with a thickness of 0.01 mm and dimensions of 30 cm ×40 cm. Both putrescine-treated and control groups were then packaged under modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) conditions using gas-permeable polyethylene-based packaging materials (Modified atmosphere packing is made of polyethylene (PE) and has an O₂ permeability of 150–300 cc/m²/day, a CO₂ permeability of 300–800 cc/m²/day, and a water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) of 0.5–1.5 g m2 day), with the in-package atmosphere adjusted to 3–5% O₂, 10–15% CO₂, and N₂ as the balance gas, and the packages were sealed by heat. All experimental groups were stored in cold storage at 4 ± 1 °C and 90–95% relative humidity, and the fruits were stored for 30 days; analyses were conducted at 0, 10, 20, and 30 days. The experiment was arranged according to a completely randomized design with three replicates per treatment, and four treatment groups were established: Control (without putrescine and MAP), putrescine, MAP, and putrescine + MAP.

Assessment of weight loss and decay rate

Fruit mass changes during storage were monitored by recording individual fruit weights using a precision digital balance (± 0.01 g). Relative weight loss was calculated as a percentage of the initial fruit mass according to Hosseini et al. [43], where the baseline value corresponded to fruit weight measured immediately after harvest and subsequent values represented measurements taken at each sampling date. Visual assessment of decay incidence was carried out by estimating the proportion of fruit surface affected by tissue breakdown. Fruits were classified into four decay categories: Absence of decay (score 0), limited decay covering up to 25% of the surface (score 1), moderate decay affecting 25–50% (score 2), and extensive decay exceeding 50% of the surface area (score 3). Analysis of soluble solid content, titrable acidity, pH, and respiration rate

The soluble solids content (SSC) was determined using a digital handheld refractometer (ATC, BX50, Turkey). Titratable acidity was quantified using a conventional titration method as described by Hanif et al. [45], in which equal volumes of fruit juice and distilled water were titrated with 0.1 N sodium hydroxide until a phenolphthalein endpoint was reached. Juice pH was measured using a laboratory pH meter (OrionStar A111, Thermo Scientific, USA). To determine respiration activity, fruit samples (100 g) were enclosed in airtight 2 L glass jars, and CO₂ accumulation over time was quantified using a Testo 535 gas analyzer (Germany). Organic acid analysis

Organic acid profiling was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography with quantification based on external standard calibration curves prepared in the range of 100–800 ppm from certified reference materials (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). Fresh fruit samples were homogenized (250 g), and a representative portion (50 g) was diluted with distilled water at a ratio of 1:3 (w/v). Acid extraction was facilitated by adding 25 mL of 0.009 N sulfuric acid followed by continuous agitation for 60 min. The extract was clarified by centrifugation at 15,000 × g for 15 min. The resulting supernatant underwent sequential purification through coarse filtration, 0.45 μm membrane filtration (Millipore, Germany), and solid-phase cleanup using SEP-PAK C18 cartridges. Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with a Bio-Rad HPX-87 H column, using 0.009 N H₂SO₄ as the eluent. Detection was conducted at 254 and 280 nm following Bevilacqua et al. [47]. Organic acid concentrations were expressed on a fresh weight basis (mg 100 g⁻¹).

Determination of vitamin C

Vitamin C analysis was carried out following extraction of 100 g of fruit tissue with 2.5% metaphosphoric acid (Sigma, M6285), which served to stabilize vitamin C during sample handling. After centrifugation at 6500 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, a clarified extract was obtained. A 0.5 mL aliquot was diluted to 10 mL with metaphosphoric acid solution and filtered through a 0.45 μm PTFE syringe filter (Phenomenex, UK). Chromatographic determination was performed at 25 °C using a Luna C18 column (250 × 4.60 mm, 5 μm; Phenomenex). The mobile phase consisted of acidified distilled water (pH 2.2, sulfuric acid), delivered at 1 mL min⁻¹. Quantification was based on UV detection at 254 nm using a diode array detector, as described by Cemeroğlu [48].

Determination of sugar composition

Sugar components were determined by HPLC following the protocol of Karkacier et al. [49] with minor procedural adjustments. Fruit samples (10 g) were homogenized with 40 mL of distilled water using an Ultra-Turrax homogenizer (IKA T18, Germany). The homogenate was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 30 min (Sigma 2-6E, Germany), and the clarified supernatant was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane filter (Millex HV). A 20 µL volume of the filtrate was injected into a Shimadzu HPLC system (Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a RID-10 A refractive index detector. Separation was performed on an Aminex HPX-87 C carbohydrate column (300 × 7.8 mm) maintained at 80 °C, with ultrapure water as the mobile phase at a flow rate of 0.6 mL min⁻¹, according to Kara et al. [4].

Phenolic compound profiling

Phenolic compound identification and quantification were conducted using HPLC based on the methodology reported by Rodriguez-Delgado et al. [17], with slight modifications. Calibration curves were prepared using authentic standards (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany), including gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, chlorogenic acid, ferulic acid, syringic acid, p-coumaric acid, o-coumaric acid, rutin, and catechin, across a concentration range of 20–2500 ppm. Dry fruit tissue (100 g) was homogenized and extracted by continuous shaking for 1 h using an orbital shaker (Unimax 1010, Heidolph, Germany), followed by dilution with distilled water (1:1, v/v). After centrifugation at 15,000 rpm for 15 min, the extract was sequentially filtered through coarse filter paper and a 0.45 μm membrane filter. Chromatographic separation was carried out using an Agilent HPLC system equipped with a HiChrom ODS column (250 × 4.6 mm, 4 μm) and methanol–acetic acid–water mobile phase systems (10:2:28 and 90:2:8, v/v/v). Detection was performed at 254 and 280 nm, and phenolic contents were expressed as mg per 100 g dry weight (dw).

Statistical evaluation

All measured variables were statistically evaluated using a two-way analysis of variance to determine the individual and combined effects of postharvest treatments (putrescine and modified atmosphere packaging) and storage time on fruit quality attributes. When significant effects were detected, mean values were compared using Tukey’s HSD test at p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 13 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and results are presented as mean ± standard error along with corresponding F and P values. Relationships among variables were explored using Pearson correlation coefficients calculated with the corrplot package in RStudio (version 2022.12.0). In addition, principal component analysis was conducted using JMP 16 (SAS Institute Inc., USA) to visualize treatment- and storage-related multivariate responses.

Results and discussion

This study evaluated the effects of putrescine treatment and modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), applied individually or in combination, on fruit quality attributes during the storage time (Table 1). The physiological responses of fruits to putrescine, MAP, and their combined application in terms of major quality parameters are presented in Fig. 1. Statistical analyses demonstrated that both storage time and the interaction between “treatment × Storage time” had significant effects on the measured variables. The results indicated that while putrescine and MAP individually contributed to the maintenance of fruit quality, their combined application exerted a stronger inhibitory effect on postharvest physiological processes. Accordingly, the joint use of putrescine and MAP was identified as the most effective approach for preserving fruit quality and delaying physiological deterioration during storage (Fig. 1). On a global scale, fruits and vegetables account for a substantial proportion of food supply chain losses, estimated at 32.9% in the United States and reaching 45–55% in developing regions due to deficiencies in cold chain infrastructure [36]. In this context, the present study demonstrates the effectiveness of the Put and MAP combination in minimizing quality losses in cape gooseberry, a climacteric fruit with high export potential but a limited shelf life, and highlights this combined treatment as a promising postharvest strategy for quality preservation.

Quality properties

As storage time advanced, both weight loss and decay incidence increased across all treatment groups. Analysis of variance showed that storage time (F = 7.48**) as well as the interaction between treatment and storage time (F = 26.01***) had a statistically significant influence on fruit weight loss. After 30 days of storage, fruits in the control group exhibited the greatest weight reduction, reaching 7.91%. In contrast, the lowest weight loss values were observed in the combined Put + MAP treatment (3.96%) and in the Put 1 mM treatment (3.97%). A similarly strong interaction effect was detected for decay rate, which was also highly significant (F = 25.22***). By the end of the storage period (day 30), the highest level of decay was recorded in the Control group at 6.69%, whereas the Put + MAP treatment was the most effective in reducing decay, with a value of 4.28%. Although all treatments showed progressive increases in weight loss and decay over time, these changes occurred more rapidly and to a greater extent in untreated fruits. Both MAP and putrescine treatments significantly mitigated these negative effects, with the combined Put × MAP application providing the greatest reduction in weight loss and decay (Table 1; Fig. 1).

The interaction between treatment and storage time had a statistically significant effect on soluble solid content (SSC) content (F = 6.33**). Overall, soluble solids content (SSC) exhibited an increasing tendency as storage progressed. In the Control group, SSC reached a maximum value of 20.36% on the 20th day of storage, whereas in fruits subjected to the combined Put + MAP treatment, SSC remained lower at 17.50%, suggesting a delay in ripening processes. With regard to respiration behavior, the initial respiration rate measured at harvest (86.61 mg CO₂ kg⁻¹ h⁻¹) declined in all treatments throughout the storage period. Nevertheless, the differences among treatments were highly significant (F = 134.08***). By day 30, the lowest respiration rate was observed in the Put + MAP group (16.36 mg CO₂ kg⁻¹ h⁻¹), while the control group maintained a comparatively higher level of metabolic activity (24.73 mg CO₂ kg⁻¹ h⁻¹). SSC values increased during the early stages of storage, particularly in untreated fruits, and reached their highest levels around day 20 before declining at later stages. In contrast, MAP and Put treatments moderated these changes, resulting in more stable SSC values over time. Respiration rate decreased markedly during storage in all treatments; however, the elevated respiration observed in control fruits was significantly suppressed by MAP and Put applications, with the most pronounced reduction consistently recorded in the combined Put + MAP treatment (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis indicated that neither storage time nor the interaction between treatment and storage time had a significant effect on titratable acidity, as reflected by the non-significant F values (F = 1.44 ns and F = 2.15 ns, respectively). Although acidity values showed a numerical decline over the course of storage, decreasing from 1.65 to 1.10, this reduction was not statistically meaningful. In contrast, evaluation of pH revealed that storage time had a significant main effect (F = 13.46***), whereas the interaction between treatments and time did not reach statistical significance (F = 2.54 ns). On average, pH values increased during storage, rising from 4.45 at the beginning to 4.80 by the end of the storage period. This upward trend in pH was more evident in the Control group, indicating a faster loss of acidity. Conversely, fruits treated with putrescine and MAP exhibited a more moderated increase in pH, suggesting a better preservation of the acid–sugar balance. Although titratable acidity decreased in all treatments as storage progressed, MAP and particularly the combined Put × MAP application effectively slowed the rate of acid degradation. As a result, higher TA levels were maintained during the later stages of storage in treated fruits compared with the control group (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Ongoing respiration and ethylene production in the postharvest period are key factors accelerating aging in fruits. In our study, it was determined that the Put + MAP combination significantly reduced the respiration rate compared to the control group (16.36 vs. 24.73 mg CO2 kg− 1 h− 1) and, in parallel, minimized weight loss and decay rates (Table 1). In the literature, it is known that MAP technology slows down respiration and inhibits microbial growth by reducing the amount of O2 and increasing the CO2 ratio in the environment [36]. Similarly, it has been reported that 1-MCP applications in cape gooseberry extend shelf life by suppressing respiration [25, 26]. The low decay rates obtained in our study support this protective effect of MAP, while also revealing the contribution of exogenous Put application. Indeed, putrescine has been reported to suppress ethylene production, a polyamine that regulates cell division and stress response, and to reduce weight loss and decay in fruits such as peach [34] and plum [33]. In this study, the fact that the Put + MAP group yielded the best results indicates that the gas composition-regulating effect of MAP and the physiological aging-delaying effect of putrescine form a synergistic mechanism [32].

Cape gooseberry is known for its complex taste profile, which balances sweetness and sourness, and its rich organic acid content [3]. Our study findings showed that organic acids, especially citric and malic acids, and sugars (fructose, glucose) underwent rapid degradation in the Control group during storage, but the Put + MAP application statistically significantly slowed this loss (Tables 2 and 3). Physiologically, fruits use organic acids and sugars as substrates to maintain their respiration activities. The decrease in respiration rate discussed in the first section directly explains the preservation of these reserves. The literature states that the sugar/acid ratio in the fruit changes with the ripening process [11] and that MAP application preserves quality criteria in fruits such as strawberries, apricots, and nectarines [38, 39, 41]. Furthermore, the information that putrescine delays ripening by preserving fruit firmness and phytochemical content [33] is consistent with the high acid and sugar preservation observed in our findings. Especially considering the fruit’s fructose-rich diabetic nature [12], we can state that the Put + MAP application plays a critical role in preserving nutritional value.

Changes in organic acid, vitamin C and sugar content

The effects of putrescine (Put), modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), and their combination (Put + MAP) on the organic acid profile (citric, malic, succinic, and tartaric acid) of fruit during storage are presented in Table 2. According to the results of the variance analysis (ANOVA) performed, both the storage period and the “Application x Storage Period” interaction were found to be statistically highly significant (p ≤ 0.001) on all organic acids examined. Citric acid, the predominant organic acid in fruits, was measured at 853.44 mg 100 g− 1 at the harvest stage. Although there was a decrease in citric acid content in all groups as storage time increased, the treatments significantly slowed this loss (Finteraction = 39.41***). On the 10th day of storage, the Put + MAP group (829.23 mg 100 g− 1) was found to be statistically indistinguishable from the harvest initial value (group a) and was the group that best preserved acidity. At the end of the storage period (day 30), the lowest citric acid content was detected in the Control group at 544.74 mg 100 g− 1. During the same period, the Put + MAP (617.26 mg 100 g− 1) and MAP (612.45 mg 100 g− 1) applications showed statistically higher acid content compared to the control group. The protective effect of the applications on malic acid is noteworthy due to the high F value (F = 105.15***) of the interaction. The malic acid level, which was initially 52.32 mg 100 g− 1, decreased to 30.27 mg 100 g− 1 in the control group at the end of 30 days. In contrast, the Put + MAP application statistically differed from all other groups at the end of 30 days with a malic acid content of 37.81 mg 100 g− 1 and maintained the highest value. The interaction effect on succinic acid content was also found to be significant (F = 14.69***). The value of 9.34 mg 100 g− 1 at harvest time decreased to 6.16 mg 100 g− 1 in the control group at the end of storage, while the decrease was more limited in the Put + MAP application (7.31 mg 100 g− 1). When tartaric acid changes were examined (F = 23.86***), the control group showed the lowest value at 15.63 mg 100 g− 1 at the end of 30 days, while the MAP application (22.31 mg 100 g− 1) and the Put + MAP application (18.60 mg 100 g− 1) significantly prevented tartaric acid loss compared to the control group.

The results of Put, MAP, and Put + MAP treatment effects on vitamin C is shown in Table 3. Analysis of variance revealed that the main effect of storage duration and interaction effect of application and storage duration were highly significant for all parameters measured (p ≤ 0.001 for vitamin C) The interaction effect was highly significant for vitamin C content (F = 28.12***), showing that the change in vitamin C content was highly dependent on the treatment applied. Vitamin C content, which was 43.30 mg/100 g at harvest, gradually decreased in all treatments during storage. However, the rate of decrease was significantly different among treatments. On the 10th day of storage, the Put + MAP (42.31 mg/100 g) and MAP (41.66 mg/100 g) treatments were effective in maintaining vitamin C content statistically at par with the harvested sample (group ‘a’). However, on day 30 of storage, the maximum decrease in vitamin C content was recorded in the control treatment, which dropped to 20.28 mg/100 g (group ‘e’). In contrast, the Put + MAP (30.83 mg/100 g) and MAP (30.53 mg/100 g) treatments significantly reduced the loss of vitamin C content compared to the control, thus showing their protective role during storage.

When examining sugar contents, which are important indicators of fruit quality, the effect of interaction is decisive on fructose (F = 7.21***), glucose (F = 11.73***), and sucrose (F = 11.58***). As a general trend, sugar levels increased until the 20th day of storage (due to climacteric ripening) and then decreased on the 30th day. However, the control group experienced this process more rapidly. On day 20, the control group reached the highest value (group a) at 4.88 mg 100 g− 1, but on day 30, it rapidly declined to 4.12 mg. At the end of the 30th day, the Put + MAP group (4.51 mg 100 g− 1) had a statistically higher fructose content than the control group. Similarly, at the end of the 30th day, the glucose level in the control group was measured as 3.35 mg 100 g− 1, while the Put + MAP group preserved sugar better with a value of 3.60 mg 100 g− 1. The sucrose content was determined to be 2.46 mg 100 g− 1 at harvest. At the end of 30 days, the control group showed the lowest level at 2.24 mg, while the MAP (2.49 mg) and Put + MAP (2.45 mg) groups maintained their stability by remaining at levels close to their harvest start values.

Changes in phenolic compounds

The effects of putrescine (Put), Modified Atmosphere Packaging (MAP), and combined (Put + MAP) applications on phenolic compounds (gallic, chlorogenic, p-coumaric, vanillic, ferulic, and caffeic acids) in fruit during storage are summarized in Table 4. According to the results of the variance analysis (ANOVA), both storage time and the “Treatment x Storage Time” interaction were found to be statistically significant for all phenolic compounds studied. The interaction effect was significant at the p ≤ 0.01 level (F = 4.18**) for chlorogenic acid, while it was highly significant (p ≤ 0.001) for gallic, p-coumaric, vanillic, ferulic, and caffeic acids. Gallic acid, the phenolic compound found in the highest concentration in fruits, was measured at 238.46 mg 100 g− 1 dw at harvest. Although a decrease was observed in all groups during storage, the effect of the interaction was significant (F = 46.18***). At the end of the 30th day of storage, the control group decreased to the lowest level at 130.49 mg (f group), while the Put 1mM application statistically limited the loss more than other groups at 146.51 mg (d group). The larger reduction of gallic acid in the Put + MAP treatment could be explained by the synergistic action of the modified atmosphere and putrescine on the metabolism of phenolics. Gallic acid, being an intermediate in the phenylpropanoid pathway, might have been further utilized in the biosynthesis of more complex phenolic compounds. Hence, the reduction observed in the Put + MAP treatment might not be due to degradation alone but to increased metabolism [42]. The effect of the treatments created a striking difference in ferulic acid content, statistically supported by a very high F value (F = 119.83***). The ferulic acid content, which was initially 12.12 mg 100 g− 1 dw, decreased to 5.85 mg in the control group at the end of 30 days. However, during the same period, the Put1mM application maintained a value of 9.33 mg 100 g− 1 dw, showing statistically superior performance (cd group) compared to both the control group and other applications. At the end of 30 days, while the control group decreased to a level of 3.45 mg, the Put + MAP group (cd group) presented a statistically higher content with a value of 4.99 mg (F = 28.69***). The value, which was 8.55 mg at the beginning of harvest, decreased to 5.11 mg in the control group at the end of storage. The Put + MAP application significantly slowed down the loss of vanillic acid, reaching 6.33 mg (de group) on day 30 (F = 42.91***). Similarly, by day 30, the Put + MAP group (4.66 mg) statistically (p ≤ 0.05) preserved the amount of caffeic acid better than the control group (3.74 mg). A process-dependent decrease was also observed in chlorogenic acid content. The value of 4.55 mg at harvest time decreased to 2.51 mg in the control group at the end of 30 days. Although the Put + MAP application (2.80 mg) was numerically higher than the control group, the statistical differences at day 30 were not as pronounced as for the other acids (group).

Physalis peruviana is a strong antioxidant source with a vitamin C content of 46 mg 100 g− 1 and a phenolic compound capacity of approximately 40 mg 100 g− 1 [5, 10]. In our study, it was determined that vitamin C and phenolic compounds (especially gallic acid, ferulic acid, and vanillic acid) decreased due to oxidation during storage, but putrescine and MAP applications preserved these bioactive compounds (Tables 3 and 4). In particular, the fact that the gallic acid content remained higher in the treated groups, despite decreasing by half in the control group after 30 days (from 238.46 to 130.49 mg dw), is important in terms of maintaining the “super fruit” quality of the fruit [29] throughout its shelf life. Literature highlights that cape gooseberry has high vitamin C and gallic acid content in different locations such as Mersin and Atacama [21, 22], and that these compounds are protective against oxidative damage [18]. Putrescine is known to be effective in controlling physiological deterioration and chilling damage that occur during storage [35]; while MAP prevents the oxidation of vitamin C and phenolics by limiting oxygen in the environment. The data in our study reveal that the combination of these two methods is a more effective strategy than individual applications in preserving the antioxidant capacity and bioactive value of the fruit.

Relationships between quality characteristics during storage, organic acids, phenolic compounds, putrescine, and MAP applications

Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that putrescine, MAP, and their interaction significantly affected postharvest fruit physiology and quality. The first two components accounted for 79.3% of the total variance, with PC1 (54.7%) primarily linked to ripening and senescence variables (organic acids, vitamin C, respiration rate, weight loss, and decay), and PC2 (24.6%) linked to sugar metabolism and soluble solids/acid balance. The control treatment moved towards the negative region of PC1 during storage, reflecting a rapid decline in fruit quality. MAP was linked to lower respiration rates and better retention of organic acids and vitamin C, while putrescine was positively correlated with glucose, fructose, and sucrose, reflecting better retention of sugar content and acid-sugar ratio. The combined application of putrescine and MAP produced the most compact and balanced distribution; it showed that respiration rate, weight loss, and decay rate were kept at the lowest levels while organic acids, vitamin C, and sugars were simultaneously preserved. When the time factor was considered, control samples showed a significant deviation towards quality loss in the days following harvest, while the Put × MAP application in particular was seen to maintain quality parameters more stable during storage. Overall, PCA clearly demonstrates that putrescine and MAP exert their effects through distinct yet complementary physiological mechanisms, and their combination represents the most effective strategy for preserving post-harvest quality (Fig. 2).

Characterization of postharvest physiological effects of putrescine and MAP treatment on organic acids, sugars and quality properties using principal component analysis (PCA)

The principal component analysis (PCA) results presented in Fig. 3 show that putrescine, MAP, and the combination of these applications have significant effects on the preservation and change of phenolic compounds during the post-harvest period. The first two principal components, explaining 87.8% of the total variance, reveal that PC1 (77.8%) is the main source of variation related to the overall accumulation and degradation of phenolic compounds, while PC2 (10.0%) reflects secondary differences between treatments and storage duration. The control group showed a wide distribution, particularly as storage time progressed (Days 20–30), diverging negatively along PC1 and showing marked phenolic losses associated with caffeic, ferulic, and p-coumaric acids. The positioning of the harvest samples on the positive side of PC1, particularly close to chlorogenic and gallic acids, suggests that these compounds were initially present at higher levels and tended to decrease during storage. MAP and putrescine applications formed narrower and overlapping ellipses compared to the control group, indicating that phenolic compounds were preserved more stably; in particular, it was understood that putrescine application limited oxidative degradation by showing a closer relationship with phenolic acids along PC1 and PC2. The Put + MAP combination showed a more balanced distribution covering different phenolic acids, indicating a combined treatment effect in preserving caffeic, ferulic, and p-coumaric acids during storage. Overall, the PCA results clearly show that the changes observed in post-harvest phenolic compounds are largely application-dependent, and that while putrescine and MAP are effective individually, when applied together they provide the highest level of phenolic stability and delay quality loss (Fig. 3).

Characterization of post-harvest physiological effects of putrescine and MAP treatment on phenolic compounds using principal component analysis (PCA)

Heat maps and hierarchical clustering analysis reveal that the physicochemical properties of fruits differ significantly depending on applications and storage duration during the post-harvest period. Red tones on the heat map represent relatively high levels of parameters, while blue tones represent low levels. Control samples were found to diverge in terms of weight loss, decay rate, and respiration rate, especially as storage time progressed, and conversely showed significant decreases in organic acids, vitamin C, and phenolic compounds. Harvest samples were characterized by high values for sugars, organic acids, and phenolic compounds, forming a cluster that clearly distinguished itself from all other treatments. MAP and putrescine applications exhibited more similar color distributions compared to the control group, indicating that quality parameters were maintained more consistently; in particular, samples treated with both putrescine and MAP formed a sub-cluster associated with lower weight loss and respiration rate, along with higher acidity, vitamin C, and phenolic compound levels. The hierarchical dendrogram structure revealed that the treatment × storage time interaction was decisive and that the Put × MAP application kept the physicochemical properties closer to harvest values even during advanced storage days. Overall, this analysis clearly shows that putrescine and MAP individually exhibit quality-preserving effects, but their combination most effectively limits post-harvest physiological deterioration, thereby maintaining fruit quality integrity at the highest level (Fig. 4).

Heatmap analysis for grouping the physicochemical properties of fruits according to applications

PCA and heatmap analyses characterize overall physiological and biochemical reactions of fruit exposed to 1 mM putrescine and MAP treatment during cold storage. Treatment differentiation resulted mainly from differences in respiration rate, phenolic compounds, organic acids and sugars. Specifically, control fruit are grouped on having higher respiration rates and higher electrolyte leakage (indicating rapid senescence and membrane breakdown). Conversely, fruit treated with 1 mM putrescine, especially in combination with MAP, is grouped for having phenolic content, sugar, organic acids higher, indicating better regulation of oxidative stress and better stabilization of membranes. The multivariate separation demonstrates not only numerical but also physiological changes in the development of delayed ripening and improved tolerance to stress under cold storage conditions. The use of both treatments appears to reduce oxidative injury and reduce metabolic activity; thereby aiding in the maintenance of the quality of harvested fruit.

According to the correlation matrix, the direction and strength of the relationships between fruit quality characteristics and organic acids, sugars, and phenolic compounds are revealed in detail depending on the treatments. Weight loss (WeL) and spoilage rate (DeR) showed high and statistically significant negative correlations with almost all organic acids (citric, malic, succinic, and tartaric acids), vitamin C, and phenolic compounds; this clearly shows that preserving these compounds limits post-harvest quality loss. In contrast, titratable acidity (TeA) and respiration rate (ReR) showed strong positive correlations, especially with citric, malic, and succinic acids and vitamin C, suggesting that acid metabolism is closely linked to respiration activity. Soluble solids content (SSC) and pH generally showed negative correlations with organic acids and phenolic compounds; an increase in pH showed a parallel trend with a decrease in acid and phenolic content. When sugars were examined, fructose, glucose, and sucrose showed strong positive correlations with each other, but their relationships with organic acids and phenolic compounds were weak or negative; this suggests that carbohydrate metabolism proceeds partially independently of acid and phenolic metabolism in the post-harvest period. Phenolic compounds (gallic, chlorogenic, p-coumaric, vanillic, ferulic, and caffeic acids) exhibited high and significant positive correlations among themselves, revealing that they share common biosynthetic and degradation pathways. Overall, the correlation analysis clearly shows that the treatments reduced weight loss and spoilage, particularly through the preservation of organic acids, vitamin C, and phenolic compounds; thus, these compounds play a key role in maintaining post-harvest fruit quality (Fig. 5).

Conclusion

This study evaluated the effects of combined putrescine and modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) on the storage life and quality of climacteric cape gooseberry (Physalis peruviana L.) grown in Mersin, Türkiye. The results showed that the putrescine + MAP (Put + MAP) treatment markedly suppressed fruit respiration, thereby slowing metabolic senescence. As a result, weight loss and decay rates were significantly reduced compared to the control and single treatments, and the physical integrity of the fruit was better preserved. This enhanced physiological stability was reflected in improved biochemical quality, as the degradation of citric, malic, succinic, and tartaric acids was significantly delayed and rapid changes in sugar metabolism were moderated, maintaining the fruit’s flavor profile. Furthermore, the Put + MAP combination had a pronounced effect on the bioactive quality of the fruit by limiting vitamin C oxidation and significantly reducing the loss of important phenolic compounds, including gallic, ferulic, p-coumaric, vanillic, and caffeic acids (p ≤ 0.05). Overall, the postharvest application of putrescine in combination with MAP emerges as an effective and practical strategy for extending shelf life, preserving nutritional quality, and enhancing the commercial value of cape gooseberry.
