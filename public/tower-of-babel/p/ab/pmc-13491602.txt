Enhanced 5-aminolevulinic acid photodynamic therapy with potassium iodide for sporotrichosis: in vitro optimization and a proof-of-concept clinical case
Authors: Yan Cui, Ruili Chen, Jingjing Tian, Shanshan Li, Junfeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSporotrichosis, a chronic fungal infection caused by pathogenic Sporothrix species, involves therapeutic challenges due to the toxicity of conventional antifungals. This study investigated the enhancement of 5-aminolevulinic acid-based photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) with potassium iodide (KI) through in vitro and a proof-of-concept clinical case.MethodsIn vitro testing on S. globosa conidia involved preincubation with 100 mM KI followed by ALA-PDT using 0.6 M ALA and 108 J/cm2 red light irradiation. The efficacy of this combined approach was systematically compared with that of administering KI after ALA-PDT and that of using ALA-PDT alone. In the clinical setting, a patient with lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis and a history of itraconazole-induced liver injury underwent three sessions of KI-enhanced ALA-PDT using 0.7 M ALA (10%) and 100 J/cm2 irradiation.ResultsPreincubation with 100 mM KI significantly enhanced the fungicidal effect of ALA-PDT against S. globosa conidia, resulting in a substantially reduced conidial survival rate compared to either administering KI after ALA-PDT or using ALA-PDT alone. Clinically, the patient treated with KI-enhanced ALA-PDT showed complete resolution of skin lesions and no recurrence during the 1-year follow-up period.ConclusionsThis study evaluates a KI-enhanced ALA-PDT regimen in a patient with sporotrichosis, providing preliminary evidence that this approach may serve as a promising therapeutic option for patients intolerant to conventional antifungal therapy.

Background

Sporotrichosis is a chronic granulomatous fungal infection caused by pathogenic Sporothrix species, primarily Sporothrix schenckii, S. brasiliensis, and S. globosa [1]. The disease typically manifests as cutaneous or lymphocutaneous lesions but may progress to systemic infection in immunocompromised individuals. Sporotrichosis is endemic in tropical and subtropical regions, including Brazil, Mexico, Peru, India, China, and Thailand, with regional variation in dominant species [2–4]. Notably, S. globosa is the predominant pathogen in Asia, particularly within Northeastern China, a highly endemic region where it is the sole identified causative species [5, 6]. Since the 2010s, zoonotic transmission via S. brasiliensis–infected cats has contributed to intercontinental spread, exacerbating the global burden of sporotrichosis [7, 8]. Consequently, the World Health Organization has designated sporotrichosis as a neglected tropical disease (NTD), underscoring its public health importance [9].

Cutaneous sporotrichosis is primarily managed with oral itraconazole, potassium iodide (KI), or terbinafine; itraconazole serves as first-line treatment because of its efficacy and safety profile [10, 11]. In severe infections, such as disseminated or systemic disease, amphotericin B is recommended until clinical stabilization, followed by itraconazole maintenance therapy [10, 11]. However, these treatments exhibit important limitations, including hepatotoxicity with itraconazole, gastrointestinal discomfort and thyroid dysfunction with KI, and nausea or neutropenia with terbinafine. Such adverse effects restrict their use in patients with hepatic disease, pregnant women, infants, and immunocompromised individuals with refractory infection [12]. Furthermore, emerging antifungal resistance within the Sporothrix complex, particularly to itraconazole and terbinafine, has been increasingly reported in recent years [13], further complicating treatment and underscoring the urgent need for novel therapeutic approaches. Among non-pharmacologic interventions, thermotherapy primarily constitutes an adjunct to antifungal therapy, whereas cryotherapy or surgical excision may be effective as monotherapy for small or solitary lesions; results vary depending on lesion size, location, and host immune status [12, 14].

5-Aminolevulinic acid-based photodynamic therapy (ALA-PDT) uses 5-ALA, a photosensitizing precursor that, upon activation by visible light, generates cytotoxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) capable of destroying infected tissue [15]. ALA-PDT has shown therapeutic potential against various fungal infections, including chromoblastomycosis, Trichophyton rubrum, Candida albicans, and Trichophyton mentagrophytes [16–19]. Our previous work demonstrated that ALA-PDT considerably reduces the viability of S. globosa conidia and yeast cells in vitro. However, the high light fluence required (162 J/cm2) can cause substantial pain and potential burns, limiting its clinical utility [20]. Optimization of ALA-PDT parameters is essential to improve tolerability and therapeutic effectiveness.

Multiple in vitro studies have shown that KI enhances PDT by reacting with ROS generated via photosensitizers under light activation, producing cytotoxic iodine species that amplify antimicrobial effects. Evidence indicates that KI greatly improves the efficacy of photosensitizers such as methylene blue, rose bengal, and toluidine blue O against a range of pathogens, including C. albicans and Staphylococcus aureus [21–23]. However, no studies have yet confirmed KI’s ability to enhance ALA-PDT or its clinical application.

In this study, we performed a series of in vitro experiments evaluating the combined effect of ALA-PDT and KI. Subsequently, we use the optimized protocol to treat a patient with lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis and hepatic impairment who was ineligible for conventional antifungal therapy.

Methods

This study consisted of in vitro experiments using clinical isolates of Sporothrix globosa and a single case report of a patient with lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis who was intolerant to standard antifungal therapy.

Eight S. globosa strains isolated from clinical cases of cutaneous sporotrichosis were used. Strain FHJU12082703 was selected as the primary test strain due to its high melanin production and virulence, along with its established use as a model strain in our previous research [20]. All strains were identified as S. globosa through conventional morphological and molecular (i.e., amplification and sequencing of the calmodulin gene) methods. Effects of KI on ALA-PDT efficacy against S. globosa

ALA hydrochloride (CAS No. 5451-09-2; Toronto Research Chemicals, Toronto, Canada) was sterilized and stored at 4 °C in the dark. KI (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in sterile water to prepare a 1 M stock solution, which was sterilized by filtration and diluted to the desired concentrations before each experiment. KI solutions were freshly prepared prior to use. The light source was a red light-emitting diode (LED) device (wavelength 633 ± 10 nm) provided by Yage Optic and Electronic Technique Co. (Wuhan, China).

This study assessed whether KI could enhance ALA-PDT efficacy, enabling the use of lower, clinically tolerable ALA and light doses. To evaluate the effect of KI addition sequence, two conditions were compared: (1) co-incubation of KI (100 mM) and ALA (0.6 M) with conidial suspensions before red LED irradiation; and (2) incubation of ALA with conidial suspensions followed by red LED irradiation and subsequent incubation with KI. Untreated conidial suspensions without KI, ALA, or LED irradiation served as controls. Conidial suspensions of the FHJU12082703 strain (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were prepared in 96-well plates (200 μL per well). All samples were incubated at 28 °C in the dark for 30 min and then irradiated with red LED light (60 mW/cm2, 108 J/cm2) in continuous wave (CW) mode (spot size 8 × 8 cm, distance 5 cm). The same experimental setup and irradiation parameters were used in subsequent experiments.

To assess the influence of KI concentration, conidial suspensions were mixed with ALA (0.6 M) and KI at concentrations of 0, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, and 100 mM, then incubated and irradiated under the same conditions. To determine the killing effect of KI-enhanced ALA-PDT on S. globosa conidia, suspensions containing KI and ALA were irradiated under identical parameters. Control groups included untreated suspensions, suspensions treated with KI or ALA alone, and suspensions exposed to irradiation without ALA or KI.

To further evaluate treatment effectiveness, seven S. globosa strains were tested under the same protocol. Conidial suspensions were treated with KI and ALA under identical irradiation conditions. Control groups included untreated suspensions and those incubated with ALA and irradiated without KI co-incubation.

After irradiation, all samples (treated and control groups) were diluted 100-fold with PBS. Then, 100 μL aliquots of the diluted suspension were spread onto PDA plates and incubated at 28 °C for 5 days. Colony counts were visually recorded, and survival rates were calculated as follows: Survival rate (%) = (colony count in experimental group/mean colony count in untreated group) × 100%. All experiments evaluating the sequence of KI addition, the KI concentration–response relationship, and the full combination killing effect were performed as three independent biological replicates. In addition, the optimized protocol was validated across seven different clinical S. globosa isolates (n = 7), with each isolate tested once. Each isolate was normalized to its own untreated control.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively, confirming that the assumptions for parametric tests were met. Student’s paired t-test was used to compare between two paired groups; one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was used for comparisons among three or more groups. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level of 5% (P < 0.05). Graphical interpretation was performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Both the KI + ALA-PDT and ALA-PDT + KI groups showed significantly reduced survival compared with the control group (P = 0.0003 and P = 0.0092, respectively). A significant difference was also observed between the two experimental groups (P = 0.0098), indicating that preincubation with KI before ALA-PDT produced a considerably stronger fungicidal effect than post-treatment KI addition (Fig. 1A). During ALA-PDT treatment, the addition of 0.01 mM, 0.1 mM, or 1 mM KI did not significantly alter the killing effect against S. globosa conidia. However, an increase in KI concentration to 10 mM reduced the conidial survival rate to 71.64 ± 4.82% [95% CI 59.66–83.61%], a significant decrease compared with that in the control group (P = 0.0044). A further increase in KI concentration to 100 mM lowered the survival rate to 39.91 ± 2.89% [95% CI 32.74–47.09%], which was significantly lower than 10 mM KI group (P = 0.006) (Fig. 1B).

To confirm that the observed killing effect resulted from enhancement by KI, control groups including KI alone, ALA + KI, and LED + KI were evaluated. No significant differences were found between these groups and the untreated control, indicating that KI alone or in combination with either ALA or LED independently had no fungicidal activity. Treatment with 0.6 M ALA and 108 J/cm2 LED irradiation yielded a survival rate of 87.94 ± 9.38% [95% CI 64.63–100.00%], slightly lower than the control; this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.1683). In contrast, when KI and ALA were co-incubated before LED irradiation, the conidial survival rate decreased to 49.50 ± 3.75% [95% confidence interval (CI) 40.20–58.80%], representing a significant difference compared with the control group and ALA-PDT group (P = 0.0003 and P = 0.0027, respectively). These results demonstrate that KI substantially enhances the fungicidal activity of ALA-PDT against S. globosa conidia (Fig. 1C). To provide a more robust statistical overview, we pooled the data for the 100 mM KI + ALA-PDT condition across Fig. 1A–C (each performed in triplicate, n = 9 independent replicates), yielding an overall survival rate of 44.90 ± 5.13% [95% CI 40.96–48.85%].

Further experiments were conducted using conidia from seven clinical isolates treated with 0.6 M ALA and 108 J/cm2 red LED irradiation, with or without 100 mM KI preincubation. Each clinical isolate was normalized to its own untreated control. Preincubation with 100 mM KI before ALA-PDT significantly reduced the survival rate compared with ALA-PDT alone (P = 0.0002) (Fig. 1D). These findings indicate that preincubation with 100 mM KI before ALA-PDT substantially enhances the antifungal activity of 0.6 M ALA combined with 108 J/cm2 red LED light against S. globosa conidia. Therefore, this condition was used for subsequent clinical treatment studies.

Case presentation

In March 2024, a 63-year-old female farmer with a history of diabetes mellitus presented to our department with a 1-year history of a rash on her right wrist. One year earlier, she had been diagnosed with sporotrichosis at a local hospital. Initial treatment consisted of oral administration of a 10% potassium iodide solution, 10 mL three times daily. After 2 days, the patient experienced severe nausea and discontinued the medication. The physician then prescribed oral itraconazole at 100 mg twice daily for 1 month. During this period, the skin lesions showed minimal improvement; the patient gradually developed fatigue and loss of appetite. Liver function testing revealed considerably elevated enzyme levels: alanine aminotransferase was 388 U/L, and aspartate aminotransferase was 186 U/L. Itraconazole was discontinued, and intermittent hepatoprotective therapy with bicyclol and magnesium isoglycyrrhizinate was administered for 1 month. The patient’s fatigue resolved, and liver function normalized after 3 months. She subsequently sought further treatment at our institution.

On examination, the patient appeared in good general condition. Dermatologic evaluation revealed two erythematous plaques on the right wrist and forearm, aligned in a linear pattern. The lesions measured approximately the size of a peanut and a coin, respectively, with mildly eroded surfaces covered by dark brown crusts. Palpation revealed several slightly firm subcutaneous nodules surrounding the plaques (Fig. 2A). The results of laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, thyroid function tests, and an activated lymphocyte panel (seven cytokines), were all within normal limits. A skin biopsy from the wrist lesion showed pseudoepitheliomatous hyperplasia of the epidermis and infectious granulomas within the dermis on hematoxylin and eosin staining (Fig. 2B). Periodic acid Schiff staining did not reveal fungal spores; however, Calcofluor White staining (Liming Bio-products Co., Ltd, Nanjing, China) demonstrated budding yeast cells, consistent with sporotrichosis (Fig. 2C). After 1 week, a single colony was isolated from the skin biopsy on a Sabouraud dextrose agar slant and subsequently subcultured on PDA plates for another week. Based on its morphological characteristics, the isolate was regarded as a member of the Sporothrix genus (Fig. 2D–F). Molecular identification of the pathogen was performed through polymerase chain reaction amplification and sequencing of the calmodulin gene. The nucleotide sequence was submitted to GenBank, confirming the isolate as S. globosa (accession number: PX250989).

Based on the clinical presentation, histopathologic findings, and fungal culture results, the patient was diagnosed with lymphocutaneous sporotrichosis. Given the history of drug-induced liver injury after systemic itraconazole therapy, treatment was initiated with KI-enhanced ALA-PDT. Before the first PDT session, topical erythromycin ointment was applied to soften the lesions, followed by CO2 laser debridement to remove necrotic crusts (Fig. 3A). The treatment protocol consisted of 100 mM KI (50 mL) applied using wet gauze covered with plastic wrap for 30 min (Fig. 3B), followed by topical application of 118 mg of a commercially available 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) hydrochloride formulation (Fudan-Zhangjiang Bio-Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), dissolved in 1 mL normal saline to a final concentration of approximately 0.7 M, used as a wet dressing for 3 h. Irradiation was performed as continuous wave mode, 8 × 8 cm spot, 20 cm distance. Light intensity was adjusted to 40–60 mW/cm2 for 20–30 min, delivering a total fluence of 100 J/cm2. After each PDT session, cold compresses were applied for 30 min per session, three to five times daily, for 5 consecutive days. At the 3-week follow-up, the patient demonstrated considerable improvement. The granulomatous erythematous plaques had greatly decreased in size and thickness. The lesion surfaces showed no purulent discharge and were covered only by thin, dry black crusts (Fig. 3C). Because of persistent crusting, erythromycin ointment was reapplied to soften the affected areas; this was followed by CO2 laser removal of surface debris. A second session of KI-enhanced ALA-PDT was then administered.

At the next 3-week follow-up, the distal wrist lesions had nearly resolved, leaving only superficial, skin-colored crusts. The proximal plaques had substantially decreased in size, with mild peripheral induration and no detectable subcutaneous nodules. The central regions displayed thin, skin-colored crusts. Without additional laser intervention, a third session of KI-enhanced ALA-PDT was performed (Fig. 3D). Two months later, the patient’s lesions had completely healed. At the 1-year follow-up, no recurrence was observed.

Discussion

Research has shown that various photosensitizers, such as methylene blue (MB) [24–27], curcumin [28, 29], Ru (II) complexes [30], and ALA [20], exert potent antifungal effects against Sporothrix species in vitro. However, to date, only methylene blue has been tested in clinical settings or animal models. Gilaberte et al. first reported clinical use of PDT for sporotrichosis [24]. Their regimen used intralesional injection of 1% MB-PDT (light fluence: 37 J/cm2) combined with intermittent low-dose itraconazole, successfully curing a patient with fixed cutaneous sporotrichosis who was unresponsive to conventional antifungal therapy. The same group later used a similar approach incorporating daylight exposure (daylight PDT) to treat another patient with the fixed form of sporotrichosis [31]. Additional reports have described successful treatment of feline sporotrichosis using topical MB-PDT (light fluence: 128 J/cm2) combined with itraconazole [32]. Although the aforementioned photosensitizers have demonstrated efficacy in vitro, ALA remains the only photosensitizer officially approved for PDT in China.

In clinical applications, higher ALA concentrations or light doses are typically used to enhance efficacy compared with in vitro protocols. For example, Zhang et al. [19] reported a case of Majocchi’s granuloma caused by multidrug-resistant T. mentagrophytes in which the clinical regimen used 20% ALA (1.4 M) combined with 100 J/cm2 red light—far exceeding the in vitro effective dose (5% ALA + 100 J/cm2, inhibition rate > 50%). Our previous work demonstrated that > 50% inhibition of S. globosa conidia required a light fluence of 162 J/cm2, which greatly carries a high risk of pain and burns, thus limiting clinical applicability and necessitating strategies to reduce the irradiation dose without compromising efficacy.

Since 2015, Hamblin’s group has demonstrated through a series of studies that various inorganic salts, such as KI, sodium thiocyanate, and potassium bromide, can act as powerful enhancers of antimicrobial photodynamic inactivation [33–37]. Among these enhancers, KI is the most promising because of its broad-spectrum antimicrobial potential and favorable safety profile. KI not only enhances the activity of methylene blue and related agents against gram-positive bacteria and fungi but also enables normally ineffective photosensitizers such as Photofrin and rose bengal to become highly active against Gram-negative bacteria. Its potentiation effect is particularly strong, achieving up to a six-log increase in microbial killing, and its safety is supported by its clinical approval for oral treatment of sporotrichosis. The underlying enhancement mechanism involves a synergistic process in which singlet oxygen generated by the photosensitizer oxidizes iodide ions (I⁻), leading to the formation of reactive iodine species (RIS), including iodine radicals (I), molecular iodine (I2), and long-lived antimicrobial species such as hypoiodite [34].

Vieira et al. subsequently evaluated the antimicrobial effects of 16 photosensitizers in combination with KI against Escherichia coli [38]. The study demonstrated that ineffective combinations often result from photosensitizers, such as crystal violet and malachite green, that fail to generate sufficient singlet oxygen to initiate the KI reaction. The results indicated that the synergistic effect depends not only on the photosensitizer class but also on its specific physicochemical properties. For example, among the cationic meso-tetraarylporphyrins, Tri-Py( +)-Me and Tetra-Py( +)-Me exhibited robust potentiation when combined with KI; their homologs––Di-Py( +)-Me opp and Di-Py( +)-Me adj––showed no such effect. In other cases, the photosensitizer’s molecular structure or charge distribution may prevent RIS from effectively interacting with microbial targets. Therefore, the capacity of a photosensitizer to produce a synergistic antimicrobial effect with KI must be verified through dedicated in vitro testing. More recent work has also demonstrated that combinations of KI with temoporfin [39], bisdemethoxycurcumin [40], or erythrosine [41] enhance antimicrobial efficacy.

Unlike conventional photosensitizers, ALA functions as a prodrug that is intracellularly metabolized into protoporphyrin IX, which generates singlet oxygen (1O2) via a Type II photochemical mechanism under 630–635 nm irradiation [15]. In our previous work, yeast-phase S. globosa cells were highly susceptible to ALA-PDT under the same in vitro parameters used in the present study (0.6 M ALA + 108 J/cm2 red LED irradiation), achieving around 80% killing [20]. Unlike our previous study, which focused on the baseline antifungal activity of ALA-PDT alone, the present work was designed to explore whether KI could serve as a practical adjunct to enhance ALA-PDT under reduced and clinically acceptable treatment parameters. In our previous work, yeast-phase S. globosa cells were more susceptible to ALA-PDT, whereas conidia were consistently less susceptible. We therefore focused on the conidial phase in the present study, as it provided a more discriminative in vitro model for evaluating the measurable potentiating effect of KI. Under these conditions, our in vitro experiments showed that 100 mM KI combined with 0.6 ALA and 108 J/cm2 LED irradiation exerted a clear synergistic effect against S. globosa conidia. Taken together, the present findings provide preliminary evidence that KI may potentiate the antifungal activity of ALA-PDT under clinically feasible conditions. However, the precise underlying mechanism was not directly investigated in the present study. We propose that KI reacts with singlet oxygen (1O2) generated during ALA-PDT to produce highly RIS [34, 38]. These RIS may not only directly amplify cellular damage but could also partially bypass the ROS-quenching capacity of fungal melanin, which is known to protect S. globosa from conventional ALA-PDT [20]. If validated, this mechanism would be particularly advantageous for treating melanin-rich or pigmented fungal infections, such as chromoblastomycosis, although further studies are required to confirm these hypotheses.

From a clinical perspective, since no prior clinical reports of ALA-PDT for sporotrichosis existed, we selected treatment parameters close to standard dermatologic practice—namely, 0.7 M ALA (10%) and a total light fluence of 100 J/cm2—combined with 100 mKI. Several considerations guided the choice of these parameters. First, a total light fluence of 100 J/cm2 was chosen because it remained within the patient’s tolerable range; although noticeable pain was experienced during irradiation, it was acceptable and did not require treatment interruption. Second, we used 0.7 M ALA, which is lower than the 1.4 M (20%) concentration recommended in the Chinese ALA-PDT guidelines [15]. This adjustment was made to reduce treatment cost and to accommodate the relatively large lesion area. Third, 100 mM KI was selected because higher concentrations with prolonged application tended to cause crystallization.

Following the above treatment protocol, the patient completed three sessions with good tolerability and no significant discomfort. Complete lesion resolution was achieved, with no recurrence during the one-year follow-up. It is noteworthy that prior to the first two sessions, erythromycin ointment and superficial CO2 laser debridement were performed as routine preparatory procedures according to the Chinese ALA-PDT guidelines [15]. These measures helped soften lesions, control secondary infection, remove melanized crusts, and enhance drug penetration. Additionally, host factors such as intact immunity and adequate tissue oxygenation likely contributed to the favorable outcome by facilitating the clearance of photodynamically damaged fungal cells, which may partly account for the superior clinical outcome compared with the modest in vitro results. However, a major limitation of this study is that it was a single case study without a control group, the specific contribution of KI cannot be clearly separated from the effects of the preparatory procedures and host factors. To address this limitation, future prospective controlled trials comparing KI-enhanced ALA-PDT, ALA-PDT alone, and a pretreatment control arm are needed to better evaluate the added value of KI and to establish broader clinical validity.

Conclusions

This study shows that KI enhances the antifungal activity of ALA-PDT against S. globosa under reduced and clinically feasible treatment parameters in vitro. In a single proof-of-concept patient intolerant to conventional antifungal therapy, KI-enhanced ALA-PDT was associated with complete and sustained clinical resolution, suggesting that this regimen may represent a potentially useful non-invasive treatment approach for selected patients with limited therapeutic options. However, the specific contribution of KI to the clinical outcome and the underlying mechanism remain unresolved. Further mechanistic studies and prospective controlled clinical investigations are needed before the broader clinical value of this regimen can be established and its optimal treatment parameters defined.
