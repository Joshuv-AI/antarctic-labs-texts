The development and validation of the short-form Family Inventory of Resources for Management among families of older people with dementia: instrument development and cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuxin Li, Chang Zan, Sanmei Chen, Shengnan Tang, Qiongqiong Zhang, Yijia Pan, Yu Sheng, Qingyan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFamily resources are crucial for older people with dementia, and the Family Inventory of Resources for Management (FIRM) is an important tool for assessing family resources. However, the 52 items of the FIRM can be overwhelming for respondents, limiting its practicality.ObjectivesTo develop a short-form Family Inventory of Resources for Management (SF-FIRM) and evaluate its psychometric properties among families of older people with dementia.MethodsWe recruited 631 families of patients with dementia from six tertiary hospitals in Beijing and Xuzhou, Jiangsu Province, China, using convenience sampling. The families were divided into Sample 1 and Sample 2. Item analysis was conducted to develop the SF-FIRM by examining the item-sub-scale correlation coefficients, Cronbach’s alpha after item deletion, critical ratios, and variable coefficients in Sample 1. The content validity, convergent validity, structural validity, and internal consistency reliability were assessed to evaluate the psychometric properties of the SF-FIRM in Sample 1 and Sample 2.ResultsThe SF-FIRM consists of seven sub-scales (Positive Evaluation, Sense of Coherence, Vitality, Balance, Mastery, Financial Well-Being, and Extended Family Social Support) and 21 items. The content validity assessment results show that both the item and scale content validity indices were 1.00. Convergent validity was supported by the correlation with family adaptation (Sample 1: r = 0.60, P < 0.01; Sample 2: r = 0.64, P < 0.01). Evidence of construct validity was established based on the following indices for Samples 1 and 2: chi-square fit index = 1.28 and 1.35, comparative fit index = 0.98 and 0.99, Tucker–Lewis index = 0.98 and 0.99, and root mean square error of approximation = 0.03 and 0.04. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients indicated good internal consistency reliability, with alpha values of 0.87 and omega values of 0.90 in Sample 1, and alpha values of 0.92 and omega values of 0.95 in Sample 2.ConclusionThe SF-FIRM is a seven-sub-scale 21-item instrument for assessing the family resources of older people with dementia that has satisfactory psychometric properties. It identifies seven key aspects of family resources in families of older people with dementia, and will facilitate the measurement of family resources in older people with dementia, playing a crucial role in enhancing the health and well-being of both patients and their caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06293-x.Keywords: Nursing, Family, Scale, Dementia, Validation study

Background

There are currently 55 million older people with dementia worldwide, and this number will almost triple by 2050 [1]. Patients with dementia typically undergo a progressive decline in their functional abilities, often necessitating the involvement of the patient’s family in disease management [2]. Family resources refer to a family’s abilities and capacities to manage stressors and their demands, which include personal, family system, and social resources [3]. Family resources play an important role when families encounter difficulties [4, 5]. Studies have shown that inadequate family resources result in significant issues, including increased negative emotions in patients, reduced quality of patient care, and an adverse impact on overall family well-being [6–8]. Due to the various levels of family resources among families of older people with dementia, an accurate measurement having sound psychometric properties would greatly assist nurses in identifying family resource levels and the specific categories of resources they lack and providing family-targeted support.

Studies have developed various family resource measurements, such as the Family Resource Scale [9], Family Resources Questionnaire [10], Family Resources Questionnaire-SCREEM [11], and Family Inventory of Resources for Management [3]. The Family Resource Scale was specifically developed for children [12–14]. It is inapplicable to older people with dementia, as the resources needed for children’s development and growth (such as nutrition and care) are fundamentally different from those needed by older people [15]. The Family Resources Questionnaire and the Family Resources Questionnaire-SCREEM measure family resources from family and social aspects. Research highlights the critical role of personal resources in sustaining family functionality during serious illnesses [16]. Notably, older adults with dementia heavily depend on the personal resources offered by their families. The Family Inventory of Resources for Management (FIRM) measures family resources from personal, family system, and social resources, and it more suitable for older people with dementia [17, 18]. The FIRM has been translated into Chinese, Persian, and Korean [5, 19, 20]. Both the original version and its different translations demonstrate good internal consistency (α = 0.85–0.91) and satisfactory convergent validity, as indicated by moderate correlations with Family Environment scales and the Caregiver Burden Inventory [3, 5, 19]. However, the structural validity of all FIRM versions remains unreported, making its precise validity uncertain. This uncertainty may stem from potential content overlap in the first two sub-scales, which could compromise the scale’s structural validity. For instance, the first sub-scale, “Family Strength I: Esteem and Communication,” encompasses various aspects including family esteem, communication, mutual support, optimism, problem-solving ability, and encouragement of autonomy among family members. Similarly, the second sub-scale, “Family Strength II: Mastery and Health,” covers elements such as the sense of mastery over family events and outcomes, family mutuality, and physical and emotional health. Given the extensive range of dimensions these two sub-scales cover, they are not suitable to be categorized within a single scale.

Additionally, the extensive items of the FIRM (approximately 55 items of different versions) impose a response burden on participants. This burden will result in reduced response rates, lower completion rates, and an overall decline in response quality [21]. There is one simplified Korean version of the FIRM currently [20], which assesses only resources related to the family system and social support, and the scale was validated with single-parent maternal families, we are uncertain whether it is suitable for families of older people with dementia.

Therefore, we first aimed to re-explore the first two sub-scales of the Family Inventory of Resources for Management. Building on this foundation, we developed a short-form Family Inventory of Resources for Management (SF-FIRM) and assess its psychometric properties.

Methods

Study design and participants

We used convenience sampling to recruit families of older people with dementia from memory-speciality or geriatric psychiatry outpatient clinics. The families were divided into Sample 1 and Sample 2. For exploratory factor analysis, a frequently cited rule is to include 5 participants per variable and at least 200 participants total [22]. The original scale has 52 items, so the sample size for sample 1 should be at least 260 participants. For confirmatory factor analysis, according to the equations of the minimum sample size (), the minimum sample size for sample 2 was 140, when the df = 168, ε = 0.05, the power (1-β) = 0.80, and the (δ is the non-centrality parameter) [23]. Sample 1 included sample 1a—four tertiary hospitals in Beijing from October 2017 to March 2018 (N = 210), and sample 1b—two tertiary hospitals in Xuzhou from February 2022 to May 2022 (N = 138). Sample 2 included families from two tertiary hospitals in Xuzhou from June 2022 to December 2022 (n = 283). Each family was asked to select an informal caregiver as a participant. The inclusion criteria for informal caregivers were (a) caring for patients with moderate-to-severe dementia diagnoses and (b) having lived with the patients for six months or longer. The exclusion criteria were (a) not being a family member of the patient (i.e., employed outside help) and (b) having cognitive dysfunction or a psychiatric episode experience that could affect their ability to cooperate. We collected the data via a questionnaire. Of the 674 questionnaires collected from the participants, 43 were excluded because of poor quality or incomplete (missing ≥ 10% of the requested information) responses. Thus, data from 631 valid questionnaires were available for analysis (93.6% valid response rate).

Instruments

General information questionnaire

The general information questionnaire used in this study asked about age (years), sex (male or female), education level (junior high school and below, high school or secondary school, college or above), and the average monthly income (Chinese yuan) of the patient and informal caregiver.

Family inventory of resources for management

We used the Chinese version of the FIRM as a basis for developing the SF-FIRM. The Chinese version of the FIRM consists of 52 items and four sub-scales: Family Strength I: Esteem and Communication; Family Strength II: Mastery and Health; Extended Family Social Support; and Financial Well-Being. Participants are asked to choose the answer that best describes the resources their family has, using the options ‘not at all’, ‘minimally’, ‘moderately’, and ‘very well’, with corresponding scores of 0, 1, 2, and 3, respectively. Approximately half (24) of the items are reverse scored due to their expression of negative meanings. The total score is calculated by summing the responses to all the items. A higher total score indicates a greater abundance of resources within the family. The Cronbach’s alpha was 0.90 [3].

Family adaptation scale

We used the Chinese version of the Family Adaptation Scale to evaluate the convergent validity of the SF-FIRM. The scale consists of 15 items and three sub-scales. The items are responded to using a 7-point Likert scale, where 1 = ‘dissatisfied’ and 7 = ‘fully satisfied’. The total scale score is the sum of all item scores and can range from 15 to 105. Higher scores indicate higher levels of satisfaction. The Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.95, and the Cronbach’s alpha for the sub-scales ranged from 0.89 to 0.90 [24].

Statistical analysis

Analyzing general information

Means and standard deviations were calculated for the normally distributed data (age), and medians and quartiles were calculated for the abnormally distributed data (income). The categorical variables were described using the composition ratio (sex, education level, and relationship with patients).

Shorten procedure of the FIRM

We firstly conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) separately on the first two sub-scales of the FIRM to clarify their structures in Sample 1. Following this, we carried out an item analysis to remove poorly performing items, leading to the development of the SF-FIRM. Finally, we assessed the psychometric properties of the SF-FIRM using both Sample1 and Sample 2.

Clarifying the structure of the first two sub-scales of the original FIRM

We performed an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) on the Family Strengths I and II sub-scales of the original FIRM using Sample 1, without any pre-established hypotheses. During the exploratory factor analysis, items were excluded if their loadings were below 0.4 across all dimensions or if the difference in loadings between any two dimensions was less than 0.1. The analysis was repeated after each item removal until all items satisfied these criteria. An acceptable result was defined as the cumulative variance explained by the retained factors exceeding 40%.

Removing poorly performing items to form the SF-FIRM

With approval from the original FIRM development team, we simplified the FIRM scale for families of patients with dementia in Sample 1. To ensure that the SF-FIRM retains a structure similar to the FIRM, which underwent exploratory factor analysis on the first two sub-scales, item analysis involved item-subscale correlation coefficients, Cronbach’s alpha after item deletion, critical ratios, and variable coefficients. Items were retained if they had (a) correlation coefficients with the total scores of the sub-scales ranging from 0.4 to 0.8, (b) a reduction in the Cronbach’s alpha of the corresponding sub-scale if deleted, (c) significantly different scores for the high and low subgroups, and (d) a variable coefficient ≥ 0.25 [25]. We retained three items for each sub-scale. If more than three items satisfied these conditions, we prioritized the remaining items with higher item-sub-scale correlation coefficients, followed by items with higher variable coefficients [26].

Evaluating the psychometric properties of the SF-FIRM

First, six experts were invited to rate the consistency of each item with family resources to assess content validity. Content validity was considered acceptable if the item-level content validity index (I-CVI) was ≥ 0.78 and the scale-level content validity index (S-CVI) was ≥ 0.90 [27, 28].

Second, we assess the convergent validity, structural validity, and internal consistency reliability of Sample 1 and Sample 2. Convergent validity was assessed by correlating the SF-FIRM with the Family Adaptation Scale. A correlation coefficient between 0.10 and 0.29 was considered weak, 0.30 to 0.49 was considered moderate, and 0.50 to 1.0 was considered strong [29]. The structural validity of the SF-FIRM was assessed by conducting a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The fit of the model was evaluated using the chi-square fit index (2/df), comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). A value of 2/df ≤ 3.0, CFI ≥ 0.90, TLI ≥ 0.90, and RMSEA ≤ 0.08 indicated good structural validity [30–32]. The internal consistency reliability was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and McDonald’s omega coefficient. A Cronbach’s alpha coefficient or a McDonald’s omega coefficient between 0.80 and 0.90 was considered perfect. The lowest acceptable cutoff points were 0.65 for Cronbach’s alpha and 0.70 for McDonald’s omega, respectively [33, 34].

CFA was performed with IBM SPSS AMOS 24.0 (IBM; Armonk, NY, USA) and McDonald’s omega coefficient was performed with Rstudio 4.2.1 (RStudio, PBC; Boston, MA, USA). The other analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 26.0 (IBM; Armonk, NY, USA).

SF-FIRM sub-scales and items

The SF-FIRM consists of 21 items and seven sub-scales: Positive Evaluation, Sense of Coherence, Vitality, Balance, Mastery, Financial Well-Being, and Extended Family Social Support. The results of the EFA are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Positive Evaluation and Sense of Coherence are new factors obtained through EFA of Family Strength I. Vitality, Balance, and Mastery are new factors obtained through EFA of Family Strength II. Financial Well-Being and Extended Family Social Support retained the structure of the FIRM. Positive Evaluation reflects family members’ agreement and approval of the family’s thoughts, desires, behaviors, and traits. Sense of Coherence reflects the family’s confidence in clarifying the nature of the problem. Vitality reflects family members’ energy and liveliness, as demonstrated by their actions, thoughts, and expressions. Balance reflects the equal contributions of family members to family responsibilities. Mastery reflects the degree of control a family has over the planned events in their lives. The Financial Well-Being items reflect the family’s perceived financial efficacy, and the Extended Family Social Support items reflect the mutual help and support given to and received from relatives. Table 2 displays the seven sub-scales and 21 items with their item-sub-scale correlations, changes in Cronbach’s alpha after item deletion, critical ratios, and variable coefficients. All 21 items showed a significant correlation with the total scores of their corresponding sub-scale, and the correlation coefficient ranged from 0.68 to 0.81. Deleting any of these items would reduce the Cronbach’s alpha for their corresponding sub-scale. Furthermore, all the items exhibited a significant difference between the high-scoring and low-scoring groups, and the variable coefficients all exceeded 0.28. The same information is displayed for the deleted items in Supplementary Table 2.

The SF-FIRM demonstrated strong internal consistency, with both the overall scale and its sub-scales exhibiting Cronbach’s alpha values and McDonald’s omega coefficients of 0.7 or higher. In sample 1, the SF-FIRM demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87 and a McDonald’s omega of 0.90. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.72 and the McDonald’s omega coefficient was 0.73 for Positive Evaluation, 0.72 and 0.75 for Sense of Coherence, 0.80 and 0.80 for Vitality, 0.72 and 0.75 for Balance, 0.74 and 0.75 for Mastery, 0.70 and 0.71 for Financial Well-Being, and 0.71 and 0.71 for Extended Family Social Support. In sample 2, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient and the McDonald’s omega coefficient of the SF-FIRM was 0.92 and 0.95 respectively. For sub-scales, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.78 and the McDonald’s omega coefficient was 0.78 for Positive Evaluation, 0.80 and 0.81 for Sense of Coherence, 0.74 and 0.78 for Vitality, 0.88 and 0.89 for Balance, 0.85 and 0.86 for Mastery, 0.91 and 0.92 for Financial Well-Being, and 0.92 and 0.92 for Extended Family Social Support.

Discussion

In the present study, we simplified the Family Inventory of Resources for Management (FIRM) into the short-form FIRM (SF-FIRM) and evaluated the psychometric properties of the SF-FIRM in families of older people with dementia. The results indicated that the SF-FIRM exhibits strong psychometric properties, comprising seven sub-scales and 21 items. It may serve as a valuable tool for assessing family resources in families of older people with dementia. This study contributes to nursing management by creating a reliable and valid shortened version of the scale.

We identified seven sub-scales of family resources among families of older people with dementia: positive evaluation, sense of coherence, vitality, balance, mastery, financial well-being, and extended family social support. The sub-scales of financial well-being and extended family social support within this structure are consistent with the original FIRM, the Chinese version of FIRM, and the Iranian version of FIRM. This alignment adheres to the Resource-Based View theory, which posits that resources can be categorized into tangible resources (financial well-being) and intangible resources (extended family social support) [35]. Due to the exploratory factor analysis conducted on Family Strength I and Family Strength II, the constructs of positive evaluation, sense of coherence, vitality, balance, and mastery differ from those in other versions of the FIRM. Family Strength I is divided into positive evaluation and sense of coherence, aligning with the Salutogenesis theory, which posits that individuals effectively utilize resources to adapt to stress through positive evaluation and enhanced sense of coherence [36]. Family Strength II is divided into vitality, balance, and mastery, aligning with the Family Ecosystem Theory’s perspective on the interaction between the family as a whole and the individual, collectively responding to stress [37]. Additionally, there are significant structural differences between the SF-FIRM and the Korean version of FIRM. The Korean version includes sub-scales such as family finances, family health, instrumental support, and emotional support. These differences might be attributed to the fact that the Korean version was developed for single mothers, differing in target population from our scale. Furthermore, cultural influences specific to Korea contribute to the structural variations observed in comparison to the SF-FIRM.

The SF-FIRM demonstrated satisfactory content validity, convergent validity, and internal consistency both in Sample 1 and Sample 2. This aligns with the original scale, which demonstrated an internal consistency of 0.89 and moderate correlations with the Family Environment scales [3]. Similarly, the Chinese version of FIRM showed a content validity index ranging from 0.83 to 1.00, an S-CVI of 0.97, and an internal consistency of 0.91 [5]. The Iranian version also evidenced an internal consistency of 0.89, with moderate correlations observed with the Caregiver Burden Inventory [19]. This may be attributed to the excellent properties of original FIRM [3], which provided a better basis for simplification. Another possible reason for the sound convergent validity and internal consistency reliability of the sub-scales is that we clarified the internal structure of Family Strengths I and II through exploratory factor analysis, which maximizes the difference between sub-scales and the consistency within sub-scales [38]. A further possible reason for the sound internal consistency reliability could be that we intended to select the items with high consistency using the alpha-if-item deleted indicator.

Strengths and limitations

This study has two main strengths. First, we simplified the original FIRM by maintaining its concept integrity and satisfying psychometric properties based on our clarified factor structures and used item analysis, including item-sub-scale correlations and Cronbach’s alpha, after item deletion of the selected items. Second, we evaluated the psychometric properties in two samples and conducted sensitivity analyses across samples from different time periods and various caregiver populations, confirming that variations in sample timing and caregiver type had minimal impact on the psychometric indicators of the SF-FIRM.

The limitations of this study include its exclusive focus on the relationship between caregivers and patients, without examining the duration of cohabitation. This omission prevents us from assessing whether variations in cohabitation duration affect the scale’s psychometric properties. Besides, the psychometric properties of the SF-FIRM were evaluated only in families of older people with dementia, with samples collected from two cities in China. Consequently, we urge caution in generalizing our findings to families with different patients or in other geographic regions.

Conclusions

The SF-FIRM is a seven-sub-scale 21-item instrument for assessing the family resources of older people with dementia that has satisfactory psychometric properties. It identifies seven key aspects of family resources in families of older people with dementia, and will facilitate the measurement of family resources in older people with dementia, playing a crucial role in enhancing the health and well-being of both patients and their caregivers.

Theoretical and practical value for healthcare of older people

The theoretical significance of this study lies in identifying seven key aspects of family resources in families of older people with dementia: Positive Evaluation, Sense of Coherence, Vitality, Balance, Mastery, Financial Well-Being, and Extended Family Social Support. These aspects align with the principles of Resource-Based View theory, Salutogenesis theory, and Family Ecosystem Theory.

The practical significance is that the SF-FIRM can be utilized by nurses to assess and strategically manage family resources in families of older people with dementia. The total score can be utilized to evaluate the family resource level. Nurses can focus on families who lack sufficient resources. Furthermore, the seven sub-scales of the SF-FIRM indicate various family resources on specific aspects. Nurses can leverage this information to focus interventions on the categories the family lacks. For example, for families with a poor sense of coherence, nurses can implement measures such as interactive education to enhance knowledge and skills in caregiving, enrich the action plan, and discuss concerns and barriers regarding the plan to improve their sense of coherence [39].
