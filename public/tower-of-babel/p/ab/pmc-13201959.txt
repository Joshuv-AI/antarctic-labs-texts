The “en bloc” shift: ipsilateral and contralateral abducens nerve palsy following microvascular decompression for trigeminal neuralgia caused by vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia. Illustrative cases
Authors: Shoko Ito, Kenzo Kosugi, Hiroaki Fukumoto, Taichi Sayanagi, Konosuke Ishikawa, Narumi Watanabe, Takenori Akiyama, Masahiro Toda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDMicrovascular decompression (MVD) for trigeminal neuralgia (TN) caused by vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia (VBD) is technically challenging due to the vessel’s size and stiffness. While ipsilateral abducens nerve (cranial nerve [CN] VI) palsy is a known complication, contralateral palsy resulting from force transmission remains unreported.OBSERVATIONSThe authors describe 2 cases of CN VI palsy following MVD for VBD-associated TN. Case 1 developed ipsilateral palsy after caudal transposition of the vertebral artery, resulting from a paradoxical medial shift of the proximal vessel segment. Case 2 presented with a rare contralateral CN VI palsy. Postoperative imaging indicated that mobilizing the rigid VBD caused an “en bloc” shift of the entire vertebrobasilar system, resulting in secondary compression of the contralateral CN VI. Both patients achieved immediate pain relief, and the palsies recovered with conservative management.LESSONSMobilizing a dolichoectatic VBD transmits mechanical force to distant segments via an en bloc shift, potentially injuring CN VI on either the operative or contralateral side. Preoperative identification of risk factors—specifically severe calcification, preexisting contralateral deviation, and minimal contralateral CSF space—is crucial. Surgeons should recognize that vascular transposition can transmit force bilaterally and must counsel patients regarding these risks. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2634

Vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia (VBD) affects the basilar and vertebral arteries in 0.05%–0.6% of the population and is identified as the offending pathology in 0.9%–5.8% of trigeminal neuralgia (TN) cases.1 In VBD-associated TN, the tortuous vessel often stretches and displaces the ipsilateral abducens nerve (cranial nerve [CN] VI), necessitating meticulous dissection during microvascular decompression (MVD) surgery to prevent iatrogenic injury.2 While ipsilateral CN VI palsy is a known risk, to our knowledge, contralateral CN VI palsy following MVD has not been reported. Here, we describe 2 cases of CN VI palsy following MVD for VBD-associated TN: one occurring ipsilaterally and the other contralaterally.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A man in his 50s presented with a 2-year history of left facial numbness, V1–V2 pain, conjunctival injection, and lacrimation. He was diagnosed with short-lasting unilateral neuralgiform headache with conjunctival injection and tearing (SUNCT). MRI revealed a tortuous left vertebral artery (VA) causing severe ventral compression of the brainstem medial to the trigeminal nerve, resulting in nerve thinning (Fig. 1A and C). Additionally, T2 hyperintensity was observed in the compressed pontine parenchyma. Conservative management with carbamazepine (600 mg/day), lamotrigine (25 mg/day), and mirogabalin (10 mg/day) proved refractory, leading to surgical intervention. Surgery was performed via a left retrosigmoid approach. Intraoperatively, the VA loop was observed compressing CN V from the inferior side (Fig. 1D). The VA, which was indenting the brainstem, was mobilized in a rotational manner to initially release the brainstem compression (Video 1). Because the vessel remained in close proximity to CN V, the VA was subsequently transposed caudally—carefully avoiding medial displacement—and secured with Teflon felt (Fig. 1E, Video 1). During the procedure, CN VI could not be visualized as the surgical view was obstructed by the large VA. Although the SUNCT symptoms resolved immediately postoperatively, the patient developed ipsilateral abducens palsy. We hypothesize that ventral transposition of the brainstem-embedded VA caused a paradoxical medial shift of the proximal vessel segment, compressing the abducens nerve. The patient was treated with a tapering course of intravenous dexamethasone (starting at 8 mg/day) and mecobalamin. Although initially presenting with complete palsy and diplopia, he showed gradual improvement. The palsy fully resolved by 6 months, and diplopia disappeared shortly thereafter. At the 1-year follow-up, the patient remained pain free (Barrow Neurological Institute [BNI] pain intensity score 1), and postoperative MRI confirmed resolution of the brainstem T2 hyperintensity.

Case 2

A 60-year-old woman presented with a 3-year history of right TN involving the maxillary (V2) distribution. Although carbamazepine (400 mg/day) initially provided relief, the pain recurred. Dose escalation to 600 mg/day was precluded by adverse effects (dizziness), prompting surgical intervention. Preoperative MRI revealed that the right anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) was directly compressing CN V. Furthermore, the dilated, tortuous right VA, exhibiting an irregular caliber suggestive of atherosclerotic changes, was pushing the AICA against the nerve; therefore, both the VA and AICA were identified as the offending vessels (Fig. 2A–C). Intraoperatively, the ectatic VA was identified coursing between the lower CNs and the vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII) (Fig. 2D), displacing the latter rostrally and compressing the caudal aspect of CN V (Fig. 2E). The VA was carefully transposed caudally, with strict preservation of the surrounding perforating arteries (Fig. 2F, Video 2). This maneuver exposed the caudal aspect of the trigeminal nerve and confirmed the vascular compression, allowing for complete decompression. Postoperatively, while the patient achieved immediate pain relief, she developed a complete contralateral abducens nerve palsy. She was managed with a 1-week course of intravenous dexamethasone and mecobalamin. At the 6-month follow-up, physical examination demonstrated recovery of full abduction in the contralateral (left) eye, although subjective diplopia persisted but continued to improve. Importantly, the debilitating facial pain remained completely resolved (BNI pain intensity score 1), substantially improving her quality of life. Discussion

Observations

In the presented cases, although the site of vascular mobilization was anatomically remote from the abducens nerve, the procedure likely caused an “en bloc” shift of the rigid, atherosclerotic dolichoectatic vessel. This global displacement resulted in secondary compression of the abducens nerve outside the surgical field of view.

While MVD for VBD-associated TN is essential given the poor outcomes of conservative management, it carries a higher risk of complications compared to cases involving smaller vessels.1,3 Mobilizing a dolichoectatic VBD requires manipulating a large, stiff vessel. This maneuver can transmit force to distant segments, potentially injuring the abducens nerve. In this context, the risk of traction injury depends primarily on the magnitude of vessel mobilization rather than the specific fixation technique used (e.g., Teflon interposition vs sling). Indeed, Inoue et al. demonstrated that in VBD-associated TN, the abducens nerve root is frequently displaced rostromedially by the tortuous artery and is often obscured behind the vessel, putting it at significant risk during blind displacement.2 Choudhari also reported a case of abducens nerve palsy following MVD for TN, attributing it to traction on the nerve that was not visualized intraoperatively.4

Clinicians must recognize that preventing this complication is challenging. Although endoscopic inspection has been proposed to prevent nerve injury, its utility is limited in severe dolichoectasia where the ectatic vessel occupies the prepontine cistern,3 and it is entirely inapplicable for monitoring the contralateral side.

Considering the limitations of intraoperative visualization techniques, monitoring of the abducens nerve using lateral rectus muscle electromyography can be helpful. However, it is not our standard practice to perform such monitoring routinely in all cases. Instead, we believe that careful preoperative imaging assessment is essential to identify patients at higher risk of abducens nerve injury. In cases in which a high risk is anticipated, monitoring of the lateral rectus muscle may be useful for intraoperative nerve protection.

Pathophysiologically, the abducens nerve is tethered at Dorello’s canal and is vulnerable to traction.5 In VBD cases, the nerve is often already chronically stretched and displaced, even in asymptomatic patients.2,6 This state of “pre-tension” lowers the threshold for injury, where even minor additional traction from surgical manipulation precipitates symptomatic palsy. Crucially, this mechanical stress can extend to the contralateral side. Giray et al. reported isolated abducens palsy caused by a contralateral dolichoectatic VA, proving that pathological force can cross the midline.7 Furthermore, Strauss et al. described contralateral hearing loss after MVD, suggesting that intraoperative manipulation can affect contralateral CNs via brainstem shift or venous congestion.8

Given the mechanism of force transmission through a rigid vascular structure, preoperative radiological assessment is paramount for identifying patients at high risk for this specific complication. Surgeons should scrutinize preoperative imaging not only for the offending vessel but also for the contralateral anatomy. Patients with a dolichoectatic basilar artery already deviating toward the contralateral side, or those with minimal CSF space around the contralateral abducens nerve, represent a high-risk cohort. In such cases, even millimeter-scale en bloc shifts can precipitate palsy. Furthermore, severe calcification of the vessel wall implies a rigid body that is more likely to transmit force to distant segments rather than absorbing it through deformation. Identification of these “anatomical predictors” should prompt a more cautious surgical strategy.

Lessons

Consequently, when performing MVD for TN caused by a dolichoectatic VBD, surgeons should be aware that vascular transposition can transmit force bilaterally. Preoperative counseling must explicitly address the risk of abducens nerve palsy, including the possibility of contralateral involvement, particularly in patients exhibiting the aforementioned risk factors.
