Antidepressant-like effects of ketamine involve CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling-mediated synaptic plasticity in the mPFC
Authors: Yong-Yu Yin, Si-Rui Sun, Hui-Ying Zhang, Peng-Wei Bi, Jia-Ning Zhao, Hao Cheng, Yun-Feng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractKetamine, a non-competitive N-methyl-D-aspartate acid (NMDA) receptor antagonist, produces rapid and sustained antidepressant actions, but the underlying molecular mechanism remains unclear. The CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling is closely related to mood disorders, and this study aims to investigate its role in ketamine’s antidepressant actions. We pharmacologically (AZD8797, a selective CX3CR1 antagonist) and genetically (intra-mPFC microinjection with AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA) manipulated the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling and investigated their effects on ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects in mice treated with corticosterone (Cort), and observed changes in synaptic plasticity in response to these manipulations. We found that 24 h after drug injection, ketamine (10 mg/kg, i.p.) significantly reversed the Cort-induced depression-like behaviors, and inhibited the overexpression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and microglial activation. Ketamine significantly improved the Cort-induced impairment in the dendritic complexity and spine densities. In addition, our ELISA results showed that ketamine significantly inhibited the activation of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling, and ketamine attenuated the upregulation of CX3CR1 and CX3CL1 expression in Cort-treated HT22 and BV2 cells in vitro. Furthermore, pretreatment with AZD8797 (0.8 mg/kg, i.p., twice a week) completely blocked ketamine’s antidepressant-like behavioral effects and eliminated ketamine-induced enhancement in the synaptic plasticity; intra-mPFC microinjection with AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA also prevented ketamine’s behavioral effects and beneficial effects on the synaptic plasticity. These findings demonstrated that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling-mediated synaptic plasticity played essential roles in ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects, which opened a new door to targeting chemokines to improve depression symptoms. Subject terms: Molecular neuroscience, Clinical pharmacology

Introduction

Depression is one of the most common psychiatric disorders and is characterized by a low mood state, anhedonia, and spiritual loss. First-line antidepressants (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, SSRIs; serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, SNRIs) have many clinical limitations, such as requiring weeks to months to produce clinical responses (4–6 weeks) and low effectiveness (50–70%) [1, 2]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel and more effective treatments.

Ketamine, as a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist, which is a racemic mixture that contains equal amounts of R-ketamine and S-ketamine [3], produces rapid and sustained antidepressant effects in rodent models [4, 5] and clinical trials [6, 7]. Many preclinical studies suggest that both enantiomers produce antidepressant-like effects, with R-ketamine showing longer-lasting effects than S-ketamine [8–10]. The mechanisms underlying ketamine’s antidepressant actions have attracted widespread interest in the field of depression. Specifically, mounting evidence suggests that the synaptic plasticity in the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) might be necessary for ketamine’s unique antidepressant actions [11, 12]. In addition, recent studies suggest that the anti-inflammatory potential of ketamine might be closely related to its antidepressant actions [13, 14]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the sustained antidepressant effects of ketamine remain largely unknown.

Microglia-driven changes in synaptic plasticity in depression are increasingly attracting researchers’ attention [15, 16]. Microglia respond to stress-triggered neuroinflammation and produce adverse effects on the synaptic plasticity in the central nervous system (CNS), which is intensely involved in the pathogenesis of depression [15]. Is it necessary for microglia-driven synaptic plasticity in ketamine’s antidepressant actions? Preclinical studies reported that ketamine rapidly increased synaptic signaling proteins and increased the number and function of new spine synapses in the PFC [17], and microglial inhibition blocked ketamine’s effects on the reduced dendritic spine density in the mPFC [18]. However, what’s the critical modulator of the microglia-driven synaptic plasticity underlying ketamine’s antidepressant actions? Understanding this vital issue may shed light on developing new treatment strategies to fight against this devastating mental illness.

Intriguingly, accumulating evidence shows that there has been much interest in the involvement of chemokines in mood-related disorders. CX3CL1 (also called fractalkine) is a critical chemokine that is primarily expressed by neurons [19]; its only receptor, CX3CR1, is exclusively expressed in microglia cells [20]. In the CNS, CX3CL1 and CX3CR1 form a signaling network between neurons and microglia, which contributes a lot to the pathophysiology of depression. For example, CX3CR1 knockout mice displayed resistance to depression-like behaviors compared to wild-type controls [21]. In addition, many studies have confirmed that the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling plays an essential role in regulating microglial activation and neuroinflammation [22, 23], which might be closely related to depression. However, to date, little is known about the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the antidepressant actions of ketamine.

Hence, to directly test our hypothesis, we pharmacologically and genetically manipulated the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling and investigated their effects on ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects in the Cort-induced model of depression; we also observed ketamine’s effects on the synaptic plasticity in the mPFC responding to these manipulations. These investigations might provide insights into the molecular mechanism underlying ketamine’s antidepressant actions.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks (Beijing SPF Laboratory Animal Technology Company, Beijing, China) were used in this study. Mice were housed in groups under standard conditions at a controlled temperature (23 ± 1 °C), humidity (40–60%), and a 12-h light/dark cycle (light on 07:00) for 2-week acclimation. Food and water were available ad libitum. Specifically, this study used male mice to avoid potential confounding effects caused by hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle in females, which could affect behavioral outcomes. All procedures followed the current laws of China and the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH publication No. 86-23, revised 2011) and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Beijing Institute of Pharmacology and Toxicology (animal ethics protocol number: IACUC-DWZX-2023-P523). All efforts are made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.

Drug and dose

Ketamine (Beijing Institute of Pharmacology and Toxicology, China) was intraperitoneal injected with 10 mg/kg [24]; AZD8797 (HY-13848, MedChemExpress), a selective CX3CR1 antagonist, was intraperitoneally injected with 0.8 mg/kg [25]; Corticosterone (Cort) hemisuccinate (Steraloids) was dissolved in regular tap water in a concentration of 25 μg/ml for mice [26]. All drugs were administered i.p. in a volume of 0.1 ml/10 g.

Cort-induced model

The Cort-induced model of depression was established as previously reported methods with minor modifications [27]. Cort was dissolved in tap water at a fixed concentration of 25 µg/mL by increasing the pH to 13 and stirring for 3–8 h. Following dissolution, pH was neutralized to 7.0–7.4 with 10 N HCl. Solutions were stored at 4 °C and used within 72 h of preparation to keep mice being exposed to fresh Cort drinking. Mice were exposed to Cort in drinking water for 4 consecutive weeks, whereas control mice received normal drinking water.

Open field test (OFT)

The OFT was adapted from previously reported methods [24]. Mice were acclimated to the experimental room for at least 60 min before the test session of OFT; mice were placed in the corner of a plastic box (40 × 40 × 20 cm), and the spontaneous locomotor activity of the mice was videotaped for 5 min and analyzed using the SMART Video Tracking System V3.0 (Beijing Zhong Shi Di Chuang Technology Development Co., Ltd. labmazev3.0). The box was cleaned with a 75% ethanol solution to remove odor, urine, or feces after each test.

Tail suspension test (TST)

The TST was performed as reported with minor modifications [28]. Specifically, the TST was performed in a white plastic chamber (55 height × 10 width × 10 cm depth), and mice were suspended at the top of the apparatus by adhesive tape placed approximately 1 cm from the tip of the tail (in a head-down position). The immobility time in the last 4 min of a total 6 min suspension was recorded: each mouse was adapted for 2 min after being suspended, and only the remaining 4 min was analyzed. Immobility was defined as the absence of any limb or body movements, except for those caused by respiration. After each test, the box was cleaned with a 75% ethanol solution to remove odor, urine, or feces.

Forced swimming test (FST)

The FST was performed as previously described with minor modifications [29]. Briefly, mice were individually forced to swim in an open cylindrical container (height: 25 cm, diameter: 10 cm) containing 18 cm of water maintained at 24 ± 1 °C for 6 min. The immobility duration in the last 4 min of the total 6-min test was recorded: each mouse was adapted for 2 min in the water, and only the remaining 4 min was analyzed. Mice were considered immobile when they ceased struggling and remained floating motionless in the water, making only those movements necessary to keep their head above water.

Sucrose preference test (SPT)

To assess the anhedonia, a core symptom of depression, we performed the SPT as previously described with minor modifications [30]. Briefly, mice were first adaptively exposed to two bottles of 1% sucrose solution (w/v) with ad libitum access for 24 h. Then, one bottle of 1% sucrose solution and another bottle of tap water were administered for another 24 h. On the last day, the position of the two bottles was switched for 24 h to avoid side preference. After the adaptation period, mice were deprived of water and food for 24 h. During the test session, SPT was conducted in an individual mouse housed in a cage with free access to two respective bottles containing 1% sucrose solution and tap water for 1 h. To prevent side preferences in drinking behavior, the position of the two bottles was switched in the middle of the testing. Water and sucrose consumption were measured as changes in the weight of fluid consumed. The sucrose preference (%) = sucrose consumption / (sucrose consumption + water consumption) × 100.

Novelty-suppressed feeding test (NSFT)

We performed the NSFT as reported methods [30]. Briefly, each mouse was placed in one corner of an open plastic box (40 × 40 × 30 cm) with five food pellets placed in the center after 24 h of food deprivation. The exploratory activity of each mouse in the plastic box was observed for 5 min, and the latency to feed was recorded. Eating behaviors were defined as chewing and biting. New food pellets were placed in the center after each test. For each experiment, all behavioral tests were conducted on the same cohort of mice to ensure consistency in baseline characteristics (e.g., age, genetic background, and environmental exposure), thereby minimizing inter-individual variability. The order of behavioral tests was arranged from least to most stressful, and behavioral tests were successively performed 60 min apart, allowing mice to recover from the last stressful test.

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vector construction

To directly identify the biological function of CX3CR1 in the mPFC, the small-interfering RNA (siRNA) was designed using the core sequence (CCGCCAACUCCAUGAACAATT) as previously reported [31]. The scrambled control-siRNA (5′-CGCTGAGTACTTCGAAATGTC-3′) and CX3CR1-siRNA (5′- CCGCCAACTCCATGAACAA-3′) were cloned into U6-MCS-CAG-EGFP and confirmed by sequencing (Genechem Tech, Shanghai, China). AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA and AAV-control-siRNA were produced in co-transfecting 293 T cells; 72 h after transduction, the medium from the 293 T packaging cells was collected and concentrated by 63000 rpm ultracentrifugation for 2 h; the titer for AAV-siRNA was detected by Q-PCR. AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA or AAV-control-siRNA was diluted to 1 × 1012 transforming units (TU)/ml and injected bilaterally in the mPFC.

Stereotactic injection

Mice were anesthetized with 3% sodium pentobarbital solution (30 mg/kg, i.p.) and fixed in the stereotaxic apparatus (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA or AAV-control-siRNA was injected bilaterally in the mPFC through 33-gauge syringe needles linked to a syringe pump (Hamilton, Reno, NV, USA), targeting the prelimbic cortex (PrL) of the mPFC (coordinates from bregma: AP = 2.43 mm, ML = ± 0.5 mm, and DV = 1.2 mm). Mice received a total volume of 2 μL of AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA or AAV-Control-siRNA at a rate of 0.2 μL/min (1 μL per side). At the end of the injection, the needle was left in place for 5 min before being slowly removed, and the animals were kept for 3 weeks to allow for AAV expression, and then behavioral tests were performed.

Cell culture and treatment

Mouse neurons (HT22, 1101MOU-PUMC001005) and microglial cells (BV2, 1101MOU-PUMC000063) were purchased from the Cell Resource Center of Peking Union Medical College (Beijing, China). HT22 and BV2 cells were first cultured in a high-glucose DMEM medium (Hyclone, UT, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries, CT, USA), respectively. The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (High Glucose, Gibco) with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator of 5% CO2. When the cells were grown to approximately 80% confluency, they were used for experiments. Ketamine and Cort were diluted in DMEM to 10 μg/ml and 100 μM, and for short-term treatment, HT22 and BV2 cells were incubated individually with 100 μM Cort (Sigma-Aldrich) for 6 h; after pretreatment with Cort, HT22 and BV2 cells were incubated with 10 μg/ml of ketamine for the next 2 h.

Immunofluorescence staining

After behavioral testing, mice were anesthetized with 3% sodium pentobarbital solution (30 mg/kg, i.p.) and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were then cut into 20-μm sections and thoroughly washed with PBS for 5 min, blocked with 10% goat serum for 2 h, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody Iba-1 (Rabbit, 1:200, ab178846; Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Secondary antibodies included anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (#8889 S; 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology) and Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG (#4408; 1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology). Coverslips were mounted on glass slides with fluoroshield containing DAPI (F6057-20 mL, Sigma-Aldrich) and incubated at room temperature overnight. Images were acquired at a size of 560 × 900 μm with a 20× NA 0.5 dry objective lens on a fluorescence microscope (Olympus IX73PIF; Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a microscope digital camera (Olympus DP74; Olympus Corporation). For analysis, at least 20 images of the mPFC were used for each group of animals, and at least three images from one slice of the brain. The number and staining area of the Iba-1+ cells were determined using ImageJ software (v 1.45J; NIH, USA), and the number was expressed as the number of Iba-1+ cells per mm² in a 20-μm-thick section. All measurements were performed by an operator blinded to the section’s identity.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, CX3CL1, and CX3CR1 levels in the mPFC were determined with ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (USCN Life Sciences & Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Mice were sacrificed after behavioral testing, and the mPFC tissues were rapidly removed and stored at −80 °C until assayed. The samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was added to 96-well microplates. Absorbance (OD) was determined using a microplate spectrophotometer at 450 nm (Spectra Max i3× Molecular Devices, Silicon Valley, CA, USA), and sample concentration was calculated using a standard curve. All samples were processed in an identical manner, enabling direct comparison of relative protein levels across groups. Results are expressed as the protein concentration in the homogenate (ng/mL or pg/mL).

Golgi-Cox staining

Golgi-Cox staining, one of the most effective techniques for studying the morphology of neuronal dendrites and dendritic spines, was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (FD Neuro Technologies, Ellicott City, MD, USA). Briefly, the brains of mice were quickly removed and rinsed with double-distilled water. Then, brains were treated with impregnation solutions (A and B) and kept at room temperature for two weeks in total darkness. Three days after transferring into solution C (at room temperature in the dark), coronal sections (100 μm thickness) of the mPFC were cut on a cryostat (CM1860 UV, Leica Microsystem Ltd., Wetzlar, Germany) at −20 °C, and were then placed on a gelatin-coated microscope slide, stained, and dehydrated. Images were taken with an Eclipse Ci-L microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software. Only well-impregnated and distinguishable neurons with unbroken dendrites were chosen in this study. Spine density was estimated in the number of spines per 10 µm of dendrite length. For the quantitative analysis, two to three neurons from each section, three sections from each animal, and six animals per group were needed.

Sholl analysis

The Sholl analysis plugin in ImageJ automatically plotted a series of concentric circles for each neuron at 10 μm intervals from the center of the cell soma to the end of the farthest process. The number of branches intersecting each circle was calculated for quantitative analysis of neuron morphology as described previously [32]. We employed the Fiji toolkit integrated in ImageJ to perform Sholl analysis directly from bitmap images; tissue sections of six mice in each group were used for the Sholl analysis of neurons.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed by an observer blinded to the experimental protocol, and all data were expressed as the mean ± S.E.M. and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). For statistical differences, one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s test was performed for comparisons of three groups; two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test was performed for comparisons of intergroup. For all tests, differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Ketamine inhibited Cort-induced depression-like behaviors and increases in CX3CL1/CX3CR1 in the mPFC

We first evaluated the antidepressant-like behavioral effects of ketamine on the Cort-induced model of depression (Fig. 1). After 28-day drinking water with Cort, mice were injected with vehicle or ketamine (10 mg/kg, i.p.), and behavioral tests were performed after 24 h of drug injection (Fig. 1A). Our results suggested that compared with the Ctrl group, mice in the Cort group exhibited a significant decrease in the sucrose-preference ratio in the SPT (F(2, 27) = 6.371, P < 0.01; P < 0.05, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 1B) and a prolonged latency to feed in the NSFT (F(2, 27) = 10.21, P < 0.001; P < 0.001, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 1C), and ketamine significantly reversed these Cort-induced behavioral deficiencies. In addition, similar effects were observed in the TST and FST. Specifically, 24 h after drug injection, ketamine significantly inhibited the increase in the immobility time in the TST (F(2, 27) = 8.103, P < 0.01; P < 0.05, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 1D) and FST (F(2, 27) = 5.932, P < 0.01; P < 0.05, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 1E). Collectively, these behavioral results indicated that ketamine robustly recused the Cort-induced behavioral deficiencies in mice.

To further investigate the molecular mechanism underlying the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine, we then performed the ELISA experiments to measure the expression levels of CX3CL1 and its receptor CX3CR1 after behavioral tests. We observed that chronic Cort treatment significantly induced increases in CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC, and ketamine could completely reverse these Cort-induced molecular changes. Specifically, 24 h after drug injection, ketamine significantly reversed the Cort-induced increase in the expression levels of CX3CL1 (F(2, 12) = 5.615, P < 0.05; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 1F) and CX3CR1 (F(2, 12) = 4.828, P < 0.05; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 1I). Taken together, these molecular results indicated the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling might be involved in the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine. To further investigate the relationship between CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling and Cort-induced depression-like behaviors, we performed a simple linear regression analysis between CX3CL1/CX3CR1 levels and the immobility time in the TST and FST, respectively. CX3CL1 levels were positively correlated with the immobility time in the TST (P < 0.05, Fig. 1G) and FST (P < 0.05, Fig. 1H); In addition, CX3CR1 levels were positively correlated with the immobility time in the TST (P < 0.05, Fig. 1J) and FST (P < 0.01, Fig. 1K). Collectively, these results further indicated that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC might be involved in the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine in Cort-treated mice.

Ketamine inhibited the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and the activation of microglia in the mPFC

We then performed the ELISA and immunofluorescence staining experiments to measure the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and observed microglial activation in the mPFC. Notably, we found that Cort led to significant increases in the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. As shown in Fig. 2, 24 h after drug injection, ketamine (10 mg/kg, i.p.) significantly reduced the Cort-induced overexpression of IL-1β (F(2, 12) = 15.99, P < 0.001; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ctrl ; P < 0.001, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 2A) and TNF-α (F(2, 12) = 9.935, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 2C). Although the increase in Cort-induced expression of IL-6 did not reach statistical significance, ketamine treatment significantly reversed this effect (F(2, 12) = 5.010, P < 0.05; P = 0.0923, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 2B). In addition, our immunofluorescence staining results suggested that microglial activation might be involved in the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine in Cort-treated mice (Fig. 2D). Specifically, chronic Cort treatment significantly activated the microglia and ketamine completely inhibited the Cort-induced microglial activation in the mPFC (F(2, 24) = 13.47, P < 0.001; P < 0.001, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.001, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 2E). These results indicated that neuroinflammation might be implicated in the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine.

Ketamine normalized the Cort-induced attenuation in the synaptic plasticity of mPFC pyramidal neurons

To further investigate the mechanism underlying the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine, we performed the Golgi-Cox staining experiments to test whether ketamine could improve the Cort-induced damages in the synaptic plasticity (Fig. 3). 24 h after ketamine injection (10 mg/kg, i.p.), the mPFC tissue was quickly extracted to perform the Golgi-Cox staining experiments (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 3B and C, our Golgi-Cox staining results suggested that 4 weeks of Cort drinking significantly reduced the total dendritic length (F(2, 12) = 10.72, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 3D), and spine density (F(2, 12) = 6.839, P < 0.05; P < 0.05, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ket; Fig. 3E), and 24 h after drug injection, ketamine completely reversed these Cort-induced changes in the synaptic plasticity of mPFC pyramidal neurons. These morphological findings that ketamine alleviated Cort-induced synaptic plasticity impairments in the mPFC might offer critical mechanistic insights into its antidepressant-like effects.

CX3CR1 antagonist attenuated the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine

To further investigate whether the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling is necessary for the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine (Fig. 4), we pretreated mice with AZD8797 (0.8 mg/kg, i.p., twice a week) for two weeks, a selective CX3CR1 antagonist, and then performed the behavioral tests to evaluate its effects on ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects (Fig. 4A). We found that pretreatment with AZD8797 significantly reversed these ketamine-induced behavioral effects in the TST (F(1, 28) = 15.83, P < 0.001; P < 0.0001, Ctrl + Veh vs. Ket + Veh; P < 0.0001, Ket + Veh vs. Ket + AZD8797; P > 0.05, AZD8797 + Veh vs. AZD8797 + Ket; Fig. 4D) and FST (F(1, 28) = 8.319, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, Ctrl + Veh vs. Ket + Veh; P < 0.05, Ket + Veh vs. Ket + AZD8797; P > 0.05, AZD8797 + Veh vs. AZD8797 + Ket; Fig. 4E). In addition, pretreatment with AZD8797 did not affect the spontaneous activity of mice and there was no significant difference in total distances of different groups (F(1, 28) = 1.898, P > 0.05; Fig. 4B). However, AZD8797 significantly blocked the effects of ketamine on the distances in the center (%) (F(1, 28) = 7.215, P < 0.05; P < 0.05, Ctrl + Veh vs. Ket + Veh; P > 0.05, AZD8797 + Veh vs. AZD8797 + Ket; Fig. 4C) in the OFT. Collectively, these results further indicated that CX3CR1 was necessary for the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine.

CX3CR1 antagonist prevented ketamine-induced improvements in the synaptic plasticity

To further investigate whether CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling mediated the ketamine-induced improvements in the synaptic plasticity (Fig. 5), we quickly extracted the mPFC tissues after all behavioral tests and performed the Golgi-Cox staining experiments to observe AZD8797’s effects on the morphological changes of mPFC pyramidal neurons (Fig. 5A-B). We found that 24 h after drug injection, AZD8797 significantly inhibited ketamine’s effects on the total number of dendritic branching points (F(1, 20) = 4.432, P < 0.05; P < 0.05, Control + Veh vs. Control + Ket; P < 0.05, Control + Ket vs. AZD8797 + Ket; P > 0.05, AZD8797 + Veh vs. AZD8797 + Ket; Fig. 5C), and the spine density (F(1, 20) = 11.34, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, Control + Veh vs. Control + Ket; P < 0.05, Control + Ket vs. AZD8797+ Ket; P > 0.05, AZD8797 + Veh vs. AZD8797 + Ket; Fig. 5F). Furthermore, we performed a simple linear regression analysis between the immobility time in the TST/FST (Fig. 4D-E) and Golgi-Cox staining results (Fig. 5C and F). Interestingly, we found that the total number of dendritic branching points was negatively correlated with the immobility time in the TST (P < 0.01, Fig. 5D) and FST (P < 0.05, Fig. 5E); Dendritic spines were negatively correlated with the immobility time in the TST (P < 0.0001, Fig. 5G) and FST (P < 0.01, Fig. 5H). Collectively, these results further indicated that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling-mediated synaptic plasticity might be involved in the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine in AZD8797-treated mice.

CX3CR1 regulated ketamine’s inhibitory effects on microglia and pro-inflammatory cytokines

In addition, we investigated how ketamine regulated microglial activation and pro-inflammatory cytokines in Cort-treated mice with AZD8797 (Fig. 6). We quickly extracted the mPFC tissues to perform the immunofluorescence and ELISA experiments after behavioral tests (Fig. 6A). We found that in the Cort-induced model, ketamine significantly inhibited Cort-induced increases in the immobility time in the TST (F(2,30) = 5.226, P < 0.05: P < 0.05, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.05, Ket vs. Cort; Fig. 6B) and FST (F(2,30) = 5.369, P < 0.05: P < 0.05, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.05, Ket vs. Cort; Fig. 6C), which were significantly reversed by AZD8797 (For the TST, t(20) = 2.322, P < 0.05, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 6B; For the FST, t(20) = 3.020, P < 0.01, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 6C). Then, we performed the double immunofluorescence staining for Iba1+ and CD68 + , a lysosomal marker reflecting the phagocytic capacity of microglia, to examine whether ketamine affected the functional outcome of microglial activation. As shown in Fig. 6D, our results revealed that Cort treatment significantly increased the ratio of CD68+ cells to total Iba-1+ cells in the mPFC (F(2,15) = 11.50, P < 0.001: P < 0.01, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.01, Ket vs. Cort), which was significantly reversed by AZD8797 (t(10) = 3.065, P < 0.05, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 6E), indicating an essential role of CX3CR1 in ketamine’s inhibition on microglial activation. Furthermore, we examined AZD8797’s effects on pro-inflammatory cytokines in response to ketamine, and found that ketamine significantly inhibited the Cort-induced overexpression of IL-1β (F(2,12) = 101.8, P < 0.0001: P < 0.0001, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.05, Ket vs. Cort; t(8) = 3.892, P < 0.01, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 6F) and IL-6 (F(2,12) = 61.09, P < 0.0001: P < 0.0001, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.05, Ket vs. Cort; t(8) = 4.675, P < 0.01, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 6G), which were significantly prevented by AZD8797; AZD8797 failed to prevent ketamine’s inhibition on Cort-induced overexpression of TNF-α (F(2,12) = 97.28, P < 0.0001: P < 0.0001, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.05, Ket vs. Cort; t(8) = 1.723, P å 0.05, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 6H). These findings indicated the CX3CR1’s role in mediating ketamine’s inhibitory effects on pro-inflammatory cytokines.

AZD8797 attenuated ketamine’s inhibition on the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in Cort-treated mice

To investigate the functional consequence of altered CX3CL1/CX3CR1 expression, we first tested the CX3CL1 and CX3CR1 expressions of different groups in vitro (Fig. 7). As shown in Fig. 7A, treatment with Cort significantly increased the CX3CL1 expression in HT22 cells, which was significantly reversed by ketamine treatment (F(2,9) = 13.28, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.01, Ket vs. Cort; Fig. 7B). In addition, as shown in Fig. 7C, we found that ketamine treatment significantly inhibited the Cort-stimulated overexpression of CX3CR1 in BV2 microglial cells (F(2,9) = 9.59, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, Cort vs. Ctrl; P < 0.01, Ket vs. Cort; Fig. 7D). Furthermore, we investigated how ketamine affected the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in Cort-treated mice, 24 h after ketamine injection, we quickly extracted the mPFC issues and performed the immunofluorescence experiment to observe changes in the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in response to pharmacological blockade of CX3CR1 with AZD8797 (0.8 mg/kg, i.p.). As shown in Fig. 7E, ketamine (10 mg/kg, i.p.) significantly inhibited the Cort-induced overexpression of CX3CL1 in the mPFC, which was significantly reversed by AZD8797 (F(2,15) = 8.961, P < 0.01: P < 0.01, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.01, Ket vs. Cort; t(10) = 3.612, P < 0.01, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 7F). In addition, as shown in Fig. 7G, we found that AZD8797 significantly attenuated ketamine’s inhibition on the Cort-induced overexpression of CX3CR1 in the mPFC (F(2,15) = 8.175, P < 0.01: P < 0.01, Ctrl vs. Cort; P < 0.01, Ket vs. Cort; t(10) = 2.962, P < 0.05, Ket vs. AZD8797, Fig. 7H). These findings indicated that ketamine might regulate the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling via CX3CR1.

CX3CR1 knockdown attenuated the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine

To further verify whether CX3CR1 is necessary for the sustained behavioral effects of ketamine (Fig. 8), we injected AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA bilaterally into the mPFC and performed behavioral tests to investigate its effects on ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects (Fig. 8A). Mice receiving AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA exhibited a ~ 70% decrease of CX3CR1 mRNA in the mPFC (t(8) = 10.95, P < 0.0001, Fig. 8B-C), indicating successful CX3CR1 knockdown using AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA. We found that CX3CR1 knockdown significantly reversed these ketamine-induced behavioral effects in the TST (F(1, 24) = 5.081, P < 0.05; P < 0.05, AAV-Control + Veh vs. AAV-Control + Ket; P < 0.05, AAV-Control + Ket vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; P > 0.05, AAV-CX3CR1 + Veh vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; Fig. 8D), and FST (F(1, 24) = 8.674, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, AAV-Control + Veh vs. AAV-Control + Ket; P < 0.05, AAV-Control + Ket vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; P > 0.05, AAV-CX3CR1 + Veh vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; Fig. 8E), indicating that CX3CR1 silencing could reverse ketamine-induced antidepressant-like effects in mice. Collectively, these results further indicated that CX3CR1 was necessary for the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine.

CX3CR1 knockdown inhibited ketamine-induced improvements in the synaptic plasticity

To further investigate whether CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling mediated the ketamine-induced improvements in the synaptic plasticity, we rapidly extracted the mPFC tissues after all behavioral tests and performed the Golgi-Cox staining experiments to observe the morphological changes of mPFC pyramidal neurons (Fig. 9). As shown in Fig. 9A and B, we found that 24 h after drug injection, ketamine significantly increased the total dendritic length, total number of dendritic branching points, and spine density; CX3CR1 knockdown significantly inhibited ketamine’s effects on the total number of dendritic branching points (F(1, 20) = 7.019, P < 0.05; P < 0.01, AAV-Control + Veh vs. AAV-Control + Ket; P < 0.01, AAV-Control + Ket vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; P > 0.05, AAV-CX3CR1 + Veh vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; Fig. 9C) and spine density (F(1, 20) = 10.61, P < 0.01; P < 0.01, AAV-Control + Veh vs. AAV-Control + Ket; P < 0.01, AAV-Control + Ket vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; P > 0.05, AAV-CX3CR1 + Veh vs. AAV-CX3CR1 + Ket; Fig. 9F). Furthermore, we performed a simple linear regression analysis between immobility time in the TST/FST (Fig. 8E-F) and Golgi-Cox staining results (Fig. 9C and F). We found that the total number of dendritic branching points was negatively correlated with the immobility time in the TST (P < 0.01, Fig. 9D) and FST (P < 0.01, Fig. 9E); Dendritic spines were negatively correlated with the immobility time in the TST (P < 0.05, Fig. 9G) and FST (P < 0.01, Fig. 9H). Collectively, these results further indicated that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC might be involved in the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine.

Discussion

Data from this study demonstrated that CX3CR1 knockdown or CX3CR1 antagonist prevented the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine and inhibited its sustained effects on the synaptic plasticity of mPFC pyramidal neurons, indicating that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 pathway-mediated synaptic plasticity might be involved in the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine, which provided new insights into investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying ketamine’s unique antidepressant effects and paved the way to new treatments for depression targeting chemokines.

The chronic Cort-induced model of depression is becoming a valuable tool in the field of depression [33–35]. Consistent with previous findings, our behavioral observations showed that chronic Cort drinking for 4 weeks induced significant depression-like phenotypes, and 24 h after drug injection, ketamine reversed these Cort-induced depression-like phenotypes. SPT is a widely recognized method for measuring rodents’ “anhedonia”, one core symptom of depression in humans. Consistent with previous findings that chronic Cort treatment leads to a remarkable reduction in the sucrose preference in the SPT [36], this study found that Cort treatment for 4 weeks significantly affected rodents’ “anhedonia” and mice exhibited less preference for the sucrose in the SPT, indicating that mice displayed significant depression-like behaviors. Accumulating evidence recognized the role of synaptic plasticity in ketamine’s antidepressant behavioral activity [37]. In our investigations, Cort decreased the dendritic complexity and spine density of mPFC pyramidal neurons, which was consistent with chronic stress-induced effects on synaptic plasticity [38]; of note, ketamine produced protective effects on the synaptic plasticity of mPFC pyramidal neurons in Cort-treated mice. Collectively, these behavioral and structural findings indicated that synaptic plasticity in the mPFC might be involved in the sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine in the Cort-induced model of depression.

Mounting evidence shows that depression was closely associated with neuroinflammation [39, 40]. In inflammatory-related models, systemic LPS injection (i.p.) induced proinflammatory cytokine production (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and caused depression-like behaviors [41, 42]. Consistent with these findings, we found that occurring with depression-like behavioral deficiencies, chronic Cort drinking for 4 weeks also increased levels of the above pro-inflammatory cytokines in the mPFC; in addition, ketamine injection normalized the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and depression-like behaviors induced by Cort. Importantly, this study provided novel evidence that ketamine remarkably inhibited the Cort-induced activation of mPFC microglia, supporting the role of anti-inflammatory action in ketamine’s antidepressant-like behavioral effects. Furthermore, how ketamine inhibits microglial activation and overexpression of chemokines and then regulates the synaptic plasticity remains unclear. Our key findings demonstrated that pharmacological blockade of CX3CR1 attenuated ketamine’s inhibitory effects on activations of microglia and CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC, indicating an important role of CX3CR1 in ketamine’s anti-inflammatory effects.

Several lines of evidence have indeed linked depression to CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling. Earlier clinical evidence showed that serum chemokine levels in a small series of patients suffering from a major depressive episode found a 5-fold increase in CX3CL1 levels compared to healthy controls [43]; recent preclinical evidence showed that chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) increased the mRNA and protein expression levels of CX3CL1 and CX3CR1 [44]. In accordance with these earlier findings, our ELISA results showed that chronic Cort treatment activated CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC, and increased levels of CX3CL1 and CX3CR1 were observed in this study; quite intriguingly, we for the first time found that ketamine inhibited the Cort-induced activation of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway, indicating CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling might be involved in ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects. Furthermore, our findings in vitro suggested that Cort treatment increased the expression levels of CX3CL1 in HT22 cells and CX3CR1 in BV2 cells, which were completely reversed by ketamine treatment. Further analysis suggested that the expression levels of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC were positively correlated with depression-like phenotypes in this study. These critical findings provided a clear clue for investigating the molecular mechanism underlying ketamine’s behavioral effects in our subsequent studies.

Hence, we first pharmacologically inhibited CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling with CX3CR1 antagonist AZD8797 to further investigate its role in ketamine’s antidepressant actions. Our behavioral results showed that AZD8797 pretreatment completely blocked the ketamine-induced antidepressant-like effects. In addition, our Golgi-Cox staining results showed that AZD8797 pretreatment inhibited the ketamine-induced enhancement in the synaptic plasticity of mPFC pyramidal neurons, which was negatively correlated with depression-like behaviors in AZD8797-treated mice. Then, we genetically decreased the expression of CX3CR1 with AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA to verify the requirement for CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling-mediated synaptic plasticity of mPFC in ketamine’s antidepressant actions. We found that intra-mPFC microinjection with AAV-CX3CR1-siRNA reversed ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects. More importantly, CX3CR1 knockdown could eliminate ketamine-induced enhancement in the synaptic plasticity of mPFC pyramidal neurons, which was negatively correlated with depression-like behaviors in AAV-treated mice. These parallel experiments further supported the involvement of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 pathway-mediated synaptic plasticity in ketamine’s antidepressant actions. To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide cutting-edge research evidence that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling-mediated synaptic plasticity was required for ketamine’s antidepressant actions.

It becomes evident that the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 pathway might be a critical modulator of the microglia-driven synaptic plasticity underlying ketamine’s antidepressant actions. Many investigations have verified ketamine’s beneficial effects on synaptic plasticity [45–47], and recent studies show that its anti-inflammatory effects are increasingly recognized [13, 14, 48]. In addition, we observed that ketamine inhibited the Cort-induced activations of microglia and pro-inflammatory cytokines in the mPFC, which were reversed by pharmacological blockade of CX3CR1. Therefore, these observations prompted us to propose that ketamine might improve the dendritic morphology via CX3CR1-mediated inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which might represent a crucial link between ketamine’s anti-inflammatory actions and its improvements on synaptic plasticity. More pervasively, this study might provide the first evidence that the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling pathway ties the anti-inflammatory effects and protective effects of synaptic plasticity of ketamine so tightly together, which opens a new door to study the molecular mechanism of antidepressant actions of ketamine in the future.

Promisingly, the CX3CL1/CX3CR1 axis may be a potential intervention target for antidepressants. Previous electrophysiological data suggest that CX3CL1 may indirectly inhibit serotonergic neurotransmission, which was the main target of current first-line antidepressants (SSRIs and SNRIs), in vitro by increasing the sensitivity of serotonergic neurons to GABA inputs [49]; it’s reported that immunohistochemical CX3CL1 expression was detected in serotonergic neurons of the dorsal raphe nucleus (DRN), a brain region that has been implicated in the regulation of neuroplasticity linked to depression [50]. Furthermore, CX3CR1-/- mice also showed higher resilience to stress-induced depression-like behaviors (such as anhedonia, anxiety, or despair) [51]. These findings demonstrated that intervening CX3CL1/CX3CR1 axis may contribute to alleviating depression symptoms.

However, as a non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist, the precise molecular link between ketamine’s NMDA receptor antagonism and CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling remains unclear. It’s proposed that ketamine’s blockade of neuronal NMDA receptors alters neuronal activity, which modulates the synthesis and release of the neuron-specific CX3CL1 to engage microglial CX3CR1 receptors and drive an anti-inflammatory shift. Future studies utilizing cell-type-specific deletion of NMDA receptors and monitoring of interstitial CX3CL1 dynamics are needed to validate this neuron-to-microglia pathway. Furthermore, given the established critical role of BDNF-TrkB signaling in the antidepressant actions of ketamine [52, 53], it will be important to investigate potential crosstalk between BDNF-TrkB and CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the mPFC. Elucidating whether these pathways interact synergistically to mediate the rapid synaptic and behavioral effects of ketamine will be crucial for a more comprehensive understanding of its mechanism of action.

Emerging evidence suggests that ketamine’s antidepressant effects require coordinated microglial actions across distinct mPFC subregions. A recent study showed that microglial BDNF modulates arketamine’s antidepressant-like effects through activating infralimbic (IL) neurons projecting to the NAc shell [54]. Separately, ketamine has been shown to alleviate depressive-like behaviors by modulating neuronal activity in the prelimbic cortex (PrL) [55]. Consistent with the latter, our findings demonstrate that microglial CX3CR1/CX3CL1 signaling specifically within the PrL is necessary for the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine in our model. Collectively, these studies highlight that ketamine’s broad antidepressant-like effects may involve coordinated but anatomically distinct microglial actions across mPFC subregions, with IL and PrL contributing through different molecular pathways.

This study had a few limitations that warrant consideration. First, all experiments were conducted exclusively in male mice, so the present findings from single-sex rodents might not provide sufficient support for our initial hypothesis. Given the well-documented sex differences in the prevalence, future investigations should incorporate female subjects to determine whether the observed mechanisms are generalizable or sex-specific. In addition, the specificity of chronic administration of AZD8797 in blocking ketamine’s antidepressant-like effects requires more validation. Thus, further investigations observing acute effects of AZD8797 on ketamine’s actions would help clarify the temporal dynamics of CX3CR1 modulation on ketamine’s antidepressant-like properties. While our AAV-mediated CX3CR1 knockdown in naive mice established its role in modulating ketamine’s behavioral effects, future studies employing the same approach in Cort-treated mice are warranted to directly verify the necessity of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling for ketamine’s behavioral effects. Last but not least, although our previous studies suggested that ketamine produced significant antidepressant-like behavioral effects in non-stressed mice [56, 57], further studies including a parallel ketamine-treated control (wild-type/non-stressed) group are needed to provide additional mechanistic context to understand its antidepressant properties.

There were a few methodological insufficiencies that should be acknowledged in this study. First, while our current findings indicated that ketamine inhibited Cort-induced microglial activation, more detailed morphological analyses are required to fully validate this conclusion. Future studies employing advanced morphometric analyses, including Sholl analysis, soma area, and process length, would be valuable to quantify the phenotypic changes of ketamine’s inhibitory effects on microglia in this model. Second, although ketamine treatment reduced both microglial activation and levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) in Cort-treated mice, further evidence is required to establish a direct causal relationship between these observations. To verify whether pro-inflammatory cytokine changes originated specifically from activated microglia, future studies employing double immunofluorescence staining to colocalize these inflammatory cytokines with microglial markers are warranted.

Taken together, our investigations for the first time provided strong evidence that CX3CL1/CX3CR1 pathway-mediated synaptic plasticity in the mPFC was involved in the antidepressant-like effects of ketamine, which opened a new door to investigating the molecular mechanism underlying ketamine’s antidepressant effects. Further elucidation of the role of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signaling in the antidepressant effects of ketamine will enable us to better understand what is required to trigger antidepressant effects in hopes of developing next-generation antidepressants.
