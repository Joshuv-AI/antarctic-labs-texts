High expression of PAX8-AS1 correlates with poor prognosis and response to fluorouracil-based chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer
Authors: Hongsheng Fang, Lingyu Han, Yun Xu, Ruiqi Gu, Guoxiang Cai, Zuguang Xia, Weixing Dai, Renjie Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We for the first time developed an integrated mRNA-lncRNA signature and unveiled the role of novel lncRNA PAX8-AS1 in guiding adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer.•High expression level of PAX8-AS1 is firstly proved to be associated with early relapse and increased chemotherapy response of stage II colon cancer.•Detection of PAX8-AS1 level potentially offers clinical value in directing personalized adjuvant therapy selection for patients with stage II colon cancer.

Introduction

Decisions regarding adjuvant therapy (ie, chemotherapy given after surgery to reduce the risk of cancer recurrence) in patients with stage II colon cancer remain controversial and challenging[1]. Although the guideline of National Cooperative Cancer Network suggested that adjuvant therapy can be recommended for stage II patients with high-risk factors, patients and physicians are usually uncomfortable forgoing adjuvant therapy for the current definition of high risk patients is clearly inadequate[[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Numerous studies conducted in stage II colon cancer patients have demonstrated that clinicopathologic features alone are not a sufficient basis for patient treatment selection[[6], [7], [8], [9], [10]].

To date, the microsatellite stability status is the only established biomarker for decision making and it is widely accepted that adjuvant chemotherapy should not be recommended for low risk patients with microsatellite instability (MSI)[11]. However, no consensus has been reached among the patients with microsatellite stability (MSS) who account for the majority of stage II colorectal cancer. Though great efforts have been made to develop mRNA or long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) based prognostic signatures in stage II diseases, no solid gene panels have been successfully used to predict the chemotherapy benefit and no biomarkers have been used in clinical practice[[12], [13], [14], [15]]. For example, Park et al. tested 5 popular genomic predictors for colon cancer prognosis using 2 independent cohorts and found that only 2 of them showed robust performance[16]. Although the predictions of the Oncotype DX Colon Cancer test (Oncotype DX) and ColoPrint (Agendia BV) are reproducible, they are not predictive of adjuvant treatment benefits for patients with stage II disease[17,18]. Up to this point, none of the gene signatures has demonstrated the capability to forecast which stage II disease patients might derive advantages from adjuvant therapy or to provide guidance for treatment choices—a key objective in the realm of personalized oncology. Taken together, novel gene panels and biomarkers are urgently needed to facilitate relapse and chemotherapy benefit prediction in stage II colon cancer patients.

In alignment with our study's overarching hypothesis and aim, our primary goal was to identify novel and effective predictive biomarkers for relapse-free survival (RFS) in stage II colon cancer patients. The study sought to identify a subset of individuals who may benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. Specifically, we conducted bioinformatic analyses to identify differentially expressed mRNAs and lncRNAs between groups with early relapse and long-term survival. Furthermore, our investigation led to the development of a groundbreaking integrated mRNA-lncRNA signature. This signature holds promise as a valuable tool for predicting RFS and assessing potential chemotherapy benefits in patients with MSS stage II colon cancer.

The novel lncRNA PAX8-AS1 exhibited the most significant upregulation in the early relapse group, with patients having high PAX8-AS1 expression levels experiencing markedly worse RFS. Liu[19] et.al demonstrate that PAX8-AS1/microRNA-25–3p/LATS2 regulated the malignant progression of ovarian cancer through Hippo signaling, Chitra[20] et.al identified the BIRC5-miR335–5p-PAX8-AS1 network as one that was associated with poor overall survival in adrenocortical carcinoma. To date, there have been no reports of PAX8-AS1 facilitating tumor invasion, research on colorectal cancer remains insufficient, our study establishes that PAX8-AS1 effectively enhances the metastatic capabilities of colorectal cancer cells.

Methods

Data sets

This study is a retrospective cohort research. Raw microarray cell intensity (CEL) files of colon cancer were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and the preprocessing of transcriptome microarray was described in our previous work[21]. Two hundreds and eight stage II cases with MSS from GSE39582 was selected as training set to identify potential relapse and chemotherapy benefit related genes. GSE17536, GSE14333, GSE33113 and GSE37892 were combined as external validation set to validate the prognostic value of signature developed in training set. Among the GEO datasets, GSE39582, GSE14333, and GSE17536 contained the samples of patients who had received fluorouracil based adjuvant chemotherapy (fluorouracil as a key component, single-agent fluorouracil, fluorouracil and oxaliplatin, fluorouracil and folic acid). Stage II colon cancer patients with MSS diagnosed in 2010–2013 with complete follow-up and treatment information were obtained from our cancer center (FUSCC) to validate the prognostic and predictive value of PAX8-AS1. When combing different datasets, the ComBat method was used to remove the potential internal and external batch effects. This method was implemented in the SVA R package and the corresponding R-code has been provided in our previous work.

Identification of relapse and chemotherapy response associated mRNAs and lncRNAs

Stage II patients in training set were separated into early relapse group (relapse within 2 years regardless of chemotherapy status) and long-term survival group (no chemotherapy treatment and no relapse after a minimum of 5-year follow-up) according to chemotherapy and follow-up information. Cases who developed relapse within 3 months were excluded. Propensity score (PS) matching analysis was performed between the two groups adjusting for T stage and tumor location. Matched ratio was set as 1:1. Before PS matching, significantly higher proportion of T4 stage patients were observed in relapse group. After PS matching, no significant differences can be found between the two groups among age, T stage and tumor location. Finally, twenty-one paired patients in the training series were selected to perform transcriptome profiling. Linear Models for Microarray data (LIMMA) method was used to generate the differentially expressed mRNAs and lncRNAs between early relapse and long-term survival samples (fold change >1.25, P < 0.05). A total of three-hundred and nine genes were differentially expressed between the two groups. Lastly, LASSO Cox regression model was conducted in cases without chemotherapy to select the most useful mRNAs and lncRNAs.

Development of risk score

Using LASSO Cox regression model, we identified a list of mRNAs and lncRNAs and constructed a multi-RNA-based classifier. A standard formula of risk score for each patient was calculated combining the expression levels of the RNAs and LASSO Cox regression coefficients. The formula was as follows:Riskscore=∑i=1nexpi*βi

Identification of biomarker for further study

Volcano plot was used to illustrate the difference of mRNAs and lncRNAs between early relapse group and long term survival group. We focus the novel lncRNA PAX8-AS1 which showed the highest upregulation in early relapse group. Patents in FUSCC cohort was then used to validate the performance of PAX8-AS1 in predicting the prognosis and chemotherapy benefit. Real time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed to determine level of PAX8-AS1

Cell culture

Four different CRC cell lines (HCT116, DLD1, RKO, and HCT8), which possess distinct aggressive features, were obtained from the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and cultured in DMEM with 10 % fetal bovine serum under a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2.

Stable transfection

To establish stable cells with PAX8-AS1/PAX8 knockdown, the corresponding shRNAs were acquired from Vigene Biosciences for the HCT116 and DLD1 cells, which were then transfected with pLKO.1-shPAX8-AS1 and pLKO.1-shPAX8-AS1 respectively. Successful PAX8-AS1/PAX8 knockdown was screened through the use of puromycin after 48 h of culture.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA from CRC cells was extracted using TRIzol reagent and was reverse transcribed with the PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix. qRT-PCR was performed on the ABI 7900HT Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) to determine the expression levels of β-actin, PAX8-AS1 were 5′- TTGCAGTGGGTAGAGCAGTG-3′ (Forward) and 5′- TGCCTTCGTGCTGACATTCT-3′ (Reverse), PAX8 were 5′- ATCCGGCCTGGAGTGATAGG-3′ (Forward) and 5′- TGGCGTTTGTAGTCCCCAATC-3′ (Reverse).

Cell viability and cell cycle measurement

Cell viability and cell cycle distribution were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay kit (DOJINDO, Japan) and the Cell Cycle Kit (BD, USA), respectively.

Western blotting

RIPA buffer (ThermoFisher scientific) was used to lyse CRC cells, followed by quantification via BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher scientific) for equal protein loading (1 μg). The resolved cellular proteins were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, and probed with specific primary antibodies against β-actin (1/1000 dilution; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and PAX8 (1/1000 dilution; Proteintech 10,336–1-AP) overnight at 4 °C. The blots were visualized using ECL (Pierce, Thermo Scientific, USA) and detected using a BioImaging System.

mRNA decay analyses

Stable cells were directly harvested (mRNA steady-state level) or treated with 5 mM actinomycin D (mRNA decay) and harvested at the indicated time points. The total RNA was isolated at the indicated time points after actinomycin D application, and each analyzed factor was validated by quantitative RT-PCR for each experiment. All data were analyzed from at least three independent experiments, and statistical significance was validated by Student's t-test.

Cell cycle

Cells are cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with 10 % FBS (Sigma-Aldrich) to the desired density, harvested by trypsinization (Gibco), and fixed in 70 % cold ethanol (Sangon Biotech). The fixed cells are then treated with RNase A (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and stained with propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich). Finally, the samples are analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and data analysis is performed with FlowJo software (BD Biosciences) to determine the cell cycle distribution.

Cell apoptosis

Cells are cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with 10 % FBS (Sigma-Aldrich) until they reach the appropriate confluence, followed by treatment with the apoptotic inducer, staurosporine (Sigma-Aldrich). After treatment, cells are harvested using trypsin (Gibco), washed with PBS (Gibco), and stained with Annexin V-FITC (BioLegend) and propidium iodide (Sigma-Aldrich). The stained cells are then analyzed using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer, and the results are interpreted using FlowJo software (BD Biosciences) to quantify the percentage of apoptotic cells.

Relative inhibition rate

To calculate the Relative Inhibition Rate (RIR) after a flow cytometry-based apoptosis assay, first determine the percentage of apoptotic cells in both treated and control groups. Then, use the formula:RIR(%)=Atreated−Acontrol(100−Acontrol)×100

This formula shows how effectively the treatment inhibits apoptosis compared to the control, with a higher RIR indicating greater inhibition.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using R 3.5.0 software, and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted for data with skewed distribution, while the Student's test was utilized for data with a normal distribution to compare the differences between groups. Relapse free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from surgery to the emergence of locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis, and the Kaplan-Meier method was employed to calculate the cumulative RFS rate, with the log-rank test used to compare the survival differences between low and high level groups. A p-value of <0.05 was considered significant. This study has been approved by Ethics Committee of Fudan university shanghai cancer center.

Results

Integrated mRNA-lncRNA signature for predicting fluorouracil response in stage II colon cancer

Three hundred and nine stage II cases from GSE17536 (n = 70), GSE14333 (n = 76), GSE33113 (n = 90) and GSE37892 (n = 73) were combined as external validation set. GSE39582, GSE14333, and GSE17536 contained the samples of patients (n = 305) who had received fluorouracil-based adjuvant chemotherapy. The raw clinical information of all patients in GEO datasets can be found in the supplementary table of our previous study[21]. A total of 446 stage II colon cancer patients with MSS and complete follow-up and adjuvant chemotherapy information in our cancer center (FUSCC) were identified as an independent validation cohort. The basic clinicopathological information of these 446 cases was showed in Table S1.

Stage II cases with treatment information (n = 305) from GSE39582, GSE14333 and GSE17536 were combined to analyze whether the developed signature can be useful for guiding adjuvant therapy. In the whole cohort, adjuvant chemotherapy showed no impact on RFS (HR: 1.364, 95 % CI: 0.727–2.557, P = 0.330, supplementary Figure 1). Further comparison analysis showed that adjuvant chemotherapy treated low risk patients showed significantly shorter 5-year survival than non-treated groups (HR: 6.918, 95 % CI: 2.403–19.913, P < 0.001, Fig. 1G). We then performed same analysis in high risk patients and found that adjuvant chemotherapy treated patients had notably improved RFS in contrast to patients without adjuvant chemotherapy (HR: 0.288, 95 % CI: 0.086–0.964, P = 0.020, Fig. 1H), suggesting that this developed signature will help to identify high risk stage II patients who will benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy.

Determining PAX8-AS1 as a novel biomarker for predicting the relapse and chemotherapy benefit

To characterize the expression of the identified 30 mRNAs and 2 lncRNAs, volcano plot was used to illustrate the difference of mRNAs and lncRNAs between early relapse group and long term survival group (Fig. 2A). It was found that lncRNA PAX8-AS1 showed the highest upregulation in the early relapse group, suggesting PAX8-AS1 may be good biomarker for prognosis and chemotherapy benefit prediction. Survival analysis in the whole combined GEO cohort showed that stage II patients with high expression of PAX8-AS1 exhibited significantly worse RFS in contrast to patients with low PAX8-AS1 expression (HR: 1.559, 95 % CI: 1.001–2.552, P < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Further comparison analysis in patients with chemotherapy information showed that adjuvant chemotherapy did not associate with elevated survival in patients with low PAX8-AS1 level (HR: 7.550, 95 % CI: 2.659–21.436, P < 0.001, Fig. 2C). Instead, adjuvant chemotherapy significantly prolonged RFS in high PAX8-AS1 level patients (HR: 0.209, 95 % CI: 0.050–0.885, P = 0.019, Fig. 2D).

Then, 446 stage II colon cancer patients were identified from FUSCC to test the prognostic and predictive value of PAX8-AS1. Based on the highest quartile, patients were divided into two groups with notably different RFS (HR: 2.835, 95 % CI: 1.736–4.628, P < 0.001, Fig. 2E). Then, we analyzed whether PAX8-AS1 alone was able to guide adjuvant therapy. In the entire FUSCC cohort, receipt of adjuvant chemotherapy was not associated with significantly improved RFS (HR: 0.659, 95 % CI: 0.392–1.108, P = 0.110, Fig. 2F). Then, we performed survival analysis in patients with different level of PAX8-AS1 and it was found that administration of adjuvant chemotherapy did not improve the RFS in patients with low PAX8-AS1 level (HR: 1.105, 95 % CI: 0.504–2.426, P = 0.910, Fig. 2G). Instead, adjuvant chemotherapy significantly prolonged the RFS in patients with high PAX8-AS1 level (HR: 0.330, 95 % CI: 0.156–0.698, P < 0.001, Fig. 2H).

To further exclude the impact of data bias, multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed in patients with low and high level of PAX8-AS1 respectively. The result showed that administration of adjuvant chemotherapy did not influence RFS after adjusting other clinicopathological features in patients with low PAX8-AS1 expression (Fig. 3A). Besides, adjuvant chemotherapy did not influence RFS in patients in whole patients (Supplementary Figure 1). Instead, adjuvant chemotherapy independently decreased the relapse risk for patients with high PAX8-AS1 expression (Fig. 3B).Fig. 3Multi-variate Cox regression analysis in patients with low (A) and high (B) PAX8-AS1 expression level.Fig 3

Basic PAX8-AS1 expression was detected in four CRC cell lines with distinct biological phenotype (HCT116, DLD1, RKO and SW480) and normal colon epithelial cell (NCM460). It was found that PAX8-AS1 was highly expressed in HCT116 and DLD1, while RKO and SW480 showed relatively low expression (Fig. 4A). Therefore, to further determine the effect of PAX8-AS1 on cell growth, we silenced PAX8-AS1 expression by using small short hairpin RNA technology in two cell lines, HCT116 and DLD1 (Fig. 4B). Then, we treated cells with 5-fluorocrail (5Fu) and the results showed that PAX8-AS1 silence significantly reduced the cell apoptosis (Fig. 4E-4F) and cell proliferation inhibition (Fig. 4G-4H) which induced by 5Fu treatment. Therefore, we hypothesized that PAX8-AS1 may regulate cell response to chemotherapy by mediating PAX8 expression. Firstly, we analyzed the function of PAX8 in colon cancer cells by silencing PAX8 expression in HCT116 and DLD1 (Fig. 5A). Consistent with the effect of PAX8-AS1, PAX8 attenuation repressed cell proliferation (Fig. 5B) and induced cell cycle arrest (Fig. 5C) notably too. After treating cells with 5Fu, the apoptosis and cell proliferation analysis showed that PAX8 attenuation significantly weak the response to 5Fu (Fig. 5D, 5E). Discussion

The prognosis always varies significantly in stage II colorectal cancer patients even with the same T stage and comparable clinicopathological features. In this study, we developed a novel method incorporating both chemotherapy status and relapse information to detect potential genes related to both prognosis and chemotherapy benefit. We for the first time constructed the first integrated mRNA-lncRNA signature that can help to identify a subset of stage II cases who will benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy and unveil the potential of novel lncRNA PAX8-AS1 as a reliable marker for guiding adjuvant chemotherapy. The further experiments focusing on PAX8-AS1 revealed the underlying mechanism of PAX8-AS1 level influencing chemotherapy response.

Thus far, clinically useful cancer biomarkers for guiding chemotherapy benefit remain rare in stage II colon cancer. Though the prognostic values of Oncotype DX[22] and ColoPrint[14] have been validated in many independent cohorts, no predictive role in adjuvant treatment benefits of these two panels has been reported. Therefore, we propose the analyzing process we used in this study was able to more thoroughly determine the relapse and chemotherapy response associated genes. Indeed, we showed that the mRNA-lncRNA signature boosted prediction performance and PAX8-AS1 is a novel and reliable biomarker for guiding adjuvant chemotherapy.

Fluorouracil is a first-line drug for colon cancer that has been used to treat patients with stage III disease for the last 20 years. It is expected that high-risk patients with stage II disease could gain survival benefit from fluorouracil adjuvant chemotherapy. Adjuvant chemotherapy has no significant effect on the average patient with stage II colon cancer, with an improvement in survival <5 % at 5 years[23]. Additionally, administration of adjuvant chemotherapy to all patients with stage II disease is unnecessary and harmful for about 75 % of patients, who are cured with surgery alone. This narrow therapeutic index underscores the importance of identifying the minority of those high risk patients who are more likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy. Although current guidelines indicate that adjuvant chemotherapy should be considered for patients with stage II colon cancer with any poor prognostic features, there is no evidence that chemotherapy improves survival in this clinical high-risk group[24]. In this study, only 25 % (the highest quartile) of stage II patients were classified into high risk groups. Among the high risk patients, administration of adjuvant chemotherapy will bring notable survival benefits. From a basic research perspective, the reasons why high-risk stage II colon cancer cases may benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy can be elucidated through an understanding of the tumor's biology and treatment mechanisms. Chemotherapeutic agents typically interfere with cancer cell growth and division through diverse mechanisms. In the case of high-risk patients, chemotherapy may help eliminate microscopic residual disease or inhibit the growth of recurrent lesions. Additionally, high-risk tumors often exhibit molecular features such as specific gene mutations, protein expression alterations, or other biomarkers that may render them more aggressive or treatment-resistant[25]. Adjuvant chemotherapy may target these specific features effectively.

PAX8-AS1 is the novel lncRNA we determined in this study that was able to guide the use of adjuvant chemotherapy in stage II colon cancer. No previous studies have attempted to investigate the role of PAX8-AS1 in colon cancer. In Myeloid Leukemia, it was found that PAX8-AS1 silencing would decrease cell viability, enhance Apoptosis, and suppress doxorubicin resistance. In this present study, we for the first time explored the effect of PAX8-AS1 on cell response to 5Fu and the result showed that PAX8-AS1 silencing would repress cell viability, induce cell cycle arrest and decrease cell response to 5Fu. 5Fu is one the most famous cell cycle specific drugs and actively proliferative cells are considered to be more sensitive to 5Fu[26,27]. The efficacy of 5Fu is associated with different phases of the cell cycle. It primarily affects cells in the S-phase (DNA synthesis phase) since this is when DNA replication is most active. Rapidly proliferating tumor cells tend to enter the S-phase more frequently, making them more susceptible to the effects of 5Fu. Knockdown of PAX8-AS1 induced cell cycle and inhibited cell proliferation of colon cancer which accounted partly for the weakened response to 5Fu in PAX8-AS1 silenced cells.

PAX8-AS1 is significantly upregulated in early relapse stage II cancer patients and is associated with poorer RFS. High PAX8-AS1 expression identifies a high-risk group, but these patients benefit substantially from 5Fu-based adjuvant chemotherapy. Mechanistically, PAX8-AS1 enhances response to chemotherapy by stabilizing PAX8 mRNA, PAX8 is a transcription factor that plays a critical role in embryonic development, particularly in the differentiation and maturation of the thyroid [28] and pancreas[29]. It is primarily implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of organ-specific malignancies, such as ovarian[30] and renal cancers[31]. Recent studies have suggested that methylation of PAX8 may serve as a potential biomarker for early detection of colorectal cancer[32]. However, the oncogenic mechanisms by which PAX8 influences colorectal carcinogenesis remain unexplored.

In this study, we have demonstrated that PAX8 regulates apoptosis and cell cycle progression in CRC cells. Additionally, in vivo experiments revealed that PAX8 knockdown significantly inhibited subcutaneous tumor growth. 5Fu exerts its anticancer effects by inhibiting thymidylate synthase, incorporating into RNA and DNA, and inducing apoptosis, primarily targeting rapidly proliferating cells[33]. We hypothesize that PAX8-AS1 may enhance the sensitivity of these rapidly dividing cells to chemotherapy. The potential mechanism could involve PAX8 impacting DNA repair pathways. If PAX8 inhibits certain DNA repair mechanisms, tumor cells may become more susceptible to chemotherapy-induced DNA damage. Alternatively, PAX might modify the tumor microenvironment, potentially enhancing angiogenesis and improving drug delivery to the tumor site, thereby increasing the effectiveness of chemotherapy. Additionally, while oncogenes typically suppress apoptosis, overexpression of PAX8 might reduce the expression of anti-apoptotic factors. As a result, when chemotherapy drugs are applied, these tumor cells could be less equipped to resist apoptosis, making them more vulnerable to treatment. We have to admitted that this is a retrospective analysis and selection bias cannot be avoided. Therefore, we are preparing to initiate a prospective clinical trial to validate the prognostic and predictive value of PAX8-AS1. In addition, further in-depth analysis is warranted to be conducted to unravel the mechanisms underlying the relationship between PAX8-AS1 and colon cancer prognosis as well as its interaction with adjuvant therapy, which holds the potential to pave the way for novel treatment modalities.

In summary, our findings show that the PAX8-AS1 can effectively classify patients with stage II colon cancer into groups at low and high risk of relapse, thereby adding prognostic value to the traditional clinicopathological risk factors. Moreover, our study showed that the PAX8-AS1 expression might be a useful biomarker for identification of patients who can benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy.

Limitations

One limitation of this study is the omission of several critical variables that could influence survival outcomes. Factors such as tumor differentiation, the status of surgical resection (R0 versus non-R0), and postoperative morbidity were not included in the analysis. The absence of these variables may have impacted the comprehensiveness of the survival assessment.
