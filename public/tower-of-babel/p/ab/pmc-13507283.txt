Heavy water labeling reveals metabolic flexibility of amino acid and polyamine pathways in mammalian cells
Authors: Alan Gonzalez-Ibarra, Missael Arroyo-Negrete, Wioleta Banaszuk-Krupa, Katarzyna Socała, Nikola Gapińska, Piotr Wlaź, Julio Cesar Torres-Elguera, Tomasz Sawoszczuk, Marek Tchórzewski, Maria Hatzoglou, Dawid Krokowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amino acid and polyamine metabolism underpins many cellular processes, such as cell growth, stress adaptation, and signaling. However, the usage of specific metabolic pathways is highly context-dependent, and there are many compensatory mechanisms in place for the biosynthesis of amino acids. Here, we establish low-dose heavy water (D₂O) labeling as a tracer to monitor amino acid and polyamine metabolism in mammalian systems. Using targeted HPLC–MS of primary amines, we quantified deuterium incorporation in mouse embryonic fibroblasts, pancreatic β-cell–derived MIN6 cells, and mouse tissues, which we then benchmarked with orthogonal tracers (13C-glucose and 15NH₄⁺). We demonstrated D₂O labels nonessential amino acids and polyamines. We validated specificity, as inhibition of key metabolic steps altered deuterium incorporation into Ala/Ser/Gly and polyamines and revealed differential engagement of branched-chain amino acid metabolism. We found that glutamine starvation induces integrated stress response-linked remodeling, increasing deuterium incorporation into Glu and glycolytic amino acids while identifying changes in amino acids efflux. Finally, in vivo short-term D₂O exposure distinguishes tissue-specific biosynthetic capacities. Collectively, these data challenge the assumption of uniform alanine labeling by D2O and demonstrate that D₂O provides a sensitive readout of metabolic flexibility, transport crosstalk, and pathway regulation across cell types and tissues.

Introduction

Amino acids play roles in cells far beyond serving as building blocks of proteins and peptides. Their intracellular levels critically regulate two major signaling pathways: sufficient abundance is required for activation of mTOR1,2, a master regulator of cellular growth, whereas deficiency triggers the integrated stress response (ISR) via activation of the cellular stress sensor kinase GCN23,4. In addition, amino acid metabolism influences stem cell differentiation and maintenance of pluripotency5–7, partly by providing substrates for epigenetic DNA modifications. Amino acids also serve as essential precursors for the synthesis of numerous metabolites, including nucleotides, glutathione8, heme9, glucosamine10, and polyamines. Polyamines, direct derivatives of amino acids, exert important cellular functions through their interactions with RNA11, ribosomes12, and proteins13. Their central role is further emphasized by the requirement of spermidine in the conserved posttranslational hypusination of the translation factor eIF5A14, a process crucial for mRNA translation15. In addition, like amino acids, polyamine accumulation also has stimulatory effects on the mTOR pathway16,17, indicating these molecules can have regulatory functionality. Amino acids also serve as precursors for signaling molecules, such as nitric oxide, sulfite oxide, neurotransmitters, and hormones, underscoring their broad regulatory potential.

Disruption of amino acid homeostasis has been associated with numerous diseases. Elevated serum levels of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) are linked to the development of diabetes18, while high levels of homocysteine—a Met metabolite—is implicated in cardiovascular disease19. Dysregulated Trp catabolism has been shown to contribute to depression20, and Glu excitotoxicity plays a role in neurodegenerative disorders. Moreover, amino acid metabolism is a central component of cancer metabolic adaptation and is increasingly explored as a therapeutic target21. Tumor cells often enhance amino acid availability via increased utilization of inorganic ammonia22, activation of specific transporters23, or upregulation of biosynthetic pathways24. Thus, understanding the complex interconnections between amino acid synthesis and catabolism is essential for elucidating disease mechanisms and developing new therapeutic strategies.

Technological advancements in analytical instruments have enabled the discrimination of metabolites with increased molecular weights resulting from the incorporation of heavy, stable, nonradioactive isotopes of carbon, nitrogen, or hydrogen derived from labeled substrates. This has driven recent progress in understanding metabolism in diverse cellular models. However, limitations arise when investigating amino acid biosynthesis using a single labeled substrate, as metabolic flexibility can result in alternative substrate utilization. In a simplified view, the carbon backbones of amino acids can be originated from either glycolysis (e.g., Ala, Ser, and Gly) or the mitochondria (e.g., Asp, Glu, and their derivatives Asn, Gln, and Pro)25,26. Notably, mitochondria can also contribute to the synthesis of Ala, Ser, and Gly through the activity of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), which catalyzes the conversion of mitochondrial oxaloacetate (OAA) into phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP)27. Consequently, using a single substrate, such as glucose clarifies only how glycolysis contributes carbon backbones under specific growth conditions or metabolic states. However, carbon backbones can also be supplied by other cellular processes, such as glutaminolysis or through the decarboxylation and deamination of other amino acids. Recent studies have further highlighted the significance of BCAAs, which, after decarboxylation to keto acids, feed into mitochondrial metabolism28. Moreover, the catabolism of Ser and Gly has been shown to support gluconeogenesis29,30, with newly formed glucose re-entering glycolysis and contributing to the synthesis of Ala, further exemplifying the complexity of amino acid biosynthesis integrating multiple substrate inputs.

To overcome these limitations in single metabolite labeling, parallel labeling with glucose and Gln has been proposed to elucidate metabolic pathways and quantify carbon flow31,32. Although this approach ignores flux from other metabolic sources and substrates and thus provides only a partial—yet broadly representative—picture of biochemical networks, further refinement is possible. Another promising approach is the quantitative analysis of Asp and Glu isotopomers, which enables discrimination between biosynthetic routes that yield identical 13C isotopologues but differ in the positional distribution of isotopic labels within the molecule, depending on the metabolic origin33. The broader challenge remains to develop a labeling strategy that universally captures amino acid synthesis regardless of pathway-specific fluxes.

Heavy water (deuterium oxide, D2O) represents a valuable tool in this context. It is cost-effective and, at low concentrations, is generally well-tolerated by cells and organisms, although concentrations exceeding 20% can interfere with vital processes, such as mitosis34–38. D2O-based labeling has been successfully applied in studies of lipid39–41, nucleotide42, and carbohydrate metabolism43,44. In systems with complete amino acid biosynthetic capacity, such as plants, D₂O has also been used to study amino acid synthesis43,45,46.

In mammalian systems, by contrast, D2O has been primarily used to track protein synthesis via the incorporation of deuterated amino acids. Ala labeling, for instance, has been shown to increase linearly with D2O concentration and is thought to be consistent across cell types after prolonged D2O treatments47–49. This property underlies its use as a proxy for estimating protein synthesis rates in vivo, a method adopted by the cores of reproducibility in physiology (CORP) initiative of the American Physiological Society50 and widely used in human studies51,52. Measurements of protein turnover in biological systems have also been conducted53,54; recent studies examining deuterium incorporation into proteins have revealed significant labeling in Ala, Asp, Glu, Gly, Pro, Ile/Leu, Met, Ser, and Val, with detectable levels across all proteinogenic amino acids54. These findings raise two important questions: (1) which amino acids are labeled by deuterium, and (2) if amino acid biosynthesis is highly regulated and cell context-dependent, why is deuterium labeling considered to be uniform? These questions suggest a need to revisit assumptions about the constancy of D2O incorporation into Ala, especially over short treatment durations, and to explore how variable biosynthetic activity might influence labeling patterns.

When cells are exposed to D₂O, deuterium atoms can be incorporated into endogenous metabolites via both non-enzymatic and enzymatic mechanisms. Labile hydrogen atoms, such as those bound to oxygen or nitrogen, can exchange rapidly and non-specifically with solvent deuterons; however, deuterons at such exchangeable sites are typically lost during sample preparation or ionization. In contrast, non-exchangeable C–H bonds formed during enzymatic reactions—such as transamination, reductive amination, or decarboxylation—can stably retain deuterium atoms incorporated during biosynthesis55. We therefore propose that deuterium enrichment detected in amino acids and polyamines predominantly reflects biosynthetic activity across metabolic pathways rather than passive exchange.

To explore this possibility, we investigated whether heavy water (D₂O) labeling can serve as a practical tracer for monitoring amino acid and polyamine metabolism in mammalian systems. Using targeted analysis of primary amines by HPLC–MS, we examined deuterium incorporation into amino acids and polyamines in cultured cells and mouse tissues. By comparing D₂O labeling with other tracers and inducing metabolic perturbations affecting amino acid and polyamine pathways, we evaluated how metabolic context influences amino acid labeling patterns and whether D₂O can provide a sensitive readout of metabolic remodeling across pathways and cell types.

Results

D2O labeling of amino acids and polyamines in mouse embryonic fibroblasts

The incorporation of deuterium from D₂O into amino acids during synthesis has been well documented in plants and unicellular eukaryotes45,46. In mammalian cells, labeling of Ala50, as well as other nonessential amino acids, such as Gly and Gln56, has been reported. However, this labeling is often regarded as constant50, and is primarily used to study protein synthesis and turnover following the incorporation of labeled amino acids46,50,54,56. The literature indicates that Ala synthesis in mammalian cells offers multiple opportunities for labeling with D₂O; specifically, deuterium can be incorporated at both the α-carbon (position 2) and the β-carbon (position 3) of Ala56, likely due to active metabolism and deuterium exchange during spontaneous keto-enol conversion of intermediates50.

To evaluate the specificity of deuterium labeling in amino acids and polyamines, primary amine groups were derivatized with APDS (3-aminopyridyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate) and subsequently analyzed by targeted HPLC–QqQ-MS in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode. Mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) were cultured for 24 h in DMEM containing Gln, supplemented up to 20% with D₂O; higher concentrations of D₂O were avoided to prevent adverse effects observed when cells are exposed to more than 30%34–38. We observed a linear, dose-dependent increase in M + 1 mass isotopomers (including expected M + D), revealing labeling of selected amino acids when D₂O was in the range of 5–20% (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1B). The relative intensity (RI) of the M + 1 mass isotopomers for those amino acids increased linearly, reflecting the incorporation of a deuterium atom during amino acid biosynthesis. RI was calculated as the ratio between the area of the M + 1 mass isotopomer and the monoisotopic species (M), expressed as a percentage. To support the reliability of isotopic ratio measurements under these conditions, an experimental comparison based on amino acids quantified in MEF cell extracts is provided in Supplementary Table S2. In contrast, the M + 2 mass isotopomers displayed either linear increases, consistent with the natural isotopic distribution arising from the M + 1 species, or parabolic profiles. Such curvature is expected when more than one independent deuterium incorporation site is present within a metabolite. In this case, M + 1 enrichment reflects the probability of a single substitution event, whereas M + 2 enrichment arises from the probability of two independent incorporation events occurring within the same molecule, which depends on both the number of potential labeling sites within the metabolite and the fractional enrichment of deuterium in the solvent. The highest labeling was observed for Pro, Ala, Asn, Asp, and Glu (Fig. 1A). For these amino acids, the M + 2 mass isotopomer enrichment profiles fit parabolic models, consistent with the presence of multiple independent deuterium incorporation sites within the molecule (M + 2D) (Supplementary Fig. 1C). As we observed only single labeling for Ser (Fig. 1A, C), we hypothesized that synthesis of Gly would provide additional opportunities for deuterium incorporation and therefore additional potential labeling sites. (model in Fig. 1C). Consistent with this, Gly showed higher M + 1 labeling than Ser (compare slopes in Fig. 1A) and a better parabolic fit for M + 2 enrichment, confirming the existence of an additional labeling site during synthesis (Fig. 1C). Put and Spd were also labeled by D₂O (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1C). As Gln was present in the growth medium, Gln itself showed no significant M + 1 enrichment up to 10% D2O, indicating primarily catabolic utilization of this amino acid under these conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Principal component analysis (PCA) supported these findings, as the loading vectors for these amino acids were oriented distinctly from those of amino acids exhibiting measurable deuterium incorporation (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Met and BCAAs, such as Val, Leu, and Ile are essential for mammalian cells; thus, labeling of these amino acids with deuterium was unexpected (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1C). Fitting of M + 1 and M + 2 enrichment profiles revealed linear enrichment of both mass isotopomers, suggesting a single labeling site for these amino acids (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1 C).

Using 10% D2O, we then assessed labeling of amino acids and polyamines within a 6-h window and confirmed rapid labeling of Pro, Ala, Asn, Asp, and Glu measured as M + 1 mass isotopomer enrichment. This labeling reached a plateau between 2 and 6 h (Fig. 1F). Similar behavior was observed for Gly labeling, whereas Ser labeling increased in a linear fashion (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1D). Labeling of Met and BCAA was also confirmed, with lower labeling for Val than Leu and Ile (Compare Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 1C). Gln M + 1 labeling was low but detectable, while labeling of aromatic amino acids, Thr and Lys was absent (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Together, these results indicate specific deuterium labeling of amino acids linked to biosynthesis in mammalian cells, with other metabolic processes contributing to BCAA and Met labeling.

In contrast, Glu and Asp—also absent from the medium—exhibited lower M + 2 enrichment than Ala, suggesting that their synthesis involves additional carbon sources beyond glucose, e.g., Gln deamination and subsequent conversion to α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). Consistent with this, no significant Gln M + 2 labeling was detected, indicating minimal glucose-derived contribution to Gln synthesis (Fig. 2B). Although Ser and Gly are present in the medium, their significant M + 2 and M + 3 labeling indicates substantial endogenous synthesis from glucose-derived carbon (Fig. 2B).

These data support active synthesis of nonessential amino acids in MEFs, with carbon input from glycolysis and the TCA cycle revealed by ¹³C-glucose tracing and consistent with D₂O labeling patterns (Fig. 2E).

15N-ammonia tracing shows Glu-dependent nitrogen redistribution into amino acids and polyamines

In mammalian cells, inorganic ¹⁵NH₄⁺ can be incorporated into Glu through the reversible action of glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH), which catalyzes the conversion of α-KG and ammonia to Glu22. Upon supplementation with ¹⁵NH₄Cl, the ¹⁵N label is first incorporated into Glu, which functions both as a substrate and an amino group donor in cellular biosynthesis (Fig. 2C). To avoid confounding metabolic effects, we used ¹⁵NH₄Cl as a surrogate label for Glu rather than supplementing labeled Glu directly, as Glu in the medium competes with cystine for uptake by the antiporter SLC7A11 (system Xc⁻), which can reduce glutathione synthesis and cause oxidative stress57.

MEFs were cultured for 24 h in medium supplemented with 0.5–5 mM ¹⁵NH₄Cl showed a linear, dose-dependent increase in Glu M + 1 mass isotopomers (Fig. 2D). Ala and Asp/Asn showed similar labeling, supporting their synthesis via Glu-dependent transamination via alanine aminotransferase (ALAT) and aspartate aminotransferase (GOT) from pyruvate (Pyr) and oxaloacetate (OAA), respectively (Figs. 1A and 2A). The M + 1 enrichment of Ala and Asp/Asn (Fig. 2D)—amino acids absent from the culture medium—indicates they are synthesized de novo using Glu as the amino donor.

The increase in the M + 2 isotopologue of Asn was positively associated with that of M + 1, but with a much lower rate of change. This suggests that the minor enrichment observed in M + 2 is more likely a consequence of the natural isotopic distribution of the 15N-labeled M + 1 species rather than the result of a true double 15N incorporation (Supplementary Fig. 2A). These findings support the notion that, in this system, Gln rather than Glu serves as the nitrogen donor for the transamination conversion of Asp to Asn, consistent with the proposed substrate specificity of asparagine synthetase (ASNS)58.

Despite both being present in DMEM, Ser and Gly showed significant M + 1 enrichment, consistent with active synthesis via phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1)-dependent transamination of 3-phosphohydroxypyruvate to 3-phosphoserine followed by conversion to Ser and subsequent serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT)-mediated formation of Gly (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Fig. 2A). Glu also acts as a direct substrate for Pro and Orn synthesis via pyrroline-5-carboxylate synthase (P5CS) and ornithine aminotransferase (OAT), respectively; Put and Spd labeling reflected continued nitrogen flow through the Glu–Orn–polyamine axis (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Interestingly, BCAAs Leu, Ile, and to a lesser extent Val, also showed M + 1 enrichment (Fig. 2D), indicating reversible BCAT transamination activity in MEFs22. The lower labeling of Val compared to Leu and Ile mirrors results from D₂O tracing (Fig. 1E), suggesting lower metabolic engagement. In contrast, no significant ¹⁵N incorporation was detected in Gln, Met, Thr, Lys, or aromatic amino acids under these conditions (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 2B), indicating no biosynthesis or Glu-dependent nitrogen incorporation.

In summary, ¹⁵N labeling from inorganic ammonia via Glu confirms de novo synthesis of Ala, Asp, Asn, Ser, Gly, and Pro, as well as polyamines Put and Spd, and demonstrates reversible BCAT activity in MEFs. Apart from Met (Fig. 2E), these labeling patterns align with deuterium incorporation from D₂O (Supplementary Fig. 2B).

To further validate that deuterium labeling of BCAAs reflects BCAT activity and accurately reflects BCAT substrate specificity, we supplied Ile and Val labeled with 15N and assessed label redistribution across the amino acid pool. As expected, we observed higher enrichment of the Glu M + 1 isotopomer with Ile than Val (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 3A), confirming the substrate preference of BCAT. Comparable M + 1 15N labeling was also observed in Ala, Asp, Asn, and Pro, consistent with Glu-mediated transamination and amino acid synthesis. Notably, 15N incorporation into Leu exceeded that of Glu, suggesting BCAT-mediated amino group transfer among BCAAs—i.e., using one BCAA as an amino donor to transaminate a branched-chain keto acid (BCKA) to regenerate another BCAA. These observations are consistent with differential substrate engagement within the BCAA pool.

To further confirm BCAA catabolism as a source of labeling with deuterium labeling and the differential utilization of substrates, we used BT2, an inhibitor of branched-chain keto acid dehydrogenase kinase (BCKDK), which phosphorylates and deactivates branched-chain keto acid dehydrogenase (BCKDH), which conducts the committing step in BCAA utilization59 (Fig. 3C). BT2 decreased deuterium labeling of Ile and Leu, while slightly increasing deuterium incorporation into Val (Fig. 3D), indicating that increased utilization of BCKA by BCKDH shifted the balance in labeling of the metabolically competitive Ile and Leu and limiting engagement of BCKA with BCATs. Interestingly, BT2 had secondary effects on amino acid metabolism: it decreased labeling of Asp, which may indicate channeling of α-KG toward transamination by BCAT rather than toward OAA synthesis in the TCA cycle, thereby restricting levels of OAA for transamination to Asp. Decreases in Ala and Put (Pro) may indicate preservation of Glu in conditions of increased BCAA utilization. In MEFs, BCAA uptake is driven by the transporter LAT160, an obligatory exchanger utilizing efflux of Gln for BCAA61. The expected competition of Gln efflux with deamination to Glu may promote Glu preservation, reflected in reduced transamination of pyruvate to Ala or reduced synthesis of Pro and Orn via P5C.

Methionine labeling arises from homocysteine remethylation

As we observed deuterium incorporation into Met (Fig. 1E) and the M + 2 mass isotopomer enrichment pattern suggested a single labeling event (Supplementary Fig. 1C), we investigated the origin of this incorporation. Notably, Met is not part of the Glu-dependent transamination network (Supplementary Fig. 2A, E). Instead, following its conversion to S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), Met functions as a methyl group donor in numerous methylation reactions. After methyl transfer, SAM is converted to S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH), which is hydrolyzed to homocysteine (Hcy). Cellular regeneration of Met from Hcy completes the methionine cycle (Fig. 3E).

We hypothesized that this final regeneration step provides opportunity for single deuterium incorporation. To test this, we replaced unlabeled Met with a ¹³C-labeled Met isotopomer (at C1, which is retained through the SAM/SAH cycle; Fig. 3E). If deuterium incorporation occurs during Met regeneration, we expected to observe an M + 2 mass isotopomer (one ¹³C + one deuterium) under D₂O exposure. As predicted, ¹³C-Met demonstrated increased M + 2 labeling (Fig. 3F).

Deuterium labeling reflects endogenous synthesis: proof-of-concept

To further link deuterium incorporation and endogenous amino acid synthesis, we tested whether this labeling could be attenuated by specific metabolic inhibitors, demonstrating its potential utility as a discovery tool. We used DL-propargylglycine (PAG), a known inhibitor of cystathionine γ-lyase62,63, which has also been reported to inhibit alanine aminotransferase (ALAT)64. This limited specificity is characteristic of pyridoxal phosphate (PLP)-dependent enzyme inhibitors, which typically act by forming stable adducts with the PLP coenzyme62,65.

We first assessed changes in the intracellular amino acid content of cells treated with PAG (2 mM) over a 6-h period using targeted quantitative analysis (Fig. 4A). In line with the reported inhibition of ALAT, we observed a dramatic decrease in Ala levels within 1 h of treatment. Since Ala is absent from the culture medium, its intracellular availability depends entirely on de novo synthesis, and PAG completely blocked the incorporation of deuterium, evidenced by loss of the M + 1 mass isotopomer (Fig. 4C). We also observed decreased levels of Ser and Gly (Fig. 4A), despite their availability in the extracellular medium. Consistent with this observation, deuterium incorporation into these amino acids was also reduced (Fig. 4C), suggesting decreased engagement of their biosynthetic pathways. Interestingly, levels of Thr, an exogenous amino acid that was not metabolically labeled in any of the previous experiments, were also decreased with PAG treatment (Fig. 2E). We hypothesized this phenomenon is due to a decrease in Thr uptake by an exchanger-type transporter and follows reduced levels of efflux substrates. Ala and Thr are substrates of ASCT266, an amino acid transporter whose activity has been confirmed in MEFs60. It is therefore possible that Ala serves as an efflux substrate supporting ASCT2-mediated uptake of Thr. ASCT2 has been described to transport some amino acids bidirectionally (such as Ala, Ser, Asn, and Thr), while Gln is primarily imported66. In PAG-treated cells, we observed decreased levels of Thr, Ser, Gly, Ala, and to a lesser extent Gln (Fig. 4A), consistent with ASCT2 dependence. Differences in inhibition likely result from compensatory transporter activity; for example, compared to Ser, Gly or Thr, Gln has numerous transporters67, including SNAT2 (Slc38a2) playing important role in sustaining Gln levels68, which has been confirmed to be active in our model of MEFs69, activated by amino acid deficiency70, and is a known target of the ATF4-dependent stress adaptation program induced during the integrated stress response (ISR)71. Such flexibility in Gln uptake could explain the relative resistance of its intracellular levels to Ala depletion and reduced ASCT2 uptake (Fig. 4B).

To explore these metabolic pathways in a relevant, more specialized cell line, we employed the well-characterized metabolic response model of mouse insulinoma MIN6 cells, which are highly specialized pancreatic β-cells. Previous studies have shown that activation of the ISR in these cells—through treatment with thapsigargin (Tg), an inhibitor of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) Ca²⁺-ATPase (SERCA) pump—leads to PERK activation and subsequent ATF4-mediated upregulation of glycolytic flux toward Ala, Ser, and Gly72. While labeling of Asp, Asn, Gly, and Ser was similar between MEFs and MIN6 cells, Gln labeling in MIN6 was much higher, exceeding 1%, indicating de novo synthesis (compare Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Labeling of Ala was also higher in MIN6 cells (Fig. 4C, D). However, despite Glu labeling being higher in MIN6 cells, labeling of its metabolic product Pro was reduced (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, there were noticeable differences in labeling of BCAAs and Met (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B) with lower deuterium incorporation in MIN6, and reduced labeling of Put, indicating lower capacity for polyamine synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B).

PAG treatment reduced Ala labeling, and decreased Ser and Gly M + 1 after 6 h, indicating inhibition of amino acid synthesis (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 4B). Labeling of Asp, Glu, Asn, and Pro remained unaffected (Supplementary Fig. 4B), consistent with our findings in MEFs and the selectivity of PAG. The only notable difference between PAG treatment in MEFs and MIN6 cells was that PAG inhibited Met M + 1 incorporation in MEFs but not MIN6 cells, suggesting lower homocysteine (Hcy) recycling into Met in this β-cell model (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B).

We also confirmed ISR induction in Tg-treated MIN6 cells by immunodetection of phosphorylated eIF2α and ATF4 proteins (Fig. 4E)72,73. We observed intracellular accumulation of BCAAs and aromatic amino acids (Fig. 4F), which are substrates for the LAT1 transporter60,71, as well as an increase in levels of glycolytic amino acids Ala, Ser, and Gly74,75 (Fig. 4F). In Tg-treated cells, D2O labeling increased in M + 1 mass isotopomers of Ala, Ser, and Gly (Fig. 4D), consistent with previous 13C-labeling from MIN6 cells showing increased flux72. We observed a decrease in Asp, Asn, and Gln labeling in stressed cells (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 4B), and an increase in incorporation of deuterium into BCAAs after 6 h (Supplementary Fig. 4B).

In conclusion, we demonstrate that deuterium labeling of amino acids—including Ala—reflects active de novo synthesis and, more importantly, can be modulated by changes in metabolism like induction of the ISR. In addition, we have identified cell-type- and context-dependent differences in deuterium incorporation in Ala over short labeling periods, challenging the assumption that Ala labeling by heavy water is constant and uniformly applicable across tissues49,50.

Metabolic labeling of polyamines with D2O

Polyamine and amino acid metabolism intersect (i) at the level of Orn synthesis from Arg or Glu, and (ii) through the use of Met as a donor in the stepwise synthesis of Spd and Spm from Put. To demonstrate metabolic labeling of polyamines with D2O, we used difluoromethylornithine (DFMO)76, an irreversible inhibitor of ornithine decarboxylase (ODC1), the rate-limiting enzyme in polyamine synthesis and the primary source of Put (Fig. 4G). Within 6 h of DFMO treatment, deuterium labeling of Put and Spd was markedly diminished, as indicated by reduced M + 1 abundance (Fig. 4H). In contrast, DFMO had no measurable effect on D-labeling of Ala, Asp, Glu, or Pro (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. 4D), indicating specificity toward polyamine synthesis.

Inhibition of Put and Spd D-labeling by DFMO could not be rescued by supplementation with exogenous Put (Fig. 4I), although total Put and Spd levels—measured as a sum of M and M + 1—were restored in DFMO-treated cells (Fig. 4J), suggesting that reduction of Put synthesis decreases levels of Spd labeling. Given that excess Put downregulates ODC1 expression through translational repression and protein degradation77–79, we assessed the effect of exogenous Put on deuterium-labeling of polyamines. We observed a significant reduction in deuterium incorporation into Put after both 6 and 24 h of Put supplementation (Supplementary Fig. 4C), consistent with negative feedback regulation of ODC1. Collectively, these results support the use of metabolic deuterium-labeling of Put—and to a lesser extent Spd—as a sensitive readout for polyamine synthesis, responsive to both pharmacological inhibition and physiological regulation.

We also assessed the metabolic impact of 5′-methylthioadenosine (MTA), a byproduct of polyamine synthesis formed during the transfer of aminopropyl groups from decarboxylated S-adenosylmethionine (dcSAM) in the biosynthesis of Spd and Spm, as MTA has been shown to inhibit both spermidine synthase and spermine synthase in vitro80. Exposure to increasing concentrations of MTA (100–300 μM) for 12 h, with a 6-h D₂O labeling window, resulted in a dose-dependent decrease in M + 1 labeling of both Put and Spd (Fig. 4K). Concurrently, there was a reduction in Met M + 1 labeling, suggesting inhibition of the methionine salvage cycle and homocysteine recycling. In contrast, Glu and Pro labeling remained stable or even increased, indicating selective metabolic disruption by MTA.

Gln donates nitrogen for multiple biosynthetic processes (e.g., synthesis of Asn from Asp, nucleotide biosynthesis, and glucosamine formation), so its absence likely activates compensatory pathways. Under Gln starvation, D₂O labeling revealed increased deuterium incorporation into Gln (Fig. 5B), consistent with activation of compensatory biosynthetic pathways. Deuterium labeling of Glu, Ala, Ser, and Gly—amino acids derived from glycolysis—was also increased (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 5A), consistent with ISR- associated metabolic remodeling. Despite increased Glu labeling, total Glu levels decreased over time, suggesting continued utilization of Glu and altered metabolic routing toward downstream pathways, such as Pro and polyamine metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Ala levels gradually declined despite enhanced labeling, while Ser levels initially rose but later declined between 12 and 24 h. Gly, in contrast, accumulated steadily, likely influenced by the extracellular availability of Ser and Gly in the culture media.

Unexpectedly, Gln starvation also enhanced deuterium labeling of BCAAs, suggesting altered BCAT activity, consistent with increased Glu labeling (Supplementary Fig. 5A), accompanied by intracellular BCAA accumulation of BCAAs (Fig. 5A). To understand the origin of BCAA accumulation, we used D₂O labeling to assess amino acid transport activity. LAT1 operates as an obligate exchanger, requiring the efflux of intracellular amino acids—commonly Gln—for the uptake of BCAAs and aromatic amino acids61. MEFs were cultured at full confluence in media at 50% reduced volume to enhance detection sensitivity, and amino acids were monitored at 20% D₂O exposure. After 18–24 h in control conditions, media levels of Gln, BCAAs, aromatic amino acids, Met, Ser, and Gly were decreased, indicating uptake, whereas levels of Ala, Asn, and Pro were elevated, indicating secretion (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. 5B). In Gln-starved cells, Asn and Ala secretion was reduced, while Pro efflux remained unchanged (Fig. 5C). This reduction may reflect decreased intracellular availability of those amino acids for transporter exchange. Alternatively, these findings challenge previous suggestions that Asn24 could substitute for Gln in sustaining LAT1-mediated transport, as one would expect to observe a compensatory increase of Asn in the media if it were used for exchange in the absence of Gln24.

BCAA uptake and utilization also declined in Gln-starved cells (Fig. 5C). However, extracellular BCAA mass isotopomers showed greater deuterium enrichment (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 5C), likely reflecting increased labeling under starvation (Supplementary Fig. 5A).

Interestingly, although Met utilization was reduced under Gln starvation (Fig. 5C), its extracellular pool showed increased enrichment in deuterated mass isotopomers (Fig. 5D). Notably, intracellular Met labeling remained unchanged regardless of Gln availability (Supplementary Fig. 5A), indicating that the rise in extracellular labeled Met (M + 1) resulted from enhanced secretion rather than increased synthesis. These observations indicate altered amino acid exchange dynamics under glutamine restriction. To test this hypothesis, we removed Met for 1 h from control cells and from cells starved of Gln for 12 h (ISR-adapted). In control cells, Met removal led to an increase in intracellular BCAAs and aromatic amino acids (Fig. 5E), indicating induction of compensatory mechanisms. In Gln-starved cells with LAT1 upregulation, Met depletion resulted in reduced intracellular BCAAs, Tyr, and Phe (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

We have extensively validated the use of heavy water (D₂O) as a metabolic marker for amino acid and polyamine metabolism and now present it as a tool to the scientific community. D₂O labeling enabled the assessment of changes in amino acid and polyamine biosynthetic activity, BCAA utilization, and the Met/Hcy cycle, supporting a multilayered description of metabolism in both cultured cells and in vivo.

Because deuterium incorporation arises from multiple enzymatic reactions that generate non-exchangeable C–H bonds during metabolite formation, enrichment of M + 1 mass isotopomers reflects the cumulative probability of labeling events occurring during biosynthesis. Changes in deuterium incorporation therefore report pathway engagement and shifts in metabolic activity across interconnected pathways. D₂O labeling provides a sensitive indicator of metabolic remodeling and biosynthetic capacity across conditions and cell types. In this context, transamination reactions, in addition to the synthesis of the carbon backbone, also provide opportunities for D₂O labeling (Fig. 1B).

Deuterium substitution can influence enzymatic reaction rates through kinetic isotope effects, and therefore, the concentration of D₂O used in metabolic labeling experiments requires careful consideration. Moderate enrichment levels are generally well tolerated in mammalian systems and have been widely used to monitor biosynthetic activity without substantial disruption of cellular metabolism. In the present study, the concentrations employed were selected to provide sufficient labeling sensitivity while maintaining normal cellular physiology. Consistent with this interpretation, deuterium incorporation increased approximately linearly with D₂O concentration for amino acids showing labeling (Fig. 1), indicating that enrichment scaled predictably with tracer availability across the range tested. Under these conditions, deuterium incorporation reflects pathway engagement and biosynthetic activity across metabolic networks, while potential kinetic isotope effects are unlikely to substantially alter the qualitative interpretation of labeling patterns.

In addition to these considerations, interpretation of deuterium labeling also depends on the sources contributing to the labeled amino acid pool. Current views on deuterium labeling of amino acids have been predominantly established on the basis of Ala, in part because, in GC/MS analysis, the fragment ions used for alanine quantification preserve biosynthetically incorporated deuterium in a consistent manner, supporting its use as a practical surrogate for D₂O enrichment. After long exposure to heavy water, Ala labeling appears constant across tissues49, allowing definition of a D2O:Ala ratio as 3.748,84, consistent with multiple labeling opportunities and the M + 1 and M + 2 Ala enrichments presented here (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1C). This may be especially important if Ala is secreted from cells, especially from muscle, where we observed the highest levels of short-term Ala labeling, as chronically labeled circulating Ala could yield uniform labeling over time. Recent studies have demonstrated deuterium labeling of all amino acids with enrichment patterns comparable to those observed in this work: highest incorporation in Ala, Asp, Glu, and Pro, intermediate incorporation in Gly, Ile, Leu, Asn, and Ser, and markedly lower labeling in Val54, confirming the findings from mice chronically exposed to 3H2O (tritiated water)85. During prolonged exposure to D2O or 3H2O, where labeling is sustained by enrichment of drinking water, enrichment of amino acids outside biosynthetic capacity (exogenous) may reflect microbiome synthesis. Alternatively, these patterns could arise from protein turnover over prolonged D₂O exposure (16–24 h). In this context, amino acids released from protein degradation may retain deuterium incorporated at non-exchangeable carbon positions during prior protein synthesis, thereby contributing to the labeled free amino acid pool.

While sample preparation minimizes contributions from exchangeable hydrogen atoms through derivatization and solvent-mediated back-exchange, it does not eliminate labeling present at carbon-bound positions. Therefore, protein turnover may represent a contributing source of labeled amino acids under these conditions. However, the condition-dependent and pathway-specific labeling patterns observed in this study are more consistent with changes in de novo biosynthetic activity than with bulk protein degradation, which would be expected to produce more uniform labeling across amino acids. In particular, the differential responses observed under metabolic perturbations, including BT2 treatment, PAG inhibition, and glutamine deprivation, suggest that labeling dynamics primarily reflect active metabolic remodeling. Nonetheless, the contribution of protein turnover cannot be fully excluded under the present experimental conditions.

The differential redistribution of amino groups among BCAAs, observed despite similar intracellular concentrations of Ile, Leu, and Val (11.02 ± 1.64%, 13.95 ± 1.96%, and 12.84 ± 1.97% mmol/mg of protein, respectively; Fig. 4A), suggests a potential regulatory role for BCATs in maintaining BCAA balance, possibly directing specific BCAAs toward catabolic pathways. The observed substrate engagement is consistent with the proposed role of BCATs in the metabolic mobilization of D-amino acid isomers: BCATs may bind D-amino acids, catalyze deamination (eliminating the stereocenter), and subsequently re-aminate to generate L-BCAAs86. This activity requires a high enzyme affinity for BCKAs and may also account for the preferential transfer of the 15N label from Ile to Leu.

These observations also extended to changes in amino acid exchange across the plasma membrane. D2O labeling further revealed changes in membrane amino acid transport, including the activity of ASCT2 (Slc1A5) and LAT1 (Slc7A5). In this context, the observed changes in amino acid labeling and extracellular profiles under Gln restriction indicate a reorganization of amino acid exchange dynamics. This suggests that, in the absence of Gln, Met may serve as an alternative efflux substrate for LAT1-mediated amino acid exchange. These findings support Met substitution for Gln as an efflux substrate to drive LAT1-mediated import of essential amino acids during Gln restriction as a possible scenario.

Removal of Gln from the media altered the ASCT2 transport profile; in control cells, Ala and Asn (both absent in DMEM) accumulated in the media (Fig. 5C), with Ala being labeled by deuterium (Fig. 5D), indicating intracellular origins. Upon Gln removal, Ala and Asn no longer accumulated extracellularly (Fig. 5C), but rather Gly levels were increased, including deuterated mass isotopomers (Fig. 5D), revealing replacement of Ala with Gly as an efflux substrate of ASCT2. It is also possible that the decrease in Ala and Asn secretion is driven by reduced intracellular availability or by increased uptake via other amino acid transporters activated during the ISR. As there is no Gln imported by any transporter, the functional interconnection of Gln with LAT1 is disrupted, with Met replacing Gln as a LAT161 efflux substrate as a plausible scenario. Overall, D₂O labeling provided a multilayer readout of amino acid homeostasis and transport dynamics under nutrient deficiency.

A complementary pattern was observed for ASCT2-mediated transport. In MEFs under control conditions, Ala production appears to support ASCT2-mediated uptake of its substrates (Gln, Asn, Ser, Gly, and Thr) with Ala acting as the counterpart efflux substrate. Decrease in Ala synthesis upon PAG treatment was instantly reflected in the intracellular decrease of Gly and Ser, and especially Thr, identifying ASCT2 as a primary Thr transporter in this context. Gln levels were only slightly affected by decrease of Ala levels, likely as a result of compensatory membrane transporters, including SNAT2 (Slc38A2)60,69.

The tissue-specific labeling patterns observed in vivo reflect known metabolic specialization across organs. The elevated glutamine labeling in brain tissues is consistent with the Glu/Gln cycle between neurons and astrocytes, where glutamate released by neurons is taken up and converted to glutamine, introducing additional opportunities for deuterium incorporation. This is further supported by the parallel increase in Asn labeling, consistent with amide nitrogen transfer from glutamine to aspartate via asparagine synthetase87. In contrast, differences between liver and muscle reflect their distinct roles in systemic amino acid metabolism. Muscle exhibited the highest Ala labeling, consistent with its role in alanine85 production and release, whereas the liver showed higher labeling of Ser and Gly88, in line with its central role in amino acid biosynthesis. The differential labeling of BCAAs, with higher enrichment in muscle and lower in liver, is consistent with tissue-specific BCAT activity and interorgan exchange of BCKAs. Finally, the elevated labeling of taurine and methionine in the hippocampus may reflect enhanced recycling within sulfur amino acid metabolism, potentially involving homocysteine remethylation pathways. Additionally, systemic circulation of labeled alanine during prolonged D₂O exposure may contribute to labeling homogenization across tissues. As D₂O is approved for use in human studies, our approach may also translate to assessing amino acid and polyamine metabolism in human tissues following interventions, such as exercise or dietary changes. Deuterium labeling may offer a valuable means to describe the metabolic flexibility of cancer cells, which often underlies their resistance to treatment, or to characterize phenotypes with dormant or stem-like properties. We envision that, following D₂O administration, analysis of deuterium incorporation into amino acids and polyamines from biopsied or resected cancerous tissue could provide valuable insights into cancer cell metabolism itself. Alternatively, resected tissue—either after enzymatic dissociation or sectioning—could be treated ex vivo in the presence of D₂O to identify unique tumor-specific metabolic vulnerabilities, thereby enabling the design of personalized approaches and supportive therapies.

Finally, these considerations are particularly relevant in dynamic physiological contexts. Recent discussions89,90 have questioned theoretical foundations for D₂O-based protein synthesis studies, particularly in the context of exercise91. Exercise activates the ISR, as indicated by ATF4 induction92, which could potentially alter alanine labeling via changes in amino acid biosynthetic capacity. This underscores the need to interpret deuterium-based metrics in the contexts of biological conditions and metabolic states.

Methods

Treatment and study design

Mouse embryonic fibroblasts93 were cultivated in high glucose DMEM (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), supplemented with 5% FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific), 2 mM glutamine, and penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C at 5% CO2. Min 6 cells were from Addexbio Technologies (cat. C0018008), maintained in high glucose DMEM with 10% heat activated FBS (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) at 37 °C at 5% CO2. The experimental protocol was approved by the Local Ethical Committee for Animal Experiments at the University of Life Science in Lublin, Poland (approval no. 88/2023). Mice (CD-1 strain, 6 weeks old, male, Charles River, Sulzfeld, Germany) were administered intraperitoneally (ip) with D2O at a volume of 0.014 ml per g of body weight and sacrificed by decapitation at 15, 30, and 60 min post injection (7 animals/time point). After decapitation, brains were removed and rinsed in ice-cold 0.9% NaCl. Brain regions, including the hippocampus, brainstem, and cortex, were dissected on an ice-cold plate and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Additionally, heart, liver, and hindlimb skeletal muscle samples were collected, rinsed in cold saline, and frozen in liquid nitrogen. All tissues were stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Labeling experiments

For 13C-glucose labeling experiments, cells were cultured in glucose- and pyruvate-free DMEM (Gibco, Cat. no. A1443001) supplemented with either uniformly labeled 13C-glucose (Merck, Cat. no. 310808) or unlabeled glucose (4.5 g L−1), 5% FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 U mL−1 penicillin and streptomycin.

For amino acid labeling, culture media were prepared from DMEM powder lacking isoleucine, methionine, threonine, and valine (US Biologicals, Cat. no. D9800-25) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled or unlabeled amino acids were added to final concentrations of 0.8 mM (isoleucine), 0.2 mM (methionine), 0.8 mM (threonine), and 0.8 mM (valine). Glucose concentration was adjusted to 4.5 g L−1, and the media were supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated FBS, 2 mM L-glutamine, and 100 U mL−1 penicillin and streptomycin.

Labeled compounds included L-isoleucine-15N (MedChemExpress, Cat. no. HY-N0771S2), L-valine-15N (MedChemExpress, Cat. no. HY-N0717S), and L-methionine-1-13C (Merck, Cat. no. 490083). For ammonia flux experiments, media were supplemented with 15NH4Cl (Merck, Cat. no. 299251). Heavy water (D₂O; Merck, Cat. no. 756822) was used for deuterium labeling experiments.

Synthesis of APDS

The derivatizing reagent 3-aminopyridyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate (APDS) was synthesized following a modified procedure described by Shimbo et al. (2009)94. Briefly, N,N′-dihydroxysuccinimidyl carbonate (DSC, 1.28 g, 5 mmol) was dissolved in 25 mL of acetonitrile at room temperature. A solution of 3-aminopyridine (470 mg, 5 mmol) in 25 mL of acetonitrile was added dropwise to the carbonate solution over approximately 2 h. The reaction mixture was then stirred for an additional 21 h at room temperature.

After completion, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was filtered. The crude product was resuspended in 2 mL of diethyl ether and filtered to afford 3-aminopyridyl-N-hydroxysuccinimidyl carbamate (APDS) as a pale solid.

Extraction and derivatization of amino acids

Amino acids were extracted from cultured mammalian cells and mouse tissues using a methanol-based procedure optimized for polar metabolite recovery. For cell samples, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Extraction was performed by adding 1.5 mL of 80% methanol acidified with 5% formic acid directly to the culture plate. Plates were gently agitated intermittently and kept at 4 °C for 2 h. The extract was transferred to microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected, and the solvent was evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure.

For tissue samples, approximately 50 mg of dissected mouse tissue (including hippocampus and other regions) was homogenized in 400 µL of 80% methanol containing 5% formic acid using a probe sonicator, followed by centrifugation under the same conditions. The supernatant was collected and dried under vacuum.

Dried extracts were reconstituted in borate buffer (200 mM, pH 8.5) using volumes adjusted to the sample size. To remove lipophilic impurities, 300 µL of dichloromethane was added, the mixture was vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min. The upper aqueous phase was collected for derivatization.

Derivatization conditions were adapted to the analytical objective. For samples derived from deuterium labeling (D₂O exposure), 100 µL of the aqueous phase was combined with 15 µL of APDS (20 mg mL−¹ in acetonitrile) and brought to a final volume of 600 µL with borate buffer. For quantitative analysis, 30 µL of the aqueous phase was mixed with 30 µL of an internal standard solution containing L-aspartic acid (13C₄, 15N), L-proline (13C₅, 15N), and norvaline (each 100 µM in 0.01 N HCl), followed by the addition of 15 µL of APDS. Reactions were carried out at 60 °C for 15 min. Derivatized samples were centrifuged briefly to remove particulates and transferred to LC vials for analysis.

LC–MS method for amino compound analysis

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Kinetex C18 column (1.7 µm, 100 Å, 150 × 2.1 mm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA) using a binary solvent system consisting of solvent A (25 mM ammonium formate, pH 6.0) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The flow rate was 0.30 mL min−¹, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 2 µL.

The gradient program was as follows: 2% B (0.0–0.1 min), 6% B (0.1–2.0 min), 13% B (2.0–6.0 min), 30% B (6.0–8.0 min), 60% B (8.1–9.1 min), and 2% B (9.2–14.2 min) for column re-equilibration.

The analysis was performed using a Nexera XR liquid chromatograph (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) coupled to a Shimadzu LCMS-8030 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer via an electrospray ionization (ESI) interface operating in positive ion mode. Source parameters were set as follows: spray voltage, 4500 V; nebulizing gas flow, 1.5 L min−1 (N₂); drying gas flow, 15 L min−1 (N₂); and desolvation line (DL) temperature, 250 °C.

Data acquisition was performed in multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode, targeting the precursor ions of APDS-derivatized amino acids and polyamines and their transitions to m/z 121.1, corresponding to a common fragment ion. MRM parameters were optimized to maximize the intensity of the quantifier ions for each analyte. The complete list of precursor ions, product ions, and retention times used for quantification is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Mass isotopomers (M + n) were monitored by selecting precursor ions shifted according to the isotopic enrichment of interest (e.g., +1, +2, +3 Da), while maintaining the same product ion (m/z 121.1).

Chromatographic peaks were identified by comparing retention times and m/z values with those of a standard amino acid mixture (type B [high range] and type AN [high range]; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan), as well as individual standards (L-glutamine, L-asparagine, and L-tryptophan).

GC–MS method for the determination of deuterated water

The enrichment of deuterium oxide (D₂O) in serum samples was determined by a gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) method based on hydrogen–deuterium exchange with acetone. For sample preparation, 50 µL of serum were mixed with 5 µL of acetone prepared in 5% acetonitrile and 5 µL of 10 M sodium hydroxide. The mixture was incubated overnight at room temperature to allow isotopic exchange between water and the acetone methyl groups. The reaction mixture was then extracted with 1 mL of chloroform, and the organic phase was dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate prior to GC–MS analysis.

Chromatographic analysis was performed using a Trace 1310 gas chromatograph (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). One microliter of the chloroform extract was injected at 250 °C in split mode (1:70) with a constant carrier gas flow of 1.2 mL min⁻¹ (helium). The oven temperature was initially set at 50 °C and held for 2 min, followed by a ramp of 40 °C min−¹ to 260 °C, and held for 2 min as the final condition.

Detection was performed using a single quadrupole ISQ mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) operated in electron impact (EI) ionization mode at 70 eV, with the transfer line and ion source maintained at 230 °C. Data were acquired in selected ion monitoring (SIM) mode, monitoring ions at m/z 58, 59, and 60 corresponding to acetone-d₀, acetone-d₁, and acetone-d₂, respectively. The ratio of m/z 59/m/z 58 was used to calculate the fractional enrichment of deuterated water relative to calibration standards of known D₂O content.

Immunodetection

Whole-cell lysates were prepared in RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 150 mM NaCl; 1% NP-40; 0.5% sodium deoxycholate; 0.1% SDS) supplemented with cOmplete, EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Cat. no. 04693132001) and PhosSTOP phosphatase inhibitor tablets (Roche, Cat. no. 04906845001), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cells were scraped directly into RIPA buffer, transferred to microcentrifuge tubes, and incubated on ice for 15 min with occasional mixing. Samples were sonicated for 60 s and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant containing total protein was collected, and protein concentration was determined using a commercial assay kit with bovine serum albumin as the standard.

Equal amounts of protein were resolved by SDS–PAGE and transferred to Immobilon PVDF membranes (Millipore) using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature in 5% nonfat dry milk prepared in PBST (PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20), and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-ATF4 (Proteintech, Cat. no. 10835-1-AP, RRID: AB_2058600; 1:1000), anti-eIF2α (Invitrogen, Cat. no. AHO0802, RRID: AB_2536316; 1:1000), anti-phospho-eIF2α (Abcam, Cat. no. ab32157, RRID: AB_732117; 1:500), and anti-α-tubulin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. no. 32-2500, RRID: AB_2533071; 1:2000).

After washing three times with TBST (Tris-buffered saline, 0.1% Tween-20), membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Cat. nos. 32430 and 32260). Following additional washes in TBST, immunocomplexes were visualized using chemiluminescent detection (Clarity Western ECL Substrate; Bio-Rad, Cat. no. 170506). Signal acquisition and densitometric analysis were performed using a digital imaging system (ChemiDoc MP, Bio-Rad).

Protein quantification in cells

Protein content in cellular samples was determined using the DC Protein Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) following a modified protocol adapted for cell lysates. Briefly, cells were solubilized in freshly prepared dissolving solution containing 1 M NaOH and 0.5% (w/w) sodium deoxycholate (DOC). Samples were incubated for 1–2 h with gentle shaking until complete cell solubilization was confirmed microscopically. The resulting lysates were transferred to clean microcentrifuge tubes for analysis.

For the assay, 20 µL of standards or cell lysates were pipetted into clean test tubes. Subsequently, 100 µL of reagent A′ and 800 µL of reagent B (cat. #5000114) were added sequentially, vortexing after each addition. Reagent A′ was freshly prepared by adding 20 µL of reagent S (cat. #500-0115) per 1 mL of reagent A (cat. #5000113). The mixture was incubated for 15 min at room temperature, after which absorbance was measured at 650 nm using a spectrophotometer.

A calibration curve was prepared using serial dilutions of a bovine serum albumin (BSA) standard ranging from 0.2 to 1.5 mg mL−1. The standard curve was freshly prepared for each assay, using the same dissolving solution as the samples to ensure matrix compatibility. Protein concentrations in cell lysates were calculated based on the standard curve.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Peak integration and quantification of all LC–MS data were performed using Skyline software (version 15.1, MacCoss Lab Software, University of Washington). Peak boundaries were manually inspected to ensure accurate integration. Quantitative and relative quantitative values were exported as area ratios or absolute concentrations derived from calibration curves.

All downstream data analysis and visualization were conducted in R (version 4.5.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using standard statistical and plotting libraries (tidyverse, ggplot2, and stats). Group comparisons were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.
