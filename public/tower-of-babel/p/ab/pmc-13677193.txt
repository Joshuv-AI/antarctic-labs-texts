Regulation of spermatozoal capacitation by serotonin
Authors: Masakatsu FUJINOKI
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mammalian spermatozoa begin swimming after ejaculation. After spermatozoa enter the oviduct, they undergo capacitation, penetrate the oocyte envelope, and fertilize the oocytes. These processes occur spontaneously but are influenced by hormones and neurotransmitters. Serotonin [5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)] is a well-known neurotransmitter found in reproductive organs. In the female reproductive system, serotonin is synthesized in the cumulus cells and affects steroidogenesis, oocyte maturation, fertilization, and embryo development. Recent studies have suggested that serotonin is also synthesized in spermatozoa and can affect motility, hyperactivation, acrosome reaction, and the success of in vitro fertilization (IVF) in hamsters, humans, mice, and rats. Importantly, the effects of serotonin on sperm function are dose-dependent, at least in hamsters, and serotonin concentrations in the female reproductive tract fluctuate during ovulation. The effects of serotonin on sperm function are mediated by 5-HT receptors (mainly 5-HT2 and 5-HT4), which stimulate Ca2+ and cyclic AMP signaling, respectively. The 5-HT2 receptor is involved in regulating motility and hyperactivation in hamsters, humans, and mice, whereas the 5-HT4 receptor is involved in regulating hyperactivation, the acrosome reaction, and IVF success in hamsters, mice, and rats. Because the 5-HT4 receptor is a common receptor in rodents associated with capacitation and IVF success and is stimulated by serotonin synthesized in spermatozoa, current evidence indicates that the 5-HT4 receptor is especially important for the regulation of rodent spermatozoal functions regulated by serotonin.

Introduction

After ejaculation and semen liquefaction, mammalian spermatozoa begin flagellar movement and swimming [1,2,3,4,5]. First, they show symmetric flagellar movement with a high beat frequency and small amplitude of lateral head displacement, resulting in linear, straight swimming (Fig. 1AFig. 1.Trajectories of mouse spermatozoal motility. This motility is referred to as activation (or non-hyperactivation). Although good spermatozoa exhibit high progressive motility, they do not actualize their fertilization abilities. Mammalian spermatozoa actualize their fertilization ability following capacitation, which occurs in oviducts. Capacitated spermatozoa hyperactivate their flagella and undergo an acrosome reaction in the head [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8]. Hyperactivation is a specialized flagellar movement that allows spermatozoa to progress through viscous oviductal fluid. Hyperactivated spermatozoa can penetrate the envelopment of the oocyte, which consists of cumulus cells and zona pellucida. The capacity for hyperactivation is positively correlated with in vitro fertilization (IVF) success [9,10,11,12]. Hyperactivated spermatozoa show asymmetric flagellar movement, with a low beat frequency and large amplitude of lateral head displacement, resulting in curvilinear, three-dimensional swimming (Fig. 1B and Supplementary Movies 1B and 2) [1, 2, 4,5,6, 13,14,15,16]. The acrosome reaction involves the exocytosis of acrosomal vesicles containing proteases and hyaluronidases. After the acrosome reaction, spermatozoa can digest and penetrate the oocyte envelope, and then bind and fuse with the oocyte [2, 3, 8].

Mammalian spermatozoa can also be capacitated under in vitro fertilization (IVF) conditions. Albumin, Ca2+, and HCO3- are essential components for capacitation in vitro [2,3,4]. Albumin removes cholesterol from the spermatozoal membrane [17] and induces Ca2+ influx through activation of CatSper Ca2+ channels, which are essential for hyperactivation [18, 19]. Under albumin-free conditions, spermatozoa do not show hyperactivation [20, 21], and spermatozoa from mice lacking the CatSper Ca2+ channel fail to show hyperactivation [19]. Both Ca2+ and HCO3- activate soluble adenylate cyclase (sAC) and increase the concentration of cyclic AMP (cAMP) [3, 5, 22,23,24]. Moreover, both Ca2+ and cAMP induce activation of protein kinases and phosphatases and regulate protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation [1, 2, 5, 6, 25,26,27,28,29]. Protein kinase A (PKA) induces activation of CatSper Ca2+ channels in mouse spermatozoa [30] but not in human spermatozoa [31]. In human spermatozoa, progesterone induces activation of the CatSper Ca2+ channel [32, 33]. Under Ca2+-deficient conditions, spermatozoa cannot maintain their motility and fail to undergo hyperactivation [20, 21]. Moreover, Ca2+ induces the acrosome reaction through activation of the CatSper Ca2+ channel [2, 3, 8, 34,35,36]. Both progesterone and the zona pellucida induce the acrosome reaction via Ca2+ influx [8, 34,35,36,37,38].

The acrosome reaction and hyperactivation are regulated by oviductal hormones and neurotransmitters, such as progesterone, estradiol, melatonin, serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT)), and γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) [4, 5, 7, 8, 39, 40]. Mammalian spermatozoa are capacitated under the effects of oviductal hormones and neurotransmitters and then undergo the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation [4, 7, 8, 40]. Serotonin is an effector of the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation. This article reviews the effects of serotonin on acrosomal reactions and hyperactivation.

Serotonin in reproductive organs, tissues and cells

Serotonin, a well-known neurotransmitter, is synthesized from tryptophan (Trp) via two reactions catalyzed by tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH) and aromatic l-amino acid decarboxylase (AADC; also known as DOPA decarboxylase) [70, 71] (Fig. 2Fig. 2.Serotonin synthesis pathway.). In the serotonin biosynthesis pathway, Trp is first hydroxylated by TPH to produce the intermediate 5-hydroxytryptophan (5-HTP). Subsequently, 5-HTP is decarboxylated by AADC to generate serotonin. In the nervous system, serotonin regulates mood and alleviates depression. Serotonin is also found in the gastrointestinal tract and platelets. Moreover, serotonin is metabolized to melatonin, which maintains the natural sleep-wake cycle [70]. Serotonin also regulates various cell functions through specific receptors known as 5-HT receptors [70,71,72,73]. 5-HT receptors belong to seven families (5-HT1–7) (Table 2Table 2.5-HT receptors and their functionsFamilySub-familyG-proteinFunctions5-HT11AGiSuppression of tmAC and decreasing of cAMP concentration1B1D1E1F5-HT22AGqActivation of PLC and increasing of IP3 concentration2B2C5-HT3Influx of cation and inducing of depolarization5-HT4GsActivation of tmAC and increasing of cAMP concentration5-HT5GiSuppression of tmAC and decreasing of cAMP concentration5-HT6GsActivation of tmAC and increasing of cAMP concentration5-HT7GsActivation of tmAC and increasing of cAMP concentrationGi, inhibioty G protein; Gs, stimulatory G protein; Gq, Gq protein; PLC, phospholipase C; IP3, inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate; tmAC, transmembrane adenylate cyclase.). The 5-HT1 receptor has five subtypes (5-HT1A, 5-HT1B, 5-HT1D, 5-HT1E, and 5-HT1F), while the 5-HT2 receptor has three subtypes (5-HT2A–C). All 5-HT receptors, except 5-HT3, are G protein-coupled receptors. 5-HT1 and 5-HT5 receptors are coupled to the inhibitory G protein (Gi) and suppress transmembrane adenylate cyclase (tmAC) and decrease cAMP. The 5-HT4, 5-HT6, and 5-HT7 receptors are coupled to stimulatory G proteins (Gs), stimulate tmAC, and increase cAMP, which induces the activation of PKA. 5-HT2 receptors are coupled to a G protein called Gq, which stimulates phospholipase C (PLC) to generate inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate (IP3) and diacylglycerol from phosphatidylinositol and phosphatidylcholine, respectively. The generated IP3 binds to the IP3 receptor, a large transmembrane Ca2+ channel located in the endoplasmic membrane. Binding of IP3 increases the intracellular Ca2+ concentration and induces the release of Ca2+ from the endoplasmic reticulum. Diacylglycerol activates a protein kinase C (PKC). The 5-HT3 receptor is a ligand-gated cation channel receptor that induces depolarization.

Serotonin and 5-HT receptors are found in various reproductive cells and tissues, such as Leydig cells, spermatozoa, oocytes, cumulus-oocyte complexes, follicular fluid, oviductal fluid, and embryos [61,62,63,64, 73,74,75,76,77]. In rats, the serotonin concentration in the oviduct was 2.06–3.34 μg/g fresh tissue [78]. In humans, the serotonin concentrations in the preovulatory follicles and cystically degenerated follicles were 14.3 ± 8.9 μg/100 ml and 12.2 ± 6.2 μg/100 ml, respectively [79]. In female reproductive organs, serotonin is released from cumulus cells and is involved in the regulation of steroidogenesis and oocyte maturation [73]. Serotonin also induces progesterone release from human granulosa cells [80]. In male reproductive organs, serotonin is released from the Leydig cells of the testes, binds to the 5-HT2 receptor, and suppresses androgen secretion [74, 75, 81]. In hamsters, serotonin is also involved in the regulation of seasonal breeding through gonadotropin-induced cAMP signaling [75]. Serotonin, 5-HT receptors, TPH, and AADC are found from spermatozoa in hamsters, rats, humans and stallions [64, 65, 76, 77, 82]. TPH-positive cells are serotonin-producing cells. TPH has been detected in the heads of human and horse spermatozoa [65, 76, 77]. Although serotonin is not always detectable in human spermatozoa, TPH activity, an indicator of 5-HTP synthesis, was detected in human spermatozoa [65], suggesting that human spermatozoa are serotonin-producing cells. Moreover, TPH and AADC were detected in hamster spermatozoa, and serotonin was detected when hamster spermatozoa were cultured in media containing Trp or 5-HTP [82]. This suggests that hamster spermatozoa possess a serotonin synthesis system and are serotonin-producing cells. Serotonin synthesized by hamster spermatozoa stimulates 5-HT4 receptors, induces the activation of tmAC and PKA, and enhances hyperactivation by activating the CatSper Ca2+ channels [82]. Serotonin-mediated enhancement of hyperactivation in hamster spermatozoa was suppressed by a TPH inhibitor and an AADC inhibitor [82], indicating that hamster spermatozoa hyperactivation is affected by autocrine serotonin. Studies in humans [65] and hamsters [82] suggest that spermatozoa are novel serotonin-secreting cells.

In the oviduct, where fertilization-related events occur, serotonin is supplied from at least three sources: the blood, cumulus cells, and spermatozoa. Blood supplies serotonin, as it is the origin of the oviductal and follicular fluids. Cumulus cells synthesize and secrete serotonin [73]. Serotonin concentrations in female reproductive organs fluctuate during ovulation [73, 78, 79]. In hamster spermatozoa, serotonin affects the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation in a dose-dependent manner [61, 62]. Because spermatozoa also synthesize and secrete serotonin [65, 82], they are a source of serotonin.

Effects of serotonin on the acrosome reaction and hyperactivation

As shown in Table 1E, serotonin induces the acrosome reaction and enhances hyperactivation in hamster spermatozoa in a dose-dependent manner [61, 62]. Research has shown that serotonin (in the micromolar range) induces the acrosome reaction through the 5-HT4 receptor [61]. Serotonin also enhances spermatozoal hyperactivation in a dose-dependent manner [62]. Serotonin in the fM to pM range enhanced hyperactivation through the 5-HT2 receptor, while serotonin in the nM to μM range enhanced hyperactivation through the 5-HT4 receptor. Moreover, serotonin blocks the effects of aging on spermatozoal hyperactivation in hamsters through the 5-HT2 and 5-HT4 receptors [83]. When a low concentration of serotonin stimulates hyperactivation of hamster spermatozoa through the 5-HT2 receptor, the PLC and IP3 receptors are activated [84] (Fig. 4Fig. 4.Proposed model for the effects of serotonin on spermatozoal functions in hamster spermatozoa. For example, the exposure of hamster spermatozoa to progesterone enhanced hyperactivation via an mPR, which activated PLC, IP3 receptor, and PKC [85]. Progesterone also enhances the hyperactivation of rat spermatozoa through mPR, activated PLC, IP3 receptor, and sAC [42]. In bulls, the release of Ca2+ from IP3 receptor-gated Ca2+ stores, Ca2+/calmodulin, and calmodulin-dependent protein kinases are all related to the regulation of spermatozoal hyperactivation [29, 86,87,88].

In hamster spermatozoa, high serotonin concentrations stimulated 5-HT4 receptors and activated tmAC and PKA, thereby activating the CatSper Ca2+ channels (Fig. 4). Following the activation of CatSper Ca2+ channels, spermatozoal hyperactivation was enhanced by the activation of sAC [84]. Signals related to tmAC and PKA regulate hyperactivation when the spermatozoa are stimulated by hormones and neurotransmitters. The exposure of rat spermatozoa to progesterone enhances hyperactivation through mPR and the subsequent activation of tmAC, PKA, CatSper Ca2+ channels, and sAC [42]. Although the 5-HT2 receptor-related hyperactivation of hamster spermatozoa is suppressed by GABA via the GABAA receptor, 5-HT4 receptor-related hyperactivation of hamster spermatozoa is not affected by GABA [66]. The progesterone-enhanced hyperactivation of hamster spermatozoa is also suppressed by GABA via the GABAA receptor [53]. Both 5-HT2 receptor-related hyperactivation and progesterone-enhanced hyperactivation are regulated by activation of PLC and the IP3 receptor [20, 84, 85]. Therefore, GABA is likely to inhibit Ca2+ signaling associated with PLC and IP3 receptors. A recent study [82] showed that hamster spermatozoa secrete serotonin synthesized from Trp. Serotonin secreted by spermatozoa only stimulates the 5-HT4 receptor, which then activates tmAC and PKA and induces the activation of CatSper Ca2+ channels (Fig. 4). Following the activation of CatSper Ca2+ channels, spermatozoal hyperactivation is enhanced through the activation of sAC.

Hyperactivation of spermatozoa in other rodents is also affected by serotonin (Table 1E) [63, 64]. In mouse spermatozoa, serotonin enhances hyperactivation through 5-HT2, 5-HT3, 5-HT4, and 5-HT7 receptors [63]. In rat spermatozoa, serotonin enhances hyperactivation via 5-HT4 receptor [64]. Serotonin stimulates tmAC and activates PKA, which in turn activates the CatSper Ca2+ channels. When spermatozoa are stimulated by hormones and neurotransmitters, signals related to tmAC and PKA regulate hyperactivation. Exposure of rat spermatozoa to progesterone enhances hyperactivation through mPR, which induces the activation of tmAC, PKA, CatSper Ca2+ channels, and sAC [42]. Moreover, in mice and rats, serotonin increases IVF success through the 5-HT4 receptor when spermatozoa are hyperactivated [63, 64].

For human spermatozoa, there are no data showing the effects of serotonin on the acrosome reaction or hyperactivation, although serotonin has been shown to increase tyrosine phosphorylation, a parallel event associated with sperm capacitation [25,26,27, 65, 89]. In the first human study [65], 5-HT1, 5-HT2, and 5-HT3 receptors were detected in the sperm. In a more recent study [77], 5-HT1, 5-HT2, 5-HT3, 5-HT4, 5-HT6 and 5-HT7 receptors were detected. All 5-HT receptors are localized in the head of human spermatozoa [65, 77].

In mammals, serotonin positively affects acrosome reaction, hyperactivation, and IVF success (Table 1E). In rodent spermatozoa, serotonin typically enhanced hyperactivation via stimulation of the 5-HT4 receptor and increased IVF success [62,63,64]. In hamsters, serotonin secreted by spermatozoa enhanced hyperactivation [82] and induced the acrosome reaction [61] through the 5-HT4 receptor. These findings suggest that in rodents, the 5-HT4 receptor and cAMP signaling are essential for regulating the acrosome reaction, hyperactivation, and IVF success.

Effects of serotonin on motility kinematics

In human spermatozoa, serotonin increases progressive motility-associated kinematic parameters, such as velocity straight line (VSL), velocity curved line (VCL), and velocity average path (VAP) [65]. Serotonin at 100 μM increased VCL but did not affect VSL or VAP. In contrast, 10 μM serotonin increased VSL and VAP but did not affect VCL. These increases in VSL, VCL, and VAP were suppressed by a 5-HT2 receptor antagonist [77]. In hamster spermatozoa, serotonin did not affect VSL, VCL, or VAP [83, 84]. Treatment of hamster spermatozoa with a 5-HT2 receptor agonist decreased VSL but did not affect VCL or VAP [84]. However, another study [83] reported that 5-HT2 receptor agonists did not affect VSL, VCL, or VAP in hamster spermatozoa. A 5-HT3 receptor agonist decreases VCL but does not affect VSL, VAP, motility, or hyperactivation in hamster spermatozoa [84]. A 5-HT4 receptor agonist decreases the amplitude of lateral head displacement (ALH) but does not affect VSL, VCL, and VAP [84]. However, in another study [83] a 5-HT4 receptor agonist did not affect VSL, VCL, VAP, or ALH in hamster spermatozoa. Based on these findings in humans and hamsters, serotonin likely affects motility kinematic parameters and hyperactivation in association with the changes in serotonin concentration that occur during ovulation [61, 62, 65, 84].

Although serotonin affects mouse and rat spermatozoa hyperactivation through 5-HT receptors, these effects are not dose dependent [63, 64]. In mouse spermatozoa, although serotonin does not affect motility kinematics, 5-HT receptor agonists do [63]. Treatment with a 5-HT2 receptor agonist decreased VSL, VCL, linearity (LIN), straightness (STR), and ALH. Treatment with a 5-HT3 receptor agonist decreased the VSL, LIN, wobbler coefficient (WOB), and beat-cross frequency (BCF). Treatment with a 5-HT4 receptor agonist decreased VCL and ALH but increased WOB. In contrast, treatment with a 5-HT7 receptor agonist did not affect motility kinematics. In rat spermatozoa, serotonin does not affect motility kinematics [64]. The effects of 5-HT receptor agonists on the motility kinematics of rat spermatozoa are not yet known.

Conclusion

Serotonin has been shown to be present in reproductive structures and cells [61,62,63,64,65, 73,74,75,76,77], and to regulate many reproductive functions [61,62,63,64,65, 73,74,75,76,77, 81]. Serotonin is synthesized in the cumulus cells of females [73] and in the testes and spermatozoa of males [65, 74, 75, 82]. Serotonin secreted from cumulus cells stimulates cumulus cells, oocytes, and spermatozoa (paracrine signaling) [62, 73], whereas serotonin secreted from spermatozoa stimulates spermatozoa (autocrine signaling) [62, 65, 82]. Paracrine serotonin (i.e., from cumulus cells) affects steroidogenesis, oocyte maturation, hyperactivation, fertilization, and embryonic development [62,63,64, 73]. In contrast, autocrine serotonin (in spermatozoa) affects various spermatozoal functions [61,62,63,64,65, 77, 82]. Serotonin increases IVF success via the 5-HT4 receptor [63, 64]. Serotonin also blocks the aging of spermatozoa [83]. In rodents, the 5-HT4 receptor appears to be essential for regulating the acrosome reaction, hyperactivation, and fertilization [61,62,63,64, 82]. According to recent research [61,62,63,64,65], serotonin is an important regulator of the acrosome reaction, hyperactivation, and fertilization.
