Can hypothermia-induced changes in QRS and QT(c) intervals predict cardiac arrest?
Authors: Anders Lund Selli, Les Gordon, Peter Paal, Evelien Cools, Erik Sveberg Dietrichs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypothermic cardiac arrest (HCA) is often caused by ventricular tachyarrhythmias in severe hypothermia. The QRS/QTc ratio may predict arrhythmia risk, but data are insufficient. Routine QRS/QTc measurements during transport and in-hospital care, along with consistent reporting, could improve understanding, management, and survival outcomes in severe accidental hypothermia cases.Keywords: Hypothermia, Cardiac arrest, ECG, Emergency medicine, Ventricular fibrillation, Ventricular tachycardia

Introduction

Accidental hypothermia (core temperature < 35 °C) is a potentially lethal condition, with hypothermic cardiac arrest (HCA) as the most significant complication [1]. The 2025 European Resuscitation Council (ERC) Guidelines include four initial signs and symptoms to determine if a hypothermic patient should be deemed unstable and in need of transport to an Extracorporeal Life Support (ECLS) center. These are: systolic blood pressure < 90 mmHg, temperature < 32° or 30 °C (depending on age and comorbidity), heart rate < 45 beats per minute and “cardiocirculatory instability” [2]. The latter term is unspecific and has for practical reasons been closely related to both low systolic blood pressure and ventricular arrhythmias. Using ECG-recordings to determine arrhythmia-risk could therefore be an important step forward in risk stratification of accidental hypothermia patients to provide better care and determine who should be preferably transferred directly to an ECLS-center.

While one review indicated that shockable rhythms i.e. ventricular fibrillation (VF) and pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) account for two-thirds of cases in witnessed HCA [3], the International Hypothermia Registry suggests that majority of HCA patients initially have asystole or pulseless electrical activity (PEA) [4]. There is limited knowledge about pathophysiology, making it challenging to provide evidence-based strategies for managing these patients.

Fifty percent of patients with severe accidental hypothermia and signs of life when rescue services arrive may have a HCA (often termed rescue collapse) [5], resulting in higher mortality [6]. The 2025 ERC guidelines describe the overall assessment and care of such accidental hypothermia patients [2] but evidence-based recommendations on preventive treatment to reduce the risk of rescue collapse are largely lacking. Implementation of ECG analysis could provide a pathway to improved survival chances if they successfully predict HCA risk.

Reversible prolongation of corrected QT-interval, QTc, has been described in patients undergoing Targeted Temperature Management after normothermic cardiac arrest [7–9], but the pathophysiology may be different from the spontaneous cardiac arrest seen in severe accidental hypothermia because it may be influenced by being post normothermic cardiac arrest and the use of peri-arrest medications. The underlying mechanisms for lethal arrhythmias and cardiac arrest during accidental hypothermia are probably heterogenous. Both prolonged repolarization, dispersion of repolarization and a mixed hypothermia effect on depolarization and repolarization have been suggested as underlying mechanisms [10].

Based on translational studies, recent attempts have explored ECG parameters as a novel way to predict accidental hypothermia patients at risk for ventricular arrhythmias during transport and rewarming [11, 12]. It has been suggested that the ratio between QRS interval (ventricular depolarization) and QTc, which is heart rate corrected QT-interval (ventricular repolarization), might predict pVT and VF. Studies suggest that a low QRS/QTc interval correlates with risk of hypothermia-induced cardiac arrest, with a suggested safe cut-off value at 0.20 [11–13]. In rabbits, QRS/QTc recordings were at their minimum at 31 °C, and higher both during severe hypothermia and mild hypothermia. This concurred with rabbit-hearts having highest risk of ventricular fibrillation at 31 °C [12]. The utility of QRS/QTc has yet to be evaluated in humans suffering from accidental hypothermia.

The aim of this work was to evaluate the prognostic clinical value of QRS/QTc based on ECG parameters in published case reports of accidental hypothermia patients.

Methods

To summarize the present available data, we evaluated published case reports that stated accurate core temperature and QRS, QT and/or QTc values. Articles with mean values of multiple patients and with photographs of ECGs without measured intervals were excluded.

Initially, a PubMed search was conducted (updated 11/11/25) using the parameters ((accidental hypothermia) AND (cardiac arrest)) AND (QTc). This found 33 articles but none were relevant to this study. Therefore, a second search was conducted. One of the authors (LG) has over time collected > 1200 general case reports of accidental hypothermia. These were searched specifically for QT and/or QTc.

Results

173 published articles mentioned QT and/or QTc in the manuscript and were included for screening. A total of 19 articles met the criteria for inclusion [14–32], with 91 separate cases of hypothermic patients (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4). Two cases reported HCA, both with ventricular fibrillation (VF) as the initial rhythm (Tables 1 and 2). A third case reported asystole as the initial ECG-recording in a hypothermic patient. This was not considered HCA due to both a high core temperature of 32.5 °C, and suspected opioid overdose as cause of the arrest. It was therefore excluded in the analysis. Of the two HCA cases, one case reported ECG before HCA [14], with a QRS/QTc of 0.26 and a core temperature of 25 °C. In the other case of HCA (reported with rectal temperature of 23.5 °C), the ECG was obtained after return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) when the temperature was 29.4 °C and showed a QRS/QTc of 0.18 [30].

Discussion

Surprisingly, we only found one report citing accurate ECG measurements in a hypothermic patient before HCA [14]. This prevents reliable evaluation of the prognostic value of QRS/QTc as a biomarker for ventricular arrhythmias during accidental hypothermia from currently available retrospective data.

In one patient the calculated QRS/QTc-ratio was 0.18 after ROSC at 29.4 °C [30], which is below the suggested safe cut-off value of 0.20 indicating a potential continued increased risk of ventricular dysrhythmias. This is what would be expected clinically at this temperature. The patient received intravenous lidocaine in the initial post-arrest period, but this is not known to affect QRS and QTc duration [33]. Furthermore, the QRS/QTc ratio is different from the patient with reported ECG before VF (rectal temperature of 25 °C) where the QRS/QTc was 0.26 [14]. In this case, initial rewarming strategies with warmed saline infusion, warmed humified oxygen and passive external rewarming were ineffective despite a perfusing rhythm. After 45 min, invasive rewarming with cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated. The ECG changed from sinus bradycardia to VF when the patient was moved to the operating room. Only repeated ECGs from the treatment period could have enabled us to assess whether changes, such as QRS/QTc-reduction, occurred prior to HCA in this case.

One interesting difference between the two HCA cases is a difference in degree of hypothermia (25 °C vs. 29.4 °C). However, it is unclear whether this is clinically significant. In the case when the ECG was reported at 25 °C before arrest [20], the QRS/QTc was above the suggested threshold of 0.20. This indicates that both repolarization and depolarization were prolonged to a similar degree and corresponds with experimental findings, where low temperatures were associated with higher QRS/QTc values and low risk for ventricular arrhythmias [12, 13]. To speculate; the relatively high QRS/QTc ratio in this case, where ECG-parameters were reported at 25 °C, 45 min prior to HCA, indicates that the patient at point of measurement had a theoretical reduced risk for pVT or VF relative to moderate hypothermia, but nevertheless arrested. This suggests that if it is present, the predictive value of QRS/QTc is valid at the moment it is measured. Utilizing this potential clinical tool necessitates continuous ECG-monitoring, and automated QRS/QTc calculation, during transport and rewarming for assessment of HCA-risk.

Future perspectives

One ECG-marker might not be sufficient to detect the risk of all hypothermia-induced arrhythmias. The repolarization disturbances seen during hypothermia are mainly thought to cause pVT and VF, which we hypothesize could be predicted by QRS/QTc. But prediction of asystole and PEA may require the development of other ECG-markers. In addition, different HCA rhythms may also be related to different stages of hypothermia. It has been suggested that the risk of pVT and VF is highest in moderate hypothermia (32–28 °C), as this corresponds to a lower threshold for inducing VF preclinically [13], whereas the risk of other HCAs increases with further decreases in body core temperature [3]. Finding ECG-parameters to detect risk of the different HCA-rhythms may thus be very helpful to optimize management of the hypothermic patient. We therefore encourage an increased focus on retrieving accurate ECG timings from accidental hypothermia patients in the future. Published case reports have recognized the potential value of QRS/QTc and hypothermia [34].

Conclusion

We plan to set up a website for anonymous reporting of QRS and QTc in hypothermic patients to enable us to definitively confirm or refute the value of QRS/QTc. This would also allow collection of sufficient data to investigate other potential prognostic ECG parameters in addition to QRS/QTc-ratio. If suitable parameters for predicting risk of HCA can be established, they could potentially help substantially improve management and survival of accidental hypothermia.
