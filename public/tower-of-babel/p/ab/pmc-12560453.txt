Patient acceptance, time efficiency, and inter-operator agreement in intra-oral scanning with fluorescence technology for caries detection
Authors: Anne-Marie Agius, Juliana No Cortes, Arthur R G Cortes, Nikolai J Attard, Gabriella Gatt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIntra-oral scanners (IOS) have been widely used to obtain digital impressions for treatment planning and to enable a full digital workflow on virtual patients. Much less is known about the use of IOS for caries detection. This study focuses on the use of the scanners for caries detection and investigates the number of images, time and patient experiences as well as differences between operators.MethodsParticipants were recruited if they had caries present, their teeth were cleaned using a prophy brush and water and then scanned in a standardised manner. Some participants were scanned a second time by another operator. The automated caries detection results as well as the number of images needed and time taken per jaw scanned was noted. Participants were also asked to complete a questionnaire regarding their experience during the scanning process.ResultsData sets (scanning time and number of images) from 78 participants were both found to be non-normal using the Shapiro Wilk test (p < 0.001). A Mann-Whitney U test revealed that both the time taken (U = 17772.500, z = -8.066, p < 0.001) as well as the number of images needed (U = 15252.000, z =-9.651, p < 0.0001) were significantly higher for the maxilla when compared to the mandible. Background

Intraoral scanners (IOS) were originally developed for taking digital impressions of teeth to enable digital treatment planning and for the design and fabrication of prosthodontic, orthodontic and other appliances. Recently, caries detection features have been included in the properties of some scanners, but research regarding their diagnostic performance is still lacking, especially for the primary dentition. These improvements in technology tie in well with the new global oral health action plan 2023–2030; with one of the six guiding principles being ‘optimising digital technologies for oral health’ [1].

This integrated caries-detection technology developed for intra-oral scanners is relatively new and there are still few studies specifically done with this type of scanner with most studies being in vitro. [2]

The IOS takes multiple images of the mouth per second, the software stitches them together and produces a 3D image. The caries detection feature of the software combines all the information gathered by the IOS in terms of both the visible light (the RGB Red, Green Blue based images) and the red and green fluorescence signals [3]. This technology offers several advantages over conventional caries detection methods such as: having an additional tool when visual detection of a carious lesion is uncertain; viewing the captured carious surfaces on a screen during and after the patient’s visit; saving the images and comparing them to images during subsequent patient visits; avoiding radiation associated with imaging techniques as well as having a basis for providing early preventive management and monitoring the results. There are also uses for digital caries detection in epidemiology and population surveys where information regarding caries prevalence and severity (along with other oral diseases) is collected and analysed. There is also scope for this technology in dental education and training [4]. Students can be taught to detect and diagnose caries from 3D images, study caries patterns as well as visualise and understand the results of preventive management by recognising the changes in caries lesions over time.

One of the factors that causes distress in both patients and operators, is gagging during dental treatment which is especially common when taking maxillary impressions [5]. IOS have been developed to facilitate impression taken for dental procedures and to provide a route into CAD/CAM (Computer Aided Design/Computer Aided Manufacturing). These devices are usually light and have small tips and their use is also being studied in terms of patient comfort and acceptance, as well as operator comfort and the associated learning curve.

Multiple studies compared conventional with digital impressions and most of them concluded that patients prefer digital over conventional impressions as they reported more acceptance and comfort and the process was found to be more time efficient [6–8]. Children also preferred a digital impression prior to orthodontic treatment and time efficiency was found to be comparable to [9] or faster than [10] taking alginate impressions. A study published in 2014 contradicted these findings and found that participants preferred conventional impressions because they were easier and quicker to do [11]. Another study found comparable patient acceptance and time efficiency between IOS and conventional impressions [12]. It can be argued that these findings also depend on the type of conventional impression material used (for e.g. alginate impressions set much faster than silicone impressions), the timing of the study (newer IOS technologies have improved accuracy and are easier to use) as well as operator preference and experience with both methods [13].

In this study, IOS are being used for their fluorescence-based caries detection feature and it would be interesting to see whether this device could potentially reduce the need for intra-oral radiographs and their associated ionising radiation and patient discomfort. Intra-oral radiographs can cause oral mucosa irritation and sometimes trigger the gag reflex in some patients [14]. Researchers have been trying to use different methods to reduce patient discomfort when taking intra-oral radiographs, especially in children, by changing the film holder [15] or the actual film/sensor [16]. In children, the conventional film seemed to be better tolerated than the sensor, however, no significant differences were found between wired sensors and conventional films in recent studies on adult patients [14, 17].

The literature also describes a learning curve when using IOS and studies show an improvement in scanning efficiency (decreased time and number of images taken per scan) with continued practice [18]. Experienced users get better scanning results and the learning curve can be different for different scanning devices some of which need less time to master sufficiently [19]. Different IOS and associated software programmes have seen improvements over time and they have been made more intuitive and easier to use. The software can guide the operator and highlight any areas on the scan with insufficient data in real time. The programmes allow for some minor discrepancies; however, the final scan sharpness depends on operator skill and dexterity. Different IOS also have different accuracy and trueness measures and short-span scanning yields better results than full arch scanning [20]. The scanning strategy (the path followed, the device movement and distance from scanned objects) was also found to have a profound impact on scanning accuracy and following the manufacturer’s recommended strategy yields significantly better results [21]. Little is known about the precision (i.e. intra-operator reliability and inter-operator reproducibility) of caries detection using fluorescence methods from intraoral scanners.

Methods

Study design

In this cross-sectional study, participants were recruited from the dental teaching clinic at random by an intermediary. Adults who were willing to participate signed an informed consent form. Any participant under the age of 18 needed consent from their legal guardians whilst separate assent forms were used for young children (4–10 years) and adolescents so that they could understand the procedure and purpose of the study. The methodology of this article forms part of a larger study [22] which compared the diagnostic performance of an Intra-Oral Scanner with Fluorescence Technology (IOSFT) namely Trios 4® (3Shape A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark), to gold-standard measures – clinical visual ICDAS categories [23] for occlusal and smooth surfaces and Bitewing radiographs for proximal surfaces for caries detection.

After initial consent and history taking, the participants’ teeth were cleaned using a prophy brush and water. All procedures were done under standardised light and ambient conditions for each participant to ensure maximum reproducibility.

Sample size

The sample size calculation for group comparisons were worked out independently for permanent and primary teeth. These were estimated following a pilot study using the Noether’s formula for dependent samples [24]. The calculation was based on differences in fluorescence outputs in caries detection with the effect size for primary teeth being greater than permanent teeth. This effect size was used considering a statistical power of 80% and a significance level of 5% for the two-sample Wilcoxon Mann-Whitney U-test. The P value hypothesis was also considered in accordance with a previous study [25] which also concluded that differences in fluorescence in primary dentition were larger. The minimum sample sizes required for permanent teeth were 91 teeth per group (with caries, no caries) and 35 per group for primary teeth. For inter-operator agreement measures, 6 participants were scanned a second time (12 dental arches and 480 premolar and molar tooth surfaces). Due to the fact that the operators had the same training in caries detection, a very high correlation was expected according to the Fischer’s Z Transformation test. The sample size required when a minimum significant correlation of 0.95 is considered, with a power of 80% at a significant level of 5%, was only 5 participants. The twelve dental arches were used as a unit for agreement measures of scanning duration and number of images, whilst 480 surfaces were analysed for automated caries detection agreement measures.

Intraoral scanning procedure and questionnaire used

The Trios 4® intra-oral scanner utilises a white and blue-violet light source (415 nm) and a long-pass filter placed in front of the scanner’s RGB (red, green and blue) sensor. Apart from the light source (RGB information) the scanner also detects red and green fluorescence. Since the scanner uses the visible light spectrum, there is no harm or risk for the participants. The software that supports the Trios4 scanner is 3Shape Unite®. The operating light of the dental chair was switched off during the scanning procedure, the mouth was dried using a 3-in-1 syringe and a lip and cheek retractor was used whenever possible. Scanning tips were calibrated whenever prompted by the software. Scanning was performed according to the recommended manufacturer’s instructions, starting with the mandible first and then proceeding with the maxilla and following the recommended scanning paths. Any missing spaces (blank patches on the 3D model) were rescanned until the 3D image looked intact. The number of images and the time taken to scan each arch was recorded on the participants’ data collection sheet. Primary molars as well as permanent molars and premolars were included in this study.

Six participants that contributed 480 tooth surfaces for analysis, were recruited randomly and consecutively by the intermediary during one particular session then underwent a second scanning procedure by a second operator (JNC) on the same dental chair and in the same room under standardised lighting conditions. The operators received the same theoretical and practical training in scanning at the manufacturing company headquarters, they received additional formal online training by the company education manager. After the training and prior to the study, the operators scanned a number of patients to become more proficient at scanning for caries detection. This accounted for the minimum scans required to achieve a plateau in the learning curve for both operators [19].

The final 3D scan was then analysed using the automated caries detection view on the software. A colour map of the teeth is automatically created and superimposed on the digital scan. A yellow colour on the teeth signifies initial caries, whilst a red colour signifies moderate/extensive caries. A normal tooth colour on the map indicates the absence of caries. These colours on the surfaces were analysed and compared for agreement measures between the two operators.

After completion of the clinical procedures, participants were asked to complete an online anonymous questionnaire in Maltese or English. A link to a questionnaire hosted by Google Forms was sent to the participants. The questions were about their actual experience with the intra-oral scanner on the day, whether they had previous experience with a conventional and/or digital impression, and how their experience on the day compares to their previous experiences, if any. They were also asked to report their satisfaction with the scanning procedure in different areas in their mouth on a Likert scale with options from 1 to 5 with 1 being ‘very uncomfortable’ and 5 being ‘very comfortable’.

To make it easier for the participants to understand and remember the different areas in the mouth, posed photos of the researcher using the scanner in different areas of the mouth were taken and used in the questionnaire for each question. Participants completed and submitted the questionnaire online and the data was analysed anonymously. Younger children who couldn’t complete the questionnaire alone did so with a guardian who guided them through the process also using the photos in the questionnaire to better understand the questions. A reminder was sent to all the participants a few days before the end of the data collection phase.

Statistical analysis

The data was analysed and organised to present descriptive statistics and to create graphs and charts. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test for the normality of the data obtained and it was found to be non-normal (p < 0.001), therefore non-parametric tests were used. All tests were performed using the same statistical software program (SPSS Statistics 26, SPSS, Inc) at a level of significance of 5%.

Results

A total of 78 participants were included in this study with the following age distributions: 4–10 years (24 participants), 11–18 years (12 participants), 19–34 years (24 participants), 35–54 years (12 participants), 55–64 (2 participants) and over 65 years (4 participants).

Out of a total of 4283 total permanent surfaces 970 total primary surfaces that were scanned and analysed, there were 487 decayed permanent surfaces and 223 decayed primary surfaces.

Scanning time and number of images

Both data sets (scanning time and number of images) were tested for normality using the Shapiro Wilk test and both sets were non-normal with the same significant p value (< 0.001).

A Mann-Whitney U test revealed a significant difference in the timing between maxillary scans and mandibular scans U = 17772.500, z = −8.066, p < 0.001. Another significant result when comparing the number of images needed per scan between the maxilla and the mandible: U = 15252.000, z =−9.651, p < 0.0001. A Mann-Whitney U test revealed a significant difference in the timing (U = 102718.500, z = 14.505, p < 0.0001) and number of images (U = 108395.500, z = 16.457, p < 0.0001) between scanning arches consisting mainly of permanent teeth and those consisting of primary teeth. Dental arches with primary teeth needed less time and images to scan.

A Spearman correlation test was used to investigate the relationship between the total scanning time and the participants’ age (r = 0.611, n = 4698, p < 0.0001) as well as the total number of images and participants’ age (r = 0.669, n = 4698, p < 0.0001). There was a significant strong direct correlation between the variables. In both cases, more time and images were needed to scan older participants.

A Kruskal-Wallis test found no significant differences in responses regarding ‘overall comfort during the procedure’, ‘experience with the digital impression’, comfort when scanning different areas of the mouth and ‘duration of the scanning procedure’ between different genders and different age groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Some interesting results were achieved in this study. The scanning duration and the number of images are automatically saved by the software programme for each arch scanned for a few days and this ensures an accurate representation and standardisation of the way this data was collected. The number of images in particular is used by the manufacturer to prompt the user when too many images are being taken as this reduces the accuracy of the stitching process. In addition, heavier files might also slow down the computer and the running of the software programme. Scanning the same areas multiple times results in inaccuracies in the final scan. It was therefore important that the operators were both trained to scan according to recommended strategies prior to the data collection phase of this study. Satisfactory scans with a lower number of images are preferable, but this requires a learning curve [18].

Significantly more time and images are needed when scanning older patients, permanent teeth and the maxilla when compared to their counterparts. Some possible explanations for this are that older patients in this study tended to have missing teeth and/or highly restored dentitions. During the scanning procedure it was noticed that it is sometimes difficult/impossible to fully capture proximal surfaces adjacent to missing teeth and the procedure was also much slower when scanning restorations. It is also possible that scanning primary molars is faster since they are smaller than permanent molars, the mouth is also smaller and there are less teeth and therefore the same size scanner tip can capture more information with one sweep over primary teeth.

With regards to increased scanning time and images for the maxilla, this can be explained by several factors, such as: operator positioning (the mandible can be scanned using direct vision and more comfortably), molar positioning and more importantly difficulty accessing the buccal and distal surfaces of the posterior molars due to the restricted space within the buccal sulcus and the scanner tip is blocked by the ramus of the mandible. Patient discomfort when scanning the distal parts of the upper molars also makes the procedure more challenging for the operators.

Some participants were re-scanned by a second operator. The minimum, maximum and median values of scanning time and number of images captured per arch were all lower for the main operator (Table 2), however these operator differences were not found to be statistically significant. When exploring the agreement between automated caries detection results of the scans of the two operators for the same arch, a substantial agreement was found. This is probably a result of the software prompting the operators when there are areas which are insufficiently scanned. This ensures that a satisfactory scan is achieved every time. An interesting result is that operators with similar skills get very similar caries detection results.

As is the case for any dental or medical procedure, patient experiences and reported outcomes will determine whether that procedure will be successful or not. The more comfortable and knowledgeable patients are with a procedure (and their practitioner), the better their experience will be and the likelihood that they would return is higher [26].

The questionnaire was anonymous so as to give the participants the ability to respond truthfully regarding their experiences. The disadvantage in this case was that the patient reported outcomes could not be paired to their data gathered clinically regarding actual scanning duration, time of day, operator experience and disease prevalence.

Overall, most participants who had experienced a conventional impression reported that the experience was good, however the results for the digital impression were actually better in comparison [27]. Most participants reported that the procedure was comfortable overall (with one person reporting discomfort during scanning and that the experience was bad). During a pilot study, the main researcher observed that a number of participants were feeling discomfort in similar areas of the mouth during scanning, so it was decided to investigate this further in the questionnaire by asking participants about comfort/discomfort in specific areas not just the overall experience.

Interesting results were achieved upon analysis of the data and participants reported the biggest discomfort when scanning the distal surfaces of the upper molars. The confined space in that part of the mouth as well as the size of the scanning tip made it a bit more difficult to access the space, especially in participants with crowded teeth and limited mouth opening. The scanner tip hits the oral mucosa over the anterior ramus of the mandible and it cannot be manipulated freely. Contrastingly, patients reported the buccal surface of the lower molars as being the least uncomfortable. The buccal oral mucosa is more flexible and can stretch to an extent without inflicting any pain. The operator also has direct vision of the area being scanned which makes it easier and faster. The differences between the comfort/discomfort during scanning the different areas of the mouth were significantly different. This also ties in well with the fact that it takes longer to scan the maxilla when compared to the mandible.

Most participants also rated the scanning duration favourably, which indicates a high tolerance of the procedure, keeping in mind that the scanning duration was much longer than that for a normal digital impression. For caries detection, the 3Shape software prompts the user to scan certain areas in more detail using the ‘blue overlay’ feature to ensure that enough information is captured for correct functioning of the automated caries detection feature. This makes the scanning time longer than that needed for a ‘status scan’ or a normal digital impression. Patients who choose to undergo treatment at university teaching clinics may also be more compliant and accepting of procedures of longer duration, so this could be one of the reasons why most participants answered favourably regarding scanning duration.

A study carried out in 2012 compared BWs with laser fluorescence and visual examination for caries detection in children and children reported less discomfort with visual examination alone [28]. It was also found that discomfort in children resulted in higher numbers of false positives or false negatives when detecting approximal caries [29]. Having high acceptance of this method of caries detection is therefore important for patient compliance and is especially important for high-risk patients who need repeated radiographs. Intra-oral scans can be taken as many times as necessary with adequate patient acceptance and no risks of ionising radiation. Additionally, sometimes, a clinician could opt for just scanning an area of concern, rather than the entire arch each time.

These results of positive patient experiences with IOS are in agreement with many studies discussed in the literature review and most patients [30], including children [31], favour digital over conventional impressions. It was also reported that patient and operator experience also varied with the type of IOS used and faster operator learning and better patient outcomes were achieved for the TRIOS scanners when compared to other brands [32].

Some limitations of this study are that the participants and/or the researcher could not reasonably compare the time taken for a conventional impression with a digital impression. With regards to the questionnaire, there could have been some bias due to ‘adult-guided’ responses as very young children needed the help of a guardian to complete the questionnaire. This study only compared automated caries detection results of similarly trained and skilled operators. It would be of great interest to explore the software’s robustness and reliability when the same patients are scanned by operators with different levels of training and scanning experience. Future studies with long-term data could be conducted to be able to work multivariate analysis and account for any errors due to confounding variables.

Conclusion

Scanning primary teeth and younger patients requires less time and number of images per arch. Similarly skilled operators can get similar automated caries detection results, showing that the IOSFT hardware and software results are reproducible and objective.

Most participants reported a positive experience with the scanning procedure. The distal part of the upper molars was the most uncomfortable area for participants during scanning and although scanning for caries detection is longer than taking a digital impression, most participants found the duration acceptable or fast. Patient satisfaction and compliance with the procedure is also beneficial as with continued improvement of the IOSFT technology, it can be considered for caries detection and monitoring.
