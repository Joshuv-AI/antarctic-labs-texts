Comparison of direct oral anticoagulants and vitamin K antagonists in the treatment of left ventricular thrombi: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hossam Elbenawi, Kirsten Lipps, Samuel Heller, David A Liedl, Raymond C Shields, Ana I Casanegra, Stanislav Henkin, Thom W Rooke, Paul W Wennberg, Waldemar E Wysokinski, Robert D McBane, Damon E Houghton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLeft ventricular thrombus (LVT) is a serious thromboembolic complication in patients with cardiomyopathies. Evidence supporting direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) therapy in LVT is limited.ObjectivesWe evaluated the effectiveness and safety of DOACs compared with vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in patients with LVT.MethodsA multisite retrospective cohort study was performed, including patients with LVT diagnosed by echocardiography from 2018-2023 who were treated with either DOACs or VKAs. Primary outcome was the incidence of stroke and systemic embolism (SSE). Secondary outcomes included LVT resolution, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality.ResultsA total of 182 patients with LVT met inclusion criteria, of which 70 (38.5%) were treated with only DOACs and 112 (61.5%) with only VKAs. The mean age was 66 years, and 74% of the patients were male. The median follow-up duration was 363.5 days (IQR, 139-950). No significant difference between the groups was observed in the incidence of SSE (DOAC, 5.7% vs VKA, 5.4%; P = 1.0), the incidence of LVT resolution (DOAC: 81.4% vs VKA: 87%; P = .35), the median time to LVT resolution (DOAC, 96 days vs VKA, 102 days; P = .22), or mortality (DOAC, 14.3% vs VKA, 11.6%; P = .6). However, VKAs were associated with a significantly higher incidence of major bleeding compared with DOACs (9% vs 1.4%; P = .02).ConclusionLVT treated solely with either DOACs or VKAs had similar incidences of SSE, LVT resolution, time to LVT resolution, and mortality. VKA therapy, however, was associated with a higher incidence of major bleeding events.Keywords: direct oral anti-coagulant, left ventricular thrombus, stroke, systemic embolism, vitamin K antagonists

Introduction

Left ventricular thrombus (LVT) is a serious thromboembolic complication that can arise in both ischemic and nonischemic cardiomyopathies. LVT formation is influenced by Virchow’s triad, which encompasses myocardial injury associated with cardiomyopathy, blood stasis resulting from left ventricular dysfunction, and a state of hypercoagulability.

In recent years, the incidence of LVT has markedly declined, particularly in patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI), primarily due to the introduction of reperfusion therapies. Before routine use of reperfusion therapy, the incidence of LVT following AMI approached 60% [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. In the modern era, approximately 1 million cases of AMI occur annually in the United States [5]. LVT, though likely underdiagnosed, is estimated to complicate 5.6% to 12.7% of AMI cases [6], which is roughly 100,000 new cases of LVT each year. Despite a substantial reduction in LVT incidence, embolic events occur in up to 4% of patients with LVT [7], underscoring the need for effective anticoagulation therapy.

Previous studies have demonstrated the overall safety and effectiveness of vitamin K antagonist (VKA) therapy in treating LVT [8]. However, VKAs require regular monitoring, have a slow onset of action, carry a higher risk of bleeding, and are more susceptible to various dietary and drug interactions. These limitations have fostered a growing interest in the off-label use of direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) therapy as an alternative treatment option [9,10].

Although the efficacy and safety of DOACs as an alternative to VKAs in patients with LVT have been evaluated in multiple small randomized clinical trials [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]], these trials have been too small to provide definitive answers (total DOAC-treated patients, n = 125), in part due to limited follow-up and incomplete reporting of clinically relevant outcomes. This study aimed to add to the literature the largest cohort to date of DOAC-treated patients compared with VKAs for the treatment of LVT.

Methods

Patient population

We evaluated the anticoagulation treatment and clinical outcomes of adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with LVT (LVTs were identified using echocardiography, with the majority of LVTs diagnosed using noncontrast 2-dimensional echocardiography) who received care at one of the Mayo Clinic Enterprise sites, which includes 3 academic medical centers and associated satellite campuses in 5 different states across the United States. Patients who had an echocardiogram between January 1, 2018, and July 31, 2023, were screened for inclusion. A natural language processing (NLP) algorithm [16] was developed to facilitate chart review and screen echocardiogram reports for the presence of LVT. An initial derivation cohort of 100 patients was used to design and modify the NLP algorithm, which was then evaluated in a validation cohort of 300 unique echocardiogram reports that had been previously classified by manual review. The NLP algorithm demonstrated 85% sensitivity and 100% specificity in identifying LVT in echocardiogram reports of the validation groups. From the cohort of patients identified by NLP, echocardiogram reports were manually reviewed to confirm LVT.

The cohort was further evaluated to identify patients who had at least 1 follow-up echocardiogram performed ≥30 days after the index study. The echocardiography reports were then reviewed manually to confirm the presence of LVT. Finally, the electronic medical records (EMRs) of all patients with confirmed LVT were screened for inclusion in the final cohort. Patients were included if they were started on anticoagulation with a VKA (ie, warfarin) or DOAC (ie, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, or edoxaban) ≤30 days after the index echocardiogram. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with an LVT initially identified by echocardiogram before January 1, 2018; patients on chronic anticoagulation therapy at the time of LVT diagnosis; patients who received >1 type of oral anticoagulation therapy during follow-up (ie, switched between DOACs and VKAs, not due to the primary outcome), and cases involving surgical thrombectomy for LVT. The study protocol was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board.

Data collection

Baseline demographic and clinical data were abstracted from the EMR. Data included body mass index, cardiac history (including myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy, and arrhythmias), other comorbidities, renal function, hematologic parameters (ie, erythrocyte count and platelet count), history of presenting embolism (defined as stroke and systemic embolism [SSE] occurring ≤30 days before the index echocardiogram), and hypercoagulability risk factors (history of venous thromboembolism, history of arterial thromboembolism, and antiphospholipid syndrome). The use of antiplatelet therapy at baseline was also noted. The Charlson Comorbidity Index and CHA2DS2-VASC score (regardless of atrial fibrillation status) were calculated using clinical chart review and International Classification of Diseases 9/10 codes at the time of LVT for use in multivariable models to address possible confounding. Echocardiographic data were abstracted from the reports in the EMR and included left ventricular (LV) size and function, including LV ejection fraction, the presence of wall motion, and LV aneurysm. An in-depth review of the EMR was conducted to confirm LVT diagnosis and to obtain data regarding the type, timing, and duration of anticoagulation initiated after the LVT diagnosis until death or the last available follow-up. In cases where anticoagulation therapies were discontinued, the timing of discontinuation was recorded. The use of concomitant antiplatelet therapy after LVT diagnosis was also recorded.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of interest was the incidence of SSE during the follow-up period while maintaining oral anticoagulation therapy. For the primary outcome (SSE), if a patient developed >1 event during the follow-up period, only the first event was considered. Key secondary outcomes included (1) incidence of LVT resolution, (2) time to LVT resolution, (3) all-cause mortality, and (4) incidence of major and nonmajor bleeding events. Events occurring while a patient was receiving a parenteral agent as a bridge to VKAs were attributed to VKAs. Major bleeding and clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding events were defined according to the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis criteria [17]. Major bleeding events were defined as fatal bleeding, symptomatic bleeding in critical areas or organs (including intracranial, intraspinal, intraocular, retroperitoneal, intraarticular, pericardial, or intramuscular leading to compartment syndrome), a decrease in hemoglobin concentration > 2 g/dL, and/or the transfusion of >2 units of blood products at any time during the follow-up period. Nonmajor events were defined as any bleeding that did not meet the criteria for major bleeding (clinically relevant nonmajor and minor bleeding) [17]. Patients were monitored until death or the date of their last clinical encounter in the EMR, whichever occurred first. For patients who underwent a heart transplant, the date of the last follow-up was the date of the transplant. All outcomes were confirmed by a manual review of the EMR.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median and IQR, while categorical variables are shown as counts and percentages. To compare continuous variables, either the Student 's t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. For comparison of categorical variables between groups, the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was applied as appropriate. Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, and comparisons were made using log-rank test. A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP v17.2.

Results

Patient population

Between 2018 and 2023, 182 patients with confirmed LVT on echocardiogram were identified. Of these, 70 (38.4%) were treated exclusively with DOACs, with 64 (35.2%) treated with apixaban and 6 (3.2%) with rivaroxaban. VKA monotherapy was used in 112 (61.5%) patients. All VKA patients had a target international normalized ratio value between 2 and 3 during treatment. Of the 70 patients treated with DOACs, 59 patients (84.2%) received a maintenance dose of apixaban 5 mg twice daily, and 5 patients (7.1%) were prescribed a reduced dose of 2.5 mg twice daily. For those who received a loading dose (n = 18), apixaban was administered at 10 mg twice daily for the first 7 days of the treatment course, followed by 5 mg daily as a maintenance dose throughout the entire treatment course. For the 6 patients (8.5%) treated with rivaroxaban, a standard dose of 20 mg once daily was used; none of these patients received a loading dose.

Baseline characteristics

The median follow-up period was 363.5 days (IQR, 139-950). The baseline demographic, clinical, and echocardiographic data are presented in Tables 1 and 2 and Supplementary Table S1. Baseline characteristics were similar between the 2 groups; however, the DOAC group had a higher proportion of male patients (86% vs 67%; P = .005) compared with the VKA group. In contrast, the VKA group had a higher proportion of White patients (96% vs 84%; P = .005).

Outcomes

SSE

A total of 16.6% of patients experienced SSE, with 30 embolic events reported overall. Of these, 19 occurred as presenting embolisms at the time of LVT diagnosis, while 10 events occurred during the follow-up period (primary outcome), 4 with DOAC, and 6 with VKA (Supplementary Table S2). The incidence of SSE did not differ significantly between the treatment groups at 6 months (DOAC, 4.3% vs VKA, 1.8%; P = .37) or during long-term follow-up (DOAC, 5.7% vs VKA, 5.4%; P = 1.0; Table 3 and Figure 1A, B).

In the VKA group, 4 out of 6 SSE events were associated with subtherapeutic international normalized ratio values. Of these, 2 occurred during initiation of therapy while bridging with intravenous heparin (Supplementary Table S3). In the DOAC group, a total of 4 events occurred; 3 events were observed in the nonloaded group on days 10, 22, and 50 of therapy, while 1 event occurred in the loaded group on day 403 of therapy after LVT resolution.

LVT resolution

The incidence of LVT resolution at 6 months and during long-term follow-up was not significantly different between the treatment groups (Table 3 and Figure 2A). At 6 months, LVT resolution was observed in 61% of patients treated with DOACs vs 61% treated with VKAs (P = 1.0). In long-term follow-up, LVT resolution was observed in 81.4% of DOAC-treated patients vs 87% of VKA-treated patients (P = .35). The median time to LVT resolution was also similar between groups (DOAC, 96 days [IQR, 67-180] and VKA, 102 days [IQR, 68-199]; P = .22; Table 3).

Mortality

The mortality rate at 6 months and during long-term follow-up did not differ significantly between the treatment groups. Mortality at 6 months was 4.3% in patients treated with DOACs and 3.6% in those treated with VKAs (P = .8). Long-term mortality was 14.3% in patients treated with DOACs and 11.6% in those treated with VKAs (P = .6; Table 3).

Bleeding

Major bleeding events were numerically higher (trend toward statistical significance) in patients treated with VKAs compared with DOACs within the first 6 months of therapy (VKA: 3.5% vs DOAC 0%; P = .05; Table 3 and Figure 1C). However, in long-term follow-up, the incidence of major bleeding was significantly higher in those treated with VKAs compared with DOACs (9% vs 1.4%; P = .02; Table 3 and Figure 1D). No significant difference was observed in the incidence of nonmajor bleeding events between the DOAC and VKA groups, both within 6 months of LVT diagnosis and during long-term follow-up (Table 3).

DOAC vs VKA in ischemic and nonischemic cardiomyopathy

Among patients specifically with ischemic cardiomyopathy (n = 117), there was no statistically significant difference between the 2 groups in terms of SSE rate (DOAC, 2% vs VKA, 6%; P = .27), LVT resolution (DOAC, 78.4% vs VKA, 86.3%; P = .26), or median time to resolution (DOAC, 100 days [IQR, 74-180] vs VKA, 129 days [IQR, 70-286]; P = .25). In patients with nonischemic cardiomyopathy (n = 50), the incidence of SSE was numerically higher with DOACs, but the difference was not statistically significant (DOAC, 17% vs VKA, 6%; P = .32). The LVT resolution rate also showed no significant difference between patients treated with DOACs and those receiving VKAs (94% vs 90%; P = .68). Furthermore, the time to LVT resolution was comparable between the 2 groups (DOAC, 84 days [IQR, 49-190] vs VKA, 99 days [IQR, 71-169]; P = .75).

Multivariable analyses

To evaluate the influence of confounding on the risk of SSE in patients receiving VKAs compared with those receiving DOACs, we used composite risk scores in logistic regression models to account for multiple variables, given the restrictions on number of variables that could be included due to the small number of events. The CHA2DS2-VASC score model demonstrated no significant difference in risk, with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.25-3.77). Similarly, concurrent dual antiplatelet therapy (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 0.28-4.20), ischemic cardiomyopathy (OR, 0.84; 95% CI, 0.22-3.46), and the Charlson Comorbidity Index score (OR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.26-3.95), when evaluated in separate models, also showed no significant difference in risk of SSE between patients receiving VKAs and those receiving DOACs (Table 5).

Discussion

VKAs have historically been the preferred anticoagulation therapy for patients with LVT [8]. The widespread adoption of DOAC therapy for other thromboembolic conditions, such as atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism [18,19], and its ease of use have raised interest in its potential use in LVT treatment. Our analysis found no significant differences in the incidence of SSE between patients treated with DOACs and those receiving VKA therapy, both in the short term (6 months) and over the entire follow-up period. Similarly, the 2 groups showed no significant differences regarding the incidence of or time to LVT resolution or all-cause mortality. However, VKAs were associated with a significantly higher incidence of major bleeding events during follow-up.

Our findings, along with those from smaller studies, suggest that DOACs are associated with a similar risk of SSE compared with VKAs [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. For example, in a study by Willeford et al. [23], which included 151 patients, the SSE rate was 6.2% for VKAs and 0% for DOACs, P = .37. Similarly, Jones et al. [22] reported that in a cohort of 101 patients, SSE rates were 5% for VKAs and 2.4% for DOACs, P = .39. In contrast, the Retrospective Evaluation of DOACs and Vascular Endpoints of Left Ventricular Thrombi (RED VELVT) study reported a higher SSE risk among patients using DOACs, with an SSE rate of 9.1% for DOACs and 4.6% for VKAs [26]. Our study found no significant difference in the incidence of SSE between patients treated with DOACs and those treated with VKAs (5.7% vs 5.4%; P = 1.0). The discrepancy could be explained by differences in study design and patient characteristics. In our analysis, the incidence of SSE remained consistent between groups both within and after the first 6 months of follow-up. Conversely, while the RED VELVET study observed a similar incidence of SSE between groups during the first 6 months, the curves between the groups diverged during the longer-term follow-up, and some patients developed SSE events after LVT resolution. Additionally, the prevalence of SSE and the rate of presenting an embolism in our study were significantly lower, suggesting that these events may have been unrelated to LVT or may be harbingers that persist despite LVT resolution. Indeed, there was a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation and prior SSE in this cohort, which supports this theory and increases the risk of late SSE events. Moreover, the methodological differences between the studies likely contributed to the differing results. The RED VELVET study attributed SSE events during VKA bridging to the parenteral anticoagulants, relied partially on patient-reported outcomes, and lacked clear documentation of dosing strategies. Additionally, patients who switched between DOACs and VKAs were added to the analysis and classified into cohorts based on their medication at the time of the event, which influenced the Kaplan–Meier analyses.

In contrast, our study included all SSE events following the initiation of either DOAC or VKA therapy. For patients who were bridged with parenteral therapy at the time of initiation of VKA, any SSE that occurred was attributed to the oral anticoagulation therapy. This “intention-to-treat-like” analysis eliminates the potential for both outcome bias and immortal time bias, which would otherwise allow early events to be attributed to DOACs but not to patients receiving VKAs who also received a parenteral bridge. We also exclusively analyzed patients who consistently remained on the same anticoagulation therapy throughout the entire follow-up period, censoring patients when anticoagulation was discontinued (unless an outcome occurred prior to this time point). Outcome data were derived from an in-depth review of the EMR, ensuring greater medical reliability compared with self-reports.

Apixaban and rivaroxaban are approved by the Federal Drug Administration to treat acute venous thromboembolism with upfront higher “loading” doses, which are not required for other indications, such as atrial fibrillation. Loading doses of anticoagulation therapy in cases of thrombotic events aim to achieve higher levels of anticoagulant more rapidly upfront, which could theoretically reduce the risk of embolic events, especially in the acute phase. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the influence of a loading dose of DOAC therapy in cases of LVT. In our study, 50% of SSE events (5 out of 10) happened within the first 8 weeks after LVT diagnosis. This highlights the elevated risk of SSE events during the early weeks following LVT diagnosis [25,[27], [28], [29]]. All early events (within the first 6 months) in patients treated with DOACs occurred in those who did not receive a loading dose. In this study, most patients with LVT who were treated with DOACs did not receive a loading dose, which we believe indicates a potential opportunity for further research, suggesting that a loading dose early in the treatment course may be a more appropriate (and more consistent with Federal Drug Administration labeling for acute thrombosis) strategy to reduce SSE in patients with LVT.

Our study also revealed no significant difference in the incidence of LVT resolution between patients treated with DOACs compared with those treated with VKAs, both within 6 months of LVT diagnosis and during the entire follow-up period. LVT resolution occurred in 61.4% of patients on DOACs and in 61% of those on VKAs within the first 6 months of LVT diagnosis. This increased to 81.4% for DOACs and 87% for VKAs during the remainder of the follow-up period. Comparable incidences of LVT resolution have been reported in previous studies with a smaller population, with no significant differences observed between DOACs and VKAs [20,21,24,[30], [31], [32]]. Iqbal et al. [20] reported thrombus resolution rates of 76% for VKAs and 65% for DOACs in a study of 84 patients with LVT. Similarly, Daher et al. [21] reported thrombus resolution rates of 70.6% for VKAs and 71.5% for DOACs in a cohort of 59 patients. Furthermore, our analysis revealed no significant difference in the median time to LVT resolution between patients treated with DOACs and those treated with VKAs (DOAC: 96 days vs VKA: 102 days). This finding aligns with the study by Seiler et al. [25], who reported comparable times to LVT resolution in both the VKA and DOAC groups (both 88 days) in a cohort of 101 patients [25].

The optimal length of anticoagulation therapy for patients with LVT remains controversial. The 2022 American Heart Association guidelines recommend treating patients with LVT with either a VKA or DOAC for a minimum of 3 to 6 months, with the possibility of extended treatment if necessary [33]. In our analysis, 20% of patients on DOACs required longer than 6 months for LVT resolution compared with 26% of those on VKAs (Figure 2B). Despite anticoagulation therapy, persistent LVT was observed in 19.6% of the DOAC group and 13% of the VKA group (Figure 2B). These findings suggest that a subset of LVT patients may need prolonged therapy (>6 months), underscoring the importance of tailored treatment strategies based on individual patient clinical characteristics.

It has been observed that DOACs (apixaban) are safer than VKAs in reducing the risk of bleeding, particularly major bleeding events, in patients with atrial fibrillation [34], though very limited data are available on their effect on bleeding risk when used for LVT. Our analysis showed that there was no difference between DOACs and VKAs related to nonmajor bleeding events. However, DOACs were associated with fewer major bleeding events compared with VKAs (1.4% vs 9%; P < .02). This aligns with a prior study by Jones et al. [22], who observed lower rates of major bleeding in patients with LVT treated with DOACs compared with those treated with VKAs (0% vs 6.7%; P = .03). One possible explanation for our finding is that concurrent dual antiplatelet therapy use was more prevalent in the VKA group (27% vs 13%), which could contribute to the increased bleeding risk observed in this group.

The current study has several limitations, including a small sample size, low number of events, nonrandomized comparisons, its retrospective design, and, particularly, the potential for unmeasured confounders to explain the differences in SSE between patients treated with DOACs vs VKAs. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in the majority of baseline characteristics. Additionally, there was no centralized review of echocardiographic images; instead, we relied on the clinical reports of each echocardiogram and local measurements of the 2-dimensional thrombus area. Lastly, data on the time in the therapeutic range for VKA therapy were not available.

In summary, our retrospective multisite study showed that patients with LVT treated with either DOACs or VKAs had similar rates of SSE, LVT resolution, and mortality, as well as a similar time to LVT resolution. Some patients may require >6 months of oral anticoagulation therapy, and in some cases, LVT may persist despite prolonged treatment. VKA treatment was associated with a higher incidence of major bleeding events when looking beyond the initial 6 months, and for patients requiring extended anticoagulation, a DOAC may be a safer option. The risk of LVT embolization is highest during the first 8 weeks after diagnosis, and a DOAC loading dose may represent a promising strategy to mitigate this risk.
