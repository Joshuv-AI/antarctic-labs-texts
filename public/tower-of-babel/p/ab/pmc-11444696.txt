Invasive ductal carcinoma of the nipple in a man
Authors: Chia-Lun Li, Chen-Yi Wu
Published: 2024

A 45-year-old man was referred to our dermatology clinic for a 6-month history of an indurated plaque on his right nipple. Treatment with a topical steroid had been unsuccessful, and the lesion was increasing in size and was mildly itchy. The patient had no known family history of malignancy or any known risk factors for breast cancer. On examination, we observed a red-brownish plaque on his right nipple (Figure 1A). We performed a skin biopsy, which showed invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast (Figure 1B), positive for estrogen receptor and progesterone receptor, and negative for human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2). Mammography showed an irregular nodule in the subareolar region of the right breast (BI-RADS category 6), with a 2.5-cm mass in the right nipple region and several enlarged lymph nodes at the right axilla on ultrasound, and no suspicious distant lesions on computed tomography scan of the chest. The patient declined genetic testing. He started oral tamoxifen and underwent a modified radical mastectomy. Final staging was T2N1aM0 with 3/30 positive lymph nodes. He received postoperative radiation and chemotherapy with doxorubicin, cyclophosphamide, and paclitaxel. He was free of recurrence 9 months postoperatively.

(A) A red-brownish, pebble stone–like, hard plaque with multiple overlying papules and nodules on the right nipple of a 45-year-old man with invasive ductal carcinoma, causing a distortion of the contour of the nipple–areola complex. (B) Tumour nests infiltrating the upper dermis with tubule formation, foamy cytoplasm, nuclear pleomorphism, prominent nucleoli, and scant mitotic activity (original magnification × 400).

About 0.5%–1.0% of cases of breast cancer occur in males, with an average lifetime risk of about 1 in 726.1,2 Patients often present with a palpable lump, which may involve the nipple and cause distortion, retraction, or bloody discharge. Risk factors include obesity, Klinefelter syndrome, cryptorchidism, orchitis, gynecomastia, cirrhosis, familial history of breast or ovarian cancer, and genetic mutations (BRCA1, BRCA2, PTEN, p53, and CHEK2).2 The differential diagnosis should include Paget disease of the nipple, adenoma, hyperkeratosis of the nipple and areola, and eczema. Paget disease may show skin thickening and enhancement of the nipple–areolar complex on imaging, as opposed to a distinct mass. Regardless of sex, patients with a long-standing or concerning plaque over the nipple should undergo breast imaging studies and biopsy.
