The microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA): a novel paradigm for overcoming radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) and potentiating the efficacy of tumor immuno-combination therapy
Authors: Yilin Chen, Shuaixuan Chen, Hang Zhang, Binbin Song, Yufen Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCombination therapy involving radiotherapy and immune checkpoint inhibitors has become a cornerstone of modern oncology; however, its therapeutic efficacy is frequently compromised by radiation-induced lymphopenia, a phenomenon traditionally viewed as an unavoidable physical consequence of radiation exposure. Accumulating evidence suggests that RIL also reflects broader host-dependent biological dysregulation, extending beyond direct lymphocyte depletion. In this review, we introduce the microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis as a conceptual and integrative framework to recontextualize RIL as a biologically modulated state influenced by the gut microenvironment. We synthesize current mechanistic and clinical evidence indicating that microbial-derived signals may shape immune competence during radiotherapy by influencing hematopoietic lineage commitment within the bone marrow, cellular stress tolerance mechanisms such as DNA damage responses and mitochondrial quality control, as well as systemic neuroendocrine stress pathways. This framework supports a shift toward a dual-modulatory strategy that integrates lymphocyte-sparing radiotherapy approaches (e.g., stereotactic body radiotherapy) with microbiota-targeted interventions (e.g., postbiotics). Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) is a cornerstone modality in oncology, widely applied across multiple solid tumors and hematologic malignancies [1–3]. However, radiation-induced lymphopenia represents a critical yet unresolved challenge in translational oncology, as it directly constrains the therapeutic efficacy of radiotherapy and emerging radio-immunotherapy strategies. Fundamentally, RT relies on the precise spatial delivery of ionizing radiation (IR) to ablate tumor cells, inducing apoptosis or irreversible proliferative arrest. While traditionally viewed as a quintessential local control strategy, the advent of immuno-oncology has precipitated a paradigm shift in our understanding of RT. It is now appreciated that RT possesses significant immunomodulatory potential beyond mere local cytotoxicity. It can modulate various facets of the immune system to mediate systemic anti-tumor immunity [4].

The most emblematic manifestation of this potential is the “abscopal effect,” wherein local irradiation of a tumor induces the regression or eradication of non-irradiated, distant metastatic lesions [5–7]. This phenomenon suggests that RT can liberate tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) [8, 9], and facilitate antigen presentation and T-cell clonal expansion, thereby igniting a systemic anti-tumor response [10]. These mechanistic insights provide a robust theoretical rationale for combining RT with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), effectively transforming RT from a solitary local “killer” into a systemic immune “primer” or “activator” [11, 12]. However, this phenomenon remains relatively rare and its clinical reproducibility is still elusive, with most evidence derived from case reports [13, 14] and limited clinical observations [15].

The RT-ICI combination strategy has achieved notable clinical milestones. The landmark PACIFIC Phase III trial demonstrated that consolidation durvalumab after concurrent chemoradiotherapy significantly prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in unresectable stage III NSCLC [16]. Similarly, the GEMSTONE-301 trial showed that consolidation therapy with sugemalimab significantly improved progression-free survival in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC without disease progression after sequential or concurrent chemoradiotherapy [17]. In the neoadjuvant setting, a randomized Phase II trial by Altorki et al. revealed that neoadjuvant durvalumab combined with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) yielded a higher major pathological response (MPR) rate compared to durvalumab monotherapy in resectable early-stage NSCLC [18]. However, such pathological improvements may partly reflect the direct cytotoxic and tumor-debulking effects of SBRT rather than purely systemic immunologic sensitization. Notably, subsequent analysis demonstrated that while SBRT increased MPR, it did not significantly modulate the systemic T-cell repertoire or confer a clear survival advantage [19], highlighting the complexity of RT as an immune modulator.

Despite the consensus in preclinical literature that RT-ICI combinations enhance host anti-tumor immunity [20–23], clinical translation remains nascent, and trial outcomes have exhibited marked heterogeneity. Indeed, some combination studies have failed to demonstrate clinical benefit. This variability likely stems from a multifaceted interplay of factors, including tumor histology, RT modalities, dose distributions, and the critical parameter of treatment sequencing. For instance, in newly diagnosed glioblastoma, the addition of nivolumab to standard radiochemotherapy failed to improve survival [24]. The PACIFIC-2 study also showed that simultaneous durvalumab with concurrent chemoradiotherapy did not improve PFS in unresectable stage III NSCLC, suggesting earlier immunotherapy integration is not universally beneficial [25]. Conversely, in selected patients with early-stage or isolated lung parenchymal recurrent node-negative NSCLC, the randomized phase II trial by Chang et al. showed that stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) combined with immunotherapy improved event-free survival compared with SABR alone, supporting the clinical potential of integrating high-precision radiotherapy with immunotherapy in appropriately selected localized NSCLC settings [26]. However, the benefit of RT is often constrained by its anatomical and biological “off-target” effects. Incidental high-dose irradiation to the heart has been correlated with sustained posttreatment lymphopenia, potentially undermining therapeutic efficacy [27]. Beyond physical parameters, RT can paradoxically promote metastasis by inducing amphiregulin (AREG), which reprograms myeloid cells toward an immunosuppressive phenotype [28]. Together, these factors—from treatment sequencing to RT-induced biological signaling—demonstrate that RT is a double-edged sword that can either prime the immune system or create a sanctuary for tumor evasion.

Accumulating evidence suggests that one of the critical drivers of this discordant efficacy is radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) [29–32]. A comprehensive meta-analysis comprising 56 studies and 13,223 patients confirmed RIL as an independent poor prognostic factor for overall survival in solid tumors (HR = 1.70, 95% CI 1.55–1.86), indicating that RT can paradoxically induce an immunosuppressive state that compromises immunotherapy efficacy [33]. Importantly, the immunosuppressive consequences of RT extend beyond numerical lymphocyte depletion. RT frequently triggers pro-tumorigenic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) by recruiting myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [34] and regulatory T cells (Tregs) [35, 36], and by stimulating the release of potent immunosuppressive cytokines such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β). These components collectively establish a tolerogenic landscape that blunts systemic anti-tumor immunity [37].

Circulating lymphocytes are the central executioners of the systemic anti-tumor immune response [20], yet they are also among the cell types most sensitive to IR [21]. CD8+ cytotoxic T cells are the primary effectors, mediating direct tumor lysis via the perforin/granzyme B pathway or Fas/FasL signaling. Furthermore, their secretion of cytokines such as interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) not only enhances cytotoxicity but also remodels the tumor microenvironment (TME) to inhibit angiogenesis [22]. CD4+ T helper cells (particularly the Th1 subset) provide essential proliferative and activation signals to CD8+ T cells via cytokines like IL-2. Additionally, B cells and the tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) formed in the peritumoral niche play a critical role in sustaining long-term immunological memory through antibody production and antigen presentation. Thus, absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) serves as a systemic rate-limiting factor for RT-ICI efficacy. Beyond circulating populations, tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs) play an indispensable role in postradiation anti-tumor activity; Arina et al. showed that a subset of intratumoral T cells can survive clinically relevant radiation doses, a phenomenon regulated by intrinsic TGF-β signaling [38]. Within our proposed framework, the gut microbiota contributes to maintaining these resident populations via systemic metabolic priming. Microbial-derived metabolites, such as short-chain fatty acids, enhance mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and DNA damage response pathways in TRMs [39, 40], thereby facilitating their adaptation to the hostile post-RT microenvironment.

The impact of RT on lymphocytes is heavily influenced by the delivery technique. Intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), characterized by numerous fractions, prolonged treatment courses, and large target volumes, often results in the repeated irradiation of a large volume of circulating blood, leading to the cumulative exhaustion of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [41, 42]. Biological outcomes also depend on dose fractionation: While high-dose radiation is necessary to trigger antigen release, strategic low-dose radiation can remodel tumor stroma to facilitate immune infiltration [43].

In this context, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) offers a distinct advantage by using steep dose gradients to minimize irradiated normal tissue volume, thereby limiting exposure of circulating lymphocytes and maintaining the peripheral immune pool while delivering high-dose radiation for antigen exposure [44, 45]. Consequently, the capacity of RT to amplify immunotherapy efficacy is not only a matter of dose intensity but is largely contingent upon its ability to physically spare circulating lymphocytes [46] while biologically optimizing the local tumor environment intensity [46–48].

Concurrently, the field of oncology is witnessing another breakthrough centered on the gut microbiota. As one of the body’s largest and most complex immune-regulatory organs, the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in cancer immunotherapy [49, 50], tumor progression [51, 52], and the biological response to radiation [53, 54]. A clear conceptual distinction should be made between two conceptually distinct but mechanistically linked processes. The first is the radiation-specific effect on the intestinal ecosystem, which is primarily determined by dose distribution, anatomical exposure, and local tissue injury. The second is microbiota-mediated regulation of systemic lymphocyte homeostasis, which reflects host-dependent biological modulation beyond direct radiation exposure.

Notably, while traditional dogma attributed RIL primarily to physical dose exposure of the bone marrow, Li et al. provided the first evidence in cervical cancer patients that intestinal radiation dose has been reported to be a stronger predictor of severe RIL than either bone marrow dose or target volume [55]. This effect is unlikely to be purely dose-dependent; rather, it reflects an interplay between radiation dose and anatomical functional specificity. The intestine is not a homogeneous organ but a collection of distinct immunological niches [56]. Specifically, gut-associated lymphoid tissues (GALT), such as Peyer’s patches [57], act as primary sites for lymphocyte maturation and systemic trafficking [56, 58]. Irradiating these high-density immunological niches may lead to more profound systemic lymphopenia than irradiating segments with lower lymphoid density [59].

Importantly, however, this concept should not be interpreted to mean that intestinal or lymphoid-field irradiation inevitably precludes benefit from RT–ICI combinations. A notable example is the phase III KEYNOTE-A18 trial in high-risk locally advanced cervical cancer [60], in which pembrolizumab combined with concurrent chemoradiotherapy followed by pembrolizumab maintenance significantly improved clinical outcomes despite pelvic irradiation involving lymphatic regions and unavoidable bowel exposure. This positive result suggests that, in certain immunologically favorable contexts and with an optimized treatment strategy, the immune-activating effects of chemoradiotherapy plus PD-1 blockade may outweigh, at least in part, the immunosuppressive consequences of pelvic irradiation [61]. Therefore, within the MLPA framework, intestinal irradiation should be viewed not as an absolute barrier to radio-immunotherapy efficacy, but as a dose–volume- and host-dependent biological modifier that may shape the magnitude, durability, and heterogeneity of therapeutic benefit.

In addition, irradiation-induced disruption of the gut microbiota and intestinal barrier integrity may further amplify systemic immune dysregulation through inflammatory signaling and impaired lymphocyte homeostasis [62–64]. Therefore, the gut (particularly GALT-rich regions) may be conceptualized as a context-dependent immune organ-at-risk (iOAR), whose biological integrity is critical for maintaining systemic immune competence.

Building on these insights, we propose a novel concept: The gut microbiota may represent a critical biological factor influencing the success of RT-ICI combinations [65, 66], specifically through the preservation of circulating lymphocyte quantity and function. We term this the “Microbiota–Lymphocyte Protective Axis (MLPA)”—a theoretical and integrative framework aiming to unify existing mechanistic and clinical observations related to RIL and radio-immunotherapy outcomes (Fig. 1).

Mechanisms by which the MLPA orchestrates lymphocyte regeneration and homeostasis

The pathophysiology of radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) is characterized by a biphasic progression: an acute phase of physical cellular injury followed by a chronic phase of lymphocyte regeneration [67]. In the context of thoracic radiotherapy for lung cancer, the radiation field encompasses the chest and lungs [47], where circulating lymphocytes traversing the pulmonary microcirculation are directly irradiated and depleted, precipitating a precipitous drop in peripheral lymphocyte counts. During this critical window, immune reconstruction relies heavily on the compensatory capacity of the bone marrow (BM) hematopoietic system [68].

The microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA) is proposed to provide essential microenvironmental support and metabolic substrates for lymphocyte regeneration via the distal regulation of the gut–bone marrow axis. A functional MLPA stimulates the differentiation of hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and accelerates lymphocyte expansion and repopulation [69, 70]. This chapter dissects the mechanisms by which the gut microbiota mediates lymphocyte regeneration across three dimensions: hematopoietic regulation, cellular radioprotection, and systemic stress modulation (Fig. 2).

Mechanistic framework of the microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA) in lymphocyte regeneration and survival following radiotherapy. Radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) evolves through an acute phase of direct lymphocyte injury followed by a chronic phase of immune reconstitution, during which the microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA) provides essential support for lymphocyte regeneration and survival through three interconnected dimensions. First, along the gut–bone marrow axis, microbiota-derived signals modulate the bone marrow niche to promote lymphoid lineage commitment: NOD1 ligands stimulate mesenchymal stromal cells (MSCs) to produce lymphopoietic factors such as IL-7 and Flt3L, while lactate engages GPR81 and promotes SCF secretion, thereby supporting hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) differentiation and lymphopoiesis. In parallel, an intact intestinal barrier and microbial homeostasis restrain LPS/TLR4/MyD88-driven inflammatory signaling, limiting myeloid skewing and preserving lymphoid regeneration potential. Second, through metabolite-mediated radioprotection, microbial metabolites—particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)—enhance lymphocyte stress tolerance by facilitating DNA damage repair, supporting metabolic reprogramming toward a memory-like phenotype, and preserving mitochondrial quality via antioxidant defense and mitophagy, thereby reducing apoptosis and promoting functional recovery. Third, via the gut–brain axis, microbiota-derived neural and metabolic signals dampen HPA axis overactivation, reduce neuroinflammation and glucocorticoid surge, and ultimately attenuate glucocorticoid-mediated lymphocyte apoptosis. Collectively, these mechanisms position the MLPA as a systems-level regulator that buffers radiation-induced immune injury and sustains systemic lymphocyte homeostasis during postradiotherapy recovery

Hematopoietic regulation via the gut–bone marrow axis: promoting lymphoid lineage commitment

The bone marrow is the primary site of adult lymphopoiesis. Emerging evidence establishes that the gut microbiota is not confined to local mucosal immunity but exerts distal control over HSC fate via the “Gut–Bone Marrow Axis” [71]. Mechanistically, this axis functions as a critical biological conduit through which microbial-derived signals modulate the bone marrow microenvironment [72] to mitigate radiation-induced hematopoietic stress. For instance, microbiota-derived NOD1 ligands can induce bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to secrete key lymphopoietic factors such as IL-7 and Flt3L [73], thereby driving HSC differentiation toward the lymphoid lineage. Furthermore, gut commensal metabolites can traverse the blood–bone marrow barrier to synergistically promote hematopoiesis. For instance, lactate translocates to the bone marrow and binds to the GPR81 receptor on mesenchymal stromal cells, stimulating the secretion of stem cell factor (SCF) to promote HSC and erythroid generation [72].

Radiation stress typically induces myeloid skewing in HSCs—favoring neutrophil production at the expense of lymphopoiesis [74, 75]. This phenomenon is likely triggered by the translocation of microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS) [76], into the circulation, which activates the TLR4/MyD88 inflammatory pathway. An intact intestinal barrier and a healthy microbiota mitigate the systemic influx of LPS, thereby reducing circulating levels of IL-1 [77] and G-CSF. This suppression of inflammatory signaling inhibits myeloid bias and preserves the potential of HSCs for lymphoid differentiation [69], effectively correcting the postradiation granulocyte/lymphocyte imbalance [78].

Moreover, Zhang et al. elucidated the core mechanism of the “Microbiota-Macrophage-Iron” axis in regulating hematopoiesis. Their work revealed that microbiota depletion traps HSCs in a state of heightened self-renewal with arrested differentiation, directly impairing the regeneration of downstream lineages, including lymphocytes [79]. Thus, the microbiota lays the foundation for lymphocyte reconstruction by ensuring the physiological transition of HSCs into differentiated lineages [80, 81]. Maintaining microbial equilibrium or supplementing with key metabolites (particularly short-chain fatty acids, SCFAs) may be pivotal for optimizing global hematopoietic recovery, including lymphopoiesis, following radiotherapy [82, 83].

Metabolite-mediated radioprotection: enhancing DNA repair and antioxidant defense

While RT directly ablates lymphocytes via DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) [84–86], a subset of cells sustains only “sub-lethal” damage. The MLPA provides specific metabolites that enhance the DNA damage response (DDR) and antioxidant defenses of these cells through epigenetic remodeling, metabolic reprogramming, and mitochondrial quality control, thereby promoting survival and functional recovery [87].

Butyrate, a gut microbiota-derived metabolite [88], acts as a natural histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitor [89, 90]. Upon uptake by effector T cells [91], butyrate inhibits HDAC activity, increasing the acetylation levels of histones (e.g., H3K27). This epigenetic modification relaxes chromatin structure, facilitating the accessibility of DDR proteins such as Ku70/80 to sites of radiation-induced DSBs [90]. This epigenetic optimization is a critical step in enhancing lymphocyte radioresistance [92]. Concurrently, butyrate suppresses the expression of pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., those related to the NLRP3 inflammasome [93–95] and IL-1β [95]), thereby augmenting long-term T-cell survival and anti-apoptotic capacity.

Beyond epigenetics, butyrate regulates T-cell biology via metabolic signaling. Under physiological conditions, butyrate activates the mTORC1 pathway, stabilizing a metabolic profile that supports the differentiation of effector T cells into a memory phenotype, which is inherently more radioresistant [91]. Studies indicate that under oxidative stress or mitochondrial dysfunction, butyrate enhances mitochondrial plasticity, boosting ATP synthesis and antioxidant defenses to facilitate cellular adaptation [91, 96]. This may confer a survival advantage to butyrate-rich cell populations in hostile environments.

Notably, butyrate exhibits a “dualistic regulatory” role: It simultaneously activates the OR51E1–RALB signaling axis in tumor cells [97] to promote radiation-associated autophagic death. Thus, it protects immune cells while potentiating the local anti-tumor efficacy of RT [97].

However, this regulatory profile appears to be concentration-dependent. Recent evidence from Yang et al. cautions that elevated systemic and intratumoral butyrate levels can paradoxically impair RT efficacy [98]. Mechanistically, while butyrate-mediated HDAC inhibition facilitates DNA repair, its accumulation may also suppress the production of critical effector cytokines, such as TNF-β, in CD8+ T cells, thereby dampening the therapy-induced anti-tumor immune response [98]. Within the MLPA framework, these findings underscore that the objective of microbial modulation is the restoration of homeostatic equilibrium rather than the indefinite augmentation of specific metabolites [99], ensuring that lymphocyte sparing does not occur at the expense of their effector potency.

Radiation injury also induces mitochondrial structural damage and dysfunction [100], leading to activation failure or early apoptosis in lymphocytes [87]. The MLPA effectively preserves mitochondrial quality. SCFAs, signaling through GPR41/43 receptors, inhibit excessive inflammation [91] and maintain epithelial barrier integrity [101]. This SCFA-mediated anti-inflammatory effect indirectly mitigates oxidative damage to mitochondria [102]. Furthermore, microbial metabolites regulate mitophagy—the selective removal of damaged mitochondria [103]. Propionate derived from Akkermansia muciniphila inhibits DRP1-mediated excessive mitochondrial fission via GPR41 and enhances PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy via GPR43 [104]. Efficient mitophagy clears compromised mitochondria, preventing Cytochrome C release and interrupting the intrinsic apoptotic cascade [105], thereby ensuring T-cell functional recovery.

Neuroendocrine-immune regulation: buffering stress hormone-mediated lymphocyte apoptosis

Radiotherapy, as a potent physiological and psychological stressor, rapidly activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [106, 107], triggering a surge in glucocorticoid (GC) secretion [108]. In particular, this activation constitutes a systemic and indirect response to localized radiation, mediated by the circulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-1β and IL-6) [109] and treatment-related distress [110], regardless of the specific irradiation site. GCs induce lymphocyte apoptosis via glucocorticoid receptor (GR)-mediated transcriptional regulation, constituting a major mechanism of stress-induced lymphopenia [111, 112]. The MLPA biologically buffers this process via the “Gut–Brain Axis,” effectively attenuating lymphopenia driven by RT-associated stress.

The vagus nerve serves as the primary signaling conduit connecting the microbiota to the HPA axis [113]. Certain commensals (e.g., specific Lactobacillus strains) activate vagal afferent fibers by secreting neuroactive substances or stimulating enteroendocrine cells [114]. These rapid neural signals modulate central sensitivity to stress [115]. Clinical and preclinical studies demonstrate that germ-free (GF) or antibiotic-treated mice exhibit HPA axis hyperactivity, whereas probiotics or microbiota restoration lowers baseline and stress-induced cortisol levels [116]. Mechanistically, the microbiota downregulates Fkbp5 expression in the pituitary. The protein encoded by Fkbp5 reduces GR sensitivity; thus, its downregulation enhances the efficiency of the HPA axis negative feedback loop [117]. Under chronic restraint stress, mice with a normal microbiota exhibit lower corticosterone (CORT) levels than GF mice [117], as the microbiota sets the activation threshold of the HPA axis by regulating GR expression in the hippocampus and hypothalamus, preventing excessive and prolonged responses to radiation stress.

Beyond direct vagal intervention, the microbiota and its derivatives provide slower but sustained support for HPA regulation via the gut–brain barrier [118]. The gut microbiota regulates neurotransmitter precursors, such as by metabolizing tryptophan into aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) ligands (e.g., indole derivatives) [119, 120]. These ligands are pivotal for maintaining intestinal epithelial integrity and local immune homeostasis [118]. This microbiota-supported homeostasis mitigates systemic low-grade inflammation [121], providing a stable peripheral environment for the HPA axis [122].

Microbiota-derived SCFAs also maintain the integrity of the blood–brain barrier (BBB) [123]. Their protective mechanism centers on antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects, specifically the inhibition of NF-κB and the activation of Nrf2—the latter being key to neutralizing NF-κB-driven inflammation [124, 125]. An intact BBB effectively prevents the influx of blood-borne inflammatory cytokines into the CNS, thereby reducing neuroinflammation, a driver of HPA axis hyperactivity and chronic stress responses. By preserving BBB integrity [126, 127], the MLPA indirectly stabilizes the central response to stress.

In summary, a homeostatic MLPA effectively dampens the RT-induced overactivation of the HPA axis, blunting peak circulating glucocorticoid levels [128]. This biological buffering directly reduces GC-mediated lymphocyte apoptosis, thereby minimizing cell death in non-irradiated regions and preserving a larger “immune effector pool” for systemic recovery.

Clinical integration and regulatory potential of the microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA)

Historically, radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) was dogmatically viewed as an inevitable, dose-dependent consequence of physical radiation exposure [55, 129]. We challenge this perspective, positing instead that RIL is a complex outcome derived from the interplay between physical radiation injury and the host’s biological background. Recent clinical evidence underscores this biological dependency; for instance, a large-scale study demonstrated that baseline lymphocyte levels significantly predict postradiotherapy lymphopenia independently of traditional dosimetric parameters [130]. This chapter integrates radiation dosimetry with host biology to explore clinical intervention strategies based on the MLPA to potentiate the efficacy of RT-ICI combinations [131].

Integrating physical protection with biological amplification

The integrity of the microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA) is a prerequisite for achieving effective synergy between radiotherapy and immunotherapy. While traditional paradigms have emphasized physical lymphocyte preservation [132]—particularly through advanced delivery techniques such as stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) [133]—emerging evidence suggests that optimal immune preservation requires a more comprehensive framework integrating both dosimetric and biological determinants.

A clinically meaningful understanding of this interaction necessitates distinguishing between scenarios in which the intestine is included within the radiation field and those in which it is not. This distinction is essential to avoid conflating radiation-specific toxicity with downstream biological amplification, and serves as a conceptual framework for interpreting the heterogeneous mechanisms underlying radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL). Specifically, this distinction enables a structured separation between primary radiation effects and secondary host-dependent biological responses.

In irradiation settings where the bowel is largely excluded (e.g., esophageal carcinoma, thoracic radiotherapy) [130, 134], the risk of radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) is predominantly driven by repeated exposure of circulating blood pools and lymphocyte-rich organs [135]. In contrast, in abdominal or pelvic radiotherapy, where intestinal exposure is unavoidable, the gut microenvironment emerges as a critical regulator of systemic immune homeostasis [136, 137].

In this context, the potential for biological amplification following radiotherapy is determined not only by the physical sparing of circulating lymphocytes but also by the preservation of the intestinal microenvironment [138], which serves as a functional foundation of the MLPA. Importantly, the contribution of intestinal irradiation should be understood as a dose–volume-dependent phenomenon rather than a binary effect [139]. Clinical evidence indicates [140] that parameters such as bowel V15, V30, and mean intestinal dose may correlate with the severity of lymphopenia, reflecting the extent of disruption to microbiota-mediated immune regulation.

Mechanistically, intestinal irradiation initiates a multi-step cascade linking local radiation exposure to systemic immune dysfunction [141]. High-dose exposure to the gastrointestinal tract disrupts mucosal barrier integrity, leading to microbial dysbiosis and the systemic translocation of pro-inflammatory mediators [99, 142, 143]. This inflammatory signaling perturbs bone marrow hematopoiesis, promotes myeloid skewing, and ultimately impairs lymphocyte regeneration [144]. In parallel, cumulative low-dose irradiation—often delivered as a “low-dose bath” in conventional fractionated radiotherapy—induces more subtle but biologically meaningful alterations in microbial composition and metabolite production [145], thereby weakening the microbiota’s capacity to sustain systemic immune homeostasis [146]. Crucially, while classical hematopoietic cytokines regulate acute recovery, the MLPA acts as a “systemic rheostat” that preconditions the bone marrow’s regenerative set-point; without a healthy microbial tonic, the hematopoietic response to physical injury becomes inherently blunted [99, 147].Together, these effects define a dose-dependent continuum through which intestinal radiation exposure modulates immune recovery via both structural and functional mechanisms.

Within this clinically contextualized framework, the MLPA should be conceptualized not merely as a theoretical construct, but as an integrative mediator that links radiation dosimetry with host biological response. By coordinately influencing hematopoietic output, inflammatory tone, and immune resilience, the MLPA provides a mechanistic bridge between radiation delivery and systemic immune competence [54, 148], offering a biologically grounded explanation for the inter-patient heterogeneity observed in RIL severity under similar treatment regimens [87].

From a therapeutic perspective, this integrated model also refines the role of SBRT. Owing to its steep dose gradients and reduced integral dose [149], SBRT effectively limits irradiation of circulating lymphocytes and minimizes low-dose exposure to surrounding normal tissues [150]. However, its microbiota- or bowel-sparing advantage should not be generalized solely according to tumor type, but should instead be interpreted according to the anatomical relationship between the target volume and the intestine. In scenarios where the target is anatomically distant from the bowel, such as upper-lobe lung tumors or other extra-abdominal lesions, intestinal irradiation is minimal or absent; therefore, the principal immunological advantage of SBRT may derive mainly from reducing the integral dose and sparing the circulating blood pool rather than from additional bowel sparing [130, 151]. In contrast, for pancreatic, abdominal, or pelvic malignancies, where the bowel is adjacent to or may overlap with the radiation field, SBRT does not necessarily confer a substantial bowel-sparing benefit [152, 153]. Even with highly conformal delivery, portions of the duodenum, small bowel, or large bowel may still receive biologically relevant doses sufficient to induce dysbiosis, barrier dysfunction [81], and downstream immune dysregulation. This distinction underscores that SBRT should be viewed as a lymphocyte-sparing and dose-optimization strategy whose microbiota-preserving effect is highly context dependent. In high-risk anatomical settings, strict intestinal dose constraints and biological interventions, such as microbiota modulation or postbiotic supplementation, may still be required to preserve MLPA integrity [154, 155].

Taken together, these findings support a dual-modality strategy in which physical dose optimization [134] and biological ecosystem preservation [54] are jointly leveraged to mitigate RIL. Within this paradigm, the intestine may be more appropriately conceptualized as a context-dependent immune organ-at-risk (iOAR), whose protection is essential for maintaining systemic immune competence and maximizing the therapeutic synergy of radiotherapy and immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Evidence for microbiota-targeted interventions

The plasticity of the MLPA has been validated through extensive preclinical and clinical intervention studies.

The dichotomy of antibiotic intervention

While antibiotic-induced dysbiosis generally compromises the efficacy of PD-1 blockade in mice [156]—underscoring microbial homeostasis as a determinant of immunotherapy response—nuances exist. Interestingly, in B16-OVA and TC-1 tumor models, targeting the gut microbiota with vancomycin synergistically enhanced radiotherapy efficacy [157]. This effect relied on the cross-presentation of tumor antigens by cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and subsequent IFN-γ production [158]. Crucially, this synergistic strategy has recently transitioned from bench to bedside. In a pilot study, Feigenberg et al. investigated gut-targeted antibiotics combined with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) in patients with early-stage NSCLC and reported preliminary associations with favorable clinical outcomes. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution because the study included only a very small cohort (n = 17), and PFS and OS were not its primary endpoints. Therefore, this study should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive clinical evidence [159]. Mechanistically, this observed effect was associated with the depletion of specific immunosuppressive taxa and the modulation of metabolic pathways, such as butyrate metabolism, thereby potentially reshaping the systemic immune landscape in a manner that may favor radiation-induced anti-tumor responses [159]. These findings highlight the complexity of microbial manipulation, suggesting that “microbiota stewardship” through targeted depletion, rather than broad-spectrum suppression, may represent a potential strategy to enhance the MLPA.

Probiotics and key species supplementation

Supplementation with specific commensals, such as Akkermansia muciniphila [160, 161], has been shown to restore sensitivity to immunotherapy [162]. Mechanisms include enhancing intestinal barrier function, inducing IL-12 secretion, and recruiting CCR9+CXCR3+CD4+T cells to the tumor bed. In irradiated mouse models, a probiotic cocktail (including L. acidophilus, L. rhamnosus, L. plantarum, B. longum, and B. lactis) significantly mitigated radiation-induced intestinal epithelial injury and partially restored microbial diversity following high-dose exposure [160]. Importantly, these radioprotective effects have been validated in human clinical trials. A Phase II randomized clinical trial [163] involving nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients demonstrated that an improved probiotic combination (B. longum, L. acidophilus, and E. faecalis) was associated with a significant reduction in the incidence and severity of radiation-induced oral mucositis. Beyond local mucosal protection, the study revealed that probiotic supplementation was associated with improvements in immune function including increased levels of peripheral CD3+ , CD4+, and CD8+ T lymphocytes, alongside reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [163]. This clinical evidence supports the concept that the MLPA may function as a systemic biological buffer, potentially translating microbial signals into enhanced lymphocyte resilience and systemic immune homeostasis during intensive radiotherapy.

Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)

Clinical trials have demonstrated that transferring fecal material from “immune responders” to “non-responders” can overcome resistance to PD-1 inhibitors [164, 165]. The clinical application of FMT is increasingly being explored beyond its traditional role as a salvage therapy to first-line integration. A Phase I trial demonstrated that combining healthy donor FMT with PD-1 inhibitors in treatment-naive advanced melanoma patients achieved an objective response rate (ORR) of 65%, with successful engraftment of donor strains correlating with increased systemic immune activation [165]. Furthermore, a recent study involving refractory solid tumors identified Prevotella merdae as a potential contributor to FMT-mediated responses. This specific taxon was found to expand the pool of peripheral effector T cells and modulate metabolic pathways to support anti-tumor immune activity [166]. These findings provide supportive evidence that the gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in overcoming immune resistance by modulating lymphocyte homeostasis and function.

The dualistic nature of MLPA regulation: resistance versus sensitization

The MLPA regulates radiosensitivity in a bidirectional manner [131]. Prospective studies indicate that the gut microbiome composition in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) correlates with their response to radiotherapy; “Non-Responders” (NR) exhibit significantly lower ecological diversity compared to “Responders” (R) [167].

Specifically, the NR phenotype is associated with an enrichment of genera such as Streptococcus and Lactobacillus. These bacteria can activate TLR2/MyD88 signaling in macrophages and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) via cell wall components like lipoteichoic acid. This activation drives MSC migration to crypt regions and induces PGE2 production, activating the EGFR-Akt pathway in intestinal stem cells [168]. This cascade enhances cell proliferation and inhibits apoptosis, thereby dampening the local cytotoxic effects of radiotherapy [169]. Furthermore, certain species (e.g., specific Bacteroides spp. and Fusobacterium) promote radioresistance indirectly by upregulating host DNA repair mechanisms or nucleotide synthesis pathways [63, 160].

Conversely, beneficial genera, typified by the butyrate-producing Roseburia, demonstrate radio-sensitizing properties [170]. Recent in vivo studies reveal that butyrate produced by Roseburia intestinalis activates the OR51E1–RALB signaling axis in tumor cells, promoting radiation-associated autophagy [63]. This enhances lethal radiation effects while improving local anti-tumor responses. This dualistic mechanism identifies microbial composition and metabolic function as a biological rheostat determining the success or failure of radio-immune synergy.

From theory to practice: clinical translation and therapeutic strategies targeting the MLPA

Although the rationale for RT-ICI combinations is well-supported by theory and basic science [171, 172], the observed heterogeneity in clinical benefit necessitates novel intervention strategies. Based on the MLPA mechanism elucidated above, we propose that the severity of RIL is not merely a passive result of physical dosing but a dynamic process actively modulated by gut microecology. Future clinical strategies must shift from passive management to active prevention [173]—moving beyond treating radiation toxicity after it occurs to actively preserving MLPA integrity via physical means and reconstructing compromised immune-microecological homeostasis via biological means.

Limitations of current interventions

Recent studies demonstrate that oral probiotics or FMT can reverse dysbiosis and mitigate radiation enteritis, improving quality of life [174–177]. A case report highlighted that FMT induced both short-term symptom relief and long-term improvement in chronic radiation enteritis by reshaping the gut microbiota [178]. Additionally, FMT has been shown to reverse immune resistance in melanoma, where transferring “responder” microbiota sensitized refractory patients to PD-L1 blockade [156, 179]

However, these existing strategies are predominantly reactive salvage therapies focused on toxicity management. The MLPA-targeted strategy we propose aims to front-load the intervention, integrating lymphocyte preservation as a core objective into the entire continuum of radiotherapy management. This rationale is based on the central role of circulating and tissue-resident lymphocytes in mediating anti-tumor immunity, as well as clinical evidence linking radiation-induced lymphopenia with inferior therapeutic outcomes [180, 181]. Accordingly, preserving lymphocyte quantity and function should be considered an important objective in RT–ICI combination strategies [38]. Realizing this goal requires a two-pronged strategy.

Risk-stratified intervention models

We propose two intervention modes tailored to patient risk profiles:

Physical protection strategy (the dosimetric approach) dosimetric sparing remains paramount

Stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), capable of delivering ablative doses to highly focused targets [182], offers inherent “lymphocyte-sparing” characteristics due to its high precision and hypofractionated schedule. This creates a favorable immunological environment for synergistic ICI therapy [183]. Clinical trials confirm that SBRT improves overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS) in oligometastatic settings, including colorectal liver metastases [184–186]. Therefore, whenever clinically feasible, priority should be given to hypofractionated techniques like SBRT to minimize the integral dose to the circulating blood pool and avoid unnecessary irradiation of lymphoid organs.

Biological intervention strategy (the ecological approach)

For patients where intestinal irradiation is anatomically unavoidable, or where severe RIL has already supervened, reconstructing the gut microecology is critical for restoring RT-ICI efficacy [160, 187].

FMT: For these patients, FMT utilizing donor material enriched with butyrate-producing bacteria (e.g., Faecalibacterium and Lachnospiraceae) [188] can reshape the ecosystem, block the LPS-driven systemic inflammatory storm, and ameliorate the immune microenvironment.

Postbiotics: The strategy of “Postbiotic” precision therapy is gaining prominence. Given the potential safety risks of live biotherapeutics in immunocompromised cancer patients, direct supplementation with key MLPA metabolites—short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and their derivatives (e.g., valerate, butyrate)—offers a safer, more promising alternative [78, 189, 190]. Evidence confirms that exogenous valerate significantly mitigates radiation-induced hematopoietic injury and promotes lymphopoiesis, while butyrate enhances CD8+ T-cell cytotoxicity and radiosensitivity [100]. Supplementing these postbiotics during radiotherapy serves to actively shield lymphocytes and sustain host immune homeostasis.

A Roadmap for clinical translation: from mechanistic concept to evidence-based validation

While the intervention modes outlined above represent a potential blueprint for MLPA modulation, their widespread clinical adoption remains constrained by a paucity of standardized validation frameworks. To bridge this “bench-to-bedside” gap, we delineate a three-phase translational roadmap (Supplementary Fig. 2) that transitions from descriptive mapping to predictive intervention and, ultimately, to integrative radiotherapy strategies.

Phase I: Prospective Mapping and Discovery of Microbial Radiosensitivity Signatures (MRS).

The principal objective of Phase I would be to characterize the “Baseline Immune-Microbial Landscape” that may predispose patients to radiation toxicity. Rather than reactive monitoring, Phase I could be strategically directed toward large-scale, multi-center prospective cohorts utilizing longitudinal multi-omics profiling. By integrating 16S/metagenomic sequencing with serum metabolomics and dosimetric parameters, researchers may facilitate the development of a microbial radiosensitivity signature (MRS) to identify “immunosusceptible” individuals prior to treatment. This approach is supported by preliminary evidence suggesting that baseline gut diversity and specific taxa (e.g., Faecalibacterium) exhibit promising predictive capacity for gastrointestinal toxicity [81, 191, 192].

Phase II: Precision Intervention Trials via “Additive” and “Subtractive” Strategies.

Phase II evaluates the efficacy of MLPA-targeted interventions in patients stratified as “high-risk” by Phase I signatures. We propose two parallel interventional avenues:

The “Additive” Path: Evaluating specific probiotic strains (e.g., B. longum BL21 [193]) or postbiotics (e.g., butyrate [175]) to potentially restore gut barrier integrity and support lymphocyte recovery.

The “Subtractive” Path: Utilizing targeted antibiotics to selectively deplete potentially immunosuppressive taxa or to leverage their inherent radioprotective properties [194, 195]. A compelling example is provided by the high-throughput screening study by Kim et al., which identified tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones as potential radioprotectors of the hematopoietic system. Specifically, these agents have been shown to significantly mitigate radiation-induced cellular damage and improve host survival, possibly by modulating DNA damage responses or reducing oxidative stress [196]. Such findings suggest a putative dual role for targeted antibiotics within the MLPA framework: not only in remodeling the microbial ecosystem but also in directly shielding host immune cells from radiation-induced attrition.

It is recommended that these trials employ “immune-centric” endpoints, such as the TCR diversity index and the kinetics of absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) recovery, to complement traditional tumor response rates.

Phase III: Systemic Integration and the “Immune Organ-at-Risk” (iOAR) Paradigm.

The prospective stage of translation involves conceptualizing the gut microbiota as a novel immune organ-at-risk (iOAR). In this phase, MLPA protection could be integrated into the radiotherapy planning system (TPS). Adaptive trials may help clarify whether combining physical dose constraints [197] (e.g., limiting V15 gut or V30 gut) with biologically-driven MLPA prophylaxis can synergistically preserve the host’s “immune reservoir” [198]. Such studies are expected to elucidate if MLPA-informed radiotherapy planning can contribute to optimizing the therapeutic index of RT-ICI combinations, potentially informing future standards for precision radiation oncology.

In summary, clinical translation strategies targeting the MLPA represent a paradigm shift in adjuvant radiotherapy: moving from passive toxicity management to active immune protection. By employing the dual levers of “physical dose reduction” and “biological ecological reconstruction,” we aim to break the vicious cycle between radiation toxicity and immunosuppression, thereby unleashing the full potential of RT-ICI combination therapy.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The microbiota–lymphocyte protective axis (MLPA) framework provides a revised conceptual lens through which radiation-induced lymphopenia (RIL) can be understood. Rather than representing a purely physical and unavoidable consequence of radiation exposure, RIL should be viewed as a context-dependent process shaped by the dynamic interplay between radiation dosimetry and host biological systems [148, 199].

In particular, when the intestine is included within the radiation field, intestinal radiation dose may represent a critical and potentially underrecognized contributor to systemic immunosuppression [200]. Through its impact on the gut microbiota, epithelial barrier integrity, and downstream hematopoietic [78] and inflammatory pathways [201], intestinal irradiation may contribute to the amplification and persistence of lymphocyte depletion beyond direct cytotoxic effects alone [141].This paradigm shift has important clinical implications. It suggests that optimization of radiotherapy should extend beyond conventional organ-at-risk constraints to include preservation of the intestinal immune ecosystem. Dose–volume parameters such as bowel V15 and V30 may therefore represent not only toxicity predictors but also surrogate markers of systemic immune preservation.

Within this framework, stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) may represent a promising strategy for minimizing immune disruption, but its advantages should be interpreted in an anatomy- and dose-distribution-dependent manner. When the bowel is anatomically distant from the target, SBRT may primarily preserve systemic immune competence by reducing integral dose and limiting exposure of the circulating blood pool. Conversely, when bowel irradiation is difficult to avoid, SBRT may still offer advantages over conventional radiotherapy through its high conformality and steep dose falloff, thereby reducing the volume of bowel receiving clinically relevant radiation doses. However, these advantages are not absolute and should be integrated with strict intestinal dose constraints and, where appropriate, microbiota-targeted interventions.

Looking forward, the integration of MLPA into clinical practice will require prospective validation through studies that combine dosimetric analysis with longitudinal profiling of the gut microbiome and immune parameters. The concept of the intestine as an “immune organ-at-risk” (iOAR) warrants further investigation and may ultimately inform the development of next-generation radiotherapy planning strategies. Importantly, the MLPA should not be interpreted as a singular mechanistic pathway, but rather as a systems-level framework integrating microbiota, immunity, and radiation biology. In summary, by bridging radiation physics with host–microbiota biology, the MLPA framework offers a more comprehensive and clinically relevant model of RIL. This integrative perspective has the potential to guide the development of personalized, immune-preserving radiotherapy approaches and to enhance the therapeutic synergy between radiotherapy and immunotherapy.
