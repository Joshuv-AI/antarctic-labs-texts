Retrospective cohort study of Gram-negative bacteraemia shows transmission of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae in a UK Intensive Care Unit during the Covid-19 pandemic
Authors: Rachel Bousfield, Ruth Kappeler, Sumita Pai, Olly Allen, Kathy Randall, Jacqueline Keane, Stephen Baker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe National incidence of bloodstream infection (BSI) caused by Klebsiella Pneumoniae increased during the Covid-19 pandemic in the United Kingdom, whilst we observed an increase in BSI caused by Gram-negative bacteria in our adult Intensive Care Unit (ICU).MethodsWe audited all Gram-negative bacterial BSIs between 13th April 2020 and 25th December 2021 in our ICU. In total, 41 organisms underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing and genome sequencing. Notable organisms isolated included 16 Klebsiella spp., 4 E. coli, and 4 Pseudomonas spp.ResultsOverall, we observed a low prevalence of multi-drug resistant (MDR) organisms causing BSI on our unit. A fifth (4/20) of E. coli and Klebsiella spp. isolates carried ESBL or AmpC genes and a single P. monteilli carried the IMP-1 gene. At least 3/16 (19%) BSI with Klebsiella spp. were likely associated with transmission between patients in nearby beds. These transmission events were associated with two hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (ST412 and ST86) and a K. aerogenes ∗002b. Half of all K. pneumoniae associated with BSI were hypervirulent, having K2 or K57 capsule type and the presence of iuc, iro, Rmp genes.DiscussionHypervirulent K. pneumoniae is an emerging problem, capable of causing a severe, disseminated infection. We suggest risk factors for transmission may include shared equipment, environmental and PPE contamination, and failure of effective hand hygiene. With COVID-19 infection endemic, if SARS-CoV-2 patients require cohort nursing in bays with sessional use gowns, we recommend these are short-sleeved, to facilitate effective hand hygiene. We argue strict Infection Control policy, including enhanced cleaning, is critical to reduce transmission of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae and advocate for enhanced national surveillance systems.Keywords: Hypervirulent, Klebsiella Pneumoniae, Gram-negative, Transmission, Intensive Care, Blood stream infection, Bacteraemia

Background

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) at Royal Papworth Hospital, Cambridge noted an increase in the overall incidence of Gram-negative bacterial blood stream infections (BSI.) This was in the context of increasing Klebsiella Pneumoniae BSI incidence and decreasing E. coli BSI incidence across the UK [1,2].

The ICU provided Extra Corporeal membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) to the region and consequently the sickest patients spent several weeks on the unit (Figure 1), frequently connected to a machine like a heart-lung bypass utilised for open heart surgery, intubated and mechanically ventilated, with multiple invasive intravenous cannulas, urinary catheters and naso-gastric feeding tubes. Royal Papworth Hospital is one of five UK ECMO centres and patients have an average length of stay of 291 days. It houses a 46 bed critical care unit where the majority of the rooms can be separated by sliding doors in non-pandemic settings, but during covid-19 pandemic these were opened to allow cohort nursing in bay areas. Patients were generally immunosuppressed due to use of high dose dexamethasone and, later in the pandemic, some received the anti-IL6 inhibitors, tocilizumab.

Photo of Royal Papworth Hospital Intensive Care Unit bed spaces.

There were global reports of outbreaks of antimicrobial resistant K. pneumoniae in ICU and inpatient settings, during the pandemic. These included K. pneumoniae ST11 (blaKPC) in Argentina [3], K. pneumoniae (blaKPC-2) in Italy [4], Klebsiella pneumoniae ST394 (blaCTX-M-15) in France [5], K. pneumoniae ST11 (blaNDM-1) in Portugal [6], and K. pneumoniae ST258 (blaKPC-2) in Brazil [7]. Causative factors were generally multifactorial and often occurred with collapse of the healthcare system associated with an over-crowed ICU, inadequately trained specialist staff, and insufficient personal protective equipment (PPE).

Classical K. pneumoniae, a hospital-associated, opportunist pathogen, causes severe infections, in part due to its ability to acquire antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes (ARGs). Hypervirulent strains cause disseminated infections of the liver, lungs, brain, eyes, soft tissues and BSI, and are associated with polysaccharide capsule type (K1,K2 or K5), and acquisition of genes encoding for siderophores aerobactin or salmochelin, genotoxin colibactin and regulators of mucoidity [8].

There were concerns that some patients on our unit had disseminated infection in keeping with that associated with this emerging pathogen. However, it was unclear if the increased overall increase in Gram-negative bacterial BSI on our unit was primarily due to an outbreak of hypervirulent pathogens, shortfalls in infection prevention and control policy or increased host susceptibility, leaving them vulnerable to infection with colonising gut strains. We undertook audit of Gram-negative bacterial BSI, including selected bacterial genome sequencing, to determine if there was an outbreak in our centre. Additionally, we explored infection control practices and PPE use on the unit.

Methods

Experimental design

We examined all Gram-negative bacterial BSI in adult inpatients, between 13th April 2020 and 25th December 2021 on the ICU at the Royal Papworth Hospital, Cambridge. This single-centre audit was locally registered, performed for infection control purposes to inform future policies, with the aim to reduce BSI incidence and improve patient care. As such, it was not necessary to seek regulatory ethical approvals as we focused on routinely collected bacterial isolates, and no patient identifiable data was collected.

Sequencing of bacterial isolates

The identified 41 bacterial isolates, previously stored at -80°C, were passaged and grown on Columbia blood agar (Merck), aerobically incubated at 37°C for 24 hours prior to extraction. The MALDI-TOF Biotyper® (Bruker) platform confirmed organism identification. Bacterial DNA was extracted using the QIAamp® DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen®, code 51304) according to the manufacturer's protocol. A Qubit™ 2.0 Fluorometer was used to quantify the DNA concentrations and 100μl of DNA from each sample was placed in a snap-cap microcentrifuge tube and shipped to Eurofins Genomics. This underwent standard library preparation and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 PE150 instrument, with 5 million paired-end reads per bacterial isolate.

Minimum inhibition concentrations

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was determined using the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) disc diffusion testing (Muller Hinton agar and antibiotic-impregnated discs.) The results had previously been internationally calibrated to EUCAST clinical breakpoints using broth microdilution for MIC determination. The calibrated tables are published on the EUCAST website [9]. A bacteria culture (0.5 McFarland standard) was plated to lawn on Muller Hinton agar and underwent aerobic incubation at 37°C for 24 hours. The zone diameter of inhibition of growth was recorded and corresponded with predetermined cut-offs indicating antimicrobial specific susceptibility or resistance. Isolates resistant to either cefpodoxime, cefotaxime or ceftazidime underwent ESBL/AmpC confirmatory testing using combination disc testing (MAST Group AmpC ESBL detection set, D68C) according to the manufacturer's instructions. [10] Isolates resistant to meropenem by initial disc diffusion testing underwent confirmatory testing (meropenem etest ® bioMerieux) and CRO testing using PCR-based testing for KPC, OXA-48-like, NDM, VIM and IMP-1-like genes (Cephid GeneXpert ® Systems, CARBA-R) [11].

Genome assembly and pangenome analysis of bacterial isolates

Bacterial isolate sequencing reads underwent genome assembly using Nextflow version 21.10.6 [12] and GHRU assembly pipeline version 2.1.2 [13]. The GHRU assembly pipeline involved processing of the sequence reads (trimmed and adapters removed using trimmomatic version 0.38 [14]) and correction of reads (lighter version 1.1.1 [15]) downsampling to 100x coverage (seqtk version 1.3 [16]) and merging of reads (flash version 1.2.11. [17]) The processed reads were assembled (SPAdes version 3.12.0 [18]) and underwent quality control (fastqc version 0.11.8 [19], multiqc version 1.7 [20] and qualifyr version 1.4.4 [21].) Species identification was performed (bactinspector version 0.1.3 [22]) and contamination checked (Confindr version 0.7.2 [23].) The ARIBA software version 2.14.4 was used to detect acquired genes using the NCBI database [24] and the pointfinder database adapted for ARIBA [25]. The 7 locus MLST type was also determined using ARIBA software version 2.14.4 [26] and the profile and alleles found in the pubmlst database [27]. Genome assemblies' mean nucleotide identity was calculated using FastANI version 1.33 [28].

Mapping analysis

Sequencing reads for each bacterial isolate dataset were mapped to a corresponding reference genomes using nf-core bactmap pipeline version 1.0.0 [29]. A pseudogenome was created for each sample and psedogenomes pairs were compared to estimate the SNP distance between them (SNP-dists version 0.8.2 [30]). The pseudogenome was constructed from the alignment with each sample having a base relative to the reference sequence but missing bases or low quality bases being encoded using “–“ and “N”. This allowed reporting of the percentage of the genome covered and reads mapped to the reference genome.

The bactmap pipeline involved trimming and removing adapters (fastp version [31]) and downsampling to 100x coverage (rasusa version 0.3.0 [32].) The processed reads were mapped to a reference (bwa mem version 0.7.17 [33]) and variants called and filtered (bcf tools version 1.11 [34].) The filtering conditions for low quality were '%QUAL<25 || FORMAT/DP<10 || MAX(FORMAT/ADF)<2 || MAX(FORMAT/ADR)<2 || MAX(FORMAT/AD)/SUM(FORMAT/DP)<0.9 || MQ<30 || MQ0F>0.1'.

The reference genomes used were: K. aerogenes ∗002b: SRR3291408, E. coli ST131: NZ_CP063777.1 Escherichia coli O25b:H4-ST131, K. pneumoniae ST86: NZ_CP026586.1 Klebsiella pneumoniae NUHL30457, K. Pneumoniae ST412: FAHZZU2591, S. marcescens: NZ_HG326223.1 Serratia marcescens Db11.

Use of publicly available interfaces

Fasta files were privately uploaded to the web-based interface tool, Pathogenwatch [35], which confirmed organism identification and ST. We sought correlation of AMR profiles, including ARG detection and inferred sensitivities for Klebsiella pneumoniae, and plasmid Inc types for E. coli. Paired fastq files were privately uploaded to the web-based interface tool, EnteroBase [36], and E. coli isolate genomes were assembled, identification confirmed and ST and serotype predicted. The ResFinder platform [37,38] was used to ascertain acquired and chromosomal ARG presence and inferred antimicrobial resistances from fasta files. The WGS reads for 41 bacterial isolates are deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject accession PRJNA1209941.

SNP distance value reference range selection

SNP distance values (between paired isolates) suggestive of recent patient-to-patient transmission are ≤ 10–18 for K. pneumonia [39,40], <10 for E. Coli [41], and ≤6 for K.aerogenes [42]. These reference ranges, based on literature, were selected to suggest transmission events on our unit.

Results

Initial observations

There were 60 BSI episodes with Gram-negative bacteria in 37 patients in the Royal Papworth ICU during the COVID-19 pandemic between 13/04/2020 and 25/12/2021. We successfully located and revived 41 frozen organisms for whole genome sequencing. These 41 isolates included 10 K. pneumoniae, 1 K. quasi-pneumoniae, 5 Klebsiella aerogenes, 7 Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, 5 Serratia marcescens, 4 Enterobacter bugandensis, 4, E. coli, 3 Pseudomonas aeruginosa, 1 Pseudomonas monteilii, and 1 Morganella morganii. During the pandemic, there was an average incidence of 3.2 cases of Gram-negative BSI per month, with 42% of these due to Klebsiella spp.; 25/60 over 19 months. The incidence in 2019 was lower, 1.5 cases of Gram-negative BSI per month, with 27% of these caused by Klebsiella spp.; 5/18 over 12 months.

We found that 4/20 (20%) cases of BSI caused by E. coli and Klebsiella spp carried either an AmpC or an ESBL. We found evidence of antimicrobial resistance carried by IMP-1 (metallo-carbapenemase gene) by P. monteilii isolate, detected by PCR (Table 2). We identified a total of 173 (chromosomal and acquired) ARG in 41 organisms causing BSI in this collection of organisms. Bacterial genotyping

There was no evidence of transmission between patients of BSI caused by the single isolates of K. quasipneumoniae (ST 1473), Enterobacter bugandensis, Morganella morganii or Pseudomonas monteilli. There was no genetic evidence of transmission between patients of BSI caused by Pseudomonas aeruginosa as these organisms were distantly related (ST253, 480, 235). There was additionally no evidence of transmission between the five patients with the seven BSIs caused by Stenotrophomonas maltophilia as they were distantly related, as determined by their different genotypes (ST511, ST1001, ∗4272, ∗26de, and ∗76e5).

The resulting ST suggested potential transmission between patients with E. coli, K. aerogenes and K. pneumonia. Of the 4 E. coli BSI, 3 isolates were ST131 and a single isolate was ST1193. Serotyping and Plasmid typing suggested a high degree of similarity between sample 31 and 51: O25:H4 serotype and IncFII/IncFIA Inc types. Of the 5 K. aerogenes BSI, 2 were ST ∗f588, which were from the same patient (sample 44 and 46), and 2 were ST ∗002b from individual patients (sample 37 and 22).

Hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae

Half (5/10) K. pneumoniae isolates were hypervirulent: 2 ST86 isolates (sample 45 and 42) and 3 ST412 isolates (sample 21, 35, and 52) and had virulence scores of ≥3 according to Kleborate platform [43]. All hypervirulent isolates had K2 or K57 capsule type and the presence of iuc, iro, rmp genes encoding for aerobactin, salmochelin (which allow increased siderophore production despite iron poor environments) and Rmp (associated with hyper-mucoidity), respectively. These 5 isolates were hypermucoid and demonstrated a positive string test: There was a viscous string >5mm long when loop touched against cultured bacterial colonies.

Only 2/10 isolates, including those with variable virulence scores, carried the clb gene islands (responsible for producing colibactin, a toxic bacterial-produced metabolite that disrupts and damages human cells). While 4/10 isolates, including those with variable virulence scores, carried the yersinabactin gene cluster ybt, (carried on the integrative conjugative element) allowing the bacteria to scavage metals from the infected host [44].

Evidence for patient-to-patient transmission

We found no evidence to suggest the transmission of E. coli ST131 between the three patients: High cgSNP distance values and low FASTANI values indicated substantial differences in their bacterial genome structure. Similarly, we found no evidence of transmission of Serratia marcescens between the two patients. There is currently no recognized sequence typing system for Serratia marcescens and cgSNP values and FASTANI were solely used to compare bacterial genomic similarities (Table 4).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study found an increase of BSI caused by Gram negative bacteria during the Covid-19 pandemic (3.2 vs. 1.5 cases per month in 2019) and this was largely associated with an increase in K. pneumoniae BSI (42% vs 27% of Gram negative BSI in 2019.) We rationalise that 3/16 (19%) Klebsiella BSI were associated with transmission between patients: responsible organisms included hypervirulent K. pneumoniae ST412, hypervirulent K. pneumoniae ST86 and K. aerogenes ∗002b. Our work highlights the value of genome sequencing to additionally refute episodes of transmission: The three E. coli ST131 BSI were genetically unrelated despite comparable antibiograms, and instead may be locally circulating organisms. Reassuringly, we did not find a high frequency of ESBL or AmpC producing organisms or CRO associated with BSI on our unit, in-keeping with known heterogenicity in bacterial AMR composition worldwide during the Covid-19 pandemic [45].

The proportion of K. pneumoniae isolates with hypervirulence varies geographically and we note high prevalence (50%) at our centre. This is significantly increased compared to historical BSI isolates from the UK where hypervirulent K. pneumoniae accounted for 5.8% of BSI with this pathogen [46]. The frequency of hypervirulent K. pneumoniae in our location were comparable to those in China and may suggest this is an emerging problem in our unit [47]. Hypervirulence factors causing hypermucoidity can render usual infection control precautions in the ICU inadequate, necessitating enhanced measures (routine patient screening, strict isolation of positive cases, disinfection of medical staff in contact with positive patients, and disinfection of rooms then left unoccupied for two weeks post-patient-discharge [48]). Our 5 hypervirulent K. pneumoniae isolates had rmp gene detected and demonstrated hypermucoidity and positive string tests. We suggest this simple laboratory test may have utility as a form of surveillance, identifying patients that could benefit from enhanced cleaning to bedspace or infection control precautions.

Discussions with our unit's staff highlighted possible issues with personal protective equipment (PPE) that may have contributed to transmission events. Examples may have included a lack of changing of gloves between patients, with a preference for the use of alcohol gel over gloved hands, or a lack of changing of long-sleeved gowns between patients preventing effective upper-hand and wrist washing. It has previously been shown that staff uniforms, PPE and hands are known to transmit Gram-negative bacteria between patients [49] and can become quickly contaminated around bedspace areas [50]. Our hospital implemented WHO guidelines [51] for dealing with patients infected with SARS CoV-2, with staff using long-sleeved gowns (for sessional use) gloves, face shields and FFP3 masks or respirators. A switch to short-sleeved gowns facilitated improved hand hygiene and,when combined with enhanced cleaning, was shown to decrease K. pneumoniae infection and environmental contamination rates in a similar UK critical care unit during the Covid-19 pandemic [52]. Other recognised risks associated with transmission events include environmental contamination, associated with faecal incontinence [53], contamination of sinks and other wastewater reservoirs [54,55]. However, as we did not perform environmental sampling, we cannot comment on the relevance of these on our unit. The exact mechanism of transmission remains unconfirmed in our patient cohort.

We hypothesize our patients were colonization with hypervirulent K. pneumoniae rapidly after exposure, as the bacteria entered the body through breeches in skin and mucus membranes, aided by bacterial biofilms on invasive devices (urinary catheters, ventilator tubing or central venous catheters) before populating the airway, gut or urinary tract. Individual patient factors (such as the reduction of immunosuppression or removal of invasive devices) may be protective and allow control of colonization and reduce risk of progression to invasive blood stream infection. Indeed, a 2-week interval was demonstrated between blood stream infection with K. pneumoniae ST 86 in patient 42 to 45, whilst a 2-month interval was demonstrated between blood stream infections with K. pneumoniae ST 412 in patient 35 to 21. Demonstration of colonization prior to blood stream infection was outside of the remit of this audit. Future research is required to demonstrate temporal relation between colonization with hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (using faecal, sputum or biofilm culture) and first blood stream infection, whilst considering potential confounding factors (such as severe immunosuppression or multiple invasive devices) in this patient cohort.

In conclusion, this retrospective cohort study of gram-negative BSI demonstrates three episodes of transmission (2 K. pneumoniae, 1 K. aerogenes) between ICU patients, during the Covid-19 pandemic. Although these organisms were not MDR, they did cause severe, disseminated disease, which is a hallmark of infection with hypervirulent Klebsiella species. There were multiple opportunities for transmission between patients and risk factors may include shared equipment, environmental and PPE contamination, and failure of effective hand hygiene. With COVID-19 infection endemic, when bed occupancy rates are critical, SARS-CoV-2 patients may require cohort nursing in bays. If sessional use gowns are required we recommend these are short-sleeved, to facilitate effective hand hygiene. We argue effective hand hygiene, alongside enhanced cleaning, is critical to reducing transmission of hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae between patients and will reduce BSI incidence. Finally, we recommend consideration of surveillance sampling (sequencing of hypermucoid isolates) in ICU settings for this emerging pathogen.
