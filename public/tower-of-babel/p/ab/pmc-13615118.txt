Community Pharmacist Preferences for Providing a Dose Administration Aid Service in Australia: A Discrete Choice Experiment
Authors: Rakhee Raghunandan, Parisa Aslani, Timothy F Chen, Sarah Norris, Andrea Torres-Robles, Noriko Sato, Kirsten Howard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDose administration aids (DAAs) support medication taking, yet little is known about how pharmacists perceive DAA benefits and challenges. This study aimed to explore community pharmacists’ preferences for attributes of a DAA service and how these influence their decision to provide this service.MethodsA discrete choice experiment (DCE) was conducted to elicit pharmacists’ preferences. Participants completed 12 choice questions, each presenting three alternatives: two DAA services and no DAA service. The questions included seven attributes: patient type, patient control over their medicines, impact on patient medication adherence, staff time, patient co-payment, government reimbursement fee and presence of a cap on government funded DAA services per pharmacy.ResultsA total of 615 community pharmacists completed the DCE survey. All attributes, except patient type and co-payment, significantly influenced preferences. Pharmacists were more likely to provide a DAA service (compared with no DAA service) when it was associated with higher government funding (pharmacy owners: OR 1.054, 95% CI 1.046–1.063, p < 0.001; non-owners: OR 1.041, 95% CI 1.036–1.046, p < 0.001). Pharmacists were less likely to provide a DAA service if there was a cap on the number of funded DAA services (OR 0.905, 95% CI 0.862–0.950, p < 0.001), or additional staff time was required (OR 0.981, 95% CI 0.976–0.986, p < 0.001).ConclusionThis DCE identified two key policy-related barriers to pharmacist provision of DAA services: inadequate government reimbursement and caps on the number of funded DAA services. These findings highlight community pharmacists’ preferences and trade-offs, offering valuable guidance to inform future redesign and implementation of more sustainable and equitable DAA services provided via community pharmacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40273-026-01632-1.

Key Points for Decision Makers

This discrete choice experiment demonstrated that all attributes, except patient type and co-payment, significantly influenced community pharmacists’ preferences for a dose administration aid (DAA) service.Avoiding a restriction on the number of funded DAA service provisions was the most valued attribute for pharmacists.The study provides insights into pharmacists’ preferences and trade-offs, which can guide sustainable implementation of pharmacy-provisioned DAA services.

Introduction

An estimated 38% of Australians have two or more chronic conditions [1], with the presence of multimorbidity increasing the complexity of therapeutic management. The use of multiple medicines can present challenges for patients, and when patients do not or cannot adhere to their medicine regimens, this can adversely impact on clinical outcomes for the patient and the health system. Factors such as forgetfulness, physical problems or lack of understanding of the condition or medicines may influence patients’ adherence to their therapy [2]. Pharmacists are part of the multidisciplinary team of health professionals who play an important role in supporting medication adherence, which is defined by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as the extent to which a person’s behaviour in taking their medication corresponds with agreed recommendations from a healthcare provider [3]. There is evidence of the positive impact of community pharmacists’ interventions on improving medication adherence and patient clinical outcomes in chronic conditions such as hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and asthma [4].

One of the most common strategies used by community pharmacists to support medication adherence in polypharmacy is the use of dose administration aids (DAAs) [5–7]. DAAs are sealed devices or packaging systems that allow the organisation of doses of medicines according to their time of administration, thereby providing patients with the correct medicine to be taken at the correct time and dose [8]. There are different types of DAAs, including compartmentalised plastic boxes, blister or bubble packs, sachet systems or automated medication dispensing devices [7, 9], which can be selected based on a patient’s needs. In Australia, community pharmacies provide patients with DAAs in the community and in aged care facilities to ensure the quality use of medicines and improve consumers’ adherence to their medicines. DAA services in Australia are funded by the government via the DAA programme [10, 11] or by patients themselves.

Studies have found a positive impact of DAA usage on medication adherence, a reduction in medication errors and a reduction in waste, as these devices may prevent the accumulation of medicines at patients’ homes by managing the packaging at the pharmacies [12]. Other reported benefits for patients using DAAs include the ability to manage their medicines, especially if polypharmacy or complex medicine regimens are present, improved medication adherence due to fewer doses being missed, and reduced patient stress [7, 13–15]. However, DAA usage also has drawbacks. For patients, this includes a loss of knowledge and control over their medicines [7, 15, 16]. Challenges for pharmacists and pharmacies to provide a DAA service relate to the process and workload involved. These include the cost and time taken to set up and provide the DAA service, workforce capacity and staff training [17–19]. In addition, infrastructure requirements—such as the need for dedicated workspaces, automation or packing equipment (including dose‑packing robots) and sufficient physical space—can impose substantial upfront fixed costs. While higher service volumes over time can reduce the marginal cost per patient, these initial capital investments may limit uptake of DAA services among smaller pharmacies or those with smaller service volumes [20, 21]. However, there is limited quantitative evidence about how pharmacists value the benefits and drawbacks of DAAs, and the extent to which these considerations influence their preferences for engaging with and providing DAAs for their patients.

The primary aim of the study was to understand what community pharmacists’ preferences were for the different characteristics (attributes) of a DAA service, compared with no service, and how these attributes impacted on the pharmacist’s decision to provide a DAA service across different patient groups.

Methods

Study Design

Pharmacists’ preferences were elicited using a discrete choice experiment. Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are a way to quantitatively measure people’s preferences for products and services and are increasingly used in the healthcare context [22]. DCEs are grounded in economic theory (random utility theory [23] and Lancaster’s theory of consumer choice [24]) and ask survey respondents to compare and choose between two or more hypothetical products or services, each described by a set of characteristics (attributes)—in this case, providing a DAA service. Respondents complete this comparison and choice multiple times, each time with slight variations in the characteristics of the services being compared [25]. These multiple observations allow us to analyse which features of the service are driving pharmacists’ preferences when choosing to provide a DAA. This DCE has been developed and conducted following best practice standards [22, 25–28].

Study Participants

The DCE participants were community pharmacists practising in Australia. A study database of national pharmacy contact details was compiled from pharmacy contact details obtained from relevant Australian State and Territory pharmacy organisations as well as publicly available information. The link to the online DCE survey was emailed directly to the 5622 pharmacies within the study database with an invitation for community pharmacists to participate in the study, with reminders sent weekly. Data were collected during April—May 2024. The study was approved by the University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (reference 2024/011).

Attributes and Levels

An initial list of possible attributes and levels was informed by a scoping review of qualitative and preference studies on DAAs, and qualitative interviews with consumers and pharmacists. Further details of the qualitative interviews are available in the evaluation report [29]. A broader set of attributes was considered at this stage, including attributes related to additional education and support for DAA use, as well as a range of potential patient benefits associated with DAA provision. However, not all initially identified attributes could be included as the large number of candidate attributes would have compromised the cognitive feasibility of the choice questions and the practicality of the experimental design. The final set of attributes and levels for the pharmacist DCE was refined through iterative discussion within the research team, which included pharmacists with expert knowledge in medication adherence and pharmacy practice. This process prioritised attributes that (i) featured strongly and consistently in the qualitative interviews, (ii) reflected evidence‑based patient benefits, (iii) captured both service delivery processes and patient‑related factors and (iv) represented characteristics of existing DAA services that were potentially modifiable in practice or policy.

Seven attributes were used to describe the DAA service and no DAA service alternatives and are summarised in Table 1. The first attribute, patient type, was categorical and described four patient characteristics. A further categorical attribute specified whether there was a cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions per pharmacy per week. The remaining five attributes were continuous variables and included patient control over their medicine, impact of the DAA on medication adherence, staff time required to provide the DAA, patient co-payment per week, and the average government reimbursement received per DAA patient per week.

Experiment Design

Based on assumed prior parameter estimates and directions, a main effects multinominal logit (MNL) D-efficient DCE design was developed (d-error 0.003) with 72 questions, each presenting two labelled DAA service alternatives, and a third fixed ‘no DAA service’ alternative. The overall design was blocked into six blocks of 12 questions, and was generated using NGENE design software version 1.3.0 [30, 31]. Traditional power and sample size calculations do not apply to DCEs; instead, a proxy for minimum sample size (S-estimate) can be derived from the experimental design [26]. An S‑estimate derived from the D‑efficient design indicated that three respondents (across the full design) could be sufficient for full design parameter identification and is interpreted as a theoretical lower bound [26, 32]. Other sample size criteria could also be considered. Orme’s rule of thumb [33] suggests a minimum of 56 respondents for main‑effects estimation, while Lancsar and Louviere’s recommendation of at least 20 respondents per block [22] implies a minimum sample size of 120. The sample size achieved for the DCE was 615 respondents.

Internal validity checks were not incorporated in this DCE to minimise respondent burden, given that participants were completing 12 choice questions. In addition, there is debate in the literature regarding the reliability of internal validity checks for identifying ‘irrational’ choices within DCEs, and their inclusion may necessitate larger sample sizes as a result of increased statistical power requirements [34].

Survey Design

The electronic DCE questionnaire was administered as a self-complete online survey using the Qualtrics survey platform (version April 2024) [35]. Participants were randomly assigned one of the six blocks of choice questions as per the experiment design. Randomisation applied only to block assignment, and the order of choice questions within each of the six blocks was fixed. The DCE questionnaire included screening eligibility and consent questions, background information, explanation of the choice questions, definitions of attributes and levels, an example choice question, 12 choice questions, and sociodemographic questions related to the pharmacist and the primary community pharmacy that they worked at (see Electronic Supplementary Material [ESM] for an abridged version). An example choice scenario for the community pharmacists is presented in Fig. 1.

Example of a choice set from the DAA community pharmacist DCE

A convenience sample (n = 5) of pharmacists was engaged to pilot the online DCE survey and provide feedback on the comprehensiveness, clarity and format of the attributes and levels as well as the overall survey design. Feedback from pilot participants resulted in minor wording refinements to improve clarity, with no substantive changes to the attributes, levels, or experimental design required. The pilot sample size was considered adequate, as the piloting phase aimed to evaluate survey comprehension and task interpretation rather than to estimate preferences [22], and any key issues in interpretation would typically be identifiable with a small number of respondents representative of the target participant group.

Analysis

R version 4.3.3 was used to generate descriptive statistics and NLOGIT version 6 was used for the DCE analyses [36, 37]. Analyses were conducted using a mixed multinomial logit (MMNL) model using 10,000 Halton draws with a panel specification to account for correlated choices within an individual [38]. Initially, all attributes were specified as random and coded as linear variables (where relevant) with normal distributions, and costs were specified as truncated triangular distributions. Before finalising the mixed multinomial logit model, interactions between attributes themselves, and between attributes and pharmacist characteristics (including pharmacy ownership, postgraduate education, metropolitan or regional location using the Modified Monash Model 2019 categorisation, age, and sex) were also examined. Log-likelihood ratio tests were used to compare model iterations. Goodness of fit of the models were also examined using log-likelihood values, pseudo R2 and the adjusted AIC (Akaike information criteria).

Results are reported as β coefficients and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs); β parameters are also converted to odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% CIs. For parameters specified as random, the standard deviation and p-value of the standard deviation are also reported. An OR of > 1 indicates that providing the DAAs was more preferred (compared with no DAA) as the attribute level increased; an OR of < 1 indicates that providing DAAs was less preferred (compared with no DAA) as the attribute level increased.

Marginal rates of substitution between variables were also estimated. Trade-offs between attributes were calculated from individual-specific parameters from the mixed multinomial logit model as willingness to accept ($) for a change in the level of specific attributes, and benefit–harm trade-offs between attributes. Results

Study Participants

Of the 726 pharmacists who accessed the anonymised survey link, 615 respondents were eligible (i.e. pharmacists practising in a community pharmacy in Australia) and provided responses, resulting in a total of 7,380 observations for analysis (Fig. 2).

Community pharmacist DCE—participant response flow chart

Table 2 provides a summary of the characteristics of the 615 community pharmacist respondents. Respondents had a mean age of 38 years (SD 10 years) with a similar gender distribution (female 48%, male 49%). Of the 615 pharmacists, 192 (31%) were pharmacy owners. Respondents were working in community pharmacies from across Australia, with the majority working in metropolitan areas (72%). Of the primary community pharmacies that pharmacist respondents worked at, the majority provided DAAs to community patients (98%), and the blister packing system was the most utilised type of DAA (96%). Pharmacists were involved in all activities related to DAA provision, but less so with preparing (61%) and delivering the DAAs (33%). Based on workforce registration data of all registered pharmacists as of 30 June 2023, our sample of community pharmacists generally represented registered pharmacists within Australia by age, state and geographic location [39, 40], although our sample included an over-representation of pharmacists from NSW (39% cf. 29%) and male pharmacists (49% cf. 36%) [39, 40].

In the final model specification, patient type and having a cap on the number of DAA provisions per week are specified as effects-coded categorical variables, with reference levels defined as older patients aged ≥ 65 years and no cap on funded DAA service provisions per week, respectively; all other attributes are modelled as linear parameters. All variables in the final MMNL model were specified as random parameters with a normal distribution, except for the cost attributes (patient co-payment, and government fee). The cost variables were specified as random parameters with truncated triangular distributions.

The following dummy-coded sociodemographic characteristics were included in the final MMNL model as they improved the model fit: metropolitan pharmacy location (vs regional/remote location), pharmacy owners (vs non-owners), females (vs males), postgraduate education (vs undergraduate education), and age (as a continuous variable). Owners (vs non-owners) were interacted with the fee attribute to explore whether there were any differences in the value attached to the government fee based on employment (ownership) status. Employment status (owners vs non-owners) was also included as a separate sociodemographic variable to explore whether there were any subgroup differences in underlying preference for the DAA service itself. Observations with missing sociodemographic data were excluded from the analyses, resulting in 732 observations being omitted from the final MMNL model estimation. In a separate model, the average government fee per patient per week attribute was interacted with both pharmacy employment status (owners vs non-owners) and with pharmacy geographical location (metropolitan vs regional/remote location) to examine the value attached to the fee attribute by owners and non-owners depending on whether they were in a metropolitan or regional/remote area, but this specification did not improve the model fit and was dropped. The final MMNL model (Table 3) had a good model fit (adjusted AIC = 1.315, log-likelihood = − 4341.755 and pseudo R2 = 0.405, which is approximately equivalent to an R2 of 0.80 in a linear regression model) [38]. Only five out of the seven attributes significantly influenced pharmacists’ preferences for choosing to provide a DAA service; patient type and level of patient co-payment did not significantly influence pharmacists’ preferences for providing a DAA. Attributes also behaved in the a priori expected directions, for example, as the average government fee per week increased, there was a higher preference for the DAA service alternative; and for each additional patient who had a decline in control over their medicines, there was a lower preference for the DAA service alternative.

The statistically significant alternative-specific constant (p = 0.011) for providing the DAA service (compared with no DAA service) indicates that there is an underlying pharmacist preference for providing a DAA service, all else being equal. As the risk of a patient experiencing a decline in control over their medicines while using a DAA increased, the preference for providing a DAA service decreased. Each additional patient (out of 100, i.e. a 1% increase) who experienced a decline in control over their medicines while using a DAA significantly decreased the preference for pharmacists providing a DAA service (OR 0.994, 95% CI 0.989–0.998, p = 0.007). For each additional patient (out of 100) whose medication adherence improved while using a DAA, pharmacists were more likely to provide a DAA service (OR 1.036, 95% CI 1.032–1.039, p < 0.001). Pharmacists placed a negative value on the additional staff time required to provide the DAA service. For each additional minute of staff time required per patient per week to provide a DAA, pharmacists were less likely to prefer the DAA service (OR 0.981, 95% CI 0.976–0.986, p < 0.001).

The level of patient co-payment per week for a DAA service did not significantly influence pharmacists’ preference for providing a DAA service (p = 0.642). Pharmacists had a significantly negative preference for a cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions that pharmacies can provide (compared with no cap) and were less likely to provide a DAA service when this constraint was present (OR 0.905, 95% CI 0.862–0.950, p < 0.001). Pharmacists were more likely to prefer a DAA service (compared with no DAA service) as the government fee for DAAs increased, with small differences between pharmacy owners (OR 1.054, 95% CI 1.046–1.063, p < 0.001) and pharmacy non-owners (OR 1.041, 95% CI 1.036–1.046, p < 0.001).

The sociodemographic characteristics of the pharmacists (i.e. metropolitan pharmacy location vs regional/remote, pharmacy owners vs non-owners, females vs males, postgraduate education vs undergraduate, and for each additional year of age) that were included in the final model did not significantly influence preferences for providing a DAA service (compared with no DAA service). However, they were retained in the final MMNL model as they improved the overall model fit. Standard deviations for random parameters suggested preference heterogeneity for all attributes except patient co-payment. There was also some suggestion of preference heterogeneity in respondents with postgraduate education and increasing age.

Trade-Offs

Trade-offs were estimated between the government fee received by the pharmacy per patient per week and other attributes to calculate willingness-to-accept estimates. These are expressed as pharmacists’ willingness to accept a reduction in the fee received to achieve specific characteristics within the DAA service (Table 4). Willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay estimates provide a useful way to compare the relative value attached to changes in the different attributes, as they are presented on the same scale (government reimbursement fee for DAA) and are directly comparable, unlike raw beta coefficients.

Willingness-to-accept calculations for all the statistically significant attributes resulted in a willingness to accept a reduction in the weekly government fee per patient received by the pharmacy for both pharmacy owners and non-owners. Overall, pharmacy owners were willing to accept a smaller reduction in the weekly government fee per patient per week for all attributes compared with the non-owners, as expected a priori.

The least valued attribute overall for all respondents was the risk of decline in medicine control for patients, which is consistent with findings of the companion consumer DCE [29]. In contrast, reductions in staff time required for DAA provision and improvements in patient medication adherence were more valued, with pharmacists willing to accept larger reductions in the government fee to achieve these outcomes. Avoiding a cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions per week was the most valued DAA service characteristic for pharmacists. Across the WTA calculations, pharmacy owners were willing to accept smaller fee reductions than non‑owners, suggesting systematic differences in valuation between the two groups (Table 4).

Benefit–harm trade-offs were also calculated, including willingness to accept additional harms (in terms of extra patients experiencing a decline in medicines control, i.e. loss of autonomy over their medicines). Pharmacists were willing to accept an additional 5.4 patients per 100 experiencing a decline in their medicine control (a 5.4% risk increase in loss of autonomy over their medicines) to achieve an extra patient per 100 with improved medication adherence (a 1% improvement in medication adherence). Similarly, pharmacists were willing to spend an additional 1.84 minutes of staff time per patient per week, to achieve an extra patient per 100 with improved medication adherence.

Discussion

This DCE study quantified community pharmacists’ preferences for providing a DAA service. It demonstrated that community pharmacists have an underlying preference for the availability of a DAA service and that they see value in providing this service to patients. This is not unexpected as pharmacists regularly provide a DAA service as part of their usual scope of practice. The Australian government also recognises the value of a DAA service and funds the national DAA programme to improve medication adherence and management; however, the funding is constrained to a capped number of DAA services per pharmacy. Other studies using different methodologies have also reported the value associated with patients using DAAs, including improved medication adherence and clinical outcomes for patients, and for pharmacists, more opportunities to enhance medicine management and adherence for patients, and to collaborate with other health professionals to facilitate a more integrated approach to patient care [4, 16, 19, 41–43].

The effects of using a DAA for patients were significantly valued by pharmacists when deciding whether to provide a DAA service or not. Unsurprisingly, pharmacists were less likely to choose to provide a DAA service as the number of people experiencing a decline in their control over their medicines increased, and they were more likely to choose to provide a DAA service as the number of people experiencing improved medication adherence increased. Pharmacists were also willing to trade-off between these harms and benefits, especially when the DAA service included improved medication adherence. Higher medication adherence is associated with improved patient outcomes and reduced healthcare utilisation, including lower mortality rates [44, 45] and fewer hospital admissions [44, 46, 47]. Within this context of DAA service provision, pharmacists may have prioritised the benefits of improved adherence and its potential to mitigate future health deterioration and reduce demand on health services, while viewing associated harms—such as reduced patient autonomy arising from medicines being pre‑packed and dosing decisions being more tightly structured—as acceptable or justified in achieving these outcomes.

Characteristics of a DAA service were also important to pharmacists when deciding to provide a DAA service. The pharmacist DCE included the current government reimbursement fee of A$6.17 per patient per week [10, 11] and for every extra dollar increase in this cost attribute, pharmacists were more likely to choose to provide a DAA service. This finding, in addition to the negative value placed on the staff time required to provide a DAA service, suggests that community pharmacists see the current government reimbursement fee as being too low and that the provision of the DAA service by pharmacists is currently perceived to be undervalued by the government. It is possible that pharmacists felt that the current government remuneration does not reflect the high cost of providing a DAA service, including the infrastructure set up, space requirements, ongoing costs, personnel, and their own time. However, pharmacists did not appear to see charging patients out-of-pocket costs as a viable solution to overcome this undervaluation of the DAA service, as charging a patient co-payment did not significantly influence pharmacists’ preferences for deciding to provide a DAA service or not.

Removing the current constraint of a cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions was the most valued attribute in this DCE for the pharmacists. Both pharmacy owners and non-owners viewed this attribute as a barrier to providing a DAA service and were willing to accept significant reductions in the government fee to avoid this constraint within a DAA service. As previously noted, differences between pharmacy owners and non-owners for some of the reimbursement-related outcomes were not unexpected. Pharmacy owners were slightly more likely than non-owners to prefer providing a DAA as the government reimbursement fee increased, and were willing to accept smaller reductions in the government fee than non-owners to avoid a cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions that pharmacies can provide. These findings reflect the fiscal responsibilities that pharmacy owners bear in addition to the clinical responsibilities of pharmacy practice, which is similar to other healthcare providers operating under fee-for-service models who have to balance financial viability with being available to provide clinical services for the community. The current cap on funded DAA services per week was increased in the 8th Community Pharmacy Agreement from 60 to 90 DAA services per week, effective July 2024 [48]. While this change enables more DAA services to be provided by pharmacists, the findings from this DCE suggest that having a cap of any capacity on the provision of funded DAA services would hinder community pharmacists’ decision and ability to provide DAA services to patients.

A recent companion DCE suggests that consumers (i.e. patients and carers) in Australia see value in the DAA service and support the continued provision of a funded DAA service by community pharmacists in Australia [29]. Therefore, it is important to ensure that community pharmacists remain willing and engaged in the provision of DAA services to patients. Collectively, these results suggest that the current government reimbursement fee for the DAA programme and cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions provided by a pharmacy are two programme aspects that are potentially modifiable to improve pharmacists’ willingness to provide DAA services, despite the extra staff time that would be required, thereby enhancing equitable access to these services for consumers. Increasing the weekly government fee for the funded DAA programme and/or removing the cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions for the pharmacy would increase the likelihood of pharmacists (both pharmacy owners and non-owners) choosing to provide a DAA service. Higher out-of-pocket costs for the consumer were not a facilitator for pharmacists choosing to provide a DAA service. While increasing consumer co-payments may offer a mechanism to enhance pharmacy remuneration, this approach does not appear to be a preferred solution by either pharmacists or consumers [29]. Pharmacists recognise the negative impact of increased patient out-of-pocket costs, including reduced DAA service uptake and the potential for harmful flow-on effects on the health outcomes of patients. The companion consumer DCE also highlighted that patients and carers had different priorities for some aspects of the DAA service, and having a doctor discuss initiation of the DAA was the most important attribute for them [29]. The observed similarities and differences in preferences between pharmacists, patients and carers highlight the importance of eliciting preferences from relevant stakeholders when evaluating health service provision and utilisation.

Strengths and Limitations

Being the first DCE in this context, this study provides a reference point for Australian community pharmacists’ preferences for supplying a DAA service. DCEs are a robust quantitative method to elicit preferences, and most of the attributes in this DCE study were statistically significant in predicting the choice to provide a DAA service. The study followed established best practice standards for conducting and analysing DCEs. The DCE had a relatively robust sample size (n = 615) considering that a recent systematic review of health-related DCEs reported a median sample size of 394 respondents (interquartile range width = 731) [49].

Our recruitment strategy, with respondents opting in to complete the DCE, may suggest that our results are not completely generalisable to all community pharmacists in Australia. However, the pharmacist respondents were diverse and generally representative by age, state, and geographical location [39, 40]. Although the DCE method is robust, its results inherently only capture preferences for attributes and levels that were included. While our attribute definition process was robust, we acknowledge that preferences may have differed if different attributes and levels had been included.

Conclusion

It is desirable for community pharmacists to continue providing a funded DAA service via community pharmacy, as patients, carers, and pharmacists attach significant value to the provision of this service to the Australian community. This DCE study has elicited community pharmacists’ preferences and trade-offs, which can help guide the implementation and delivery of pharmacist-provisioned DAA services. It has identified and quantified the two most significant barriers to community pharmacists wanting to provide a DAA service, which are the lack of adequate government reimbursement and the current constraint of a cap on the number of funded DAA service provisions by the pharmacy. It is useful for policy makers and funders to recognise that these are two aspects of the current DAA service that are potentially modifiable. The quantitative nature of the DCE outputs helps these decision makers to understand how the identified barriers can be modified to optimise the likelihood of pharmacists engaging and providing a DAA service that is sustainable, equitable to access, and improves health outcomes for consumers. The findings from this DCE and the companion consumer DCE [29] highlight the necessity of considering the similarities and differences in preferences of the relevant stakeholders in any redesign of the funded DAA programme, if service uptake and programme impact are to be maximised.
