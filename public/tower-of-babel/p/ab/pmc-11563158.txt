Band adhesions in a virgin abdomen: A case report
Authors: Olivier Iryivuze, Yasa Abdullahi Mohamed, Yusuf Mohamud Haile
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSmall bowel obstruction secondary to adhesions in a virgin abdomen is encountered very rarely. Its rarity may cause a delay in diagnosis that can lead to complications among which is sepsis and death. In contrast, On-time diagnosis constitutes one of many pillars of the best outcome for the patient.Case presentationWe present a case of a 46-year-old female exhibiting symptoms of small bowel obstruction that persisted for five days. Her previous surgical history was negative. Introduction

Intestinal obstruction is a common surgical emergency, accounting for one-fifth of admitted patients with acute abdominal pain [1]. Small bowel obstruction (SBO) accounts for 12–16 % of emergency surgical admissions and 20 % of procedures, with adhesions being the most common cause [2]. Four-fifths of patients that develop small bowel obstruction secondary to adhesions have undergone an abdominal surgery in the past [3]. SBO caused by congenital band adhesions is rare but can lead to severe and life-threatening complications.

We report a case of a 46-year-old patient who had no prior surgical history and presented with acute intestinal obstruction due to band adhesions. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE guidelines [4].

Case presentation

We present a case of a 46-year-old married African woman who presented to the accident and emergency (A&E) department of our rural hospital with a history of constipation for five days that led to an abdominal distention for 48 h before admission.

She was a known hypertensive patient on hypertension medications who presented with sudden onset of colicky abdominal pain, relieved by vomiting. Vomits were odorless, were not stained with blood, and contained food. The patient had failed to pass stools and flatus for five days and reported 3 episodes of vomiting in the 24 h before admission. She reported no similar history among family members but reported a previous history of constipation.

Her pulse was 110 beats per minute, regular, with normal volume, synchronous, and no radio femoral delay other peripheral pulses were intact, and the respiratory rate was 22 cycles per minute, others vitals were within normal range.

The abdomen was grossly and symmetrically distended, moving with respiration, the umbilicus was centrally located and inverted, with no visible peristalsis, visible pulsations, scar or previous operations, striae, or prominent veins. The abdomen was soft with no palpable mass. The digital rectal examination revealed an empty rectum. The bowel sounds were absent. The random blood sugar level was 104 mg/dl, and the full blood count revealed a neutrophilic leukocytosis (WBC- 14.09 × 109/l [4.0–12.0], Neutrophils 8.22 × 109/l [2.0–7.5]. An erect plain abdominal x-ray showed dilated bowel loops with multiple air-fluid levels with no air under the diaphragm (Fig. 1). Ultrasound scan was non functional at that moment and the hospital lacked sophisticated imaging modalities such as computed tomography (CT) scan and Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI).Fig. 1Anteroposterior erect abdominal x-ray.Fig. 1

Anteroposterior erect abdominal x-ray.

After initial resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics administration, and nasogastric tube insertion, an emergency exploratory laparotomy was done with findings (Fig. 2) of three bands around the small bowel (Fig. 2B, C, D), at the jejunoileal level, being a barrier to the small bowel content; and other multiple adhesions between the walls of the intestines. We did adhesiolysis and resection of 8 cm of the small bowel comprising parts of the distal jejunum and the proximal ileum. We did as well an end-to-end anastomosis due to one of the adhesions that could not be adhesiolysed without injuring the bowel. The patient recovered uneventfully.Fig. 2Intraoperative findings, Fig. 2A showing the overview of the findings, Fig. 2B, C, D showing respectively the three band adhesions causing the obstruction of the small bowel.Fig. 2

Discussion

After abdominal surgery, the normal wound healing may end with adhesion formation and this last is the main cause of small bowel obstruction in developed countries [1]. Despite the theory of adhesions after surgery i.e. acquired, some do present SBO secondary to adhesions without undergoing any surgery and these last are called congenital band adhesions [2]. Adhesions, malignancy, foreign body, volvulus and internal hernia are the leading causes of small bowel obstruction in the absentia of previous abdominal surgery [5]. Adhesions can be solitary or matted with congenital adhesions mostly being solitary and acquired being more likely matted [6] different from our findings in this case where we found matted adhesions in a virgin abdomen. Congenital adhesions are adhesions that form without any history of abdominal surgery and they might result from an abdominal inflammatory process that went incognito [7] or as remnants of physiological organogenesis [8,9], however we were not able to identify what would have caused the congenital adhesions in our patient.

The clinical picture of SBO due to congenital adhesions does not differ from SBO caused by acquired adhesions and is composed of abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and constipation. The physician ought to be alert and seek for other causes of SBO during the physical examination such as abdominal wall hernia groin hernia and search for signs of bowel ischemia and peritonitis. [7] Leukocytosis with left shift and increased levels of lactate and C-reactive protein (CRP) may mimic peritonitis and bowel ischemia, but their absence does not exclude ischemia [10].

The imaging investigations for SBO are diverse with different authors having different views on their use. The starting imaging investigation in patients with SBO in Low and Middle Income Countries (LMICs) is the abdominal radiography, with supine abdominal radiography able to detect bowel loop dilatation and the presence of fluid without gas in the small bowel loops whereas the upright radiographs detect air-fluid levels [11], sensitivity and specificity of plain X- rays for SBO range between 60 and 70 %, but it lacks sensitivity and specificity in identifying the cause of the SBO or its main complication, namely, strangulation, the reason why the Bologna guidelines [12] consider it to be an investigation of limited value and does not recommended it as part of the work-up of suspected SBO.

Abdominal ultrasound scan (AUSS) is good at identifying dilated bowel loops but fails to detect the location of the adhesion and to help differentiate precisely among potential causes of obstruction. Ultrasound Scan (USS) can be used as the first-line imaging study in patients with SBO where the CT is absent or contraindicated. In adults, the USS is used in pregnant women to prevent radiation exposure to the fetus [11].

Computed Tomography (CT) scan has become the imaging modality in patients with SBO, as recommended by the American College of Radiology (ACR) appropriateness criteria and Bologna guidelines and can identify the transition zone and signs of closed-loop mechanism [[13], [14], [15], [16]] and CT has a high sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing SBO in comparison with abdominal x-ray (91%and 89 % respectively) [17]. Magnetic resonance Imaging (MRI) has a 95 % sensitivity and 100 % specificity in the detection of SBO [18] and can as well determine the site and cause of obstruction [19]. The presence of bowel ischemia dictates the decision of surgery, however, it cannot be predicted using clinical signs and symptoms alone, the reason for doing a multidetector CT scan.

In addition to the Bologna guidelines [12], the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma recommends a nonoperative treatment as management of adhesive SBO, when appropriate, instead of routine immediate surgery [20,21]. The non-operative management comprises bowel decompression, fluid resuscitation, and water-soluble contrast agents as per the Bologna guidelines [12,22]. It is safe and efficacious in 70 % of SBOs caused by adhesions [12] and its success depends on the use of water-soluble contrast agents with 17 % failure whereas nonuse of water-soluble contrast agents resulted in 50 % failure [23], however, conservative management, compared with surgery, is associated with higher recurrence rates and lower disease-free intervals [24]. There is a success rate of 95.6 % and a low morbidity rate of 3.1 % when nonoperative management was considered and 88.6 % success and 26 % morbidity in the operative group [5].

A surgical approach is considered in case of a non-resolving obstruction. An emergency operation should be considered in the presence of peritonitis, strangulation, and ischemia. Even though some consider a nonoperative approach as the starting point, others consider that exploratory laparotomy is still mandatory since SBO in a virgin abdomen can be caused by other etiologies such as malignancy, internal hernia, and bezoars as the most prominent causes [25]. The current trending option for surgery these days i.e. laparoscopy has been considered, however, a higher rate of negative explorations was reported following laparoscopy compared to open surgery [3], but negative finding can also reach 40 % with open surgery.

In rural hospitals of LMICs, plain radiography and ultrasound are the available imaging and their combination with the presence of signs and symptoms of deterioration of the condition of the patient ought to determine the following decision. In cases of non-resolving SBO or the presence of signs and symptoms of peritonitis and bowel ischemia, exploratory laparotomy is the ideal treatment for those patients with a subsequent finding of the real etiology of small bowel obstruction.

Conclusion

A negative surgical history should not annihilate the suspicion of band adhesions in small bowel obstruction. Early diagnostic imaging is helpful. When this is not possible, a laparotomy should be done if clinically indicated. The patient had an uneventful recovery.
