Fracture of Dual-Mobility Polyethylene Liner in a Primary Total Hip Arthroplasty: A New Complication to a Modern Design: A Case Report
Authors: Evan P. Bailey, Bryce T. Hrudka, Bailey J. Ross, Ajay Premkumar, Jacob M. Wilson, Galen E. Berdis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We present the first documented case of polyethylene liner fracture in a primary dual-mobility cup (DMC) total hip arthroplasty leading to intraprosthetic dislocation. A 50-year-old female with a history of hip dysplasia and leg length discrepancy underwent primary total hip arthroplasty with a DMC. She presented with an anterior intraprosthetic hip dislocation. Revision surgery revealed a fractured highly cross-linked polyethylene liner. The liner and fragments were removed and a new liner was implanted. This case underscores the potential mechanical limitations of highly cross-linked polyethylene in DMC configurations.

Introduction

Highly cross-linked polyethylene (XLPE) liners, introduced in the early 2000s, have revolutionized total hip arthroplasty (THA) by significantly reducing wear, osteolysis, and aseptic loosening [1]. This advancement has established XLPE as the gold standard liner option in primary THA. These liners have led to improved implant longevity compared to ultra-high-molecular weight polyethylene (PE) liners [2]. Despite their success as liners in standard THA, the use of XLPE liners in dual-mobility designs (DMC) is a relatively recent application. DMCs, designed to mitigate dislocation risks, traditionally employed conventional ultra-high-molecular weight PE due to its proven track record and relatively lower fragility [3]. However, more recently, many companies have begun to utilize XLPE liners for their dual mobility systems.

The transition to using XLPE in DMC systems was driven by its successful application in reducing long-term complications associated with PE wear in THA and its increasing usage in total knee arthroplasty [1]. However, concerns exist regarding the mechanical demands and unique articulations in DMC systems combined with XLPE decreased ability to resist crack inception [4].

Herein, we present a case involving a 50-year-old female with a history of left THA, with a modern DMC presenting with an anterior left intraprosthetic hip dislocation. During revision surgery, it was found that the XLPE liner was fractured. This highlights a mechanical limitation of XLPE in DMC configurations and the importance of thorough material evaluation when changes are made to new systems.

Case history

The patient is a 50-year-old female with a past medical history significant for anemia and chronic kidney disease. She had a complex history with regards to her left hip including hip dysplasia, coxa vara, and a resultant 3-cm leg length discrepancy (Fig. 1). Her body mass index was 24.49 at the time of surgery. She was treated with a direct anterior left THA using a DMC with features as follows: 44-mm Zimmer G7 OsseoTi® multiholed acetabular cup (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN), a 32-mm dual-mobility liner (22 mm + 0 CoCr inner head), 15-mm Zimmer Wagner cone stem with a 125-neck angle (Fig. 2). Her initial recovery was uneventful except for some minor iliotibial (IT) band pain as expected due to her congenitally shortened abductor tendon. A year and a half after THA, the patient began to experience mechanical symptoms of locking, catching and subjective instability. Initial radiographs at that time were unrevealing. The symptoms were most noticeable when doing low-impact exercises like yoga or flat-level walking. Her pain was so severe it affected her ability to work her desk job. The patient was worked up for infection with inflammatory labs and aspiration which were negative. Greater trochanteric bursa and iliopsoas injections provided minimal relief. Advanced imaging including computed tomography (CT) was obtained and did not reveal any evidence of implant loosening or mechanical failure. Three months later, she presented to the emergency department with severe pain and inability to bear weight on her left leg after performing yoga exercises. The patient denied any history of trauma. Initial examination in the emergency department revealed the left leg to be shortened and externally rotated. Plain radiographs were taken which demonstrated an anterior dislocation of the left THA with no PE halo surrounding the head ball or visualized in the surrounding soft tissues (Fig. 3). Closed reduction in the emergency department was unsuccessful. The patient ultimately decided to attempt a closed reduction in the operating room with potentially staged revision if unsuccessful due to lack of availability for necessary implants at initial presentation. Fluoroscopic imaging redemonstrated an anterior hip dislocation. During the procedure, eccentric reduction of the inner head ball into the acetabular component was obtained concerning for IPD (Fig. 4). Two days later, the patient underwent open reduction with modular exchange of head ball and liner via a direct anterior approach. During the approach, an abundance of reactive synovial tissue was present in the joint. Intraoperatively, the XLPE liner was found to be fractured but remained within the acetabular component (Fig. 6). The liner was removed along with multiple PE fragments. The acetabular component and metal mobility liner were stable and well-seated. A new 22 mm + 3, 12/14 taper inner head ball with an outer 32-mm shell was chosen to improve the patient’s leg length discrepancy and provide more stability. Intraoperative testing, including hip external rotation to 90° with maximum hip extension, and postoperative imaging revealed a stable, well-fixed reconstruction (Fig. 7).Figure 6Patient’s fractured XLPE insert.Figure 7AP pelvis status after revision THA. The patient's recovery involved active engagement in prescribed physical therapy and regular outpatient follow-ups to assess the stability of the implant. She has reported significant improvement in her IT band symptoms and resolution of the mechanical symptoms at her 3-month follow-up appointment. The patient will return for her 1-year follow-up later this year.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first documented case of a primary DMC prosthetic hip dislocation as a result of a fractured XLPE insert. DMCs are commonly used in the revision setting to mitigate dislocation risk. Recently there has been an increase in use in the primary setting for patients at high risk of postoperative dislocation. A recent systematic review of dislocations after DMC placement found the rate of dislocation in the revision setting to be 3.27% and in the primary setting to be 0.23% [5].

IPD, or the dislocation of the outer PE liner from the inner head, is a complication unique to the DMC system. Our case represents a unique mechanism of IPD given it resulted from PE fracture and loss of the retentive ability of the liner. This is one of the rare cases of late IPD in modern DMC implants that is not iatrogenic [6,7]. As described, it is essential for surgeons to recognize the unique challenges associated with a DMC prosthetic dislocation due to high rates of iatrogenic IPD with attempted closed reduction [8]. De Martino et al. reported certain characteristics that are unique to DMC implants which may be helpful for recognizing a DMC implant on plain films. The Stryker implant has a second cobalt chrome liner that can be seen on the radiograph while the Anatomic DM is not hemispherical as it has an iliopsoas cut out on the rim [9]. A halo-like radiodensity around the inner femoral head can also clue in providers that they are dealing with a DMC. If the radiodensity is present in the soft tissues, also known as the bubble sign, and/or the femoral head is eccentrically reduced, iatrogenic IPD should be suspected [10,11].

With changes to the dual mobility system, including the use of XLPE, late IPD due to poly wear is anticipated to become obsolete. The incidence of IPD with the early DMC designs has been reported between 2 and 4% [[12], [13], [14]]. The incidence of IPD is now reported to be between 0-2% with the contemporary components [5,15,16]. Previously, it was thought that IPD did not occur with femoral head sizes below 28 mm [8,9]. Our case is the second reported incidence of a dislocation with a 22 mm head size [17]. Another unique feature to our case was the patient’s history of hip dysplasia which is known to lead to versional abnormalities of both the femur and acetabulum. It is possible that this could increase the risk of dislocation and impingement. However, we believe the component sizes and material properties are most likely the cause of the fractured poly liner.

Perez et al. was the first to present a fractured XLPE liner in a dual-mobility system in the revision setting [18]. Comparing the 2 cases may give insight into why these failures occur. The patient in this paper, as well as the patient presented by Perez et al, was implanted with the G7 OsseoTi® acetabular components. Given the sizes, both had a thin XLPE liner and failure in both occurred around 2 years after the initial surgery. Perez et al previously postulated that wires or bone cement debris, notching caused by a trochanteric hook plate or metallosis all could be causes of the liner fracture in their case. Given that many of these are revision specific, they do not apply to our case. Prior authors have had concerns over potential issues with the introduction of the XLPE liners into DMC due to its material properties [3]. While gamma irradiation of PE in inert gas leads to less volumetric wear and a longer life of the poly, it conversely makes XLPE brittle and more prone to fracture [19,20]. In both cases, the liner measured less than 5 mm, 4.6 mm in our case, and around 4.8 mm in the revision case presented by Perez [18]. The thin PE liner may theoretically increase the risk of poly fracture during assembly of the implant when it is impacted onto the metal femoral head. Although there has been no correlation between liner thickness and implant failure to date in the THA literature, it seems plausible that a thin XLPE liner in a DMC may be higher risk for fracture [21].

Summary

While it is impossible to know with certainty when the XLPE liner broke in our patient, it seems plausible that the prodromic symptoms the patient was experiencing are explained by this fracture. Propagation of the fracture may have led to catastrophic failure of the coupling mechanism and IPD. Whether the fracture had been present since the implant was constructed vs occurred over time due to cyclic loading of the PE remains unknown. As of now, poly fracture remains a diagnosis of exclusion. We cannot definitively recommend obtaining advanced imaging such as CT or magnetic resonance imaging when working up a patient for poly fracture as the liner is difficult to visualize with these studies. However, mechanical symptoms of locking, catching or subjective instability as seen in our patient can clue providers into this diagnosis once other causes of pain are ruled out. Either way, it is important to continue to investigate the material properties and potential causes of failure of the modern DMC to minimize morbidity as the implant continues to gain popularity.
