Ferritin nanoparticle-based Nipah virus glycoprotein vaccines elicit potent protective immune responses in mice and hamsters
Authors: Shaohong Chen, Xinghai Zhang, Yanfeng Yao, Shengdong Wang, Kangyin Li, Baoyue Zhang, Tianxi Ye, Li Chen, Yan Wu, Entao Li, Bichao Xu, Pei Zhang, Xia Chuai, Yong Ran, Rui Gong, Huajun Zhang, Sandra Chiu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Nipah virus (NiV) is a zoonotic paramyxovirus in the genus Henipavirus that is prevalent in Southeast Asia. NiV leads to severe respiratory disease and encephalitis in humans and animals, with a mortality rate of up to 75%. Despite the grave threat to public health and global biosecurity, no medical countermeasures are available for humans. Here, based on self-assembled ferritin nanoparticles (FeNPs), we successfully constructed two candidate FeNP vaccines by loading mammalian cells expressing NiV sG (residues 71–602, FeNP-sG) and Ghead (residues 182–602, FeNP-Ghead) onto E. coli-expressed FeNPs (FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead, respectively) through Spycatcher/Spytag technology. Compared with sG and Ghead alone, FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead elicited significant NiV specific neutralizing antibody levels and T-cell responses in mice, whereas the immune response in the FeNP-sG immunized group was greater than that in the FeNP-Ghead group. These results further demonstrate that sG possesses greater antigenicity than Ghead and that FeNPs can dramatically enhance immunogenicity. Furthermore, FeNP-sG provided 100% protection against NiV challenge in a hamster model when it was administered twice at a dose of 5 μg/per animal. Our study provides not only a promising candidate vaccine against NiV, but also a theoretical foundation for the design of a NiV immunogen for the development of novel strategies against NiV infection.

Introduction

Nipah virus (NiV) is an emerging bat-borne zoonotic virus that causes a wide spectrum of diseases, ranging from mild to severe respiratory illness or life-threatening encephalitis, in animals and humans (Singh et al., 2019). Since it was first reported in 1998, NiV has caused almost annual outbreaks in Malaysia, Singapore, Bangladesh, and India (Chua et al., 1999; Liew et al., 2022) and has been listed as one of the top 10 priority viruses by the World Health Organization (WHO). However, there are currently no prophylactics or therapeutics approved for human use (Gomez Roman et al., 2022).

NiV, belonging to the genus Henipavirus, subfamily Paramyxvirinae and family Paramyxoviridae, is a negative-sense single-stranded RNA enveloped virus (Chua et al., 2000). Like other paramyxoviruses, NiV has six genes encoding glycoprotein (G), fusion protein (F), nucleoprotein (N), phosphoprotein (P), matrix protein (M), and polymerase protein or large protein (L) (AbuBakar et al., 2004; Mohandas et al., 2023). Among these, the surface proteins G and F, which mediate viral and cellular membrane attachment and fusion for cellular entry, are predominantly recognized by the host immune system and are the main targets for NiV vaccine development (Eaton et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2022). For instance, a recombinant adenovirus-associated virus vaccine expressing G, as well as recombinant vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) and canarypox vaccine vectors expressing G or F, could completely protect hamsters from NiV challenge (Guillaume et al., 2004; Ploquin et al., 2013; DeBuysscher et al., 2014). Although many NiV vaccine candidates are in progress, none have passed phase III clinical trials to confirm their effectiveness, safety, and persistent protection (Geisbert et al., 2021).

Nanoparticle carriers are regarded as highly promising vaccine platforms (Smith et al., 2013). Several proteins have been reported to naturally form nanoparticles and have been used for antigen delivery and enhancement of immune stimulation. One such protein is ferritin, which can autonomously assemble into nanoparticles with a unique cage-like structure and is currently widely studied and applied as a vaccine scaffold (Lee and Wang, 2006). For example, using SpyCatcher/SpyTag (SC/ST) technology (Zakeri et al., 2012), multiple groups linked the spike protein or receptor binding domain (RBD) of SARS-CoV-2 to ferritin from Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori), which dramatically improved antigenicity compared to that of unlinked antigens (Ma et al., 2020; Kang et al., 2021), showing a promising platform for vaccine development.

In this study, we constructed two FeNP vaccine candidates by loading NiV sG (FeNP-sG) or Ghead (FeNP-Ghead) expressed in mammalian cells into E. coli expressing FeNPs through Spycatcher/Spytag technology. Mice immunized with FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead produced significantly greater levels of antibodies and antigen specific T-cell responses than those immunized with sG or Ghead proteins, which was further confirmed in a hamster model, and a 5 ​μg dose of FeNP-sG provided 100% protection against NiV challenge.

Materials and methods

Cells and viruses

293T (ATCC ACS-4500), Vero E6 (GDC146, CCTCC) and Vero (ATCC CCL-81) cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, California, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum [FBS, Gibco or BI (Biological Industries Israel Beit-Haemek Ltd., IL)] and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 ​°C and 5% CO2. 293F cells (cat#R79007) were maintained in SMM 293-TII expression medium (Sino Biological, Beijing, China) at 37 ​°C incubating shaker at 130 ​rpm, 8% CO2 and 80% humidity. NiV Malaysia (NiVM, CSTR: 16533.06.IVCAS 6.7489), NiV Bangladesh (NiVB, CSTR: 16533.06.IVCAS 6.7488) and Hendra virus (HeV, CSTR: 16533.06.IVCAS 6.7487) sourced from the National Virus Resource Center at the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, were propagated in Vero E6 cells.

Protein purification and nanoparticle preparation

The codon-optimized genes encoding soluble G domains (sG, residues 71–602) and G head domains (Ghead, residues 182–602) of the NiV Bangladesh strain (GenBank: AWT50994) were cloned and inserted into the mammalian expression vector pCAGGS with a signal peptide from the human IFNα1 protein at the N-terminus and with SpyTag (ST) followed by an 8× ​His tag at the C-terminus, the sequence of the protein is shown in the supplementary data. The plasmids were transfected into 293F cells with PEI [PEI MAX-Transfection Grade Linear Polyethylenimine Hydrochloride (MW 40,000, Catalog No: 24765-1), Polysciences, USA], followed by the addition of 3.8 ​mM valproic acid (Sigma-Aldrich®, USA) 2 ​h later. Five days later, the cultures were harvested via centrifugation at 8000 ×g for 15 ​min, after which the supernatants were collected. After filtration through a 0.2 ​μm membrane, the supernatants were passed through Ni-Excel agarose (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

SpyCatcher-Ferritin protein (SC-FeNP) with a 6× ​His tag at the N-terminus was expressed in BL21 E. coli with the pProEX HTb vector (GenBank: QID05027.1; WP_000949212), the sequence of the protein is shown in the supplementary data. After induction with IPTG for 8 ​h, the bacteria were harvested by centrifugation at 10,000 ×g for 15 ​min and resuspended in lysis buffer (50 ​mM Tris-HCl, 250 ​mM NaCl, pH 8). After being lysed by a high-pressure homogenizer (Aquarius Scientific Ltd, USA), the inclusion bodies were harvested by centrifugation at 13,000 ×g and 4 ​°C for 15 ​min. The inclusion bodies were solubilized with lysis buffer supplemented with 8 ​M urea at 4 ​°C overnight; after centrifugation at 13,000 ×g and 4 ​°C for 15 ​min, the supernatants were cleared with a 0.22 ​μm filter and then loaded onto a HisTrap 4FF column and eluted with lysis buffer containing 8 ​M urea and 500 ​mM imidazole. The purified protein was dialyzed to remove urea. NiV sG-ST or Ghead-ST were mixed with SC-FeNP at a molar ratio of 4:1 or as indicated; after incubation at 21 ​°C overnight, a Superose 6 size exclusion column was used to purify the conjugated FeNP-sG or FeNP-Ghead, which were then concentrated, aliquoted and stored at −80 ​°C.

Animal immunization and challenge

Six-to eight-week-old female BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., and 4- to 6-week-old female golden Syrian hamsters were obtained from Wuhan Institute of Bio Products. All animal experiments were approved by the ethics committee (WIVA45202302), and NiV challenge was performed in the biosafety level 4 (BSL-4) facility of the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Animals in each group were randomly divided into 6 groups and immunized with a prime-boost regimen at intervals of 28 days. Each animal was intramuscularly administered a dose of 5 ​μg of sG-ST or Ghead-ST or the corresponding FeNP-sG or FeNP-Ghead containing the same amount of sG-ST or Ghead-ST adjuvanted with an equal volume of AddaVax (InvivoGen, Carlsbad, USA). Fourteen days after the boost, the mice were sacrificed and splenocytes were harvested for detection of cellular immune responses. Twenty-eight days after the boost, the hamsters were challenged intraperitoneally with 1000 LD50 of the NiV Malaysian strain.

ELISA

NiV sG-ST protein was coated onto 96-well high binding plates (BIOFIL, Guangzhou, China) at 1 ​μg/mL in PBS at 4 ​°C overnight. The plates were washed 3 times with PBS supplemented with 1% Tween-20 (PBST) and blocked with PBST supplemented with 1% BSA at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h. Then, the animal sera were serially diluted 3-fold starting at 1:100 and incubated at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h. After 3 washes, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse or anti-hamster IgG (H ​+ ​L) (ABclonal, Wuhan, China) at a 1:40,000 was added and incubated for another 1 ​h at 37 ​°C. After three washes, 100 ​μL TMB (Solarbio, Beijing, China) was added and the mixture was incubated for 15 ​min at 37 ​°C, followed by the addition of 100 ​μL of stop solution (Solarbio). The optical density at 450 ​nm was measured with a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices).

Flow cytometry of specific T cells

Spleens were taken from sacrificed mice, homogenized with a syringe cap, and passed through a 70 ​μm cell strainer (Falcon, BD, USA). Erythrocytes were lysed with red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio). After centrifugation, the splenocytes were resuspended in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and seeded into 96-well round bottom plates (1 ​× ​106/100 μL/well). For intracellular cytokine staining (ICS), the splenocytes were stimulated overnight with a peptide pool covering NiV sG with 15 amino acid residues for each peptide with 10 overlaps (2 ​μg/mL for each peptide, China Peptides Co., Ltd.). After stimulation, cells were stained with BD Horizon™ Fixable Viability Stain 780, anti-CD3e (145-2C11, BD), anti-CD4 (GK1.5, BD) and anti-CD8a (53-6.7, BD) at 4 ​°C for 30 ​min. After fixation and permeabilization, cells were intracellularly stained with anti-IL-4 (11B11, BD), anti-TNF-α (MP6-XT22, BD), anti-IL-2 (JES6-5H4, BD) and anti-IFN-γ (XMG1.2, BD) for 40 ​min at room temperature. Staining data were collected by BD LSR Fortessa (BD), and the percentages of cytokine-specific T cells were analyzed with FlowJo software.

Enzyme-linked immune absorbent spot (ELISpot)

Splenocytes were resuspended in serum-free medium for ELISpot, seeded onto precoated mouse IFN-γ and IL-4 ELISpot plates (5 ​× ​105/100 μL/well, Dakewe Biotech Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) and stimulated as described above. Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, 50 ​ng/mL) was added to the cells as a positive control, whereas the negative control received only serum-free medium. Antigen-specific cells were detected according to the manufacturer's protocol and measured using a CTL ImmunoSpot Analyzer and ImmunoSpot software (Cellular Technology).

Pseudovirus neutralization assay

Vesicular stomatitis pseudoviruses with the G and F proteins NiV or HeV (VSVΔG-GFP/NiVG ​+ ​F or VSVΔG-GFP/HeVG ​+ ​F) were generated as previously described (Zhao et al., 2021). Briefly, plasmids encoding the G and F genes of NiV or HeV were transfected into 293T cells using Lipofectamine 2000 according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen, USA). One day later, the cells were infected with VSVΔG-GFP at an MOI of 3 for 2 ​h, and the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS after two washes. One day later, the supernatant containing VSVΔG-GFP/NiVG ​+ ​F or VSVΔG-GFP/HeVG ​+ ​F was collected and clarified by centrifugation at 2,500 ×g for 15 ​min.

To measure serum neutralizing activity, sera from mice and hamsters were serially diluted threefold starting at 1:50 in DMEM supplemented with 2.5% FBS, incubated with an equal volume of pseudovirus at 20,000 infectious units/mL at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h, and were added to Vero E6 monolayer cells in 96-well plates. One day later, fluorescence was measured using an Operetta high-content imaging system (PerkinElmer). The inhibition percentage for each dilution was calculated as follows: inhibition percentage ​= ​1 − (fluorescent number of each dilution/fluorescence number of input virus) ​× ​100%, and the EC50 was analyzed using GraphPad Prism software with the inhibitor vs normalized response (variable slope) model.

Plaque reduction neutralization assay (PRNT)

PRNT was applied to determine authentic virus neutralizing activity. Sera serially diluted threefold starting at 1:50 were incubated with an equal volume of 600 ​PFU/mL of NiV Bangladesh or Malaysia strains or HeV at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h. After incubation, the 24-well plates containing Vero E6 cell media were removed and 250 ​μL of the sera-virus mixture was added for incubation at 37 ​°C for 1 ​h. The mixture was added to Vero E6 monolayer cells in 24-well plates. After another 1 ​h of incubation, the mixture was replaced with DMEM containing 2.5% FBS and 0.9% carboxymethyl cellulose. The plates were incubated in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 ​°C for 3 days, fixed with 8% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.5% crystal violet. The neutralization percentage was calculated as (1 − plaque number/plaque number without serum) ​× ​100%. The NT50 was analyzed using GraphPad Prism software with the “inhibitor vs normalized response (variable slope)” model.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses were performed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests with GraphPad Prism software. The results are expressed as the mean ​± ​SEM. A value of P ​< ​0.05 was considered statistically significant, NS, not significant; ∗P ​< ​0.05; ∗∗P ​< ​0.01; ∗∗∗P ​< ​0.001; ∗∗∗∗P ​< ​0.0001.

Results

Design and characterization of FeNP-based sG and Ghead nanoparticle vaccines

NiV G is a type II transmembrane protein with a cytoplasmic domain of 40 residues at the N-terminus, a transmembrane domain and an ectodomain termed soluble G (sG). sG is composed of an immunodominant receptor-binding head domain (Ghead) and a stalk domain (Dong et al., 2020). To construct sG and Ghead nanoparticles, sG and Ghead were genetically fused with SpyTag followed by an 8× ​His tag (Fig. 1A) and expressed and purified from the supernatant of 293F cells (Fig. 1B); H. pylori ferritin was fused to SpyCatcher with a 6× ​His tag at the N proximal, which was expressed as an inclusion body in E. coli (Fig. 1A and B), and spontaneously formed nanoparticles (SC-FeNPs, Fig. 1C and D) after refolding against urea gradients. SC-FeNP was incubated with sG-ST and Ghead-ST at various molar ratios (Supplementary Fig. S1), and a ratio of 1:4 was chosen to produce the nanoparticles FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead. Then, the FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead nanoparticles were purified using size-exclusion chromatography. FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead had less retention volume than did FeNP (Fig. 1C), while FeNP, FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead exhibited homogeneous particles under an electron microscope (Fig. 1D), indicating successful preparation of FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead.Fig. 1Construction and purification of sG and Ghead-based nanoparticle vaccine candidates. To further confirm whether sG-ST and Ghead-ST were successfully loaded onto the nanoparticles, ELISA was performed to measure their binding activity with two neutralizing antibodies (nAbs), m102.4 and HENV (Dong et al., 2020). Equal concentrations of sG-ST, Ghead-ST, FeNP-sG, and FeNP-Ghead were coated on ELISA plates and m102.4 recognized FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead much better than the unconjugated forms sG and Ghead (Fig. 1E). The same binding pattern occurred for the HENV antibody (Fig. 1F), which indicates that conjugation with FeNP might enhance the immune response in vivo and that FeNP-sG and FeNP-Ghead could induce a cross-immune response against NiV G and HeV G.

Immunization with nanoparticle vaccines induced an effective antibody response in mice

To evaluate the immunogenicity of the nanoparticle vaccines in vivo, BALB/c mice were immunized intramuscularly with 5 ​μg of sG-ST or Ghead-ST or the corresponding amount of FeNP-sG or FeNP-Ghead containing an equal molar amount of sG-ST or Ghead-ST (Fig. 2A). Two weeks after primary immunization, all four vaccines elicited high titers of sG-specific IgG; the second dose increased the antibody levels by 3–8 times, and FeNP-sG was superior to the other vaccines (Fig. 2B and 2E). Then, the neutralizing activity of the immune sera was determined against VSV-based pseudovirus harboring NiV G (NiV pV), and the results were consistent with the binding activity. Notably, boost vaccination increased nAb levels by 50–100 times, and FeNP-sG showed more significant effects (Fig. 2C and 2F). The immune sera maintained cross-neutralization against HeV (Fig. 2D and 2G). The FeNP-sG nanoparticle vaccine elicits a greater cellular immune response in mice

To determine whether the nanoparticle vaccines induced cellular immune responses, we analyzed the T cell population within the spleens of vaccinated mice by flow cytometry and ELISpot assays. Two weeks after boost vaccination, splenocytes were stimulated with a peptide pool covering the sG. For flow cytometry analysis, the cell population was gated as described (Supplementary Fig. S2). We found that all four vaccine candidates successfully induced specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses, with profound CD4+ T cells expressing TNF-α or IL-2 and CD8+ T cells expressing IFN-γ or TNF-α (Fig. 3A and B). In particular, compared to the other candidates, FeNP-sG elicited up to 5.9% of IL2-expressing CD4+ T cells and 2.9% of TNF-α-expressing CD8+ T cells, representing 4.8- and 3.5-fold greater changes, respectively, than those in the control group. However, for IL-4-expressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, no significant differences were observed among the four groups of vaccinated animals. IFN-γ- and IL4-expressing splenocytes were also tested with ELISpot (Fig. 3C–3E). The vaccinated groups had significantly more specific splenocytes than the control group, and FeNP-sG showed outstanding superiority in eliciting IFN-γ-expressing splenocytes than the FeNP-Ghead group (Fig. 3D). Immunization with the FeNP-sG nanoparticle vaccine induced an effective antibody response in hamsters

To further investigate immunogenicity and protective efficacy against NiV infection, hamsters were immunized with two doses of sG-ST or FeNP-sG on day 0 and day 21 (Fig. 4A). Sera were collected on day 21, day 35 and day 49 and tested for sG-specific IgG binding and nAb levels. As shown in Fig. 4B, FeNP-sG elicited significantly higher levels of sG-specific IgG than sG-ST, by 10, 22 and 4 times on day 21, day 35 and day 49, respectively, and for the nAb titer against VSVΔG-GFP/NiV G ​+ ​F, the differences were even greater, by 17, 95 and 37 times, respectively (Fig. 4C). The sera on day 49 were subjected to an authentic virus neutralization test through PRNT. The neutralization ability of the sera from the FeNP-sG immunization group was approximately 20 times greater than that of the sG-ST immunization group against both the NiV Bangladesh and Malaysia strains (Fig. 4E and 4F). The FeNP-sG nanoparticle vaccine conferred complete protection against lethal NiV challenge in hamsters

On day 52, the hamsters were challenged intraperitoneally with the NiV Malaysian strain at a dose of 1000 LD50. The animals were weighed daily for 14 days and maintained until 28 days post infection (dpi). Significant weight loss was observed at 5 dpi for the control animals, which died at 6 dpi (Fig. 5A and B). Slight weight loss was found in the sG-ST vaccinated animals at 11 dpi, while the weight of the FeNP-sG vaccinated group continued to increase, and all animals in both vaccination groups survived until 28 dpi, when the study ended. Discussion

In humans, NiV can cause rapidly progressing severe illness characterized by deadly encephalitis and/or severe respiratory symptoms, but no licensed vaccines against this pathogen have been approved (Geisbert et al., 2021). The attachment envelope glycoprotein (G protein) of NiV is a promising target for designing candidate vaccines. There are four types of candidate vaccines targeting the G protein: protein subunit vaccines, vectored vaccines, mRNA vaccines, and DNA vaccines. Protein subunit vaccines have a better safety profile because they directly use antigenic proteins without requiring transcription or translation inside the human body. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that polymeric subunit vaccines are capable of inducing more neutralizing antibodies than monomeric vaccines (Butkovich et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2021). Recently, nanoparticles, which possess multiple protein monomers on one particle, have been considered a highly promising vaccine platform, with a tremendously enhanced immune response against antigens (Zhang et al., 2019). The advantage of nanoparticle vaccines is their ability to modulate several properties, including optimizing antigen multivalency, shape, size, structure, and charge, as well as lymph node trafficking, which can enhance capture and presentation by dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages, thereby improving the antibody response (Butkovich et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022).

The accurate design of vaccine antigens is one of the key points in vaccine development. Previous studies have shown that in rhesus macaques vaccinated with NiV sG or HeV sG, the majority of antibodies in the serum target the Ghead domain (Liew et al., 2022). However, it has been reported that the Ghead proteins of NiV elicit only a limited cross-reactive antibody response (Khusro et al., 2020). In this study, the vaccine was derived from the NiVB, as it has been spilling over into humans almost annually in Bangladesh for the last two decades (Gurley et al., 2017). However, it has been reported that hamster models infected with NiVM demonstrated enhanced viral replication, pathology, and mortality rates compared to those infected with NiVB (DeBuysscher et al., 2013). Thus, in this study, we chose a more virulent strain to evaluate the efficacy of the current vaccines in hamster. We compared the immunogenicity of sG and Ghead and found that sG was better than Ghead in the induction of the humoral immune response.

At present, Equivac® HeV, a recombinant HeV sG subunit vaccine approved for the equine anti-HeV vaccine in Australia, is the first commercialized vaccine against a BSL-4 agent and is in clinical development as an emergency vaccine countermeasure in humans for Nipah virus outbreaks (Geisbert et al., 2021). The HeV sG subunit vaccine has been shown to cross-protect cats, ferrets, horses, and nonhuman primates from lethal challenge with NiVM, NiVB, or HeV (McEachern et al., 2008; Satterfield et al., 2016). In this study, we developed ferritin nanoparticle vaccine candidates based on the NiV sG and Ghead proteins, both of which were able to induce abundant antigen-specific antibodies and neutralizing antibodies in mice and hamsters. In this study, the levels of neutralizing antibodies in mice and hamsters induced by two doses of FeNP-sG were greater than those induced by two doses of sG. However, the vaccines doses used in our study were based only on the widely used monomeric dose. Since nanoparticle vaccines can induce higher titers of NT50 than monomers, the dose of nanoparticle vaccines that induce sufficient nAbs could be lower than that of an antigenic protein alone. In our challenge study, hamsters in the nanoparticle vaccine group did not exhibit any weight loss, which was greater than that observed for sG alone throughout the challenge process.

Conclusions

In summary, immunization with safe and efficient vaccines to achieve full protection against NiV infection is urgently needed, and nanoparticle vaccines do not pose specific environmental biosafety concerns for manufacturing procedures. In this study, we have achieved protection in hamsters against a lethal NiVM challenge using FeNP-sG vaccine in conjunction with AddaVax adjuvant. In addition, we provide proof-of-concept that nanoparticles could be used as a promising platform for the development of vaccine candidates against NiV and other highly pathogenic viruses.
