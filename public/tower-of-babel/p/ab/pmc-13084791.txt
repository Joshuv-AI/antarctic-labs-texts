Impact of non-pharmaceutical interventions during the early phase of COVID-19 pandemic on paediatric ICU admissions in Canada and France: a multicentre descriptive cohort and interrupted time-series analysis of non-SARS-CoV-2 viral admissions
Authors: Marika Matsumoto, Michael Levy, Daniel Garros, Shinya Miura, Patricia Mariani-Kurkdjian, Bonita Lee, Stéphane Dauger, Philippe Jouvet, Atsushi Kawaguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic led many countries to implement non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), substantially altering paediatric healthcare. This study evaluated the impact of NPIs on morbidity and non-SARS-CoV-2 infections in paediatric intensive care units (PICUs) during the first wave of the COVID-19 pandemic.MethodsA descriptive, retrospective cohort study was conducted across three geographically distinct PICUs in Canada and France, covering January 2016 to July 2020. To assess the impact of NPIs, disease categories, patient morbidity, the length of PICU stay, mortality and the spectrum of non-SARS-CoV-2 viruses detected from April to July in 2016–2019 were compared with the same period in 2020. Additionally, a time-series analysis of non-SARS-CoV-2 virus-related PICU admissions was performed.ResultsAmong 12 873 patients, median age was 28.8 months (IQR 5.4–111.0), 32.3% required invasive mechanical ventilation, median PICU stay was 3.0 days (IQR 1.6–5.0) and mortality was 3.2%. Comparing the prepandemic and pandemic eras during April–July, the proportion of respiratory admissions declined significantly (29.4% vs 19.4%, p<0.01), while that of postoperative admissions remained stable (25.2% vs 22.9%, p=0.23). No significant differences were seen in ventilation use, length of stay or mortality. Time-series analysis showed a decreasing trend in non-SARS-CoV-2 viral admissions after the index date: OR 0.57 (95% CI 0.31 to 1.02) in weeks 0 to <2, 0.14 (0.08 to 0.25) in weeks 2 to <4 and 0.04 (0.02 to 0.06) in weeks 4 to 6.ConclusionNPIs implemented during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic influenced PICU utilisation and reduced non-SARS-CoV-2 viral infections. Quantitative estimates of such impacts can inform surge capacity planning and the time-limited implementation of NPIs in future pandemics.Keywords: Epidemiology, Public Health, COVID-19

Introduction

On 11 March 2020, WHO declared COVID-19 a global pandemic.1 Governments in many countries implemented non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), including stay-at-home orders, school closures and closure of non-essential businesses to mitigate viral spread and maintain healthcare capacity. These interventions altered healthcare delivery across age groups, including paediatrics.

Several studies have explored the impact of the pandemic on paediatric intensive care units (PICUs), reporting changes in admission patterns and morbidity.2,6 Others have examined viral epidemiology; however, these are often limited to non-PICU data or single-centre data.7,10 Comprehensive investigations into the spectrum of non-SARS-CoV-2 viral infections and their temporal association with NPIs within PICUs remain relatively scarce.

This study addresses that gap by analysing data from three geographically distinct PICUs in Canada and France during the first wave of the pandemic. We assessed changes in PICU morbidity and non-SARS-CoV-2 viral pathogens before and after the implementation of NPIs and performed time-series analysis to evaluate the association between NPIs and trends in viral infection-related admissions. This study revisited the early phase of the pandemic—before vaccines were widely available—to evaluate the impacts of NPIs on PICU utilisation and to inform preparedness for future crises.

Methods

Study design and settings

This descriptive and retrospective cohort study analysed data from two Canadian PICUs: Stollery Children’s Hospital (STOL) in Edmonton, Alberta and CHU Sainte-Justine (CHUSJ) in Montreal, Quebec, and one French site, Robert-Debré (RDB) in Paris. All three PICUs operated as closed intensive care units (ICUs). STOL maintained a 16-bed general PICU with no changes during the COVID-19 pandemic. CHUSJ operated a combined Cardiac Care Unit and general PICU (24 ICU and eight post-ICU beds), which expanded to 32 and six beds, respectively, during the pandemic. RDB operated a 20-bed general PICU, reduced to 14 beds during the pandemic due to the conversion of paediatric beds to adult use. All three are academic tertiary paediatric hospitals, with stable catchment areas and patient cohorts throughout the study periods. As part of pandemic response measures, non-urgent surgeries were postponed at all three institutions. The initial state of emergency was declared on 14 March 2020, in Montreal and on 17 March 2020, in Edmonton, while a lockdown was implemented in Paris on 17 March 2020. Prior to these declarations, several NPIs, including gathering restrictions and school closures, had already been implemented (online supplemental tables 1 and 2). NPIs remained in effect throughout the postimplementation period of the study in all three regions. NPIs were defined as lockdown, gathering restrictions, school closures, closure of non-essential businesses (eg, gyms and theatres), restrictions of restaurant operations, mask-wearing and hand hygiene measures.

Data used

We included all children admitted to three PICUs from 1 January 2016 to 31 July 2020. We obtained the following items from the administrative databases in each unit: patients’ characteristics, reasons for PICU admission, virus pathogens when tested, use of invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), PICU length of stay (LOS) and mortality. Admission reasons were categorised as respiratory disease, postoperative management, diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), trauma, intoxication, burns and others for the purpose of analysis. Non-SARS-CoV-2 infections were defined as a positive viral panel test within 7 days before or after the PICU admission date.

Methodology for detecting viruses

Dual respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) and influenza A/B PCR, along with the FilmArray Respiratory Panel (FARPT) (BioFire Diagnostics, USA), were employed for viral detection at RDB. FARPT detects adenovirus, common coronaviruses (229E, NL63, OC43, HKU1), influenza A subtypes (H1, H1N1-2009, H3), influenza B virus, human metapneumovirus (hMPV), parainfluenza virus types 1/2/3/4, RSV types A/B, human rhinovirus/enterovirus (RV/EV), Bordetella pertussis, Chlamydia pneumoniae and Mycoplasma pneumoniae. CHUSJ and STOL employed NxTAG Respiratory Pathogen Panel (Luminex, USA), which detected the same list of respiratory pathogens as FARPT, except for B. pertussis.

Statistical analysis

First, we described the baseline characteristics of patients in the full cohort. Then, we extracted data from April to July annually (2016–2020) to compare patient characteristics, morbidity and viral pathogens before and after the implementation of the NPIs. We defined the prepandemic era as the combined data from April to July of 2016–2019, while the same period in 2020 was designated as the pandemic era. Continuous variables were summarised as medians with IQRs and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, with comparisons made using χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, conducted in EZR (Easy R; Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Japan) V.1.61. P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Second, to assess the impact of NPIs on PICU admissions for non-SARS-CoV-2 infections during the first wave, we conducted a focused time-series analysis using the full dataset. We divided eligible children into 26 consecutive two-week periods, labelled from week −16 to week 9, with March 17 as the index date (the first day of week 0). For the combined analysis of data from three regions, March 17 was selected over March 14 (when Montreal declared its state of emergency) because it aligned with the state of emergency declaration in Edmonton and lockdown implementation in Paris. To account for potential seasonal variation in viral infections,9 we first assessed annual trends to confirm consistency across years. We then applied a multivariable regression model with indicator variables for each 2-week period, using log-transformed admission counts as the outcome, to estimate the expected number of admissions had the pandemic not occurred (online supplemental appendix 1). For weeks with zero admissions, a small constant (0.1) was added prior to log transformation. The estimated admissions were compared with the actual observed admissions to evaluate the temporal impact of NPIs on non-SARS-CoV-2 infections. We focused on the first 6 weeks following the index date, hypothesising that effects would manifest within this period, consistent with previous studies showing nadirs in admission numbers within 4 weeks of lockdown implementation.11,16 Additionally, we performed a multivariable regression, assuming a constant NPI effect across the subsequent 2-week intervals. To assess model assumptions, we evaluated residual autocorrelation using the Durbin-Watson test. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative constants (0.5 and 1.0) for zero-admission periods. These analyses were performed using STATA V.17 (StataCorp, Texas, USA) and Python V.3.13.11.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research. This study was a secondary analysis of existing data with no direct patient contact.

Results

Summary of the cohort

The total number of PICU admissions during the study period was 12 873, with 3113 children at STOL, 4769 at CHUSJ and 4991 at RDB. Males accounted for 56.7% (7302/12 873), median age was 28.8 months (IQR 5.4–111.0). Respiratory diseases were the leading cause of PICU admission (37.9%, 4885/12 873), followed by postoperative management (21.7%, 2794/12 873). The proportion of IMV was 32.3% (4159/12 873). The median PICU LOS was 3.0 days (IQR 1.6–5.0) and mortality was 3.2% (407/12 873) (table 1).

Reasons for admission

During the prepandemic era, two major reasons for PICU admission were respiratory diseases and postoperative management, together accounting for 54.6% of the total admissions. The proportion of respiratory diseases significantly decreased from 29.4% to 19.4% (p<0.01) during the pandemic era, while the proportion for postoperative management remained stable (25.2% prepandemic vs 22.9% during the pandemic era; p=0.23). Among other causes, although the absolute number of DKA cases was small, the proportion of admissions for DKA increased from 2.1% to 5.4% (p<0.01), and the proportion of intoxications also went up from 3.3% to 6.6% (p<0.01). No significant differences were observed in admissions due to trauma or burns between the prepandemic and pandemic eras (table 2).

When comparing regional trends, although the absolute number of admissions for both respiratory diseases and postoperative management decreased across all three PICUs during the pandemic era, the proportions of these categories relative to total admissions at each institution showed differing patterns between CHUSJ and STOL versus RDB (online supplemental tables 3 and 4). At CHUSJ and STOL, the trends reflected those observed in the overall cohort: the proportion of respiratory disease admissions significantly decreased—from 23.8% to 12.5% at CHUSJ (p<0.01), and from 29.9% to 15.4% at STOL (p<0.01)—while the proportion of postoperative admissions remained stable (34.6% to 34.3%, p=0.94 at CHUSJ; 27.3% to 25.0%, p=0.61 at STOL). In contrast, RDB did not show a significant change in the proportion of respiratory admissions (34.3% vs 29.6%, p=0.18), but exhibited a significant decline in the proportion of postoperative admissions (14.6% to 8.6%, p=0.02). Regarding other reasons for admission, DKA admissions increased at RDB (from 3.4% to 9.0%, p<0.01) and STOL (from 1.9% to 6.6%, p<0.01), while remaining relatively stable at CHUSJ (from 0.9% to 1.5%, p=0.50). The proportion of intoxication-related admissions increased only at STOL, rising from 0% to 8.8% (p<0.01). No significant differences were observed in the proportions of burn or trauma admissions between the prepandemic and pandemic periods across any of the three regions (online supplemental table 3 and online supplemental figure 2).

Viral pathogens

Comparing prepandemic (April–July of 2016–2019) with pandemic era (April–July 2020), the proportion of admission numbers with non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viral infections decreased from 17.1% (608/3552) to 6.0% (38/634) during the pandemic era (p<0.01). During the prepandemic era, RV/EV was the most frequently identified virus (52.1%, 369/708 among the total number of detected viruses), followed by parainfluenza (14.7%, 104/708) and RSV (11.3%, 80/708). Despite an overall reduction in virus detection during the pandemic era, RV/EV remained the most frequently detected virus (62.8%, 27/43) (table 3). Incidentally, SARS-CoV-2 was detected in 15 cases (RDB: 10, CHUSJ: 5, STOL: 0) (online supplemental table 3).

Impact of NPIs on non-SARS-CoV-2 infections

Of the 12 873 children in the cohort, 3140 were admitted with non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viral infections (804 at STOL, 1057 at CHUSJ and 1279 at RDB) during the full study period (January 2016–July 2020). Whereas the previous section was restricted to comparing data from April to July 2016 to 2019 vs April–July 2020, this section analyses the entire study period but is limited to patients with non-SARS-CoV-2 viral infections. Their median age was 10.0 months (IQR 2.0–32.7 months). The most frequently identified virus was RSV (34.2%, 1238/36 200), followed by RV/EV (31.6%, 1144/3620), influenza (9.3%, 336/3620), adenovirus, parainfluenza, hMPV and common coronaviruses. A total of 24.6% (773/3140) of the children required IMV. The overall mortality rate of those children was 2.5% (79/3140), and the median length of PICU stay was 3.2 days (IQR 2.0–6.0 days) (table 4).

After the index date, admissions for non-SARS-CoV-2 infections showed a decreasing trend in the first 2 weeks, then declined significantly in 2–6 weeks relative to projections based on the prior 4 years (figure 1). The ORs comparing observed and projected admissions postlockdown were 0.57 (95% CI 0.31 to 1.02) for weeks 0 to <2, 0.14 (95% CI 0.08 to 0.25) for weeks 2 to <4 and 0.04 (95% CI 0.02 to 0.06) for weeks 4 to <6. In a secondary analysis, assuming a constant effect of the NPIs across each 2-week period, the ORs were 0.35 (95% CI 0.30 to 0.41) for weeks 0 to <2, 0.12 (95% CI 0.09 to 0.17) for weeks 2 to <4 and 0.04 (95% CI 0.03 to 0.07) for weeks 4 to <6. The Durbin-Watson test statistic was 2.84, indicating no substantial autocorrelation. Sensitivity analyses using alternative constants (0.5 and 1.0) for zero admission periods yielded consistent effect estimates, confirming robustness to this methodological choice.

Discussion

This study across three PICUs in two countries showed an overall reduction in PICU admissions during the first wave of the COVID-19 global pandemic, especially for respiratory diseases, consistent with international reports.2,46 17 18 Although reductions in viral infections during the pandemic have been quantitatively analysed in paediatric emergency departments using time-series models,19 such analyses remain scarce in the PICU setting and most prior studies have been descriptive.2,46 17 18 By applying time-series analyses to PICU data from three geographically distinct regions, our study provides quantitative evidence of a temporal association between NPIs and reduced admissions for non-SARS-CoV-2 infections in critically ill children.

Respiratory disease and postoperative management are among the leading reasons for PICU admission. In our study, respiratory admissions decreased, consistent with previous reports. The decline likely reflects NPIs such as school closures, mask-wearing, hygiene promotion and gathering restrictions, as widely documented in the literature.19 20 Although the absolute number of postoperative managed admissions declined, likely due to deferred elective procedures ahead of COVID-19 surges, the overall proportion of postoperative management admissions remained stable, suggesting preserved capacity for urgent surgery. Several studies also reported reduced PICU surgical volume, and some, like ours, found stable proportions despite lower admission numbers.2,617

Regional analysis revealed similar trends at CHUSJ and STOL during the pandemic, with decreased respiratory admissions and stable proportions of postoperative admissions. In contrast, RDB exhibited a relatively stable proportion of respiratory admissions despite a decline in absolute numbers, alongside a decreased proportion of postoperative admissions (online supplemental table 3 and online supplemental figure 2). This divergence, particularly regarding the fact that the proportion of respiratory diseases at RDB did not decline during the pandemic era, unlike at the other two institutions, could be partly explained by a shorter duration of school closures in Paris and the continued circulation of respiratory viruses, particularly RV/EV, which were more frequently detected at RDB. Although the implementation differed—lockdown in Paris versus states of emergency in Alberta and Quebec—the overall NPIs were broadly similar across the three regions. NPIs were intensified in March, maintained in April and gradually eased beginning in May, with phase I easing in May and phase II in June. One key difference was the duration of school closures: while all three regions implemented school closures in mid-March, schools in Paris, the region of RDB, were mandatorily reopened on June 22, whereas schools in Edmonton, the region of STOL, and Montreal, the region of CHUJS, remained closed until September, following the summer break (online supplemental tables 1 and 2). A further contributing factor may be RDB’s higher baseline rate of respiratory admissions in the prepandemic (34.3% vs 23.8% at CHUSJ and 29.9% at STOL) (online supplemental table 3).

Importantly, we observed no significant changes in key PICU morbidity indicators, including the proportion of IMV, median PICU LOS or mortality rates between the prepandemic and pandemic era. This consistency likely reflects institutional adaptability and the relatively limited paediatric burden of COVID-19 during the first wave. Previous studies have reported mixed findings regarding PICU morbidity, with some indicating no change and others reporting increased severity.2,610 17 Given the substantial decline in overall admissions, interpreting mortality in isolation is challenging. As suggested in previous studies, incorporating validated severity scoring systems such as the Paediatric Index of Mortality (PIM)-2 or PIM-3 may be necessary to assess the severity.5 21

Notably, despite small absolute numbers, DKA and intoxication cases increased, consistent with previous reports. Several studies have noted increased incidence of DKA and new-onset type 1 diabetes during the pandemic, possibly due to delayed care, psychological stress or SARS-CoV-2-related immunological imbalance.22,25 Maintaining timely access to paediatric care during public health crises remains a critical priority. Increasing public awareness of the risks of delayed presentation may help reduce such impacts.

As for the increasing trend in intoxication cases, it may reflect heightened psychological stress among children and adolescents, exacerbated by school closures and social isolation. Although our data did not distinguish between unintentional and intentional ingestion, previous studies have reported increased rates of self-harm and intentional poisoning during the pandemic,26 27 highlighting the need for robust mental health support systems for youth during periods of societal disruption.

Regarding trauma and burn-related PICU admissions, we found no significant change in proportions during the early phases of the pandemic. A study from the USA reported a decrease in paediatric trauma cases at a level 1 trauma centre; however, their cohort included both inpatients and outpatients, whereas our analysis focused exclusively on critically injured cases.28 Although we did not analyse the mechanisms of injury, it is possible that restrictions on outdoor activity and closer supervision at home contributed to fewer general trauma cases, while social isolation and limited access to support services may have increased the risk of abuse-related injuries. Studies on changes in child abuse incidence during the pandemic remain inconclusive.28,30

With respect to non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viral infections, CHUSJ and STOL reported very few detections during the first wave, while RDB continued to detect a broader range of viruses, although with an overall reduction. As mentioned above, one possible explanation is the shorter duration of school closures in the Paris region. School closures have been shown to mitigate viral transmission under certain conditions.31 Regional epidemiological differences may also have played a role. Between April and July, RV/EV was the most frequently detected virus at RDB, despite an overall decline in respiratory viruses. This aligns with studies showing RV/EV persistence, while RSV and influenza nearly disappeared.710 32,34 The mechanism behind the persistence of RV/EV remains unclear, but it may be due to reduced sensitivity to NPIs or resistance to alcohol-based sanitisers, being non-enveloped.34 Notably, our data indicate an upward trend in RV/EV detection at RDB even before the pandemic (online supplemental table 4 and online supplemental figure 1). This difference in epidemiological patterns may be related to regional factors, including climatic conditions, as Canada is much colder than France during this period.

Time-series analysis revealed a marked decline in non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viral admissions during the first wave, coinciding with the implementation of NPIs. While similar methodologies have been employed in paediatric emergency department studies,19 few have been applied in the PICU context. Our approach to projecting expected admissions based on historical seasonal trends and comparing them with observed data strengthens the evidence for a temporal relationship between NPIs and reduced virus-related PICU admissions.

Taken together, the overall burden on PICUs during the first wave appeared relatively limited compared with adult ICUs.35 This likely reflects the low incidence of severe paediatric COVID-19 and a reduction in two major drivers of PICU admissions, respiratory disease and postoperative management, primarily due to a marked decline in illness caused by non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses. This decline was likely driven by NPIs and reduced elective surgeries as hospital measurements. These findings underscore the need for flexible, coordinated critical care planning during health crises, exemplified by reports showing that the relatively low paediatric burden allowed reallocation of PICU resources to overwhelmed adult ICUs.36 37

While this analysis focuses on the early phase of the pandemic, later reports indicate a resurgence of non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses and increased paediatric admissions in subsequent waves.38 39 To fully understand the pandemic’s long-term impact on PICU, further research covering broader timeframes is essential.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis was limited to the first wave, excluding peak seasons for some respiratory viruses. Second, although time-series modelling revealed temporal associations between NPIs and reduced viral admissions, causality cannot be established. Other factors, such as changes in parental care-seeking behaviour or higher thresholds for PICU admission, particularly during the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, may have played a role. Additionally, the model assumes temporal continuity and may not capture unexpected disruptions or unmeasured confounders. Third, viral testing practices may have changed over time during the early pandemic and throughout the study period, and testing policies may have differed across institutions; both factors could have influenced virus detection but were not systematically assessed.

Conclusion

In conclusion, by analysing the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, this study offers critical insights into how NPIs influenced PICU admissions in the face of high uncertainty and limited resources. The early changes in patterns of admission and disease morbidity underscore the importance of flexible, scalable healthcare systems, including the strategic integration of ICU capacities. In the face of a potential future pandemic, it is imperative to be prepared to make prompt and efficient operational decisions. These decisions must be based on epidemiological descriptions derived from the evidence in various environmental and social conditions.
