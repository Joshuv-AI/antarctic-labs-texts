An efficient Alzheimer’s disease detection by NV classifier with BWTDL approach using MRI image
Authors: Pande Sandeep Dwarkanth, Raju Anitha, Gade Harish Babu, SK Hasane Ahammad, Hanen Karamti, Abhinav Kumar, Ankit Dilipkumar Oza, Demmelash Mollalign Moges
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s Disease (AD), which affects the elderly population worldwide, is an acute degenerative disease. Hence, to treat the disease, early diagnosis is required. For obtaining an accurate diagnosis, various methods have been developed by the prevailing research. However, improvement is still needed. Therefore, this article proposes a Neglect VGGNet (NV) classifier detection utilizing a Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) image. Primarily, the input MRI images are taken from different dimensions. By using Edge Quality Indexed Lee Filter (EQILF), Contrast-Limited Adaptive Histogram Equalization (CLAHE), and the morphological operation process, all the dimensions are pre-processed. Thereafter, the axial view is given to the segmentation process for segmenting the brain tissues utilizing the Frechet K-Means (FKM) algorithm. From the segmentation outcome, the hippocampus and neocortex have similar structures. Thus, to obtain accurate AD detection, separation is needed. Therefore, to find the difference, this research methodology uses Bellman Weighted Temporal Difference Learning (BWTDL). Afterward, the features are extracted from all the brain tissues; then, by utilizing the Sin Functional Lyrebird Optimization Algorithm (SFLOA), the important features are selected. By using a Point by Point Chord-based Structural Relational Graph (PPC-SRG), the graph is constructed from the sagittal view, and the matrix is generated. Moreover, by using the Canny Edge Aware Pyramidal Deformable (CEAPD) approach, the mapping is constructed from the coronal view. Thereafter, for predicting the AD, the selected features, generated matrix, and constructed map function are given to the NV classifier. The Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) dataset is utilized for the performance analysis. As per the experimental evaluation, the proposed methodology attains 98.6% accuracy, which is higher in contrast to the prevailing mechanisms.Keywords: Alzheimer Disease (AD), Edge Quality Indexed Lee Filter (EQILF), Frechet K-Means (FKM), Bellman Weighted Temporal Difference Learning (BWTDL), Canny Edge Aware Pyramidal Deformable (CEAPD) approach, Neglect VGGNet (NV) classifier

Introduction

A type of dementia that causes progressive cognitive decline and widespread neuron death is named AD [1]. AD has become one of the major public health issues in recent days. If amyloid beta plaques as well as insoluble proteins are settled in the cerebral cortex and hippocampus, AD is hypothesized [2]. AD causes the brain’s hippocampus as well as cerebral cortex to shrink. The cholinergic basal forebrain is related to the cognitive decline in AD, thus affecting the entire cerebral cortex [3]. The nucleus basalis, the diagonal band of Broca, ventral basal ganglia, substantia innominate, and the medial septal nucleus are encompassed in the basal forebrain region [4]. Memory loss, visual changes, thinking skills degradation, confusion, and psychotic symptoms are the symptoms of AD. The AD’s effects could arise from the age of early 60’s [5]. Psychotic symptoms, which affect most persons, are the general non-cognitive neuropsychiatric symptoms of AD [6]. Fig. 1 displays the view of the normal brain and the AD-affected brain.

Model diagram for normal brain vs Alzheimer’s brain

The Neglect VGGNet (NV) classifier is a transformed deep learning model built upon the standard VGG architecture. Unlike traditional CNNs or ResNet, NV integrates attention layers that emphasis on underrepresented regions in the MRI scans—particularly the hippocampus and neocortex, which are critical for Alzheimer’s diagnosis. These attention-enhanced layers help in acquiring subtle textural variations often missed by conventional architectures.

Likewise the Bellman Weighted Temporal Difference Learning (BWTDL) method is employed to distinguish between brain regions with similar intensities but inconsistent pathologies. Inspired by reinforcement learning, it updates weights based on time-sequenced structural features, helping to hear progressive degeneration patterns in Alzheimer’s cases.

Nevertheless, from a clinical point of view, classifying AD and its related neurological disorders and Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is a very difficult task [7]. AD causes some parts of the brain to decline; hence, earlier detection of AD is significant [8]. Earlier detection is helpful as it provides appropriate treatment to stop it from happening anytime soon; otherwise, there are no medicines or treatments for curing AD [9]. For the classification of AD, various imaging systems, namely Positron Emission Tomography, resting state MRI, MRI, structural MRI, and functional MRI techniques are employed. Among these, MRI performs well in the detection of AD [10]. AD classification exploited neuroimaging, biomarker research, big data, and the application of Machine Learning (ML) approaches in recent years. Biomarkers, namely cerebrospinal fluid amyloid-beta and tau proteins provide valuable insights into AD [11].

To efficiently detect AD and MCI, the existing methodologies for AD classification used diffusion tensor imaging. To predict AD, most of the existing work uses ML techniques like Support Vector Machine (SVM) and conventional mechanisms like Deep Learning (DL) [12]. The neuro-imaging-based AD diagnosis is enhanced by the DL methodologies, namely Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs); however, they exhibit reduced accuracy in the AD classification [13]. CNNs are utilized by some research works to classify each matrix feature and combine them to create a feature vector that attains 82% accuracy [14]. Some existing works also utilize Laser ablation inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry imaging approach, but they are not efficient [15]. Hence, to detect AD, improvement is still required. Thus, to effectively detect AD, an efficient model, which utilized sagittal, axial, and coronal views of the MRI images and a Neglect VGGNet-based classifier, has been proposed.

Recent advancements in medical image analysis have highlighted the impact of transformer-based architectures and transfer learning techniques. One notable study applied a regularized transformer with adaptive token fusion to improve the detection of Alzheimer’s disease from brain MRI scans, offering enhanced spatial context modeling. Another investigation employed an adaptive filtering strategy combined with multi-level alignment guided by clinical radiology reports, significantly advancing the diagnosis of tuberculosis and pneumonia in chest X-rays. Additionally, a vision transformer model, CTBViT, was developed for tuberculosis classification, integrating efficient token processing and randomized classifiers to achieve reduced computational complexity. These methods reflect the broader trend of incorporating attention mechanisms and pretrained networks into medical diagnosis pipelines. In contrast, our approach utilizes a hybrid optimization-based framework with neuro-inspired learning components, contributing to interpretability and decision-making efficiency while remaining distinct from transformer-centric models.

Literature survey

C.M. Chabib et al. [16], propounded the Deep Convolutional Curvelet Transform system to detect AD earlier by employing MRI. In multi-classification tasks, the Leave-One-Group-Out (LOGO) cross-validation technique was utilized. The brain regions related to AD MRI images were efficiently and accurately identified by this model. However, the LOGO approach was affected by time and power complexities while utilizing large datasets; hence, the performance was degraded.

F. U. R. Faisal and G. R. Kwon [17] employed whole-brain MRI to automatically detect AD and MCI. For classifying the brain’s MRI images, CNN was utilized. This model effectively classified the brain images into MCI, AD, and Cognitively Normal (CN) groups. Yet, the CNN approach did not encode the position and orientation of the input image, which resulted in the misclassification results.

H. Guo and Y. Zhang [18] elucidated earlier detection of AD using resting state Functional MRI (FMRI) and enhanced DL algorithm. For differentiating the natural aging and disorder progression, this model used a specialized network of autoencoders. The standard deviations were reduced by the DL model, which also achieved more reliability. If this model was fine-tuned too much, then DL models resulted in overfitting problems, thus affecting the identification process.

M. M. S. Fareed et al. [19], recommended a CNN architecture for earlier detection of AD in MRI scans. Here, a synthetic oversampling technique was utilized. For visualizing the heat map, a Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) was employed. The model superiorly detected the earlier AD and successfully displayed the heat map on the brain. Nevertheless, this Dl technique’s accuracy was compromised owing to the imbalanced dataset.

S. Sharma et al. [20], proffered the VGG16 feature extractor and DL-centered CNN model to identify the AD exploiting MRI scans. To classify the images into 4 forms, namely non-demented, very mild demented, mild demented, and moderate demented, neural networks were utilized. For identifying AD, this model attained superior precision, f1-score, accuracy, and recall. Yet, VGG16 had huge parameters, which increased the training time of the disease prediction.

L. Sathish Kumar et al. [21], explicated the earlier detection of AD from MRI brain images using the AlexNet approach. To recover significant features efficiently from MRI images, AlexNet, also known as CNN architecture, was utilized. In the detection of AD, the AlexNet approach yielded very good accuracy. Furthermore, the identification process took more time since the image count was increased. This may affect the performance of the prediction model.

M. EL-Geneedy et al. [22], investigated an MRI-based DL technique to accurately identify AD. Shallow CNN architecture as well as 2D T1-weighted MRI images were utilized by this model. It effectively classified the MCIs into moderate dementia, mild dementia, and very mild dementia with good accuracy. However, the research directly passed the raw input to the classifier. Hence, the raw input data had more noise, which might produce errors during classification and it affected the system’s performance.

A. Ebrahimi et al. [23], explored the detection of AD from MRI images by using deep sequence modeling. To extract the sequence of features from MRI scans, the pre-trained Residual Network (ResNet-18) was utilized. Sequence-based modeling prevented millions of parameters at the time of training and achieved better classification results. Nevertheless, sequence-based modeling was affected by gradient problems since it struggled to capture long-term dependencies.

B. Li et al. [24], elucidated the detection of AD as well as MCI from multi-feature brain structural MRI utilizing the age correction technique. To recognize the cross-sectional biomarkers, AD, and MCIs, the SVM classifier was used. Concerning specificity along with accuracy, this model provided better results. Yet, the SVM model was difficult to understand and interpret, which resulted in low sensitivity measures. Therefore, the performance and detection process of the model were affected.

F. de Vos et al. [25], propounded pre-trained MRI-centric classification techniques for AD classification. For the classification process, the resting-state functional MRI, diffusion MRI, and anatomical MRI scans were utilized by this model. These three models calculated grey matter density, fractional anisotropy, and functional connectivity of resting state networks, respectively. The grey matter density model performed better in the classification process and gained superior results. Moreover, during classification, there were many false positive and false negative results obtained in the diffusion MRI; hence, the accuracy was degraded.

Recent developments in medical image analysis and deep learning closely align with the direction of our research. For instance, the adoption of multi-resolution attention mechanisms, such as the Visual Mamba, has significantly advanced retinal disease detection by capturing diverse spatial features [26]. Additionally, source-free unsupervised segmentation strategies have improved MRI interpretation for the knee by mitigating reliance on labeled target domain data [27]. In the context of neurodevelopmental disorders, transformer-based models with multi-task learning capabilities have shown promise in accurately detecting autism spectrum conditions [28]. For dementia diagnosis, graph-based adversarial autoencoders have proven effective in modeling functional brain networks [29]. The field of ophthalmology has also benefited from innovations in multi-segment localization and topology-aware vessel enhancement, enabling more precise detection of retinal abnormalities [30, 31]. Moreover, contrastive learning methods are being leveraged in EEG-based emotion recognition to isolate subject-invariant and temporally stable features [32]. Together, these contributions highlight a broader trend in medical AI toward interpretable, domain-adaptive, and structurally aware models—principles that are central to the proposed NV classification framework.

In the preprocessing stage, EQILF was employed due to its superior edge protection and denoising performance. A comparative analysis using PSNR and SSIM values disclosed that EQILF retained structural integrity more effectively, with a PSNR of 29.4 dB as opposed to 26.7 db from median filtering. Results and discussion

This phase assesses the research methodology’s performance by comparing it with numerous traditional works according to several quality metrics. Moreover, the proposed system is implemented on the working platform of PYTHON. Here, to depict the model’s supremacy and reliability, the performance and comparative evaluations are done for the proposed framework.

Beyond accuracy, precision, recall, and specificity, the model underwent additional assessment through AUC-ROC curves, which depicted the classifier’s ability to distinguish between different outcomes at various threshold levels. The NV classifier achieved an AUC of 0.987, signifying outstanding discriminative ability.

The suggested NV classifier was evaluated alongside both deep learning and conventional machine learning models. Although CNN-based architectures such as VGG16, ResNet, and AlexNet demonstrated moderate effectiveness, the NV model surpassed their performance considerably. Furthermore, a baseline SVM classifier, which was trained on manually crafted features, attained an accuracy of 86.2%, notably lower than NV’s impressive 98.6%. This highlights the advantages of combining deep learning with feature engineering.

Conclusion

This paper proposed a well-ordered framework called NV-based AD prediction using MRI. The neglect layer is incorporated by the proposed NV, which leads to high computational efficiency and accurate classification. To perform segmentation, an effective EQILF is established in the proposed model that improves the computation process. Likewise, for identifying the optimal features, SFLOA is integrated, which upgrades the classification outcomes. Also, the proposed work’s performance is analyzed by the ADNI dataset. Hence, the evaluation outcomes demonstrated that the proposed NV achieved better accuracy (98.6%), sensitivity (96.8%), and specificity (96%). Similarly, the proposed FKM obtained a clustering time of 28,982 ms, thus showing a lower time complexity. Also, the proposed NV attained FNR and FPR of 0.042% and 0.045%, respectively, which proved the model’s reliability. As per the experimental outcomes, the proposed system outperformed other existing works in the AD classification. Yet, during contrast enhancement of MRI, irrelevant noise may arise, which limits the system’s performance.

The notable enhancement in classification accuracy achieved by the NV classifier can be credited to its attention-guided architecture and an optimized preprocessing pipeline. However, to mitigate concerns regarding overfitting, dropout layers, cross-validation, and external testing methods were implemented.

The limitations of this approach include potential biases within the dataset and difficulties associated with real-time integration. Future studies will aim to explore real-world applications, domain adaptation, and the incorporation of explainability frameworks to facilitate safe adoption in clinical settings.

Limitations and future work

Though the proposed NV framework shows state-of-the-art performance at the task of classification, it comes with several shortcomings to take into account. Limiting generalizability, the study is based only on data from the ADNI dataset and cannot be readily extrapolated to other imaging centers or different demographic groups or acquisition protocols. There are two major drawbacks for SFLOA: on the one hand, consistent with its good performance in selecting informative features, it becomes increasingly time-consuming when the dimension of data grows, causing difficulty in its real-time application in clinical laboratories. Furthermore, it was not well-tested about the capability of the model to withstand noise and imaging artifacts. This work can be extended in many directions such as external validations, deployment of SFLOA on other heterogeneous datasets (optimizing the kernel models to improve computational efficiency), incorporating explainable AI methods for improved clinical trust and transparency.
