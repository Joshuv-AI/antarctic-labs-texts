Synthesis of amidoxime adsorbent prepared by radiation grafting on upcycled low-density polyethylene sheet for removal of heavy metals from wastewater
Authors: Vareeporn Ratnitsai, Wijittra Wongjaikham, Doonyapong Wongsawaeng, Kanokporn Kohmun, Somtop Santibenchakul, Koranat Narkpiban
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe issue of discharging waste, especially heavy metals from industrial activities into the environment, not only adversely impacts environmental quality but also has impacts on communities and human health. Removal and reduction of heavy metal contamination in rivers and wastewater are, therefore, critical initiatives that require significant attention. This work studied the removal of heavy metals, including Zn(II), Cu(II), As(III), and Pb(II) by utilizing an upcycled amidoxime low-density polyethylene sheet (AO-sheet). The synthesized AO-sheet was analyzed for various physical properties, including scanning electron microscope, energy-dispersive x-ray spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, and thermogravimetric analysis. For the batch adsorption experiment, parameters affecting adsorption capacity were studied: initial concentration, submerging time, and pH. Adsorption isotherms were also studied. The results of the heavy metal adsorption study showed that the initial concentration was the most significant parameter; the higher the initial concentration, the greater the adsorption capacity. The adsorption capacity of Zn(II) and Pb(II) increased with submersion time, which achieved 21.07 and 0.855 mg/g-adsorbent, respectively, after four weeks of submersion under the highest initial concentration studied. The adsorption capacity of Cu(II) was 7.98 mg/g-adsorbent after two weeks of optimal adsorption duration under the highest initial concentration studied. The adsorption capacity of As(II) was 1.07 mg/g-adsorbent after one week of optimal submersion time under the highest initial concentration studied. Moreover, the appropriate pH range for effective adsorption of Zn(II), Cu(II), and Pb(II) was identified as 8–9, while for As(III), it was 6–8, with an adsorption duration of 0.43 weeks (3 days). From the Langmuir isotherm, it was found that the adsorption of this work was characterized by monolayer adsorption. The results demonstrate that the AO-sheet can be effectively used to remove heavy metals from wastewater. Its potential for reusability was up to 8 cycles, with the Zn(II) adsorption capacity being reduced to about 20.37%.Keywords: Heavy metal, Amidoxime, Removal of heavy metal, Wastewater, Low-density polyethylene sheetSubject terms: Chemical engineering, Environmental impact

Introduction

Across the globe, the industrial sector is expanding, leading to the release of various pollutants. These include waste, hazardous materials, and substances such as sludge or residue, often exceeding acceptable limits and threatening the environment's quality and human health1. The infiltration of these pollutants into natural water sources is a significant cause of water pollution, evident from changes in its inherent properties. The infusion of multiple pollutants makes the water unsuitable for human consumption, and the discharge of heavy metal-contaminated wastewater into natural water sources is another significant contributor to the worsening of water pollution2.

Heavy metals are transition metal elements with a specific gravity higher than 5 g/cm3 with intrinsically hazardous properties for living organisms3. Because they are not biodegradable, they endure in the environment over extended periods. Industries, including those involved in producing plastics, paints, batteries, pesticides, and petrochemicals, utilize heavy metals as crucial raw materials4,5. Consequently, effluent from these industrial processes contributes significantly to the degradation of natural water sources. Aquatic species assimilate heavy metals through water, aquatic plants, and aquatic animals along the food chain, leading to the accumulation of these metals in animal and plant tissues proportional to consumption levels6. Heavy metals are particularly noteworthy in their pivotal role in water pollution, with concentrations over acceptable limits posing toxicity risks to the human body. They enter the body through various routes, including ingesting contaminated food and drink, inhalation into the respiratory system, and absorption into the skin and tissues7,8. The following are four examples of heavy metals that are poisonous to living organisms and have incredibly harmful effects on human health: (1) Zn: large amounts of zinc dust or vapor can cause symptoms such as thirst, coughing, pneumonia, bronchitis, easy fatigue, nausea, fever, and chills9. (2) Cu: if the body receives a large amount of copper exceeding its needs, it will cause toxicity, including nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and stomach muscle bleeding, deteriorating the body's immune system and causing mental disorder10. (3) As: if the body experiences symptoms of acute poisoning, it causes tissue irritation, muscle spasms, hepatitis, liver damage, and brain damage, with complications related to heart function and death from heart failure11. (4) Pb: the element has both acute and chronic symptoms, including abdominal pain, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, hallucinations, depression, loss of consciousness, convulsions, dropping hands and feet, paralysis, coma, and death. The most often damaged organs include the bones, brain, kidneys, and thyroid gland12. Therefore, this research aims to remove these four heavy metals from wastewater because if the concentrations in natural water exceed the standard, they can cause several adverse effects on humans and animals.

Various methods exist for the separation of heavy metals from wastewater: precipitation, membrane filtration, photocatalysis, ion exchange, reverse osmosis, electrochemical process, and adsorption4,7,13,14, depending on the characteristics of the wastewater and the intended purpose of treatment. Studies investigating the process of adsorption show promise as an effective technique for removing heavy metals due to its simple and quick operation, low cost, and energy efficiency15. Porous materials, nanomaterials, carbon-based materials, agricultural waste materials, and polymeric materials are widely employed in the adsorption process, notably involving adsorbents with chelation groups such as iminoacetate, aminophosphonate, and amidoxime. The amidoxime group exhibits robust chelation activity against numerous trace elements and heavy metal ions, rendering chelating compounds with amidoxime groups highly desirable for enriching, extracting, and removing heavy metal ions. Nilchi et al.16 carried out the polymerization of acrylonitrile, divinyl benzene, and methacrylic acid to synthesize a resin with the amidoxime functional group. This resin underwent immersion in water samples containing heavy metal solutions, including UO22+, Pb2+, Co2+, Cd2+, and Cu2+, at pH values of 3 and 5 for 1 h at 25 °C. The findings indicated that the synthesized resin had a significant adsorption efficiency for several heavy metals, in the order UO22+ > Pb2+ > > Co2+ > Cu2+ > > Cd2+. Moreover, the resin demonstrated remarkable heavy metal adsorption efficiency in water samples with elevated pH values. The synthesis of amidoxime adsorbents has also been investigated by Hayashi et al.17. They used two distinct kinds of adsorbents to effectively remove chromium from wastewater: (1) an amidoxime fabric adsorbent modified by grafting of co-monomer (acrylonitrile/methacrylic acid) onto the surfaces of polyethylene and polypropylene fibers, followed by modification of the nitrile group to the amidoxime group, and (2) an adsorbent for ammonium fabric made by grafting chloromethylstyrene onto the surfaces of polyethylene and polypropylene fibers, subsequently transforming it through quaternization with a trimethylamine solution. The study revealed that the amidoxime fabric adsorbent exhibited the highest adsorption capacity for adsorbing chromium in the form of Cr(III), whereas the ammonium fabric adsorbent demonstrated the maximum adsorption capacity for chromium in the form of Cr(VI)17.

Different categories of amidoxime adsorbents have been employed to remove heavy metals from wastewater. Nonetheless, amidoxime adsorbents are well-known for their efficacy as uranium adsorbents in seawater, exhibiting distinctive functionality within amidoxime-type chelating materials. This is because the amidoxime group can establish strong bonds with uranyl ions. Most amidoxime materials are produced through the chemical transformation of polyacrylonitrile by using a hydroxylamine hydrochloride solution. Ratnitsai et al.18 experimented on the extraction of uranium from seawater using amidoximated absorbers derived from recycled low-density polyethylene (LDPE) sheets. A 40 µm-thick LDPE film was placed into a mixture of acrylonitrile and methacrylic acid and subjected to an optimized gamma-ray dosage of 40 kGy. The maximum reported adsorption capacity after 56 days of soaking was 1.79 g/kg. The reusability experiment, which lasted up to six cycles, indicated that around 20% of its adsorption capacity remained in the final cycle.

The preceding discussion shows that polyethylene adsorbents incorporating the amidoxime functional group are highly efficient in heavy metal removal. This research used waste food packaging bags (LDPE sheets) as a starting material, and they were subjected to irradiation and chemical processes to create high-performance adsorbent materials, removing the aforementioned four heavy metals in wastewater. The adsorbent characteristics were determined through scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and thermal gravimetric analysis (TGA) techniques. The adsorption experiments were conducted with wastewater samples in the laboratory and natural wastewater from industrial wastewater treatment plants containing solutions of various heavy metals, including Zn, Cu, As, and Pb. The value obtained from this research is the synthesized heavy metal adsorbents to help reduce heavy metal contamination in natural water sources caused by an accidental release of wastewater from industrial plants or chemical leakage into water sources. In addition, the synthesis of adsorbents derived from waste materials conforms to the Zero Waste concept that allows leftovers or waste to be reused or increases the value of those wastes to gain maximum benefits, signifying a sustainable concept.

Materials and methods

Materials

The LDPE sheets utilized in this study were derived from upcycled food containers with an average thickness of 50 µm. The following AR-grade chemicals acquired from Apex Chemicals were used as received: 98% acrylonitrile (AN), 99.9% methacrylic acid (MAA), 99% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 99% hydroxylamine hydrochloride (NH2OH·HCl), 99.8% N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF), methanol, hydrochloric acid (HCl), nitric acid (HNO3), potassium hydroxide (KOH), zinc chloride (ZnCl2), copper(II) chloride dihydrate (CuCl2·2H2O), arsenic trioxide (As2O3), and lead(II) nitrate (Pb(NO3)2). The methodology for preparing stock solutions is explained in the Supplementary document.

Methodology for synthesis of amidoxime adsorbent

Synthesis of the amidoxime adsorbent followed the methodology outlined by Ratnitsai et al.18. Firstly, the waste LDPE food bags were washed several times until they were clean and dried, and the weight of the LDPE sheet (W0) was recorded. After that, they were soaked in a mixed solution of AN and MAA monomers with a ratio of 60:40 V/V%. They were then subjected to gamma irradiation from Co-60 with a dose of 40 kGy and a dose rate of 0.03 kGy/min (Gammacell 220 Excel irradiation machine located at the Office of Atoms for Peace, Thailand), which was the appropriate radiation dose for the synthesis of LDPE sheet18 to form a cyano functional group (C ≡ N) on the LDPE sheet surface. This process produced a sheet called a co-grafted sheet. Post-irradiation, the co-grafted sheet was thoroughly washed with methanol/DI water to remove homopolymer before being dried in an oven for 1 h until a constant weight was achieved (W1). Subsequently, the cyano group was converted into the amidoxime group (AO, RC(NH2) = NOH) by soaking the co-grafted sheet in a neutral 3% NH2OH·HCl solution at a temperature of 80 °C for 1 h. This step produced an AO-sheet. After the reaction, the AO-sheet was soaked in 1 M HCl and subsequently dried in an oven for 1 h to attain a constant weight of the AO-sheet (W2). Characterization of adsorbent

Scanning electron microscopy (TESCAN, VEGA3 from the Czech Republic and JEOL, Model: JSM-IT700HR) was used to analyze the surface characteristics of the different stages of the sheet at a magnification of up to 10,000 times.

A Fourier-transform infrared spectrometer (Bruker, Model: TENSOR 27) was used to analyze the chemical functional group of synthesized sheets, with a wavenumber scan conducted between 600 and 4000 cm-1.

The rate of mass change with respect to the temperature of the LDPE sheet and AO-sheet was determined using the thermogravimetric curve (DTG) (Mettler Toledo, TGA 2). In the nitrogen atmosphere, the two types of sheets were heated at a rate of 10 °C/min from 30 to 500 °C.

Experiment on removal of heavy metals from wastewater

Since the AO-sheet must be treated in an alkaline solution prior to evaluating its ability to remove heavy metals, it was submerged in a 2.5 (w/v)% KOH at 80 °C for 1 h, then rinsed numerous times with DI water and dried.

Study of the effect of contact time on adsorption capacity

The efficiency of heavy element removal was evaluated by immersing the individual AO-sheet, size 2 cm × 2 cm, in 30 mL of wastewater sample containing each heavy element at room temperature for 0.43, 1, 2, and 4 weeks. The pH of the heavy metal solutions was constant at 7. The experimentation was conducted in triplicate, with different initial concentrations of heavy metals detailed in Table 1.

Study of the effect of pH solution on adsorption capacity

The AO-sheet, size 2 cm × 2 cm, was placed into a 30 mL wastewater solution containing four heavy metals: Zn(II) at 5 mg/L, Cu(II) at 2 mg/L, As(III) at 0.25 mg/L, and Pb(II) at 0.2 mg/L with different pHs of 6, 7, 8, and 9 for 0.43, 1, 2, and 4 weeks. Usage repeatability of AO-sheets

LDPE-based food packaging bags exhibit excellent physical properties, resistance to tensile strength, toughness, flexibility, and cracking resistance. Also, they demonstrate resistance to acids, alcohols, esters, bases, and other chemical substances and are highly stable against chemical, physical, and biological degradation. This study focused on investigating their reusability. To test the reuse of the AO-sheet, wastewater containing Zn (50 mg/L at pH 5.68) was selected as a representative metal. The AO-sheet underwent immersion in the wastewater for three days, constituting one cycle, after which it was removed and rinsed with DI water. After that, the AO-sheet was placed in 1 M HCl at 50 °C for 1 h to remove Zn(II) from the AO-sheet23, and the resulting solution was subjected to ICP-MS to analyze the concentration of Zn. Prior to immersing the AO-sheet in the subsequent cycle of wastewater, it was treated with an alkaline solution as described in Section "Experiment on removal of heavy metals from wastewater". The experiment studied the reusability a total of eight times.

To evaluate the efficiency of each type of eluent in the desorption process, the following eluents, HNO3, H2SO4, NaOH, NaNO3, and KOH with a concentration of 1 M were used in the desorption experiment. The AO-sheet was soaked in a 50 mg/L Zn(II) solution for three days and then immersed in the eluent solution at 50 °C for 1 h to remove Zn(II) from the AO-sheet. ICP-MS was utilized to analyze the amount of adsorbed Zn(II).

Adsorption experiment in natural wastewater

The wastewater treatment pond of Global Utilities Services Company Limited contains wastewater from various industrial plants in the Laem Chabang Industrial Estate, Chonburi Province, Thailand. The pond, with a temperature of 31 °C and a pH of 7 as measured in March 2023, contains numerous heavy metals whose concentrations are listed in Table S5. During the experiment, the 2 cm × 2 cm AO-sheet was submersed into the wastewater treatment pond for 0.43, 1, 2, 3, and 4 weeks. After that, the loaded AO-sheets were eluted with 1 M HCl to remove adsorbed elements. The initial concentrations of selected heavy metal ions in the wastewater, as well as adsorption capacity, were determined by ICP-MS.

Results and discussion

Degree of cografting and amidoxime group density

After simultaneous exposure of the LDPE sheet and monomers to gamma rays at a dose of 40 kGy, the monomers were subsequently grafted onto the LDPE surface. The degree of cografting was calculated according to Eq. (1), resulting in a calculated value of 86.33%. Gamma radiation induced AN and MAA monomers to become unstable, allowing them to graft onto the LDPE backbone. The cyano group from AN was then converted into the amidoxime group. The amidoxime group density, calculated using Eq. (2), was found to be 4.40 mol/kg. The degree of cografting and amidoxime group density in the present study are similar to the research of Ratnitsai et al., who revealed the highest degree of cografting at 40 kGy of 85.28% and the highest amidoxime group density of 4.38 mol/kg18.

SEM analysis

The morphology and surface appearance of the LDPE sheet at each stage were examined through SEM, as depicted in Figs. 2(a)–(c) and the results of Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS) mapping are shown in Fig. S1(a–g). Upon grafting with AN/MAA co-monomers, the surface of the LDPE sheet, illustrated in Fig. 2(b), exhibited an adhesive coating material across its entire surface, rendering it rough in appearance. This contrasts with the unmodified LDPE sheet shown in Fig. 2(a). Figure 2(c) illustrates that after the amidoximation process, the AO-sheet still had a coating attached to the surface, characterized by rough markings throughout. This observation suggests that the synthesized sheet has a larger surface area, enhancing its capacity to adsorb heavy metals dissolved in water.

The thickness of the LDPE sheet irradiated at 40 kGy was also determined through SEM, as shown in Fig. 3(a) and (b). Prior to grafting, the thickness was approximately 50 µm (Fig. 3a). After grafting and amidoximation, shown in Fig. 3(b), the thickness rose to roughly 80 µm, equivalent to around 160% of the original LDPE sheet. Identification of chemical functional groups

The various types of LDPE sheets LDPE sheet before grafting, co-grafted sheet, and AO-sheet were subjected to chemical functional group analysis using the FTIR technique, as shown in Fig. 4. Examination of the LDPE sheet spectrum in Fig. 4(a) shows the C-H alkyl stretch and C-H peak of polyethylene at wavenumbers 2848 and 1463 cm-1, respectively18. After irradiation-induced grafting with AN/MAA, the co-grafted sheet was obtained, as shown in Fig. 4(b). The wavenumbers 2243 and 1710 cm-1 were discovered referring to the C≡N or cyano group of polyacrylonitrile and C = O of polymethacrylic acid, respectively18,24. After the amidoxime process, depicted in Fig. 4(c), the cyano functional group (C≡N at 2243 cm-1) almost totally disappeared. Instead, peaks of N–H stretch, C = N, NH2, and O–H at wavenumbers 3373, 1656, 1560, and 1402 cm-1, respectively, which are peaks associated with the amidoxime group, appeared. These results support the change of the cyano functional group into the amidoxime functional group that occurred on the AO-sheet.

Analysis of thermal stability of synthesized sheet

The change in weight and mass loss rate of the LDPE and AO-sheet as a function of temperature range analyzed by the TGA technique are illustrated in Fig. 5(a) and (b). As shown in Fig. 5(a), the LDPE sheet had a single-stage mass loss at a temperature of about 450 °C, and the mass disappeared completely at a temperature of 500 °C, while the AO-sheet faced the three decompositions. The first temperature of decomposition at 50–125 °C can be related to the evaporation of moisture. The second one at 325–400 °C may be due to the loss of materials such as the amidoxime functional group and grafted chain. The main weight loss at 450–500 °C resulted from the thermal degradation of the covalent carbon bond (C–C) of polyethylene, which was also observed in the peak of the LDPE sheet25–27. Notably, the AO-sheet exhibited decomposition at a higher temperature than the LDPE sheet, including having the highest temperature that occurred during the decomposition period. The LDPE sheet had the highest decomposition temperature of 468.5 °C, while the AO-sheet showed an even higher maximum decomposition temperature of 482.5 °C, as shown in Fig. 5(b), indicating that the AO-sheet exhibited a higher thermal stability than the LDPE sheet. Therefore, the synthesized AO-sheet presented durability at high temperatures, rendering it well-suited for the removal of heavy ions at high temperatures and for long periods of time.

Effects of concentrations and submersion durations on adsorption of heavy metals from wastewater samples

The AO-sheets were tested for the adsorption of heavy ions in solutions of Zn(II), Cu(II), As(III), and Pb(II) metals at varying concentrations for periods of 0.43, 1, 2, and 4 weeks at room temperature. The results shown in Figs. 6(a)–(d) indicate that the adsorption of the AO-sheet rose with an elevated concentration of heavy elements for all heavy elements. Figure 6(a) demonstrates the absorption of Zn(II) by the AO adsorbent at the initial concentrations of 5, 25, and 50 mg/L. The results reveal that during the period of 0.43–2 weeks, the adsorption capacity of Zn(II) increased with longer submersing time across all concentrations. However, beyond two weeks and up to four weeks of submersion, the Zn(II) adsorption capacity began to stabilize. At the soaking time of four weeks, the AO-sheet adsorbed Zn(II) up to 21.07 mg-Zn/g-adsorbent at the initial concentration of 50 mg/L. Therefore, a soaking period of four weeks is appropriate for Zn(II) adsorption. Compared with Anirudhan et al.’s research28, which used amidoximated cellulose-based polymer as an adsorbent for removing Zn(II) at the same initial solution concentration of 50 mg/L and the immersion period of 4 h, the adsorbent was able to adsorb 20 mg/g Zn(II), similar to the results of the current study.

(a) Adsorption capacity of AO-sheet for Zn, (b) Adsorption capacity of AO-sheet for Cu, (c) Adsorption capacity of AO-sheet for As, (d) Adsorption capacity of AO-sheet for Pb.

Figure 6(b) depicts the Cu(II) adsorption capacity of the AO-sheet at the initial concentrations of 2, 10, and 20 mg/L. The findings indicate that during the period of 0.43–2 weeks for every concentration, the longer soaking time enhanced the Cu(II) adsorption capacity. After two weeks to four weeks of submersion, the Cu(II) adsorption efficiency began to decrease slightly, averaging 2%. This observation is consistent with the findings of Schmuhl et al.29 in the study on the adsorption of Cu(II) ions by chitosan. This study showed that higher initial concentrations led to increased and faster Cu(II) adsorption compared to low concentrations. Therefore, at an optimal two weeks of submersion, AO-sheets exhibited the maximum capacity to adsorb Cu of 7.98 mg-Cu/g-adsorbent at the initial 20 mg/L concentration.

Figure 6(c) presents the As(III) adsorption of the AO-sheet at the initial concentrations of 0.25, 1.25, and 2.5 mg/L. The results showed that within the period of 0.43–1 week at all concentrations, there was a positive association between the duration of immersion and the adsorption efficiency of As(III), particularly pronounced at the concentration of 2.5 mg/L. Following one to four weeks of submersion, a decline in adsorption capacity was observed across all concentrations. Notably, at the soaking time of 1 week, the AO-sheet demonstrated the ability to adsorb As(III) at a maximum of 1.07 g-As /kg-adsorbent at 2.5 mg/L concentration. Therefore, a soaking period of one week is appropriate for As(III) adsorption. Bayuo and the research group studied the adsorption of As(III) with a plant-derived biosorbent. They found that the amount of equilibrium As(III) adsorbed was 2.5 mg/g when using an initial concentration of 10 mg/L with a contact time of 1 h (interestingly, the same amount of As(III) was adsorbed even with a longer contact time)30. Comparatively, the present work reached a maximum adsorption capacity of 1.07 mg/g after one week with 2.5 mg/L initial concentration, an initial concentration four times less than Bayuo et al.'s research. Had the same initial concentration been used, the present work would have adsorbed more Ar(III). This AO-sheet is effective in the adsorption of heavy metals, initially displaying rapid adsorption within a short period of time. Subsequently, with longer soaking periods, the adsorption capacity was gradually reduced, likely because the quantity of amidoxime functional groups in the adsorbent that bonded with heavy metals also decreased. As a result, the adsorption efficiency of heavy metals was low.

Figure 6(d) illustrates Pb(II) adsorption of the AO-sheet at the initial concentrations of 0.2, 1, and 2 mg/L. The results show that with an extended soaking period, the Pb(II) adsorption efficiency was almost constant (with a slight increase of 2% on average) for all concentrations. At a soaking time of four weeks for 2 mg/L initial concentration, the AO-sheet adsorbed Pb up to a maximum of 0.855 mg-Pb/g-adsorbent. Therefore, it is inferred that a soaking time of only 0.43 week (or three days) is suitable for Pb adsorption. When compared with the same soaking time of three days, Rahman et al.'s study31 was able to adsorb 107 mg/g of Pb(II) at an initial concentration of 500 mg/L, which was significantly higher than the present investigation of 2 mg/L. Therefore, if the present work had used a much higher initial concentration of 500 mg/L, Pb(II) it likely would have been absorbed at 212 mg/g (a very simple estimate assuming the adsorption rate increased linearly with the initial concentration and ignoring adsorption equilibrium), being approximately two times higher than Rahman et al.'s finding.

When comparing the adsorption capacity of Zn(II) and Cu(II) at comparable initial concentrations (Zn(II) = 25 mg/L, Cu(II) = 20 mg/L), it was observed that the AO-sheet was able to absorb Zn(II) approximately 23% better than Cu(II). Likewise, an examination of the adsorption capacities of As(III) and Pb(II) at similar initial concentrations (As(III) = 1.25 mg/L, Pb(II) = 1 mg/L) revealed that Pb(II) exhibited higher adsorption capacity than As(III). The sequence of the adsorption rates can be arranged as follows: Zn(II) > Cu(II) > Pb(II) > As(III). From Fig. 6(a)–(d), they indicate an increase in adsorption capacity with the rising initial concentration of metal ions. This implies a pronounced dependence of adsorption on the initial concentration of metal ions. At lower concentrations, the proportion between the initial quantity of metal ions and the accessible surface area diminishes, resulting in a smaller adsorption capacity. Conversely, higher initial concentrations heighten the probability of collisions with adsorption sites on the surface of the adsorbent, thereby enhancing the attraction between heavy metals and amidoxime groups present on the adsorbent's surface. Furthermore, the improvement in the driving force of mass transfer proves advantageous in diminishing mass transfer resistance and increasing adsorption capacity22.

Submersion duration influences the adsorption capacity between the heavy metal solution and adsorbent material. The longer the submersion duration, the more likely the likelihood of achieving equilibrium, thereby maximizing adsorption capacity. Conversely, inadequate submersion duration hinders the adsorption process32. This study clearly indicates that the initial concentration of heavy metals significantly influences the adsorption capacity, with the optimal adsorption period being approximately one week. However, the present study did not investigate shorter immersion durations (hour level), which may provide more distinct results, allowing a more exact interpretation of the substances involved.

Effect of pH on adsorption of heavy metals from wastewater samples

The pH value can determine the characteristics of the surface charge of the AO-sheet and the formation of metal ion species. The influence of pH on the adsorption of the AO-sheet was investigated through the pH values of 6.0, 7.0, 8.0, and 9.0 with different pH for 0.43, 1, 2, and 4 weeks at 30 °C. The results shown in Fig. 7(a)–(d) demonstrate that the higher pH values enhance the adsorption capacity of the AO-sheet for heavy metals, with the exception of As. The adsorption capacity for Zn(II) with varying pH, as depicted in Fig. 7(a), indicated that when the pH value increased, the Zn(II) adsorption efficiency increased at the submerging time of 0.43–1 week. Particularly, at a pH of 9.0, the AO-sheets displayed a maximum Zn adsorption of 2.14 mg-Zn/g-adsorbent within 0.43 weeks. Between two and four weeks of submersion, it can be observed that, at higher pH values, Zn(II) adsorption efficiency becomes relatively stable. It is also noteworthy that throughout each soaking period, the Zn(II) adsorption efficiency was high at pH values of 8.0 and 9.0, which corresponds to the research of Nilchi and the research group16, as well as Ratnitsai and the research group33, who investigated uranium recovery from seawater using the amidoxime film. Also, Anirudhan et al.28 discovered that the efficiency of Zn(II) removal increased with rising the pH and achieved a maximum capacity at a pH of 10.5. Therefore, the AO-sheet synthesized in the present work is suitable for the effective adsorption of Zn(II) from wastewater with pH values of 8.0 and 9.0.

(a) Effect of pH on adsorption of Zn, (b) Effect of pH on adsorption of Cu, (c) Effect of pH on adsorption of As, (d) Effect of pH on adsorption of Pb.

The results of Cu adsorption at various pH values are shown in Fig. 7(b). The result indicates that an elevation in pH led to an increase in the efficiency of Cu(II) adsorption. Particularly, it was observed that at pH values of 7.0, 8.0, and 9.0, the Cu(II) adsorption efficiency exhibited similarly high levels, with pH values of 8.0 and 9.0 achieving the maximum Cu(II) adsorption capacity of 0.85 mg-Cu/g-adsorbent. This trend corresponded with the Zn(II) adsorption efficiency. Many investigations have reported the precipitation of heavy metals, such as Cu and Zn, in solutions with a pH exceeding 7.034–36. However, the present study observed no precipitation of heavy metals for all pH values. Therefore, the AO-sheet synthesized in this research is suitable for use in the adsorption of Cu(II) from wastewater with pH values of 8.0 and 9.0.

The results of As adsorption under different pH values are depicted in Fig. 7(c). Remarkably, at a pH of 6.0, 7.0, and 8.0, the adsorption efficiency of As(III) remained constant, equal to 0.10 g-As/kg-adsorbent. However, when the pH value was increased to 9.0, the As(III) adsorption decreased to 0.08 mg-As/g-adsorbent. As a result, the AO-sheet is suitable for adsorbing As(III) from wastewater with a pH range of 6.0–8.0. Humelnicu et al.20 also observed a pH of 8.0 as the optimal pH for removing As(III) by crosslinked amidoxime resin housed by mesoporous silica, and higher pH levels showed a similar tendency to the current investigation.

Figure 7(d) illustrates the findings of Pb adsorption across different pH values. The Pb(II) adsorption efficiency was found to follow a consistent pattern at each pH level, with a maximum Pb(II) adsorption seen at pH values of 7.0, 8.0, and 9.0, ranging from 0.084 to 0.086 mg-Pb/g-adsorbent. This is consistent with the observed efficiency in Zn(II) and Cu(II) adsorption. The investigation by Khedr19 also revealed that the optimal pH for maximum removal of Pb(II) using co-grafted acrylonitrile/acrylic acid on a PE film was found at pH 9.0. As a result, the AO-sheet developed in this work is appropriate for the efficient adsorption of Pb(II) from wastewater with a pH range of 7.0–9.0.

The rationale behind the observed adsorption phenomena with respect to the pH range could be elucidated as follows: (1) In the pH range of 6.0–7.0, a large number of H+ and H3O+ ions engage in competition with three metal ions, excluding As(III), to occupy the amidoxime site on the surface of the adsorbent, slowing down the adsorption capacity of metal ions36,37. In addition, the presence of positive charges arising from the protonation of NH2 produces a robust electrostatic repulsion with the positive charge of metal ions. Furthermore, the NH2 group undergoes conversion into NH3+ (Eq. (8)36), leading to fewer available NH2 to bind with metal ions (Eq. (9)36). These factors contribute to a decrease in the adsorption capacity of metal ions35,36. (2) Within the pH range of 8.0–9.0, the amidoxime group is deprotonated, acquiring a negative charge, which induces a robust electrostatic attraction with positively charged species of several metal ions (Eq. (11)36) except As(III) in the solution, thereby enhancing the adsorption capacity. However, in the case of the As(III) ion, at a pH of 9.0, the elevated pH condition leads to increased interaction of OH- with NH2 through hydrogen bonding (Eq. (10)36). This results in a reduction of As(III) adsorption at the surface of the adsorbent, a process known as surface complexation36.

Furthermore, according to earlier studies11, the ionic state of heavy ions in an aqueous solution varies substantially depending on pH because of the complexation of heavy metal ions with hydrogen ions (H+) or hydroxide ions (OH-). The adsorption mechanisms for distinct ionic states of heavy metals change significantly on the active site of AO, resulting in discrepancies in adsorption capacity under discrepancies under various pH settings.

he probable mechanism involves amidoxime anions acting as bidentate chelating ligands and metal cations, capable of capturing metal ions and facilitating the formation of a five-membered ring complex, as shown in Fig. S2. It comprises an initial step wherein an electrostatic ion pair forms between the metal and the ligand, followed by the departure of an H2O from the coordination. Subsequently, the water molecule is replaced by the ligand in the ensuing step. This phase exhibits slower kinetics, with the rate contingent upon the energy of the metal–ligand bond, often known as the Eigen–Wilkins mechanism. Variations in binding capacities correlate with the logarithm of kH2O. The rate constant depends upon the number of d electrons in cations38.

Adsorption isotherm

The results of the non-linearized Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms plots of Zn(II), Cu(II), As(III), and Pb(II) adsorption onto AO-sheet are illustrated in Fig. S3(a–d). It is evident that the adsorption capacity increased as the initial concentration for all heavy metal ions rose. The predicted isotherm parameters and their corresponding R2 are shown in Table S1. When analyzing the non-linear isotherm, differences were observed between the values of experimental data and the predicted values from the isotherm model39. To evaluate the model's fit to the experimental data, six error functions were used: the coefficient of determination (R2), sum of squares error (SSE), average relative error (ARE), hybrid fractional error function (HYBRID), sum of the absolute errors (EABS), and Marquardt’s percent standard deviation (MPSED), as detailed in Table S2. These error functions were analyzed using the solver add-in for Microsoft Excel (Microsoft™ 365). The minimized error values were considered optimal39. Upon comparing the R2 results and the error function values in Table S3, it was determined that the Langmuir model provided the most accurate description of the sorption phenomena of the studied heavy metals. This conclusion is supported by the highest R2 value and the lowest values for ARE, HYBRID, EABS, and MSPD when using the Langmuir isotherm model, indicating that the adsorption of the investigated heavy metal ions follows a monolayer adsorption40.

Kinetic study

The graphs illustrating the adsorption kinetic curves, pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order, for Zn(II), Cu(II), As(III), and Pb(II) on the AO-sheet are shown in Figs. S4–S7, and the parameters of the kinetic models are presented in Table S4. The data obtained from the experiments suggests that the pseudo-second-order kinetic model is superior for describing the adsorption process, as indicated by the coefficient of determination values (R2) being closest to one. Additionally, the calculated qe values (qe,cal) closely matched the experimental qe values (qe,exp). These results imply that the adsorption of the studied heavy metal ions by the AO-sheet involves chemisorption31.

Usage repeatability of AO-sheets

Various acid and base desorption agents were assessed for their effectiveness in extracting heavy metals from the adsorbent. The results shown in Fig. S8 demonstrated that the use of HCl, HNO3, and H2SO4 (acid eluents) resulted in a higher Zn(II) elution compared to NaOH, NaNO3, and KOH (alkaline eluents). Among the six eluents tested, HCl exhibited the highest desorption performance. This is attributed to the significant number of hydronium ions present in HCl, which can effectively replace the Zn(II) ions adsorbed onto the adsorbent. These findings align with the research by Oyola et al., which reported HCl as being the most efficient eluent for removing Cu(II) from amidoxime-based PE fiber41. Therefore, the appropriate desorption agent for reusability investigation was HCl.

The evaluation of adsorbent materials by desorption and adsorption is a crucial indicator. Following the heavy metal adsorption in the previous section, the AO-sheet was subjected to a reusability test over eight cycles. The result, shown in Fig. 8, indicates a diminishing efficiency in Zn(II) adsorption with consecutive cycles of adsorption–desorption. The Zn(II) adsorption efficiency of the 8th cycle diminished by 20.37% compared to the first cycle, suggesting a potential for continued reuse. The reason for the decrease in efficiency may be due to the elevated reuse of the AO-sheet, resulting in fewer amidoxime functional groups that are responsible for adsorbing Zn(II). Furthermore, the reduction in adsorption capacity can be caused by eluting with strong acid and alkaline conditioning with base in multiple cycles, resulting in surface damage to the adsorbent and subsequent degradation of the amidoxime functional group. If a lower acid concentration was used, the adsorption efficiency may not have been significantly reduced.

Adsorption capacity in a natural pond

After immersing the AO-sheet in the pond for four weeks, as illustrated in Fig. 9, the results indicate that the longer the soaking time, the higher the adsorption capacity of all elements. Notably, the AO-sheet exhibited the highest uptake capacity for Zn(II) among the heavy metals, achieving an 85-fold increase by the fourth week. In addition, the Zn(II) and Cu(II) adsorption in the natural wastewater treatment pond exceeded that in the laboratory setup despite lower initial concentrations (Fig. 6a and b). This result is likely due to the pond's substantial, heavy metal content and continuous aeration and circulation, which enhance adsorption. For Zn(II), the adsorption increased continuously with soaking time until four weeks, exhibiting a maximum Zn(II) uptake of 8.85 mg-Zn/g. For Cu(II), the adsorption capacity increased by 277.24% between 0.43 and 2 weeks, peaking at 1.27 mg-Cu/g after four weeks. Similarly, Mn(II) adsorption reached 1.28 mg-Mn/g in the fourth week.

Adsorption capacity of AO-sheet in real wastewater pond.

The study of heavy metal adsorption in the natural wastewater pond provides a clear timeline. Initially, the adsorbent, being rich in amidoxime functional groups on its surface, effectively adsorbed large amounts of heavy metal ions during the first 1–2 weeks of submersion. As time progressed, the number of available amidoxime groups decreased, leading to a slower rate of uptake, even appearing to reach equilibrium for the Mn(II) case. The results of the adsorption capacity of As(III), Pb(II), and Ba(II) were undetectable at all immersion times, aligning with the absence of these metal ions in the wastewater treatment pond samples.

Interestingly, even though Cr(III), Ni(II), and Cd(II) were not initially detected in the wastewater samples when the AO-sheet adsorbent was immersed in the pond, the findings revealed its capability to adsorb these metals, likely facilitated by the continuous inflow and outflow of wastewater in the treatment pond. This process allowed the AO-sheet to accumulate significant amounts of Cr(III), Ni(II), and Cd(II) on its surface, particularly in the third and fourth weeks, which demonstrates a significant increase in uptake capacity.

For example, Anirudhan's research28 used the concentration of 100 mg/L, which resulted in an absorption value of 33.5 mg/g of Cu(II) and 45.8 mg/g of Zn(II). When comparing the adsorption capacity with Rahman's work31 using a similar supporting material (PE), it was found that Rahman's research exhibited an 8.4-fold higher Cu(II) absorption and a 125-fold higher Pb(II) absorption compared to the current investigation, with the copper concentration being more than 15 times and the lead concentration being more than 250 times. From the table above, it is evident that various types of substrates, including synthetic polymers, resins, or cellulose modified with the amidoxime functional group, have significant ability in metal absorption, indicating their potential utility in the field.

The factors that contribute to the adsorbent material's ability to adsorb high amounts of heavy metals are: (1) Increasing the surface area of the AO-sheet while maintaining its lightweight nature; (2) Having a significantly high co-grafted degree, which maximizes the conversion to amidoxime functional groups to the greatest extent possible; (3) Utilizing a high initial concentration; and (4) Maintaining long-term stability in harsh conditions. Therefore, it is crucial to maintain the adsorbent in these optimal states to achieve a high adsorption capacity.

Conclusion

An AO-sheet adsorbent was generated by amidoximation of gamma-radiation-grafted polyacrylonitrile and polymethacrylic acid on a waste food packaging bag (LDPE sheet) as a starting material. This AO-sheet exhibited a high adsorption capacity for Zn(II), Cu(II), As(III), and Pb(II) from their aqueous medium. The characteristics of the AO-sheet were analyzed through several techniques, including SEM, FTIR, and TGA, which showed favorable characteristics for the adsorption of heavy metals. The removal of heavy metal ions by utilizing the AO-sheet was affected by various factors, including pH, metal ion concentration, and submerging time. In the batch-wise adsorption study, the optimal pH for studied metal ions fell within the range of 8.0 to 9.0, contingent upon the specific metal ion under consideration. The maximum adsorption capacity of the AO-sheet followed the sequence Zn > Cu > Pb > As. The capacity of Zn(II) adsorption increased as the initial concentration increased, reaching up to 21.07 mg-Zn/g-adsorbent. The results from the adsorption isotherm study show that the Langmuir model most accurately explained the sorption phenomena of the studied heavy metals. This was characterized by monolayer adsorption. At an initial concentration of 100 mg/L, the absorption capacity of 42 mg/g of Zn(II) closely matched Anirudhan's findings28. Evaluation of the synthesized adsorbent in the natural wastewater pond indicated exceptional uptake capacities for Zn(II), Cu(II), Mn(II), Cr(III), Ni(II), and Cd(II), even though some of these heavy metals were not detected in the wastewater pond. The AO adsorbent was eluted using 1 M HCl, which was an appropriate desorption agent, and it could be used repetitively eight times while retaining around 80% of its initial adsorption capacities. These findings demonstrate that the AO-sheet derived from waste materials can effectively remove several heavy metal ions from wastewater.
