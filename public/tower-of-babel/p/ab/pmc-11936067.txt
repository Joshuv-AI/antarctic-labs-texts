A Perfect Storm: Case of Chronic Albumin Loss, Multi-Morbidity and Antibiotic Resistance
Authors: NAYAK SNEHASIS, SAYED ZAFAR
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Comorbidity is described as the presence of many health disorders in one individual, which may be connected or arise independently. We report a 61-year-old male patient with a complex history of recurrent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and chronic hypoalbuminemia presenting to the emergency department due to sudden left lower limb swelling, pain, coldness, and bluish discoloration of the foot, leading to a severe infection necessitating a below-knee amputation. Even after extensive workup, the etiology behind hypoalbuminemia remained unknown with Protein-losing enteropathy (PLE) suspected, but not confirmed. This is a very peculiar case and highlights the challenges in managing such patients while emphasizing the need for a multidisciplinary approach in long-term care.

Introduction

Low blood albumin, also known as hypoalbuminemia, occurs in a variety of clinical circumstances and can be caused by increased loss, decreased production, or redistribution of albumin.

Albumin also plays a significant role in regulating plasma oncotic pressure [1].

Additionally, hypoalbuminemia may serve as an indicator of negative health outcomes [2].

Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) on the other hand is a disorder in which blood clots form in deep veins, typically in the legs.

The combination of chronic hypoalbuminemia, recurrent DVT, and severe infections is quite uncommon, but these conditions could happen simultaneously in certain patients.

In this report, we present a unique case that illustrates all of these conditions and aim to establish a connection with existing research on hypoalbuminemia, thrombosis, and infections.

Case Report

On March 19, 2021, a 61-year-old man came to the emergency room with a one-day history of sudden swelling, pain, coldness, and blue discoloration in his left lower limb.

He had a notable history of recurrent deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and had been on warfarin therapy for over 15 years.

The patient additionally mentioned a severe infection in the same leg, stemming from a minor wound caused by a fall, which led him to seek hospital care.

After an initial evaluation, a duplex ultrasound and CT angiogram were conducted.

The CT angiogram showed significant narrowing of the lumen in the left femoral stent, along with a brief segment of complete blockage at the knee level, consistent with critical limb ischemia.

The right lower limb also showed attenuation in the popliteal artery and proximal anterior tibial artery.

Patient was referred to the surgical department, where a thrombectomy was planned.

Surgical debridement prior to Silvercel™ dressing

As part of his management, the patient’s anticoagulation therapy was switched from warfarin, which he had been on for 15 years, to apixaban to better manage his DVT.

Additionally, antibiotics were administered to treat the infection; however, the response to the treatment was minimal.

On April 4, 2021, surgical debridement was performed to remove necrotic tissue and clean the infected area, with Silvercel™ dressing applied to promote healing (Figures 1, 2).

Silvercel™ dressing on the wound

However, despite these efforts, no significant improvement was observed. Shortly after surgery, the patient suffered a stroke.

Given his existing anticoagulation therapy with apixaban (Eliquis 5mg twice daily), no new intervention was deemed necessary by the treating physicians.

Post-stroke seizures were managed with levetiracetam (Keppra), and the patient did not require any additional anti-stroke medication.

Blood tests prior to the stroke revealed high thyroid-stimulating hormone and low thyroxine levels, leading to a prescription of levothyroxine for hypothyroidism.

Severe hypoalbuminemia was also noted and managed with increasing albumin dosages.

Despite ongoing treatment, the infection persisted, necessitating a left below-knee amputation on 23 April 2021 (Figure 3).

Left below-knee amputation done after failed treatment modalities

Post-amputation, a mild wound infection was managed with daily dressing changes.

The patient’s condition improved with nasogastric feeding and oral fluids, allowing for discharge on 4 May 2021.

The discharge medications were initially prescribed for one month are as follows:

Apixaban 5mg twice daily;

Acetylsalicylic Acid 81mg once daily;

Rosuvastatin 10mg once daily;

Senna 8.6mg once daily;

Omeprazole 20mg once daily;

Furosemide 20mg twice daily;

Levetiracetam 250mg twice daily;

Phenytoin 100mg every 8 hours;

Paracetamol 1000mg every 6 hours for one week;

Carvedilol 6.25mg twice daily;

Cefuroxime Axetil 500mg twice daily for the infection;

Chlorhexidine Gluconate 0.2% Mouthwash Solution three times daily.

The patient had been an administrative coordinator at several factories, with no history of smoking or drinking and no genetic predisposition in his family.

He had a solitary kidney; as he lost one kidney due to renal artery thrombosis resulting in kidney atrophy and a history of hypoalbuminemia and anasarca for 15-20 years, initially managed with 200mL of intravenous albumin 25% and spironolactone, and later with furosemide when weight increased significantly.

Kidney and liver function tests were normal.

He had ischemic heart disease with a percutaneous coronary intervention performed 9 months prior to presentation, a small liver haemangioma, a long-standing history of hyperlipidaemia and a femoro-popliteal stent placed in 2014, which subsequently thrombosed.

Celiac screening in 2017 was negative.

Medications prior to presentation included diuretics (furosemide and spironolactone), anticoagulants (warfarin, then apixaban), leptin analogs, DPP-4 inhibitors, atorvastatin, levothyroxine, levetiracetam, aspirin and omeprazole.

In 2022, he was hospitalized for infection with rash, cellulitis of the testes, anasarca and ascites.

He developed resistance to several antibiotics and was treated with piperacillin/tazobactam, then was upgraded to meropenem with clindamycin, along with intravenous heparin and drainage for ascites.

He was discharged after 3 weeks.

Further investigations included alpha-antitrypsin, serum and prealbumin tests in July 2022, revealing high anti-trypsin and albumin levels in stool, suggestive of protein-losing enteropathy (PLE).

Fourteen biopsy specimens from the colon and rectum were taken to rule out amyloidosis or microscopic colitis, all of which were normal.

Follow-up histopathology showed no abnormalities.

Colonoscopy yielded a Boston bowel preparation scale score of 1-2-1, with retroflexion in the rectum revealing internal haemorrhoids but otherwise normal findings up to the cecum.

A rapid urease test was negative, but the definitive cause of hypoalbuminemia remained unknown.

Current management includes apixaban, atorvastatin, levothyroxine, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors, spironolactone, furosemide, aspirin, omeprazole, and supplementation with zinc, calcium and vitamin B12.

Continued management of recurrent infections is ongoing.

Mirtazapine and escitalopram were added in 2023 for depression.

As of 22 July 2024, the patient underwent routine blood tests including a complete blood count (CBC), liver function tests (LFT), kidney function tests (KFT), urine analysis, and assessment of Protein C, Protein S, and antithrombin III levels.

The results revealed persistently low serum albumin levels despite a normal urine analysis.

Protein S activity was also found to be lower than normal.

New findings included elevated aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, alongside elevated neutrophil and lymphocyte counts.

To manage the elevated liver enzymes, the patient was prescribed Hepasyl™ capsules for 10 days.

Additionally, antibiotics were administered for 2 weeks to address any underlying infections contributing to the abnormal blood results.

Discussion

Hypercholesterolemia causes reduced wound angiogenesis and delayed wound healing because LDL has antiangiogenic qualities due to its antiproliferative effects.

Plasma hypercholesterolemia slows wound closure and inhibits angiogenesis throughout the healing process [3].

Development of peripheral artery disease is independently correlated with hyperlipidaemia and is linked to major consequences, such as a high risk of amputations, stressing the need of prevention [4, 5].

Hypoalbuminemia has been found to be strongly associated with higher morbidity and death in patients with ischemic stroke.

Several investigations found a higher frequency of hypoalbuminemia in ischemic stroke patients, with in-hospital death rates significantly higher in those with low albumin levels [6, 7].

The link between reduced blood albumin levels and early cardiovascular events and death in ischemic stroke patients is well-documented, emphasizing the importance of close monitoring and treatment of albumin levels [8].

Low levels of serum albumin have been also associated with poor outcomes both in the general population and in patients with cardiovascular diseases like PAD, peripheral artery diseases [9].

Hypoalbuminemia predicts infectious consequences in non-infectious disorders and is associated with the onset and severity of certain infections.

It is an independent risk factor for death in Clostridium difficile infections, with altered pharmacokinetics resulting in low medication concentrations at the infection site [10, 11].

Antimicrobial drugs' efficacy and possible toxicity are influenced by protein binding.

Hypoalbuminemia considerably influences the pharmacokinetics of some antibiotics such as beta-lactams and ceftriaxone, boosting the likelihood of adverse drug responses [12].

In the context of Virchow's triad, hypoalbuminemia is associated with endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability and venous stasis, all of which contribute to thrombosis.

Some studies link endothelial dysfunction to hypoalbuminemia [13].

Although the association between hypoalbuminemia and hypercoagulability is debatable, while one study suggests that albumin has a procoagulant impact [14], while other report that hypoalbuminemia is a risk factor for excessive anticoagulation [15].

Hypoalbuminemia also raises red cell lysophosphatidylcholine, which increases blood viscosity [16], resulting in venous stasis.

Therefore, hypoalbuminemia could be linked to endothelial dysfunction, hypercoagulability and venous stasis-all of which are major components of Virchow's triad-so it is possible that it plays a significant role in increasing the risk of thrombosis [17].

PLE is an uncommon cause of low protein levels.

The incidence of PLE is unknown due to a lack of frequent screening and a variety of causes.

The presence of alpha-1-antitrypsin in feces serves as a diagnostic test for PLE [18].

Treatment for PLE focuses on managing the underlying condition, with supportive treatments addressing symptoms such as edema [18].
