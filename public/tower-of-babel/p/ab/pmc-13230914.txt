Investigation of the damping characteristics of grouted coal gangue with different particle gradations under multistage triaxial cyclic loading
Authors: Yutao Wang, Mingming He, Ning Li, Jing Wang, Yanzhe Tian, Xuge Mao, Xinxing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding and optimizing the damping behavior of grouted coal gangue under complex cyclic stress conditions is of significant importance for guiding grouting design, improving mine safety, and enhancing the long-term durability of engineering structures. Triaxial multistage cyclic tests were conducted on artificial specimens with varying confining pressures and particle gradations to elucidate the evolution of damping in grouted coal gangue under cyclic loading. The stress and strain response, as well as the variation of dynamic shear modulus, damping ratio, and damping coefficient with the number of loading cycles, were investigated. The results indicate that the grouted coal gangue exhibits a staged loading and steady cyclic response under multistage cyclic loading, with irrecoverable deformation accumulating progressively with increasing stress levels. Confining pressure enhances the cyclic stability and dynamic stiffness of the specimens by suppressing crack opening and particle slippage, thereby improving skeleton interlocking and load-bearing continuity. Within each stress level, the dynamic shear modulus demonstrates a rapid increase followed by fluctuating evolution trend with cycle number but decreases overall with rising stress. Elastic energy density and energy dissipation density evolve in stages, exhibiting an initial rapid decline followed by gradual attenuation during mid-cycles. The dissipation energy density fluctuating more intensely under high stress levels. The damping ratio and damping coefficient generally decay and converge with increasing cycles and display a negative correlation with the dynamic shear modulus. These findings reveal the coupled effects of confining pressure and particle gradation on the damping characteristics of grouted coal gangue, providing an experimental basis for assessing the stability and fatigue damage of grouted structures under cyclic loads.

Introduction

With the continual advancement of energy extraction and mining engineering, grouted coal gangue filling technology has been widely applied in the resource utilization of mining waste and surrounding rock support systems1–5. The damping characteristics of grouted coal gangue reflect its energy dissipation capacity and inelastic deformation behavior, serving as a crucial indicator for evaluating its dynamic stability and fatigue resistance6–10. Grouted materials are often subjected to multistage cyclic loading in practical engineering, including in-situ stress, vibrational loads, and mechanical impacts11–16. This loading induces highly nonlinear, stress level dependent, and cumulative damage effects in the damping response of the material15,17–19. Therefore, investigating the damping behavior of grouted coal gangue under complex cyclic stress conditions is essential for optimizing grouting design, enhancing mine safety, and prolonging the service life of engineering structures3,20–22.

In recent years, cyclic loading tests have shown that rock materials typically exhibit pronounced stress-strain hysteresis under periodic loading23–26. The area enclosed by the hysteresis loops is used to quantify the energy dissipated in a single cycle, providing a foundation for the calculation of dynamic energy indicators such as damping ratio and damping coefficient27–30. The complex coupled relationships between damping parameters and stress amplitude, strain amplitude, confining pressure, and loading path have been progressively revealed29,31,32. Damping parameters generally exhibit nonlinear evolution with stress or strain levels and demonstrate strong sensitivity to the applied loading path during staged or multi-level cyclic loading33–35. Energy dissipation is influenced by confining pressure through the regulation of crack closure and normal stress at contact surfaces, which affects frictional s`liding and crack opening-closing processes, leading to significant variations in damping response36,37. The different strain paths induce stage-dependent evolution of the damping ratio, damping coefficient, and dynamic elastic modulus under triaxial staged cyclic loading38,39. A clear correlation is observed between the damping parameters and the dynamic modulus27,28,33,34. However, most of these studies focus primarily on natural rock or cemented geomaterials under relatively simple loading paths, and the understanding of damping evolution in artificial granular–cemented composites such as grouted coal gangue remains limited.

In addition, particle size gradation plays a crucial role in governing the internal structure and mechanical behavior of granular composite materials40. Previous studies have demonstrated that variations in particle gradation significantly influence the formation of load-bearing skeletons, interparticle contact conditions, and the stability of the internal structure41–44. Well-graded particle systems generally exhibit stronger interlocking and greater structural stability, whereas poorly graded materials tend to experience more pronounced particle rearrangement and frictional sliding during loading42. These microstructural differences directly affect energy dissipation mechanisms and consequently the damping response of the material40. Despite its significance, the influence of particle gradation on the damping characteristics of grouted coal gangue under cyclic loading has received relatively limited attention in existing studies.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on single stress amplitudes or simple cyclic paths. Grouted materials are typically subjected to staged, multilevel, and incrementally increasing cyclic loads in engineering practice23–25,45. The coupled effects of varying confining pressures and loading stages significantly influence the internal structural evolution and energy dissipation processes of the grouted material under triaxial stress conditions45. However, the current understanding still exhibits two key deficiencies. First, the evolution pattern of damping parameters in grouted coal gangue under triaxial multistage cyclic loading has not been systematically clarified. Second, the coupled influence of confining pressure and particle gradation on the damping behavior and energy dissipation mechanisms of grouted coal gangue remains insufficiently understood. These limitations make it difficult to accurately evaluate the dynamic stability and fatigue performance of grouted structures under realistic engineering stress conditions. Therefore, a systematic investigation of the damping characteristics and the evolution in grouted coal gangue under triaxial multistage cyclic loading is necessary. Special attention should be given to the coupled effects of confining pressure and particle gradation to more accurately replicate realistic engineering stress conditions.

In this study, triaxial multistage cyclic tests were conducted on artificial grouted coal gangue specimens with varying confining pressures and particle gradations. The evolution of dynamic shear modulus, damping ratio, and damping coefficient under successive loading cycles was systematically investigated, with particular emphasis on the coupled effects of confining pressure and particle gradation on damping characteristics. The primary innovation of this work lies in the integration of these dual variables with multistage cyclic loading, enabling a more realistic simulation of engineering conditions. The findings elucidate the combined influence of confining pressure and particle gradation on the damping behavior of grouted coal gangue, providing a robust experimental basis for assessing the stability and fatigue damage of grouted structures under cyclic loading.

The energy response of rock can be employed to characterize the evolution of its internal microstructure under cyclic loading32. Pre-existing pores and microcracks are progressively compacted and closed by repeated loading35. The initiation and propagation of new cracks are induced by stress concentration and cumulative damage21. As a result, the macroscopic mechanical properties are gradually degraded22. The external energy absorbed by rock during each cycle can be divided into two components46–48. One component is stored as elastic strain energy, which is released during unloading. The other component is consumed through irreversible processes, including crack propagation, plastic deformation, frictional sliding, and viscous damping, often accompanied by the release of acoustic and thermal energy. This latter portion is referred to as dissipated energy. From an energy perspective, the energy consumed by damping within a single cyclic period is equivalent to the area enclosed by the stress and strain hysteresis loop for that cycle. In other words, the geometric area of the hysteresis loop directly corresponds to the energy dissipated in a single cycle. Methodology

Experimental apparatus

The experimental platform employed in this study is the WDT-1500 multifunctional material testing system, housed at the Institute of Geotechnical Engineering, Xi’an University of Technology, as shown in Fig. 2. The system is composed of a triaxial compression module and a direct shear module, with control and data acquisition being conducted via the EDC fully digital servo controller from DOLI, Germany. The pressure loading component is equipped with a self-balancing pressure chamber and axial and radial strain gauges, enabling high-precision, synchronous measurement of axial and circumferential deformations of the specimen. An acoustic emission monitoring system was integrated into the triaxial testing platform and operated synchronously with the cyclic loading and data acquisition systems to monitor the internal damage evolution of the specimens. This setup enabled real-time recording of acoustic signals together with mechanical parameters such as stress and strain, capturing the initiation, propagation, and coalescence of microcracks during loading. The equipment is capable of supporting a range of mechanical tests, including uniaxial and triaxial compression, shear, and fatigue tests under complex stress paths. Key performance specifications include an axial deformation measurement range of 0 to 10 mm and a radial deformation range of 0 to 5 mm. The system provides a maximum triaxial confining pressure of 80 MPa and a dynamic loading capacity of up to 1000 kN. Dynamic displacement can be controlled within 0 to 0.5 mm, and the cyclic loading frequency ranges from 0 to 10 Hz. These configurations fully satisfy the experimental requirements for triaxial cyclic loading tests under constant confining pressure and axial cyclic stress conditions. Confining pressure was first applied using the confining pressure system at a loading rate of 0.5 MPa/s during the experiment. The computer-operated system then controlled the apparatus to raise the loading platform and apply axial pressure under stress-controlled conditions. After completing cyclic loading at each stress level, the test proceeded to the next stress level and continued until specimen failure, at which point the experiment was terminated. Throughout the procedure, experimental data were recorded in real time by the data acquisition system. These data included axial stress, axial strain, and confining pressure. This information enabled analysis of the specimen’s mechanical response and damping characteristics under different stress levels.

Specimen preparation

Artificial rock specimens were prepared using a coal gangue particle-grout cementitious system to simulate the composite medium formed after grouting and consolidation. The aggregate consisted of coal gangue particles, classified into three size ranges: 0–2 mm, 2–5 mm, and 5–10 mm, as shown in Fig. 3a. The selection of these particle size ranges is based on previous studies40 and practical considerations in grouting applications, ensuring representative particle skeleton formation and reproducible mechanical behavior under cyclic loading. The cementing material comprised fly ash, ordinary Portland cement, and water, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The specimen mix proportions and grouping were determined according to Table 1.

To investigate the effects of confining pressure and particle gradation on fatigue response, two variable schemes were established. In the first group, the confining pressure variable specimens D1-1, D1-2, D1-3, and D1-4 were used. A fixed coal gangue particle distribution of 20% (0–2 mm), 30% (2–5 mm), and 50% (5–10 mm) was maintained. The grout was composed of 40% fly ash, 38% cement, and 22% water by mass. Confining pressures of 0.5 MPa, 1 MPa, 2 MPa, and 3 MPa were applied to the respective specimens. In the second scheme, the particle gradation variable specimens D1-1, D1-5 and D1-6 were prepared under a constant confining pressure of 1 MPa. The coal gangue particle distributions were varied, with D1-5 having a higher fine-particle fraction and D1-6 a higher coarse-particle fraction, to examine the influence of gradation on mechanical behavior. The grout composition for cemented coal gangue specimens D1-5 and D1-6 consisted of 41% fly ash, 36% cement, and 23% water by mass. The coal gangue particle distribution was varied to investigate the influence of gradation on mechanical behavior.

After casting 150 mm cubic blocks, cores of 50 mm diameter were drilled using a coring machine and subsequently processed with a stone cutter and grinder to obtain standard cylindrical specimens of 50 mm diameter and 100 mm height. Coal gangue fractions were weighed according to Table 1 and pre-mixed to ensure uniform aggregate gradation during specimen preparation. The grout was prepared according to target mass fractions by dry-mixing fly ash and cement, followed by addition of water to form a homogeneous paste. The paste was mixed with the aggregate and continuously stirred until particles were uniformly coated without noticeable agglomeration or segregation. The mixture was poured into 150 mm cubic blocks using layered filling and vibratory compaction to minimize porosity variations. Slight variations in the mass proportions of the grouting materials among the six specimens were intentionally introduced to accommodate differences in coal gangue particle gradation and ensure uniform specimen quality. Variations in particle size distribution affect packing density and void volume, with coarser particle specimens exhibiting larger interparticle voids and finer particle specimens exhibiting smaller voids. To achieve complete filling, minimize porosity variations, and ensure adequate coating of the particle skeleton, the composition of the cementitious paste (fly ash, cement, and water) was slightly adjusted for each specimen. It was left to set at room temperature for 24 h, then demolded and labeled (D1-1 to D1-6), with the specimen dimensions and mass recorded. The specimens were cured for 28 days under natural laboratory conditions, with an ambient temperature of 20 °C and a relative humidity of approximately 65%. After curing, the end faces were ground to ensure flatness and parallelism required for triaxial loading. Prior to testing, specimens were left at room temperature for 24 h to reduce the influence of surface free water on test results.

Experimental method

According to Table 1, the confining pressures for the specimens are assigned as follows: D1-4, D1-1, D1-2, and D1-3 are subjected to 0.5 MPa, 1 MPa, 2 MPa, and 3 MPa, respectively, while D1-5 and D1-6 are both subjected to 1 MPa. The confining pressure was applied to the pressure chamber via the pressure control system and maintained stable. Cyclic loading was conducted under stress-controlled conditions with five stress levels. The corresponding axial maximum and minimum stresses were set as follows: stress level 1, σmax = 1.2 MPa, σmin = 0.8 MPa; stress level 2, σmax = 2.4 MPa, σmin = 1.6 MPa, stress level 3, σmax = 3.6 MPa, σmin = 2.4 MPa; stress level 4, σmax = 4.8 MPa, σmin = 3.2 MPa; and stress level 5, σmax = 6.0 MPa, σmin = 4.0 MPa. The corresponding stress amplitudes are 0.4 MPa, 0.8 MPa, 1.2 MPa, 1.6 MPa, and 2.0 MPa, respectively. At each stress level, 200 loading cycles were applied with a cosine waveform at a frequency of f = 0.2 Hz and a loading rate of 0.5 kN/s. The cyclic loading was conducted under stress-controlled conditions, and the testing system maintained stable waveform control with a loading accuracy of ± 0.5% of full scale throughout the loading process. The data acquisition frequency was set at one measurement every 0.2 s, ensuring reliable recording of the cyclic stress-strain response. The triaxial cyclic loading stress level parameters are summarized in Table 2. To ensure the reliability and repeatability of the experimental results, each group of tests was conducted three times under identical experimental conditions. The final results presented in this study correspond to the average values of the repeated tests, and abnormal data were excluded after consistency verification.

Results

Evolution of deformation under triaxial cyclic loading

The axial stress and strain curves of the six specimens under staged stress levels are characterized by a staged loading with steady cyclic behavior pattern, as shown in Fig. 4. The curves form relatively dense hysteresis loops within each stress level, indicating that the specimens enter a stable cyclic stage under constant stress amplitude. The hysteresis loops shift continuously to the right along the strain axis as the stress level progressively increases from 1 MPa to 5 MPa, reflecting the gradual accumulation of irrecoverable deformation. The loops are relatively narrow with higher equivalent stiffness at lower stress levels, indicating that deformation is primarily accommodated by pore and pre-existing microcrack closure and re-interlocking of the particle skeleton. The loops progressively widen and the secant slopes decrease with increasing stress levels, reflecting stiffness degradation and enhanced energy dissipation due to intensified internal slippage, friction, and damage propagation. Specimens D1-1 and D1-2 failed at a stress level of 3 MPa, D1-3, D1-4, and D1-6 failed at 4 MPa, while D1-5 remained intact after cyclic loading through all five stress levels. The cemented coal gangue specimens D1-4 subjected to a confining pressure of 0.5 MPa exhibited a higher failure stress than those tested specimens D1-1 and D1-2 at 1 MPa and 2 MPa. This behavior is primarily due to insufficient lateral confinement at low confining pressure. This facilitates the localized initiation and propagation of cracks, allowing the specimen to accumulate higher axial stress before failure. In contrast, higher confining pressures suppress crack opening and promote the dispersed evolution of damage. This also accelerates the fatigue degradation of the cemented structure, enhances energy dissipation and ultimately reduces the peak failure stress under cyclic loading.

The confining pressure effect is particularly pronounced in the stress and strain response. When the confining pressure variable group (a)-(d) is compared, it is evident that the cyclic response at higher stress levels becomes more stable as confining pressure increases from 0.5 MPa to 3 MPa. Consequently, more loading cycles can be withstood by the specimens without sudden strain instability. Longer plateau-like cyclic segments are displayed in the curves at high stress levels. This reflects that crack opening and localized tensile failure are suppressed by confining pressure. As a result, the deformation mechanism is shifted from brittle cracking-dominated behavior to frictional sliding and shear-dominated deformation. For example, D1-1 (confining pressure 1 MPa) experienced sudden failure after only a few cycles at the third stress level, with strain remaining below 0.8%. In contrast, D1-2 with a confining pressure of 2 MPa, stably completed multiple cycles at the same stress level. The strain gradually accumulated to 2.4%, indicating that increased confining pressure significantly enhances the cyclic deformation capacity and suppresses abrupt crack propagation. The hysteresis loops become more scattered when the confining pressure decreases to 0.5 MPa (D1-4). Axial deformation fluctuates more intensely during cycling. This indicates that the aggregate cement interfaces within the specimen are prone to nonuniform slippage under low confining pressure.

Gradation differences also influence the evolution of the cyclic response. Under the same confining pressure, specimen D1-5 containing 40% 2–5 mm coal gangue, remained intact throughout the five stress levels. D1-6 with 35% 2–5 mm coal gangue, failed at the fifth stress level. D1-1 with 30% 2–5 mm coal gangue, failed as early as the third stress level. Additionally, the stepped plateau segments during cyclic loading widen as the proportion of medium sized particles (2–5 mm) increases, while the pre-failure strain accumulation decreases. These results indicate that increasing the proportion of medium-sized coal gangue enhances skeleton support and interlocking within the gradation system. It also optimizes the aggregate structure and improves the overall load-bearing capacity and strength of the specimens.

The stress and strain amplitude responses of the six coal gangue specimens under staged stress-level loading are shown in Fig. 5a. A pronounced stepped pattern is exhibited by all curves, with strain amplitudes gradually evolving within each stress level and relatively flat plateau segments being formed. A sudden increase in strain amplitude is observed when the stress amplitude is transitioned from a lower to a higher level, with the curve exhibiting a stepwise transition from one plateau to the next. The combination of plateau and transition features indicates that an evolution from stable deformation accumulation to stage-wise structural rearrangement is undergone by the specimens under staged cyclic loading. The terminal stress levels vary significantly among specimens. D1-1 and D1-2 terminate at lower stress amplitudes. D1-3 and D1-4 extend to medium-high stress amplitudes, while D1-5 and D1-6 reach the highest stress amplitudes and maintain stable responses. The maximum strain amplitudes of specimens D1-1, D1-2, D1-3, D1-4, D1-5, and D1-6 were 0.0104%, 0.012%, 0.0192%, 0.0312%, 0.0401%, and 0.0457%, respectively. Insufficient lateral confinement at low confining pressure (0.5 MPa) facilitates the concentration of crack damage and enables the accumulation of relatively high stress prior to failure.

Meanwhile, systematic differences in strain amplitude among the specimens reflect variations in cyclic equivalent stiffness and deformation resistance at the same stress amplitude. Overall, stronger skeleton support and more stable particle interlocking load-bearing structures are indicated by specimens that withstand higher stress levels while maintaining a slower increase in strain amplitude. The evolution of strain amplitude with total cycle number at different stress levels is presented in Fig. 5b. Strain amplitude exhibits an initial rapid decline followed by stabilization within each stress level and cumulative increases as the cycle progresses, reflecting the progressive accumulation of cyclic deformation. An abrupt jump in strain amplitude is observed at stress-level transition points, with jump magnitude increasing with higher stress levels, indicating more pronounced structural rearrangement and damage propagation. The rate of strain amplitude growth differentiates during stress levels III-IV, with specimen D1-6 at high stress levels showing markedly accelerated growth. Strain amplitude enters a sharply increasing region at stress level V, corresponding to unstable damage propagation. This process intuitively illustrates the progressive mechanical behavior of elastic deformation, damage propagation and instability failure.

Response of the dynamic shear modulus

The evolution of the dynamic shear modulus Gd of coal gangue specimens under different stress levels (SL) with cyclic loading is presented in Fig. 6. A distinct two stage behavior of rapid increase followed by fluctuating evolution is exhibited by all specimens in the initial stage after entering a given stress level. At the microscale, the initial rapid increase in Gd is attributed to particle rearrangement, microcrack closure, and progressive engagement of interparticle contacts and cemented interfaces, which reinforce the load-bearing skeleton. These processes occur rapidly during the early cycles, leading to a swift enhancement of the equivalent shear modulus. The specimens primarily bear loads through friction and interlocking as the skeleton gradually stabilizes. The rate of Gd evolution then slows markedly, exhibiting a quasi-stable response. Overall, Gd decreases with increasing stress level, indicating that intensified cyclic shear perturbations promote microcrack initiation and propagation, thereby weakening the equivalent shear stiffness of the particle skeleton. For specimen D1-4, with SL at 1 MPa, 2 MPa, 3 MPa, and 4 MPa, the average Gd values are 2.974 GPa, 2.695 GPa, 2.093 GPa, and 1.895 GPa, respectively, with within-level increases of 83.33%, 38.46%, 32.00%, and 23.08%. This demonstrates that Gd increases more significantly at low stress levels, while the growth rate diminishes at higher stress levels. For the confining pressure-variable specimens D1-1 to D1-4, the reinforcing effect of confining pressure is evident. D1-3 with a confining pressure of 3 MPa, shows average Gd values of 4.243 GPa, 3.892 GPa, 3.723 GPa, and 3.291 GPa for SL of 1 MPa, 2 MPa, 3 MPa, and 4 MPa, respectively. The Gd values remain at higher levels. In contrast, D1-4 with a confining pressure of 0.5 MPa, reaches a peak Gd of 3.547 GPa. It exhibits larger cyclic fluctuations at low confining pressure. This reflects that high confining pressure restrains inter-particle slippage, enhancing skeleton stability. For the specimen D1-6, with higher medium-sized coal gangue content, the average Gd values at SL of 1 MPa, 2 MPa, 3 MPa, 4 MPa, and 5 MPa are 3.527 GPa, 3.274 GPa, 2.782 GPa, 2.299 GPa, and 1.881 GPa, respectively. A slight decrease in Gd at SL 5 MPa is observed. This is attributed to the secondary compaction effect of the cementitious paste following the reduction of fine particles. It indicates a reverse regulatory influence of specimen parameters on Gd at high stress levels. Overall, Gd of D1-6 remains higher than that of D1-5, reflecting that a lower fine-particle fraction allows coarse particles to form a more stable skeleton. Both specimens stabilize after more than 20 cycles, entering a stage of plastic adjustment and deformation stabilization. A marked reduction in Gd growth rate is observed. In summary, the evolution of Gd in coal gangue grouted specimens results from the combined effects of confining pressure, stress level degradation, and particle skeleton gradation. High confining pressure and a larger proportion of coarse particles effectively enhance stiffness stability under cyclic loading.

Figure 7a–f illustrate the evolution of dynamic shear modulus Gd with shear strain Rd for each coal gangue specimen. A nonlinear decay of Gd with increasing Rd is exhibited by all six specimens, reflecting the degradation of equivalent stiffness caused by the accumulation of irrecoverable deformation and damage evolution during cyclic shearing. The Gd of specimens D1-1, D1-2, D1-3, D1-4, and D1-5 decreased with increasing shear strain, with average reductions of 110.34%, 37.68%, 38.48%, 42.42%, and 34.18% under different stress levels. Variations among specimens additionally highlight the regulatory effects of confining pressure and particle gradation on dynamic stiffness. In the confining pressure variable group (D1-1 to D1-4), the distribution of data points and decay slopes under the same SL show systematic differences. The data are shifted toward low Rd and high Gd, with Rd growth suppressed under higher confining pressure. This indicates that crack opening and shear slip are constrained by confining pressure, thereby helping to maintain higher equivalent shear stiffness. Quantitative comparison shows that increased confining pressure reduces the sensitivity of Gd to Rd. For instance, D1-2 with a confining pressure of 2 MPa, exhibits a 133.33% decrease in Gd as Rd increases from 0.00312% to 0.00728%. In contrast, D1-4 with a confining pressure of 0.5 MPa, shows a decrease of 83.33% under the same stress level, demonstrating that confining pressure enhances resistance to interface slip and mitigates shear strain-induced stiffness degradation. For the particle gradation variable specimens D1-5 and D1-6, D1-6 consistently maintains higher Gd across the entire shear strain range. Its decay is more gradual, illustrating that a higher proportion of coarse particles enhances shear stiffness. The coarse-particle skeleton is less prone to slip or fracture under shear strain, enhancing structural stability. In the optimized gradation specimen D1-5, the rate of Gd reduction with Rd decreases by 1.789% compared to D1-6 at stress level of 5 MPa. This indicates that a well-designed aggregate gradation improves skeleton support and interlocking. It also enhances resistance to shear deformation and weakens the negative correlation between Gd and Rd.

Energy characteristics and damping ratio evolution

Figure 8 presents the evolution of elastic energy density We with cycle number for six coal gangue specimens under different stress levels. We exhibit a staged behavior, it declines rapidly during the initial cycles, approximately the first 10–20 cycles. A slow decay phase is observed during the mid-cycles from 20 to 180 cycles. The We shows slight recovery or enhanced fluctuations in the later cycles, at some medium to high stress levels from 180 to 200 cycles. The pronounced initial decrease of We indicates rapid structural adjustment and accumulation of irreversible deformation under cyclic shearing, reducing the recoverable deformation component and lowering elastic energy storage. During the mid-cycle phase, the particle skeleton stabilizes and the material response exhibits cyclic conditioning while We fluctuations converge to a relatively constant level. The We increases with rising stress level, indicating that higher external stress amplitudes enable greater recoverable energy per cycle. However, a late-stage rise or fluctuation of We is observed at medium to high stress levels. Figure 10 illustrates the evolution of damping ratio for six coal gangue grouted specimens under cyclic loading. The damping ratio is observed to decrease with increasing cycle number at different stress levels. The decline is pronounced during the initial loading cycles, followed by a gradual convergence in later cycles. This behavior can be attributed to the early-stage disruption of preexisting particle contacts and cementitious structures. During this stage, non-elastic deformations, including particle rearrangement and microcrack initiation and slip, are concentrated. Energy dissipation is found to vary markedly across different regions, including particle friction and crack opening and closure, resulting in pronounced damping fluctuations. Particle contacts are stabilized as cycling progresses. Microcrack slip and opening amplitudes become more uniform. The energy dissipation mechanism is transitioned from localized random dissipation to overall uniform dissipation. This leads to a reduction in fluctuation amplitude and eventual convergence of the damping ratio. The specimens D1-1, D1-2, D1-3, and D1-4 exhibit sudden increases in damping at certain stress levels, which can be attributed to progressive internal instability. Discussion

Effects of confining pressure on triaxial cyclic parameters response

Coal gangue specimens experience structural conditioning and progressive damage accumulation under cyclic loading. The dynamic stiffness and energy dissipation are controlled by multiple coupled mechanisms. In addition to the effects of cycle number and stress level, confining pressure plays a key role in triaxial cyclic loading. It strongly influences the dynamic response by regulating normal constraints, particle interlocking, pore compaction, and force-chain stability. It also affects interface sliding and shear localization, redistributing energy dissipation between frictional sliding and damage-induced mechanisms. Consequently, the dynamic shear modulus serves as an indicator of equivalent stiffness. The damping ratio and damping coefficient serve as indicators of energy dissipation. All of these parameters are systematically dependent on confining pressure under identical stress levels. On this basis, the responses of these parameters to confining pressure are analyzed to elucidate its controlling role in dynamic stiffness and energy dissipation characteristics.

The evolution of Gd, damping ratio, and damping coefficient with confining pressure under different stress levels of 1–4 MPa is illustrated in Fig. 12. The Gd increases monotonically with confining pressure, as shown in Fig. 12a. When the confining pressure increases from 0.5 MPa to 3 MPa, Gd increases by 42.67%, 44.40%, 78.08%, and 73.64% under SL of 1 MPa, 2 MPa, 3 MPa, and 4 MPa, respectively. This indicates that increased confining pressure effectively enhances the normal constraint and particle interlocking. Densification of the granular skeleton is promoted, and force-chain transmission is stabilized. Gd decreases with increasing stress levels at a given confining pressure due to intensified particle sliding, crushing, and interface degradation under larger cyclic shear amplitudes. The response of the damping ratio to confining pressure exhibits a stress level dependence, as shown in Fig. 12b. At low stress levels of 1 and 2 MPa, the damping ratio is found to first increase and then decrease with confining pressure, reaching peak values of 0.72 and 0.80 at 2 MPa, respectively. This suggests that frictional sliding and internal structural adjustment are most active under moderate confining pressure, leading to maximum energy dissipation. The non-monotonic variation of the damping ratio with confining pressure arises from the interplay between particle interlocking, interfacial sliding, and the initiation and propagation of microcracks. An increase in confining pressure initially enhances particle interlocking and contact stiffness at low stress levels. This promotes energy dissipation through micro-slip and frictional sliding at particle contacts, leading to an increase in the damping parameters. However, as the confining pressure continues to rise beyond a certain threshold, the material becomes more compact and the relative movement between particles is restricted, reducing frictional dissipation and internal microstructural rearrangements. This reduction in dissipative mechanisms results in a subsequent decrease in the damping ratio and damping coefficient, producing the observed non-monotonic trend. The damping ratio becomes insensitive to confining pressure or shows a slight decrease at stress levels of 3 MPa and 4 MPa. Energy dissipation is dominated by shear localization, damage accumulation, and repeated interface sliding, which weakens the influence of confining pressure. The damping coefficient follows the same variation trend with confining pressure as the damping ratio, as shown in Fig. 12c.

Evolution of the relationship between dynamic shear modulus and damping ratio

The evolution relationship between the damping ratio and dynamic shear modulus of the six coal-gangue grouted specimens is presented in Fig. 13. Overall, a pronounced negative correlation is observed between the dynamic shear modulus Gd and the damping ratio. The damping ratio exhibits a sustained increasing trend as Gd decreases. This relationship remains highly consistent across different stress levels, confining pressures, and particle gradations, indicating strong universality. The reduction in Gd is indicative of progressive degradation of the load-bearing skeleton. This degradation is fundamentally caused by the initiation and propagation of internal microcracks and by the transition of interparticle contacts from tightly interlocked states to relatively loosened states. Nonelastic deformation behaviors such as particle sliding and crack opening and closure are intensified during this process. As a result, energy dissipation mechanisms are increasingly activated and are manifested macroscopically as an increase in the damping ratio. A positive correlation is observed between the attenuation rate of Gd and the growth rate of the damping ratio at a given stress level. A more pronounced decay of Gd is accompanied by a more substantial increase in the damping ratio at stress level of 2 MPa. The distributions of Gd and damping ratio are more concentrated for the specimen D1-3 under high confining pressure. In contrast, markedly greater dispersion is observed for the specimen subjected to low confining pressure. This indicates that confining pressure stabilizes the coupled response between stiffness degradation and damping evolution by suppressing the development of skeletal damage.

Coal gangue grouted composites were investigated under varying confining pressures and particle gradations through triaxial stepwise cyclic loading tests. The cyclic mechanical behavior, dynamic shear modulus evolution, damping ratio variation, and energy dissipation characteristics of the specimens were elucidated. The results demonstrate that confining pressure and particle gradation significantly influence stiffness stability and energy dissipation capacity. They also regulate crack sliding behavior and skeletal load-bearing mechanisms. This indicates a gradual progression from elastic deformation through damage propagation, ultimately leading to instability and failure. Scientific guidance is provided by these findings for the engineering design, stability assessment, and fatigue life prediction of coal gangue grouted backfill systems. Valuable references and methodological insights are also offered for the investigation of dynamic mechanical behavior in similar composite skeletal media under cyclic loading. These findings have significant practical implications for deep resource exploitation and mine safety assurance.

Conclusion

Triaxial cyclic loading tests were conducted on cemented coal gangue specimens with different particle gradations under varying confining pressures and stress levels to investigate the response of the damping characteristics to confining pressure and particle gradation. The main conclusions are summarized as follows:

(1) A clear stage-dependent mechanical response is exhibited by coal gangue grouted composites during stepwise cyclic loading. Irreversible deformation accumulates progressively with increasing stress level. At low stress levels, the response is dominated by pore compaction and particle rearrangement, whereas frictional sliding and microcrack propagation become dominant at higher stress levels. This transition reflects the evolution of the internal structure from a compaction-controlled stage to a damage-dominated stage under cyclic loading.

(2) The dynamic shear modulus initially increased rapidly with the number of cycles and subsequently fluctuated around a stable level, while decreasing with increasing stress level. It increased with both confining pressure and the proportion of coarse particles in the gradation. There is a nonlinear decreasing trend between Gd and shear strain Rd. Under high stress conditions, the optimized gradation specimen D1-5 compared with specimen D1-6 exhibited a 1.789% reduction in the attenuation rate of Gd with respect to Rd. These results indicate that higher confining pressure and optimized particle gradation facilitate the formation of a more stable load-bearing skeleton, thereby restraining interparticle sliding and mitigating stiffness degradation during cyclic loading.

(3) Both elastic energy density and dissipated energy density initially decreased rapidly with the number of cycles and then tended to fluctuate around a stable level, while increasing with stress level. The damping ratio and damping coefficient first increased and then approached a stable state with increasing confining pressure. A reverse increase for failed specimens in damping ratio was observed at the final stage of cycling. The damping ratio and damping coefficient initially increase and then decrease with increasing confining pressure, reaching peak values at a confining pressure of 2 MPa. A negative correlation was observed between the dynamic shear modulus and the damping ratio. Overall, the damping characteristics is governed by the coupled effects of confining pressure and particle gradation, which jointly regulate stiffness evolution and energy dissipation mechanisms in grouted coal gangue.
