An investigation into the diet of elephant fish (Callorhinchus milii) in the waters of Aotearoa/New Zealand
Authors: Kat U C Cooper, Ashley A Rowden, Darren Stevens, Brittany Finucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChondrichthyans (sharks, rays and chimaeras) are increasingly understood to be an important part of ocean ecosystems, but their ecological role is unclear. Callorhinchus milii (elephant fish) is a chimaera species endemic to Aotearoa/New Zealand and southern Australian waters. Its trophic ecology is poorly defined, despite its commercial and recreational importance across much of its range. This study used stomach content analysis to generate a more comprehensive understanding of the diet of C. milii, and to identify biological and/or environmental predictors that may explain any variation in diet. Crustaceans, in particular pagurids and brachyurans, such as Lophopagurus spp. and leucosiid crabs, were the most important prey type (56% index of relative importance, IRI), followed by molluscs (30% IRI). The single most important prey type was leucosiid crabs, which occurred in nearly half of all digestive tracts and had a %IRI of 26%. Depth was found to be the greatest predictor of variation in C. milii diet, with samples taken from greater depths containing more bivalve molluscs and polychaetes, and those from shallower depths containing more gastropod molluscs and brachyuran crabs. These findings were consistent with studies of other shallow water callorhinchids and deep‐water chimaeras around the world. While C. milii have been identified as at risk from climate change, the findings of this study suggest a flexible, generalist diet that may provide some resilience to environmental change caused by anthropogenic impacts.Keywords: chimaera, chondrichthyan, feeding ecology, holocephalan, stomach contents analysis

INTRODUCTION

Chondrichthyans (sharks, rays and chimaeras) play critical roles in the structure, function and resilience of the diversity of ecosystems they inhabit (Dedman et al., 2024; Heithaus et al., 2022). There is increasing research effort to describe and define these roles, and how ecosystems may adapt to ongoing chondrichthyan population changes caused by anthropogenic impacts such as the changing climate and overfishing (Dedman et al., 2024). Despite efforts to understand the roles of sharks and rays (e.g., Heithaus et al., 2008), there is a paucity of knowledge on the oldest extant order of Chondrichthyes—the Chimaeriformes. Chimaeriformes or chimaeras, comprise 54 species across three families (Finucci et al., 2024). This primitive group of fish occurs in all oceans except polar waters, and from shallow coastal waters to depths greater than 3000 m (Didier et al., 2012). Chimaeras may be elusive in nature, although some species are reported in large aggregations, often as bycatch in fisheries (Ford et al., 2017), suggesting at least some species account for a notable proportion of biomass within the ecosystems they inhabit. Chimaeras are likely to play important ecosystem roles both as prey (Hocking et al., 2020) and as predators; however, few studies have examined the trophic ecology of chimaeras (Dunn et al., 2010; Finucci et al., 2017; Lisney, 2010; Macpherson & Roel, 1987).

The Australasian region is recognised as a global hotspot for chimaera diversity, with 18 species reported from Aotearoa/New Zealand and/or Australia (Finucci et al., 2021). The elephant fish Callorhinchus milii Bory de Saint‐Vincent, 1823, is one of three recognised species in the family Callorhinchidae, and one of the few chimaeras occurring across the waters of the two countries, reported from off southern Australia and throughout the Aotearoa/New Zealand region (Didier et al., 2012; Weigmann, 2016). Callorhinchids differ from most other chimaeras in that they are typically found at depths less than 200 m, and as shallow as 10 m in the summer months when they move inshore to reproduce (Nelson et al., 2016; Roberts et al., 2015). Also known as plough‐nosed chimaeras, callorhinchids also have a unique plough shaped snout, which is thought to be used to obtain prey from the seafloor (Didier et al., 2012; Rizzari & Finucci, 2019).

C. milii are considered abundant across much of their Aotearoa/New Zealand range, particularly off the east coast of the South Island where they have been heavily exploited since the 1930s (Fisheries NZ, 2017). Despite their commercial importance, relatively few ecological studies have been conducted on C. milii. Aotearoa/New Zealand is embracing an ecosystem‐based approach to fisheries management (Cryer et al., 2016; Talbot‐Jones et al., 2025), particularly in the marine sector, as the need to understand the connections between organisms in the marine environment, and the roles that exploited species play becomes apparent. To date, most research on C. milii has focused on inputs specific to fisheries assessment and stock monitoring, with little effort focused on their ecological role. C. milii are thought to feed largely on crustaceans and molluscs, though this knowledge was based on limited sampling, restricted geographic range, and is dated to when C. milii were first exploited (Gorman, 1963; Graham, 1939; Thompson & Anderton, 1921). The present study provides a contemporary and comprehensive analysis of the diet of C. milii in Aotearoa/New Zealand waters, while exploring the biological and environmental predictors that may influence diet variation.

METHODS

Sample collection

C. milii were collected and subsampled from incidental catch (bycatch) of research trawl surveys conducted by Earth Sciences New Zealand (previously the National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research, NIWA) onboard RV Kaharoa. Samples were taken during two voyages along the west and east coasts of the South Island of Aotearoa/New Zealand during March–April of 2023 and May–June of 2024, respectively. Sampling strata were defined by location and depth, occurring on trawlable habitats between 10 and 400 m depth using a two‐panel trawl net with a 60‐mm knotless cod‐end. Tows were completed at a speed of 3 knots and generally covered around 3 nautical miles (MacGibbon et al., 2024). The midpoint of the start and end position for each tow was used as the capture location for samples (Figure 1). For each C. milii specimen sampled, fork length (in centimetres, FL), weight (in grams, g), sex and maturity stage were recorded at sea. Maturity was externally assessed by clasper development in males and female maturity was assessed internally based on reproductive tract development. Digestive tracts were removed at sea and kept frozen for processing in the laboratory.

Sample processing

In the laboratory, digestive tracts were thawed and weighed whilst full, and again when empty, to obtain total content weight. Digestive tract state, fullness, condition and digestive state were recorded following protocols modified from Dunn (2009) (see Tables A1 and A2). Digestive tract contents were rinsed with water into a petri dish and identified to the lowest taxon possible. Next, a fragmented prey count was estimated from the number of eyes, heads, mouthparts, paired otoliths, tails or telsons or other anatomical parts able to be traced to a single individual. The number of individual prey items was recorded, and wet mass of each prey type was weighed to the nearest 0.1 g. Abiotic items, gut parasites, and unidentifiable prey were recorded but excluded from analysis.

Statistical analyses

A species‐sample accumulation model was calculated to determine if the sample size was sufficient to describe C. milii diet (Oksanen, 2024). To assess biological or environmental predictors that may influence C. milii diet, a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used (Lek et al., 2011). Diet was analysed using prey weight (mass), which was standardised by the weight of each prey item as a proportion of the total prey weight of each digestive tract. Weight was used under the assumption that weight is a more accurate description of prey importance than prey number or occurrence, as many prey items were of varying sizes so a prey item may constitute a high number but contribute little to actual prey mass, or likewise have high occurrence but weigh very little, or vice versa (Hyslop, 1980). The following biological and environmental predictors were used in the PERMANOVA: specimen weight and sex, sample area, depth and bottom temperature. Depth (m) was defined as the average between the minimum and maximum depth recorded for a trawl tow and binned into 20 m depth intervals. Area was defined by coast and latitude: samples taken on the west coast as West Coast South Island (WCSI), samples taken on the east coast and between 43° S and 44° S as northern East Coast South Island (nECSI) and samples taken below 44° S as southern East Coast South Island (sECSI). A principal component analysis (PCA) was used to describe dietary patterns across sex, depth and sampling area of the sampled elephant fish (Virgós et al., 1999). All statistical analyses were performed using R (R Core Team, 2021) and a standard 0.05 significance test level was used throughout (but with precise results <0.05 reported in Table 2 below).

Ethics statement

Samples were collected from existing research trawls and were not identified as a species of particular importance to iwi under Schedule 2 of NIWA's MPI (Fisheries) Special Permit 842‐3. No further ethical considerations were required.

RESULTS

C. milii samples were taken from between 9 and 125 m depths and 42° to 46° S (Figure 1). Males were generally found at much greater depths than females. The samples comprised 56 males (53 adults and three juveniles), 65 females (28 adults and 37 juveniles) and the sex of six individuals was not available. Fish length ranged from 14.6 to 91.2 cm FL (mean = 66.6 cm FL) and weighed 28 to 8160 g (mean = 3179 g). Males ranged from 45.5 to 80.7 cm FL (mean = 63.2 cm FL) and weighed 1270 to 5452 g (mean = 2585 g). Females ranged from 14.6 to 91.2 cm FL (mean = 69.5 cm FL) and weighed 28 to 8160 g (mean = 3699 g). These data generally reflected the length and weight trends reported in previous trawl data (Beentjes et al., 2022; Beentjes et al., 2023), although more males of smaller sizes were reported in previous trawl surveys.

Of the 127 digestive tracts examined, 122 (96%) contained prey items. Five tracts (4%) were either empty or only contained parasites and were not included in the analysis. Total prey weight per digestive tract ranged from 0.47 to 74.18 g (mean = 19.60 g) and half of the samples (n = 61) contained 15.77 g or less total prey weight. Only 14 tracts were classified as full (11.5%), 25 were three quarters full (20.5%), 16 were half full (13.1%), 44 were a quarter full (36.1%) and 23 (18.9%) only contained trace prey remains.

There were 3844 individual prey items identified across 67 taxa. Many prey items were highly fragmented or digested to the point that only hard parts remained (e.g., the shell or opercula of gastropods), making species‐specific identification difficult. A total of 32 prey items were identified to species level, 2 to genus level and 20 to family level (Table 1). The remaining 13 prey items could only be identified to the taxonomic level of order or class. The species‐sample accumulation plot reached an asymptote at 60 prey types, indicating the prey sample size was sufficient to describe C. milii diet (Figure A1).

Of the prey items classified at higher taxonomic levels, brachyurans were the most important to C. milii diet (34.7% IRI), followed by bivalves (20.5% IRI), pagurids (18.2% IRI), gastropods (13.9% IRI) and polychaetes (7.9% IRI) (Table 1). Nearly all (93.7 %F) digestive tracts contained crustaceans, while nearly 90% contained molluscs. Most identified crustaceans were of the infraorder Brachyura (81% F), with leucosiid crabs (Leucosiidae) alone occurring in 46.5% of samples. Brachyura and leucosiid crabs contributed to 34.7% and 26.3% IRI, respectively. Hermit crabs (Paguridae) were also important prey, identified in 63.8% of digestive tracts and contributing to about a quarter (24.2%) of total prey weight, resulting in a relative prey importance of 18.2%. Among molluscs, bivalves were the most important prey item (20.6% IRI). Of the identifiable bivalves, species of the family Mesodesmatidae were the most important prey item overall (5.9% IRI) but only occurred in 21.3% of digestive tracts. Gastropods were difficult to identify; the species Antisolarium egenum (Gould, 1849) was the most common species identified, occurring in 6.3% of tracts but only contributing 0.4% to overall prey item importance.

Polychaete worms were also frequently identified and were recorded in 56.7% of digestive tracts. Most polychaetes (22.5% N) were identified as belonging to the family Aphroditidae. Echinoderms and teleost fish were also recorded but found at considerably lower frequency and weight, and contributing little to relative prey importance: 1.6% and 0.6%, respectively. While echinoderms such as ophiuroids (brittle stars) could be sometimes counted by the number of central discs present, advanced digestive state prevented identification beyond class. Similarly, teleost prey could be identified and counted by the presence of remains and scales, but no otoliths were found for species level identification.

Predictors of variation in diet

The PERMANOVA analysis indicated C. milii diet is defined largely by depth, with area and weight (a proxy for fish size) also having a small significance (Table 2). The p‐value threshold for greatest significance (depth) was 0.001, and that for moderate significance to be 0.05 (area and weight). Sex was not found to have a significant impact on variation in diet (Table 2). By organising prey types by higher identification order (family or class) and depth range of C. milii capture, differences in diet were more pronounced (Figure 3). The proportion of gastropods decreased considerably with depth (from 24.6% at <20 m to 6.4% at 60–80 m), whereas the proportion of bivalves increased (from 18.2% at <20 m to 32.7% at 80+ m). Both pagurids (20.6%) and brachyurans (39.4%) were proportionally most abundant at 60–80 m depth. In contrast, other crustaceans were most abundant at shallow depths (11.4% at <20 m). Samples from depths >80 m contained the greatest proportion (30.9%) of polychaetes.

Biplot of principal component analysis (PCA) of Callorhinchus milii prey items grouped by higher taxonomic level identification (family or class).

The PCA (Figure 3) showed clear differences in diet by depth (Figure 4). Differences in diet were most pronounced in C. milii sampled at shallower depths (less than 20 m and, to a lesser extent, between 20 and 40 m) and those at greater depths (60–80 m). Shallow‐dwelling C. milii consumed more echinoderms, polychaetes, teleosts and non‐brachyuran or pagurid crustaceans, and by prey proportion, these individuals consumed more gastropods (Figures 2, 3, 4). Conversely, C. milii sampled from greater depths had diets containing more brachyuran and pagurid crabs. C. milii from mid‐depths (40–60 m) were highly clustered and contained higher proportions of bivalves and gastropods (Figures 2, 3, 4).

C. milii sampled from different locations also showed differences in diet (Figure 4). The C. milii sampled from nECSI had diets more dominated by pagurids, while the diet of sECSI C. milii was generally consistent of Brachyura and other crustaceans, polychaetes, and echinoderms. The small sample size from WCSI prevented any robust interpretation of diet pattern characterisation, although it appeared to be consistent with diets from the ECSI, as shown by the overlap in distribution (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

C. milii diet

C. milii diet generally consisted of benthic invertebrates, particularly crustaceans and molluscs, which is largely consistent with previous studies (Edgar & Shaw, 1995; Graham, 1939; Perrior, 2022; Thompson & Anderton, 1921). However, we found greater diversity in prey items, including the prevalence of polychaete prey, which was only previously suspected using stable isotope analysis (Perrior, 2022). The most common polychaete prey item was Aphroditidae spp., which may be abundant in the area (MacGibbon et al., 2024) and the ease with which it is identified compared to other polychaetes. Goniadidae were also reasonably common prey. Perrior (2022) identified the particular significance of polychaetes to elephant fish diet in Aotearoa/New Zealand waters using stable isotope and fatty acid analyses.

The overall dominance of crustacean prey, particularly brachyuran crabs, mainly leucosiid crabs (Leucosiidae) and the pie crust crab Metacarcinus novaezelandiae (Hombron & Jacquinot, 1846), alongside the relative importance and diversity of mollusc prey items had not been previously recorded as prey items for C. milii (Graham, 1939; Thompson & Anderton, 1921). Hermit crabs were important here, with 11 species identified, but were not mentioned in previous research. Pacific bobtail squid [Sepioloidea pacifica (Kirk, 1882)] were usually digested and thus contributed little to overall prey mass, but were found in 3.2% of digestive tracts, indicating potential importance to diet. Previously, other or unspecified crustaceans, unspecified cephalopod, benthic, bivalve or other molluscs were listed in studies (Edgar & Shaw, 1995; Perrior, 2022; Thompson & Anderton, 1921).

There were several prey items previously recorded that were not found in the current study, such as pillbox crabs (Halicarcinus sp.), krill (Euphausiacea spp.), the shrimp Periclimenes yaldwyni Holthuis, 1959, several species of molluscs (Myllita vivens (Finlay, 1926), Nucula nitidula Adams, 1856, Turbonilla zealandica (Hutton, 1873), Solemya parkinsonii Smith, 1874, and Notosetia spp.) and jellyfish (Scyphozoa) (Graham, 1939). However, the absence of these taxa from this study does not rule out these species from occurring in the survey area (e.g., jellyfish were reported in the trawls connected to the samples used in this study, MacGibbon et al., 2024). The samples and those used in previous studies may have been collected from different areas or at different times of year when some prey taxa may have been more abundant as time of sampling was not reported in these studies (Graham, 1939; Thompson & Anderton, 1921). Prey identification may have also been confounded by digestion or C. milii's crushing dentition. Alternatively, the absence of certain prey items here may indicate a change in prey species abundance over the 85 years between studies, potentially due to environmental change or anthropogenic activities, or simply reflects the improvement in taxonomic understanding over the last 85 years (Keegan et al., 2022; Pinkerton, 2017; Tuck et al., 2017). Research using stable isotope and fatty acid analyses identified seasonal variation in C. milii diet, potentially linked to increased availability of teleosts and cephalopods as a result of fisheries discards and cephalopod senescence (Perrior, 2022). Scavenging behaviour has been observed in other chimaera species (Finucci et al., 2017), and therefore may have occurred in this study, potentially explaining the incomplete teleost remains reported here.

Predictors of variation in C. milii diet

Depth was shown to be the greatest indicator of change in C. milii diet, followed by sample area (latitude and longitude) and individual weight (a proxy for size). Sex was not a significant predictor of diet variation, although female C. milii generally fed on more polychaetes, echinoderms and other crustaceans, while male C. milii appeared to feed on more Brachyura and pagurids, though these distinctions in diet between the sexes were less pronounced than with depth and area. Depth segregation by sex is common in chondrichthyans and has been reported previously in chimaeras around Aotearoa/New Zealand (Finucci et al., 2018). The non‐selection of sex in the model may, however, be accounted for by the model selecting for fish weight, which is correlated with both sex and length, and the trend of female C. milii generally being larger than male C. milii (Francis, 2010).

The PCA supported those of the PERMANOVA analysis and provided more information about the relationship between the factors and C. milii prey items. From the plotted PCA results, depth again showed the strongest relationship with C. milii diet. Most obviously, samples taken from shallow depths, less than 20 m, differ from samples taken from greater depths, more than 60 m, with the former containing proportionately more echinoderms, gastropods, teleosts and non‐brachyuran or pagurid crustaceans, and the latter containing more brachyuran and pagurid crabs. The proportion of polychaetes in C. milii diet increased considerably in samples taken from depths greater than 80 m, though sample size from these depths was smaller. This finding supports previous research that showed C. milii from Aotearoa/New Zealand waters to be found deeper and consume more polychaete prey than C. milii from Australia (Perrior, 2022). PERMANOVA indicated a significant relationship between C. milii diet and area, and when examined by area (nECSI, sECSI and WCSI) the PCA showed that some dietary overlap among the areas but those from the sECSI had a wider dietary range. That is, C. milii sampled from sECSI fed more on brachyurans and other non‐pagurid crustaceans, polychaetes and echinoderms, whereas C. milii from the nECSI fed mostly on pagurids. Small sample size prevents robust interpretation of the results for the WCSI samples; however, the samples here appear similar to both northern and southern East Coast samples.

Similar dietary trends have been found in other callorhinchid species, particularly C. callorhynchus (Linnaeus, 1758), which has shown variation in diet with area (Bahamonde, 1950; Di Giacomo, 1990; Di Giacomo et al., 1994; Roman et al., 2020; Silva‐Garay et al., 2018), sex and size (Aedo et al., 2009; Di Giacomo, 1990; Roman et al., 2020). Like C. milii, there is some variation in the diet of C. callorhynchus with both sex and size, with males preferring brachyuran crabs and larger fish typically feeding on larger organisms and more of those with hard shells (Aedo et al., 2009; Di Giacomo, 1990; Roman et al., 2020). Notable variation in C. callorhynchus diet has been reported over its geographical range, with individuals occurring on the Atlantic shelf side of their South American range predominantly feeding on molluscs, while those found on the Pacific side feeding on predominantly crustaceans (Aedo et al., 2009; Di Giácomo & Perier, 1996; Molina et al., 1980; Roman et al., 2020). While some variation in diet with area was found in the current study, sampling did not span the full extent of C. milii range in Aotearoa/New Zealand, notably the southern North Island and western South Islands. This study also did not include samples from Australia where the species' diet has not been comprehensively quantified. Increased spatial sampling may confirm spatial differences in diet, as observed in C. callorhynchus. Seasonal variation could also be observed given sampling over different seasons, as has been seen in C. milii in Australia (Perrior, 2022). Variation in diet with depth was not observed in other callorhinchid species, but may be present in other chimaera species, such as Chimaera monstrosa Linnaeus, 1758, where ontogenetic variation in diet and depth preference have been observed (Moura et al., 2005; Mulas et al., 2021). Similarly, Dunn et al. (2010) found that depth was a determining factor in niche separation between chimaera species Harriotta raleighana (now Harriotta avia Finucci, Didier, Ebert, Green & Kemper, 2024), Hydrolagus bemisi Didier, 2002, and Hydrolagus novaezealandiae (Fowler, 1911). Further differences in C. milii diet may be found by sampling beyond the sampling depth here (125 m) to the deepest (~200 m) part of the species known depth range in Aotearoa/New Zealand. As this work was restricted by samples made available from research trawl surveys, future collaborations with the fishing industry may allow for sampling across a wider range of areas and depth ranges and across seasons.

Implications

Comprehensive diet studies provide the foundation for many fisheries management applications and are becoming increasingly important for understanding how environments will respond to the changing climate. Ecosystem‐based management, guided by research such as comprehensive diet studies, allows for the integrated management of the interactions within a system, including human use (Katsanevakis et al., 2011). Aotearoa/New Zealand fisheries are gradually shifting to a more ecosystem‐based management approach, with the aim of understanding all aspects of exploited marine systems (Fisheries NZ, 2022). Such an understanding should extend to appreciating the trophic connection that exploited species such as C. milii have with benthic invertebrates. For example, understanding what a species is feeding on can help to understand the potential impact that changing climate can have on species. Further, understanding what environmental factors influence any changes in diet can help predict potential resilience or vulnerability to climate change.

C. milii have been identified as a species in Aotearoa/New Zealand waters to be impacted by warming waters and are predicted to move to greater depths as water temperatures increase (Dunn, 2009). The results here showed depth to be the most important influence on C. milii diet, and a shift to deeper waters may also result in a shift in C. milii diet. One potential change may be an increased reliance on polychaetes as a primary prey item. The impact of this change in diet, good or bad, on C. milii is unknown at this time. While moving deeper is a viable response to warming waters generally, C. milii will still need to migrate inshore annually for reproduction to environments predicted to be vulnerable to climate change and otherwise at risk from anthropogenic impacts (Evans & Thrush, 2023; Francis, 2010; Thomas et al., 2025). Knowing what C. milii are feeding on at these shallower depths (predominantly, gastropods, bivalves and brachyurans) can help determine their level of vulnerability and the scale of impact. However, knowing C. milii are generalists, this will likely provide them with some dietary resilience to these impacts.
