A handheld near infrared scanner for the detection of acute traumatic intracranial hemorrhage
Authors: Sebastian D’Amario, Joshua Bougadis, Nicole S Coverdale, Bryn Hoffman, Joseph Y Nashed, Jason D Riley, Douglas J Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Archeoptix NIRD device is a handheld near infrared scanner for intracranial hemorrhage of at least 3 mL in volume located up to 3.5 cm from the scalp. It generates an image that approximates hemorrhage location on a schematic head. We report a prospectively collected cohort of scans from patients with traumatic brain injury and CT-confirmed intracranial hemorrhage, alongside healthy control subjects, to evaluate the utility and accuracy of this technology. Patients with hemorrhage were recruited from the neurosurgical service within 24 h of trauma for a single scan, and also controls with no history of head trauma or neurological symptoms. Blinded reviewers judged each scan for presence or absence of hemorrhage and whether hemorrhage position matched CT. Thirty-seven patients with hemorrhage and 40 controls were scanned. Reviewers identified hemorrhage in 37/37 patients and no hemorrhage in 40/40 controls, with correct localization in 35/37 scans. Repeat passes were sometimes required for user-induced errors from external light exposure or loss of detector contact, occurring more often in hemorrhage cases and modestly increasing time to complete subsequent paths. The Archeoptix NIRD device shows promise as a point-of-care or remote diagnostic tool, and further work can fully establish sensitivity, specificity, and user experience.Keywords: Intracranial hemorrhage, Traumatic brain injury, Near infrared, Point of care diagnosticsSubject terms: Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Point of care detection of intracranial hemorrhage has been pursued as a means of early detection following head trauma1,2. The primary cases for point of care hemorrhage detection are to provide rapid screening and triage in remote and rural environments, in first responder applications and in front line military applications. There have been several advances in this area in recent years with biochemical screens using blood and saliva3,4, electroencephalogram based technologies5, transcranial doppler or ultrasound based technologies6, and near infrared spectroscopy7–10.

Here we present a pilot study assessing the utility of a near infrared detection device called the Archeoptix NIRD (Archeoptix Biomedical Inc., Kingston, Ontario, Canada)11. This device is a handheld detection system that is housed in a small case that includes a handheld probe attached by wire to an interface and battery pack and a laptop computer (Fig. 1). The handheld probe is passed over the scalp in 7 passes in the sagittal plane equally spaced from the right frontotemporal region and sequentially to the left with passes initiated anteriorly in the frontal area and completed as posteriorly as possible on or towards the occiput. Scan passes are guided through a graphical user interface that assesses the data integrity of each pass before guiding the user to make the next pass. Prior to scanning a static calibration scan is taken over a region with no hair such as the forehead, chest, shoulder or thigh to account for skin tone.

Graphical user interface of the Archeoptix NIRD demonstrating the location of 7 passes required for full head coverage (A). The handheld device (B) can be operated from a single hand (C). The device has a surrounding rubber shroud that shields from external light (D). The device has a single light source and two paired detectors for near and deep detection.

The NIRD scanner uses a light-emitting diode that projects an 806 nm light into the intracranial space. The amount of returning light, which is reduced in the presence of blood due to absorption of light by blood in this spectrum, is collected by detectors that rest firmly against the scalp. All data generated by the NIRD scanner is a normalization of the difference between a pair of near and a pair of far detector channels, where near channels represent signals from superficial structures (scalp, skull) and far channels represent signals from deep structures (brain, dura, abnormal pool of blood in the intracranial space within 3.5 cm of the outer surface of the scalp).

The data is processed by calculating the ratio of signal detected at the receiving sensors, providing a comparison measure between shallow and deep tissue. The requirement for shallow tissue detection is to avoid any issues with superficial bleeding such as scalp hematoma or laceration, bloody hair or prominent scalp vessels affecting the output of the device. Deep tissue detection identifies bleeding in the intracranial space. The device samples light absorption at a frequency of 4000 Hz resulting in 80 000 to 200 000 datapoints per 7 pass study.

A bleed is indicated by a measurable reduction in signal from the far sensors relative to the near sensors. This represents an increase in absorption in the near infrared spectrum within the intracranial space after discounting any near surface events (Fig. 2). A positive hemorrhage is determined as an absolute cutoff of the absorption ratio of less than 0.18. If this threshold is reached then a hemorrhage is signaled in the data set. Healthy brain tissue has a ratio in the range 0.8–3.0. Values greater than 3.0 signify signal from light dispersion in spinal fluid or an error. If light from the exterior leaks into the shrouded detector due to an elevation of the shroud an error is displayed. Similarly, if the light source elevates off the scalp and saturates the detectors inside the shroud an error is displayed. Data is plotted on a three dimensional avatar of the human head to provide an approximate location of a bleed using custom code written in MATLAB (Mathworks, Natick, MA).

Processed data from a single patient demonstrating the d5 ratio of near and far signal across a single pass in a hemorrhage positive patient. Hemorrhage detection is based on the d5 of the first peak where a value of 0.18 or below is consistent with hemorrhage (red) and normal tissue peaks between 0.8–3.0 (blue). Peaks detected in the range above 3.0 represent light dispersion in spinal fluid. In the case of light leak from external light or internal saturation due to loss of contact of light source with the scalp the histogram shifts to the right with maximal peaks in the range above 3.0.

Results

Scans were performed on 37 patients with intracranial hemorrhage (Age range 29–85, mean age 56(SD19), 24 Male:13 Female). Scans were collected from 40 control subjects (Age range 21–72, mean age 33(SD17), 18 Male:22 Female).

Hemorrhage cases were comprised of acute subdural hematoma (21), mixed acute subdural hematoma and contusion (12), epidural hematoma (3) and intraventricular hemorrhage (1). Data was collected on average 8 h (SD6.3) following traumatic event.

There was 100% agreement in reviewer interpretation of results. Reviewers identified hemorrhage in 37 of 37 (100%) subjects with intracranial hemorrhage. Performance of the device was positive for both superficial (Fig. 3) and Deep (Fig. 4) hematoma locations. In this group 35 of 37 (95%) hemorrhages were confirmed as matching the location of bleeding based on CT scan. One scan demonstrated a positive result on the contralateral side from an epidural hematoma that was subsequently determined to be due to the scan being erroneously collected from left to right (Fig. 5). The second incorrectly localized scan placed a left posterior frontal contusion and subdural hematoma in a more anterior position and was subsequently determined to be related to initiating the first, second and third scan passes more posteriorly on the head due to a contusion and laceration on the forehead. Reviewers identified no evidence of hemorrhage in 40 of 40 (100%) control subjects.

Right sided acute subdural hematoma displayed on the graphical user interface (A). CT confirmed acute bleeding in the same region (B and C).

Right sided hematoma with thalamic hemorrhage and intraventricular hemorrhage in the right occipital horn. Displayed on the graphical user interface (A). CT confirmed acute bleeding in the same region (B and C).

Left sided epidural hematoma displayed on the right side of the graphical user interface (A). CT confirmed acute bleeding in opposite side of the head (B and C). This was later confirmed to be caused by user error where scans were collected from left to right.

Scanning was undertaken in supine position for all hemorrhage cases. This resulted in shorter scan passes due to reduced access to the occipital portion of the scalp when patients were unable to flex the neck due to obtundation or presence of a cervical collar. Scan paths were shortened to avoid scalp injuries (contusions, lacerations and subgaleal hematoma) to reduce risk of patient discomfort. Control scans were obtained in seated position. On average, 18(SD5.4) passes were required to achieve 7 viable scan passes per hemorrhage patient whereas 9(SD3.4) were required to achieve 7 viable passes in control patients.

We observed systematic differences in scan path duration by error type and by the number of scan attempts per path. Relative to well-performed “good” passes (mean 4.19 s), light-leak errors were longer (4.59 s) and saturation flagged passes were slightly shorter (3.95 s; Fig. 6A). Following an error path, the next subsequent good pass was modestly shorter compared to a good pass on a first-try (4.08 vs. 4.29 s; −0.21 s), suggesting an immediate corrective tightening of technique (Fig. 6B). If the next path after an error is another error, the user is more likely to slow down (3.49 to 3.94 s; +0.45 s), which unintentionally increases the chance of lifting the device and admitting external light (Fig. 6C). These data indicate that brief, overly rapid passes tend to saturate, whereas longer, hesitant passes are more prone to light leaks, reinforcing the value of steady pressure and continuous contact in user training.

Path length for all passes: good passes, known short passes (incomplete contact), light-leak errors (device lifted off head), and saturation-flagged passes (device probes lifted from scalp) (A). Good passes split by sequence, comparing first good passes in a block with good passes immediately after an error, with the post-error good pass typically shorter (B). Error passes split by sequence, comparing the first error in a block with errors that follow another error, with repeated errors tending to be longer than first errors (C).

Adverse events caused by the scan included minor pain reported in 13 hemorrhage patients and 11 control subjects. There were no abrasions caused by scan passes.

Discussion

This pilot study demonstrates feasibility of the Archeoptix NIRD device in the detection of acute hemorrhage following traumatic brain injury. The device performed with excellent accuracy in identifying the presence of hemorrhage and in 95% of cases correctly placed the hemorrhage in the anatomical location of the hemorrhage identified on CT scan. Notably, the incorrect location placements were attributable to operator deviation from the prescribed scanning protocol, indicating that routine training and adherence to the device instructions should mitigate these errors. The device correctly identified no hemorrhage in 100% of control subjects scanned. These data suggest that sensitivity and specificity of the device are high; however, larger sample sizes are required, preferably in a blinded study, to calculate sensitivity and specificity.

The device shows promise as a screening tool for traumatic brain injury that can be utilized at the point of injury, in ambulances or in remote and rural locations to facilitate early transport and triage decisions that can heavily influence patient outcome8–10,12,13. The device has potential for use in emergency facilities that do not have CT capabilities to improve and expedite triage decisions for patients with trauma and minimal symptomatology. Finally, the device may benefit larger emergency room facilities to evaluate trauma cases where CT may not be indicated based on clinical guidelines, but added confidence is required by emergency personnel due to concerning mechanism of injury or other influencing factors14–17. Similarly, prior work has demonstrated the use of CT following traumatic brain injury increases in when emergency personnel have limited cognitive bandwidth during high volume periods or at the end of a shift18.

Given the penetration limitations of near infrared light into the skull there is a limitation of detecting hematoma within 3.5 cm of the scalp surface8,10. This pilot study included only hemorrhage within 3.5 cm of the scalp surface with the exception of an intraventricular hemorrhage in the occipital horn of the lateral ventricle. The device correctly identified this intraventricular hemorrhage suggesting that detection of deeper hemorrhage is possible; however, this requires further testing to validate the reliability of the device to detect deep hemorrhage. The use of this device to detect post-traumatic hemorrhage is promising given that the majority of clinically significant traumatic hemorrhage occurs in a superficial location19. Of note, traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage was not assessed in this pilot study. It is likely that small volume (< 3mL) or deep subarachnoid hemorrhage will not be identified by the device9,10. This must be further evaluated in larger, blinded studies.

Patient age is a limitation of this study where the majority of patients were between the ages of 20–60. Brain atrophy can result in greater distance between the scalp surface and the parenchyma with cerebrospinal fluid intervening. Therefore, older patients and those brain atrophy from pre-existing conditions may have unreliable results with the NIRD device. Further evaluation in older patients and patients with pre-existing brain atrophy are required to evaluate device accuracy in these instances. Similarly, chronic subdural hematoma with aged blood or blood breakdown products were not evaluated in this study and will require further evaluation.

Conclusion

The Archeoptix NIRD device is a promising device for the detection of acute intracranial hemorrhage following trauma. Hemorrhage volume of 3mL located within 3.5 cm of the scalp surface can be reliably detected. The device is small, hand held, does not use ionizing radiation and is simple to use, making it suitable for portable and remote and rural applications. Further studies are required to fully assess the sensitivity and specificity of the device. Furthermore, future studies must evaluate the utility of the device in specific use cases such as ambulance based assessments and remote and rural areas where users with varied degrees of medical knowledge will be required to utilize the device.

Methods

Methods were approved by the Queen’s University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (HSREB#6019089). All research was performed in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki. For intracranial hemorrhage subjects patients aged 18–85 were recruited from the Queen’s University Neurosurgery inpatient service. Patients diagnosed with acute subdural, mixed acute on chronic subdural, acute epidural and intracerebral hematoma within 3 cm of the surface of the scalp diagnosed by Computerized Tomography (CT) were recruited. All patients were scanned within 72 h of presentation with intracranial hemorrhage. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients directly, or in cases where patients had a decreased level of consciousness, written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s family. Scans were obtained by the Principal Investigator or a trained Research Associate or Medical Student. A single image of the patient’s CT scan was saved with no patient identifiers recorded. Scan results were blinded from clinicians to ensure treatment decisions were not influenced by scan results.

Control subjects without bleed were collected from a population of normal participants aged 18–80 who had no history of head trauma or intracranial pathology. Subjects were recruited through a study poster on the Queen’s University campus. Scans were undertaken by a trained Research Associate or Medical Student.

Patients and Subjects were assigned a study number that was associated with all data files generated by the NIRD scanner as well as pertinent images of CT scan data. No patient or subject identifiers were recorded.

Two blinded reviewers were presented with the NIRD scan results displayed as a three dimensional rendering with hematoma denoted by a red area and normal tissue as blue. Control patients did not have an associated CT scan and were deemed normal based on recruitment criteria. Control scans were included in the data review. Reviewers commented on the presence/absence of a hematoma and the location of the hematoma. After all data were reviewed the reviewers were provided with a single CT axial image of the patients with hematoma and asked whether the location for the hematoma on CT matched the location of hematoma displayed on the NIRD.
