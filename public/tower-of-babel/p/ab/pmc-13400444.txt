Vestibular functional assessment using the caloric test in mice with arsanilic acid–induced vestibular dysfunction
Authors: Shotaro Harada, Yoshihisa Koyama, Takao Imai, Hidenori Inohara, Shoichi Shimada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The vestibular system contributes to postural control, eye movements, spatial perception, and autonomic regulation. Its dysfunction causes vertigo, nausea, postural instability, which gradually improve via central plasticity known as vestibular compensation. Experimental vestibular compensation studies commonly use unilateral vestibular neurectomy or chemical/labyrinthectomy models, with vestibular function assessed by vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) analysis, vestibular evoked myogenic potentials, and vestibular sensory evoked potentials. However, these methods require specialized equipment. In contrast, the caloric test, widely used clinically, offers a simple means of evaluating lateral semicircular canal function, yet its use in animal studies remains limited.In this study, a chemical vestibular lesion was induced using arsanilic acid, and vestibular function was evaluated with the caloric test and VOR analysis. In normal mice, cold-water stimulation elicited nystagmus whose direction reversed with head position. In the arsanilic acid-induced vestibular dysfunction mice, day 3-lesioned side stimulation showed no significant difference in maximum slow-phase velocity (MSPV) compared with normal mice. In contrast, MSPV was reduced on the unaffected side compared with the affected side and controls. Conversely, VOR analysis demonstrated a significant gain increase in the arsanilic acid-induced vestibular dysfunction mice. Thus, the caloric test may provide complementary information on unaffected-side functional modulation during early vestibular compensation that is not captured by VOR analysis alone.The caloric test effectively assessed normal and impaired vestibular function and, with VOR analysis, may clarify vestibular compensation mechanisms. Given its simplicity, the caloric test is a promising complementary method for assessing vestibular function in mouse models and may aid studies of vestibular compensation.

Introduction

The vestibular system contributes to postural control, ocular motor regulation, perception, and autonomic function. Its impairment results in vertigo, nausea, postural instability, and ocular motor abnormalities, leading to substantial reductions in quality of life. However, vestibular dysfunction gradually improves through a recovery process known as vestibular compensation, and near-complete recovery is generally achieved [1]. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying vestibular compensation, animal models of unilateral vestibular dysfunction—such as unilateral vestibular neurectomy, surgical labyrinthectomy, and chemical labyrinthectomy—have been developed and extensively studied [2].

Models requiring surgical procedures have primarily been used in relatively large animals, including rabbits, cats, and rats. In contrast, chemical labyrinthectomy does not require specialized surgery and can therefore be applied to small animals such as mice, making it a useful method for generating vestibular dysfunction models. Vestibular function in these models is typically assessed using vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) analysis [3] [4], vestibular evoked myogenic potentials[5], and vestibular sensory evoked potentials [6]. However, these techniques require specialized equipment and are limited to experimental environments.

In clinical practice, the caloric test is widely used as a standard method for evaluating vestibular function. This test involves irrigating the external auditory canal with cold water or body-temperature water control to induce endolymphatic flow in the lateral semicircular canal and assessing the resulting nystagmus to evaluate the function of each semicircular canal individually [7]. Although the caloric test is simple and widely used in humans, its application in animal studies remains limited.

Therefore, in the present study, we generated a chemical model of vestibular dysfunction in mice using arsanilic acid [8], and examined whether vestibular compensation could be evaluated using the caloric test in comparison with VOR analysis.

Materials & methods

Animals and study approval

Twenty-five male C57BL/6 J mice (7 weeks old, 17–20 g; mean 19 g) were used in the experiments (Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan). Food and water were provided ad libitum, and the animals were maintained at a room temperature of 23–25 °C. The mice were randomly assigned to three groups: a validation group for caloric testing (n = 5), a unilateral vestibular dysfunction group (n = 10), and a normal control group for angular VOR test (n = 10). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The experimental protocols were approved by the Committee of Animal Experiments of Osaka University (approval number 24–085-004). The study complied with the ARRIVE guidelines for reporting animal research and the American Veterinary Medical Association Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. Every effort was made to minimize the number of animals used and to reduce their suffering.

Vestibular lesion

Anesthesia was induced by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture containing 100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine. To induce vestibular damage, p-arsanilic acid was injected into the middle ear cavity through the external auditory canal. p-Arsanilic acid sodium salt (0.4 M, pH 7.2; Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) was dissolved in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB), and 50 μL of the solution was administered into the left ear. As a control, 50 μL of 0.1 M PB was injected into the right ear. All solutions were removed 5 min after injection. Vestibular function was evaluated 3 days after administration.

Caloric test and nystagmus assessment

The mice were stimulated in two head positions: one with the nose elevated by 40° and the other with the nose lowered by 40°. Head position was controlled using a custom-made tilting platform, with the angle adjusted and fixed at 40° using a goniometer to ensure consistent positioning across animals. This apparatus was the same as that used in our previous static tilt experiments [9] and allows rotation in 10° increments via a gear mechanism. For head fixation, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), followed by local anesthesia with 1% lidocaine. A small midline scalp incision was made, and a Z-shaped bracket with screw holes was attached to the skull using dental cement. Caloric testing was performed 24 h later in awake animals, with the head firmly immobilized. Tilting the body by 40° shifted the orientation of the lateral semicircular canal from the earth-horizontal axis toward the vertical axis. In each position, 10 mL of distilled water (DW) was injected into the left or right external auditory canal over 20 s using a 10-mL syringe (Terumo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) fitted with the external sleeve of a 24G Surflash™ I.V. catheter (Terumo Corporation). This volume and rate (10 mL over 20 s; mean 0.5 mL/s) were selected based on preliminary experiments and previous reports [10,11] to reliably induce nystagmus (onset 5–10 s) while minimizing mechanical stimulation by gentle irrigation from the external auditory canal entrance. A perforation was made in the lower half of the tympanic membrane to enable transtympanic delivery of arsanilic acid; consequently, distilled water could also enter the middle ear cavity during caloric testing, although the primary aim was external auditory canal irrigation. Two types of DW were used for the injections—ice-cold DW and 37 °C DW—and a 5-min interval was allowed between injections of different temperatures. Continuous irrigation with ice-cold water was required due to the short mouse ear canal, as milder temperatures did not reliably induce nystagmus; water temperature was verified using a digital thermometer immediately prior to irrigation. During each injection, eye movements were monitored to confirm the presence of nystagmus. The slow-phase velocity of the caloric nystagmus was measured until it reached its maximum, and this value, referred to as the maximum slow-phase velocity (MSPV), was calculated using recordings from both eyes. Eye movements were recorded under illuminated conditions using an infrared camera (sampling rate: 30 Hz; GR200HD2-IR, Shodensha Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). Image acquisition from both eyes was synchronized using a color quad processor (SG-202II; Daiwa Industry Co., Ltd., Kanagawa, Japan). The cameras were positioned directly beside the eyes.

Angular vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) test

This analysis was conducted with reference to previous studies [3,12]. Mice were secured to the platform using the same head fixation method employed in our caloric tests. Semicircular canal function was measured 24 h after installation of the apparatus. Each mouse was secured to a cylinder at the center of the device using the attached head bracket. The experiment was conducted in the dark, and the apparatus automatically rotated horizontally in a sinusoidal pattern at frequencies of 0.3, 1, and 2 Hz. Mouse eye and apparatus movements were recorded using two high-speed infrared cameras (sampling rate: 240 Hz; Sentech, Kanagawa, Japan), and images of the eyes and apparatus were synchronized using StreamPix software (NorPix, Montreal, Canada). The maximum angular velocities were 79.04, 62.80, and 62.80°/s. All experimental equipment (metal plate, cylinder, rotating plate, and camera fixture) was manufactured by Bio-Medica (Osaka, Japan). The apparatus was rotated 7–14 times for each measurement and 3–5 times for eye movement analysis. Each mouse was assessed once at each frequency. Eye movements were monitored on a display, and the measurement was repeated if abnormal movements were detected.

Eye movement analysis

Images of eye movements were saved as JPEG files (645 × 485 pixels). The pupil margin was approximated as an ellipse, and all calculations followed a previously reported method. The inertial coordinate frame (x, y, z) was defined with the x-axis perpendicular to the image plane (positive forward), the y-axis parallel to the horizontal axis (positive left), and the z-axis parallel to the vertical axis (positive upward).

In each image, the pupil appeared as an ellipse, and the extension of its minor axis always passed through the eye's rotation center on the face. Thus, the rotation center was derived from the minor axis. Using the distance (r) between the ellipse center and the rotation center, together with the ratio of the minor to major axis lengths, the pupil rotation radius (R) was calculated as follows:r=R1−(lengthoftheminoraxis/lengthofthemajoraxis)2

The pupil center coordinates on the image plane (yp, zp) were obtained, and the relative coordinates to the eye rotation center (0, yc, zc) were expressed as (yp − yc, zp − zc).

The horizontal eye rotation component was then calculated using the following equation:θarctan⁡((yp−yc)/R2−(yp−yc)2−(zp−zc)2)

Horizontal eye movement was defined as positive when directed nasally for the right eye and temporally for the left eye.

Calculation of vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) gain

Two markers were placed on the rotating table, and their movements were recorded using a 240-Hz infrared high-speed camera positioned 30 cm from the table and aligned perpendicular to the rotation axis. From the recorded images, the coordinates of the two markers, (xt1, yt1) and (xt2, yt2), were obtained. The rotational position of the table was then calculated using the Excel “atan2” function applied to the coordinate differences [atan2(xt2 − xt1, yt2 − yt1)], with positive values indicating counterclockwise rotation. The eye and turntable positions were extracted from the recorded images, and their angular velocities were calculated as previously described [13]. Eye velocity and slow-phase eye velocity were obtained using established methods [14,15]. Turntable velocity was fitted to the equation vt sin(2πft + pt), and using the same frequency f, eye velocity was fitted to ve sin(2πft + pe). The sinusoidal angular VOR gain was then calculated as ve/vt. In addition, based on these data, the maximum angular eye velocities were compared between clockwise (CW) and counterclockwise (CCW) rotations.

Tissue sample preparation and hematoxylin–eosin staining

Under combined anesthesia (0.3 mg/kg medetomidine, 4.0 mg/kg midazolam, and 5.0 mg/kg butorphanol), the mice were perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PB. Temporal bones containing the cochlea and semicircular canals were removed, fixed overnight, and decalcified in 10% EDTA for one week. The inner ears were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and mounted on MAS-coated slides (Matsunami Glass, Osaka, Japan). After deparaffinization, sections were rehydrated and stained with hematoxylin and eosin solution (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Corporation, Osaka, Japan). Stained samples were examined using a Keyence microscope (BZ-X700: KEYENCE Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

Statistical analyses

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error. Statistical analyses were performed using Statcel 4 (Excel statistical software). Bartlett's test was applied to confirm homogeneity of variance. For the analysis shown in Fig. 2, comparisons between the affected and intact ears within the arsanilic acid-induced vestibular dysfunction group were performed using a paired t-test. Comparisons between each ear of the vestibular dysfunction group and the normal control ear were performed using an unpaired t-test. For comparisons between two groups across multiple frequencies (Fig. 3a), A linear mixed-effects model analysis was applied. The fixed effects included group and frequency as main effects, with the interaction term specified as group × frequency. In Fig. 3b, Student's paired t-test was used.

The correlations between VOR gain obtained at each rotational frequency and the MSPV induced by ice-water caloric stimulation were analyzed. In addition, the relationships between the maximum angular eye velocity during VOR testing at 1 and 2 Hz (for each rotation direction) and MSPV during caloric stimulation were evaluated. Correlation coefficients were calculated using Pearson's correlation analysis.

A *p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

To determine whether the caloric test can be used to evaluate vestibular function in mice, the left external auditory canal of five normal mice was irrigated with ice water, and nystagmic responses were observed under different head positions. The mouse lateral semicircular canal is tilted by approximately 32° relative to the horizontal plane in the normal position [16]. Therefore, the mice were tilted by 40° in the anterior–posterior direction, and the nystagmus induced in each position was evaluated. In the nose-up position (40° extension), in which the lateral semicircular canal becomes vertical, the quick phase of the nystagmus was directed toward the right ear (contralateral to the stimulus) (Fig. 1a). In contrast, in the nose-down position (40° flexion), in which the orientation of the lateral semicircular canal is reversed, the quick phase was directed toward the left ear (ipsilateral to the stimulus) (Fig. 1a). Additionally, the MSPV of the nystagmus was 69.3 ± 11.8°/s in the 40° nose-up position and 29.8 ± 7.9°/s in the 40° nose-down position (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, no nystagmus was observed in either position when 37 °C body-temperature water control was used for irrigation.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that vestibular function in mice can be reliably assessed using the caloric test.

Using ten mice in which vestibular dysfunction was induced by transtympanic injection of arsanilic acid into the left ear, caloric testing was performed 3 days after administration. When the mice were positioned with the nose elevated by 40°, irrigation of the intact right ear with ice water elicited nystagmus with fast phases directed toward the left ear, and the MSPV was 40.4 ± 4.7°/s (median: 32.1°/s). In contrast, irrigation of the lesioned left ear induced nystagmus with fast phases directed toward the right ear, and the MSPV was 68.1 ± 5.7°/s (median: 61.1°/s) (Fig. 2). The MSPV was significantly higher with left-ear irrigation than with right-ear irrigation (p = 0.024). After arsanilic acid administration, the MSPV induced by cold-water irrigation of the left ear did not differ significantly from that in normal mice (p = 0.933). In contrast, cold-water irrigation of the right ear showed a trend toward a difference compared with normal mice (p = 0.054). As in normal mice, no nystagmus was observed in either ear when 37 °C body-temperature water control was used. Next, to validate the findings obtained from the caloric test, VOR analysis was performed. In additional ten normal mice, the sinusoidal angular VOR gains were 0.51 ± 0.030 at 0.3 Hz, 0.55 ± 0.033 at 1 Hz, and 0.54 ± 0.026 at 2 Hz. In contrast, in arsanilic acid–treated mice, the gains were 0.54 ± 0.042 at 0.3 Hz, 0.66 ± 0.043 at 1 Hz, and 0.73 ± 0.056 at 2 Hz (Fig. 3). In addition, comparison of maximum angular eye velocity by rotation direction during the VOR test (Fig. 3b) showed that, at 1 Hz, the maximum angular velocities were 44.6 ± 1.8°/s during CW rotation and 44.6 ± 1.8°/s during CCW rotation. At 2 Hz, the values were 46.1 ± 2.0°/s (CW) and 48.3 ± 2.4°/s (CCW). No significant differences were observed between CW and CCW rotations at either frequency (1 Hz: p = 0.97; 2 Hz: p = 0.07), indicating no apparent directional asymmetry. Because estimation of maximum angular velocity at 0.3 Hz was more variable, likely due to the larger amplitude of eye movements, directional comparisons were limited to 1 and 2 Hz, where measurements were more stable.

These results indicate that VOR responses in the arsanilic acid–induced vestibular dysfunction model are increased across frequencies without significant directional asymmetry, and are consistent with the caloric test findings.

Discussion

Three days after intratympanic arsanilic acid administration, the caloric test showed no change on the affected side but a marked reduction on the unaffected side. In contrast, VOR analysis revealed increased gain in the vestibular dysfunction model, and mild structural abnormalities were observed in the lateral semicircular canal crista. Together, these findings indicate that combined caloric testing and VOR analysis clarify vestibular compensation.

Based on Bárány's endolymph convection theory, the caloric test induces temperature-dependent nystagmus via endolymph flow, enabling independent assessment of each lateral semicircular canal [8]. Although widely used clinically, it is rarely applied in animal studies. To ensure proper endolymph convection in mice, the lateral semicircular canal must be oriented vertically. Clinically, the human canal is tilted ∼30° from horizontal, so patients are tested supine with 30° head flexion [18]. Because the mouse lateral semicircular canal is tilted ∼32° from horizontal [16] and angles >40° obscure the pupil via the otolith–ocular reflex [9], the tilt was set to 40°. Clinically, 30 °C water is used as a cold stimulus [19]; however, ice water was applied here to enhance nystagmus detection. To validate caloric-evoked ocular responses in mice, head-up (40°) and head-down (40°) positions were tested. Ice-water stimulation induced nystagmus with opposite fast-phase directions depending on head position (Fig. 1a), whereas 37 °C water elicited no nystagmus in either position. These findings align with clinical caloric responses [17,20] and support the reliability of the caloric test in mice.

Next, we applied the caloric test to a mouse model of vestibular dysfunction induced by transtympanic arsanilic acid. MSPV was 68.1 ± 5.7°/s in the affected (left) ear and 40.4 ± 4.7°/s in the intact (right) ear (Fig. 2). Compared with normal mice, MSPV was unchanged on the affected side but showed a trend toward reduction on the intact side compared with normal controls. Interestingly, stimulation of the intact side produced a smaller MSPV than stimulation of the lesioned side. Previous studies on vestibular compensation have primarily focused on changes on the affected side, such as hyperactivity of the lesioned vestibular nuclei [21,22], and, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have specifically examined alterations on the contralateral unaffected side. In contrast, the results of our VOR analysis were consistent with those reported previously. Together, these findings show that the caloric test detects intact-side functional changes, highlighting its importance for comprehensive vestibular compensation analysis.

It should be noted that caloric responses and rotational VOR assess different frequency domains and underlying mechanisms; therefore, some degree of dissociation between these measures is expected. In this context, the reduced MSPV on the intact side may reflect specific aspects of vestibular compensation, such as contralateral suppression or commissural modulation, that are not captured by sinusoidal angular VOR gain alone. Accordingly, our findings indicate that the caloric test offers complementary information to VOR analysis and may contribute to a more comprehensive evaluation of vestibular compensation.

In rats and guinea pigs, static symptoms associated with vestibular imbalance, such as spontaneous nystagmus, postural deviation, and vertigo, reportedly disappear within 2–3 days due to vestibular compensation [23]. In the present study, spontaneous nystagmus was also absent by day 3 after arsanilic acid administration. Previous studies using the same model have shown marked vestibular dysfunction and spontaneous nystagmus during the first 1–2 days after arsanilic acid treatment, followed by gradual recovery thereafter [8]. Consistent with these results, Ito et al. reported that spontaneous nystagmus disappeared by day 2 following arsanilic acid treatment [8], suggesting that vestibular compensation was similarly progressing in mice.

During the progression phase of vestibular compensation, it has been shown that inhibitory commissural pathways between the vestibular nuclei [24] and inhibitory pathways from the vestibulocerebellum to the vestibular nuclei [25] enhance suppression of the unaffected side, resulting in a transient increase in neuronal activity in the vestibular nuclei on the affected side [21]. Consistent with this mechanism, the present caloric test demonstrated that the MSPV was reduced on the intact side compared with that in normal mice, whereas it recovered to a level comparable to that of normal mice on the affected side (Fig. 2).

In addition, VOR testing revealed an increased gain compared with that in normal mice (Fig. 3). However, the correlation between VOR and caloric test results (Table 1) was limited, with significant associations observed only at the low-frequency range (0.3 Hz), whereas no significant correlations were found at mid-to-high frequencies (1–2 Hz). This discrepancy likely reflects fundamental physiological differences between the two tests: VOR primarily represents bilateral vestibular input and central compensatory mechanisms at mid-to-high frequencies, whereas the caloric test emphasizes unilateral vestibular function and asymmetry at extremely low frequencies [26,27]. In particular, although VOR responses tend to recover relatively early due to central compensation after vestibular injury, caloric responses continue to sensitively reflect side-to-side asymmetry, which may account for the limited agreement between the two measures [26].

In conjunction with the vestibular compensation mechanisms described above, rotational stimulation of the semicircular canals is known to induce commissural inhibition [28], which may have further enhanced neuronal activity on the affected side. Moreover, it should be noted that rotational and caloric stimuli differ fundamentally in their modes of action: rotational stimulation induces endolymph flow via angular acceleration, resulting in excitation of one semicircular canal and inhibition of the contralateral side, whereas caloric stimulation (particularly cold irrigation) suppresses neural activity in the stimulated canal through thermally induced endolymph convection. Thus, these two stimuli provide physiologically opposite inputs (excitation vs inhibition) to the same canal, making direct one-to-one comparisons inherently limited [26,29]. In a unilateral vestibular labyrinthectomy model using squirrel monkeys, sinusoidal angular VOR gain was reported to increase transiently during the progression phase of vestibular compensation, reaching levels comparable to those of the control side by day 10 after lesioning [22]. Together, these results suggest that transient vestibular hyperactivity during compensation contributed to the increased sinusoidal angular VOR gain in the arsanilic acid-induced vestibular dysfunction mice. Therefore, the limited and condition-dependent relationship observed in this study likely reflects the frequency-dependent properties of the vestibular system and the influence of central compensation, rather than inconsistency between the two measurement modalities.

Based on these results, the present study demonstrates that caloric testing is a valuable method for assessing the degree of bilateral inhibitory modulation associated with vestibular compensation.

Morphological analysis revealed mild damage in the crista ampullaris of the lateral semicircular canal on the lesioned side treated with arsanilic acid. According to Ito et al., vacuole formation appears as early as 1 day after arsanilic acid administration, whereas more extensive vacuolation and structural disruption develop by day 7 [8]. Thus, the relatively mild morphological changes observed at day 3 may have allowed the vestibular end organs to retain responsiveness to stimulation. In addition, transient hyperactivity associated with vestibular compensation may have contributed to the enhanced responses observed on the lesioned side. However, the histological findings were qualitative and descriptive only and do not establish the severity or completeness of vestibular hair-cell damage. Therefore, these observations should be interpreted as supportive evidence of vestibular pathology rather than definitive evidence of lesion extent.

This study has several limitations. First, the time course of vestibular compensation was not fully characterized, as assessments were performed at a single time point (day 3). Previous studies have shown that vestibular dysfunction peaks around day 2 after arsanilic acid–induced lesions, remains pronounced around day 3 (representing a peak or early stabilization phase), and begins to recover by approximately day 7 [8,30]. Thus, additional time points (e.g., day 1, day 7, and day 10) would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the dynamic changes in vestibular function. In particular, the temporal profile of spontaneous nystagmus (e.g., presence or absence at Day 1 and Day 2) was not evaluated in this study, which limits confirmation of acute vestibular asymmetry. Although previous studies using this model have reported marked vestibular dysfunction and spontaneous nystagmus during the first 1–2 days after arsanilic acid administration [8], these early changes were not directly assessed in the present study. However, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility of applying the caloric test in a mouse model, and day 3 was selected as a representative early-phase time point.

Second, the lesion model has inherent limitations. The transtympanic arsanilic acid model may not produce a fully uniform or completely destructive lesion, and variability in lesion extent cannot be excluded. Importantly, distinguishing between a true destructive lesion and a transient irritative state remains difficult in the present study, and our data do not allow us to definitively resolve this issue. In addition, because a definitive complete-lesion control (e.g., surgical labyrinthectomy) was not included, it remains unclear whether the preserved caloric response on the treated side reflects incomplete lesioning or potential methodological influences. Furthermore, the use of the contralateral ear as an internal control, while commonly employed in unilateral vestibular lesion models, has inherent limitations because vestibular function is not independent between sides due to commissural interactions. Therefore, the “intact” side may also reflect compensatory changes rather than a true normal state, as previously reported [31,32]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies incorporating a complete lesion model, along with evaluation at later time points (e.g., ≥7 days after arsanilic acid administration), will be necessary to better characterize the lesion and the progression of vestibular compensation.

In this study, we demonstrated that the caloric test—which is widely used in clinical practice—can evaluate not only normal vestibular function in mice but also vestibular compensation following injury. Because the caloric test can be performed in a relatively simple manner, its combined application with VOR analysis may provide a valuable approach for assessing vestibular function, including vestibular compensation, in mouse models.
