Traditional Chinese medicine combined with conventional Western medicine for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shengtao Cui, Xiaofei Xie, Jingjing Yang, Yao Xiao, Wei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic cough following respiratory infection in children is often refractory to conventional therapies. However, the current clinical evidence regarding the efficacy of traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) remains inconclusive and lacks systematic synthesis, leaving clinicians without clear guidance. This study systematically evaluated the efficacy and safety of TCM for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children to support clinical decision-making.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Wiley Online Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang databases. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of TCM for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children were included. Meta-analyses were performed using RevMan 5.4 and Stata 16.0.ResultsA total of 23 RCTs involving 2,126 children were included. Meta-analysis demonstrated that, compared with conventional Western medical treatment alone, TCM combined with conventional treatment significantly improved the overall clinical effectiveness rate [risk ratio (RR) =1.19, 95% confidence interval (CI) (1.15, 1.24), P<0.001], shortened the time to cough resolution [mean difference (MD) =−3.19, 95% CI (−4.42, −1.97), P<0.001] and the time to disappearance of pulmonary rales [MD =−2.00, 95% CI (−2.47, −1.54), P<0.001], and significantly reduced cough symptom scores [MD =−1.32, 95% CI (−1.69, −0.95), P<0.001]. For secondary outcomes, combined TCM therapy significantly reduced TCM syndrome scores, improved immune function indicators [immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin M (IgM), and immunoglobulin G (IgG)], and decreased levels of inflammatory biomarkers [C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-4 (MCP-4)]. No statistically significant difference was observed between groups in the incidence of adverse events.ConclusionsTCM used as an adjunct to conventional Western medical treatment may provide additional benefits in improving clinical outcomes and alleviating symptoms in children with post-infectious chronic cough. However, due to limitations in study quality and heterogeneity, further high-quality, well-designed studies are warranted to strengthen the evidence base.Keywords: Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), chronic cough, respiratory infection, children, systematic review

Highlight box

Key findings

• This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) combined with conventional Western medicine significantly improved clinical outcomes, shortened cough duration, alleviated symptoms, and modulated immune and inflammatory responses in children with chronic cough following respiratory infection.

What is known and what is new?

• Chronic cough following respiratory infection is a common pediatric condition associated with persistent airway inflammation, cough hypersensitivity, and airway mucosal injury. Conventional Western medical treatments are widely used, but their efficacy varies, and some children continue to experience prolonged symptoms. TCM has been increasingly applied as an adjunctive therapy; however, the overall evidence regarding its efficacy and safety remains uncertain.

• This study provides a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating TCM combined with conventional Western medicine for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children. The findings demonstrate its potential benefits in improving clinical effectiveness, reducing cough duration and symptom severity, and regulating immune and inflammatory responses, while not increasing adverse events.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• TCM combined with conventional Western medicine may be considered as a potential adjunctive treatment strategy for children with chronic cough following respiratory infection. However, standardized treatment protocols and well-designed, high-quality randomized controlled trials are needed to further confirm its effectiveness and safety.

Introduction

Chronic cough following respiratory infection is a common clinical condition in pediatric practice. It is defined as a cough persisting for more than four weeks after the resolution of acute respiratory infection symptoms (1), in the absence of other identifiable causes such as cough variant asthma, upper airway cough syndrome, or gastroesophageal reflux-related cough (2). As an important subtype of pediatric chronic cough, post-infectious cough has a consistently high incidence rate in children (3). Infants and young children are particularly susceptible due to immature immune systems and underdeveloped airway mucosal barrier function (4). Moreover, the prevalence of this condition increases markedly during seasons with frequent climatic fluctuations (5). Clinically, it is characterized by a persistent dry cough or cough with minimal sputum, often worsening at night or in the early morning. When prolonged, it can significantly impair sleep quality, appetite, and physical growth, while also imposing substantial caregiving burdens and financial strain on families. Consequently, post-infectious chronic cough in children represents a pressing clinical challenge in pediatrics.

The pathogenesis of chronic cough following respiratory infection in children has not yet been fully elucidated. Current evidence suggests that it is closely associated with post-infectious airway mucosal injury, persistent inflammatory responses, heightened cough reflex sensitivity, and airway hyperresponsiveness (6). Following infection with viruses, bacteria, or Mycoplasma pneumoniae, airway epithelial cells may become damaged and desquamated, leading to exposure of sensory nerve endings. This results in upregulation of peripheral and central cough reflex sensitivity. Even after the infection is controlled, abnormal cough reflexes may persist (7), ultimately leading to chronic cough (Figure 1). In clinical practice, conventional management of post-infectious chronic cough in children includes symptomatic and supportive therapies such as antitussives, antihistamines, expectorants, leukotriene receptor antagonists, inhaled corticosteroids, and, in selected cases, antibiotics or mucolytic agents (8). Current international guidelines also recognize the potential role of non-pharmacological and herbal-based therapies, including honey and certain herbal preparations, in cough management (9,10). Nevertheless, treatment efficacy remains variable, and some patients continue to experience prolonged or recurrent symptoms despite conventional therapy. Therefore, there remains a clinical need for safe and effective adjunctive therapeutic strategies.

Conceptual framework of chronic cough in children. TCM, traditional Chinese medicine.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has been used for centuries in the management of pediatric respiratory diseases. Guided by the principles of holistic regulation and syndrome differentiation, TCM interventions aim to restore internal balance, regulate “Qi” dynamics, eliminate phlegm and dampness, and enhance the body’s defensive capacity (11). TCM encompasses a comprehensive therapeutic system that includes Chinese herbal medicine, patented Chinese medicines, acupuncture, moxibustion, pediatric massage (tuina), and other non-pharmacological interventions (12). Although herbal and plant-derived therapies are also incorporated into some contemporary Western and integrative approaches for cough management, TCM is characterized by its distinct theoretical framework and individualized syndrome-based treatment strategies. In recent years, a growing number of clinical studies have reported favorable outcomes of TCM interventions for pediatric chronic cough (13). Nevertheless, the overall quality, methodological rigor, and consistency of these studies remain unclear. Existing reviews have largely focused on adult populations, single therapeutic modalities, or heterogeneous causes of chronic cough (14). Moreover, few reviews have specifically addressed chronic cough following respiratory infection in children—a distinct clinical subgroup with unique pathophysiological characteristics and therapeutic needs. Due to the lack of comprehensive and methodologically robust synthesis, current evidence cannot be effectively translated into clinical practice.

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively evaluate the efficacy and safety of TCM interventions for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children. By synthesizing evidence from available clinical studies, we sought to provide evidence-based support for clinical decision-making and identify gaps to inform future research. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0205/rc).

Data sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted across both English and Chinese electronic databases. English databases included the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Wiley Online Library. Chinese databases included China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) and the Wanfang Data Knowledge Service Platform. The search covered literature from database inception to January 2026.

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms using Boolean operators. Search strategies were adapted for each database (Table S1). Reference lists of included studies were also screened manually to identify additional eligible studies.

Study selection and data extraction

Two investigators independently screened studies by reviewing titles, abstracts, and full texts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Data were independently extracted using a standardized Excel form, including author, publication year, sample size, participant characteristics, intervention details, treatment duration, and outcome measures. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third investigator. Corresponding authors were contacted when necessary to obtain missing data.

Risk of bias assessment

The methodological quality of included RCTs was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2.0). The following domains were evaluated: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants, personnel, and outcome assessors, completeness of outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential sources of bias. Each domain was rated as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “unclear risk”. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third investigator.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 and Stata 16.0. For dichotomous outcomes, effect sizes were expressed as RR with corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI). For continuous outcomes, MD with 95% CIs were used when identical measurement instruments were applied; otherwise, standardized mean differences (SMDs) were calculated when different measurement scales were used.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-square test and I2 statistic. A fixed-effect model was used when I2≤50% and P>0.10; otherwise, a random-effects model was applied. Sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed when substantial heterogeneity was detected for primary outcomes.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, Egger’s test and Begg’s test. The certainty of evidence for primary and safety outcomes was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system and classified as high, moderate, low, or very low. A two-sided P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study selection

A total of 264 records were identified through database searching. After removal of 20 duplicates, 244 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Full-text assessment was performed for 49 potentially eligible studies, of which 26 were excluded based on the predefined criteria. Ultimately, 23 studies were included in the meta-analysis. The study selection process is shown in Figure 2.

Study flow diagram. CNKI, China National Knowledge Infrastructure; RCT, randomized controlled trial.

Study characteristics

A total of 23 RCTs (15-37) involving 2,126 pediatric patients were included, with 1,062 patients in the treatment groups and 1,064 in the control groups. The studies were published between 2013 and 2025. Most participants had chronic cough following Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, (approximately 18 studies), while the remaining studies involved common respiratory infections, recurrent respiratory tract infections, or pneumonia. The mean age of participants ranged from 4.1 to 8.9 years, with a balanced sex distribution.

Most studies evaluated integrative therapy combining TCM and Western medicine. TCM interventions mainly included oral herbal formulations such as Libai Xiefei Pingchuan Decoction, Yangyin Qingfei Decoction, Qingfei Huatan Decoction, Sangju Decoction, and Yupingfeng Oral Liquid. External therapies included pediatric tuina and gua sha combined with acupoint application. Control groups received conventional Western medical treatment alone, most commonly azithromycin, montelukast, ambroxol, or nebulized budesonide.

The treatment duration was predominantly 14 days (17 studies), with a minority of studies employing treatment courses of 7 days, 28 days, 8 weeks, or 3 months. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Risk of bias assessment

All included studies were RCTs. Twenty studies clearly described random sequence generation, most commonly using random number tables, whereas three studies mentioned randomization without specifying the method. Allocation concealment was not reported in any study. Only one study explicitly reported lack of blinding, and one study reported blinding of outcome assessors. Blinding procedures were otherwise unclear in most studies. Outcome data were generally complete, and no obvious selective reporting was identified. Two studies were judged to have other potential sources of bias. The overall risk of bias assessment is presented in Figure 3.

Meta-analysis results

Primary outcomes

Overall clinical effectiveness rate

Overall clinical effectiveness was reported in 21 RCTs involving 1,946 participants. Clinically effective response was achieved in 918/972 patients (94.44%) in the intervention group and 771/974 patients (79.16%) in the control group. The pooled effects of the primary outcomes and the corresponding sensitivity analyses are presented in Figure 4 and Figure 5, respectively. Meta-analysis showed that integrated Chinese and Western medicine significantly improved overall clinical effectiveness rate compared with conventional Western medicine alone [risk ratio (RR) =1.19, 95% CI (1.15, 1.24), P<0.001], with low heterogeneity (I2=0%) (Figure 4A). However, results should still be interpreted cautiously due to methodological differences among studies. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis showed that excluding any single study did not materially change the pooled effect estimate (Figure 5A).

Subgroup analyses were performed to assess result consistency across treatment settings. By TCM intervention type, both herbal medicine [RR =1.20, 95% CI (1.16, 1.25), P<0.001; I2=0%] and physical therapy [RR =1.13, 95% CI (1.05, 1.23), P=0.002; I2=35%] showed superior efficacy versus conventional treatment, with no significant subgroup differences (see Figure S1A). By Western medicine regimen, combined therapy significantly improved clinical effectiveness in both the azithromycin subgroup [RR =1.18, 95% CI (1.13, 1.24), P<0.001; I2=22%] and non-azithromycin subgroup [RR =1.20, 95% CI (1.14, 1.27), P<0.001; I2=0%] (see Figure S1B). Consistent benefits were also observed in both the montelukast subgroup [RR =1.21, 95% CI (1.13, 1.30), P<0.001; I2=0%] and non-montelukast subgroup [RR =1.19, 95% CI (1.14, 1.24), P<0.001; I2=0%] (see Figure S1C).

Time to disappearance of cough

Eight RCTs involving 890 patients reported time to cough disappearance. Integrated Chinese and Western medicine significantly shortened cough duration compared with conventional treatment alone [MD =−3.19, 95% CI (−4.42, −1.97), P<0.001]. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2=98%, P<0.001) (Figure 4B). Sensitivity analysis did not materially alter the pooled estimates (Figure 5B).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential heterogeneity sources. By TCM intervention type, both herbal medicine therapy [MD =−3.09, 95% CI (−4.36, −1.83), P<0.001; I2=98%] and physical therapy [MD =−4.67, 95% CI (−8.32, −1.02), P=0.01] significantly shortened cough duration, with no significant subgroup differences (see Figure S2A). By Western medicine regimen, both the azithromycin subgroup [MD =−4.07, 95% CI (−6.08, −2.06), P<0.001; I2=98%] and non-azithromycin subgroup [MD =−1.91, 95% CI (−3.28, −0.54), P=0.006; I2=93%] showed consistent benefits, with no significant subgroup differences (see Figure S2B). Similarly, both the montelukast subgroup [MD =−1.48, 95% CI (−2.56, −0.40), P=0.007] and non-montelukast subgroup [MD =−3.44, 95% CI (−4.78, −2.11), P<0.001; I2=98%] favored combined therapy, subgroup differences were statistically significant (P=0.03) (see Figure S2C).

Time to disappearance of pulmonary rales

Six RCTs involving 728 patients reported time to disappearance of pulmonary rales. Meta-analysis showed that integrated Chinese and Western medicine significantly shortened the duration of pulmonary rales compared with conventional treatment alone [MD =−2.00, 95% CI (−2.47, −1.54), P<0.001], with substantial heterogeneity (I2=78%, P<0.001) (Figure 4C). Sensitivity analysis showed that excluding any single study did not materially change the pooled effect estimate, but the source of heterogeneity could not be identified (Figure 5C).

As included studies mainly used herbal medicine therapy, subgroup analysis by TCM intervention type was not performed. Subgroup analyses based on conventional Western medicine regimens showed consistent benefits in both the azithromycin subgroup [MD =−2.12, 95% CI (−2.86, −1.37), P<0.001; I2=85%] and non-azithromycin subgroup [MD =−1.86, 95% CI (−2.53, −1.18), P<0.001; I2=44%], with no significant subgroup differences (see Figure S3A). Similar findings were observed in the montelukast and non-montelukast subgroups, without significant subgroup differences (see Figure S3B).

Cough symptom score

Ten RCTs involving 894 patients reported cough symptom scores. Meta-analysis showed that integrated Chinese and Western medicine significantly improved cough symptom scores compared with conventional treatment alone [MD =−1.32, 95% CI (−1.69, −0.95), P<0.001], with substantial heterogeneity (I2=99%, P<0.001) (Figure 4D). Sensitivity analysis showed that excluding any single study did not materially change the pooled effect estimate, but the source of heterogeneity remained unclear (Figure 5D).

Subgroup analyses by TCM intervention type showed significant benefits in both the herbal medicine subgroup [MD =−1.14, 95% CI (−1.50, −0.78), P<0.001; I2=99%] and physical therapy subgroup [MD =−1.76, 95% CI (−3.00, −0.52), P=0.005; I2=93%], with no significant subgroup differences (see Figure S4A). Similar trends were observed in the azithromycin [MD =−1.25, 95% CI (−1.64, −0.86), P<0.001; I2=99%] and non-azithromycin subgroups [MD =−1.46, 95% CI (−2.66, −0.26), P=0.02; I2=99%] (see Figure S4B), as well as the montelukast [MD =−1.09, 95% CI (−2.53, 0.34), P=0.14; I2=99%] and non-montelukast subgroups [MD =−1.42, 95% CI (−1.85, −0.98), P<0.00001; I2=99%], without significant subgroup differences (see Figure S4C).

Secondary outcomes

Total TCM syndrome score

Three RCTs reported total TCM syndrome scores. Meta-analysis showed that combined therapy significantly reduced TCM syndrome scores compared with conventional treatment alone [MD =−1.76, 95% CI (−2.46, −1.07), P<0.001] (see Figure S5).

Immunoglobulins

Three RCTs each reported IgA, IgM, and IgG outcomes. Compared with conventional treatment, combined therapy significantly improved IgA [MD =0.30, 95% CI (0.25, 0.36), P<0.001], IgM [MD =0.24, 95% CI (0.18, 0.29), P<0.001], and IgG levels [MD =1.71, 95% CI (1.01, 2.41), P<0.001] (see Figure S6).

Inflammatory biomarkers

Eight RCTs reported data on CRP, five on IL-6, four on TNF-α, and three on MCP-4. Meta-analysis showed that combined therapy significantly reduced CRP [MD =−4.65, 95% CI (−6.54, −2.76), P<0.001], IL-6 [SMD =−1.53, 95% CI (−2.57, −0.48), P<0.001], TNF-α [MD =−12.77, 95% CI (−22.88, −2.65), P=0.01], and MCP-4 levels [MD =−25.54, 95% CI (−31.66, −19.43), P<0.001] the conventional treatment (see Figure S7).

Adverse events

Ten studies reported adverse events. Meta-analysis showed no statistically significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the combined treatment group and the conventional treatment group [RR =1.00, 95% CI (0.61, 1.65), P>0.99] (see Figure S8). A total of 34 adverse events occurred in the control group and 22 in the combined treatment group. Reported events included gastrointestinal symptoms, fatigue, headache, dizziness, rash, hypersensitivity reactions, gastric discomfort, throat discomfort, and lethargy.

Publication bias assessment

Egger’s and Begg’s tests showed no significant publication bias (all P≥0.05). For cough resolution time and pulmonary rales disappearance time, fewer than 10 studies were included, limiting funnel plot interpretation; however, Egger’s and Begg’s tests still detected no publication bias (Table S2). Funnel plots for overall clinical effectiveness, cough symptom scores, and adverse events were approximately symmetrical, and statistical tests likewise indicated low risk of publication bias (Figure 6).

GRADE levels of evidence

According to the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence for the primary and safety outcomes was low to very low (Table 2). All included studies were conducted in China and generally showed high risk of bias, mainly due to insufficient reporting of blinding and allocation concealment. Substantial heterogeneity further reduced consistency and precision. Although TCM interventions may improve clinical effectiveness and cough-related outcomes in children with post-infectious chronic cough, the findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis comprehensively evaluated the efficacy and safety of TCM interventions for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children. Based on pooled data from 23 RCTs involving a total of 2,126 pediatric patients, the results showed that, compared with conventional Western medicine alone, TCM combined with conventional treatment was associated with statistically significant improvements in overall clinical effectiveness, shorter time to cough resolution and disappearance of pulmonary rales, and greater reductions in cough symptom scores. In addition, combined therapy demonstrated beneficial effects on certain inflammatory and immune-related indicators without increasing the risk of adverse events. However, the GRADE assessment indicated that the overall quality of evidence for primary outcomes and safety ranged from low to very low, suggesting that the current evidence base remains limited and that the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion of the results

The findings of this study indicate that TCM combined with conventional treatment significantly improved the overall clinical effectiveness rate in children with post-infectious chronic cough (RR =1.19), with very low heterogeneity across studies (I2=0%). However, despite the low statistical heterogeneity, the results should still be interpreted cautiously because the included studies differed in several aspects, including TCM intervention types and treatment duration. In addition, most studies lacked blinding and relied on study-defined “clinical effectiveness” outcomes, which may have been influenced by subjective assessment bias. Therefore, the apparent consistency of treatment effects across studies may partly reflect shared methodological limitations rather than true uniformity of intervention effects. Although the overall clinical effectiveness rate is a commonly used composite outcome in pediatric studies in China and may involve a certain degree of subjectivity, it still provides a practical reflection of overall symptom improvement. Subgroup analyses further showed generally consistent effect directions across different TCM intervention types and conventional treatment regimens, suggesting that these factors were unlikely to materially influence the overall conclusions. Although the included TCM interventions differed in modality, including herbal medicine, external therapies, and other non-pharmacological approaches, they were all applied within the theoretical framework of TCM and aimed to alleviate post-infectious cough-related symptoms through regulation of inflammatory responses and restoration of functional balance. Subgroup analyses based on intervention type showed generally consistent directions of effect, supporting the feasibility of evaluating these interventions within an integrated TCM framework. Nevertheless, given the substantial clinical diversity among interventions, the pooled estimates should still be interpreted cautiously.

In terms of objective symptom improvement, TCM combined therapy also demonstrated clear advantages in shortening cough resolution time and the time to disappearance of pulmonary rales. Cough duration is an important indicator of disease burden in post-infectious chronic cough, as persistent coughing not only affects children’s quality of life but may also increase caregiver anxiety and healthcare utilization (38). The pooled results showed that combined treatment shortened cough resolution time by approximately 3 days and pulmonary rales disappearance time by about 2 days, indicating potential clinical relevance. Similarly, the meta-analysis of cough symptom scores supported the potential benefit of TCM combined therapy in reducing cough severity. However, substantial heterogeneity was observed for these outcomes (I2 ranging from 78% to 99%). Although subgroup analyses based on TCM intervention type and conventional treatment regimen were performed, substantial heterogeneity remained within most subgroups, and no significant subgroup differences were observed overall. These findings suggest that the predefined subgroup factors were unlikely to fully explain the observed heterogeneity. Other clinical and methodological differences, including disease severity, treatment duration, baseline patient characteristics, and outcome assessment timing, may also have contributed to the heterogeneity in symptom-related outcomes.

Secondary outcome analyses showed that TCM combined therapy was superior to conventional treatment alone in reducing levels of inflammatory markers (CRP, IL-6, TNF-α, and MCP-4) and increasing immunoglobulin levels (IgA, IgM, and IgG). These findings provide biological support for the potential mechanisms of TCM interventions. Post-infectious chronic cough is thought to be associated with persistent low-grade airway inflammation, increased neural sensitivity, and immune dysregulation following airway mucosal injury (39). Certain Chinese herbal formulations are believed to have effects such as clearing the lung, resolving phlegm, tonifying qi, nourishing yin, and modulating immune function, which may contribute to symptom relief by suppressing inflammatory mediator release, improving mucosal barrier function, and enhancing host immune defense (40). However, current mechanistic evidence is largely derived from animal studies (41) or small-scale clinical investigations, and high-quality mechanistic studies incorporating multi-omics or immunological approaches are still needed. In addition, approximately half of the included studies used azithromycin-based regimens in the control group, whereas the remaining studies mainly employed symptomatic or anti-inflammatory treatments. Current pediatric respiratory guidelines recommend antibiotics for certain conditions, such as protracted bacterial bronchitis, although amoxicillin—clavulanate is generally considered first-line therapy, while macrolides such as azithromycin are more commonly reserved for atypical pathogens or suspected pertussis (42). Therefore, the effectiveness of azithromycin-based background therapy may have varied across different clinical settings. In some studies, the observed treatment benefits may partly reflect the adjunctive effects of TCM in the context of potentially limited antibiotic responsiveness. Because most included studies did not employ blinding or placebo controls, subjective outcomes such as symptom improvement and overall clinical effectiveness may have been influenced to some extent by contextual or expectation-related effects. Nevertheless, the observed improvements in several inflammatory and immune-related indicators suggest that the potential benefits of TCM may not be explained solely by these factors.

Safety is a critical consideration in pediatric therapeutic strategies. The pooled analysis showed no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between TCM combined therapy and conventional treatment, suggesting acceptable overall tolerability. Reported adverse events were mostly mild and reversible, primarily involving gastrointestinal or dermatological reactions, and no serious adverse events were reported. However, the quality of adverse event reporting varied across studies, with some trials providing only brief statements such as “no obvious adverse reactions”, raising concerns about potential underreporting. Therefore, although no clear safety signals were identified, potential risks cannot be completely excluded. Future trials should adopt standardized and detailed adverse event reporting to improve the reliability of safety evidence.

Relationship to and comparison with other studies

Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses on chronic cough have mainly focused on adult populations or pooled chronic cough of different etiologies (43), resulting in substantial heterogeneity in study populations and disease mechanisms. In addition, reviews of TCM interventions have often focused on single treatment modalities (44) and rarely provided a systematic evaluation of pediatric post-infectious chronic cough as a distinct clinical subgroup (45). To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review and meta-analysis specifically focusing on chronic cough following respiratory infection in children, a population with a clearly defined etiological background and unique pathophysiological characteristics. By avoiding the mixed analysis of heterogeneous types of chronic cough, this study reduces potential bias and enhances clinical relevance.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it is the first systematic review and meta-analysis focusing specifically on TCM interventions for chronic cough following respiratory infection in children, addressing a gap in the literature where most studies have involved adult or mixed populations with heterogeneous etiologies. Second, the study strictly followed the PRISMA guidelines and systematically searched multiple major Chinese and international databases. All included studies were RCTs, enhancing the comprehensiveness and representativeness of the findings. Third, beyond clinical symptom outcomes, this review integrated analyses of inflammatory markers, immune function indicators, and safety outcomes, providing a multidimensional evaluation of the potential mechanisms and clinical value of TCM combined therapy. Finally, sensitivity analyses, publication bias assessments, and GRADE evaluations were conducted to systematically appraise the quality and robustness of the evidence, supporting more cautious and evidence-based clinical interpretation.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the TCM interventions varied considerably in formulation type, administration route, treatment duration, and combination strategies with conventional therapies, which may have affected the comparability of pooled estimates. Second, outcome definitions and assessment methods were not fully standardized among studies, potentially contributing to heterogeneity and limiting the interpretability of pooled results. Finally, several secondary outcomes, including immunoglobulin indicators and certain inflammatory biomarkers, were reported in only a limited number of studies with relatively small sample sizes, reducing the robustness and generalizability of these findings.

Clinical implications and future research directions

Despite these limitations, this study provides relatively comprehensive evidence supporting the potential role of TCM interventions in the management of post-infectious chronic cough in children. From a clinical perspective, TCM combined therapy may serve as a potentially useful adjunct to conventional treatment, particularly for children with prolonged symptoms, recurrent episodes, or suboptimal response to standard therapies. However, given the substantial clinical heterogeneity and generally low certainty of evidence, current findings should be interpreted as supportive rather than definitive evidence of efficacy. Future research should prioritize the following areas: (I) well-designed, adequately powered, multicenter RCTs with rigorous randomization, blinding, and allocation concealment; (II) standardized diagnostic criteria, intervention protocols, and outcome measures to improve comparability across studies; (III) enhanced monitoring and reporting of safety outcomes and long-term efficacy; (IV) further exploration of potential sources of heterogeneity; (V) in-depth exploration of the mechanisms underlying TCM interventions to strengthen biological plausibility.

Conclusions

TCM combined with conventional treatment may offer potential advantages in improving clinical symptoms of post-infectious chronic cough in children, as well as in modulating inflammatory and immune parameters, with an overall acceptable safety profile. However, because background therapies varied across the included studies and azithromycin may not represent optimal antibiotic treatment in some clinical settings, the independent contribution of TCM to the observed effects requires further clarification. Given the limited quality of the current evidence, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require further validation through high-quality studies.
