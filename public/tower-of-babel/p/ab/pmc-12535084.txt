Fixed-effect or random-effect models? A methodological reappraisal of subgroup analyses in mesenchymal stem cell therapy for knee osteoarthritis
Authors: Shanshan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We commend Cao et al. for their systematic review demonstrating the efficacy of intra-articular mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy in alleviating pain and improving function in patients with non-surgical knee osteoarthritis (OA). However, we reanalyzed their subgroup analyses to evaluate the methodological implications of statistical model selection (fixed-effect vs. random-effect models) on result reliability. In dose-stratified analyses, Cao et al. applied fixed-effect models to low-dose (I2 = 0%) and high-dose (I2 = 80%) MSC subgroups. Upon reanalysis using random-effect models, the high-dose group showed no statistically significant differences in Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) total scores compared to the control group at 6 months [MD = 8.75; 95% CI (−2.10, 19.61); P = 0.11] or 12 months [MD = 12.68; 95% CI (−4.96, 30.32); P = 0.16], contrasting with Cao et al.’s original findings. The low-dose subgroup, with no heterogeneity, yielded identical results across both models. Similarly, in cell-source stratification (adipose-derived MSCs [ADMSCs] vs. bone marrow-derived MSCs [BM-MSCs]), reanalysis of ADMSCs using random-effect models demonstrated significant 6-month WOMAC improvement [MD = 9.32; 95% CI (3.73, 14.92); P = 0.001] but non-significant 12-month differences [MD = 12.90; 95% CI (−1.76, 27.55); P = 0.08], diverging from Cao et al.’s conclusions. BM-MSCs results remained consistent due to negligible heterogeneity (I2 = 0%). These findings underscore that fixed-effect models artificially narrow confidence intervals in heterogeneous populations, overestimating clinical significance. Our results align with Cochrane guidelines, emphasizing that random-effect models better accommodate inter-study diversity, yielding conservative and clinically generalizable estimates. This critique reinforces the necessity of transparent statistical model selection in meta-analyses, particularly when subgroup heterogeneity may influence therapeutic interpretations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04650-6.

Main text

We read with great interest the article by Cao et al. entitled "Efficacy and safety of mesenchymal stem cells in knee osteoarthritis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials" [1]. We congratulate Cao and colleagues on their significant contributions, which demonstrated that intra-articular MSC therapy achieves substantial pain relief and functional improvement in patients with non-surgical OA, highlighting its potential as an optimized therapeutic strategy. However, we wish to critically discuss the reliability of subgroup analysis conclusions in their study, particularly regarding the choice of fixed-effect versus random-effect models and its direct impact on result interpretation.

Dose stratification

Study design and model selection

In Cao et al.’s study, the intervention group was stratified into low-dose and high-dose subgroups based on administered intra-articular MSC doses [1]. WOMAC total scores were assessed for both intervention and control groups at 6 and 12 months, with 6-month WOMAC and 12-month WOMAC serving as outcome measures. Fixed-effect models were uniformly applied for subgroup analyses. We contend that random-effect models would have been more appropriate. While the low-dose subgroup exhibited no heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), the high-dose subgroup showed substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 80%), with I2 values ≥ 75% indicating high variability across studies.

Methodological rationale

As outlined in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.5), fixed-effect meta-analyses inherently disregard heterogeneity [2]. Although fixed-effect pooled estimates are often interpreted as the "best approximation" of intervention effects, heterogeneity implies distributed rather than singular true effects. Consequently, fixed-effect models may produce artificially narrow confidence intervals (CIs), misrepresenting the generalizability of findings. In contrast, random-effect models account for inter-study heterogeneity, generating wider CIs and more conservative interpretations. If random-effects models are applied to subgroups, statistical inferences reflect variation in mean effects across subgroups. Crucially, fixed-effect and random-effect methods yield identical results only in the absence of heterogeneity [2].

Reanalysis outcomes

Reanalysis using random-effect models revealed no statistically significant differences between the high-dose MSC group and controls in 6-month WOMAC [MD = 8.75; 95% CI (−2.10, 19.61); P = 0.11] (Fig. 1A) or 12-month WOMAC [MD = 12.68; 95% CI (−4.96, 30.32); P = 0.16] (Fig. 1B), diverging from Cao et al.’s conclusions [1]. These findings reaffirm that fixed- and random-effect models generate concordant results in homogeneous subgroups (I2 = 0%). However, in subgroups with significant heterogeneity (I2 ≥ 75%) [2], random-effect models are methodologically preferable due to their conservative nature and alignment with clinical diversity.

Cell source stratification

Study design and model selection

Cao et al. further stratified the intervention group into ADMSCs and BM-MSCs subgroups. Fixed-effect models were again applied to analyze 6-month and 12-month WOMAC scores [1]. We argue that random-effect models are similarly warranted here, as the ADMSCs subgroup exhibited moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I2 = 58% at 6 months; I2 = 88% at 12 months). Fixed-effect models remain inappropriate in such contexts for the reasons previously outlined.

Reanalysis outcomes

Reanalysis with random-effect models showed that the ADMSCs subgroup had significant 6-month WOMAC improvement versus controls [MD = 9.32; 95% CI (3.73, 14.92); P = 0.001] (Supplement Fig. 1A), aligning with Cao et al.’s conclusion despite methodological differences [1]. However, the 12-month WOMAC difference became non-significant [MD = 12.90; 95% CI (−1.76, 27.55); P = 0.08] (Supplement Fig. 1B), contradicting their reported significance [1]. In contrast, the BM-MSCs subgroup (with negligible heterogeneity) yielded identical results to Cao et al.’s at both timepoints (Supplement Fig. 1A, B) [1].

This discrepancy underscores the critical influence of model selection on subgroup interpretations, particularly in heterogeneous populations.

Conclusion

Our reanalysis emphasizes that fixed-effect models risk overestimating precision in heterogeneous subgroups (e.g., high-dose or ADMSC groups), whereas random-effect models provide conservative, clinically realistic estimates.
