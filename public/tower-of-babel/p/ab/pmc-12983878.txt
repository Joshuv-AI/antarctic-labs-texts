Fluorination augments tumor engagement and antitumor immunity of a D-peptide-based radiotheranostic agent for combinatorial immune checkpoint blockade therapy
Authors: Siqi Zhang, Can Liu, Qingshuang Lu, Wenhao Liu, Jiang Wu, Jianjing Liu, Lina Tong, Kuo Zhao, Qichen Hu, Xueyao Chen, Linger Li, Xuekai Song, Feng Wang, Dong Dai, Rui Wang, Kuan Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProgrammed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-targeted radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) combined with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) represents a promising combinatorial treatment because of the “magic” bystander and abscopal effects of RPT. However, the overall efficacy of this combinatorial approach is often constrained by limited tumor retention and insufficient accumulation of existing radiopharmaceuticals. D-peptides, with their superior metabolic stability, offer a promising modality to overcome these limitations. We previously reported [68Ga]/[64Cu]DPA, which exhibited favorable in vivo stability. Nevertheless, its inadequate tumor retention hampers therapeutic efficacy and immune activation, highlighting the need for further optimization to achieve sustained target engagement.MethodsHerein, fluorine modification-directed rational design was used to optimize DPA’s target engagement and retention, thereby boosting its immune-activating potential in RPT. Following two rounds of screening, a series of fluorinated D-peptides was synthesized. Their in vivo performance was assessed by positron emission tomography (PET)/CT imaging in tumor-bearing mice and subsequently in patients with cancer. The therapeutic efficacy of the corresponding 177Lu-labeled fluorinated radiopharmaceuticals was further investigated to verify their potential in enhancing antitumor immunity.Results[68Ga]D3-6, which incorporates a pentafluorinated phenylalanine residue, exhibited optimal tumor uptake and prolonged retention, achieving 22.43±1.63%ID/g at 4 hours post-injection. A preliminary clinical study further validated its diagnostic efficacy across multiple cancers. Additionally, [177Lu]D3-6 boosted both local and systemic antitumor immunity and, in combination with anti-programmed cell death protein-1 therapy, elicited superior tumor inhibition.ConclusionThis study highlights the potential of fluorinated D-peptide radiopharmaceuticals as powerful PD-L1 targeting radiotheranostics, enabling more effective systemic antitumor efficacy in RPT and ICB combination therapy.

Background

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is overexpressed in many cancers and plays a critical role in suppressing immune surveillance.1 PD-L1-targeted radiotheranostics has emerged as an effective strategy for patient stratification, treatment monitoring, and cancer therapy.26 Increasing evidence suggests that radiopharmaceutical therapy (RPT) can convert “cold” tumors to “hot” tumors, thereby enhancing the antitumor immunity.710 Combining RPT with tumor immunotherapies (eg, immune checkpoint blockade, ICB) holds the potential to boost their therapeutic efficacy.11 12 Furthermore, RPT can stimulate the systemic immune responses, inducing inhibition of distant, non-irradiated tumors.1317 However, to achieve robust synergistic antitumor immune effects, there is an urgent need for radiopharmaceuticals with favorable in vivo stability, high tumor engagement, and prolonged retention, which remain largely undeveloped.

D-peptides, composed of D-enantiomeric amino acids, possess stereochemical configurations opposite to their L-counterparts, conferring high resistance to proteolytic degradation while maintaining comparable binding affinity for L-peptides.1821 We previously reported PD-L1-targeted D-peptide radioligands, [64Cu]/[68Ga]DPA, which demonstrated significantly improved in vivo stability compared with most L-peptide-based PD-L1 targeting radioligands, thereby enhancing tumor-to-background contrast and pharmacokinetics.22 23 A pilot first-in-human positron emission tomography (PET)/CT imaging study further confirmed the potential of D-peptides in radiopharmaceutical development.3 24 However, improving target engagement remains crucial to enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of DPA as an RPT vector.

Fluorinated amino acid substitution has emerged as an effective strategy for optimizing the physicochemical properties of peptides.25 Fluorinated residues mimic natural amino acids while stabilizing peptide and protein structures with minimal disruption to native conformations. Moreover, fluorination enhances in vivo stability and strengthens protein-ligand interactions.26 27 As strong hydrogen bond acceptors, fluorine atoms stabilize protein-peptide binding and prolong peptide circulation time. The unique electronic properties of fluorine, as the most electronegative element, further enhance cell permeability.26 28 29 In particular, even in 18F-labeled peptides, fluorine exerts profound effects on modulating physicochemical behavior, underscoring its critical value for optimizing radiopharmaceutical performance in vivo.

To improve the antitumor efficacy of RPT and ICB combination therapy, we aimed to develop more effective PD-L1 targeting radiopharmaceuticals with improved target engagement and prolonged retention. Building on [68Ga]DPA, we hypothesized that integrating the “magic” effect of the fluorine atom might optimize the performance of DPA by simultaneously improving targeting binding and tissue penetration. To achieve this, the D-alanine scanning of the DPA sequence was first performed to determine the critical residue for target engagement, followed by selective substitution with various fluorine-containing D-amino acids. Protein-level binding assay and cell-based experiments were then conducted to screen for the optimal sequences. PET/CT imaging in tumor-bearing mice identified that [68Ga]D3-6, incorporating a pentafluorinated phenylalanine, exhibited significantly improved tumor uptake and retention time. A subsequent first-in-human PET/CT study of [68Ga]D3-6 further validated its diagnostic efficacy across multiple cancers. Subsequent therapeutic experiments demonstrated that fluorination enhanced the ability of radiopharmaceuticals to activate both local and systemic antitumor immunity, as indicated by increased T-cell effector functions and antigen presentation not only in [177Lu]D3-6-treated tumors but also in systemic compartments such as spleens and draining lymph nodes. Notably, combining [177Lu]D3-6 with ICB therapy achieved superior cell-killing efficacy. Collectively, these findings highlight the essential roles of D-peptide and fluorination modifications in enhancing tumor retention and accumulation, thereby promoting both local and systemic antitumor immunity.

Materials and methods

Reagents and equipment

All reagents required for peptide synthesis were obtained from J&K Scientific (Beijing, China), Mackin (Shanghai, China), and Titan (Shanghai, China). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis was performed using a Biacore S200 (Cytiva, Uppsala, Sweden). PD-L1 protein (HY-P73361) was obtained from MCE (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA). Confocal microscopy was conducted using an integrated fluorescence microscopy imaging system (BZ-X810, Keyence, Osaka, Japan). Flow cytometry was carried out using Agilent NovoCyte Opteon Flow Cytometer Systems (Agilent, California, USA). The small animal imaging system was purchased from the Guangzhou Guangyi Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). Gallium-68 was obtained from Nanjing First Hospital (Nanjing, China) via a 68Ge/68Ga generator (ITM, Munich, Germany). PET/CT imaging was conducted in an Inveon microPET/CT scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany). Lutetium-177 [177LuCl3] with a radionuclidic purity of 98% was obtained from DC AMS Pharm (Beijing, China). The precursors of the radiopharmaceuticals were confirmed using liquid chromatograph mass spectrometer (Waters, Massachusetts, USA). Radioactive stability was detected using a radioactive high-performance liquid chromatography (radio-HPLC, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The 2480 Wizard auto γ-counter (PerkinElmer WIZARD2 2480, Massachusetts, USA) was applied to measure the radioactivity (counts per minute, CPM) for in vivo and in vitro evaluations.

The mouse primary antibody for PD-L1 (A19135), the rabbit primary antibody for β-tubulin (A12289), the rabbit primary antibody for GAPDH (A19056), the rabbit primary antibody for Ki67 (A20018), the rabbit primary antibody for interferon (IFN)-γ (A23929), anti-mouse secondary antibody (AS003), anti-rabbit secondary antibody (AS014) are purchased from Abclonal (Wuhan, China), the anti-CD45-FITC (11-0451-82), anti-CD4-APC (17-0042-81), anti-CD8-BV421 (404-0081-82), anti-PD-1-APC (17-9985-80), anti-IFN-γ-PE (12-7311-81), anti-CD62L-APC (17-0621-81), anti-TOX-PE (12-6502-80), anti-CD103-PE (12-1031-81), anti-MHC-II-APC (17-5321-81), anti-F4/80-eFluor 450 (48-4801-80), anti-CD206-PE (Thermo) antibodies were purchased from Thermo (Waltham, USA), and anti-CD11b-APC-CY7 (101226), anti-CD3-PerCP-cy5.5 (100218), anti-XCR1-BV421 (148216), anti-PD-L1-PerCP-cy5.5 (124334), anti-iNOS-PE (696805) were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, USA). The anti-mouse fluorescent-labeled secondary antibody (AB150113) and Hoechst staining dye solution (AB228551) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, England). The nivolumab (anti-programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)) (A2002) used in vivo was purchased from Selleck (Houston, USA).

Peptide synthesis and purification

All the peptides were synthesized using the Fmoc-based solid-phase synthesis strategy. Following the incorporation of the final amino acid into the peptide sequence, β-alanine was conjugated as a linker, connecting with fluorophore Sulfo-Cyanine5 Succinimidyl Ester (CY5) (145368-14-1, Beijing Tangchuan Sci-Tech, Beijing, China) or chelator 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane acid (DOTA) (BD290368, Bidepharm, Shanghai, China). All peptides were purified by HPLC, then lyophilized, and stored at −20℃.

Cell lines and animal study

The A375-PD-L1 (human melanoma) cell line overexpressing PD-L1 was kindly provided by Jiangsu Institute of Nuclear Medicine, NHC Key Laboratory of Nuclear Medicine. The 4T1 (mouse triple-negative breast cancer) cell line (CL-0007), the CT26 (mouse colorectal cancer) cell line (CL-0071), and MCF-7 (Michigan Cancer Foundation-7) (CL0149) cell line were obtained from Procell Life Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). TheA375-PD-L1 cell line, 4T1 cell line, and MCF-7 cell line were grown in high-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (Gibco, 11872093) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Vistech, SE100-011) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Gibco, 15240062) in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37℃. The CT26 cell line was grown in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640) medium with 10% FBS and 1% P/S in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37℃.

All animal studies were conducted following the principles and procedures outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Ministry of Health, China). The experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences and Peking Union Medical College (IMM-S-25–0079). BALB/c nude and BALB/c mice (6–8 weeks) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Beijing, China). The tumor-bearing mice were subjected to small-animal PET/CT imaging and indicated treatments when the tumor volume reached 100 mm3. In compliance with ethical guidelines, neither the tumor allografts nor xenografts that exceeded 2 cm in any dimension are used (as per IACUC protocol limits), and no mice exhibited severe abdominal distension (defined as a ≥10% acute increase in original body weight). To establish tumor-bearing mice, A375-PD-L1 cells (1×106 cells/tumor) and MCF-7 cells (1×106 cells/tumor) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of BALB/c nude mice. For MCF-7 tumor-bearing mice, estrogen was administered daily at a dose of 0.2 mL (0.15 mg/mL). For the 4T1 and CT26 tumor-bearing mice, cells were injected into the two sides of the back of BALB/c mice (1×106 cells/tumor).

Flow cytometry analysis

A375-PD-L1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at densities of 1×105 cells per well and cultured for at least 24 hours. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 10 µM of CY5-labeled peptides. After incubation for 2 hours, the cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) three times and dissociated into single cells using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25200056). Subsequently, flow cytometry was performed at an excitation wavelength of 650 nm to assess the uptake of the peptides by the A375-PD-L1 cells.

Small animal living fluorescence imaging

CY5-labeled peptides (final concentration of 100 µg/100 µL/per mouse) were intravenously injected into A375-PD-L1 tumor-bearing mice. Small animal imaging system performed imaging at 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 hours post-injection (p.i.). The fluorescence intensity of the tumors was outlined in the software, and the grayscale values of the tumor region were plotted using GraphPad Prism V.8.0.2.263 software.

Furthermore, to determine whether intratumorally administered drug physically disseminates from the injection site to distant tumors, we injected 100 µg of CY5-D3-6 into the primary tumor (right back) of 4T1 tumor-bearing mice. Fluorescence intensity in the injected tumor and distant tumor was quantified at 30, 60, and 120 min p.i. to assess spatial distribution.

Molecular docking and analysis of binding-pocket residues

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock V.1.5.7. The original structure of PD-L1 (PDB ID: 4ZQK) was prepared by removing waters and ions followed by constrained minimization. The active binding site of PD-L1 protein is x=2.722, y=49.971, and z=105.938. The initial conformations of the PD-L1 ligand were generated and optimized according to the MMFF94 force field. 10 docking poses of each ligand were generated, of which the best-scoring pose was selected. Modeled protein-ligand complexes from docking were loaded into Discovery Studio 2019 client software to generate two-dimensional ligand-residue interaction profiles and generate interactions between the protein and ligands.

Evaluation of cell permeability by confocal microscopy

A375-PD-L1 cells were cultured in confocal dishes (Cellvis, D35-14-1.5P) at a density of 1×105 cells/dish. The next day, the adherent cells were washed with PBS and incubated with CY5-DPA, CY5-D3, and CY5-D3-6 for 1, 4, 8, and 12 hours at 37°C. After being washed with PBS three times, 4’6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (1 µg/mL) solution was then added to stain the nuclei at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. The double-stained cells were observed and imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope (BZ-X810, Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Surface plasmon resonance assays

The PD-L1 protein (20 µg/mL in 200 µL NaAc pH=4.5) was covalently immobilized on COOH-sensor chips by the EDC/NHS chemistry. All peptides were diluted in SPR buffer (4-(2-Hydroxyethyl)piperazine-1-ethanesulfonic acid, HEPES) to different concentrations and injected into the chip from low to high concentrations. In each cycle, a 200 µL sample was flowed through the chip for 5–6 min at a constant flow rate of 20 µL/min. Finally, the affinity parameters of the binding reactions were calculated and analyzed by Trace Drawer software (Ridgeview Instruments AB, The Kingdom of Sweden).

Western blot

A375-PD-L1 and 4T1 cells or tumors were lysed with Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B, Shanghai, China). The lysate cell mixture was sonicated, centrifuged (4°C, 12,000 rpm, 20 min), and the supernatant was collected. BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, P0009, Shanghai, China) was used to measure the total protein concentration. Equal amounts of the protein (20 µg) were loaded on a 10% sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and electrophoresed at 90 V for 20 min, followed by 180 V for 60 min. The protein was then transferred to a methanol-activated polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane at 400 mA for 60 min at 4℃. After being blocked with 5% skim milk at RT for 3 hours, PVDF membranes were incubated with primary anti-PD-L1 antibody (1:2,000 in 5% skim milk) and anti-GAPDH antibody (1:50,000, in 5% skim milk) overnight in a shaker at 4℃. Anti-mouse horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled secondary antibody and anti-rabbit HRP-labeled secondary antibody (1:5,000, in Tris Buffered Saline with Tween-20, TBST) were then added and incubated at RT for 2 hours. The immunoblots were visualized by enhanced chemiluminescent substrates (32106, Thermo, Massachusetts, USA), and imaged using a chemiluminescence imager (Boygene, BG-gdsAUTO 730, Beijing, China).

Radiolabeling and stability evaluation

The precursor was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of the peptide in a mixture of Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and water (1:1, v:v) to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. After that, the solution (20 µL) was added to the sodium acetate solution (1 mL), and 5 mL of hydrochloric acid mixture with 68GaCl3 solution or 177LuCl3 solution were also added for radiolabeling. The mixture was incubated at 95℃ for 10 min, and then purified through a C18 column (WAT020515DL, Waters, Massachusetts, USA). Radio-HPLC was used to analyze the radiolabeling yield. To evaluate radiolabeling stability, all 68Ga-labeled peptides were mixed with 10% FBS and PBS buffer (pH=7.4) and incubated at 37℃ for 2 hours.

Small animal PET/CT imaging

The 68Ga-labeled peptides dissolved in 100 µL of saline were intravenously injected (3.7 MBq, 100 µCi, per mouse) into the A375-PD-L1 tumor-bearing mice (n=3). Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen for 10 min before each imaging. PET/CT imaging was conducted at 30, 60, 120, and 240 min p.i. The CT data from the PET/CT examination were reconstructed in the transverse plane as 0.1 mm thick sections. The following parameters were used for imaging: 80 kV, 100 mA, and 0.32 s per rotation. The uptake of radiopharmaceuticals in the tumor, heart, liver, and kidney was calculated in the unit of the percentage injected radioactivity dose per gram (%ID/g), which was performed on the Inveon Research Workplace software.

Ex vivo biodistribution study

Normal mice (n=3) and A375-PD-L1 tumor bearing mice (n=3) were intravenously injected with [68Ga]DPA and [68Ga]D3-6 (1.85 MBq, 50 µCi, per mouse) and then euthanized at 5, 30, 60, 120, and 240 min p.i. Blood and other major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach, large intestine, small intestine, pancreas, muscle, bone, brain, salivary gland, testicle, thyroid, bladder, adrenal, eye, esophagus, prostate, tear gland, and gallbladder) or tumors were quickly harvested in tubes and weighed. The 2480 Wizard auto γ-counter (PerkinElmer, WIZARD2 2480, Massachusetts, USA) was used to measure the radioactivity (CPM) of each sample, from which the corresponding uptake (%ID/g) was further calculated.

Safety evaluation of [68Ga]D3-6

A single-dose intravenous toxicity study of [68Ga]D3-6 was conducted in BALB/c mice as part of the preclinical safety assessment. Mice were randomly divided into a control (saline) group and a [68Ga]D3-6 treatment group, each comprising three males and three females. [68Ga]D3-6 was administered via tail vein injection at a single activity of 100 µCi per mouse. Body weight and behavioral observations were recorded on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15 p.i. At the experiment endpoint, blood samples were collected for biochemical analyses. Major organs (lungs, liver, brain, spleen, kidneys, and heart) were collected, fixed, paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E for systematic histopathological evaluation.

PET/CT imaging of [68Ga]DPA and [68Ga]D3-6 in patients

Five patients were enrolled in the study. A 56-year-old woman diagnosed with adenocarcinoma (40%), mainly characterized by complex glandular structures, and sarcomatoid carcinoma (60%), received 188.70 MBq (5.1 mCi) of [68Ga]D3-6 to detect the lymph node and other metastases. The patient received the treatment of osimertinib. Following osimertinib therapy, the PD-L1 Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) decreased to 10%. A 48-year-old woman with lung cancer (PD-L1 TPS 5%) and a 74-year-old woman with melanoma received 203.50 MBq (5.5 mCi) and 236.80 MBq (6.4 mCi) of [68Ga]D3-6, respectively. A 79-year-old man with stage IV esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (PD-L1 TPS 55%) received 234.95 MBq (6.35 mCi) of [68Ga]DPA. Additionally, a 73-year-old man with prostate cancer complicated by multiple lymph nodes, bone, and liver metastases, and additionally diagnosed with left renal clear cell carcinoma post-nephrectomy, was referred for radiopharmaceutical diagnostic evaluation. He received 260.11 MBq (7.03 mCi) of [68Ga]DPA to detect the lymph node and other metastases.

Whole-body PET/CT scans were performed 60 min p.i. Throughout the study and during a 24-hour follow-up period, no adverse events or clinically detectable pharmacologic effects were reported in any of the patients who received [68Ga]DPA or [68Ga]D3-6. Vital signs remained stable before and after injections.

Therapeutic efficacy evaluation

When the volume of both tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomly divided into six groups (G1 group: saline, G2 group: [177Lu]D3, G3 group: [177Lu]D3-6, G4 group: anti-PD-1 group, G5 group: [177Lu]D3+anti-PD-1, G6 group: [177Lu]D3−6+anti-PD-1, (n＞6). [177Lu]D3 and [177Lu]D3-6 (18.5 MBq, 500 µCi, 100 µL in saline) were intratumorally administered in the right back of mice, which were considered as primary tumors. The anti-PD-1 (200 µg per mouse) was intraperitoneally injected on day 1 and 4 p.i. Then, the growth of both sides of the tumors was monitored every second day. The tumor volume was calculated using the following equation: volume=(width2×length)/2. Mice were euthanized when the volume of tumors reached above 2,500 mm3. Survival of the tumor mice was monitored carefully every day.

On day 10 of the therapy experiment, mice were injected with [68Ga]D3-6 (3.7 MBq, 100 µCi in saline) to monitor the expression of PD-L1.

Ex vivo analysis of tumor immune-microenvironments

To investigate local and systemic antitumor immunity, primary and distant tumors, spleens, and draining lymph nodes from 4T1 and CT26 tumor-bearing mice (three mice in each group) were obtained at day 7 post-injection of radiopharmaceuticals and homogenized in PBS (pH 7.4) containing 1% FBS to generate a cell suspension. T cells were analyzed using three flow cytometry panels (panel 1: CD45+CD11b−CD3+CD4+CD8+; panel 2: CD45+CD11b−CD3+CD8+PD-1+IFN-γ+; panel 3: CD45+CD11b−CD3+CD8+CD62L+TOX+). Dendritic cells (DCs) were analyzed by the panel of CD103+XCR+CD11b+MHC-II+PD-L1+. Macrophage cells were analyzed by two panels (panel 1: CD45+CD11b+F4/80+iNOS+MHC-II+PD-L1; panel 2: CD45+CD11b+F4/80+CD206+). Additionally, tumor proteins from different groups were collected and analyzed by western blot to assess the expression of IFN-γ and PD-L1.

Evaluation the in vivo safety of RPT and combination therapy in tumor-bearing mice.

Primary and distant tumors, as well as organs including the liver, kidney, brain, heart, lung, pancreas, and spleen of six groups, were collected on day 13 post-injection of radiopharmaceuticals. All tissues were fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formalin for long-term preservation. Then the samples were embedded in paraffin and frozen sectioned at a thickness of approximately 10 µm. Sections were stained with Harris hematoxylin for 5 min, rinsed in running tap water, differentiated in 1% acid ethanol, and blued in 0.2% ammonia water. Subsequently, sections were counterstained with eosin for 2 min, dehydrated through graded alcohols, cleared in xylene, and mounted with resinous mounting medium. At the treatment endpoint, blood samples were collected for serum biochemical analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining of Ki67 and PD-L1 in tumors

Primary and distant tumors were dissected from the mice post-injection of radiopharmaceuticals. The tumor tissues were immediately fixed in 4% neutral-buffered formalin for long-term preservation. The tumor samples were then embedded in paraffin and frozen sectioned at a thickness of approximately 10 µm. Following blocking with 3% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) for 2 hours at RT, the sections were incubated with anti-PD-L1 (1:1,000) (day 10 samples) and anti-Ki67 (1:1,000) (day 13 samples) primary antibodies diluted in 3% BSA overnight at 4℃. AB Flo 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (1:100) (AS076, ABclonal) and AB Flo 647-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:100) (AS060) were added to the samples and incubated for 1 hour at RT, respectively, and finally incubated with DAPI for 10 min at RT. Images were taken on an integrated fluorescence microscopy imaging system (BZ-X810, Keyence, Osaka, Japan).

Statistical analysis

All results are expressed as means±SEM, as indicated in at least three independent experiments. The two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test was used when two groups were compared. The statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism V.8.0.2.263. Statistical significance is indicated as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, or ***p<0.001 or #p<0.05, ##p<0.01, or ###p<0.001. The symbol “*” represents differences compared with DPA or the control group; the symbol “#” represents differences in pairwise comparisons.

Results

D-alanine scanning of DPA to identify critical residues for tumor uptake

A375-PD-L1 cells and tumor-bearing mice with high PD-L1 expression were used for both in vitro and in vivo evaluations (figure 1A,B). PET/CT imaging revealed insufficient tumor uptake and retention time of [68Ga]DPA (figure 1C). To improve its in vivo performance, we aimed to optimize its binding capacity, hydrophobicity, and cell permeability. D-alanine scanning, which provides information on the contributions of each amino acid, was first performed to determine the critical residue for fluorinated modification. Each D-amino acid in DPA was individually replaced with D-alanine, generating a series of derivative peptides (D1–12) that conjugated with a β-alanine mini-linker and the chelator DOTA (figure 1D; online supplemental figures S1–13).

D-alanine scanning identifies key amino acids of DPA. (A–B) PD-L1 expression in A375-PD-L1 and 4T1 cells and tumors was evaluated through western blot assay (A) and flow cytometry analysis (B). (C) PET/CT imaging of [68Ga]DPA in A375-PD-L1 tumor-bearing mice at 30 and 60 min after tail vein injection of 3.7 MBq radiopharmaceuticals. Tumors were indicated by white dashed circles (n=3). (D) Illustration of D-alanine scanning of DPA peptide. (E) PET/CT imaging of [68Ga]D1-12 in A375-PD-L1 tumor-bearing mice at 30 min after tail vein injection of 3.7 MBq radiopharmaceuticals. Tumors were indicated by white dashed circles (n=3). (F–G) Tumor-to-heart (F) and tumor-to-liver (G) ratios of [68Ga]DPA and [68Ga]D1-12 based on PET/CT imaging. All results are expressed as means±SEM, as indicated in at least three independent experiments. The two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t-test was used when two groups were compared. The symbol “*” represents differences compared with the [68Ga]DPA; *p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001. PD-L1, programmed death-ligand 1; PET, positron emission tomography.

Substitution of D-tyrosine at residue 3 (D3) with D-alanine resulted in significantly enhanced binding affinity compared with DPA according to SPR (online supplemental table S1). PET/CT imaging in A375-PD-L1 tumor-bearing mice revealed that [68Ga]D3 displayed superior tumor-to-liver (T/L) and tumor-to-heart (T/H) ratios at both 30 and 60 min p.i. (figure 1E–G). [68Ga]D3 demonstrated increased tumor uptake and improved pharmacokinetics, as reflected by reduced accumulation in the heart and liver (online supplemental figures S14A–C). Notably, substitution of D-aspartic acid at residue 6 (D6) and D-threonine at residue 7 (D7) also showed improved tumor uptake, but was accompanied by high accumulation in normal organs (online supplemental figure S14A–C), likely due to non-specific binding or increased hydrophobicity. Additionally, D5 showed a significant loss of binding affinity, indicating that residue 5 (D-arginine) is essential for binding. The replacement of D-arginine with other D-amino acids or its analogue resulted in unfavorable binding capacity and PET/CT imaging profiles, which was therefore not considered for further modification (online supplemental table S1,figures S14–17).

Collectively, substitution of D-tyrosine at position 3 with D-alanine provided the most favorable balance between target engagement and pharmacokinetics, achieving satisfactory tumor bioavailability and minimal off-target organ accumulation. Therefore, D3 was selected for subsequent fluorinated D-amino acid substitutions.

Fluorinated D-amino acid substitutions improved the tumor uptake and retention time

We first identified the critical residue suitable for fluorinated D-amino acid substitution. Previous studies have shown that the non-polar residues, especially phenylalanine, play an important role in membrane anchoring and permeability.30 Our molecular docking simulations (online supplemental figure S15B) indicated that the aromatic ring of the C-terminal D-phenylalanine is positioned in a relatively exposed and flexible region of the peptide, which interacts with the PD-L1 binding interface. These suggest that residue 1 (D-phenylalanine) might be critical for physicochemical properties optimization. Substitution of D-phenylalanine with other non-polar or analogs in D3 showed lower affinity and tumor uptake (online supplemental table S1, figures S18–21). Therefore, D-phenylalanine was considered for subsequent fluorination.

D-phenylalanine was replaced with its derivatives containing varying fluorinated groups and numbers of fluorine atoms, generating peptides D3-1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 (figure 2A,B; online supplemental figures S22–27). Flow cytometry and confocal imaging demonstrated that D3-6, with pentafluorinated modification, showed enhanced cellular uptake compared with D3 (figure 2C–E). SPR results further confirmed that D3-6 exhibited a high binding affinity with PD-L1, with the KD of 0.051 nM (figure 2F). Additionally, molecular docking simulations revealed that the fluorinated D-phenylalanine in D3-6 formed additional hydrogen bonds with SER117, facilitating PD-L1 interaction. Notably, D3-6 formed the most hydrogen bonds and the most interactions with PD-L1 (online supplemental figure S15B).

These results suggest that the heart signal observed in PET/CT primarily reflects blood pool activity rather than myocardial accumulation, alleviating concerns regarding potential heart toxicity. Therefore, [68Ga]D3-6 demonstrated high binding affinity, enhanced membrane permeability, significantly improved tumor uptake and retention time, without inducing notable normal organ accumulation. Together, these properties ensured [68Ga]D3-6 of excellent tumor bioavailability without unexpected off-target toxicity.

PET/CT imaging in patients confirmed the diagnostic efficacy of [68Ga]D3-6 across various cancers

Based on the favorable preclinical findings, a preliminary clinical study was initiated to evaluate the diagnostic efficiency of [68Ga]D3-6 compared with [68Ga]DPA. Patient 1 was a 56-year-old woman diagnosed with biphasic lung cancer (approximately 40% adenocarcinoma and 60% sarcomatoid carcinoma, confirmed by morphology and immunohistochemistry; PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) of 95%), presenting with cervical and mediastinal lymph nodes, left adrenal gland, and multiple bone metastases, receiving osimertinib therapy. Following osimertinib therapy, her PD-L1 TPS decreased to 10% (online supplemental figure S35A). [68Ga]D3-6 PET/CT at 1 hour p.i. revealed intense focal uptake in an irregular tumor mass in the right lung apex mass (Standardized Uptake Value maximum, SUVmax=3.1) (figure 4A, upper panel). The mediastinal lymph node metastasis exhibited an SUVmax of 3.6 (figure 4A, middle panel), and the lumbar vertebral metastasis had an SUVmax of 2.4 (figure 4A, lower panel). Additional metastatic lesions with measurable uptake included: right supraclavicular region (SUVmax=2.4), left adrenal gland (SUVmax=1.6), left scapula (SUVmax=1.8), left ischium (SUVmax=1.8), right ilium (SUVmax=1.8), and sacrum (SUVmax=2.4). PET signals align with the reduced tumor PD-L1 expression after chemotherapy.32 These results suggest the potential of [68Ga]D3-6 for monitoring the prognosis of osimertinib therapy. Patient 2 was a 48-year-old woman with primary lung cancer and intrapulmonary as well as left iliac bone metastases. The primary lesions had a PD-L1 TPS of 5% (online supplemental figure S35B). [68Ga]D3-6 PET/CT showed an SUVmax of 1.6 for the primary tumor (figure 4B, upper panel) and clearly identified the left iliac bone metastasis (SUVmax=3.1) (figure 4B, lower panel). Patient 3 was a 74-year-old woman with a primary melanoma lesion on the right plantar region with no evidence of metastasis, and a PD-L1 TPS of 10% (online supplemental figure S35C). [68Ga]D3-6 PET/CT revealed an SUVmax of 1.6 for the primary lesion (figure 4C).

[177Lu]D3-6 combined with anti-PD-1 therapy achieved superior whole-body tumor inhibition

[177Lu]D3-6, with prolonged tumor retention, amplified the systemic antitumor immunity

To elucidate the immunomodulatory mechanisms underlying the combination therapy of [177Lu]D3-6 with anti-PD-1, we systematically analyzed the immune phenotypes in 4T1 and CT26 tumor-bearing mice. Following intratumoral administration of [177Lu]D3-6, significant local and systemic immune remodeling was observed, indicating by growth inhibition in both primary and distant tumors (figure 5). This systemic antitumor effect may result from immune activation at the primary tumor site, which subsequently triggered a systemic immune response.

We first investigated the immune remodeling within the TME of the primary tumor, analyzing T cells, DCs, and macrophages. Among T cells, the proportion of CD8+T cells (cytotoxic T lymphocytes, CTLs) increased significantly after RPT, whereas the proportion of CD4+T cells decreased. This trend was particularly pronounced in mice treated with [177Lu]D3-6 alone or combined with anti-PD-1 (figure 7A and D; online supplemental figure S39A). Given the potent antitumor efficacy observed, we propose that this shift is related to the clonal expansion of tumor-specific CD8+T cells. Their increased proportion within the T-cell pool led to a relative reduction in the proportion of CD4+T cells.3941 Further functional analysis of CTLs revealed upregulated expression of the effector molecule IFN-γ, indicating enhanced cytotoxic function after RPT (figure 7B and D; online supplemental figure S39A,S45A,B). Additionally, expression of exhaustion markers (thymocyte selection associated high mobility group box protein, TOX and PD-1) decreased significantly, while the expression of the memory marker CD62L increased (figure 7C,D; online supplemental figure S39A,S40A,C). These functional improvements were most pronounced in the [177Lu]D3-6 treated group compared with the [177Lu]D3 treated group and further enhanced by combination with anti-PD-1 therapy. [177Lu]D3-6 and its anti-PD-1 combination also exhibited consistent effect on DCs and macrophages. Regarding DCs, the proportions of both conventional dendritic cells (cDC1 and cDC2 subsets) increased following RPT, suggesting an overall enhancement in antigen-presenting capacity (figure 7E, F and H; online supplemental figure S39B). The cDC1 contributes to the activation of CD8+T cells,42 thereby further promoting the initiation of local antitumor immunity. For macrophages, the proportion of M1-like macrophages (iNOS+MHCII+PD-L1+) increased, while the proportion of M2-like macrophages (CD206+) decreased (figure 7G,H; online supplemental figure S39C,S40B,D,S45C–F and S46), indicating a shift in the tumor immune microenvironment (TME) toward an antitumor phenotype. In distant tumors, the trend in T-cell subset proportions (CD8+, CD4+) (figure 7I and L, and online supplemental figure S39D) and functional markers (IFN-γ, TOX, PD-1, CD62L) (figure 7I,L; online supplemental figure S39D and S41) was consistent with those in the primary tumor, with more evident in [177Lu]D3-6 compared with [177Lu]D3 and saline. Further enhancement was observed on combination with anti-PD-1. Together, these data underscored the essential role of [177Lu]D3-6 and its anti-PD-1 combination in activation of antitumor immunity in both primary and distant tumors.

To understand the initiation and propagation mechanisms of this systemic immunity activation, we further analyzed the tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs) and the spleen. The tdLNs serve as a key secondary lymphoid organ where tumor antigens first drain and are crucial for initiating antitumor immunity in primary tumor.43 In the tdLNs, the proportion of CD8+T cells (figure 8A and D; online supplemental figure S42A) and their expression of IFN-γ (figure 8B and D; online supplemental figure S42A) were significantly increased after RPT and combination therapy, while the expression of exhaustion markers (TOX and PD-1) declined, similar to those in tumors (figure 8C and D; online supplemental figure S42A,S43). CD62L expression did not increase significantly (online supplemental figure S43). Although the proportion of CD4+T cells showed a decreasing trend in the combination therapy group, the change was not statistically significant. Some studies reported that the high levels of IFN-γ produced during a potent antitumor response may affect the survival and expansion of CD4+T cells in secondary lymphoid organs, contributing to the maintenance of a relatively stable proportion.43 44 In the spleen, the overall trends for T cells, DCs, and macrophages were consistent with those observed in the tumors, indicating that the antitumor immunity of the spleen was also in an activated state (figure 8E–L; online supplemental figures S42,S44). Similarly, the proportion of CD4+T cells in the spleen showed no significant change. IFN-γ may also play a regulatory role in the spleen, working in concert with mechanisms in the tdLNs to maintain the relative homeostasis of T-cell subsets in systemic immune organs. Similar efficacy was also observed in the CT26-tumor bearing mice, further indicating the reproducibility of [177Lu]D36 and its anti-PD-1 combination in triggering systemic antitumor immunity for killing whole-body tumor lesions (online supplemental figures S47,S48).

Collectively, we propose a mechanistic circuit for the [177Lu]D3-6 and its anti-PD-1 combination: (1) [177Lu]D3-6 induces local tumor cell death, releasing antigen and damage signals (2); it reprograms the local myeloid compartment (enhancing DCs function, promoting M1 polarization) to foster antigen presentation and immune activation; (3) tumor-specific CD8+T cells are primed and expanded in tdLNs and the spleen, exhibiting enhanced function and reduced exhaustion; (4) concurrently, anti-PD-1 relieves inhibition on newly primed T cells in tdLNs and effector T cells in the TME, unleashing their full cytotoxic potential; and (5) systemically activated T cells circulate and attack distant lesions, generating a potent abscopal effect.

Discussion

D-peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals have shown promise for targeted cancer diagnosis and therapy, yet their clinical translation was limited by suboptimal tumor accumulation and retention. To solve these limitations, this study aimed to incorporate fluorinated D-amino acids into the D-peptide radioligands and investigate their effect on in vivo performance and antitumor immunity. The lead candidate, pentafluorinated phenylalanine substitutes (D3-6), represented enhanced cell membrane permeability and binding affinity. In preclinical PET/CT, [68Ga]D3-6 demonstrated a tumor uptake at least 10-fold higher than that of [68Ga]DPA, with tumor PET signals continuously increasing over 4 hours p.i., reaching more than 20%ID/g. In contrast, [68Ga]DPA showed few tumor uptakes observed at 1 hour p.i. These findings confirm that fluorinated D-amino acid substitution could serve as a potent strategy for enhancing the tumor uptake and retention of D-peptide-based radiopharmaceuticals. A preliminary clinical study further confirmed its diagnostic efficacy across multiple cancers. Recently reported potential radiotracer, [18F]AlF-PCP2, exhibited favorable tumor uptake in both tumor-bearing mice and patients with cancer (head-and-neck and glioma).45 46 [68Ga]D3-6 showed comparable binding affinity and tumor uptake in preclinical studies, with tumor radioactivity up to 15% at 120 min p.i. We also found that [68Ga]D3-6 exhibited lower liver and kidney accumulation within 240 min p.i., as indicated by the PET/CT imaging and ex vivo biodistribution studies, highlighting its potential for safety and efficient RPT. In patients, [68Ga]D3-6 successfully detected most of the tumor lesions, including primary and metastatic tumors, demonstrating its favorable binding capacity and specificity for tumor PD-L1. The modest tumor uptake may be attributed to its limited circulation time in the bloodstream, which hampers sufficient accumulation in tumors. Although improved tumor retention was observed in tumor-bearing mice for [68Ga]D3-6, the pentafluorinated phenylalanine substitutes are possibly not enough to maintain stability in the human body. In the future, increasing the number of fluorine atoms possesses an efficient strategy to further improve the in vivo circulation time in the bloodstream and enhance tumor uptake.

This study confirmed that the [177Lu]D3-6, incorporating a pentafluorinated phenylalanine residue, showed improved capacity for activating the antitumor immunity of both local and distant tumors compared with [177Lu]D3 without fluorinated modification, which was also confirmed by the robust whole-body tumor inhibition when combined [177Lu]D3-6 with anti-PD-1. Immunophenotyping results demonstrated that fluorinated D-peptides combined with anti-PD-1 showed enhanced antitumor immune activation effect. In both primary and distant tumors, this combination increased the proportion of M1-like macrophages, elevated CTLs (CD8+T cells) infiltration, and relatively reduced CD4+T cells proportion. CTLs displayed enhanced effector function, with upregulated IFN-γ and CD62L expression, alongside decreased exhaustion markers TOX and PD-1. In parallel, cDC1 showed elevated PD-L1 expression, accompanied by increased cDC2 numbers in primary tumors, indicating enhanced antigen-presenting capacity. Furthermore, the shift in macrophages from M2 to M1 phenotype was observed in the primary tumor, further suggesting the improved antitumor efficacy driven by a pro-inflammatory state transition. Systemic immune activation was also evident in the spleen and tdLNs, where similar shifts in CTL differentiation and myeloid cell profiles were observed. These data collectively confirm that [177Lu]D3-6 and its ICB combination strategy allow for robust tumor cell killing, persistent tumor antigen release, and improved antigen presentation, leading to antitumor immunity activation in the whole-body tumors, which was not evident in RPT without fluorination modification.

The inherent proteolytic stability of D-peptides, resulting from their mirror-image configuration, which evades recognition by natural proteases, provides a robust foundation for sustained immune stimulation.47 48 Regarding fluorinated modifications, they promote the in vivo performance of the D-peptide through three mechanisms. First, fluorinated amino acids endow the peptides with strong hydrophobicity,49 thereby facilitating cell membrane penetration,50 51 as confirmed by flow cytometry and confocal imaging. Enhanced cell permeability allows deeper infiltration into tumor lesions, allowing prolonged tumor retention and persistent cell killing. Second, fluorination stabilizes the peptides against proteolytic degradation, allowing the longer circulation time in the bloodstream, as shown by the ex vivo biodistribution of [68Ga]D3-6. Furthermore, [68Ga]D3-6 showed rapid clearance from normal organs, suggesting that high proteolytic stability ensures in vivo integrity, reducing the risk of off-target possibility of degraded peptides. Consequently, fluorinated D-amino acid substitution is a suitable strategy for simultaneously improving the tumor bioavailability and in vivo safety of radiopharmaceuticals. Third, fluorine tunes the electronic properties of peptide radioligands. Owing to its high electronegativity, fluorine induces C–F bonds with pronounced dipole moments and low polarizability, altering peptide hydrophobicity and amphiphilicity.52 This property enhances the interactions between peptides to proteins,53 as supported by the molecular docking simulation of D3-6.

Radiopharmaceuticals have revolutionized the cancer treatment concept by directly inducing DNA damage in tumor cells.54 However, adverse events are also observed in clinical practice due to the unexpected off-target effects, including myelosuppression, xerostomia, and xerophthalmia.55 In situ cancer vaccines demonstrate unique advantages, enabling the double strike against tumors by directly killing the primary tumors and inducing a systemic antitumor immunity.5658 Intratumoral [177Lu]D3-6 could achieve significant whole-body tumor inhibition with limited normal organ damage. Our findings demonstrate that [177Lu]D3-6 can function as a novel in situ cancer vaccine, achieving whole-body antitumor efficacy while minimizing toxicity to healthy organs. The therapeutic efficacy could be further improved by combining with ICB therapy.

Despite the benefits of fluorination, there are potential limitations, such as the risk of increased lipophilicity, which can promote hepatobiliary accumulation and lead to high background signal in the liver and intestines. This is a well-documented challenge in radiopharmaceutical development. In the case of [⁶⁸Ga]D3-6, we note that its calculated logP value (−0.75) indicates a carefully balanced hydrophobicity. Crucially, the in vivo biodistribution data demonstrate that [⁶⁸Ga]D3-6 did not exhibit significantly elevated liver accumulation compared with its predecessors and was primarily cleared renally. This suggests that for the D3-6 peptide, the degree of fluorination we introduced enhanced membrane permeability and tumor retention without pushing the molecule into a lipophilicity regime that would compromise its favorable pharmacokinetic profile through dominant hepatobiliary clearance. This underscores the importance of fine-tuning, rather than merely maximizing fluorination in molecular optimization.

In summary, this study demonstrated the potential of fluorinated D-amino acids in improving the tumor retention and accumulation, which subsequently facilitates the antitumor immunity of radiopharmaceuticals. [68Ga]D3-6 with pentafluorinated phenylalanine exhibited enhanced tumor accumulation and prolonged tumor retention, with tumor uptake of 22.43±1.63%ID/g 4 hours p.i., which also showed promise for patient cancer diagnosis. Furthermore, we also provided a combination therapy using fluorinated D-peptide radiopharmaceuticals and anti-PD-1 with higher capacity for local and systemic immune activation, enabling the robust whole-body antitumor efficacy. Thus, fluorinated D-amino acid substitution is a valuable strategy for the development of effective radiopharmaceuticals and ICB combined therapy, highlighting its essential role in broadening the clinical utility and radiotheranostic efficiency across multiple cancers.
