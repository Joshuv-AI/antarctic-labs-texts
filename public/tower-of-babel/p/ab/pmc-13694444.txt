Regimen-Related Oral Mucosal Injury: Pathobiology, Clinical Consequences and Rationale for Intervention Across Cancer Treatment Modalities
Authors: Stephen Sonis, Hannah Wardill, Alessandro Villa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of ReviewMucosal injury of the alimentary tract (mucositis) has been a consistent, markedly symptomatic, and clinically disruptive side effect of conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy and radiotherapy regimens since their inception. Despite its impact, mucositis remains a significant unmet clinical need as available treatment options are sparse. This review uses oral mucositis as a predicate regimen-related toxicity to provide a comparative discussion of the pathobiology, clinical features, and risk factors of regimen-related mucosal injury by therapy class.Recent FindingsThe alimentary tract, especially the mouth, has not escaped collateral damage from newer forms of cancer therapy including targeted agents, immunotherapy, and antibody-drug conjugates. A critical assessment of the field reveals persistent challenges that have hindered the development of effective, mechanistically-based treatments across both conventional and emerging therapy classes.SummaryRecognizing the consistent observation that regimen-related toxicities routinely occur in clusters, common pathobiological underpinnings are noted in support of unified, systemic mitigation strategies.

Introduction

In the two plus decades since cancer regimen-related mucosal injury (mucositis) was reviewed by Sonis (2004) [1], the number of oncologic treatment options has expanded dramatically. Added to the risk of classical mucositis initiated by cytotoxic therapies are new forms of mucosal damage associated with targeted therapies and immunotherapy [2]. Damage to normal oral mucosa (oral mucositis, OM) is among the most ubiquitous and historically recognized adverse events associated with non-surgical cancer therapy. In 1932, described the mouths of patients receiving radiation therapy for oral cancer: “The parts are reddened, oedematous, and swollen-inflammatory reaction” that tellingly “looks worse than at any time during the disease” [3]. The significance of mucositis as a dose-limiting toxicity was noted in the earliest iterations of chemotherapy as reported by Farber in a 1948 [4] description of treatment outcomes in patients receiving aminopterin for acute leukemia.

The symptomatic impact of OM is profound and cogently captured in a 1998 poem by Anita Hart Balter, a patient receiving chemotherapy and published in the New England Journal of Medicine [5]:

A fish hook lodges in my throat.

Spittle, kindergarten paste, thickens everything – even vision.

Mouth, pocked with sores & blisters, swollen ulcerated tongue.

Topside, sandpapered with number 7 coarsest grade.

Taste buds, saliva glands, seared.

Cool water, corrosive acid now.

The tongue rests; teeth become enemies.

Coiled steel razored wire atop dentate prison walls.

Only moans escape my lips. I cannot eat or speak.

Inside a howl festers.

Pain lengthens time.

Among the more than one million patients in the United States receiving drug or radiation therapy for oncologic indications annually, nearly 50% will develop OM. Severe OM (preventing consumption of solid foods) occurs in 20–40% of patients receiving conventional chemotherapy, 60–85% of patients receiving conditioning regimens prior to hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) and virtually every patient undergoing radiation therapy for head and neck cancer (HNC) [6, 7]. While mucositis is reported as highly frequent events, its true prevalence has been clouded by a lack of universally accepted severity grading scales, inconsistent reporting, and inaccurate diagnosis and coding, especially in real-world populations [8].

Historically, mucositis was irrevocably linked to cytotoxic forms of cancer therapy such as radiation and chemotherapy. It is now clear that mucosal injury, especially in the oral cavity, is an inescapable side effect of almost every form of non-surgical cancer therapy. Clinically significant mucositis is noted in 50% of patients treated with antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) which target antigens such as TROP2 that are common to both tumor and normal mucosa [9]. The painful, aphthous-like lesions associated with mTOR inhibitors (mTOR inhibitor-associated stomatitis; mIAS) occur in up to 55% of patients and are, if untreated, dose-limiting [10]. Likewise, mucositis also occurs with regularity in patients being treated with tyrosine kinase inhibitors [11, 12] and immune-related mucosal lesions are seen in up to 10% of patients receiving the most common forms of immunotherapy such as checkpoint inhibitors [13, 14].

OM has a significant impact on the clinical and fiscal burden of cancer treatment as well as affecting patients’ tolerance of optimum anti-cancer therapy. Radiation-associated mucositis in head and neck cancer patients is a frequent cause of emergency room visits and associated with unplanned hospitalization rates ranging from 17% to 36% [15, 16]. Anticipated or existing mucositis often results in the need for gastrostomy feeding tube placement [17].

Critically, mucositis undermines patients’ ability to tolerate optimum care as it is a cause for radiation treatment breaks or alterations in chemotherapy drug doses or schedules [18, 19]. The ramifications on clinical outcomes are profound. In the case of radiation therapy, even a single day unplanned break in treatment is associated with a 1.2% reduction in tumor control [20, 21].

The cumulative consequences of severe OM are well illustrated by patients having the condition because of aggressive conditioning regimens prior to HSCT [22]. Even small increases in mucositis severity confer the risk of an additional day of fever, a 2.1-fold increase in risk of significant infection, 2.7 additional days of TPN, 2.6 additional days of injectable narcotic therapy, 2.6 additional days in hospital, and a 3.9-fold increase in 100-day mortality risk (P < .01) [23].

For patients receiving myelosuppressive chemotherapy agents independent of HSCT protocols, mucositis catalyzes the risk of bacteremia leading to febrile neutropenia (FN) and sepsis as breaches in oral epithelial integrity provide a clear portal of entry for an expanded oral pathogen population [24]. Among patients hospitalized with leukemia, ulcerative OM was associated with an increased risk of febrile neutropenia (33.9% of patients with mucositis vs. 12.9% without mucositis); for patients with multiple myeloma, mucositis increased the risk of FN by 29.4%. Mortality among the same cohort was at least twice as likely in patients with mucositis [25].

The fiscal repercussions of severe OM are impressive with incremental costs ranging from $5,000 to $30,000 for patients being treated with radiotherapy for HNC, and $3,700 per chemotherapy treatment cycle [26]. In patients hospitalized following HSCT, the extended length of hospitalization resulted in incremental costs exceeding $80,000 [23]. Among hospitalized patients, the additional resource burden conferred by mucositis results in prolonged length of stays and dramatic incremental costs; total charges for inpatient stays for individuals treated for leukemia with mucositis were $269,514 (LOS 22.2 days) compared to $55, 492 (LOS 4.9 days) without the condition [25]. Although not specifically studied with newer forms of cancer therapy, there seems little doubt that the incremental costs associated with OM driven increases in healthcare resource use will markedly contribute to the overall cost of care.

Here, we provide a contemporary and comparative perspective on the various forms of regimen-related mucosal injury. We focus on OM as it is probably the most comprehensively studied form of regimen-related mucosal damage. Ultimately, we aim to increase the visibility of this persistent clinical challenge and catalyze focused research efforts to accelerate meaningful advances in patient care.

Mucositis Due to Cytotoxic Therapy

The most studied forms of mucositis are associated with cytotoxic therapy in the form of drugs (conventional chemotherapeutic agents [including antibody-drug conjugates in which the payload is a cytotoxic]) and radiation therapy. The clinical presentation of mucositis in these cases is similar and corresponds to the schedule and dose of the delivered agent.

Clinical Manifestations

The initial clinical changes associated with OM are visible on direct or indirect (endoscopic) examination and present as mucosal atrophy and erythema typically beginning within 4 to 5 days after drug infusion or at cumulative radiation doses of around 10 Gy (Fig. 1) [2]. At this early stage, symptoms are mild and consist of mucosal burning, similar to a food burn, easily managed with over-the-counter topical or systemic analgesics. By a week after drug infusion or cumulative radiation doses of 30 Gy to 40 Gy (assuming a daily 2 Gy dosing regimen), the integrity of the mucosa is breached, and frank ulceration develops usually of the non-keratinized mucosa (soft palate, buccal and alveolar mucosa, ventral and lateral tongue). Ulcers progress in both size and depth; confluence is not unusual, although ulcerative tissue often abuts normal mucosa. The ulcerative stage is symptomatically the most significant and is often associated with intractable pain requiring opioid analgesics. Ulcers are usually covered with a pseudomembrane consisting of fibrin and dead cells, frequently a breeding ground for pathogens. Self-resolution is the norm (day 21 after chemotherapy infusion and 4–6 weeks after the completion of radiation) with healing occurring from the wound edges and proceeding under the pseudomembrane. Chronic mucositis persists in a small percentage of cases [27].

Pathobiology

Initially attributed solely to direct epithelial cell injury, OM is now understood to result from a complex, dynamic process that includes interactions between epithelial, stromal, vascular and immune processes.

Despite differences in their trajectory, chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-associated mucositis share key mechanistic features: oxidative stress, cytokine amplification, microvascular injury and microbiome perturbation. At the outset, the direct DNA damage and strand breaks within the basal layers of epithelium are accompanied by the rapid generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [28] resulting in the activation of the innate immune response as injured cells release endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs/CRAMPs), and subsequently engage pattern-recognition receptors and trigger downstream signaling [29]. These early events activate transcription factors and pathways including NF-κB, p53, WNT, PI3K/AKT, MAPK, and nitrogen metabolism pathways, leading to the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, stress-response mediators, adhesion molecules, and apoptosis-related genes (stage 2). Signal amplification (stage 3) occurs through positive feedback loops (e.g., TNF-mediated NF-κB and JNK activation), intensifying tissue injury. Although these molecular events begin immediately after treatment, the timing of their clinical consequences varies depending on the nature of the effected epithelium. Ulcerated mucosa subsequently becomes a conduit for translocation of bacteria and a colonization platform where cell wall products further propagate inflammation via macrophage activation and cytokine release. Healing (stage 5) is driven by submucosal and extracellular matrix derived signals that stimulate epithelial migration, proliferation, and differentiation, ultimately restoring mucosal integrity [30].

Chemotherapy

Among patients being treated for the most common tumors (breast, colorectal, lymphoma), multiagent regimens confer the highest risk of OM typically is associated with dose and treatment cycle. Mucositis risk tends to be grea in the first cycle of treatment for regimens containing 5-fluorouracil (e.g. FOLFOX and FOLFIRI), anthracyclines and cyclophosphamide, especially when followed by docetaxel in dose dense regimens [31–33]. Whether bolus or continuous infusion with drugs such as 5-FU impact risk is unclear.

Mucositis is a common side effect of HSCT conditioning regimens and is directly associated with the intensity of the conditioning regimen, with severe OM observed in 46% of multiple myeloma patients treated with high dose melphalan and 42% of NHL patients treated with BEAM, with an odds ratio for OM of 9.27.

Radiation Therapy

OM is among the most established and consistent toxicities of radiation therapy to the head and neck [34]. Its severity is associated with the cumulative radiation dose, radiation field, daily fraction size, and radiation source. Among patients at highest risk of OM are those who are being treated for cancers of the mouth (oral cavity), nasopharynx, and oropharynx, although mucosal injury is also seen in patients being treated for cancers of the hypopharynx and larynx. While conventional treatment is delivered in daily 2 Gy fractions (5 days per week for a total cumulative dose of around 70 Gy), accelerated fractionation schemes have been explored because of superior tumor response but ultimately rejected because of the toxicity burden [35, 36]. Current standard of care dictates the use of concomitant chemotherapy, primarily cisplatin, with radiation. Whether either of the two most common cisplatin dosing schedules, weekly 40 mg/m2 or tri-weekly 100 mg/m2 is superior with respect to tumor response and toxicity risk has been hotly debated [37] with an unclear conclusion, although concomitant regimens yield a higher risk of mucositis compared to radiation alone. Substitution of other chemotherapeutic agents such as carboplatin or 5-FU for patients intolerant of cisplatin does not alter mucositis risk, nor does the use of concomitant targeted therapies such as EGFR inhibitors [38].

Patient-Related Factors

Mucositis risk is influenced by a range of factors that are independent of treatment intensity. To better determine, non-treatment risk predictors, studies have sought to establish risk prediction models in patients normalized by cancer diagnosis and treatment. Among factors associated with mucositis risk are sex, age, BMI, comorbidities, oral health, history of tobacco use and genomics [39, 40]. Noteworthy is the lack of consistency between studies, which seems likely to be due to variations in analysis, especially exclusion of multivariate analysis, inconsistent toxicity reporting and medical coding and the lack of a uniform assessment scale.

Results of several studies suggest that women are at higher toxicity risk than men. Using data from almost 24,000 patients enrolled in 202 clinical trials which included patients treated with immunotherapy, targeted therapies and standard chemotherapy regimens, noted that the risk of a severe toxicity was 34% higher in females vs. males [41]. Similar trends were reported in a murine OM model of fractionated dosing of x-radiation [42, 43] and in patients receiving aggressive conditioning regimens prior to autologous stem cell transplantation [43].

Completion of the Human Genome Project and cost efficiencies for genotyping technology catalyzed a surge of interest in understanding the contribution of genomics to mucositis risk and led to a proliferation of candidate gene and genome-wide association studies. While study results often identified genes or SNPs that were associated with biological pathways thought to be important in mucositis pathogenesis, their clinical utility was limited by a lack of replicability when applied to independent study populations [44]. This shortcoming could be attributed to the inconsistencies of study population demographics and treatment, AE reporting, and genomic assessment methods. In contrast, the use of genotyping to identify mutations associated with fluoropyrimidine metabolism has proven to be an effective predictor of severe toxicities, including mucositis [45].

However, it is likely that “biological” risk is the cumulative result of multiple “omic” interactions including metabolomics, epigenomics, and proteomics – a strategy which has proven feasible in other toxicity predictions such as cognitive impairment [46]. Optimistically, the ability to accumulate and analyze exceptionally large amounts of data using a range of AI tools could enable composite outputs which are associated with risk, but only if data inputs and mucositis outcomes are standardized.

Risk Prediction Models

The enthusiasm of translational applications of AI to oncology has prompted initiatives to develop risk prediction models for OM using a variety of approaches and study populations [47]. The power of the results of these studies, whether using case-control or cross-sectional designs, has largely been limited by sample size, consistency of toxicity assessment, and the reliance on retrospective data [48]. Consequently, findings for both chemotherapy- and radiotherapy-associated mucositis risk aligns with reasonable consistency on static factors such as age, sex, comorbidities, treatment variables and disease characteristics but fall short in their failure to include more dynamic “omics” data as a component of the inputs. However, as emerging data accumulates to assess the risk contribution of genomics, microbiomics, metabolomics and other “omic” layers opportunities for big data analytical methods which incorporate artificial intelligence methods (especially machine learning) continue to emerge [49].

The Role of the Microbiome in Mucositis Risk and Pathobiology

The possibility that the resident microbiome might impact the risk and trajectory of mucositis has been considered for more than five decades. Early failures of antimicrobial treatment or selective decontamination to mitigate mucositis reinforced the notion that mucositis was not a primary infectious process [50–52].

However, three lines of evidence now support a significant, nuanced role for the microbiome in influencing mucositis trajectory and consequences: (i) concurrent microbial changes that occur in parallel or following mucositis [53–55] (ii) efficacy of various microbiome-directed therapies, and (iii) modified gastroininal mucositis risk in murine models in which microbiota-to-host communication is blocked [56]. Of interest, preliminary data in patients being treated for cancers of the head and neck also suggests that differences in the resident microbiome prior to the start of cancer therapy impact mucosal toxicity risk [57, 58] and that shifts in the oral microbiota are associated with the OM trajectory during radiation [53].

Changes in the resident mucosal microbiota associated with mucositis are initially characterized by a loss of commensal microbes, leading to expansion and possible domination of bacteria with pathogenic potential [59]. However, definitive determination of whether the microbiome is an epiphenomenon to mucositis – i.e. an innocent bystander that changes in response to mucositis – or truly an active participant in its genesis and pathobiology has been hampered by the inability of translatable models to consistently replicate the highly dynamic and complex host-microbes interactions both within and beyond oncological settings [30, 60–62]. The observation that biological markers of OM were reduced in mice germ free treated with 5-fluorouracil provides additional weight to the impact of the microbiome on elements in the mucositis biological pathways [63]. The report describing accelerated mucosal repair following radiation in antibiotic treated rats [57] supports the notion that the microbiome is most influential in affecting the reparative phases of mucositis.

Management of Mucositis Due to Cytotoxic Therapy

Despite its legacy and prevalence, effective preventive or treatment options for OM are disappointingly limited and largely dependent on symptom control [64]. Treatment and prevention strategies which target nodes within the pathogenic scheme have met with marginal success. Benzydamine hydrochloride, a topical anti-inflammatory agent demonstrated efficacy in mitigating OM associated with head and neck radiation but was not effective when concomitant chemotherapy was used [65]. The successful application of palifermin, recombinant human keratinocyte growth factor (KGF) in preventing severe OM in patients receiving stomatotoxic conditioning regimens prior to autologous HSCT for the treatment of hematopoietic cancers was viewed as a breakthrough [66]. And palifermin’s mechanistic pleiotropism provided evidence supporting the complexity of mucositis pathogenesis [67]. Subsequent studies in patients with hematologic malignancies confirmed palifermin’s efficacy in that patient population [68], but the presence of KGF receptors on tumors of epithelial origin and the theoretical potential for palifermin to stimulate proliferation hampered its broader use.

The breadth of the biological effects of low-level laser therapy has stimulated interest in the utility of photobiomodulation (PBM) as an intervention for OM. Results of clinical trials have generally been supportive of its efficacy, particularly in OM prevention in adults undergoing HSCT and in patients receiving radiation for HNC [69]. However, like palifermin, PBM’s biological robustness, especially relative to its stimulation of proliferative signaling pathways could, negatively impact the response of tumors in some cases [70]. Consequently, caution has been suggested in using PBM in cases of HNC in which visible cancers of the mouth or oropharynx are present [71].

Studies with small molecules which interfere with levels of oxidative stress by supplementing superoxide dismutase [72], stimulating nrf-2-mediated transcription [73], and scavenging free radicals [74] suggest that such a strategy has merit. Attempts to pharmacologically interfere with activation of the innate immune response have been less consistent in their ability to mitigate OM [73, 75].

More technologically subtle is the application of oral cryotherapy in mitigating OM in patients receiving chemotherapy drug infusion. Whether applied with a device or simply ice chips [76, 77], the vasoconstriction resulting from cold-induced vasoconstriction of mucosal vessels results in lower tissue levels of mucotoxic drug. Successful demonstrations of efficacy have led to cryotherapy as an intervention in patients undergoing autologous HSCT with high-dose melphalan or receiving bolus 5-fluorouracil chemotherapy, with demonstrated efficacy in reducing severe OM (risk ratio 0.49, 95% CI 0.31–0.76) [78]. Alternatives to systemic opioids for pain management has led to the investigation of topical formulations of doxepin [79]. The National Comprehensive Cancer Network also suggest pregabalin (category 2B), gabapentin, or topical morphine 0.2% mouthwash for OM-associated pain [80, 81], ketamine [82], ibuprofen, and morphine [83]. The unmet need associated with OM and the breadth of potential biological targets has also stimulated the investigation of natural substances, especially those with anti-inflammatory activity, as possible interventions. The list of agents ed is broad and includes honey, glutamine, propolis, vitamin E, and zinc, among others [84].

Similarly, repurposed small molecules such as clonidine [85], melatonin [86], pentoxifylline [87], SAMITAL [75], recombinant human IL-11 [88], probiotics (Streptococcus salivarius K12 [89], and curcumin [90] have all been assessed, but require validation in larger trials.

Mucositis Associated with Antibody Drug Conjugates (ADCs)

ADCs provide a delivery platform in which cytotoxic agents integrated with tumor antigen targeted monoclonal antibodies optimize tumor-specific delivery of cytotoxic drugs while, in theory, sparing normal tissue damage [91]. However, the off-tumor expression of tumor-associated antigens such as TROP2 (e.g. Dato-DXd) on normal epithelium such as the oral mucosa has resulted in OM being a significant ADC-associated toxicity [92–95].

Clinically, ADC-associated OM replicates OM induced by other forms of cytotoxic chemotherapy and is characterized by painful ulcerations of the non-keratinized mucosa. Ulcers are irregular, often covered by a pseudomembrane, and have an average time to onset of 7–11 days after infusion [96] (Fig. 2). Incidence and severity vary across agents and are influenced by antigen expression patterns, linker chemistry and payload choice.

Ulceration of the right buccal mucosa consistent with antibody–drug conjugate–associated mucositis

Pathobiology

The pathogenesis of Dato-DXd associated mucositis appears to be consistent with the canonical paradigm of cytotoxic tissue injury. In this context, internalization of the topoisomerase I inhibitor payload (DXd) within normal oral epithelial cells is likely the dominant initiating event to precipitate mucositis development. The extent of mucosal injury may be amplified by a bystander effect, whereby released DXd diffuses beyond the initially bound cell and induces damage in adjacent, antigen-negative mucosal cells [97]. Additionally, antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC) may occur when Fc receptor-expressing effector cells-including macrophages, dendritic cells, and neutrophils, engage the Fc domain of surface-bound ADCs. Because Fc receptor-positive inflammatory infiltrates are predominantly observed during later stages of mucositis, particularly after ulceration has developed, ADCC plausibly worsens severity and delays resolution rather than initiating mucosal injury [98].

Risk Factors

While ADC payload is clearly associated with mucositis risk, so too are the patient-related risk factors shared which conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy regimens. Unique to ADC-associated mucositis is the likelihood that both the density of targeted antigen expression by normal cells and the levels of payload diffusion significantly contribute to risk (Table 1).

ADC Mucositis Management

Management of ADC mucositis is similar to that of other patients at risk for and suffering from mucositis caused by cytotoxic regimens. Routine symptom screening at each treatment cycle, prompt initiation of bland oral rinses, and dietary modification for mild disease is helpful. Progressive or severe toxicity necessitates escalation to intensive analgesia, nutritional support, and consideration of temporary treatment interruption or dose modification [9]. While the prophylactic use of topical steroid rinses has been suggested, the lack of mechanistic rationale and clinical evidence mandates additional study [96, 99].

Mucositis Due to Targeted Therapy and Immunotherapy

mTOR-Inhibitor Associated Stomatitis (mIAS)

In contrast to the relatively non-uniform presentation of cytotoxic therapy-induced OM, newer targeted agents produce distinct patterns of mucosal injury. Perhaps, the best described are lesions associated with inhibitors of the mTOR complex 1 (such as everolimus and temsirolimus; mTOR-inhibitor associated stomatitis (mIAS)) [100] which occur with an incidence rate of 25% for everolimus, 27% for temsirolimus, and 55% for ridaforolimus [101]. mIAS ulcers present as distinct, painful, well-defined, aphthous-like lesions of the movable oral mucosa typically 4–5 days after dosing. Lesions are characterized by a distinct border of perilesional erythema and a central gray, necrotic center, but no pseudomembrane (Fig. 3). While most common during the first cycle of treatment, the timing when lesions develop is unpredictable. Lesions self-resolve in about a week.

Aphthous-like ulceration of the left lateral tongue consistent with mTOR inhibitor–associated stomatitis

Pathobiology

While clinically indistinguishable from recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS), mIAS’ pathobiology is distinct. Mechanistically, mTOR inhibition disrupts epithelial homeostasis by suppressing proliferation, survival, and metabolic signaling in basal keratinocytes. Organotypic models of human oral mucosa demonstrate that mTOR inhibitor (everolimus) exposure induces ulceration in three stages: direct mTORi induced epithelial injury, induction of pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and activation of the innate immune response with subsequent recruitment of inflammatory cells [102] with consequent dose-dependent epithelial disorganization, reduced proliferative activity, increased apoptotic signaling, and early epithelial injury. These effects occur in the absence of microbial stimuli and are accompanied by a selective increase in IL-6. Apoptosis represents a key effector mechanism in mIAS but differs in both initiation and spatial distribution from that observed in recurrent aphthous stomatitis (RAS) or cytotoxic mucositis. The observation that mTOR inhibitors suppress pro-survival pathways and promote apoptosis at low drug concentrations is likely mediated, at least in part, through mTORC2 complex inhibition as it plays a critical role in cell survival signaling. When overlaid on the continuous turnover required to maintain oral mucosal integrity, apoptosis of basal and transit-amplifying cells results in epithelial renewal failure, predisposing to focal ulceration. Parallel mTOR inhibition induces autophagy, a tightly regulated catabolic process central to cellular stress responses. While autophagy may initially function as a protective mechanism, its sustained activation in the context of mTOR blockade may facilitate or amplify apoptotic pathways. The interplay between autophagy and apoptosis in mIAS appears context-dependent and may contribute to heterogeneity in lesion onset, severity, and duration [103].

Resident immune cells within the lamina propria may modulate early epithelial injury without driving a classical inflammatory cascade. mTOR inhibition has been shown to activate p38 MAPK signaling in macrophages, resulting in IL-6 release, which may influence epithelial stress responses and wound dynamics. Unlike RAS, where secondary necrosis and damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) signaling amplify innate immune responses in a “top-down” manner [104], mIAS appears to follow a “bottom-up” model, initiated by basal epithelial dysfunction.

Ulceration in mIAS thus reflects the convergence of impaired epithelial proliferation, apoptosis, and dysregulated stress responses, rather than cytokine-driven tissue destruction. Resolution occurs through restoration of epithelial renewal once mTOR-inhibitors exposure is reduced or interrupted, explaining the typically self-limited nature of lesions and the paradoxical reduction in incidence observed in some patients during subsequent treatment cycles.

Risk Factors

Data associated with the determination of mIAS are limited (Table 1). A 2016 meta-analysis of 1455 patients receiving everolimus across solid tumor trials, noted that lower BMI (≤ 25 kg/m²), younger age (< 65 years), and absence of diabetes history were associated with increased risk and earlier onset of mIAS, though these associations may be confounded by indication and other factors [105].

mIAS Management

Topical steroid therapy (e.g., dexamethasone elixir) used prophylactically is currently the standard of care [106] although severe cases may require systemic steroids. Alternatively, directed application of steroid gels or creams such as clobetasol or intralesional injections of triamcinolone may be used to treat established lesions [107].

Oral Toxicity Induced by Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (TKIs)

Anti-EGFR monoclonal antibodies (panitumumab, cetuximab) and VEGFR inhibitors (sorafenib, sunitinib, pazopanib, cabozantinib) cause mucosal burning and OM. Among the most common TKIs are those that inhibit EGFR, VEGF/VEGFR or multi-kinase pathways and all may cause oral mucosal damage manifesting as erythema, oral dysesthesia, cheilitis and ulcerations [11]. The frequency of oral involvement varies by agent from about 20% with axitinib and sorafenib to 35% with sunitinib and cabozantinib [12]. Notably, in almost all cases the mucositis severity has been reported as mild to moderate [108].

Pathobiology

EGFR blockade interferes with keratinocyte proliferation, migration and differentiation, which explains the frequent co-occurrence of oral mucosal changes and acneiform rash and the finding that oral manifestations form part of a broader epithelial toxicity pattern that includes cutaneous eruptions, paronychia and hand-foot skin reaction [109–111]. VEGF pathway inhibition compromises angiogenesis and wound healing, predisposing to delayed epithelial repair and ulceration. In contrast to the non-specificity of cytotoxic drugs, the underlying mechanism is primarily on-target modulation of signalling in normal epithelia resulting in a clinical presentation that tends to be patchy and erosive rather than uniformly ulcerative.

Risk Factors and Management

Patient-related factors such as age, performance status, and baseline oral health have not been consistently associated with TKI-related mucosal injury across studies, likely reflecting the heterogeneity of both agents and patient populations studied [2, 112].

Topical corticosteroids are first-line therapy, with intralesional steroids considered for localized ulcers recalcitrant to topical treatment. Supportive measures include dietary modifications, good oral hygiene, and topical anesthetics for pain management [12]. Management is largely empirical and symptom-directed with GABA analogues (gabapentin, pregabalin) or benzodiazepines (topical or systemic) [113].

When severe, oral lesions can be challenging to manage and may lead to dose interruptions or reductions, potentially compromising disease control in patients with advanced malignancies for whom TKIs may represent the primary or only effective systemic option [114, 115].

Immunotherapy-Associated Mucosal Pathology

OM secondary to ICTs reflects dysregulated host immunity rather than direct cytotoxicity. Consequently, the relatively small percentage of patients who develop ICIT-associated oral lesions (about 7%-10%) have a concomitant elevated risk for non-GI, skin, joint, thyroid and neurological adverse events [116]. The oral lesions of ICI toxicity replicate the most common oral immune-mediated diseases such as oral lichen planus, mucous membrane pemphigoid, erythema multiforme and Stevens-Johnson syndrome [116] (Fig. 4), which manifest as painful vesiculobullous or lichenoid lesions with concurrent erythema, blistering, and ulceration anywhere in the mouth or oropharynx and typically develop within the first three months of ICI therapy, with 50.9% occurring within 3 months and 85.5% within 12 months [13].

Management of Immune Related Mucosal Adverse Events

Management focuses on immunomodulation rather than dose adjustment alone. Local measures include high-potency topical corticosteroids, intralesional injections and topical calcineurin inhibitors. For moderate to severe disease, systemic corticosteroids and, in refractory cases, steroid-sparing agents such as mycophenolate, methotrexate, rituximab or other biologics are used. ICI therapy maybe be discontinued for patients unable to eat or with severe oral pain [117].

The Mechanistic and Clinical Relationship Between Oral Mucositis and Other Mucosal and Non-Mucosal Toxicities

While accumulating evidence over the past three decades has validated the core understandings of mucositis pathobiology, it is now clear that regimen-related toxicities rarely occur as isolated, tissue-specific events. As evidenced by the observation that patients develop toxicity clusters, many toxicities, even when clinically distinct, arise from shared mechanisms, including activation of conserved apoptosis-enabling and inflammatory pathways such as p53 signaling, mitochondrial priming and caspase activation, which occur across multiple tissues simultaneously.

Radiation dermatitis and mucositis exemplify this shared biology. Although they present in different organs, both reflect cytotoxic injury to epithelial stem cells, subsequent barrier breakdown, DAMP signalling, cytokine release, microbiota disruption and secondary immune activation [118, 119]. This same pathobiological framework also applies to non-epithelial tissue injury, with direct cell death and impaired hippocampal neurogenesis detected acutely after chemotherapy, followed by secondary neuroinflammation [120] and cognitive impairment [121]. Systemic symptoms arising from focal treatment further support this interconnected biology. For example, patients receiving localized breast irradiation frequently develop fatigue, despite the radiation field being relatively small and anatomically distant, with fatigue severity correlating with elevations in soluble IL-6 [122].

Whilst these represent only select examples, they collectively demonstrate that toxicities are not independent entities but organ-specific manifestations of overlapping pathological processes. Studies of symptom and toxicity clusters [123] have attempted to move beyond a reductionist view by describing the co-occurrence of multiple symptoms in the same patient; yet these clusters have largely remained confined within traditional organ systems (e.g., “gastrointestinal cluster”), missing the opportunity to ask why disparate symptoms emerge together and which systemic mechanisms link them.

The dynamic and sequential nature of some symptom clusters suggests that certain toxicities may act as upstream drivers of others, rather than simply co-occurring due to parallel local injury. Among regimen-related toxicities, mucositis stands out as a likely catalyst – initiating local and systemic inflammatory cascades capable of shaping downstream toxicities across the body. Breakdown of the mucosa triggers profound inflammatory responses, creating self-perpetuating feedback loops that amplify local injury and, once a threshold is crossed, radiate outward to influence distal organs. This is driven in part by exposure of basolateral immune-surveillance receptors such as TLR4 and NOD-like receptors, which are not only exposed due to mucositis but also overstimulated by the dysbiotic microbiome. A dysregulated mucosal-microbiota-immune interaction drives cytokine release and immune-cell recruitment, exacerbating mucosal injury and delaying repair to a point that the sheer extent of mucosal injury overwhelms innate immune capacity, enabling microbial translocation and significantly increasing the risk of bloodstream infection/bacteraemia.

Febrile mucositis highlights the induction of fever as a sequela to mucosal inflammation (and not infection) [24] – a condition which is mimicked by the cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [124]. Characterized by surges in IL-6, IFN-γ and IL-1, CRS destabilizes endothelial integrity and disrupts the blood-brain barrier (BBB), exposing the brain to systemic inflammatory mediators that drive neuroinflammation, oedema and neurological toxicity [125]. A similar framework may apply to conventional chemotherapy, where elevations in S100β (a marker of BBB disruption) and IL-6, together with reductions in the neurotropic factor, BDNF, accompany perceived cognitive decline [126]. Comparable inflammatory mechanisms have also been implicated in other regimen-related toxicities including cardiovascular dysfunction [127], fatigue [128] and neuropathic pain [129]. While each toxicity may begin with direct cellular injury, their severity and persistence are strongly shaped by systemic inflammatory biology. These observations support a paradigm in which mucositis may function as “toxicity zero” - the upstream insult that initiates systemic inflammatory and immunological cascades capable of driving multiple secondary toxicities across the body. Importantly, this framework underscores the need for system-level management strategies that prevent “the first domino from falling” rather than managing each downstream toxicity in isolation.

Conclusion

Mucositis, an iconic toxicity of conventional cytotoxic forms of cancer therapy, is also a common side effect of evolving forms of non-surgical treatment which affects millions of cancer patients worldwide. Cumulatively, the number of patients suffering from mucositis continues to expand, adding to symptom burden and negatively impacting the physiological and fiscal burden of treatment. While the traditional notion that regimen-related damage to the oral mucosa, and epithelium in general, was solely the consequence of direct cell injury has been advanced to more a comprehensive biological thesis, such progress has not translated into the development of an effective, mechanistically-based intervention. Hopefully, the significance of the clinical need, the viability of the commercial opportunity, and the potential for systemic, multi-target interventions will stimulate interest in the development of potential treatments.
