Nomogram model to predict the probability of spontaneous abortion in human assisted reproductive technology
Authors: Fei Li, Xiaoyan Duan, Mingming Li, Xing Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpontaneous abortion is a prevalent complication in pregnancies achieved through assisted reproductive technology (ART), posing significant risks to both maternal and fetal health. Despite the importance of preventing this outcome, no accurate predictive model for spontaneous abortion currently exists.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from 15,723 patients who achieved pregnancy via in vitro fertilization (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) between December 2015 and February 2021. Patients were stratified into abortion and non-abortion groups based on pregnancy outcomes. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors, which were subsequently incorporated into a nomogram for predicting spontaneous abortion risk in ART patients.ResultsAmong 15,723 patients who conceived through ART, 2,336 (14.86%) experienced spontaneous abortion, while 13,387 (85.14%) did not. Multivariate logistic regression identified female age (OR 1.050; 95% CI 1.029–1.061; P < 0.001), male age (OR 1.102; 95% CI 1.086–1.115; P < 0.001), follicle-stimulating hormone level (OR 1.044; 95% CI 1.015–1.069; P < 0.001), and anti-Müllerian hormone level (OR 0.891; 95% CI 0.862–0.925; P < 0.001) as independent risk factors for spontaneous abortion. The nomogram demonstrated good predictive performance, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.746 (95% CI 0.707–0.784) in the derivation cohort and 0.718 (95% CI 0.699–0.737) in the validation cohort.ConclusionFemale age, male age, follicle-stimulating hormone level, and anti-Müllerian hormone level are independent predictors of spontaneous abortion in ART-conceived pregnancies. The nomogram model provides an effective and intuitive clinical tool for individualized risk assessment.

Background

Spontaneous abortion, defined as the loss of an intrauterine pregnancy before 20 weeks of gestation, is the most prevalent adverse pregnancy outcome encountered in clinical practice [1, 2]. This condition imposes substantial psychological and physical burdens on patients, particularly those undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) for infertility [3]. Despite extensive research, the etiology of spontaneous abortion remains complex and incompletely understood [1, 4]. Given that prevention is paramount to management, identifying key risk factors and developing accurate predictive models for miscarriage is essential. Such models would enable clinicians to stratify patients by risk, predict individualized miscarriage probability, and provide evidence-based, personalized counseling to optimize patient care.

Since the advent of ART in 1978, an increasing number of women have achieved conception, however, ART does not eliminate the risk of subsequent pregnancy loss [5]. The incidence of first-trimester pregnancy loss following fresh embryo transfer has been reported to be 14.9% [6]. Numerous studies have investigated the risk factors for miscarriage in the context of ART. For instance, Shuai et al. identified maternal age, number of embryos transferred, body mass index, and frozen-thawed embryo transfer as independent risk factors for early spontaneous abortion in ART cycles [3]. Hahn et al. demonstrated that advanced maternal age adversely affects oocyte quality and endometrial receptivity, thereby contributing to pregnancy loss [7]. Similarly, Du Fossé et al. reported that advanced paternal age is associated with an increased risk of spontaneous abortion [8]. Furthermore, certain ART procedures themselves may compromise embryo quality and alter endometrial receptivity, both of which are recognized risk factors for miscarriage [9]. Despite these insights, large-sample, systematic investigations that integrate multiple risk factors into a comprehensive predictive model for spontaneous abortion in ART patients remain scarce.

Given the significant burden of spontaneous abortion in infertile patients, this retrospective cohort study analyzed clinical data from 15,723 women who successfully conceived via ART at the First People’s Hospital of Shangqiu and the Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University between December 2015 and February 2021. We developed a nomogram model to investigate the factors influencing spontaneous abortion in ART patients. This nomogram offers a graphical representation of each variable, enabling users to easily compute predicted probabilities. It represents an intuitive new clinical tool [10]. To date, no logistic regression-based nomogram has been specifically developed to predict spontaneous abortion risk in this context. The establishment of such a model would equip clinicians with a valuable tool to improve risk stratification and provide evidence-based guidance to optimize patient care. Of note, our group previously published a threshold-effect analysis of factors associated with spontaneous abortion in ART [11]. However, that study did not provide a practical, patient-level risk prediction tool, and it considered only female factors while overlooking male factors. The present study extends that work by developing a nomogram based on a more recent and homogeneous dataset, focusing on clinical utility and providing a practical tool for individualized risk prediction.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Shangqiu (Approval No. SQSY/202003256). Given its retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Shangqiu. All procedures adhered to the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study analyzed data from 15,723 women who achieved clinical pregnancy following IVF/ICSI between December 2015 and February 2021. All patients underwent a single fresh embryo transfer cycle following controlled ovarian stimulation. Participants were stratified into two groups based on pregnancy outcome: the spontaneous abortion group (n = 2,336) and the ongoing pregnancy group (n = 13,387). Patient characteristics, including female age (range 20–48 years; mean 31.2 ± 5.8 years), infertility duration (range 0.5–21 years; mean 3.8 ± 2.9 years), basal follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH; range 0.3–33.7 IU/L; mean 7.2 ± 2.2 IU/L), basal luteinizing hormone (LH; range 0.2–25.9 IU/L; mean 5.6 ± 3.8 IU/L), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH; range 1.2–23.1 ng/mL; mean 3.5 ± 2.3 ng/mL), were collected.

We compared clinical and laboratory parameters between patients who experienced spontaneous abortion and those who did not, aiming to identify statistically significant differences. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were subsequently performed to determine independent risk factors. Based on these factors, a nomogram was constructed to predict the risk of spontaneous abortion in ART patients. To validate the model’s predictive performance, bootstrapping with 1,000 resamples was employed. Patients were uniquely identified by their medical record numbers and randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 3,086) and a validation cohort (n = 12,637).

Ovarian stimulation protocols

Ovarian stimulation was performed using either a gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist (GnRH-a) protocol or the GnRH antagonist protocol. For patients undergoing the GnRH-a protocol, a single dose of 3.75 mg long-acting GnRH agonist (Diphereline, Ipsen, France) was administered on days 2–3 of the menstrual cycle. Ovarian response was monitored by transvaginal ultrasound and serum hormone level measurements. The initial gonadotropin dose was determined based on the patient’s age, antral follicle count (AFC), body mass index (BMI), and previous ovarian response to stimulation. Subsequent dose adjustments were made according to individual follicular development.

For patients undergoing the GnRH antagonist protocol, ovarian stimulation was initiated with 112.5–300 IU recombinant FSH (rFSH; Puregon, Organon, The Netherlands) on days 2–3 of the menstrual cycle. The rFSH dosage was adjusted based on ovarian response, follicle size, and serum hormone levels. A daily dose of 0.25–0.5 mg GnRH antagonist (Cetrotide, Pierre Fabre, Aquitaine Pharm International) was initiated on day 6 of rFSH stimulation or when the leading follicle reached a mean diameter of 14 mm, and continued until the day of trigger. For both protocols, final oocyte maturation was triggered when at least one follicle reached a mean diameter of 20 mm, or when 60% of follicles measured ≥ 16 mm. Trigger was administered using 2000 IU urinary human chorionic gonadotropin (u-hCG; Livzon Pharmaceuticals, China) combined with 250 µg recombinant hCG (r-hCG; Merck Serono). Transvaginal ultrasound-guided oocyte retrieval was performed 36–37 h after trigger. Luteal phase support was initiated after oocyte retrieval and continued until pregnancy assessment.

Outcome definition

Spontaneous abortion was defined as the loss of an intrauterine pregnancy confirmed by ultrasound before 20 weeks of gestation, consistent with established criteria [12].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 3.6.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables, including female age, male age, basal FSH, basal LH, basal estradiol (E2), basal progesterone (P), AMH, and prolactin, were tested for normality and homogeneity of variance (Bartlett’s test). Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using Student’s t-test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. To identify independent risk factors for spontaneous abortion, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables with P < 0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. Odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

According to the results of multivariate Logistic regression analysis and the regression coefficients of relevant variables, the corresponding nomogram prediction model was established by R language software, and the prediction performance of the nomogram model was verified by Bootstrap repeated sampling for 1000 times. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine the optimal critical value, corresponding sensitivity and specificity of nomogram model prediction. The area under the ROC curve was 0.50–0.70 for low accuracy, 0.71–0.90 for medium accuracy, and higher than 0.90 for high accuracy. P < 0.05 indicates statistical significance.

Based on the four independent factors identified by multivariate analysis, a nomogram for predicting the risk of spontaneous abortion was constructed (Fig. 1). The predictive model was represented by the following equation: Logit(p) = -7.48028 + 0.04316 × (female age) + 0.10784 × (male age) + 0.00526 × (basal FSH) − 0.13877 × (AMH). The study cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort (n = 3,086) and a validation cohort (n = 12,637) at a ratio of approximately 1:4 using a random number generator in SPSS 22.0. No significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the two cohorts (Table 3).

Calibration plots for both the training and validation cohorts showed good agreement between the predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, indicating satisfactory model calibration (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Assisted reproductive technology (ART) is widely employed in the management of infertility, enabling an increasing number of patients to achieve conception [13]. However, ART does not mitigate the risk of spontaneous abortion. In fact, prevention and proactive management of spontaneous abortion are of greater clinical significance than treatment alone [14, 15]. Therefore, accurate assessment and early identification of risk factors for spontaneous abortion are essential to enable timely clinical evaluation. Our study explored the risk factors of spontaneous abortion in patients receiving ART, univariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that female age, male age, basic FSH, and AMH were independent risk factors for spontaneous abortion. The objective of this study was to construct a nomogram model using large-scale clinical data to identify abortion-related risk factors. By quantifying and visualizing the results of logistic regression analysis, the model enables the calculation of variable contributions through graphical representation, thereby allowing prediction of abortion risk in ART patients. The nomogram provides an intuitive presentation of predicted outcomes, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.746 (95% CI 0.707–0.784), indicating favorable predictive accuracy and specificity. Unlike conventional clinical models in assisted reproduction, this nomogram offers enhanced practicality and intuitive interpretation, allowing clinicians to readily estimate the probability of abortion during ART treatment cycles based on individual patient characteristics.

Previous studies have suggested that ART does not increase the risk of spontaneous abortion, with reported abortion rates of approximately 14.7% among ART-conceived pregnancies [16, 17]. The risk of spontaneous abortion was 10.1% among women aged 20–29 years, compared with 39.3% among those older than 43 years, and 13.1% among pregnancies conceived using donor oocytes [18]. These findings are consistent with a large Chinese study conducted in 2017, which reported a 14.9% incidence of early pregnancy loss among ART patients undergoing fresh embryo transfer [19]. Additionally, the incidence of early spontaneous abortion following ART has been estimated at approximately 10.6% [20]. Regardless of the precise figures, these rates represent a substantial clinical concern, particularly considering the significant time and financial investment associated with ART. Nevertheless, current evidence indicates that the underlying causes of miscarriage remain incompletely understood [21, 22]. Furthermore, no predictive model currently exists that integrates individual patient parameters and laboratory data to guide clinical management. For assisted reproduction specialists, minimizing the risk of spontaneous abortion is of paramount importance. In this context, we identified independent risk factors and developed an accurate prediction model for estimating miscarriage probability.

Female age emerged as an independent predictor of spontaneous abortion, demonstrating a positive correlation wherein advanced age was associated with increased abortion risk. It is well established that both the quality and quantity of oocytes decline with advancing age [23, 24], accompanied by decreased cytoplasmic ATP content and an increased proportion of embryos with chromosomal structural abnormalities [25]. The incidence of spontaneous abortion among ART patients aged over 35 years is significantly higher than that observed in women under 35 [26]. Moreover, advancing age is associated with diminished endometrial receptivity and abnormal uterine blood flow, both of which contribute to an increased risk of pregnancy loss [27]. Similarly, our study identified advanced male age as an independent risk factor for spontaneous abortion in ART patients. A meta-analysis encompassing 975 original studies demonstrated a significant association between advanced paternal age and spontaneous abortion, with pooled risk estimates of 1.04 (95% CI 0.90–1.21) for ages 30–34, 1.15 (95% CI 0.92–1.43) for ages 35–39, 1.23 (95% CI 1.06–1.43) for ages 40–44, and 1.43 (95% CI 1.13–1.81) for ages ≥ 45 years [8]. Another study involving naturally conceived pregnancies reported a 1.26-fold increased risk of spontaneous abortion in men over 35 years of age, an effect that was independent of alcohol consumption [28]. Despite differences in study populations, these findings are consistent with our own observations. Kleinhaus et al. reported that the risk of spontaneous abortion in males over 40 years of age was 1.6 times higher, independent of female age and other confounding factors [29]. Furthermore, Frattarelli et al. demonstrated that the incidence of spontaneous abortion increased with paternal age, with rates of 41.5% among men over 50 years compared to 24.4% among those under 50 [30]. Advancing age is associated with multiple factors that impair DNA replication and repair mechanisms, increasing the likelihood of DNA fragmentation and subsequently contributing to pregnancy loss [31, 32]. The observed relationship between male age and spontaneous abortion risk underscores the importance of incorporating genetic counseling for older men undergoing ART.

Beyond the effects of parental age, our study identified basal FSH and AMH as significant contributors to spontaneous abortion risk in ART. In assisted reproduction, diminished ovarian reserve is characterized by elevated basal FSH levels and reduced AMH concentrations [33, 34]. Ovarian reserve and endometrial receptivity are critical determinants of successful embryo implantation and subsequent development [35], consequently, fluctuations in these hormonal markers may predispose patients to spontaneous abortion. Additionally, poor ovarian reserve often necessitates the use of higher doses of exogenous gonadotropins during ovarian stimulation, which may adversely affect the endocrine milieu and oocyte quality [36, 37]. Based on these research results and the Nomogram model, we can predict the probability of spontaneous abortions based on the results of laboratory examinations and the individual parameters of infertile patients in ART.

Our research group previously published a study that examined threshold effects of female age, male age, FSH, and AMH on spontaneous abortion risk. That study focused on identifying nonlinear relationships and cut-off points using smooth curve fitting and two-piecewise linear regression, but it did not offer an individualized risk prediction tool. Moreover, it lacked male laboratory measurements, relying solely on female factors does not fully capture the true risk of spontaneous abortion. In contrast, the current study has developed a nomogram grounded in logistic regression analysis. This nomogram is capable of generating a probability of spontaneous abortion that is specific to each patient, thereby offering direct applicability to clinical decision-making processes.

This study has several strengths, including its novel nomogram approach for predicting spontaneous abortion risk in ART patients, large sample size, and robust analytical methodology. Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design inherently limits the ability to exclude all potential biases, and selection bias may influence the results in unforeseen ways. Pregnancy is a dynamic and prolonged process during which numerous factors may influence outcomes, including environmental exposures, adverse obstetric history, and unhealthy lifestyle behaviors—all of which are recognized risk factors for spontaneous abortion. Additionally, unpredictable factors affecting pregnancy outcomes could not be fully accounted for, representing an inherent limitation of this study. Furthermore, while the nomogram model demonstrates utility in predicting spontaneous abortion probability and provides valuable reference information for clinicians and patients, no predictive model is without imperfection. Future research incorporating larger prospective cohorts and more comprehensive data collection methodologies will be essential to further refine and enhance the predictive accuracy of nomogram-based approaches.

In conclusion, the nomogram prediction model developed in this study effectively estimates the probability of spontaneous abortion risk in the context of ART treatment. Compared with traditional logistic regression models, the nomogram offers distinct advantages in terms of simplicity, intuitive interpretation, and practical applicability [38, 39], rendering it a valuable tool with significant potential for clinical implementation.
