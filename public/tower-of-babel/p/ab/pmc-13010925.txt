A deep-learning-based early warning system for abnormal eye conditions in chickens
Authors: Yu-Chieh Chen, Jen-Hung Huang, Hsiu-Yun Hu, Ying-Chieh Chen, Ming-Wen Wu, Ming-Kun Hsieh, Yao-Chuan Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn current poultry production practice, farmers are required to frequently enter their poultry houses and visually inspect their chickens to assess flock health. This practice is not only time-consuming and labor-intensive but also increases the risk of introducing contaminants into poultry houses and increasing disease transmission. In Taiwan, a typical poultry house houses approximately 15,000–20,000 red-feathered native chickens. Routine inspections are performed three times daily, and each inspection requires approximately one hour to complete. In the present study, a deep-learning-based early warning system was developed for detecting abnormal eye conditions in chickens. A pan–tilt–zoom camera was used to capture images from different areas of a poultry house and automatically detect and quantify the proportion of abnormal eye conditions. After these images were annotated into two categories, namely normal and abnormal, data augmentation techniques were applied to expand the training dataset. A You Only Look Once v7 deep learning model was then used to train the system, achieving a precision of 0.944, a recall of 0.831, and an F1 score of 0.884. The trained model was subsequently deployed in an enclosed commercial poultry farm to monitor three production cycles of red-feathered native chickens. Time-lag analysis demonstrated that the strongest associations occurred at lag periods of 3–7 days, with the highest correlation (ρ = 0.7304) observed at a 5-day lag in the July–August cycle. These findings indicate that increases in abnormal eye conditions may precede mortality by several days, supporting their role as an early warning indicator of flock health deterioration. A fixed-grid threshold scan identified an optimal abnormal eye proportion of approximately 4.5%, which achieved high sensitivity with acceptable false positive rates and demonstrated cross-cycle consistency. Overall, the proposed early warning system can serve as a digital chicken health monitoring tool that enables timely alerts and interventions, thereby reducing labor burden and potential economic losses.Keywords: Chicken eye, Deep learning, Pan–tilt–zoom camera, Digital health management

Introduction

In recent years, consumer demand for red-feathered native chickens has increased substantially in Taiwan. Red-feathered native chickens typically have a longer production cycle of approximately 70–80 days than commercial broilers, resulting in prolonged cumulative exposure to environmental pathogens under commercial rearing conditions. In addition, compared with highly standardized broilers, red-feathered native chickens are subject to less intensive selective breeding, leading to greater phenotypic variability and less uniform external morphology. These biological and management characteristics may increase health monitoring complexity and disease susceptibility in commercial production systems. However, poultry diseases continue to negatively affect the supply of chickens, leading to substantial economic losses. Disease outbreaks in chickens typically result from multiple interacting factors. For example, fighting among chickens may result in lacerations and open wounds, which in turn increase the risk of infection (Wakenell, 2016). Environmental conditions also play a crucial role in chicken growth and health. Inadequate control of gases and dust in poultry houses can result in severe respiratory disorders (Shankar, 2008). Odors and dust levels also vary with housing systems and seasonal conditions, and residual dust may indirectly contribute to the incidence of disease in chickens (Szablewski et al., 2022). Respiratory diseases are often associated with clinical signs such as nasal discharge, tearing, and coughing and may also be accompanied by ocular wetness and facial swelling (Priyanka et al., 2019). In the case of infectious laryngotracheitis, chickens may exhibit severe ocular and nasal discharges and diphtheritic lesions throughout the trachea (Rojs et al., 2021). Research into the relative efficiency of different transmission routes for infectious laryngotracheitis, including direct contact, airborne transmission, and dust-borne transmission, has revealed that direct contact is the fastest route (Yegoraw et al., 2021). Avibacterium paragallinarum can cause infectious coryza, in which facial swelling may occur within 48 h after infection and mortality may occur within 7 days (Saravanan et al., 2023). If this symptom is not properly managed, it may compromise flock health and facilitate rapid pathogen dissemination, thereby adversely affecting growth performance and egg production.

Currently, poultry health assessments largely depend on farm workers entering poultry houses to visually inspect chickens and make experience-informed judgments, which makes precise and quantitative disease monitoring difficult. With advancements in sensing technologies, more objective and quantifiable monitoring approaches have been developed and adopted to monitor poultry health. For example, Raj and Jayanthi (2018) used a system based on the Internet of Things (IoT) to identify diseased chickens at an early stage. Cameras have also been deployed in poultry houses for data collection, analysis, and processing (Aziz et al., 2020). Multiple sensors can be used for poultry monitoring, including environmental sensors, acoustic sensors, optical sensors, and motion sensors (Wu et al., 2022). Related monitoring and tracking technologies include image processing, computer vision, and acoustic analysis (Verma et al., 2023). More recently, artificial intelligence, cloud computing, and edge computing have been integrated for early disease detection (Goyal et al., 2024). Carpentier et al. (2019) employed an acoustic approach based on the detection of sneezing sounds to monitor respiratory problems in chickens. Cuan et al. (2022) combined sound feature extraction with deep learning to detect Newcastle disease by extracting audio features from poultry vocalizations for early outbreak detection. Guo et al. (2020) used computer vision technology to monitor the spatial distribution of chickens in experimental areas, such as drinking, feeding, and resting areas. Bakar et al. (2023) relied on image-based extraction of comb chromaticity to identify infected chickens and mitigate the risk of disease outbreaks in poultry houses. Despite these attempts, certain disease-associated clinical signs associated with disease remain insufficiently addressed in existing detection systems. For example, comb status, ocular abnormalities, and feather changes have been suggested as early warning indicators of poultry diseases (He et al., 2022).Nevertheless, the aforementioned diverse approaches to poultry disease detection can enhance poultry health surveillance and welfare management.

Health monitoring is a key research topic in modern poultry farming, with relevant studies particularly focusing on automated disease and health condition detection through computer vision, image processing, and deep learning. Intelligent monitoring not only improves production efficiency but also enhances animal welfare and supports the sustainable development of the poultry industry. Mehdizadeh et al. (2015) used images to identify biomechanical variables related to broiler feeding behavior. Wang et al. (2019) used the Faster Region-based Convolutional Neural Network model to automatically detect abnormal feces and prevent the spread of disease in chickens. Chen et al. (2021) localized different body regions and conducted deep feature analysis to identify disease-infected chickens. Čakić et al. (2022) utilized camera systems combined with IoT edge devices to count chickens and detect dead chickens. Li et al. (2021) used a multi-scale attention-based deep convolutional neural network for poultry image segmentation and analysis. Yang et al. (2023) used algorithms based on U-Net and pix2pixHD to detect chickens in cages. With the advancement of object detection technologies, relevant applications have expanded from simple single-object tasks to more diverse and complex activities, such as classifying pigeons on the basis of local contour edge features and sex-related characteristics (Chen et al., 2022) and extracting facial features to classify the age of yellow-feathered chickens (Jia et al., 2022). Data augmentation strategies such as MixUp, Cutout, and CutMix have also been used with deep learning models to capture facial images of chickens of different ages (Ren et al., 2022). To enhance monitoring capabilities in poultry farms, facial sex recognition has been integrated into poultry house inspection robots (Wu et al., 2023).

You Only Look Once (YOLO) is a deep learning model that is widely used in poultry research. Since the first YOLO model introduced in 2016 (Redmon et al., 2016), multiple enhanced versions have been developed. YOLOv4 has been used to identify dead chickens and detect chicken faces in poultry houses (Liu et al., 2021; Ma et al., 2022). YOLOv5 has been applied to track multiple chickens and evaluate their walking trajectories and directions for behavioral monitoring (Neethirajan, 2022). Autoencoders combined with YOLOv6 have been employed to detect and classify poultry diseases (Nguyen et al., 2023). YOLOv7, introduced in 2022, has higher computational efficiency and detection accuracy compared with the previous YOLO versions (Wang et al., 2022). Enhanced algorithms built on YOLOv7 have been reported to accurately monitor small waterfowl targets (Lei et al., 2023). YOLOv5, YOLOv7, and YOLOv8 have been trained using datasets consisting of visible and thermal images to detect pathological signs in broilers (Elmessery et al., 2023). A cyclic consistent migration neural network has been used to remove cage-door mesh patterns from images, and YOLOv8 has been used to detect the locations of combs and eyes (Yu et al., 2024). Overall, various poultry studies have focused on head and facial detection for health monitoring, with comb and ocular information being particularly informative for evaluating health status.

With ongoing technological advancements, poultry farms have gradually introduced environmental sensors and automated environmental control systems to enhance indoor environmental management and production performance. Despite these changes, farm personnel are still required to enter poultry houses on a daily basis to identify abnormal signs, including those related to body size, vigor, and external features (e.g., combs, eyes, feathers, and legs); perform culling or removal; administer medication; and adjust husbandry procedures. Frequent poultry house entry increases the risk of disease introduction and transmission, and manual inspection imposes a substantial labor burden. In Taiwan, a typical poultry house covers approximately 1,800–2,000 m² and accommodates 15,000–20,000 red-feathered native chickens. Routine inspections are conducted three times daily, with each inspection lasting approximately one hour. Moreover, experience-informed judgments are inherently subjective and may lack accuracy. In large-scale flocks and facilities, comprehensively and accurately capturing flock health conditions is challenging, which increases the risk of missed detection. Therefore, developing a system for monitoring poultry health is of utmost importance.

In this study, a deep-learning-based early warning system was developed to detect abnormal eye conditions in chickens (Fig. 1). A pan–tilt–zoom (PTZ) camera was installed in a commercial poultry house and configured with multiple pan and tilt angles and zoom levels to capture images reflecting ocular conditions. These images were uploaded to a cloud server for annotation, and data augmentation was then conducted to train a deep learning model. After model training, the proposed system was able to perform chicken eye detection, automatically capture images along a predefined acquisition route, and then upload these images to the cloud for daily health analysis. The results were then visualized on a poultry early warning platform. In case the proportion of abnormal eye conditions exceeded a user-defined threshold, the system alerted farm personnel to initiate appropriate interventions, such as removing diseased chickens and administering medication. “Early warning” is defined as the ability of the system to provide a measurable lead time before increases in mortality occur. Specifically, early warning refers to the detection of abnormal eye proportion elevations that precede mortality spikes by several days, as quantified through time-lag association analysis.

Materials and methods

Controllable PTZ camera system

A PTZ camera (GK5341C-X30-PE, GLOBAL-KING; New Taipei City, Taiwan) was installed in a commercial poultry house to collect images. This camera has a resolution of 2,592 × 1,944 pixels, a pan range of 360°, and a tilt range of 0° to 90° and is capable of 30× optical zoom. Fig. 2 displays a functional illustration of this camera. The camera can be controlled rapidly and accurately by adjusting its pan and tilt angles, thereby enabling image capture from different positions. In contrast to fixed cameras, the aforementioned camera’s viewing angle and focal length can be dynamically adjusted to cover a wider breeding area. This flexibility facilitates image acquisition in commercial poultry houses and allows for the monitoring of flock health conditions across different zones, thus mitigating the need for farmers to frequently enter poultry houses.

Functional illustration of the PTZ camera: (a) pan, (b) tilt, and (c) zoom.

To determine suitable acquisition configurations for capturing chicken eye images, multiple shooting angles and zoom settings were used. The camera was configured to capture images from multiple shooting angles along a predefined acquisition route, with automated image capture performed every 3 h.

A total of 12 pan angles, 6 tilt angles, and 6 zoom levels were considered in this study (Table 1). Pan angles were sampled every 30° to cover different areas in the poultry house while avoiding large obstacles such as feed bins, fences, and pillars. In general, tilt angle and zoom level directly affect the field of view. Smaller tilt angles require higher zoom levels, whereas larger tilt angles require lower zoom levels. In the present study, the proposed model was used to identify suitable shooting angles and zoom levels. Six tilt angles were evaluated in combination with six zoom levels. For each parameter combination, 100 images were randomly sampled from the image database for a single production cycle to estimate the average number of detectable chicken eyes. In addition, 10 images containing visible chicken eyes were selected for manual annotation and testing, and the detection performance achieved under each parameter setting was then assessed.

Definition of abnormal eye conditions and dataset construction

Red-feathered native chickens were the subjects of this study. Variations in feather appearance, comb morphology, and head proportions observed in the images reflect natural biological differences related to sex and age, as well as environmental lighting conditions, rather than differences in breed. Changes in ocular appearance were divided into two categories: normal and abnormal. Typically, healthy chickens exhibit bright, clear eyes without secretions or swelling and with a naturally dry appearance. By contrast, unhealthy chickens may exhibit redness, swelling, suppuration, and increased secretions, such as abnormal fluid discharge, purulent discharge, or crust formation. Representative examples of different chicken eye conditions are depicted in Fig. 3. When ocular or nasal secretions are present, bedding materials, dust, and feathers may adhere to the affected area, potentially aggravating wounds or triggering secondary bacterial infections. The classification criteria and annotation guidelines shown in Fig. 3 have been jointly established by animal science experts and veterinarians. Three annotators participated in the labeling process. Images were cross-checked for consistency after annotation. When discrepancies in eye-condition labels were identified, the corresponding images were excluded from the final dataset to ensure annotation consistency.

Example images of different chicken eye conditions.

Open-source annotation software LabelImg was used to annotate chicken eyes from the captured images. An example is depicted in Fig. 4. Before annotation, the images were manually screened, and those with blur, overexposure, or backlighting were excluded. The dataset covered the full growth cycle of native chickens, with a total of 1,000 images annotated. Table 2 presents the dataset distribution, revealing a training to validation to test split ratio of 8:1:1. The dataset was partitioned to ensure balanced representation across production cycles and camera shooting angles. Images captured from different time points and acquisition settings were distributed across subsets without overlap, and no duplicated images were shared among the training, validation, and test datasets.

Chicken eye detection model

YOLO was selected as the chicken eye detection model. YOLO is a deep-learning-based object detection algorithm that performs detection in a single stage. YOLO first divides an input image into fixed grids and then predicts object classes and bounding box locations within each grid cell, enabling object detection as a unified task. Because of its single-stage architecture, YOLO is relatively simple and computationally efficient and can directly analyze full images, making it suitable for the real-time monitoring of ocular changes in poultry houses.

After the annotation of normal and abnormal eyes, a YOLOv7 model was trained using open-source code. The batch size was set to 16, and the number of epochs was set to 600, with the remaining training hyperparameters set to default values. Because YOLOv7 requires a fixed input size (a default size of 640 × 640 pixels), the original camera images (2,592 × 1,944 pixels) were resized to 640 × 640 pixels to reduce memory usage and accelerate the training process. Model training was performed using two NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti GPUs.

During model training, the Intersection-over-Union (IoU) threshold was set to 0.5. This threshold is used to determine whether the predicted bounding box sufficiently overlaps with the ground-truth box. The adopted model also outputs a confidence score in the range of 0 to 1, with a higher score indicating greater confidence. During testing, increasing the confidence threshold can improve precision but may increase the frequency of missed detection, whereas reducing the threshold can increase the frequency of true detection but may also increase the frequency of false detection. After model training, a confusion matrix was used to evaluate detection performance for normal and abnormal eyes. For example, when the model predicts an abnormal eye and the ground truth is also abnormal, it the case is counted as a true positive (TP). When the model predicts an abnormal eye but the ground truth is not abnormal, it the case is counted as a false positive (FP). When an abnormal eye exists in the image but is not detected, it the case is counted as a false negative (FN). In object detection tasks, true negative (TN) are typically not emphasized. Field experiment setup and analysis

Field experiments were conducted on a commercial poultry farm that is equipped with an enclosed negative pressure ventilation poultry house and is located in Xianxi, Changhua County, Taiwan. The poultry house has evaporative cooling pads, with red-feathered native chickens raised on the floor. The floor area of the poultry house is approximately 80.0 m × 23.0 m × 4.5 m and is divided into a rooster zone and hen zone. Each production cycle lasts for nearly 3 months, with approximately 18,000 chickens produced per cycle. To prevent excessive crowding, which may lead to stress, fighting, and health problems, fences are installed to create subzones, thereby controlling the stocking density and chicken count in each zone. The farm owner records daily chicken mortality counts.

Network infrastructure and a PTZ camera were installed in the poultry house (Fig. 5) to monitor the chickens’ ocular health. The camera was mounted on a beam, approximately 1.6 m above the floor, by using L-shaped angle steel. Fig. 6 depicts the floor plan of the camera installation site. After the camera was configured with 40 position parameters, automated capture was performed on a daily basis. A total of 40 images were captured at 09:00, 12:00, 15:00, and 18:00 every day, with 160 images obtained per day. These images were then uploaded to the laboratory cloud server and used for chicken eye detection and to calculate the proportion of chickens with abnormal eye conditions. Such an integrated AI–network–cloud pipeline reflects a cross-layer system architecture that encompasses model performance, computational efficiency, and operational considerations (He, 2025).

Operational threshold determination for early warning

To establish a practical and data-driven alert threshold for deployment, a retrospective threshold optimization analysis was conducted. Mortality spikes were defined as days on which the daily mortality count exceeded the cycle-specific mean plus one sample standard deviation. This definition was adopted to identify abnormal increases in mortality relative to baseline fluctuations within each production cycle. An early warning was considered successful if an alert triggered at day t was followed by a mortality spike within the subsequent 5 days (i.e., within a 5-day lead window), based on the time-lag association results observed in this study. The abnormal eye proportion threshold was systematically scanned from 1% to 10% at increments of 0.5%. For each candidate threshold, daily alerts were generated when the abnormal eye proportion exceeded the specified threshold. The alert performance was evaluated by calculating TP, FP, FN, and TN, defined relative to the occurrence of mortality spikes within the subsequent 5-day window. Specifically, a TP was defined as a day on which the abnormal eye proportion exceeded the candidate threshold and a mortality spike occurred within the following 5 days. A FP was defined as a day on which the abnormal eye proportion exceeded the threshold but no mortality spike occurred within the subsequent 5 days. A FN was defined as a day on which the abnormal eye proportion did not exceed the threshold, yet a mortality spike occurred within the following 5 days. A TN was defined as a day on which the abnormal eye proportion did not exceed the threshold and no mortality spike occurred within the subsequent 5 days.

To identify the optimal operational threshold, the Youden’s index was used as the selection criterion. Results and discussion

Effect of data augmentation on detection performance

In chickens, abnormal eye conditions are relatively challenging to detect across different ages, resulting in a substantially larger number of normal eye annotations compared with abnormal eye annotations and thus an imbalanced dataset. In cases of class imbalance, model training may become biased toward the majority class, leading to low performance on the minority class. Therefore, in this study, data augmentation was applied to the original training dataset, and the trained model’s performance before and after augmentation was compared.

Overall, the sensitivity of chicken eye detection is influenced by the confidence threshold. A detection result is considered valid only when the confidence score exceeds a predefined threshold. In the present study, F1score curves obtained before and after data augmentation were compared. Before augmentation, the F1score sharply decreased when the confidence threshold exceeded approximately 0.8, with the highest F1score (0.78) achieved at a threshold of 0.502 [Fig. 7(a)]. After augmentation, the F1score sharply decreased when the confidence threshold exceeded approximately 0.9, with the highest F1score (0.88) achieved at a threshold of 0.811 [Fig. 7(b)]. Table 4 presents a detailed comparison of the test results obtained with and without data augmentation. After data augmentation, the testing Precision increased from 0.840 to 0.944, the testing Recall increased from 0.736 to 0.831, and the testing F1score increased from 0.785 to 0.884. These results indicate that the data augmentation process improved detection performance.

The examples cover diverse scenarios, including varying chicken densities, lighting conditions, and viewing angles. The model generally demonstrated reliable localization and classification of both normal and abnormal eye conditions. However, occasional failure cases were observed. Missed detections occurred primarily when the eye region was partially occluded, when chickens occupied a small portion of the image, or under motion blur conditions. In some instances, abnormal eyes were misclassified as normal, particularly when pathological features were subtle. Detection performance tended to improve when chickens occupied a larger proportion of the image, whereas smaller apparent target sizes increased the likelihood of missed detections. These observations suggest that future improvements may include multi-scale training strategies or adaptive zoom control to enhance small-target detection.

The training duration increased after data augmentation due to the expanded dataset size. Specifically, training required approximately 4.35 hours for the original dataset and 8.44 hours for the augmented dataset.

Inference speed evaluation

Inference speed was evaluated using the independent test dataset to ensure unbiased measurement. All test images were resized to 640 × 640 pixels and processed sequentially on an NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti GPU. The average inference time was 26.5 ms per image, corresponding to approximately 37.7 frames per second (FPS).

Considering that the monitoring system captures images periodically rather than continuously in video-stream mode, this inference speed is sufficient to satisfy real-time processing requirements in commercial poultry production settings.

Monitoring and analysis in commercial production settings

Three production cycles were recorded from 2023 to 2024 at the study site. The daily proportion of abnormal eye conditions and the number of dead chickens were examined over each full cycle from 31 days of age to market age. The results obtained for the cycles from April 7 to May 19, 2024, from July 5 to August 16, 2024, and from October 16 to November 27, 2024, are displayed in Fig. 10(a)–10(c), respectively. In the October–November cycle, data for day 52 were missing because of a power outage at the poultry house.

Proportion of abnormal eye conditions and mortality count of chickens from 31 days of age to market age: (a) cycle from April 7 to May 19, 2024, (b) cycle from July 5 to August 16, 2024, and (c) cycle from October 16 to November 27, 2024.

Among the three cycles, the cycle from July 5 to August 16, 2024, exhibited the highest proportion of abnormal eye conditions. This result was possibly attributable to the reuse of bedding during that cycle. The reuse of bedding may have contributed to changes in environmental conditions. However, environmental variables were not directly measured in this study. Therefore, their potential contribution to abnormal ocular phenotypes remains speculative. In addition, an increase in the proportion of abnormal eye conditions was observed as the chickens aged, consistent with the potential influence of gases and dust (Shankar, 2008). Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed moderate to moderately strong positive correlations (ρX,Y = 0.47–0.69) between the proportion of abnormal eye conditions and the mortality count, indicating that eye abnormalities may serve as a potential early warning indicator of flock health deterioration. An increase in the proportion of abnormal eye conditions may reflect underlying environmental or health stressors that might also be associated with an increased rate of mortality. A limitation of this study is that environmental variables such as ammonia concentration, carbon dioxide levels, and dust accumulation were not directly monitored. These factors may influence both ocular conditions and mortality, potentially acting as confounding variables in correlation analysis. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as indicative of temporal relationships rather than direct causal effects. Future studies integrating environmental sensors with image-based health indicators may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms.

Time-lag association analysis between abnormal eye proportion and mortality

Across the three production cycles, the strongest associations were not consistently observed at lag 0 but instead occurred at lag periods ranging from 3 to 7 days, as shown in Table 6. In the April–May cycle, the highest correlation was observed at lag 7 (ρX,Y = 0.6567), followed by lag 6 (ρX,Y = 0.6146). In the July–August cycle, which exhibited the highest overall abnormal eye proportion, the strongest correlation was observed at lag 5 (ρX,Y = 0.7304), with consistently high correlations also observed at lags 1, 3, and 6 (ρX,Y > 0.71). In the October–November cycle, the highest correlation occurred at lag 3 (ρX,Y = 0.5032), although the overall correlations were comparatively lower than those in the other two cycles. Collectively, these findings indicate a potential early-warning lead time of approximately 3–7 days prior to observable increases in mortality.

Operational threshold determination for early warning

A fixed-grid alarm threshold scan ranging from 1% to 10% (increment = 0.5%) was performed to determine an operational early warning threshold, as shown in Fig. 11(a)–11(c). In the April–May cycle, optimal abnormal eye proportion thresholds ranged from 4.0% to 6.5%, achieving a sensitivity of 1.00 with a FPR of 0.065. In the July–August cycle, the optimal threshold range was 4.5% to 5.0%, achieving sensitivities of 1.00 and 0.875 with corresponding FPR 0.167 and 0.042, respectively. In contrast, predictive performance in the October–November cycle was comparatively weaker, and although several thresholds between 3.5% and 6.5% yielded moderate performance, sensitivity remained substantially lower than in the other two cycles. Notably, thresholds around 4.5% were consistently identified in the two higher-signal production cycles. Based on cross-cycle consistency and the balance between sensitivity and false-alarm control, an operational threshold of approximately 4.5% is suggested for practical deployment. Because the threshold optimization was performed retrospectively using the same dataset, independent validation in future production cycles is warranted to further confirm generalizability.

Conclusion

In this study, a deep-learning-based early warning system was developed to detect abnormal eye conditions in chickens. This system uses a PTZ camera to automatically capture chicken eye images in commercial poultry houses. The system employs a YOLOv7 detection model to localize normal and abnormal chicken eyes and calculate the proportion of abnormal eye conditions, and it can upload results in real time to a poultry early warning platform for long-term recording.

A total of 1,000 images were collected to establish a dataset covering the complete growth cycle of native chickens. The dataset was partitioned to ensure balanced representation across production cycles and camera shooting angles. Images captured from different time points and acquisition settings were distributed across subsets without overlap, and no duplicated images were shared among the training, validation, and test datasets. Because the numbers of normal and abnormal eye annotations were imbalanced, data augmentation was applied to increase the number of abnormal eye samples. The augmentation strategy involved random rotation and brightness and saturation adjustments. To prevent data leakage, augmentation techniques were applied exclusively to the training dataset, whereas the validation and test datasets were kept unchanged for unbiased performance evaluation. Together with the original training images, 2,150 chicken eye images and annotation files were obtained. This augmented dataset was then used to train the YOLOv7-based chicken eye detection model of the proposed system. Evaluation on the test dataset revealed that the detection model achieved a higher performance on the augmented dataset than on the nonaugmented one, with its Precision, Recall, and F1score on the augmented dataset being 0.944, 0.831, and 0.884, respectively.

The trained model was used to evaluate the effects of the camera shooting angle and zoom level on the detection performance. The image acquisition parameters were selected on the basis of two criteria: an average number of detected eyes greater than 1 and an F1score greater than 0.8. A tilt angle range of 0° to 30° and a zoom level range of 4× to 24× were selected, with pan angles set at 30° intervals for daily image acquisition and detection.

Long-term monitoring revealed an increasing proportion of abnormal eye conditions toward the later stages of the production cycle. Consistently positive Pearson’s correlation coefficients were observed (ρX,Y = 0.6083, 0.6877, and 0.4719), indicating a stable association between the proportion of abnormal eye conditions and the number of dead chickens across different periods. Time-lag association analysis revealed that increases in the proportion of abnormal eye conditions preceded rises in mortality by approximately 3–7 days across different production cycles. This temporal precedence supports the interpretation of abnormal eye conditions as a practical early warning indicator. Cross-cycle threshold optimization indicated that an abnormal eye proportion of 4.5% provides a practical operational threshold, achieving high sensitivity with controlled false-alarm rates. This data-driven decision rule enhances the practical implementation of the proposed early warning system in commercial poultry production. Overall, the proposed early warning system for chicken eye abnormalities can serve as a digital health monitoring tool that enables timely alerts and interventions, thereby reducing labor burden and potential economic losses.
