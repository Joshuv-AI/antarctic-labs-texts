Restoration of LAMP2A expression in old mice leads to changes in the T cell compartment that support improved immune function
Authors: Cara A. Reynolds, Sandra Pelka, Floralba Gjergjova, Inmaculada Tasset, Rabia R. Khawaja, Kristen Lindenau, Gregory J. Krause, Evripidis Gavathiotis, Ana Maria Cuervo, Fernando Macian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SignificanceImmunosenescence, the decline in immune system function observed with age, is associated with increased susceptibility to disease and subsequent increased morbidity and mortality in older individuals. In this study, we show that the age-related decline in a selective type of autophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), contributes to reduced proliferation and function of T cells. Additionally, we demonstrate that T cell immune function can be improved in aged mice by genetically or pharmacologically boosting CMA activity. This work unveils CMA as a potential target for immunotherapeutic interventions in the aging immune system.

Aging is associated with a decline in immune system function, known as immunosenescence, which contributes to increased susceptibility to disease. T cells, one of the major players of the adaptive immune system, undergo important age-related changes in both number and function (1, 2). As organisms age, their capacity to produce T cells against novel antigens is markedly reduced (3). Furthermore, processes such as antigen recognition, costimulation, and clonal expansion—required for T cell activation and function—are also altered, thereby impairing T cell function (4). A growing number of studies support that loss of proteostasis, one of the well-established hallmarks of aging, contributes to reduced T cell function in aging (5). In this work, we explore the impact that the malfunctioning of a selective type of autophagy, a component of the cellular proteostasis network, has on T cell function.

Autophagy is a process through which cells degrade cellular components within lysosomes and recycle the products of this degradation for further use (6). Three different forms of autophagy coexist in mammalian cells: macroautophagy, chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), and microautophagy (endosomal microautophagy in mammalian cells) (7–9). Each of these forms of autophagy varies in the type of cargo that they degrade and in the regulatory mechanisms and steps that mediate cargo targeting and subsequent transport into the lysosome for degradation. Coexistence of these different types of autophagy provides cells with the ability to respond to multiple types of stress and to adjust their cellular organelles and proteome to better cope with those stressors (10, 11). A growing number of studies support the concept that age-associated changes in autophagy contribute to the functional decline observed in many organs and systems. These autophagic changes also participate in the development of age-associated diseases, by preventing cells from maintaining adequate homeostasis throughout their lifespan (12, 13).

This work focuses on CMA, a type of autophagy able to selectively target specific cytosolic proteins to the lysosome and mediate their transport directly across the lysosomal membrane for degradation (7). All CMA substrates bear a pentapeptide targeting motif in their sequence that, when recognized by the heat shock cognate 71KDa protein (HSC70), promotes their delivery to the lysosomal surface (14). There, HSC70-bound cargo interacts with the single-span lysosomal membrane protein lysosome-associated membrane protein type 2A (LAMP2A), which then multimerizes to form a translocation complex (15, 16). After unfolding, the targeted protein translocates into the lysosomal lumen, with assistance of a luminal form of HSC70, and it is rapidly degraded (16, 17). Although basal CMA activity contributes to maintenance of cellular homeostasis, CMA is also part of the cellular response to stress, becoming maximally activated upon starvation, oxidative stress, genotoxic insults, or metabolic challenges (7). CMA contributes both to quality control as well as to the degradation of fully functional proteins to terminate their function, thus regulating multiple cellular processes (i.e., metabolic pathways, cellular differentiation, circadian rhythms, etc.) (7). Consequently, failure of CMA in conditions such as aging (18), leads to both defective protein homeostasis and deregulation of important cellular functions. The decline of CMA activity with age has been attributed in most cells and tissues to reduced levels of LAMP2A, a limiting component for this type of autophagy (19).

We have previously shown that CMA contributes to CD4+ T cell activation. In response to T cell receptor (TCR) engagement, CD4+ T cells activate CMA and selectively degrade negative regulators of TCR signaling; this allows sustained T cell activation, which leads to increased T cell proliferation and cytokine production (20). In those studies, we also observed that T cells in both old humans and old mice showed reduced LAMP2A expression, especially in response to TCR engagement (20). However, how this decrease might affect the T cell compartment in old organisms, and whether an increase in the active pool of LAMP2A protein could enhance T cell function with age remain unclear.

In this study, we first used a mouse model with systemic loss of LAMP2A to recapitulate the aging defect in CMA and analyzed its impact on the T cell compartment. LAMP2A is the only spliced variant of the Lamp2 gene required for CMA, since no other protein variant has the cytosolic tail amino acid composition required for CMA substrate binding (21). Thus, in our models, the loss of LAMP2A leads to a loss of CMA (16, 22). We show that systemic loss of CMA does not affect number and distribution of T cells but does lead to defective T cell responses. Considering these findings, we next utilized two different mouse models to increase the levels of LAMP2A in vivo in old mice and evaluate the consequences of maintaining LAMP2A expression with age on T cell function. Our results support that approaches to sustain LAMP2A expression in old T cells improve the age-associated immunosenescent phenotype that defines T cells in older organisms (23, 24).

Results

Loss of LAMP2A Expression has an Inhibitory Effect on T Cell Responses.

We have previously shown that CD4+ T cells that lack LAMP2A display a defect in sustained T cell activation due to their inability to degrade specific inhibitors of TCR signaling, including Rcan1 and Itch (20). To expand on those studies and confirm that systemic CMA loss, as the one observed in aging, also impacts effective T cell activation, we measured the expression of activation markers and the engagement of cell metabolic functions in total CD4+ T cells isolated from wildtype mice or mice with systemic lack of LAMP2A (25). Our experiments confirmed the requirement of CMA for efficient TCR-engagement-induced T cell activation. In their resting state, expression of the activation marker CD44 and L-selectin marker CD62L was comparable between the LAMP2A-deficient and wildtype CD4+ T cells. However, following activation for twenty-four hours with anti-CD3/anti-CD28 coated beads, LAMP2A-deficient CD4+ T cells showed reduced upregulation of the activation marker CD44 (Fig. 1A). They also demonstrated reduced CD25 upregulation and decreased downregulation of CD62L (Fig. 1A).

CMA has been shown to play important roles in the regulation of cellular metabolism through selective removal of fully functional enzymes to terminate their function (26, 27). Considering the well-characterized metabolic shift—from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis—that T cells undergo during TCR activation (28), we set to determine whether changes in cellular metabolism may play a role in the reduced activation observed in the LAMP2A-deficient CD4+ T cells. To that end, we measured glycolysis and oxygen consumption rates in these cells using Seahorse technology. Parameters such as basal glycolysis, glycolytic capacity, and glycolytic reserve were activated in a comparable manner in wildtype and LAMP2A-deficient T cells following anti-CD3/anti-CD28-mediated stimulation. However, nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption—often attributed to processes such as NADPH-oxidase-derived reactive oxygen species formation (29, 30)—showed clear differences that depended on the presence or absence of LAMP2A in those cells, and consequently, on their capacity to activate CMA (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, several metabolic parameters showed increased intersubject dispersion (variance) within the group when we compared T cells from LAMP2A-deficient mice to T cells from their wildtype counterparts. Using the F test to evaluate significant differences between variances in activated T cells, we found that basal respiration, maximal respiration, and nonmitochondrial respiration showed a significant increase or a marked trend toward an increase in intersubject variation within the LAMP2A-deficient group (Fig. 1B). This increased heterogeneity, also a noticeable feature in aging T cells (31), may be due to activation of different levels of compensation in response to CMA loss in each mouse. We also performed stable isotope labeling using glucose-C13 and found that LAMP2A-deficient T cells showed increased ADP and AMP levels, a common sign of compromised overall energy status (32) (Fig. 1C). In agreement with this observation, we found increased AMP-to-ATP and ADP-to-ATP ratios (Fig. 1C), increased lactic acid production and decreased citric acid levels (Fig. 1D) in activated CMA-deficient T cells when compared to activated wildtype T cells.

Overall, our data support that CMA loss impacts the metabolic output of activated CD4+ T cells, and possibly their energy homeostasis.

LAMP2A Loss does not Affect Distribution of Thymocyte and Peripheral T Cell Populations.

We next evaluated the consequences on the T cell compartment of the systemic loss of LAMP2A that occurs in mice as they age using whole body LAMP2A-deficient mice (25). We found that in LAMP2A-deficient mice, thymocyte distribution resembled the distribution observed in wildtype mice, with similar numbers of CD4− CD8− double negative (DN), CD4+ CD8+ double positive and single positive (CD4+ or CD8+) thymocytes (Fig. 2 A and B). Furthermore, DN thymocytes at various stages of development (DN1 to DN4, according to the expression of CD44 and CD25), also showed similar distribution in both wildtype and LAMP2A-deficient mice (Fig. 2C). Contrary to our previous studies in mice with T-cells specific deficiency in LAMP-2A, where we found a 20-30% decrease in peripheral CD4+ and CD8+ T cells (20), analysis of mature splenic T cells from whole body LAMP-2A-deficient mice revealed no significant differences with wildtype both in total numbers and in percentages of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells from total splenocytes (Fig. 2D). Furthermore, we did not detect changes in the percentage of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells in the naïve or memory T cell compartments upon systemic blockage of CMA when compared to control mice (Fig. 2D). Because animals in this study were housed under pathogen free conditions, future studies including pathogen challenges will be necessary to investigate whether systemic CMA loss may have a direct effect on the reported accumulation of memory T cells with age, which occurs due to frequent encounter with various antigens throughout life.

Sustained Expression of LAMP2A with Age Enhances T Cell Responses.

Although systemic loss of CMA in young unchallenged mice had no significant effect on the number of T cells or the distribution of naïve and memory populations, the fact that we observed changes in expression of T cell activation markers CD44 and CD25 and in the metabolic properties of LAMP2A-deficient T cells (Fig. 1), motivated us to explore the impact of preserving LAMP2A expression with age in mice. We utilized a mouse model [Cre-ERTxRosa26-STOPflox/flox-LAMP2A, hereafter LAMP2A overexpressor (L2AOE)] where a copy of human LAMP2A is systemically expressed under the Rosa26 promoter, upon 5 tamoxifen injections given every-other-day at 12 mo of age (Fig. 3A) (25, 27). We have previously confirmed efficient deletion of the stop codon and subsequent expression of hLAMP2A in all tissues, including hematopoietic stem cells that will give rise to T cells through the rest of the mice lifespan (27). This model allows us to explore the consequences of preventing the age-dependent decrease of LAMP2A in T cells (20) and thus, preserving CMA activity in T cells with age.

We confirmed by qPCR that hLAMP2A was still expressed in CD4+ T cells isolated from 24-mo-old L2AOE mice, while expression of endogenous mouse Lamp2a remained unchanged (Fig. 3B). Next, we analyzed whether expression of hLAMP2A in old mice was sufficient to up-regulate CMA activity in T cells by crossing L2AOE mice with a transgenic mouse model expressing a fluorescent CMA reporter (KFERQDendra) (33, 34). As described before, this reporter allows us to monitor CMA as the arrival of the artificial fluorescent CMA substrate to lysosomes (detected as fluorescent puncta) (33, 34). As shown in Fig. 3C, we observed a clear increase in the number of green Dendra+ fluorescent puncta present in T cells 10 mo after tamoxifen injection when compared with T cells from vehicle (corn oil)-injected animals. This confirmed that the LAMP2A transgene expressed in these mice preserved higher CMA activity in T cells until late in life.

Analysis of the T cell compartment revealed that the changes expected in old mice (e.g., decreased naïve cells, increased percentage of memory T cells, or changes in the numbers of total CD4+ or CD8+ T cells) were still present in the L2AOE mice (Fig. 3D, please note that control mice in these studies include pools of littermate wild-type, hL2Af/f, and Cre-ERTxRosa26 mice injected with vehicle because separate analysis did not reveal differences among these three groups). Therefore, these results corroborated those seen in LAMP2A-deficient mice and supported that reduced CMA activity may not be responsible for the changes in naïve and memory populations that usually occur in old mice. Interestingly, this is not the case for other immune cells, as we found that LAMP2A KO mice present with significantly lower frequencies of B cells and an increased presence of Natural Killer T (NKT) cells in the spleen, which has also been reported in aging (35) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Though we did not see major effects on the distribution of naïve and memory T cell populations in old L2AOE mice, we decided to test whether preserving LAMP2A expression with age could ameliorate some of the functional consequences of aging in T cells. We isolated splenocytes from 24 mo old control and L2AOE mice in which LAMP2A expression was induced at 12 mo of age. Upon staining with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE), we activated them for 3 d using anti-CD3/anti-CD28 coated beads, to assess T cells’ ability to proliferate when activated through TCR engagement. Staining with anti-CD4 and -CD8 and flow cytometric analysis revealed that, as expected, activated CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from young mice underwent multiple divisions following TCR engagement (Fig. 3E). In contrast, both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells from old control mice showed a profound reduction in their proliferative capacity (Fig. 3E), signaling a defective response to activation with age. CD4+ T cells from 24 mo old L2AOE mice proliferated almost as much as T cells from young mice, and this effect was even more marked in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3E). These data support that, by preventing the age-associated loss of LAMP2A expression, T cells in old L2AOE mice conserved their capacity to respond to TCR engagement. Interestingly, we did not detect an increase in production of IL-2 by T cells, even when LAMP2A expression was sustained with age (Fig. 3 F, Top). However, we found that the expected increase in inflammatory cytokine production by T cells with age was prevented in T cells from old L2AOE mice. In fact, T cells from L2AOE mice showed interferon-gamma (IFNγ) levels similar to those seen in young mice T cells (Fig. 3 F, Bottom).

To further understand whether maintaining better CMA activity would also lead to improved overall proteostasis in T cells, we assessed the accumulation of K48-linked ubiquitin-containing proteins in these cells. Polyubiquitinated proteins are usually targeted to the proteasome for degradation to maintain cellular homeostasis but accumulate with age as components of the proteostasis network fail (5). We found that the accumulation of K48-ubiquitinated proteins observed in old T cells was reduced in T cells from the old L2AOE mice (Fig. 3G) supporting the idea that maintaining functional CMA with age can improve the overall proteostatic status of old T cells, as previously reported for other tissues in old mice (25, 27, 36). We also used Proteostat staining for protein inclusions and found that T cells from old L2AOE mice displayed significantly reduced staining intensity as compared to T cells from the old control group (Fig. 3H), thus confirming our findings of improved overall proteostatic status in T cells from old mice with preserved CMA activity.

Preserving CMA Activity with Age Reduces Number of T Cells that Express PD1 or Foxp3.

One of the characteristics that define specific T cell populations in old mice is the increase in frequency of CD4+ or CD8+ T cells that express the inhibitory receptor programmed cell death protein 1 (PD1). PD1-expressing T cells show a reduced ability to respond to antigen engagement (37, 38). Considering the improved ex vivo response to anti-CD3/anti-CD28-induced activation in T cells from old L2AOE mice (Fig. 3C), we decided to evaluate the expression of the inhibitory receptor PD1 in these cells. As expected, we found that the frequency of PD1-expressing CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was significantly increased in 24 m old control mice compared to young mice (Fig. 4 A, Left). Interestingly, while overexpression of hL2A in old mice only led to a discrete, nonsignificant trend toward reduced frequency of PD1-expressing CD4+ T cells, the frequency of PD1-expressing CD8+ T cells in old L2AOE mice was comparable to that observed in young mice and significantly lower than that observed in old control mice (Fig. 4 A, Left). Interestingly, we also noted that in contrast with the very high dispersion of frequencies of PD1+ T cells observed in old control mice, with some mice having more than 40% of CD8+T cells expressing PD1 and others as low as 2%, intersubject variability within the young control mice and old L2AOE mice groups was remarkably similar for the frequency of PD1+CD8+ T cells but not for PD1+CD4+ T cells (Fig. 4 A, Right) (31). Although future studies are needed, these differences suggest that preventing the age-dependent decline of CMA may have a more pronounced functional impact on CD8+ T cells than on CD4+ T cells. Overall, compared to old control mice, the old L2AOE mice demonstrated a more uniform cellular homeostasis maintenance, which translated to significantly reduced dispersion in the frequency of PD1-expressing T cells.

We also evaluated whether preserving CMA activity in T cells until late in life impacted the CD4+ CD25+ Foxp3+ regulatory T cell (Treg) compartment. Although the age-associated functional changes of Tregs are still unclear, most studies agree that Treg populations increase with age (39). While we did not observe a significant difference in the frequencies of Tregs among the young, old, and old L2AOE groups (Fig. 4 B, Left), we found a trend toward higher distribution/variance of Tregs in old control mice when compared to young mice (Fig. 4B), that was fully corrected in the old L2AOE mice (Fig. 4 B, Right).

In summary, preservation of functional CMA until late in life corrected the age-related changes in frequency of PD1+CD8+ T cells and variance of Tregs, further supporting that certain aspects of T cell biology may be more susceptible to the loss of CMA with age than others.

Preserved LAMP2A Expression Prevents Specific Age-Related Changes in the T Cell Proteome.

Since CMA is a protein degradation pathway and contributes to timely remodeling of the cellular functional proteome (27, 40), we used proteomic analysis to gain a better understanding of how the loss of CMA may contribute to T cell aging, and how restoring CMA activity may improve T cell function with age. First, we carried out quantitative differential proteomics in resting T cells isolated from young (4 mo) control and old (24 mo) control and L2AOE mice (SI Appendix, Tables S1, S2). Overall, 6,780 proteins were identified, of which 2,895 had significant differences across genotypes (P < 0.05). Comparison of the T cell proteome of the two old groups with that from young mice revealed that levels of 1,905 proteins changed with age in control and L2AOE mice. However, a subset of the age-related changes in T cells from old control mice (252 proteins) was no longer observed when CMA activity was preserved until late in life (Fig. 5A). Analysis of the directionality of the changes with age in protein levels between old control and old L2AOE mice T cells showed both proteins whose cellular levels increase with age but not in the L2AOE mice T cells (potential candidates for CMA degradation), as well as proteins with reduced levels in T cells from old control mice but not from old L2AOE mice (changes secondary to CMA remodeling of the proteome) (Fig. 5B).

Using coincidence in Reactome (41) and STRING pathway analysis (42), we identified enrichment (false discovery rate P < 0.05) in old control mice of proteins involved in several pathways that could account for the changes in T cell homeostasis and function that occur with age. These pathways include, among others, cell metabolism, response to starvation, translation initiation, and cytokine signaling (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Interestingly, the sustained expression of LAMP2A in old T cells prevented some of the changes noted in the old control mice and at least partially corrected others (Fig. 5 C, Left and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C). In general, enrichment pathway analysis only indicates changing pathways but not whether their functional outcome will be up-regulated or down-regulated, since that depends on the specific function of the proteins enriched within each respective pathway. However, because comparisons in changes for each protein in L2AOE T cells were done relative to the age-related changes observed in T cells from old control mice, it was possible to conclude whether a specific functional pathway equally changed with age or remained unchanged from young mice when CMA was preserved. Indeed, pathway analysis of the more than 200 proteins with changed abundance in T cells from old control mice but not in old L2AOE mice revealed correction of age-related changes in two major categories, immune system and metabolism (including processes related with protein metabolism such as protein translation and posttranslational modifications) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Within these categories, we noted that pathways involved in immunosenescence, including IL-37 signaling (43), integrin signaling (44), apoptosis mediated by BH3 proteins (45), or translation initiation (46), were all corrected by sustaining CMA activity in old CD4+ T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). It is important to note that given the wide spectrum of functions/functional pathways that CMA regulates, it is unlikely that restoring just one pathway will have such a dramatic effect as the one we observed when we restored CMA activity. We also noticed specific changes in protein levels that occurred only in T cells from old L2AOE mice, but not in the control old mice (Fig. 5 C, Right and SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). We propose that these L2AOE-specific changes could be due to the activation of additional mechanisms that may help prevent age-associated changes in T cells, including both general processes (e.g., cellular response to stress, ATP production) (Fig. 5C) and processes of putative specific relevance in T cells (e.g., changes in NFkB or calcium signaling) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Given the observed improvement in L2AOE T cells’ proteostasis (Fig. 3 G and H), we found it interesting that specific proteins related with proteostasis (e.g., protein ubiquitinization, endocytosis) were among those whose levels changed only or more pronouncedly in the L2AOE group. A more detailed analysis of the proteins included in these functional terms revealed reduction in a large subset of proteins involved in ubiquitinization (including ligases, transferases, and auxiliary proteins) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B), which correlates with the overall reduction in ubiquitinated proteins noted in the L2AOE T cells (Fig. 3G). It is possible that reduced quality-control ubiquitinization may be a consequence of more efficient removal of proteins by other pathways that are less dependent on ubiquitinization, such as CMA and other forms of lysosomal protein degradation, as we found elevated levels of a group of lysosome/endosomal proteins in old L2AOE T cells when compared with control old T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C and D).

We used a similar approach to compare the ability of T cells to change their proteome in response to activation (Fig. 5 D–H). As in resting cells, old CD4+ T cells also showed clear differences in their ability to up-regulate or down-regulate specific proteins following activation (Fig. 5 D–E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). In fact, only half of the changes that occur upon activation in young CD4+ T cells (951 proteins with increased and 717 with decreased levels) were detectable in old mice, which interestingly displayed instead additional changes not noted in young mice cells (Fig. 5 D and E). Pathway analysis revealed differences in the major functional pathways that responded to activation in young and old T cells (Fig. 5F). However, many of these differences observed in the old control mice were no longer noticeable in the old L2AOE mice, which supports the idea that maintaining CMA activity with age may prevent many aspects of aging in T cells (Fig. 5 D–F). Furthermore, comparison of the resting and activated proteomes of T cells from young, old, and old L2AOE mice revealed higher resemblance between the proteome of young and old L2AOE T cells both under resting and activated conditions (Fig. 5 G, Top). Similarly, the activation-associated Z scores (calculated from the changes in protein abundance induced by activation) were more similar between young and old L2AOE mice T cells (Fig. 5 G, Bottom). Reactome (Fig. 5F) and STRING (Fig. 5H) analysis of the pathways that did not respond to activation in old mice T cells but were recovered in old L2AOE mice T cells, included pathways with direct effects on activation-induced responses, such as the regulation of T cell proliferation and cytokine signaling (additional examples of changes in specific pathways with age and their behavior in old L2AOE T cells are shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S5B).

Overall, the differences in the proteome composition of T cells from old control and old L2AOE mice support that CMA may not only help maintain homeostasis as T cells age but it may also play a central role in ensuring efficient activation-induced responses.

Pharmacological CMA Reactivation in Old Mice Prevents Aging Features of the T Cell Compartment.

Though we first used a genetic approach as a proof-of-principle to demonstrate that restoring CMA activity with age could improve T cell function, we next evaluated whether using compounds that activate CMA would have comparable results. We used recently developed first-in-class small molecule compounds to selectively boost CMA activity, at least in part, by increasing LAMP2A expression (27, 47, 48). Using one of these compounds (CA39, hereafter referred to as CMA-Act1) (Fig. 6A), we first confirmed its ability to increase Lamp2a mRNA and LAMP2A protein levels in activated T cells from young mice in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Fig. 6 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Treatment of T cells from young mice with CMA-Act1 in vitro for 48 h also led to increased frequencies of IFNγ and IL-2 producing CD4+ T cells, as well as increased secretion of both IFNγ and IL-2 following activation, with no effect on resting T cells (Fig. 6 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). We confirmed that the effect of CMA-Act1 was dependent on CMA, as for example the frequency of IL-2 producing CD4+ T cells did not increase upon drug treatment in LAMP2A-deficient T cells (Fig. 6F).

Using medicinal chemistry, we have recently developed a derivative of CMA-Act1 (CA77.1, hereafter referred to as CMA-Act2) (Fig. 6A) suitable for in vivo administration because of its favorable pharmacokinetics and absence of blood or major organs’ toxicity after 5 mo of daily treatment (49). We confirmed that CMA-Act2, when administered to mice in vivo, was effective in increasing both T cell Lamp2a mRNA levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C) and CMA activity (measured with the KFERQDendra reporter; SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). In vivo oral administration of CMA-Act2 [30 mg/kg bw, (49) to 18 mo old mice for 4 mo resulted in a small yet significant increase in IL-2 production, a trend toward decreased IFNγ secretion and significantly increased TCR-induced proliferation in CD4+ T cells from those mice (Fig. 6 G–I). These results are similar, albeit of smaller magnitude, to those observed in the T cells from L2AOE old mice as compared to control old mice (Fig. 3). Interestingly, we also found lower numbers of PD1 expressing CD4+ T cells and reduced interindividual dispersion in these values in the CMA-Act2 treated mice compared to old mice administered only vehicle (Fig. 6J).

These results reproduce what we had observed in the genetic model with preserved CMA activity and demonstrate that reactivation of CMA activity may constitute a therapeutic approach to improve T cell responses in old organisms.

Discussion

Numerous reports have identified the importance of CMA in the maintenance of cellular homeostasis with age in different organs (12, 27, 36, 49, 50). Here, we show that this form of autophagy is also crucial to sustain T cell function in old mice and that preventing the decline in CMA activity, that occurs in T cells starting by midlife, is sufficient to preserve T cell function. Furthermore, in support of the translational potential of these findings, we demonstrate that pharmacological activation of CMA in already old organisms also leads to phenotypic and molecular changes supportive of improved T cell activity.

The lack of differences in the distribution of naïve and memory T cells both in whole body L2AKO mice or mice where expression of LAMP2A was preserved until late in life, when compared to age-matched control mice, may be a consequence of the lack of pathogen challenges in our studies. In fact, our previous studies using mice with a T cell-specific deficiency in LAMP2A showed a decreased T cell effector response upon rechallenge with a high dose of Listeria monocytogenes (20), suggesting that CMA may play an important role in memory T cell function. Future studies in which mice are challenged should provide more information regarding memory T cell accumulation. However, in this current work with unchallenged mice, we cannot conclude whether CMA loss with age contributes to the accumulation of T cell memory populations in old mice. Regardless, restoring levels of LAMP2A when mice are already 12 mo old should not have a major effect on the distribution of those populations. Instead, we found differences in other parameters that support the idea that maintaining CMA activity with age may prevent some of the characteristics of the T cell immunosenescent phenotype that occurs in old mice. For instance, the frequency of T cells expressing the inhibitory receptor PD1, whose expression increases with age in T cells, was clearly reduced in old mice with preserved CMA activity. Cells expressing PD1 likely accumulate in response to chronic exposure to antigens throughout life and lead to reduced effector T cell function since they behave like exhausted T cells (37, 38). Although the exact mechanisms that cause the elevated PD1 levels in old T cells are not fully known, our data suggest that the loss of CMA activity in those T cells may contribute to the increase in the fraction of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing PD1. While transmembrane proteins such as PD1 are unlikely to directly undergo degradation via CMA, the recently identified inhibitory effect of CMA blockage on endocytosis (49) could contribute to the elevated surface levels of PD1 in aging T cells. Interestingly, efforts to maintain LAMP2A expression with age reduced the fraction of PD1 expressing T cells and likely contributed to their enhanced function. Indeed, we found that T cells from old transgenic mice with preserved CMA activity show levels of TCR-engagement-induced proliferation similar to those seen in young T cells, and clearly higher than those of control old T cells. Whether this effect is due to changes in PD1 expression or other mechanisms needs to be further investigated. In fact, our proteomic analysis supports that CMA controls a plethora of processes related to T cell function. The loss of this form of autophagy with age would compromise these mechanisms and, therefore, should lead to reduced T cell responses.

It is also possible that whole-body LAMP2A-deficient mice may activate compensatory mechanisms in their T cells that prevent differences when compared to control mice under homeostatic conditions. For example, given the role of NADPH oxidase-derived reactive oxygen species in T cell activation and in the generation of an efficient and long-lasting immune response (51), we propose that the increased nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption that we observe in the activated LAMP2A-deficient CD4+ T cells may be a compensatory mechanism of these cells to address their reduced response to TCR engagement. An additional difference when comparing our findings in T cell function in whole body L2A-deficient mice with the previously analyzed T cell–specific L2AKO mice (20), is the involvement of other immune cells on the regulation of T cell function. Under physiological conditions, CD4+ T cell activation relies on interaction of CD4+ T cells with professional antigen-presenting cells (APC), such as dendritic cells (52). The impact of CMA deficiency within APCs has not been characterized. However, considering our results showing reduced activation of LAMP2A-deficient CD4+ T cells in vitro and the increased nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption within LAMP2A-deficient CD4+ T cells as a potential compensatory mechanism, it is possible that CMA-deficient APCs may also undergo changes that affect their ability to activate T cells in vivo.

Recently, it has been reported that the loss of LAMP2 in thymic epithelial cells leads to defects in the generation of CD4+ T cells, which exit the thymus with a limited TCR repertoire (53). While it is tempting to speculate that the role of CMA during thymocyte development may be mainly restricted to cells in the thymic epithelium, we must first consider that we did not observe any effect in the whole body L2AKO mice, which also involved loss of this gene within thymic epithelial cells. Additionally, in that report the entire Lamp2 gene was deleted, affecting not only CMA but also other processes, including macroautophagy and lysosomal degradation of RNAs—functions attributed to the other LAMP2 protein variants (54, 55). To determine whether CMA plays a role in this process, it will be necessary to selectively delete Lamp2a in the thymus epithelium and observe if it recapitulates the defects seen in mice where the total Lamp2 gene is missing.

As organisms age, it is usual to see higher heterogeneity on changes in a variety of parameters from animal to animal. This likely reflects differences in the ability of old organisms to adapt to the stress imposed by aging and to cope with the age-associated functional loss through compensatory mechanisms. We found that deletion of LAMP2A increased intersubject variability in different metabolic outcomes and that, conversely, sustained expression of LAMP2A in old mice decreased intersubject variability in their frequency of PD1-expressing T cells and Tregs. The higher dispersion of values in the old mice group is likely a consequence of differences in the ability of old mice to compensate for the loss of CMA activity with age in T cells, which at least in part, results from reduced LAMP2A expression in those cells (20). Triggering of compensatory mechanisms could be proportional to the magnitude of the age-related physiological loss of LAMP2A expression in each specific mouse.

There are conflicting data regarding how aging affects the Treg compartment. While thymic and peripheral differentiation of Tregs appears to be reduced during aging, the total number of Tregs are augmented (56). This is likely due to homeostatic expansion and decreased cell death because of lower levels of the proapoptotic protein Bim (57). While some initial studies reported no functional alteration in aged Tregs (56), other studies have found that populations of old Tregs present functional defects that include reduced capacity to suppress Th17 cells (58), reduced recruitment to sites of injury to participate in tissue repair programs (59) and increased accumulation in the visceral fat that leads to altered adipose tissue metabolism and increased insulin resistance (60). Although we did not explore Treg function in these mice, it was evident that the accumulation of Tregs that we found in old mice was markedly reduced in the old L2AOE mice, identifying another aspect of T cell biology that may be regulated by CMA as T cells age. Although we still do not have a full picture of how this may occur, our proteomic analysis offers some clues as to the mechanisms that CMA may modulate to prevent changes in Treg populations with age. Our findings in an ongoing parallel project addressing how CMA modulates Treg function in young mice point to a crucial role of CMA in this process, supporting that the loss of CMA activity with age may have a clear impact on the presence and function of Tregs in old mice.

We want to emphasize our reasons for choosing in this study a mouse model that restores LAMP2A expression with age in almost all tissues (Lamp2a expression is induced from the Rosa26 promoter). First, we wanted to ensure that LAMP2A expression would be sustained as mice age [this has been shown for genes expressed under the Rosa26 promoter (61). Additionally, our objective was to analyze the effect on T cells of the systemic changes in LAMP2A expression that occur with age. Other models, such as a CD4-ERT-Cre inducer, could have helped to specifically address the consequences of sustained LAMP2A expression in T cells with age, but would have prevented us from assessing the contributions of other cell populations that may be crucial in cooperating with T cells to maintain their activity (e.g., dendritic cells). In addition, the previously confirmed tamoxifen-induced expression of hLAMP2A in hematopoietic stem cells until late in life (27) was an efficient way to maintain hLAMP2A expression in T cells without the need for repeated tamoxifen injections throughout the mice life-span. Although it will certainly be interesting to use T cell-specific models to explore cell-intrinsic changes, here we tried instead to recapitulate the physiological aging setting and to address whether full body restoration of LAMP2A expression would result in improved T cell responses and a healthier T cell compartment. Analyzing the systemic effect of modulating CMA was also important to support the therapeutic value of our observations, since most drug-mediated interventions will likely be systemically administered. To this aim, we used CMA activating compounds both in vitro and in vivo, and in T cells from young and old mice, to analyze the direct effect of these interventions in T cells and if systemic boosting of CMA would lead to comparable results as those seen in the genetic model. Our studies did show that CMA-activating compounds, which act by repressing the endogenous inhibitory effect of the retinoic acid receptor alpha on CMA and thus efficiently induce LAMP2A expression (47), lead to a healthier T cell compartment in already old mice. These compounds have recently been shown to improve different physiological and pathological conditions associated with reduced CMA activity affecting the bone marrow, retina, and brain (27, 36, 48, 49). In this study, we show that these compounds also enhance T cell responses, reduce numbers of PD1-expressing cells, and lower frequencies of Tregs in old mice. It is worth noting that the overall effect on T cell response of our pharmacological activation of CMA in vivo was of reduced magnitude than the one observed in the T cells from L2AOE mice. We attribute these differences to the fact that in the genetic model, T cells up-regulate expression of hL2A since 12 mo of age, and consequently CMA activity is fully functional throughout life, whereas in the pharmacological studies, treatment was initiated at 18 mo of age, when CMA activity is already markedly reduced. These data support, therefore, that reactivating CMA in old individuals may be beneficial to maintain T cell function and enhance, for instance, their response to pathogens or vaccinations. We do not know yet how late in life we would be able to activate CMA and still see a positive effect in T cells. However, our current experimental conditions of providing CMA-activating compounds to mice that are already 18 mo old offer insights to support that activation of CMA may constitute a viable therapeutic approach to boost T cell function and, therefore, improve the overall health status of old individuals, even when administered late in life.

Materials and Methods

Mice and Cells.

Male 3- to 4-m-old C57BL6/J mice from The Jackson Laboratory (JAX 000664) were utilized in experiments with only young wildtype mice. Whole body LAMP2A deficient mice (25) and conditional LAMP2A overexpressing mice (L2AOE) were generated using Cre-recombination methodology and expression of LAMP2A was induced with tamoxifen as previously reported (25). KFERQ-Dendra2 reporter mice (33) were crossed with L2AOE mice to confirm CMA activation. The CMA activator (CA-Act2 30 mg/Kg b.w. per mouse and day) was administered orally in the form of sucralose gelatin agar pills as before (27, 49). Control mice received drug-free pills (vehicle). Mouse T cells were isolated from collected spleens, thymus, and lymph nodes (axillary, inguinal, and cervical) using antibody-coated microbeads and cultured as described previously (20). T cell activation was performed using anti-CD3/anti-CD28 coated beads and cells were analyzed by flow cytometry after surface staining. All antibodies, clones, isotypes, and catalog numbers used in this study are provided in SI Appendix, Table S3 and additional experimental details are included in SI Appendix, Supplementary Information.

Cellular Energetics and Metabolic Flux.

Cultured T cell glycolysis and rates of oxygen consumption were measured in real-time using the Agilent Seahorse enhanced metabolic assay platform assay kits in a Seahorse XF pro analyzer. Metabolic flux in resting and activated T cells was determined upon incubation in C13-glucose using CE-TOFMS analysis.

Protein and mRNA Analysis.

Protein abundance was analyzed in cell lysates using SDS-polyacrylamide electrophoresis and immunoblot with the antibodies of interest. Cytokine secretion was quantified by Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) using a sandwich antibody system as before (20). Analysis of the cellular proteomes of resting and activated T cells was performed by tandem mass spectrometry for multiplexed proteomics. Gene expression was quantified using real-time PCR after normalization to the housekeeping gene actin.

Statistical Analyses.

All data presented are mean ± SEM. All statistical analyses were done using GraphPad Prism software (www.graphpad.com). The two-tailed unpaired T test was used to compare two samples and One- or Two-way ANOVA with post hoc analysis to compare more than two groups or different conditions in several groups, respectively. Conformation of data to a normal distribution was confirmed to apply a parametric test. We used an F test to compare changes in the variance of specific parameters among samples. Significance was defined when the P value was 0.05 or lower. Every experiment was performed in at least three independent replicates, unless otherwise stated, and no data were excluded from the study. Replicates of experiments in culture were performed on different days and from cells isolated from different animals to confirm reproducibility of the procedures. Data collection and analyses were done blinded to the samples. Blinding was lifted only for the final statistical analysis.
