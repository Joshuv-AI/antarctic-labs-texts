Attention performance in emergency physicians: the effect of shift duration and cardiac arrest cases
Authors: İbrahim Halil Yasak, Hüseyin Avni Demir, Eyyup Sabri Şeyhanlı, Ramazan Giden, Esat Barut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEmergency departments (EDs) are high-acuity clinical environments characterized by a high demand for sustained attention and significant cognitive load. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of shift timing and high-stress clinical events, such as cardiac arrest, on the attentional performance of emergency medicine specialists.MethodsThis prospective observational study was conducted in the emergency department of a tertiary care hospital, enrolling 23 emergency medicine specialists. Participants’ attentional performance was measured using the mobile Stroop Color-Word Test. Assessments were performed at two-hour intervals during 8-hour shifts and also pre- and post-cardiac arrest events. The collected data were analyzed in relation to variables including shift type, number of cardiac arrests, and resuscitation success.ResultsStroop scores differed significantly by shift type (p = 0.021, η² ≈ 0.07), with the highest attentional performance observed during the evening shift and the lowest during the night shift. A significant decline in attentional performance was noted following cardiac arrest events (median difference: −3 points, p < 0.001; r = 0.59), a pattern that was consistent across all shift types. Furthermore, post-resuscitation attentional levels were significantly higher after successful outcomes (Return of Spontaneous Circulation, ROSC) compared to unsuccessful ones (p = 0.017; r ≈ 0.30). Mixed-effects multivariate analysis revealed that working the night shift (β = −3.1), an increased number of cardiac arrests (β = −0.8 per event), and an unsuccessful ROSC outcome (β = −2.4) were all independently associated with lower attentional performance.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that night shifts, frequent exposure to cardiac arrests, and unsuccessful resuscitation outcomes adversely affect the attentional performance of emergency physicians. These findings suggest that physicians are more cognitively vulnerable in the ED, particularly during night hours and following high-stress clinical situations. These results underscore the need for a re-evaluation of shift scheduling and the development of structural interventions to support physicians after critical incidents, with the ultimate goal of enhancing clinical safety.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-026-01529-4.

Introduction

The emergency department (ED) is characterized by high patient volume, diagnostic uncertainty, and the frequent need for rapid, critical decision-making [1]. The cognitive abilities of emergency physicians, particularly attention, cognitive flexibility, and inhibitory control, are fundamental to ensuring accurate diagnoses, timely interventions, and overall patient safety [2]. However, the demanding nature of emergency medicine exposes physicians to numerous factors known to negatively impact cognitive function, including prolonged work hours, sleep deprivation, and high-stress clinical events [3, 4].

Shift work, an intrinsic component of emergency care, is a primary contributor to cognitive impairment. Disruption of the natural circadian rhythm, especially during night shifts, has been consistently linked to decreased vigilance, slower reaction times, and an increased rate of medical errors [5, 6]. While the subjective experience of fatigue is well-documented, objective quantification of its impact on specific cognitive domains like selective attention during a clinical shift remains an area of active investigation.

Beyond systemic factors like shift schedules, acute high-stress events, such as the management of a cardiac arrest, impose a significant cognitive load on physicians [7]. The combination of intense focus, rapid procedural execution, and emotional stress during resuscitation can lead to “cognitive tunneling” and subsequent mental exhaustion [8]. The immediate cognitive aftermath of such an event, and how it relates to the clinical outcome (e.g., successful or unsuccessful resuscitation), is not well understood.

The Stroop Color-Word Test is a robust and widely validated neuropsychological tool used to measure selective attention and inhibitory control—the ability to suppress a prepotent but incorrect response [9]. Emergency physicians must make clinically critical decisions requiring intense focus while managing multiple high-acuity patients simultaneously in high-stress environments, necessitating objective assessment tools that can evaluate cognitive performance under such demanding conditions [10].

This study was designed to bridge a gap in the literature by using the Stroop test to prospectively and objectively measure the real-time effects of shift timing, the acute stress of managing cardiac arrest, and the outcome of resuscitation on the attentional performance of emergency medicine specialists. We hypothesized that night shift work and involvement in cardiac arrest events would be associated with a significant decline in attention levels.

Methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was conducted in the emergency department of a tertiary university hospital between February 1, 2025, and March 1, 2025. ED sees approximately 30,000 patients per month and serves as a regional emergency medical center. The study protocol was prepared in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Harran University Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval number: HRU/25.12.39). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Study population

Of the 30 emergency medicine specialists invited to participate in the study, 25 accepted. Two of these participants were excluded from the analysis due to incomplete Stroop measurements. Failure to complete the Stroop Color-Word Test within the predefined time frame or failure to perform the planned measurement after cardiac arrest was defined as “missing data.”

A total of 25 emergency medicine specialists with at least three years of post-specialization experience were initially invited to participate. All participants provided written informed consent. Two specialists were excluded from the final analysis due to incomplete data collection, resulting in a final cohort of 23 participants. Inclusion criteria were: (1) currently working full-time as an emergency medicine specialist, (2) having at least three years of ED experience, and (3) providing voluntary consent. Exclusion criteria included any known neurological or psychiatric condition that could affect cognitive performance or the use of medication known to influence attention.

Data collection and instruments

Demographic and Professional Data: A standardized questionnaire was administered at enrollment to collect data on age, gender, years of experience in emergency medicine, years of experience in the critical care (red) area, total years of practice, and lifestyle habits (alcohol and tobacco use).

Cognitive performance assessment

Cognitive performance, particularly selective attention and inhibitory control, was assessed using the Stroop Color-Word Test [9]. The mobile version of the Stroop test, which measures attention selectivity and cognitive flexibility, was used in the study (Fig. 1). Participants practiced with the application before starting the test to familiarize themselves with the process. The test interface displays three color names: one at the top and two at the bottom; however, the color in which the words are written may differ from the color they represent. Participants were asked to select the word at the bottom that indicated the color of the word at the top. Correct answers were scored + 1, incorrect answers were scored − 1, and the final score was determined at the end of the 60-second time limit. According to the information provided by the app developer, the test score is directly proportional to the level of concentration.

Data collection was integrated into the physicians’ regular work schedules and comprised two main components:

Intra-shift Measurements: During each participant’s standard 8-hour shift (Shift 1: 08:00–16:00; Shift 2: 16:00–24:00; Shift 3: 00:00–08:00), the Stroop test was administered four times at two-hour intervals, beginning at the start of the shift. Variables

Dependent variable

The Stroop Test score, a continuous variable representing the level of attention.

Independent variables

Demographic data (age, gender), professional experience (years), lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol), shift type (coded as Shift 1, 2, or 3), measurement time-point, occurrence of a cardiac arrest (pre- vs. post-event), ROSC status (successful vs. unsuccessful), and the total number of arrests managed during a shift.

ROSC was defined as return of circulation evidenced by a palpable pulse and/or measurable blood pressure. Sustained ROSC was defined as the absence of need for chest compressions for 20 consecutive minutes with persisting signs of circulation, consistent with Utstein-style reporting [12].

Statistical analysis

Initial data exploration and preliminary analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v25.0. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical methods. Descriptive statistics for non-normally distributed data were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and/or median (interquartile range, IQR), while categorical data were presented as counts (n) and percentages (%). For preliminary comparisons, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used for between-shift group comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired pre- and post-arrest comparisons, and the Mann–Whitney U test for comparisons between groups based on ROSC status. Effect sizes (e.g., r, η²) were reported where appropriate.

To account for the non-independence of repeated measurements within physicians, the primary inferential analyses were conducted in R. Given that multiple observations were collected from each physician, data were clustered within individuals. Consequently, Linear Mixed-Effects Models (LMM) were employed as the primary analytical approach, with a random intercept specified for each physician to account for this intra-subject correlation.

The model assessing the effect of shift work included shift type as the primary explanatory variable, with adjustments for covariates such as age, sex, alcohol consumption, and smoking status. For the cardiac arrest analysis, the model included timepoint (pre/post), shift type, and their interaction term (timepoint × shift) as fixed effects. Estimated Marginal Means (EMMs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived from these models. Pairwise comparisons were adjusted for multiple testing using the Holm-Bonferroni method.

Model assumptions were visually inspected using residual-versus-predicted plots and Normal Q-Q plots. To assess the robustness of the findings, bootstrap-based 95% CIs (1,000 simulations) were calculated for the fixed-effect coefficients of the LMMs. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Software and packages

All analyses were performed in the R environment, utilizing packages such as lme4, lmerTest, and emmeans.

Effect of shift type on stroop scores: linear mixed-effects model

To account for the within-physician correlation of repeated measures, a linear mixed-effects model was fitted with a random intercept for each physician. The Estimated Marginal Means (EMMs) and their corresponding 95% Confidence Intervals (CIs) derived from this model are presented in Table 4. The EMMs were 26.53 (95% CI: 24.10–28.96) for Shift 1, 30.11 (95% CI: 27.68–32.53) for Shift 2, and 25.43 (95% CI: 23.01–27.86) for Shift 3, respectively.

Pairwise comparisons, with Holm’s adjustment for multiple testing, revealed that Shift 2 was associated with significantly higher Stroop scores compared to Shift 1 (mean difference = 3.58, p = 6.86e-08). Conversely, Shift 3 was associated with significantly lower Stroop scores compared to Shift 1 (mean difference = -1.10, p = 0.0464). The difference between Shift 2 and Shift 3 was also highly significant (mean difference = 4.67, p = 1.13e-10) (Table 5). Discussion

This study reveals that the attentional performance of emergency physicians is significantly affected by shift timing and high-stress clinical events such as cardiac arrest. As one of the few prospective observational studies to utilize the mobile Stroop Color-Word Test, it provides a rare, real-time, and objective assessment of fluctuations in attentional performance within a clinical setting. The findings make the inherent cognitive load of emergency medicine practice tangible and underscore the need to re-evaluate healthcare delivery to enhance both physician well-being and patient safety.

The impact of shift work on cognitive performance

One of the most striking findings of this study is the pronounced negative effect of the night shift on attentional levels. Stroop scores declined significantly during the night shift, whereas they were highest during the evening shift. This finding is consistent with the extensive literature demonstrating the adverse effects of circadian rhythm disruption and chronic sleep deprivation on cognitive functions like attention and reaction time, and the broader risk of occupational burnout in healthcare settings [5, 6, 13, 14]. Specifically, cognitive performance is known to peak during the evening hours, often referred to as the “wake-maintenance zone,” before declining into the “circadian nadir” during the night [15]. This physiological pattern should be a key consideration in shift scheduling. Furthermore, the emergence of the evening shift as the period of optimal performance suggests a more dynamic time-attention relationship than the conventional view that simply identifies the night as the highest-risk period.

The acute cognitive load of cardiac arrest

The decline in attentional performance observed after high-stress events like cardiac arrest (median drop from 31 to 27; p < 0.001) was highly significant, with a medium-to-large effect size (r = 0.59). This result corroborates the cognitive neuroscience literature, which describes the inhibitory effects of stress on the prefrontal cortex [16–18]. Acute stress can alter dopamine and norepinephrine levels, weakening “top-down” control mechanisms, which in turn can lead to phenomena such as attentional deficits and “cognitive tunneling.” The fact that this decline was consistent across all shifts indicates that the stressor suppresses cognitive function irrespective of the time of day.

Psychological and cognitive reflections of clinical outcomes

Another intriguing finding was that post-resuscitation attentional performance was significantly higher following successful outcomes (ROSC) compared to unsuccessful ones (median 30 vs. 26; p = 0.017). This aligns with psychophysiological theories suggesting that the positive affect generated by a successful outcome may buffer the negative cognitive effects of stress [19]. Conversely, unsuccessful resuscitations can be demoralizing, potentially inducing secondary trauma, and leading to a more pronounced decline in attention. This finding highlights the importance of post-event psychological support, not only for emotional well-being but also for the preservation of cognitive capacity.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its prospective design, the use of the Stroop test—an objective and validated neuropsychological tool—and its execution in a real-world clinical setting. The application of a mobile version of the Stroop test enabled real-time monitoring of attention throughout shifts and during critical events, providing a direct assessment of cognitive fluctuations in clinical practice, as opposed to controlled experimental environments.

However, the study also has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 23) and single-center design limit the generalizability of the findings. The recruitment of participants on a voluntary basis may have introduced selection bias, meaning the sample may not be representative of all emergency physicians. Additionally, the presence of researchers could have influenced participant behavior (the Hawthorne effect), potentially inflating Stroop performance.

A significant methodological point is the handling of repeated measures. Although later corrected, some initial analyses assumed independence between observations from the same physician. This assumption can lead to underestimated standard errors, narrower confidence intervals, and an inflated risk of Type I errors. The subsequent adoption of linear mixed-effects models (LMM) with a random intercept for each physician was a crucial step to address this dependency and enhance the validity of the primary inferences.

Furthermore, while the Stroop test is a valid measure of selective attention and inhibitory control, it assesses only one facet of cognitive function. The study did not evaluate other dimensions of executive function, such as decision-making, working memory, or task switching, which could have been assessed with a broader neuropsychological test battery [17]. The clinical significance threshold for a change in the Stroop score has also not been defined, which makes it challenging to interpret the practical implications of the observed differences. Finally, time-varying confounders (e.g., accumulating fatigue, emotional state during an event) were not explicitly controlled for in the models. The findings are observational and should be interpreted as associations rather than causal relationships.

Conclusion

This single-center, observational study demonstrates that night shifts, management of cardiac arrests, and unsuccessful resuscitation outcomes significantly diminish the attentional performance of emergency physicians. The findings objectively quantify the inherent cognitive load in emergency medicine and identify specific time periods and event types during which physicians are under the greatest cognitive stress.

By employing repeated measures with the Stroop test, this study captured dynamic changes in attention, revealing the post-resuscitation period as a critical point of cognitive vulnerability. It also highlights the divergent psychological and cognitive impacts of successful versus unsuccessful resuscitation outcomes on physician performance.

These findings underscore the need for healthcare institutions to develop and implement strategies aimed at fatigue mitigation, physician well-being, and post-event recovery protocols. Future large-scale, multi-center studies employing comprehensive neurocognitive assessments are warranted to validate these results and provide a robust evidence base for structural changes designed to enhance patient safety.
