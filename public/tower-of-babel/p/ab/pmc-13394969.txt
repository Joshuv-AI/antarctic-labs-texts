Characterizing Cutaneous α‐Synuclein Deposition and Seeding Activity in Parkinson's Disease Subtypes
Authors: Yuting Jin, Tianyuan Yang, Minglei Liu, Haiyang Luo, Jing Yuan, Ke Zhang, Xinsheng Han, Jing Yang, Yuming Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCutaneous phosphorylated α‐synuclein (p‐syn) and α‐synuclein seeding activity are promising biomarkers for Parkinson's disease (PD), but their clinical value remains uncertain due to disease heterogeneity. This study evaluates these two biomarkers in PD patients to inform phenotype‐specific diagnosis and disease severity assessment.MethodsWe compared the diagnostic performance of cutaneous p‐syn immunostaining and α‐synuclein seed amplification assay (syn‐SAA) in 108 PD patients and 60 controls. Additional analyses assessed the influence of clinical subtypes on p‐syn distribution (positive rates and diffusion coefficients across regions), and syn‐SAA characteristics, along with their correlations with clinical scales.ResultsCutaneous p‐syn immunostaining showed 100% specificity and 75% sensitivity, whereas syn‐SAA showed 93.3% specificity and 86.1% sensitivity. Among the clinical subtypes, a significant regional difference in the diffusion coefficient was observed only in the suspected REM sleep behavior disorder subgroup, with higher values in the distal leg than in the posterior cervical region (p = 0.001). The diffusion coefficient correlated with the severity of both motor and non‐motor symptoms (all p < 0.05). No significant differences in syn‐SAA positivity rates or parameters were found across clinical subtypes. Syn‐SAA parameters were consistent across biopsy sites and associated with disease duration and non‐motor symptom severity, but not with motor symptom severity.InterpretationThese findings highlight complementary roles for skin biomarkers in PD. P‐syn deposition shows regional α‐synuclein aggregation, reflecting clinical heterogeneity and supporting phenotype‐specific diagnosis and severity assessment, whereas α‐synuclein seeding activity enables early diagnosis and may serve as a potential biomarker for monitoring disease progression.

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD) is a common and incurable neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the pathological aggregation of α‐synuclein in both the central and peripheral nervous systems [1, 2]. Although the diagnosis of PD is primarily based on clinical criteria, the heterogeneity of symptoms and disease progression frequently leads to diagnostic inaccuracies. Definitive diagnosis still relies on postmortem identification of Lewy bodies containing α‐synuclein in the brain. CSF α‐synuclein seed amplification assay (syn‐SAA) has demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy and near‐perfect concordance with neuropathology, but its requirement for lumbar puncture limits widespread use. Therefore, developing a reliable and sensitive assay for detecting pathological α‐synuclein in easily accessible peripheral tissues is critical for enabling early or differential diagnosis, assessing disease severity, and guiding therapeutic strategies.

Peripheral tissues such as the skin, salivary glands, submandibular gland, olfactory mucosa, and enteric plexuses have emerged as accessible sites for detecting pathological α‐synuclein aggregates [3, 4, 5, 6]. Among these, the skin holds particular promise as a minimally invasive diagnostic matrix for PD, owing to its embryological derivation from the same germ layer as the central nervous system, as well as its high diagnostic accuracy, safety, reproducibility, and amenability to repeated sampling across all disease stages [7, 8, 9]. Recent cutaneous biomarker research has primarily focused on detecting phosphorylated α‐synuclein (p‐syn) and α‐synuclein seeding activity [10, 11, 12]. Immunostaining of skin biopsies from patients with PD reveals p‐syn deposition within autonomic nerve terminals [1, 6, 13, 14]. Peripheral α‐synuclein pathology may precede brain involvement [15, 16, 17]. Notably, p‐syn‐associated autonomic dysfunction can manifest during the presymptomatic or prodromal stages of PD [16, 17, 18], suggesting the potential of p‐syn as an early diagnostic biomarker. The syn‐SAA leverages the prion‐like seeding activity of α‐synuclein to initiate misfolding and aggregation in patients with PD. This self‐perpetuating cascade drives pathological amplification through the recruitment of endogenous α‐synuclein, which robustly propagates throughout the brain‐peripheral axis [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. Several studies have demonstrated the high accuracy of this method in detecting α‐synuclein aggregates in brain tissue and CSF, with recent verification in skin [24, 25, 26]. Thus, cutaneous α‐synuclein seeding activity may be a promising biomarker for preclinical diagnosis while providing mechanistic insights into disease progression.

Immunostaining and syn‐SAA provide complementary qualitative assessments of α‐synuclein pathology: immunostaining visualizes p‐syn deposition in cutaneous nerves via the Ser129‐phosphorylated epitope, assessing the morphology of aggregated p‐syn; whereas syn‐SAA detects seeding activity by exploiting the prion‐like properties of misfolded α‐synuclein. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated substantial heterogeneity in clinical and prodromal PD, with patients exhibiting diverse clinical presentations, pathological mechanisms, and spatiotemporal progression patterns [27]. Understanding this variability may help elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the disease and guide the development of phenotype‐modifying therapies. However, research on these cutaneous biomarkers remains predominantly qualitative, lacking validated quantitative methods to stratify PD subtypes or measure disease severity and progression. Therefore, we conducted a prospective study to compare the diagnostic accuracy of the two biomarkers for PD and to evaluate their correlations with disease severity and clinical subtypes, thereby establishing the utility of α‐synuclein as a peripheral biomarker.

Methods

Patients with PD were consecutively recruited from the Department of Neurology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University between January 2023 and February 2024, irrespective of disease stage or treatment modality. We recruited 108 patients meeting the 2015 Movement Disorder Society Clinical Diagnostic Criteria for PD [28] and 60 controls without neurodegenerative diseases. Controls were confirmed symptom‐free by two neurologists. Exclusion criteria were first‐degree family history of PD, known monogenic PD‐related mutations, and clinical features suggestive of atypical or genetic parkinsonism. All participants underwent neurological examination, and demographic characteristics (sex, age, disease duration, education) were recorded.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (No. 2019‐KY‐294) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with all participants providing written informed consent.

Motor function was assessed using the Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS‐UPDRS‐III) and subtyped via tremor/postural instability‐gait difficulty (PIGD) scores. Disease stage was classified as early‐stage (Hoehn and Yahr [H–Y] stage 1–2.5) or advanced‐stage (H–Y stage 3–5), comprising 87 and 21 patients, respectively. Non‐motor symptoms were assessed using the Non‐Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS), and cognitive impairment (CI) was defined as a score of < 26 on the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) at enrollment [29]. REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) was screened using the RBD Questionnaire‐Hong Kong (RBDQ‐HK) due to the limited availability of polysomnography (the gold standard for RBD diagnosis). The RBDQ‐HK is a validated self‐reported tool with good diagnostic performance for probable RBD in Chinese populations at a cutoff score of 17 [30], and participants scoring ≥ 17 were classified as suspected RBD. Neurogenic orthostatic hypotension (OH) was diagnosed as a ≥ 20/10 mmHg decrease in blood pressure within 3 min of standing/head‐up tilt without significant heart rate change [31].

Skin Biopsy

All participants underwent 3‐mm punch biopsies at the posterior cervical region (C7, 2 cm lateral to the C7 vertebra) and distal leg (DL, 10 cm proximal to the lateral malleolus). Among the 108 patients with PD, the first 59 provided paired samples from both sites for immunostaining and syn‐SAA. No inter‐site differences in α‐synuclein seeding activity were detected (Table S3), consistent with a previous two‐laboratory study [32]. Given that the most proximal (C7) site yields slightly higher diagnostic accuracy [11, 32], we optimized the protocol accordingly. Thus, the remaining 49 patients and all 60 controls provided two C7 samples (one for immunostaining and one for syn‐SAA) and one DL sample (for immunostaining only).

Immunostaining

Cutaneous p‐Syn Detection

In accordance with a previously published protocol [33, 34], skin samples were immediately fixed in Zamboni fixative solution for 18–24 h and subsequently immersed in cryoprotectant overnight at 4°C. The tissue blocks were cut into 50‐μm sections using a cryostat (CM1950, Leica, Germany). Immunostaining was performed on every sixth section, with at least four sections analyzed per specimen. Double immunofluorescence staining was performed overnight using primary antibodies against protein gene product 9.5 (PGP9.5) (1:6000, mouse, Bio‐Rad, USA) and p‐syn (1:800, rabbit, Abcam, UK). The secondary antibodies used included anti‐mouse Alexa Fluor 594 (1:600, OriGene, USA) and anti‐rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:800, OriGene, USA). Sections were imaged using fluorescence microscopy (Nikon ECLIPSE Ni‐U, Tokyo, Japan) with a high‐magnification objective (40×). Immunostaining analysis revealed that p‐syn‐positive fibers were colocalized with PGP9.5‐positive nerve fibers, thereby confirming their presence. The analysis was independently conducted by two blinded examiners, who reached a complete consensus on the positive criteria.

Cutaneous p‐Syn Quantification

The number of positive results for each dermal structure in each tissue section was recorded, and p‐syn deposition was quantified for each biopsy site as follows: (1) Sectional distribution—scoring 1/4 per positive section (0–1 range) across four cryosections per specimen; (2) Structural involvement—recording deposits in nerve fibers across five distinct structures (subepidermal neural plexuses, dermal nerve bundles, sweat glands, blood vessels, and arrector pili muscles) with a score of 1/5 per affected structure (0–1 range). The diffusion coefficient (DC) was calculated as follows: DC=∑Positive sections4×∑Affected structures5

Quantitative analysis of p‐syn was performed at each biopsy site, and the overall DC was calculated as the average across the two sites for each participant.

Seeding Activity Assay

Preparation of Skin Samples

Skin punch biopsy samples (approximately 25 mg each, 3 × 3 mm) included the epidermal and dermal layers and were stored at −80°C. Each sample was prepared separately and washed three times with Tris‐buffered saline (TBS). The net weight of each sample was determined, and a 10% (w/v) skin homogenate was prepared in lysis buffer containing 2 mM calcium chloride and 0.25% (w/v) collagenase A (Roche) dissolved in TBS. The homogenates were incubated at 37°C with oscillation at 350 rpm for 4 h, followed by grinding using a bullet blender (JXF STPRP‐24) at 65 Hz for 2 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 500 g for 5 min at 5°C. The resulting supernatant was collected and stored at −80°C for subsequent Syn‐SAA.

Syn‐SAA

The seeding activity of α‐syn aggregates was assessed using real‐time quaking‐induced conversion (RT‐QuIC) analysis of skin samples, as previously described [35], with minor modifications. The reaction mixture consisted of 40 mM phosphate buffer (pH 8.0), 170 mM NaCl, 0.1 mg/mL recombinant human wild‐type α‐synuclein, 20 μM thioflavin T (ThT), and 0.001% sodium dodecyl sulfate. For each reaction, 98 μL of the α‐synuclein RT‐QuIC mixture, six 0.8 mm silica beads, and 2 μL of skin homogenate (seed material) were added to individual wells of a 96‐well plate. The plates were sealed with plate sealer film (Thermo Fisher) and incubated at 42°C in a BMG FLUOstar Omega plate reader under the following cyclic conditions: 1 min of shaking (400 rpm double orbital) followed by 1 min of rest, repeated for 60 h. ThT fluorescence was measured every 90 min using excitation and emission wavelengths of 450 ± 10 nm and 480 ± 10 nm, respectively, with values expressed in relative fluorescence units (RFU). The positivity threshold was set as the mean fluorescence of negative controls plus three standard deviations (SDs), with each sample tested in quadruplicate. A sample was considered positive if ≥ 2/4 replicates exceeded the threshold, and a participant was defined as positive if at least one biopsy site tested positive. When both sites were positive, the specimen with the highest ThT fluorescence intensity was selected. For syn‐SAA‐positive samples, the following kinetic parameters were derived from the ThT fluorescence curves by calculating the mean values across positive replicates:

Fmax: Maximum ThT fluorescence intensity.

Tlag: Lag phase duration (time to fluorescence threshold).

T50: Time to reach 50% of Fmax.

Vmax: Maximum slope of fluorescence amplification.

AUC: Total fluorescence activity (area under the curve).

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 21.0 (IBM, USA). Data are presented as means ± SD or percentages. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent t‐test (for normally distributed data) or the Mann–Whitney U test (for non‐normally distributed data). Comparisons involving multiple groups were performed using one‐way ANOVA with LSD post hoc test for normally distributed data, and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test (for expected cell counts < 5). Bonferroni correction was applied to two sets of outcomes: (1) p‐syn positivity rate and DC (2 outcomes × 12 comparisons each; α = 0.05/12≈0.00417); and (2) syn‐SAA positivity rate and kinetic parameters (Fmax, Tlag, T50, Vmax, AUC) (6 outcomes × 5 comparisons; α = 0.05/30≈0.00167). For both sets, p‐value below the respective threshold was considered statistically significant.

Using clinical diagnosis as the reference standard, we calculated sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy for each assay, and used Cohen's kappa to assess concordance between p‐syn immunostaining and syn‐SAA. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was further performed to evaluate the overall diagnostic performance of each test and to assess the added value of combining both assays, independent of predefined qualitative cutoffs, with the AUC and 95% CI calculated accordingly. Correlations between disease variables and both p‐syn DC and syn‐SAA parameters were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation analyses, as appropriate for the data distribution. For all other analyses not involving these multiple comparisons, statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Cutaneous p‐Syn Detected by Immunostaining in Patients With PD

All participants underwent skin biopsies at two sites (C7 and DL). Immunostaining analysis demonstrated p‐syn colocalization with PGP9.5 in 75% (81/108) of patients with PD, showing characteristic dot‐like and linear morphologies. In contrast, no such signals were detected in any controls. The p‐syn deposits were predominantly observed in the subepidermal neural plexus, dermal nerve bundles, sweat glands, blood vessels, and arrector pili muscles, but were absent in intraepidermal nerve fibers (Figure 1).

Diagnostic Efficacy of Cutaneous p‐Syn Immunostaining Versus Syn‐SAA in PD

ROC curve analysis showed that both p‐syn immunostaining and syn‐SAA achieved high diagnostic accuracy for PD: p‐syn immunostaining had an AUC of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.822–0.928; sensitivity 75%, specificity 100%; p < 0.001), and syn‐SAA had an AUC of 0.897 (95% CI: 0.844–0.950; sensitivity 86.1%, specificity 93.3%; p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed between the two AUC values (p = 0.485). Combining both assays yielded an AUC of 0.978 (95% CI: 0.957–0.999, p < 0.001), indicating improved diagnostic accuracy (Figure 3, Table S1). The two assays showed concordant results in 128 of 168 cases (76.2%), with a Cohen's kappa of 0.526 (p < 0.001), indicating moderate agreement (Table S2). Discordant cases were predominantly p‐syn negative/syn‐SAA positive (28/168, 16.7%).

The present findings revealed that p‐syn deposition was significantly correlated with the severity of clinical symptoms in patients with PD. Compared with p‐syn‐negative patients, p‐syn‐positive patients exhibited significantly higher H‐Y stages (2.4 ± 0.5 vs. 2.0 ± 0.6, p = 0.003), NMSS scores (40.5 ± 30.6 vs. 21.1 ± 18.5, p < 0.001), and lower MMSE scores (21.2 ± 5.2 vs. 23.9 ± 5.2, p = 0.010). No significant intergroup differences were observed in sex, age, disease duration, or motor severity (Table 3).

Analysis of Syn‐SAA Parameters in PD Subtype

The α‐synuclein seeding activity was analyzed in the C7 and DL sites of 59 patients with PD. No significant differences in syn‐SAA positivity rates were observed between these sites (64.4% vs. 78.0%, p = 0.104). Similarly, comparative analysis of syn‐SAA parameters (Fmax, Tlag, T50, Vmax, and AUC) revealed no statistically significant differences between the two sites (all p > 0.05; Table S3). Syn‐SAA‐positive patients had higher NMMS scores than syn‐SAA‐negative patients (38.1 ± 30.3 vs. 20.8 ± 14.1, p = 0.028), whereas no significant differences were observed in sex, age, disease duration, MDS‐UPDRS‐III score, H–Y stage, or MMSE score (Table 3). Stratified analysis by PD clinical subtypes revealed no statistically significant differences in syn‐SAA positivity rates or any of these kinetic parameters across disease severity (H‐Y stage), non‐motor symptom profiles (including RBD, OH, or CI), or motor subtypes after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (all p > 0.00167; Table 4).

Correlation of Syn‐SAA Parameters With PD Severity and Disease Progression

Correlation analyses revealed significant associations between syn‐SAA parameters and clinical metrics (Figure 4B). Disease duration correlated negatively with Tlag (ρ = −0.221, p = 0.033) and T50 (ρ = −0.224, p = 0.031) but correlated positively with Vmax (ρ = 0.248, p = 0.017) and AUC (ρ = 0.218, p = 0.036). The H–Y stage demonstrated a strong negative correlation with T50 (ρ = −0.390, p < 0.001). The NMSS score was inversely associated with Tlag (ρ = −0.236, p = 0.023) and T50 (ρ = −0.307, p = 0.003). In cognitive assessments (MMSE), Fmax (ρ = −0.216, p = 0.038) and AUC (ρ = −0.253, p = 0.015) showed negative correlations with scores, whereas Tlag (ρ = 0.257, p = 0.013) and T50 (ρ = 0.249, p = 0.016) showed positive correlations. Notably, no kinetic parameters were associated with motor severity (MDS‐UPDRS‐III score; all p > 0.05). These results indicate that syn‐SAA parameters (e.g., Tlag and T50) are associated with disease progression, non‐motor symptoms, and cognitive function.

Correlations of p‐Syn Diffusion Coefficients With Syn‐SAA Parameters in PD

Correlations between DC and syn‐SAA parameters were analyzed in 59 patients with PD who provided both C7 and DL samples for immunostaining and syn‐SAA (Figure 4C). At the C7 site, Vmax correlated positively with DC (ρ = 0.428, p = 0.007), but no other parameters showed significant associations. At the DL site, DC correlated positively with Fmax (ρ = 0.436, p = 0.002) and AUC (ρ = 0.488, p < 0.001), and negatively with Tlag (ρ = −0.535, p < 0.001) and T50 (ρ = −0.468, p = 0.001). Vmax showed a marginally positive correlation with DC at the DL site (ρ = 0.298, p = 0.044).

Discussion

Accurately diagnosing PD in living patients remains challenging. With brain biopsy impractical, reliable detection of pathological α‐synuclein in peripheral tissues is essential. CSF syn‐SAA offers high diagnostic accuracy for PD (83.9% vs. clinical diagnosis alone, rising to 93.6% when combined with DAT‐SPECT) [36], but lumbar puncture is invasive, unsuitable for routine or serial testing, and contraindicated in some patients (e.g., those on anticoagulation). DAT‐SPECT also faces resource constraints. Autonomic symptoms, reflecting early postganglionic sympathetic damage, often precede motor manifestations in PD, and the skin—with its extensive autonomic innervation—serves as a valuable target for detecting pathological α‐synuclein aggregates, making skin punch biopsy a minimally invasive alternative.

However, the diagnostic utility of p‐syn immunostaining and syn‐SAA in skin biopsies remains uncertain. To address this, we directly compared both assays in skin biopsies from the same PD cohort. Both assays demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy in detecting pathological α‐synuclein in patients with PD. Syn‐SAA showed a sensitivity of 86.1% and specificity of 93.3%, consistent with a previous report that skin RT‐QuIC achieved diagnostic accuracy comparable to CSF RT‐QuIC [37], supporting skin punch biopsy as a valid alternative to CSF analysis. In contrast, p‐syn immunostaining achieved a sensitivity of 75% and a specificity of 100%, as it was negative in all control subjects. The two assays showed moderate concordance, with discordant cases predominantly exhibiting a p‐syn negative/syn‐SAA positive pattern, consistent with previous reports [12]. This discordance likely arises from fundamental differences in detection principles: p‐syn immunostaining visualizes established phosphorylated aggregates, reflecting local pathological burden, whereas syn‐SAA detects trace amounts of α‐synuclein seeding activity, potentially before extensive phosphorylation occurs. Furthermore, unphosphorylated α‐synuclein aggregates in patient skin may be detected solely by syn‐SAA via their prion‐like seeding activity, but not by p‐syn immunostaining using phosphorylation‐specific antibodies [12]. Notably, positive syn‐SAA results in individuals without a clinical diagnosis of synucleinopathy may reflect the presence of α‐synuclein co‐pathology, as documented in Alzheimer's disease and other tauopathies [38, 39]. Alternatively, they may indicate a clinical prodromal stage or undetected early pathology of synucleinopathy [40]. This functional complementarity positions p‐syn immunostaining as a confirmatory diagnostic tool and syn‐SAA as a sensitive screening test for individuals with subtle or prodromal features.

Given the clinical heterogeneity of PD, a single biomarker cannot fully capture the disease. Therefore, further exploration of both assays as quantitative indicators of disease severity and subtype stratification is warranted. To this end, we analyzed the effects of clinical subtypes and scales on p‐syn distribution and syn‐SAA parameters. In the suspected RBD subgroup, p‐syn positivity was 75% at the DL versus 45% at the C7, and p‐syn DC was significantly higher at the DL than at the C7; no significant site‐to‐site differences were observed in the non‐RBD subgroup. These findings are compatible with the brain‐first versus body‐first subtype hypothesis of PD [15, 41, 42]. In the body‐first (RBD‐positive) subtype, α‐synuclein pathology originates in the peripheral nervous system and spreads centrally, leading to a distal‐predominant (DL > C7) pattern, consistent with the length‐dependent vulnerability of autonomic axons. In the brain‐first (RBD‐negative) subtype, a proximal‐dominant (C7 > DL) gradient is expected [43]. Although Liu et al. detected this gradient using the same RBDQ‐HK classification in a larger, more homogeneous cohort, its absence in our non‐RBD subgroup likely reflects limited statistical power and clinical heterogeneity, not a contradiction to the brain‐first framework. These findings indicate that the site‐specific burden of p‐syn deposition varies across clinical subtypes and anatomical sites, directly reflecting the clinical heterogeneity of PD and providing information that may assist in subtype differentiation, particularly for the RBD subgroup. In contrast, syn‐SAA showed no significant differences in positivity rate or kinetic parameters between the two sites, consistent with its detection principle that trace seeding activity may rapidly disseminate across peripheral sites, diminishing regional differences, and thus did not differentiate PD patients across clinical subtypes or capture clinical heterogeneity.

The p‐syn‐positive PD patients had more advanced H‐Y stages, higher NMSS scores, and lower MMSE scores than p‐syn‐negative patients. Cutaneous p‐syn DC at the DL, C7, and their average (mean DC) correlated positively with NMSS, H‐Y stage, and UPDRS‐III, and negatively with MMSE. Notably, all correlations were consistently stronger at the DL than at the C7 site. The positive associations with disease stage (H‐Y) and motor severity (UPDRS‐III) are consistent with previous immunostaining studies, which reported significant correlations between cutaneous p‐syn burden and both measures [1, 44]. The link between higher p‐syn burden and worse non‐motor symptoms is supported by the correlation of cutaneous p‐syn deposition with autonomic dysfunction [44], a major non‐motor component. Regarding CI, our observed negative correlation aligns with a previous study showing that baseline skin α‐synuclein oligomer load independently predicts longitudinal MMSE decline [45]. The stronger clinical correlations at the DL than at the C7 may be explained by the length‐dependent vulnerability of autonomic nerve fibers, as longer axons in the distal limb are more susceptible to retrograde accumulation of misfolded α‐synuclein. Direct evidence from patients with PD shows length‐dependent small‐fiber loss (both somatic and autonomic, with greater loss at distal sites, consistent with a dying‐back pattern), and patients with higher p‐syn load exhibit more severe small‐fiber loss [46]. Robust clinical correlations have been established using DL biopsies [44]. Quantitative immunostaining of cutaneous p‐syn, particularly at the DL site, may serve as a valuable biomarker for assessing disease severity across motor, non‐motor, and cognitive domains in PD.

Enhanced seeding activity correlated with longer disease duration, higher H‐Y stage, more severe non‐motor symptoms (higher NMSS), and worse cognitive performance (lower MMSE), but not with motor severity (UPDRS‐III). Consistent with prior reports [8, 32], longer disease duration and higher H‐Y stage were associated with increased peripheral seeding activity, supporting the progressive accumulation of misfolded α‐synuclein seeds. This finding aligns with a CSF study linking seeding activity to cognitive decline [47], and with cutaneous studies linking it to both cognitive deficits and non‐motor symptoms [32]. A large PPMI CSF study reported no association between baseline syn‐SAA parameters and longitudinal UPDRS‐III changes [48], consistent with our finding. However, that study additionally found no associations with non‐motor or cognitive progression, which differs from our positive correlations in these domains. This discrepancy likely arises from differences in biological matrices: CSF syn‐SAA reflects central seeding activity, while cutaneous syn‐SAA directly detects peripheral autonomic nerve pathology. Given that non‐motor symptoms and CI are closely linked to peripheral α‐synuclein accumulation, cutaneous syn‐SAA may be more sensitive to these clinical features than CSF syn‐SAA. Thus, cutaneous syn‐SAA kinetics may reflect peripheral pathology linked to non‐motor and cognitive deficits, but not motor symptom severity, which is primarily driven by central dopaminergic degeneration. Given its correlation with disease stage and certain non‐motor symptoms, seeding activity may serve as a potential indicator of disease progression.

We analyzed the relationship between cutaneous p‐syn DC and syn‐SAA parameters at two sites (C7 and DL). At the DL, DC correlated with all syn‐SAA parameters, whereas at C7, DC correlated only with Vmax. This regional discrepancy may be explained by the length‐dependent vulnerability of autonomic axons and the peripheral‐to‐central propagation model of PD, though independent validation is lacking. These findings are descriptive in nature and require confirmation in future studies with larger cohorts and mechanistic approaches.

Our study has several limitations. First, diagnoses were clinical without autopsy confirmation. Incorporating well‐validated objective biomarkers (e.g., CSF syn‐SAA or DAT‐SPECT) could reduce diagnostic uncertainty and improve the precision of future studies. Second, our sampling strategy may have underestimated diagnostic accuracy. To minimize patient burden, a single‐site (C7) syn‐SAA protocol was applied in some patients, potentially missing the patchy distribution of α‐synuclein aggregates. Third, genetic testing for monogenic PD was not performed. Therefore, despite negative clinical screening for family history and known mutations, undiagnosed monogenic forms cannot be excluded. This is relevant, as genetic background influences pathological features: cutaneous p‐syn deposition differs between non‐carriers and patients with LRRK2, GBA, or SNCA mutations [49], and CSF syn‐SAA positivity varies across these genetic subtypes [40]. Fourth, cognitive assessment was limited to the MMSE (low sensitivity for mild CI in PD) and RBD assessment lacked polysomnographic confirmation, both potentially introducing misclassification. Future studies should employ more sensitive cognitive assessments (e.g., Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA]) and polysomnography to validate our findings. Fifth, the cross‐sectional design limits analysis of the temporal dynamics of α‐synuclein pathology and neurodegeneration. Recent evidence suggests that SAA kinetic measures in CSF can predict longitudinal clinical decline in PD [50]. Therefore, longitudinal studies of cutaneous p‐syn deposition and syn‐SAA kinetics are needed to validate these peripheral biomarkers as minimally invasive predictors of disease progression and to map spatiotemporal propagation patterns of α‐synuclein across PD subtypes.

In conclusion, both cutaneous p‐syn immunostaining and syn‐SAA offer high diagnostic accuracy for PD, supporting the clinical use of minimally invasive skin biopsy. Notably, p‐syn deposition reflects regional α‐synuclein aggregation and correlates with clinical heterogeneity, supporting phenotype‐specific diagnosis and assisting in disease severity assessment, whereas α‐synuclein seeding activity provides superior early diagnostic sensitivity and may help monitor disease progression. These complementary signatures highlight the need to explore cutaneous α‐synuclein patterns across PD subtypes to guide phenotype‐specific diagnosis, severity evaluation, and progression monitoring.
