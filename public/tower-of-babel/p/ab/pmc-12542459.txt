Carnosic acid alleviated periodontitis by inhibiting ferroptosis via the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway
Authors: Jiatong Zou, Rui Wang, Jiahui Yu, Xiantao Chen, Qi Zhao, Yushen Li, Yifei Dai, Jiajun Xin, Bowei Wang, Zhihui Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis, caused by abnormal iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation, has been linked to pathogenic processes in several disorders. Its function and regulating mechanisms in periodontitis are still unclear, nevertheless. As a naturally derived phenolic diterpenoid molecule, carnosic acid (CA) serves multiple biological roles, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and cytoprotective properties. Its potential for intervention in periodontitis and ferroptosis warrants further exploration.MethodsIn this investigation, we combined network pharmacology analysis with in vitro and in vivo experimental validation to systematically evaluate the mechanism of action of CA intervention in periodontitis. Through an intersectional analysis of drug targets, ferroptosis-related genes, and periodontitis-related genes, potential core targets were identified, and GO/KEGG enrichment analysis was performed. The results suggest that Nrf2 is at the core of the protein interaction network and is significantly enriched in antioxidant response and iron homeostasis regulation pathways. Subsequently, changes in ROS, MDA, GSH, SOD, Fe²⁺, and other indicators, as well as the expression of ferroptosis-related indicators (GPX4, SLC7A11, FTH1), were detected in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cell models and rat periodontal ligation models. The key role of Nrf2 was verified using the Nrf2-specific inhibitor ML385.ResultsNetwork pharmacology results indicate that ferroptosis is crucial in the potential mechanism of CA action on periodontitis. Nrf2 is the core regulatory molecule connecting CA, periodontitis, and ferroptosis. The experimental results revealed that CA dramatically lowered ROS and MDA levels in cells and periodontal tissues, inhibited the accumulation of Fe²⁺, elevated the contents of GSH and SOD, and GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 expression. Mechanistic studies have found that CA restores the antioxidant and iron homeostasis regulatory system by activating the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis, thereby inhibiting the vicious cycle of ferroptosis. After Nrf2 was blocked by ML385, the anti-ferroptosis and anti-inflammatory effects of CA were significantly weakened.ConclusionThe current research is the first to elucidate that CA can alleviate periodontitis pathological damage by suppressing ferroptosis via activating the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis. This enriches the pharmacological action spectrum of CA and provides new targets and a theoretical basis for periodontitis intervention strategies based on ferroptosis regulation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07048-1.Keywords: Periodontitis, Carnosic acid, Ferroptosis, Nrf2, Network pharmacology

Introduction

Periodontitis is an inflammatory disease that primarily affects the tissues supporting the teeth. It has risen to become the sixth most common chronic condition, impacting approximately 19% of adults worldwide [1]. The progression of periodontitis is thought to be driven by a combination of inflammatory and immune responses induced by periodontal pathogens [2]. Extensive clinical and scientific studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress plays a key role in the pathogenesis of periodontitis [3]. It not only triggers a localized inflammatory response [4] but also destroys the periodontal tissue structure [5], ultimately leading to the resorption of the alveolar bone [6]. Currently, antibiotics are commonly used in clinical practice to treat periodontitis. However, it is impossible to avoid causing adverse consequences, including the development of resistance in the causative organisms, the occurrence of toxic drug reactions, and suppression of immune function [7]. As a result, finding innovative therapeutic procedures is crucial to improve outcomes for periodontal patients and to minimize the side effects of existing therapies.

Oxidative stress affects the onset and development of periodontitis. Studies have found that oxidative stress not only triggers local inflammatory responses but also promotes lipid peroxidation and triggers ferroptosis, further exacerbating periodontal tissue destruction and local inflammatory responses [8]. The mechanism of ferroptosis is mainly iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, leading to the accumulation of lipid peroxides, disruption of cellular metabolism, and ultimately cell death [9]. In periodontitis lesions, iron ion levels were significantly higher than in healthy tissues. This offers circumstantial proof that ferroptosis plays a part in the etiology of periodontitis [10, 11]. Furthermore, it was found that activation of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) inhibited ferroptosis in oral epithelial cells and alleviated tissue destruction caused by Porphyromonas gingivalis infection [12]. This implies that ferroptosis could be a novel target for periodontitis intervention.

The activity of GPX4, a key antioxidant enzyme for scavenging intracellular ROS and inhibiting phospholipid peroxidation, is dependent on glutathione (GSH) [13]. When GPX4 activity is reduced or GSH levels are lowered, lipid peroxide accumulation and the Fenton reaction further promote ferroptosis [14]. The regulation of ferroptosis also involves several factors. SLC7A11 acts as the core unit of the system Xc⁻, mediating cystine uptake for GSH synthesis and providing essential reducing substrates for GPX4 [15]. In addition, ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), which maintains iron homeostasis and reduces free iron accumulation, can block lipid peroxidation driven by the Fenton reaction [16, 17]. Together, these factors form the core defense against ferroptosis. Meanwhile, Nrf2, as an essential modulator of cellular antioxidative stress, contributes to the prevention of ferroptosis by activating downstream antioxidant genes such as GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 [18]. In chronic inflammatory disorders like periodontitis, the Nrf2 pathway is thought to be a potential defense against oxidative stress and ferroptosis [19].

The pathological features of periodontitis mainly include enhanced oxidative stress, disturbed iron homeostasis, and persistent chronic inflammatory response, which together drive periodontal tissue destruction. According to earlier research, modulation of oxidative stress levels and ferroptosis pathways can effectively alleviate inflammatory injury in periodontal tissue. Therefore, finding interventions that possess antioxidant, iron homeostasis regulation, and anti-inflammatory properties may become a new strategy for periodontitis treatment. Carnosic acid (CA) is a phenolic diterpene compound commonly found in plants of the Labiatae family [20]. Studies have shown that CA exhibits significant antioxidant [21] and anti-inflammatory [22] properties in various disease models and can regulate the ferroptosis process by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway [23]. CA has also been proven to have good anti-inflammatory effects in chronic inflammatory illnesses like pneumonia [24], arthritis [25], and cardiac inflammation [26]. It is noteworthy that periodontitis shares many common pathological mechanisms with the above diseases, including oxidative stress, inflammatory response, and ferroptosis. Studies have found that CA treatment can inhibit inflammatory factor IL-27 stimulation in oral epithelial cells, preliminarily revealing the potential of CA to intervene in periodontitis [27]. However, there is currently a lack of research evidence on whether CA can improve the three pathological features of periodontitis through Nrf2-mediated ferroptosis regulation. Based on this, this study will combine network pharmacology analysis and in vitro and in vivo experimental evidence to probe the potential effect of CA in regulating oxidative stress and ferroptosis mechanisms in periodontitis. It is hoped that this will provide theoretical support and target evidence for related drug development (Fig. 1).

Results

Prediction of CA targets and functional enrichment analysis reveal its potential ferroptosis regulatory mechanism

As indicated in Fig. 2A, the PubChem database was used to get basic information on CA, including its PubChem CID, CAS number, CANONICAL SMILES, 2D and 3D structures. Based on a combined search of literature and databases, a total of 120 potential targets for CA were screened and identified. To further clarify the interactions between CA targets, a protein interaction network was constructed using the STRING database and imported into Cytoscape 3.10.0 for visualization analysis. As seen in Fig. 2B, the “compound-targets” network contains a total of 121 nodes (including CA and its 120 targets) and 1,045 connecting edges, with an average node connectivity of 17.273. This suggests that CA has complex interactions with multiple key targets and has broad biological regulatory potential.

A Sankey diagram shows the relationship between enriched functions and their corresponding targets. Bubble diagram shows significantly enriched molecular function items, protein–protein interaction (PPI) intensity, and significance levels.

To investigate the functional characteristics of these targets, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed based on the STRING platform. The results revealed that CA targets were significantly enriched in multiple key molecular functional items (Fig. 2D), such as bradykinin receptor binding, JUN kinase activity, AMP deaminase activity, protein serine/threonine kinase binding, ICAM-3 receptor activity, and so on. Fig. 2C displays the enrichment relationship between each functional item and specific genes. The above enrichment results suggest that CA might be crucial for cellular stress responses, signal transduction, inflammation regulation, and metabolic homeostasis, all of which are important components of the mechanism of ferroptosis. According to earlier research, ferroptosis mainly involves processes such as polyunsaturated fatty acid metabolism, GSH depletion, GPX4 inactivation, and iron ion homeostasis disruption. Among these, signaling pathways such as AMPK, JNK, and TP53 participate in oxidative stress sensing and cell death regulation.

This study identified multiple core genes closely related to ferroptosis among CA targets, including TP53, MAPK8, MAPK9, PPARG, JUN, and others. For example, TP53 affects the intracellular antioxidant system by regulating SLC7A11/GPX4. The MAPK pathway can participate in the production of lipid peroxides. PPARG regulates lipid metabolism and ROS levels. JUN kinase is essential for oxidative stress signal transduction. The aforementioned findings raise the possibility that CA may intervene in ferroptosis-related biological processes to a certain extent by regulating the above-mentioned key targets and their related pathways, thereby affecting the survival status of cells in an inflammatory environment. These results provide theoretical support and target evidence for subsequent verification of the therapeutic role of CA in periodontitis through the regulation of ferroptosis.

Screening and analysis of potential targets of CA on periodontitis

To identify potential targets of CA in regulating the ferroptosis process in periodontitis, this study integrated information from multiple databases. From OMIM, DrugBank, GeneCards, and PharmGkb, 1,827 periodontitis-related targets were identified (Fig. 3A). 407 ferroptosis-related targets were found by searching the FerrDb database. Combining the predicted targets of CA, the intersection of the three was identified, and a Venn diagram was plotted (Fig. 3B). The results showed that there were 8 intersecting genes: NFE2L2 (NRF2), TP53, JUN, PTEN, MAPK8, SIRT1, MDM2, and FABP4. This suggests that these genes may be the core target genes for CA intervention in periodontitis ferroptosis.

B Venn diagram of the targets of CA, periodontitis, and ferroptosis. And the PPI of intersecting genes. C GO annotations are divided into three categories: BP, CC, and MF. D KEGG pathway analysis.

The PPI network constructed based on the STRING database (Fig. 3B) shows that NFE2L2 is closely associated with key regulatory genes such as TP53, JUN, SIRT1, and MAPK8 in the network and is located at the core hub position. The transcription factor Nrf2, encoded by NFE2L2, has been widely reported to be a negative regulator of ferroptosis. It prevents lipid peroxidation and iron-dependent cell death by activating the transcription of antioxidant genes, including GPX4 and SLC7A11. Combining the common features of oxidative stress, iron ion deposition, and lipid peroxidation in periodontitis lesions, it is suggested that CA may exert a protective effect by activating the Nrf2 signal, enhancing antioxidant capacity, and reducing lipid ROS accumulation.

To further reveal the functional characteristics of the intersection targets, they were imported into the DAVID database for GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genome (KEGG) enrichment analysis. GO analysis yielded a total of 58 biological process (BP) entries, 12 cell component (CC) entries, and 17 molecular function (MF) entries (Fig. 3C). 59 signaling pathways were identified in the KEGG enrichment analysis, which mainly involved pathways in cancer, endocrine resistance, atherosclerosis, etc. (Fig. 3D). It is worth noting that these targets are significantly enriched in processes and pathways closely related to oxidative damage, such as “response to oxidative stress,” “cellular response to hypoxia,” and “chemical carcinogenesis–reactive oxygen species.” At the same time, the p53, Wnt, FoxO, and other signaling pathways enriched in this study all have cross-regulatory relationships with Nrf2, further suggesting that Nrf2 may be a key node in this regulatory network.

Based on the comprehensive analysis results and previous studies, it is speculated that Nrf2 is the core factor in CA regulating ferroptosis in periodontitis. Therefore, selecting it as the key target for subsequent research has a clear theoretical basis and research value.

CA mitigated LPS‑induced injury in RAW264.7 cells

First, we evaluated how CA affected the viability of RAW264.7 cells at different concentrations and intervention times. It was found that CA at concentrations of 30 µM and below had no discernible impact on cell survival with short-term (12 h) intervention (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4A). However, after the intervention was prolonged to 24 h, CA at 20 µM and above began to show significant cytotoxic effects (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4B). On this basis, the intervention effect of CA on lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (1 µg/mL)-induced RAW264.7 cell damage was further explored. After pretreatment with CA for 4 h, LPS stimulation was performed. The results showed that 1 µM and 5 µM CA had no statistical significance in improving cell viability. However, the reduction in cell viability brought on by LPS was successfully reversed by 10 µM CA (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4C-D). Based on this, to ensure that the drug acted within the safe range, the intervention concentration of 10µM was chosen for further tests. The LDH release assay also confirmed that LPS stimulation significantly increased cell membrane permeability, leading to a large amount of LDH release. Whereas after CA pretreatment, the level of LDH release was significantly reduced (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4E). It suggests that the cell membrane damage was alleviated. In addition, the ELISA assay further revealed that LPS stimulation prompted a massive secretion of inflammatory factors TNF-α and IL-6, reflecting a dramatic activation of the inflammatory response. However, the above-mentioned inflammatory factor levels were significantly reduced by the intervention of CA, suggesting a good anti-inflammatory potential (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4F-G).

Taken together, these results indicate that CA not only protects RAW264.7 cells from LPS-induced viability damage and cell membrane damage at non-cytotoxic concentrations. It also demonstrated a favorable regulatory effect in preventing the release of inflammatory factors, which provided a preliminary experimental basis for its potential anti-inflammatory and protective properties.

CA alleviated LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells ferroptosis

To determine how CA regulates LPS-induced ferroptosis in RAW264.7 cells, we first examined the intracellular ROS levels. According to fluorescent probe DCFH-DA staining, LPS treatment dramatically increased intracellular ROS accumulation in RAW264.7 cells, whereas CA effectively alleviated LPS-induced cellular ROS levels (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5A). Further testing of oxidative stress-related indices showed that LPS stimulation significantly elevated the content of lipid peroxides, MDA, while dramatically lowering intracellular GSH and SOD levels. In contrast, the intervention by CA was able to significantly reverse the above changes, reduce MDA levels, and restore GSH and SOD content (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5B-D), suggesting that CA has a role in alleviating LPS-induced oxidative stress. In addition, the results of the ferric ion assay showed that LPS stimulation led to a notable rise in intracellular Fe²⁺ content, whereas CA successfully prevented Fe²⁺ accumulation caused by LPS stimulation (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5E). At the level of molecular mechanisms, results from RT-qPCR and WB demonstrated that mRNA and protein expression of GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 were significantly down-regulated by LPS stimulation. Whereas intervention by CA considerably restored the expression of the above molecules (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A-D). Furthermore, to clarify whether LPS-induced cell damage is primarily mediated by ferroptosis, we used ferroptosis-specific inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1). The findings showed that Fer-1 treatment markedly alleviated LPS-induced cell damage and reversed the downregulation of key ferroptosis molecules under inflammatory conditions (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6E-H). Notably, CA exhibited similar protective effects and molecular regulatory effects as Fer-1.

When combined, these results show that LPS triggers ferroptosis in RAW264.7 cells by inducing ROS accumulation, iron overload, and downregulating the expression of GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1. CA effectively alleviates LPS-induced cell damage by targeting and regulating this ferroptosis pathway. The rescue effect of Fer-1 further confirms that ferroptosis is the primary mechanism underlying LPS-induced cytotoxicity.

Nrf2 serves as the key target for CA in alleviating LPS-induced ferroptosis in RAW264.7 cells

To examine whether Nrf2 mediates the inhibitory impact of CA on ferroptosis, we conducted molecular docking experiments. The results indicated that CA and Nrf2 had a binding energy of −6.451 kcal/mol, indicating a strong binding affinity (Fig. 7A). According to three-dimensional structural modeling, the CA molecule was inside Nrf2’s binding sites. Furthermore, CA’s remarkable affinity for Nrf2 was highlighted by two-dimensional modeling, which showed that it generated hydrogen bonds and other non-covalent interactions with Nrf2 at the binding sites. Then, the Nrf2 expression and localization were examined by immunofluorescence. The findings demonstrated that LPS stimulation greatly prevented the nuclear translocation of Nrf2, while CA intervention effectively promoted Nrf2 nuclear translocation and increased its accumulation in the cell nucleus (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7B). RT-qPCR and WB results further revealed that CA dramatically restored the expression levels of Nrf2 under LPS stimulation (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7C-D). It is worth noting that Fer-1 treatment also significantly upregulated Nrf2 expression (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7I). These results imply that CA may prevent ferroptosis via the Nrf2 pathway, hence protecting cells.

To test this hypothesis, we further applied ML385, an Nrf2-specific inhibitor, to block the ability of CA to inhibit LPS-induced ferroptosis. CCK-8, LDH release, and ELISA assays showed that ML385 partially or completely counteracted the protective effect of CA against cell damage brought on by LPS (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8A-D). In terms of ferroptosis indexes, ML385 significantly reversed the inhibition of MDA production induced by CA under LPS, while reducing the restorative effect of GSH, SOD, and Fe²⁺ levels (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8E-H). Meanwhile, ML385 significantly reduced CA-upregulated GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 expression (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9A-D). In addition, in comparison to the CA + LPS group, the CA + LPS + ML385 group exhibited a considerable drop in Nrf2 mRNA, protein, and nucleoprotein levels (p < 0.05) (Fig. 9E-F). In summary, CA attenuates LPS-induced RAW264.7 cell injury by triggering the Nrf2 signaling pathway, enhancing antioxidant defense, and inhibiting ferroptosis. Nrf2 is a key molecular target for CA to exert its protective effects, and the reversal effect of ML385 further confirms this conclusion.

CA slows the progression of periodontitis in rats and mediates periodontal protection via Nrf2

We created a rat periodontitis model and evaluated the efficacy and safety of CA in vivo to further confirm its method of action. HE staining results revealed no significant morphological damage to the livers, hearts, lungs, spleens, and kidneys of the rats after CA and ML385 interventions, suggesting that the protocol has good biosafety (Supplementary Fig. 1). Micro-CT analysis showed CA treatment significantly improved the alveolar bone volume and structure when compared to the periodontitis model group. This was demonstrated by a reduction in bone resorption in the root bifurcation area and a decrease in alveolar bone resorption (Fig. 10B). Quantitative results showed that CA considerably decreased CEJ-ABC distance while increasing percent bone volume (BV/TV) and bone trabecular thickness (Tb. Th), and improving bone trabecular number (Tb. N) and bone trabecular separation (Tb. Sp) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 10C). Conversely, improvements in these bone parameters were partially or completely offset by combined ML385 treatment. HE staining further validated the above changes in bone tissue. The periodontal tissues of the model group showed a massive infiltration of inflammatory cells, especially neutrophils and macrophages aggregates, accompanied by severe tissue destruction (Fig. 11A). Inflammatory cell infiltration was significantly reduced, and periodontal structure was significantly improved after CA intervention. Masson staining results supported this observation, with the CA group showing attenuated collagen fiber degradation and enhanced collagen repair, while the combined ML385 weakened its repair effect. In summary, CA significantly attenuates periodontitis-associated bone destruction and soft tissue inflammation by activating the Nrf2 pathway. The antagonistic effect of ML385 further confirms that Nrf2 is a key target in CA-mediated periodontal protection.

CA alleviated ferroptosis in periodontitis rats through the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway

Further in vivo experiments verified the function of CA in suppressing ferroptosis via the Nrf2 pathway in periodontitis rats. As shown in Fig. 11B-D, CA intervention was able to significantly reduce the MDA level in the gingival tissues of periodontitis rats, as well as increase the GSH and SOD levels, thus effectively alleviating the oxidative stress induced by periodontitis (p < 0.05). Additionally, CA downregulated periodontitis-induced Fe²⁺ levels, suggesting that iron homeostasis disturbances were corrected (p < 0.05) (Fig. 11E). Immunohistochemistry and RT-qPCR results further confirmed that CA promoted the expression of Nrf2 and its downstream ferroptosis inhibitors GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1 in gingival tissues (p < 0.05) (Fig. 12A-E). These alterations suggest a clear in vivo anti-ferroptosis effect of CA. Notably, combining the Nrf2 inhibitor ML385 with CA dramatically reduced its protective impact against ferroptosis, in which MDA levels were elevated, GSH and SOD levels were reduced, and Fe²⁺ levels were re-upregulated, while Nrf2 and related protein expression were also markedly downregulated. At the same time, ML385 accelerated the pathologic progression of periodontitis. Taken together, these findings imply that CA may help slow down the progression of periodontitis in vivo by activating the Nrf2/GPX4 pathway and reducing ferroptosis.

Discussion

Periodontitis not only damages the tissues that support the teeth, impairs chewing ability, and lowers quality of life, but is also strongly linked to the emergence of other systemic diseases, posing a major risk to human health [28]. Despite the current understanding of the pathogenesis of periodontitis, clinical treatments are still limited, especially the lack of precise interventions targeting the pathogenesis. Therefore, the development of potential drugs with multiple targets that can alleviate inflammation and arrest the loss of periodontal tissues has significant significance and application.

This study combined network pharmacology and experimental validation to systematically evaluate the possible mechanisms of action of CA in the treatment of periodontitis. Network pharmacology analysis revealed a strong correlation between ferroptosis and CA intervention in periodontitis. Among the intersection targets, Nrf2 (NFE2L2) is not only a potential target of CA but also closely related to periodontitis and ferroptosis, and occupies a central position in the protein–protein interaction network. Recent investigations have found that iron ion concentrations in the serum of patients with periodontitis are significantly higher than healthy people [29]. Furthermore, GPX4 activity is significantly negatively correlated with clinical attachment loss in periodontitis [30]. In a Porphyromonas gingivalis infection model, Fer-1 can reverse the downward trend in osteogenic differentiation capacity [31]. These findings collectively support the presence of ferroptosis characteristics, such as oxidative stress, iron ion deposition, and lipid peroxidation, in periodontal disease lesions [32]. Given Nrf2’s central regulatory role in ferroptosis, we hypothesize that CA may alleviate ferroptosis-mediated tissue damage by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway, regulating the antioxidant system, and inhibiting lipid ROS accumulation. Therefore, this study focuses on Nrf2 as a key intervention target and explores the mechanisms surrounding Nrf2-mediated ferroptosis regulation.

The experimental results confirmed the above speculation. Abnormal activation of ferroptosis was observed in both the in vitro LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cell model and the in vivo ligature-induced periodontitis rat model. This was manifested by significantly elevated levels of ROS, MDA, and Fe²⁺, as well as downregulation of GSH, SOD, and expression of anti-ferroptosis factors like GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1. Among them, MDA is the result of lipid peroxidation, and its elevated level directly reflects the extent of oxidative damage to the cell membrane. When its levels are elevated, indicating the occurrence of ferroptosis [33]. GSH is an essential cofactor for lipid peroxidation scavenging by GPX4. Its depletion signals the collapse of the antioxidant defense system and is a sign of the initiation of ferroptosis [34]. SOD, as an antioxidant enzyme, is responsible for scavenging superoxide anion (O₂·⁻) and is the initial line of defense against ROS-mediated damage. Its reduced activity indicates increased cellular oxidative stress, which promotes ferroptosis [35].

Due to the anatomically isolated nature of the gingival pockets, periodontal disease is confined to the area of the pockets. And the flow rate of gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) in the periodontitis area is 40 times higher than in the healthy area [36]. For this reason, localized administration of adjunctive therapies is often used in clinical practice. Therefore, in the present study, we used in vivo local injection of CA delivery to overcome its low bioavailability for oral and intravenous administration [20]. In animals, 1 mg/kg CA is sufficient to activate Nrf2 to act as an anti-inflammatory agent [37]. The local drug delivery approach guarantees both a greater drug concentration in the periodontal pocket and the ability to direct the drug to the lesion site. It also circumvents drug resistance, dysbiosis, and general adverse effects that may be associated with systemic administration [38]. After CA intervention, these indicators improved significantly, while inflammatory cell infiltration and bone resorption in periodontal tissue were significantly reduced. Further mechanism validation showed that CA can restore the homeostasis of the antioxidant system and block the ferroptosis process by activating the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory and tissue-protective effects. The Nrf2-specific inhibitor ML385 therapy partially or totally negated the protective effects of CA, including a rebound in ferroptosis markers, exacerbated lipid peroxidation, and weakened antioxidant defense. This validated the central regulatory role of Nrf2 in the anti-inflammatory and anti-ferroptosis effects of CA.

In contrast to previous literature, the protective role of CA in neurological, cardiovascular [26], and inflammatory diseases [39] through the Nrf2 pathway has been documented. However, the study is the first to extend the molecular mechanism of CA-Nrf2-ferroptosis to the pathological regulation of periodontitis and reveals that CA coordinates triple protective effects in periodontitis through the Nrf2/GPX4 axis. It is worth noting that CA exhibits the following therapeutic advantages over traditional antioxidants or anti-inflammatory drugs: First, the commonly used antioxidant N-acetylcysteine is also an inhibitor of ferroptosis. However, its ability to directly remove ROS is limited. It mainly indirectly inhibits lipid peroxidation by maintaining GSH levels and cannot directly regulate iron storage or chelate free iron [40]. The phenolic structure of CA enables it to interact directly with ROS, particularly lipid-free radicals. It can also activate the Nrf2 hub to synchronously upregulate SLC7A11, FTH1, and GPX4. Thereby, CA can synergistically inhibit the core drivers of ferroptosis from multiple key sources. Secondly, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen can effectively relieve inflammation by inhibiting cyclooxygenases (COX), but they carry the risk of exacerbating oxidative stress [41]. CA not only reduces the release of pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-1β, but also enhances the antioxidant capacity of cells through Nrf2 activation, breaking the vicious cycle of “inflammation-oxidative stress-tissue destruction.” Finally, the experiment confirmed that CA significantly reduced alveolar bone resorption, and this bone protection effect is more advantageous than that of some natural compounds [42]. This suggests that CA may alter the pathological process of periodontitis rather than merely alleviating symptoms. These advantages enable CA to integrate and regulate multiple effects with a single drug. As a natural compound, CA is safer than synthetic Nrf2 activators such as bardoxolone, which carries risks of liver and kidney toxicity [43]. Additionally, by intervening at multiple targets, it reduces the risk of drug resistance, thereby exploring new intervention strategies for the precise treatment of periodontitis.

Although this study has systematically revealed the mechanism by which CA suppresses ferroptosis and improves periodontitis by activating the Nrf2/GPX4 signaling axis, there is still room for further improvement. Nrf2, as a crucial transcription factor for oxidative stress defense, has a large and complex downstream regulatory network. In addition to GPX4, molecules such as HO-1 and NQO1, as well as multiple signaling pathways, may synergistically mediate the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of CA. In addition, there is a possibility that Nrf2 interacts with signaling pathways such as PI3K/AKT and MAPK. Whether it plays a synergistic regulatory effect in CA intervention in periodontitis deserves further exploration. Conversely, ferroptosis is not a distinct mode of cell death, and its crossover and mutual regulation with apoptosis and autophagy have been demonstrated in a variety of diseases [44]. However, in periodontitis, a chronic inflammatory disease, the relationship between these modes of cell death and their dynamics under CA intervention still lacks systematic studies. The synergistic mechanism of several types of cell death in the development of periodontitis can be analyzed in depth by combining the technical means of multi-omics, single-cell sequencing, and gene editing. In addition, this study was based on macrophage versus rodent models, and the interventional effects of CA have been well validated. However, due to differences between rodent models and human pathological conditions, large animal models and preclinical studies are required to further assess the safety and effectiveness of CA. In addition, CA can be used as a therapeutic agent in local drug delivery (LDD) systems in the future. Enhance lesion enrichment through macrophage-targeted modification and extend effective concentration maintenance time through sustained-release technology. A new model of periodontal treatment that achieves “precise intervention, minimal side effects, and maximum efficacy” to promote its clinical application in periodontitis treatment.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of chemical structures and action targets of CA

PubChem [45] was utilized to obtain the relevant information on CA, such as 2D Structure, 3D Conformer, SMILES, CAS, etc. Then, target prediction was carried out by entering the required information about CA into SwissTargetPrediction, PharmMapper [46–48], Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA), and GeneCards. The obtained target information was combined, de-emphasized, and then standardized in Uniprot to obtain CA action targets.

Network analysis and enrichment analysis of CA targets of action

Create a protein interaction network for potential CA targets in the STRING database. Select “Multiple proteins”, import the predicted target information, and select “Homo sapiens” under “Organisms” for search. In the settings, select 0.4 as the minimum required interaction score. After obtaining the PPI data, export it as a TSV file. Subsequently, the file was imported into Cytoscape 3.10.0 software to construct and visualize the protein interaction network between CA and its targets. On this basis, the enrichment analysis function provided by the STRING platform was used to assess whether there is a direct or indirect association between CA-related targets and ferroptosis regulatory pathways, in order to further explore their potential mechanisms of action.

Acquisition of potential action targets of CA, periodontitis, and ferroptosis

Target searches were performed using Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM), DrugBank, GeneCards, and PharmGkb. Obtaining targets for periodontal disease through merging and deduplication. Download genes related to ferroptosis from the Ferrdb website and standardize them according to the options “Driver,” “Suppressor,” and “Marker”. Use the Venny online website to identify the intersection of CA, periodontitis, and ferroptosis as potential targets. Import the intersection target information into the STRING database, obtain PPI data, and export it.

Go analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis

Import potential targets into the David Bioinformatics platform. Choose “OFFICIAL_GENE_SYMBOL” as the identifier, “Homo sapiens” as the species, and “Gene list” specified as the list type. Next, tick the items related to GO analysis projects, including BP, CC, and MF, along with KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Lastly, the visualization was performed through the Wei Sheng Xin online platform.

Cell culture and treatment

The Mouse Mononuclear Macrophage Cells, designated as RAW264.7 (CBP60533), were bought from the Cell Bank in Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in an environment of DMEM (EallBio, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Procell, China).

To imitate inflammation, RAW264.7 cells were stimulated with LPS (1 µg/mL, Sigma, USA) for 24 h. CA intervention protocol: RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with varying doses of CA (Solarbio, China) for 4 h, followed by the addition of LPS stimulation and the addition of the Nrf2-specific inhibitor ML385 (5 µM, MedChemExpress, China). Fer-1 intervention protocol: RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with Fer-1 (10 µM, Selleckchem, USA) for 1 h, followed by the addition of LPS stimulation [49].

Cell viability assay

In a 96-well plate, 1 × 104 RAW264.7 cells were seeded per well and incubated for 24 h. After cell apposition, drug interventions were performed according to the experimental groups. Specifically, they included: CA group: various CA concentrations (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 µM) were given and pretreated for 4 hours; LPS group: treated with LPS (1 µg/mL) for 24 h; CA + LPS group: after 4 hours of CA pretreatment, LPS was added and co-incubated for 24 h; CA + LPS + ML385 group: based on the CA pretreatment, simultaneous ML385 (5 µM) intervention was added, followed by co-incubation with LPS for 24 h; and CA + Fer-1 group: after 1 h of Fer-1 pretreatment, LPS was added and co-incubated for 24 h. Each medium received 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (NCM Biotech, China) following each treatment, and it was subsequently incubated for three hours at 37 °C. Following the incubation, the OD value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA) to evaluate cell vitality.

LDH release assay

1 × 104 cells per well of cells were sown into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Then, CA, LPS, and ML385 were used to treat the cells at an indicated concentration and time. The lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release reagent (Beyotime, China) was introduced to the maximal enzyme activity control 1 h before the assay and mixed thoroughly. To proceed, take 120 µl of supernatant from each well, mix it with 60 µl of LDH working solution, and incubate at room temperature for 30 min without light. Finally, the intensity of absorption at 490 nm was measured with a microplate reader.

ELISA assay

In a 96-well plate, 1 × 104 RAW264.7 cells were seeded per well. Subsequently, the prescribed medications and duration were administered to treat the cells in each group. After a 24-hour treatment period, the supernatant of the cells in each group was gathered and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to get rid of cell debris. The supernatants were utilized to test IL-6 (ml098430, Enzyme-linked, China) and TNF-α (mIC50536-1, Enzyme-linked, China) levels in mice.

ROS determination analysis

The intracellular accumulation of ROS was assessed using the kit (Beyotime, China). Dilute the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA to 1:1000 using serum-free culture medium, and incubate with the treated cells for 20 min in the dark at a steady temperature of 37 °C. After discarding the supernatant, the excess probe was removed by gentle washing with serum-free culture medium 3 times. Immediately after washing, images were captured and viewed through the use of a fluorescence microscope (FITC channel).

Malondialdehyde, glutathione, and superoxide dismutase assays

Cells were cultivated on 6-well plates and collected using a cell scraper following a 24-hour drug treatment. Pre-cooled PBS (1 mL) was added to the cells and homogenized on ice using an ultrasonic cell crusher. After centrifuging the homogenate for 10 min at 4 °C and 3000 rpm, the supernatant was gathered. Using kits (Beyotime; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute; Beyotime), the guidelines were followed in determining the MDA, GSH, and SOD levels.

A quantity of rat gingival tissue was taken, and nine times the volume of ice-cold saline (mass/volume ratio 1:9) was added to create a 10% tissue homogenate. After 10 min of centrifuging at 3000 rpm and 4 °C, gather the supernatant. Referring to the method of cellular assay, oxidative stress-related indicators in gingival tissues were detected using the same MDA, GSH, and SOD kits according to the instructions.

Detection of ferrous ion (Fe2+) content

Ferrous ion (Fe2+) was extracted from RAW264.7 cells and gingival tissues using the extract from the Ferrous Ion Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, China). Cell and tissue supernatants were collected and prepared as in the previous section, and the rest followed the kit instructions. At the end of the reaction, the absorbance was measured using an enzyme meter at 593 nm. The concentration of Fe²⁺ was calculated for each group was then determined by plotting a standard curve from the standards.

RNA extraction and RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from RAW264.7 cells and rat gingival tissues utilizing a total RNA extraction kit (Yeasen, China). NanoDrop 2000 identified the purity and concentration of RNA. Reverse transcribe RNA (Yeasen) into cDNA. To perform real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR, Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (No Rox) (Yeasen) was utilized, and the CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (Bio-Rad) completed the amplification reaction. Sangon Biotech synthesized particular primers, and Supplementary Table 1 displays the specific sequences. The internal standard for quantification was GAPDH. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT technique.

Molecular Docking

The 2D structural files for CA were obtained from the PubChem database and then loaded into the Schrödinger Maestro software. Using the LigPrep module to obtain optimized ligands. The amino acid sequence of Nrf2 was converted to a PDB file using SwissModel, which was then loaded into the Schrödinger Maestro software and de-optimized for the receptor using the Protein Preparation Wizard with default settings. Finally, molecular docking was performed utilizing Schrödinger’s Glide module.

IF staining

RAW264.7 cells were cultivated in laser confocal dishes. After apposition, different drug treatments were given according to the experimental grouping. After 24 h of treatment, following a 10-minute fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, the treated cells underwent three PBS washes. Permeabilization was accomplished by using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After that, RAW264.7 cells were placed on a shaker and given three 5-minute washes with immunostaining wash buffer. Using an immunohydrogel staining blocking buffer to inhibit the cells for 10 min. Following that, the cells were exposed to the Nrf2 primary antibody (1:300, 80593-1-RR, Proteintech) for the whole night at 4 °C. The cells were rinsed 3 times for 5 min each, the next day, using Immunol staining wash buffer (Beyotime). It was then incubated for 1 h against the light with a secondary antibody (1:200, A0423, Beyotime). After 3 washes with PBS, incubate with DAPI (1 µg/mL) for 5 min and 3 more washes. Finally, the slices were observed and photographed under a laser confocal microscope after being sealed with a sealer containing an anti-fluorescence quencher.

Western blotting (WB)

RIPA lysis buffer (NCM, China) with protease inhibitors was employed to extract total proteins from RAW264.7 cells. Nucleoproteins were extracted using the Nucleoprotein and Cytoplasmic Protein Extraction Kit (KeyGEN BioTECH, China). Protein concentration was measured by the BCA technique (NCM). Protein samples were combined with SDS loading buffer and boiled for 5–10 min at 95 °C to denature them. Samples were subsequently spiked at 30 µg protein per lane. Protein samples were transferred to 0.2 μm polyvinylidene fluoride membranes after being separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (NCM). 3 TBST rinses lasting 5 min each were performed after membrane transfer. Place the membrane on a shaker in NCMBlot blocking buffer (NCM) for 20 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies against Nrf2 (1:1000), GPX4 (1:1000, ab125066, Abcam, UK), SLC7A11 (1:1000, T57046, Abmart), FTH1 (1:1000, T56955, Abmart), β-actin (1:1000, GB15003-100, Servicebio) and Lamin B1 (1:500, WL01775, Wanleibio) were incubated on the membranes for a whole night at 4 °C. After being rinsed 3 times with TBST the next day, the membrane was incubated with secondary antibody (1:5000, SA00001-1, SA00001-2, Proteintech) for 1 h at room temperature on a shaker. And then washed again 3 times. Finally, the protein expression level was analyzed by developing the membrane using ECL chemiluminescent reagent (NCM).

Animal experiments

Male, six-eight-week-old, pathogen-free Wistar rats weighing roughly 200 g were obtained from Changchunshi Yisi Experimental Animals Technology Limited (license number: SCXK (JI) 2020–0002). The rats were kept in a controlled environment with free access to food and water. The ambient parameters included a 12-hour light/dark cycle, a steady temperature (20–26 °C), and a humidity level of 45–65%. All animal experiments were approved (Ethics Approval No. 2024557) and supervised by the Jilin University Animal Ethics Committee and were carried out according to the institutional animal care guidelines.

Following a week of acclimation feeding, rats were divided into five groups at random (n = 8): control group, CA group, periodontitis group, CA + periodontitis group, and CA + periodontitis + ML385 group. To construct a periodontitis model, 0.2 mm orthodontic ligature wire was used to ligate the bilateral maxillary first molars of rats. On the 2nd day after ligation, use a mouth opener to expose the maxillary molar region of the relevant group of rats, and inject CA (1 mg/kg/day [37]) (or PBS control) into the gingival sulcus of the first molar once daily for 14 consecutive days. The ML385 group of rats received daily intraperitoneal injections of ML385 at a dose of 30 mg/kg/day [50] starting from the second day after ligation until the end of the experiment. Daily observation of the rat condition. Fourteen days following ligation, rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium phenobarbital (150 mg/kg). The viscera, maxilla, and teeth were isolated and stored in 4% paraformaldehyde. For additional examination, gingival tissues were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Micro-CT analysis

To determine the degree of bone resorption in rats, the samples were scanned using a micro-CT system (SkyScan 1176, Bruker). The raw data obtained were used for 3D reconstruction of the maxilla using SkyScan software. The distance between the dentin-enamel junction (CEJ) and the alveolar bone crest (ABC) was measured to determine the extent of alveolar bone resorption. Simultaneous quantification of trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), trabeculae number (Tb.N), and bone volume/tissue volume percentage (BV/TV) was performed for a comprehensive assessment of bone microarchitecture.

H&E, masson, and IHC staining

Overnight, tissue samples from the rat heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin embedding was performed, and Sect. (4 μm thickness) were cut. For 24 h, the teeth and maxilla were similarly treated in 4% paraformaldehyde. This was followed by continuous decalcification using a 10% EDTA decalcification solution (pH 7.2) for approximately 8 weeks at room temperature. After decalcification was completed, the same paraffin embedding and 4 μm sections were performed. HE staining (Solarbio), Masson’s trichrome stain (Solarbio), and Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining were applied to sections of paraffin. During IHC staining, antigen repair was first carried out in a microwave oven using citrate buffer (pH 6.0) on high for 2 min and 30 s and on low for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase was inhibited with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature. Subsequently, nonspecific binding was prevented with 5% BSA. Primary antibodies (Nrf2, GPX4, SLC7A11, and FTH1) were incubated on sections for an entire night at 4 °C. The following day, after 3 PBS washes, secondary antibody (1:200) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. The film underwent 3 more PBS washes, and the color was developed using DAB (Solarbio), and the film was sealed after hematoxylin re-staining. HE and Masson staining steps were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The staining was photographed under a microscope. Some of the staining results were analyzed semi-quantitatively by ImageJ software to assess the area or intensity of positivity.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) was applied to analyze all data. The mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) is displayed for the results. For multiple groups of data that were normally distributed and had equal variances, one-way ANOVA was employed. For nonparametric data that did not meet the assumptions of normality or equal variances, the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was used. The threshold for statistical significance was p < 0.05.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current finding clarified that CA is effective in alleviating periodontitis by preventing ferroptosis, attenuating oxidative stress, and suppressing inflammatory responses via Nrf2 pathway activation. CA, as a natural multidimensional regulator, will be a viable medication option for periodontitis precision intervention. In the future, the combination of structural modification of CA, optimization of drug delivery, and the combination of other therapeutic means is expected to achieve a more efficient and safe intervention strategy for periodontitis. This will also improve patients’ quality of life and prognosis.
