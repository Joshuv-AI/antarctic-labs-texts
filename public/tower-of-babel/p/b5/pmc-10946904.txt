Artificial intelligence in medicine: has the time come to hang up the stethoscope?
Authors: David Lyell, Farah Magrabi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe question of whether the time has come to hang up the stethoscope is bound up in the promises of artificial intelligence (AI), promises that have so far proven difficult to deliver, perhaps because of the mismatch between the technical capability of AI and its use in real‐world clinical settings. This perspective argues that it is time to move away from discussing the generalised promise of disembodied AI and focus on specifics. We need to focus on how the computational method underlying AI, i.e. machine learning (ML), is embedded into tools, how those tools contribute to clinical tasks and decisions and to what extent they can be relied on. Accordingly, we pose four questions that must be asked to make the discussion real and to understand how ML tools contribute to health care: (1) What does the ML algorithm do? (2) How is output of the ML algorithm used in clinical tools? (3) What does the ML tool contribute to clinical tasks or decisions? (4) Can clinicians act or rely on the ML tool? Two exemplar ML tools are examined to show how these questions can be used to better understand the role of ML in supporting clinical tasks and decisions. Ultimately, ML is just a fancy method of automation. We show that it is useful in automating specific and narrowly defined clinical tasks but likely incapable of automating the full gamut of decisions and tasks performed by clinicians.Keywords: artificial intelligence, machine learning, software, medical device, clinical decision support systems, human factors and ergonomics

Introduction

Any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic.

Arthur C. Clarke

‘We should stop training radiologists now’, 1 ‘Full self‐driving next year’ 2 and ‘An AI (artificial intelligence) lawyer is set to appear in court in a world first. Does it spell the end for costly legal fees?’ 3

It was not so long ago that the picture painted in movies was of a cataclysmic war where the AI once entrusted to defend us evolves to see humanity as its enemy. However, contemporary headlines would have us believe that the downfall of humanity will be far more mundane: the machines will take our jobs.

Just as science fiction about cyborgs sent back from the future to terminate the future leader of the human resistance requires the suspension of disbelief, some of the discourse around AI seems to be asking us to do the same. As of 2023, new radiologists still need to be trained, Elon Musk continues to promise full self‐driving next year for a ninth successive year 2 and the AI lawyer is somewhat of a misnomer—in reality, a smartphone app designed to assist a person self‐representing in court; a ‘platform for legal information and self‐help’, which ‘does not constitute legal advice’. 4 Its inaugural appearance in court was abandoned after its developers were threatened with prosecution for the unauthorised (i.e. unlicenced) practice of law. 5

Has AI failed to live up to the promise or has it been overpromised? The difficulty appears to stem from the conflation of technological capability to perform expert tasks with being an expert. The capability of AI to detect cancers in screening mammography is not the same as being a radiologist.

Irrespective of whether the promise represents a vision for the future yet to be achieved, or exaggeration, there is a substantial mismatch between the technological capability for AI to do something versus how it can be used in real‐world clinical practice. If this mismatch causes technologies to appear sufficiently advanced as to be indistinguishable from magic, the antidote is to focus on the specifics.

Bringing specificity to AI and machine learning

Let us start with specific terminology. AI describes intelligence demonstrated by computers, i.e. ordinarily associated with human intelligence. The field dates back to the 1950s, where the method of such intelligence was human‐crafted rules, e.g. display a contraindication alert if nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drug are prescribed to a patient taking warfarin. Specifically, what we are discussing in the 2020s is AI achieved using machine learning (ML).

ML is the family of computational reasoning methods, which includes deep learning, where machines learn from examples. For example, an ML algorithm can be trained to detect cancers in mammograms using mammograms labelled by human experts, as normal or cancer. The algorithm then learns for itself how to differentiate between the two. The result is an algorithm that, when presented with a previously unseen mammogram (as input), can provide a classification of whether the case is normal or suspicious for cancer (output; see Fig. 1).

While the prowess of ML algorithms for detecting disease is well reported in the scientific literature (e.g. Reference 6), the ability to detect melanoma from photographs is not the same as a diagnosis made by a clinician, which involves biopsies and other assessments, and can be relied on for prescribing treatment. Likewise, many cutting‐edge ML algorithms reported in the scientific literature are just that, disembodied algorithms not immediately deployable for use with patients. Again, there is a need for specificity.

To be useful in the real world, algorithms need to be embedded into tangible tools that can be used by clinicians at the point of care (see Fig. 2). One specific subgroup of tangible real‐world ML tools are ML‐enabled medical devices (ML devices). 7 , 8 By definition, any software intended for use in the diagnosis, management or prevention of disease is a medical device 9 and is subject to regulation by the Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) in Australia.

Relationship between machine learning (ML) algorithms, ML tools and ML medical devices.

This perspective shares key lessons learned from analysing the indications for use of ML devices, 8 as real‐world artefacts of ML tools currently usable in clinical practice. There are four questions we can ask of ML tools to understand what they do and what they contribute to the provision of health care. To illustrate, we examine two ML medical devices approved by the TGA for clinical use in Australia and seek specific answers from their respective indications for use. Drawing on those answers, the role of ML in medicine and to what extent ML is likely capable of replacing human clinicians are discussed. The two ML devices we will examine are BriefCase by AiDoc, 10 a Class I (lowest level of potential harm) medical device indicated for computer‐aided triage and notification (triage) of suspected positive findings of intracranial occlusions from computed tomography (CT) angiography (CTA) and several other conditions, and Profound AI by iCad, 11 a Class IIb medical device (moderate to high level of potential harm) indicated for computer‐aided detection (CAD) of suspicious findings for breast cancer from full‐field digital mammography (FFDM) or digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT).

Question 1: what does the ML algorithm do?

ML algorithms return an output for a given input (Fig. 1), providing a good formula for specific answers. Profound AI detects ‘soft tissue densities (masses, architectural distortions and asymmetries) and calcifications’ from FFDM images and DBT slices. 11 BriefCase 10 is indicated to detect the following findings: intracranial haemorrhage from nonenhanced head CT; M1 vessel occlusion and brain aneurism from head CTA; cervical spine fracture from nonenhanced cervical spine CT; pulmonary embolism from CT pulmonary angiogram; intra‐abdominal free gas from abdomen CT; incidental pulmonary embolism, rib fracture, COVID‐19 and pulmonary nodules from chest CT and pneumothorax from frontal chest radiograph.

Critically, ML can only detect what it has been trained and validated to detect, and nothing else. Further, ML results are only valid when using the same data type as it was trained and tested with. Therefore, as reported in the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG) public summary, BriefCase only detects vessel occlusions affecting the M1 artery and only from head CTA. 10 Accordingly, BriefCase cannot be relied on to detect any other occlusions, or to detect M1 occlusions using other modalities.

The importance of these distinctions and associated complexities were highlighted in April 2022 by the US medical device regulator, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), in a letter to health care providers about the Intended Use of Imaging Software for Intracranial Large Vessel Occlusion (LVO). 12 The letter stated that ‘information from real‐world use suggests that providers may not be aware of the intended use of these devices’ with specific mention of the risks posed by use to ‘evaluate vessels for which it was not designed’, which could lead to ‘delayed or missed diagnoses’ and may ‘result in worsened outcomes for patients’. 12 In June 2022, the FDA approved modifications to BriefCase to ‘further clarify its intended use – as analysing specifically M1 large vessels’, 13 where prior indications referred only to ‘large vessel occlusions’. 14 A subsequent approved modification expanded these indications to findings of ‘Large Vessel Occlusions (LVO) – MCA‐M1, PCA‐P1, ACA‐A1, ICA, Basilar and Medium Vessel Occlusions (MeVO) – MCA‐M2, MCA‐proximal M3, PCA‐P2, PCA‐proximal P3, ACA‐A2, ACA‐proximal A3 and Vertebral‐V4’. 15 At the time of writing, the ARTG public summary states that BriefCase is only indicated for M1 vessel occlusions. 10

The risks posed by these misunderstandings and the benefits offered depend on how ML algorithm outputs are used by ML tools, what those tools contribute to the provision of health care and the extent to which they can be relied on. Each of these questions is covered in the sections that follow.

Question 2: how is output of the ML algorithm used in clinical tools?

Having identified that ML algorithms are trained to produce a useful output for a given input, such as whether there is a vessel occlusion demonstrated in a head CTA, the next key question is how are the outputs of these algorithms used in clinical tools?

The answer to how ML algorithm output is used is where good science risks becoming science fiction. The scientific literature is abound with articles reporting evaluations in which ML algorithms performed as well as or better than human experts for detecting pathology. (e.g. Reference 6) However, detecting these pathologies represents only a small and very specific subset of tasks performed by clinicians. More importantly, detecting pathology consistent with disease is not the same as diagnosing that disease, which inevitably involves clinical correlation. BriefCase's vessel occlusion module only knows what is contained in the head CTA but nothing about the patient's present neurological symptoms or prior history.

Here, it is useful to: (1) identify the task clinicians seek to accomplish with ML tools, and appreciating that it may be one element of a larger task, and (2) distinguish the detection of pathology consistent with disease from the diagnosis of a disease, where diagnosis is a clinician‐based task involving clinical correlation that can be relied on as a basis for treatment.

Ultimately, how the output of ML algorithms is used is a decision made by its developers based on their implementation of that algorithm into a tangible ML tool and the intended use of that tool. While the algorithms of both Profound AI and BriefCase detect pathologies indicative of disease, how each device uses those algorithmic outputs differs.

Profound AI is a computer‐assisted detection and diagnosis (CAD) device that marks the contour of detected lesions or calcification clusters and provides a confidence score of the presence of malignancy for each finding and the overall case. 16 It is indicated for concurrent use while reading FFDM or DBT studies, to ‘assist interpreting physicians in identifying soft tissue densities and calcifications’, with a specific stipulation that detections and scores ‘may be confirmed or dismissed by the interpreting physician’. 11 That is, Profound AI provides information to the interpreting clinician, who is responsible for making a final decision on the case.

In contrast, BriefCase is a computer‐aided triage and notification (triage) device ‘intended to assist … in workflow triage by flagging and communication of suspected positive findings’ for the conditions it detects. 10 The indications specifically note it is ‘not intended to be used as a diagnostic device’. 10

The difference between CAD and triage devices will become more apparent as we answer the next two questions regarding how these ML tools contribute to the provision of health care and whether clinicians can rely on them. But for now, it is important to note that the indications for use describe how ML tools may be used by clinicians and for what purposes.

The FDA's letter regarding triage devices for LVO 12 explicitly reminded clinicians that ML devices can only be used for their intended purpose, in particular, that triage devices ‘only flag radiological exam is with suspected findings and should never be used as a replacement for informed interpretation by an imaging physician’, and that triage ‘devices cannot rule out the presence of an LVO’, and an LVO may still be present despite the absence of any notification.

Question 3: what does the ML tool contribute to clinical tasks or decisions?

Next, we need to understand how ML tools contribute to tasks and decisions in the provision of health care, and how they can deliver benefit.

Profound AI is a CAD device the functions as a second opinion that clinicians may consider in making their decision. 17 Its value lies in what it can contribute to that decision, 17 by drawing attention to subtle but important findings that might otherwise be missed. The relationship between CAD and clinicians is synergistic and may benefit in cases where clinicians are uncertain, provided that CAD findings are correct. 17 However, there is good evidence that incorrect CAD findings degrade clinician performance, especially for more difficult cases. 18 , 19

On the other hand, triage devices aim to benefit by enabling cases with suspected findings for time‐sensitive conditions, such as ischaemic stroke, to be prioritised for reading. Here, the goal is to reduce door‐to‐intervention time. A similar philosophy underlies LumineticsCore (formerly IDx‐DR) by Digital Diagnostics, 20 which was heralded as the world's first autonomous medical AI. LumineticsCore detects diabetic retinopathy from fundus imaging. 20 It is intended to be used for screening in general practice, where positive results are used as the basis for referral to an ophthalmologist for diagnosis and management. LumineticsCore aims to facilitate increased screening rates among previously unscreened populations by enabling screening in primary care settings.

Question 4: can clinicians act or rely on the ML tool?

The promise of AI is one of autonomous ML agents capable of replacing human experts. Yet ML tools can only be considered autonomous when users can rely or act on the information provided. It is evident, however, that the ability to rely on ML tools is determined by their indications for use, many of which stop well short of autonomy. Profound AI is one example of a nonautonomous tool, where findings are used to assist interpretation and final decisions are left to clinicians. 11 Autonomously functioning ML tools do exist: BriefCase is one such example whereby clinicians may rely on it to read notified cases as a matter of priority.

The differences are the type of task performed and how the ML tool outputs are intended to be used. CAD devices such as Profound AI assist clinicians with diagnosis, whereas triage devices such as BriefCase only notify the presence of cases with suspected time‐critical findings. So, while radiologists need to interpret all assigned cases per their standard of care, they can rely on notifications to prioritise.

In general, based on our analysis of 49 unique ML devices, there are three ways in which ML tools currently support health care tasks (see Fig. 3). 8

Assistive is characterised by overlapping tasks shared between the ML tool and the clinician, designating clinician responsibility for decisions. Notably, 47% of the tools analysed were assistive, including Profound AI and other CAD devices that identified pathologies consistent with disease for clinicians to review. 8

Autonomous information provides information that can be relied on for use in making decisions but it does not provide a decision in relation to the clinical task. Notably, 24% of analysed tools provide autonomous information as an input to clinician decision‐making. These often provide some type of quantification, such as volumetric quantification of brain structures, which may be considered by clinicians in the evaluation of dementia. 8 A specific example is IcoBrain, 21 which quantifies the volume of brain structures from magnetic resonance imaging or CT scans, contributing information that may inform assessments for dementia.

Autonomous decision provides a decision that clinicians can rely or act on. This category encompasses the remaining 29% of analysed tools, which provide autonomous decisions. 8 BriefCase and LumineticsCore are two such examples.

The key characteristic distinguishing assistive devices is the presence of indications stipulating clinician responsibility for the task outcome. For example, the ‘clinician retains the ultimate responsibility for making the pertinent diagnosis based on their standard practices’, 22 ‘patient management decisions should not be made solely on the results’ 23 or that information provided is ‘subject to verification by a qualified clinician’. 24 These indicated caveats prohibiting reliance on ML output appear to be a key sticking point holding back some of the loftier promises of autonomous ML from being realised.

The difference in the level of autonomy demonstrated by these devices is contingent on the task it performs, such as diagnosis or triage. Importantly, ML tools performing high‐level tasks, such as diagnosis, were all assistive, whereas ML tools performing intermediary tasks supplementing clinician workflows, such as triaging priority imaging studies, were more likely to be autonomous.

Has the time come to hang up the stethoscope?

Ultimately, ML is just a fancy method of automation, where machines perform tasks otherwise done by humans. 25 While the concept of automation in health care has a long history, the use of ML algorithms as the logic enabling automation is more recent, enabling partial or full automation of new types of tasks or better performance in the automation of existing tasks. Indeed, ML is especially suited to tasks involving imaging, exemplified by Profound AI and BriefCase.

The questions then become: what clinician tasks can be automated using ML tools and are these tools sufficiently capable of performing the full range of tasks necessary to automate clinicians out of existence?

By examining the indications of use for ML tools, such as Profound AI and BriefCase (Table 1), it becomes clear that the current state‐of‐the‐art ML tools are limited to performing specific and narrowly defined tasks, with varying degrees of autonomy depending on the intended use of the tool. 8 The most autonomous ML tools supplement rather than replace clinician tasks. To date, none is capable of replacing clinicians.

Most importantly, we need to be specific and appreciate the distinctions between disembodied ML algorithms and ML tools usable in real‐world clinical practice, between detecting pathologies consistent with disease and diagnosis of disease and between the ability to perform expert tasks versus being an expert.

When dialogue around AI starts sounding so advanced that it seems magical, the solution is to ask the specific questions we have proposed: What does the ML algorithm do? How is the algorithm used in clinical tools? What does the tool contribute to the provision of health care? Can clinicians rely on it?

It is unlikely that any combination of ML tools is capable of replacing human clinicians in the foreseeable future. It is clearly not time to hang up the stethoscope. However, having established the value of ML for computational reasoning, it is time we moved away from generalised discussion of the magical promises of disembodied ML algorithms and anthropomorphised machines and instead focus on how ML tools can contribute to the tasks and decisions in the provision of health care and how they can provide tangible real‐world benefit. We need to make the discussion real. We need to be specific.
