Phenotypic evaluation and mapping QTLs for drought tolerance in an interspecific RIL population from a Setaria italica × Setaria viridis cross
Authors: Tianpeng Liu, Jihong He, Kongjun Dong, Lei Zhang, Yawei Li, Yiyou Chen, Rituraj Khound, Dipak K Santra, Tianyu Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDrought tolerance is an important breeding objective for improving the yield of cereal crops. Identifying key drought-tolerant loci/genes from Setaria viridis and Setaria italica is essential for enhancing the yield and stress resistance of foxtail millet via molecular breeding technologies.ResultsHere, an interspecific recombinant inbred line (RIL) population was constructed by crossing the S. italica cultivar Yugu1 with the wild S. viridis H1. Phenotypic evaluation and QTL mapping for drought tolerance were conducted from the grain filling to the maturity stage. Five strong drought-tolerant lines were screened using the drought resistance index (DRI). Phenotypic variance analysis indicated that the period of duration was the key factor affecting drought tolerance in the population. Furthermore, the RIL population was subjected to whole-genome sequencing, and a high-density linkage map was constructed, comprising 3,029 bin markers spanning 709.34 cM with an average interval of 0.24 cM. A total of 10 QTLs were detected for panicle number per plot (PNP), grain weight per plant (GWP), and 1000-grain weight (TGW). Eight QTLs were associated with PNP, and the favorable alleles at all loci except qPNP4.1 were derived from Setaria viridis H1. The favorable alleles of the other two QTLs for GWP and TGW were all from Yugu1. Based on functional homology alignment, five genes located within three PNP-related QTL intervals (Seita.5G420900, Seita.7G306400, Seita.5G312700, Seita.5G319000, Seita.5G319500) were predicted to modulate the tillering, grain yield under drought stress conditions.ConclusionThese findings provide an important basis for drought-tolerant breeding and the dissection of the drought tolerance mechanisms in foxtail millet and its close species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09406-9.Keywords: Setaria italica, Setaria viridis, Drought tolerance, Recombinant inbred line, Phenotype, QTL

Introduction

The genus Setaria is a member of the tribe Paniceae, subfamily Panicoideae, and family Poaceae, which includes 99 species [1, 2]. Two species within the genus Setaria, green foxtail (Setaria viridis) and foxtail millet (Setaria italica), have emerged as model genetic systems owing to their small stature, short life cycle, self-compatibility, small diploid genome, and high transformation efficiency [3, 4]. Green foxtail is an invasive weed widely distributed in temperate, tropical, and subtropical regions [3]. Foxtail millet is a cereal species domesticated from the wild green foxtail, exhibiting drought-hardy and higher water use efficiency than maize, wheat, and sorghum [5]. Therefore, S. viridis and S. italica are recognized as ideal model genetic systems for studying drought tolerance mechanisms in cereal crops [6, 7]. However, some loci/genes associated with wild characteristics underwent selective sweeps during domestication [8], and these regions may harbor drought resistance-related loci. Therefore, it is essential to identify drought resistance loci using segregating populations derived from wild relatives and cultivated species.

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) on natural populations and quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping using segregating populations are the most widely used approaches for identifying drought tolerance loci/genes in foxtail millet. A GWAS of 425 foxtail millet samples identified 77 QTL regions and 6 genes associated with drought tolerance [9]. Eighteen QTLs controlling drought tolerance at the germination and early seedling stage in Setaria were identified on 190 F7 recombinant inbred lines (RILs) derived from a cross between a S. italica cultivar Yugu1 and a wild S. viridis genotype [10]. Multiple QTLs for stomatal density and canopy temperature, which are linked to water use efficiency (WUE), were detected in a RIL population derived from a S. italica × S. viridis cross [11]. A meta-analysis integrating 448 original QTLs identified 41 genomic regions and 104 potential candidate genes associated with drought tolerance and yield-related traits in foxtail millet [12]. In addition, omics approaches have also been applied to screen drought resistance genes/proteins in foxtail millet. Comparative transcriptomics identified thousands of key genes responsive to drought stress in three foxtail millet cultivars [13]. Time-series transcriptomics identified 1257 circadian clock-related genes and revealed a temporal network of drought-responsive and crosstalk between drought stress and the circadian clock in foxtail millet [14]. Integrated transcriptome and metabolome analysis identified 566 differentially expressed genes related to drought response at the booting stage of Setaria italica [15]. Employing comparative proteomics, 2474 differentially expressed proteins were identified in foxtail millet seedlings under drought conditions [16]. These QTLs, genes and proteins laid a foundation for functional characterization in plants’ response to drought stress, yet only a few drought resistance genes have been functionally validated. Studies have confirmed that the SiEPF gene improves water use efficiency and drought tolerance by regulating stomatal density in foxtail millet [17]. Transgenic foxtail millet seedlings overexpressing STTM164 showed significantly enhanced drought tolerance [18].

There is no report identifying drought-tolerant QTLs/genes on a high-density linkage map from S. italica and S. viridis under field environments from the grain filling to the mature stage. The annual precipitation in Dunhuang City of Gansu Province is less than 40 mm, and its climate is arid, providing a natural experimental environment for evaluating drought tolerance in crops from the grain-filling to the grain-maturity stage [19]. The period from the grain filling to the maturity stage is crucial for yield formation in cereal crops, during which yield and yield components are highly susceptible to drought stress. Interspecific populations carry the drought-tolerant genomic segments from both parental species. Therefore, mining QTL/genes related to yield components (panicle number per plot, grain weight per plant, and 1000-grain weight) in drought-tolerant breeding lines using interspecific populations is necessary to dissect the drought-tolerance mechanisms in foxtail millet. In the study, 185 RILs derived from a cross between S. italica and S. viridis were evaluated under two contrasting water/irrigation field conditions for two consecutive years, aiming to map drought tolerance-related loci in foxtail millet.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

An interspecific RIL population consisting of 185 F2:9 lines was constructed by crossing Yugu1 and H1 in our lab. Yugu1 is a S. italica cultivar widely cultivated in China. H1 was collected from roadsides at Huining Experimental Station, Gansu Academy of Agricultural Sciences. It is a wild green foxtail (S. viridis) accession with stronger drought tolerance compared to Yugu1 [20]. The cross was made in Sanya, Hainan Province, in the winter of 2017. Then, the F2:9 RIL population was developed following the single seed descent (SSD) method, growing two generations each year until 2022. The two parents, as well as the RIL population, were subsequently planted in Dunhuang City in 2022 and 2023.

Field experiment

The RIL population and its parents were planted during summer to autumn in 2022 and 2023 at the Dunhuang Experimental Station of Gansu Academy of Agricultural Sciences (40.159°N, 94.638°E). A two-factor split-plot design was adopted for all field experiments. The main factor was water supply treatment, with two levels: normal water supply (N) and drought stress (D). The subplot factor consisted of 185 recombinant inbred lines, which were arranged in sequence. Each treatment was replicated three times. Each line in each replicate was planted into a two-row plot with a row length of 1 m, row spacing of 40 cm, and plant spacing of approximately 10 cm (Fig. S1). According to the standard, insufficient water supply (i.e., no irrigation) from the filling stage to maturity causes drought stress, and a soil moisture content below 7.5% in the 0–20 cm soil layer at the filling stage represents drought stress conditions [19]. Therefore, sufficient irrigation was applied under both normal water and drought conditions. Normal water supply plots were irrigated during the grain filling to maturity stage, whereas no irrigation was given to drought-stressed plots during these stages. For the normal water supply treatment, drip irrigation at a rate of 450 m³ ha⁻¹ was applied after sowing (April 28, 2022; April 30, 2023), at the seedling stage (June 4, 2022; June 7, 2023), booting stage (July 10, 2022; July 13, 2023), and grain filling stage (August 16, 2022; August 19, 2023). For the drought stress treatment, drip irrigation at 450 m³ ha⁻¹ was only applied after sowing (April 28, 2022; April 30, 2023) and at the seedling stage (June 4, 2022; June 7, 2023). Soil moisture content was measured using five-point sampling method at 7 days after each drip irrigation, at the seedling stage (June 11, 2022; June 14, 2023), booting stage (July 18, 2022; July 21, 2023), grain filling stage (August 24, 2022; August 27, 2023), and grain maturity stage (September 20, 2022; September 18, 2023), respectively. The formula for calculating soil moisture content is: Soil moisture content (%) = (fresh soil mass - dry soil mass) /dry soil mass × 100%. The average value represents the measured value of soil moisture content of each soil layer. The monthly average temperature and monthly precipitation during the growth period of the RIL population are provided by the Dunhuang Experimental Station of Gansu Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Phenotyping and statistical analysis

Following the methods of Lu [21] and Liu et al. [22], 12 agronomic and yield-related traits (period of duration (from seedling to maturity stage), plant height, peduncle length, diameter of the main stem, panicle length, panicle diameter, leaf length, leaf width, straw weight per plant, panicle weight per plant, grain weight per plant, and 1000-grain weight) were measured for 185 recombinant inbred lines and the parents under normal water supply and drought stress conditions. For the other 11 traits except the period of duration, five individual plants were randomly sampled from each plot for measurement. After maturity, the traits of the five plants were measured individually. The average trait value per plot was considered as phenotypic value for each plot in individual replicate. The grain weight of all panicles of each plot was recorded as plot yield (Table S1). The R package “ggsignif” and “lme4” were used for pairwise analysis and broad-sense heritability, respectively. Broad-sense heritability was calculated using the following formula: h2 = Vg/(Vg+Vge/L + Vgy+Vr/LY), where Vg, Vge, Vgy, and Vr represent the variance components of genotype, genotype and environment interaction, genotype and year interaction, and residual error, respectively. L and Y denote the number of environments (normal water supply (N) and drought stress (D)) and the number of experimental years, respectively.

Drought evaluation

According to the method of Li et al. [23], the DRI of 185 recombinant inbred lines was calculated. The formula for DRI is as follows: DRI = (Ya/Yw)×(Ya/Yad), where Yw and Ya represent the plot yield under normal water supply and drought stress treatments, respectively, and Yad is the yield of the control lines Yugu1 or H1 under drought stress treatment. Subsequently, the drought tolerance grades of 185 recombinant inbred lines were classified according to the stepwise classification method for drought resistance evaluation proposed by Lu et al. [24].

Genotyping analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from fresh young leaves of two parents and 185 RILs according to a modified CTAB method [25]. DNA degradation and contamination of all lines were monitored on 1% agarose gels. DNA purity and concentration were checked and measured by referencing to the protocol of Liu et al. [22]. High‑throughput sequencing based on the Illumina HiSeq platform was used to genotype the two parents and the RIL population, following the protocol described by Liu et al. [22]. Raw reads obtained from sequencing were filtered to remove adapter sequences and low-quality bases, yielding clean reads. The clean reads of all samples were aligned to the reference genome of Setaria_italica_v2.2 [2], with an average coverage of 90.52% and an average sequencing depth of 9.53. The sequencing depths of the two parents, Yugu 1 and H1, were 21.29 and 30.98, respectively (Table S2).SNP calling for all samples was performed using GATK (version 3.7) software package based on the following criteria: (1) Fisher test of strand bias (FS) ≤ 60; (2) HaplotypeScore ≤ 13.0; (3) Mapping quality (MQ) ≥ 40; (4) Quality depth (QD) ≥ 2; (5) ReadPosRankSum ≥ − 8.0; (6) MQRankSum > − 12.5; (7) DP ≥ 8 reads. The resulting variation was further screened according to the following conditions: (a) SNP and InDel are polymorphic between the parents; (b) The proportion of missing genotype in lines is less than 25%; (c) The frequency of minor alleles is not less than 20%; (d) Heterozygosity of lines is less than 25%.

According to the Hidden Markov Model (HMM) described by Xie et al. [26], the filtered SNP and InDel dataset generated a total of 3,029 bin markers. Genotypes A and B in 185 RILs represent those derived from H1 and Yugu1, respectively.

Linkage map construction and QTL detection

QTL IciMapping software was used for the construction of the linkage map and QTL mapping. Inclusive composite interval mapping (ICIM) was applied to detect QTL. A threshold of log of odds (LOD) ≥ 2.97 determined by 1,000 permutation tests and a false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 indicated the presence of a QTL. Positive additive effects indicated that alleles originating from H1 increased the phenotypic value, while negative additive effects indicated that alleles derived from Yugu1 increased the phenotypic value. The QTL nomenclature was designated beginning with the letter “q”, followed by the trait abbreviation as mentioned above, the chromosome number, and the QTL serial number.

Candidate gene identification

The physical interval spanned by the flanking markers was defined as the physical interval of each QTL. To screen for drought tolerance-related genes, rice, a model cereal crop, was used for gene homology comparison. Specifically, genes were extracted from the confidence intervals on the reference genome Sitalica_312_v2.2, and then subjected to homologous alignment against the Oryza sativa v7.0 reference genome in Phytozome 13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/blast-search). The homologous rice gene IDs was retrieved from the China Rice Data Center (https://www.ricedata.cn/gene/) to get gene function and annotation information. Homologs to genes related to PNP, TGW, and drought tolerance in rice were predicted as candidate genes in foxtail millet.

Results

Soil moisture, air temperature, and precipitation

To evaluate the degree of drought stress, soil moisture content in the 0–100 cm soil layer was measured in the field. Under the water supply condition during the seedling stage and booting stage (D), the average soil moisture content of the 0–100 cm soil layer in the filling stage was 7.0-7.6%, while it was 11.7–12.5% under normal water supply (N) (Fig. 1a, b). According to the criterion that drought stress occurs when soil moisture content in the 0–20 cm layer is below 7.5% [19], water supply restricted to the seedling and booting stages resulted in the RIL population being exposed to drought stress from the filling stage to maturity. In addition, air temperature and precipitation data during the growth period of the RIL population (May to September) showed relatively high temperatures and extremely low precipitation (13.07 mm and 24.64 mm), ensuring the effectiveness of drought stress treatment in the study (Fig. 1c, d).

Comparative analysis of parental yield and yield components

Across two years, under both normal water supply and drought stress conditions, the parent Yugu1 exhibited higher values for yield and other related traits than the parent H1, except for panicle number per plot (Fig. 2). Pairwise analysis showed no significant differences in the panicle number per plot and 1,000-grain weight of Yugu1 between different water treatments, whereas plot yield and grain weight per plant differed significantly (Fig. 2a-d, i-l). In contrast, H1 showed significant differences in the other three traits except for 1000-grain weight (Fig. 2e-h, m-p). These results indicated that the effect of drought stress on the yield of Yugu1 was mainly reflected in grain weight per plant among the three yield components, while the panicle number per plot and grain weight per plant of H1 play an important role under drought stress, indicating that the yield changes of H1, compared with the cultivar Yugu1, are regulated by more genetic factors under drought stress.

Analysis of yield and yield components in the RIL population

Phenotypic analysis of yield and yield components in 185 recombinant inbred lines showed that plot yield, panicle number per plot (PNP), grain weight per plant (GWP), and 1000-grain weight (TGW) exhibited an approximately normal distribution, consistent with the genetic characteristics of quantitative traits (Fig. 3). Under drought stress, except for 1000-grain weight, panicle number per plot, and grain weight per plant were significantly lower than those under normal water supply conditions (Fig. 3), indicating that drought stress exerted significant effects on these two yield components. Based on the differential characteristics of three yield component traits, we further analyzed the broad-sense heritability of PNP, GWP, and TGW based on the RIL phenotypic data in normal water supply (N) and drought stress (D) across 2 years. The results showed that the broad-sense heritability of all three traits was higher than 0.6 (Table 1), implying that genetic factors contributed more than environmental factors to the three yield-related traits.

Evaluation of drought tolerance for recombinant inbred lines

The drought tolerance grades of the 185 recombinant inbred lines were consistent across the two years using parent Yugu1 or H1 as the control (Table 2, Table S3). It indicated that strong drought-tolerant lines could be screened using the drought resistance index combined with the classification method with either parent as the reference [23]. Based on the drought resistance index (DRI), 29, 132, and 24 recombinant inbred lines were identified as strong (grade1), median (grade2) and weak (grade3) drought-tolerance lines in 2022, respectively, while 31, 120 and 34 lines were classified into the three corresponding categories in 2023 (Table 2). Of these lines, 5 recombinant inbred lines were repeatedly identified as strong drought-tolerance lines (grade1), and 10 lines were repeatedly identified as weak drought-tolerance lines (grade3) (Table 2).

Phenotypic characteristics of lines between grade 1 and 3 levels

Comparative analysis of variance was carried out using the means of 12 agronomic and yield-related traits measured under normal water supply and drought stress in 15 RILs (5 strong drought-tolerant (grade1) and 10 drought-susceptible (grade3) lines) across two years (Fig. 4). Under normal water supply and drought stress, the five strong drought-tolerant lines (grade1) differed significantly only in plant height and peduncle length (Fig. 4a, b; Fig. S2), while the ten weak drought-tolerant lines (grade3) differed significantly in five traits: plant height, panicle diameter, straw weight per plant, panicle weight per plant, and grain weight per plant (Fig. 4c-g; Fig. S3), suggesting that the strong drought-tolerant lines had stronger adaptability to drought environment. Under normal water supply, eight out of the twelve measured traits differed significantly between strong and weak drought-tolerant lines. Except for the growth period, main stem diameter and straw weight per plant, strong drought-tolerant lines exhibited significantly higher values in panicle length, peduncle length, straw weight per plant, panicle weight per plant, grain weight per plant, and 1000-grain weight than weak drought-tolerant lines (Fig. 4h-o; Fig. S4). Similar trends in trait performance were observed under drought stress (Fig. 4p-w; Fig. S5). These results suggest that short period of duration combined with relatively higher values of other traits represents the phenotypic characteristics of drought-tolerant lines.

Linkage map construction

After filtering, a total of 1,022,638 SNPs and 124,654 InDels with polymorphism were identified between Yugu1 and H1. The order of variation abundance of SNPs and InDels on each chromosome was: Chr. 9, 3, 1, 5, 8, 7, 6, 2, and 4 (Table 3). These variations were integrated into 3,029 bin markers using the HMM [26] (Table S4). A linkage map was constructed based on the bin markers, covering nine linkage groups with a total of 709.34 cM. The genetic distance between adjacent markers ranged from 0.17 to 0.35 cM, with an average interval of 0.24 cM (Table 3). Analysis of pairwise recombination fractions and LOD scores indicated that no abnormal segregation was observed for any of the markers in the bin map (Fig. S6a, b).

QTLs for drought tolerance

A total of 10 QTLs were detected under normal water supply and drought stress environments (Table S5). Among these, 7 QTLs were detected exclusively under normal water supply, 3 QTLs were solely identified under drought stress (Fig. 5). The LOD scores of these QTLs ranged from 3.28 to 7.61, explaining 8.18% to 14.83% of the phenotypic variations. Except for qPNP4.1, a QTL controlling PNP on chromosome 4, the positive-effect alleles from all other QTLs for PNP were derived from H1. In contrast, the positive-effect alleles of the two QTLs for GWP and TGW were contributed by Yugu1 (Fig. 5; Table S5).

QTLs detected under normal water supply

Seven QTLs were identified under normal water supply. Specifically, three QTLs, qPNP5.1, qPNP7.1, and qTGW5.1 were detected under normal water supply in 2022, and four QTLs, qPNP5.2, qPNP5.3, qPNP9.1, and qGWP4.1, were detected in 2023 under normal water supply conditions. The H1 contributed the additive effects of all the QTLs for PNP, whereas the additive effects for the two QTLs for GWP and TGW were provided by Yugu1 (Fig. 5; Table S5).

QTLs detected under drought stress

Only three QTLs were detected under drought stress, all of which were PNP QTLs mapped in a single-year drought trial. They are distributed on chromosomes 4, 5 and 7, respectively. Among them, the additive effects of qPNP4.1 for PNP was contributed by Yugu1, while these of qPNP5.4 and qPNP7.2 were derived from the H1 (Fig. 5; Table S5).

Candidate genes for drought tolerance

Homologous alignment was performed against the rice reference genome to identify drought tolerance-related genes. The results showed that five candidate genes located within 3 QTLs (qPNP5.1, qPNP5.3, and qPNP7.2) were homologous to genes regulating tiller, yield, and drought tolerance in rice (Table 4). The candidate genes Seita.5G420900 within the qPNP5.1 interval were homologous to plastochron 2 (PLA2) [27] and leafy-head2 (LHD2) [28] in rice, respectively, all of which affected tiller number. Seita.7G306400 located at qPNP7.2 was homologous to the NAC transcription factor (OsNAC10) [29], which enhances drought tolerance and grain yield in rice under field drought conditions. Seita.5G312700, Seita.5G319000, and Seita.5G319500 were homologous to Aux/IAA protein (OsIAA6) [30], WRKY transcription factor 13 (OsWRKY13) [31], and vesicle trafficking gene (OsRab7) [32], respectively, all of which are closely related to the drought tolerance in rice.

Discussion

Period of duration is a key determinant of drought tolerance in the RIL population

Foxtail millet is an underutilized crop that is widely cultivated globally due to its strong tolerance to drought stress in arid and semi-arid regions worldwide [33]. However, the drought tolerance varies significantly among different foxtail millet varieties. Agronomic traits, yield and yield-related traits are the major concerns of breeders. Screening effective identification indicators can provide a basis for the breeding and development of drought-resistant foxtail millet cultivars [34]. Relative panicle weight, grain weight per panicle, plant height, rachis length, and TGW can be used as the morphological indicators for drought resistance identification at the booting stage in foxtail millet [35]. Grain weight per ear, yield, ear weight, and ear length can serve as indicators for drought-resistance evaluation at the mature stage [34]. Plant height and spike weight can be adopted as indices for drought tolerance identification of different genotypes of foxtail millet during the entire growth period [36]. All the above findings were obtained from the natural populations consisting of diverse germplasm, whereas few studies have focused on the key identification indicators of drought tolerance using biparental segregation populations. In this study, lines with strong and weak drought tolerance were screened using the drought resistance index (DRI), and their main agronomic, yield and yield-related traits were compared. The results showed that growth period was a key determinant of drought tolerance in the RIL population under drought stress from grain filling to maturity. Under drought stress, growth period was significantly correlated with yield and DRI (Fig. S7). This was mainly because genotypes with a shorter growth period completed panicle development earlier under drought stress during the late growth stage, whereas those with a longer growth period suffered more severe negative effects throughout the reproductive growth phase, leading to a more substantial reduction in final yield. Our result is similar to what was reported in rice [37].

Setaria viridis contributed favorable alleles of tillering under drought stress.

Foxtail millet was domesticated from green foxtail (S. viridis), which exhibits vigorous branching and wide habitat adaptability to harsh environments such as high temperature, drought, and salinization [38, 39]. During the domestication process, several agronomic traits including shattering [6], tillering, and axillary branching [40] underwent directional selection and improvement to become more suitable for cultivation. However, the drought tolerance of the cultivated foxtail millet has become weaker than that of the wild ancestor, S. viridis during domestication. Mining favorable drought-tolerance alleles from S. viridis is an important way to improve drought tolerance in foxtail millet. Only Qie et al. [10] found that S. viridis contributed the favorable alleles for lateral root number and coleoptile length decrease under seedling drought stress. In the present study, 89% of the favorable alleles for QTLs related to PNP were contributed by S. viridis H1 (Table S5). PNP is closely related to the number of tillers and is one of the key determinant factor to the yield of foxtail millet [41]. Therefore, under drought stress from the filling to the mature stage, S. viridis contributed favorable alleles (increased value) of tillering to ensure higher yields in the offspring lines of the biparental segregation population.

Candidate genes involve several key signaling pathways related to drought tolerance

Plant drought tolerance is a complex trait involving several key signaling pathways, including the reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging system, abscisic acid (ABA)-mediated signaling pathway, and jasmonic acid (JA) signal transduction pathway [42–44]. Seita.5G312700 is homologous to Aux/IAA protein (OsIAA6), which can inhibit tiller bud growth by positively regulating the auxin transporter OsPIN1 and the tillering inhibitor OsTB1, thereby regulating tiller number in rice and participating in the ABA-mediated drought resistance pathway [30]. Seita.5G319000 showed homology to WRKY transcription factor 13 (OsWRKY13), which participates in drought and disease resistance by regulating the expression of upstream and downstream genes of salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) [31]. Seita.5G319500 showed similarity to OsRab7. Overexpression of OsRab7 can improve grain yield and enhance drought and heat tolerance in transgenic rice by regulating the content of osmotic adjustment substances, antioxidant enzyme activities, and the expression of abiotic stress-responsive genes, which is closely related to the ROS scavenging system [32]. PLA2 and LHD2, homologous to Seita.5G420900, act downstream of the gibberellin (GA) signaling pathway and regulate leaf maturation and tiller number [27, 28]. Furthermore, OsNAC10 (homolog of Seita.7G306400) is specifically expressed in rice roots, can increase root biomass, enhance drought resistance, and thereby improve grain yield under drought conditions [29]. The functional similarity indicates that the candidate genes are involved in the above signaling pathways. Drought stress promotes the specific expression and interaction of candidate genes to improve the drought tolerance in the biparental progeny lines, but their exact functions still need further verification.

Conclusion

In the constructed interspecific population, five highly drought‑resistant lines were screened according to the DRI, and the period of duration was identified as a key factor governing drought resistance in the interspecific recombinant inbred lines. A high‑density genetic map was constructed, including 3,029 bin markers, covering a total length of 709.34 cM with an average marker interval of 0.24 cM. Ten QTLs associated with yield component traits were identified. Seita.5G420900, Seita.7G306400, Seita.5G312700, Seita.5G319000 and Seita.5G319500 were predicted to be drought resistance-related candidate genes. These findings supply vital breeding resources and a theoretical basis for exploring the molecular mechanism in foxtail millet.
