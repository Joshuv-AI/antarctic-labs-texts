Perils of Permanent Fillers: A Case Report of Necrosis of the Upper and Lower Lip
Authors: Aref Nassar, Fadi Sleilati, Samer Abou Zeid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:This article discusses a case of total upper and lower lip necrosis caused by a foreign-body reaction to permanent filler injections, highlighting the risks associated with cosmetic procedures. Permanent fillers can lead to significant tissue damage, necessitating aggressive surgical treatment for their removal and reconstruction. The use of free tissue transfer, specifically the radial forearm free flap, is highlighted as an effective method for restoring lip function and aesthetics. Overall, this report underscores the importance of careful consideration and awareness of potential adverse outcomes in cosmetic procedures involving permanent fillers.

In recent years, cosmetic injections have surged in popularity as a minimally invasive approach to aesthetic enhancement. Although these procedures offer rapid results with minimal downtime, they are not without risk. Most complications are mild and resolve spontaneously; however, severe adverse events such as vascular compromise, infection, and tissue necrosis have been reported.1 Early complication rates for hyaluronic acid fillers are approximately 0.07% overall and 0.18% for lip injections,2 whereas permanent fillers carry substantially higher risk, with up to 64.6% of patients experiencing complications and 15.3% requiring surgical removal.3

Lip fillers are commonly used to increase lip volume, enhance definition, correct asymmetry, elevate the oral commissures, and smooth perioral rhytides. Complications may be classified by severity (mild, moderate, severe), timing of onset, and ischemic versus nonischemic nature.4 Permanent fillers can provoke early or late adverse reactions, including induration, hematoma, pain, discoloration, edema, infection, and foreign body granulomas.5,6 A subset of patients develops a giant-cell foreign body reaction, necessitating removal of the injected material. Histologically, these lesions consist of histiocytes, epithelioid cells, multinucleated giant cells, and variable inflammatory infiltrates.7 Management often requires aggressive surgical excision of all affected tissue, which may lead to disfigurement worse than the original aesthetic concern.8 We report a case of upper and lower lip necrosis following permanent filler injection complicated by granuloma formation and infection, successfully treated with free flap reconstruction.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 40-year-old woman presented to our clinic 10 years after undergoing permanent filler injection to the upper and lower lips for augmentation. Several months following the procedure, she developed multiple granulomatous nodules, persistent edema, and lip discoloration. Histopathologic examination of biopsy specimens revealed granulomatous inflammation with histiocytes and multinucleated giant cells consistent with foreign body granulomas.

She underwent multiple operations at another institution to remove these granulomas from the upper and lower lips using skin grafts, tongue flaps, and other local flaps for reconstruction following soft-tissue resection. The results were neither aesthetically nor functionally acceptable. Her skin grafts were complicated by Pseudomonas soft-tissue infection, resulting in necrosis of the upper and lower lips. Upon presentation to our department, the patient exhibited necrosis of the upper and lower vermilion, involvement of the oral commissures, drooling, speech alteration, and oral incompetence (Fig. 1).

Patient with total upper and lower lip necrosis at presentation.

Under general anesthesia, she underwent simultaneous reconstruction of the upper and lower lips. For the upper lip, the remaining upper lip subunit was deepithelialized, and a 4 × 5 cm radial forearm free flap was harvested for reconstruction (Fig. 2). The radial artery was anastomosed end-to-end to the right facial artery, and 1 vena comitans was anastomosed to the anterior jugular vein. The orbicularis oris muscle was intact and preserved. The lower lip was reconstructed using a stair-step advancement flap.9

Reconstruction of the upper lip using a forearm free flap and the lower lip using a stair-step advancement flap.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient subsequently underwent 3 minor revision procedures for flap defatting and shortening of the upper lip. Final outcomes demonstrated satisfactory functional and aesthetic restoration (Figs. 3 and 4). At 6-month follow-up, the patient demonstrated complete oral competence with no leakage of saliva or liquids, normal speech intelligibility, and no drooling (Drooling Severity and Frequency Scale 1/1).

DISCUSSION

Injectable fillers have become an integral component of aesthetic practice but are not without risk. Adverse effects may range from transient erythema and swelling to catastrophic outcomes such as blindness, necrosis, or infection.

Permanent fillers, in particular, can provoke severe inflammatory responses leading to foreign body granuloma formation and extensive tissue destruction.8 In advanced cases, complete surgical excision is necessary, followed by soft-tissue reconstruction to restore form and function. Total lip necrosis secondary to permanent filler injection, however, remains rare, with few documented cases in the literature. This rare presentation highlights the destructive potential of permanent fillers and the complexity of their surgical management.

Lip reconstruction aims to achieve optimal aesthetic and functional outcomes, including competence, mobility, and oral sphincter continuity. The reconstructive approach depends on defect characteristics (partial versus full thickness, size). Although local flaps such as the Abbe, Estlander, or Karapandzic flaps may be suitable for smaller or moderate defects, extensive lip loss is challenging to repair using regional tissues alone. In such cases, free tissue transfer offers the most reliable option for large or complex defects.

The radial forearm free flap is most commonly used due to its thin and pliable nature.10 Additionally, the palmaris longus tendon can be woven into remaining portions of the orbicularis oris or modiolus. Application of the described technique for facial reconstruction is usually used for cancer treatment, congenital pathologies, and posttrauma defects. We used this free flap to reverse complications of a devastating cosmetic procedure to restore lip function and aesthetics.

This case underscores the critical importance of prevention when using injectable fillers. Although careful technique and patient selection can minimize complications, adverse events from permanent fillers can be irreversible. Thorough patient counseling regarding both immediate and delayed risks is essential, as early recognition may reduce severity, but reconstruction may ultimately be required in cases of extensive tissue damage.

CONCLUSIONS

Permanent fillers can cause severe complications such as granuloma formation and lip necrosis, requiring complex reconstruction. This case highlights the role of free tissue transfer, particularly the radial forearm free flap, in restoring both function and aesthetics after filler-induced tissue loss.
