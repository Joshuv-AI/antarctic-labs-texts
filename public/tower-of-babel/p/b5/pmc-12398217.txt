Association between cardiometabolic index and sarcopenia: 2011–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination survey (NHANES)
Authors: Yi Shen, Jiayu Zhang, Xiuyue Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe cardiometabolic index (CMI) is a reliable marker used to assess the degree of obesity and lipid metabolism disorders in individuals. Emerging evidence indicates that disorders of lipid metabolism and sarcopenia share a common pathophysiologic basis. However, few studies have investigated the association between CMI and sarcopenia. Therefore, this study aimed to find the possible correlation between CMI and sarcopenia.MethodsThis study used cross-sectional research methods to investigate data on CMI, sarcopenia, and other covariates among participants in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2011 to 2018. We explored the relationship between CMI and sarcopenia through multivariate linear and logistic regression, and we performed sensitivity and subgroup analyses. Also, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and area under the curve (AUC) were utilized to evaluate the performance of CMI in identifying sarcopenia.ResultsOf the 4,808 adults aged 20 to 59 included in this study, 428 (8.90 %) were identified as having sarcopenia. A significant nonlinear association between CMI and sarcopenia was demonstrated in a generalized additive model (GAM). After inclusion of all covariates, CMI showed a significant association with the prevalence of sarcopenia (OR = 1.173(1.086–1.267), P < 0.001). CMI got an AUC of 0.69 in identifying sarcopenia. Finally, subgroup analyses showed that the association between CMI and sarcopenia was particularly pronounced in individuals who differed in marital status, poverty-to-income ratio, alcohol consumption, patients without diabetes, and patients with hypertension (P < 0.05).ConclusionThis study demonstrated that an elevated CMI was associated with an increased prevalence of sarcopenia. The finding suggests that CMI could serve as a potential marker for sarcopenia, highlighting the need for further research to explore the relationship between dyslipidemia and sarcopenia.Keywords: Cardiometabolic index, Sarcopenia, Dyslipidemia, NHANES, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a systemic skeletal muscle disorder consisting of persistent loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function that primarily affects the elderly [1]. The global prevalence of sarcopenia has been reported [2] to be 8 %–36 %. Sarcopenia not only affects the quality of life of older adults [3,4] but is also associated with a variety of adverse health outcomes, including falls, fractures, cognitive impairment, depression, and even death [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. At the same time, sarcopenia imposes a significant economic burden on the US healthcare system. According to relevant data, direct medical costs due to sarcopenia amount to $18.5 billion [9]. This figure is expected to continue to rise as the population ages [10].

Patients with sarcopenia are often accompanied by insulin resistance, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and other metabolic disorders, and metabolic disorders will, in turn, exacerbate the development of sarcopenia, forming a vicious circle. Therefore, the relationship between lipid metabolism and sarcopenia has received increasing attention from researchers in recent years. One study [11] found that disorders of lipid metabolism were significantly associated with the development and progression of sarcopenia; another study [12] showed that total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels in sarcopenia patients showed significant decreases. Yet others have reported conflicting results. According to a study conducted in China [13], individuals in the sarcopenia group exhibited significantly higher TG levels compared to those without sarcopenia. While the association between lipid metabolism disorders and sarcopenia has been explored in various studies, the evidence remains mixed and requires further clarification. Given the complexity of these relationships and the need for more definitive evidence, further research is essential to understand the potential role of lipid levels as indicators of sarcopenia risk.

The cardiometabolic index (CMI) was first proposed in 2015 by Wakabayashi and Daimon [14], who modified the lipid accumulation product (LAP) formula. They constructed the CMI by replacing TG and waist circumference (WC) with TG/ high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), respectively. Thus, CMI reflects not only the degree of obesity in an individual but also the level of blood lipids. Previous studies have shown that the CMI has promising applications in several areas, including the assessment of cardiovascular disease [15], diabetes mellitus [16], osteoporosis [17], and depression [18]. Yet whether CMI is robustly linked to sarcopenia remains uncertain and warrants further evidence.

Therefore, the present study investigated the association between CMI and sarcopenia based on cross-sectional data from the NHANES database from 2011 to 2018. The results of this study provide evidence for the potential role of lipids in sarcopenia.

Methods

Study population

Data for this study came from 4,808 participants in NHANES from 2011 through 2018. A total of 34,348 were disqualified for the following reasons: (1) age < 20 years (n = 16,539); (2) missing WC, height, and TG data (n = 1,3420); (3) missing Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) data and BMI data (n = 4,389). Ultimately, the study included 4,808 individuals (Fig. 1). All participants provided written informed consent by the NHANES protocol, which was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Research Ethics Review Board.

Flowchart of screening of participants in this study.

Definition of CMI

The CMI is derived from the product of WHtR and the TG to HDL-C ratio [14]. The units of WC and height are centimeters (cm), and the units of TG and HDL-C are mmol/L and mmol/L, respectively. The CMI is calculated using the following formula:

WHtR = WC (cm)/Height (cm);

CMI = TG (mmol / L)/HDL-C (mmol /L) × WHtR.

Definition of sarcopenia

From 2011 to 2018, DXA scans were administered to participants aged 20 to 59. This approach allowed us to assess appendicular lean mass (ALM), which is a key indicator for evaluating muscle mass [19]. According to the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH), sarcopenia is determined by the ratio of ALM to BMI. In this study, ALMBMI of less than 0.789 kg/m2 in male participants or 0.512 kg/m2 in female participants was considered to be sarcopenia [20].

Covariates included in this study

The covariates in this study included sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and the disease history of the participants. Of these, sociodemographic information included age, sex (male/ female), marital status (married/ unmarried/ widowed), educational attainment (less than high school/ higher than high school), ethnicity (Mexican American/ Other Hispanic/ Non-Hispanic White/ Non-Hispanic Black/ Other), and socioeconomic status measured by the poverty income ratio (PIR), categorized as <1.5, 1.5–3.0, >3, not clear. Lifestyle factors included smoking status (yes/ no), alcohol consumption (yes/ no), and disease history, including hypertension and diabetes mellitus, and patients with hypertension and diabetes mellitus were categorized into dichotomous groups (yes/ no). The methodology for collecting these variables is well documented in the NHANES Web site Survey Methodology and Analysis. https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/analyticguidelines.aspx.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted according to Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) guidelines using appropriate NHANES sampling weights and taking into account complex multistage whole cohort surveys. In descriptive analyses, weighted t-tests for continuous variables, and weighted chi-square tests for categorical variables, the analyses outlined the baseline characteristics of individuals categorized according to sarcopenia status, with categorical variables given as percentages and continuous variables reported as means and standard deviations. To examine the association between CMI and sarcopenia, multivariate regression models were used in three different models that accounted for the NHANES complex sampling design (sampling weights). In Model 1, no covariates were adjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for sex, age, and race. Model 3 was adjusted for sex, age, race, education level, marital status, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes status, and hypertension status.

Age, sex, race, education level, PIR, marital status, smoking status, alcohol use, diabetes, hypertension and CMI were performed to prove no severe collinearity (both variance inflation factor [VIF] < 2). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and area under the curve (AUC) were also utilized to evaluate the performance of CMI in identifying sarcopenia.

In the sensitivity analysis, the CMI was converted from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (quartiles) to assess its robustness. We also explored the nonlinear relationship between CMI and sarcopenia using generalized additive modeling (GAM). Subgroup analyses were performed using stratified multivariate logistic regression models stratified by factors including sex, age (<30/ 30–45/ >45), ethnicity, marital status, and educational attainment, PIR, tobacco use, alcohol use, hypertension, and diabetes. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R software (4.1.3) and Empower software.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 4,808 subjects were included in this study, of which the mean age was (39.42 ± 11.43) years. Of these participants, 49.58 % were male and 50.42 % were female. Of these, 25.71 % were Hispanic, and 74.29 % were non-Hispanic. The mean CMI was (0.72 ± 1.07). Moreover, the prevalence of sarcopenia was 8.90 % in this study. Regarding age, race, education level, PIR, drinking status, and health status (including hypertension and diabetes), there were significant differences between the two groups (P < 0.05). Typically, patients diagnosed with sarcopenia are more likely to be male, Mexican-American, older, drink alcohol, be less educated, be economically disadvantaged, and have high blood pressure and not have diabetes. In addition, higher CMI was observed in individuals with sarcopenia compared to those without sarcopenia. Table 1 shows weighted demographic baseline information for the included participants.

Correlation between CMI and sarcopenia

The present study explored the relationship between sarcopenia and CMI; as shown in Table 2, the risk of sarcopenia increased with increasing CMI. In model 3, the prevalence of sarcopenia increased by 17.3 % per unit increase in CMI [OR = 1.173, 95 %CI:(1.086, 1.267), P < 0.001], and this relationship was also present in model 1 [OR = 1.292, 95 %CI:(1.196, 1.395), P < 0.001], model 2 [OR = 1.209, 95 %CI:(1.117, 1.307), P < 0.001] were also present. Therefore, we further analyzed the CMI after dividing it into quartiles. In the fully adjusted model, the highest quartile of the CMI demonstrated a stronger significant correlation with the prevalence of sarcopenia [OR = 4.193, 95 %CI:(2.810, 6.256), P < 0.001] compared to the lowest quartile.

ROC curve analysis

In this study, ROC curve analysis was employed to evaluate the predictive performance of the CMI. The ROC curve analysis revealed that the optimal cutoff value of the CMI was 0.462. At this cutoff point, the sensitivity was 0.78, indicating the proportion of true positive cases that were correctly identified by the CMI was 78 %, while the specificity was 0.53, which means the proportion of true negative cases that were accurately detected was 53 %. The AUC was 0.69 (95 %CI:(0.67, 0.72) suggesting that the CMI has a moderate ability to distinguish between the presence and absence of the outcome of interest. An AUC value of 0.69 implies that the CMI has a certain level of predictive value, although it is not extremely high (Fig. 2). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed a P-value of 0.693, indicating that the model fitted the data well and that there was no significant difference between the predicted results of the model and the actual observed results.

Subgroup analysis

We performed a subgroup analysis to assess whether the relationship between CMI and sarcopenia is stable and reliable across populations and settings, as shown in Fig. 3. Interactions showed that gender, age, race, education level, and smoking status did not significantly affect the positive association between CMI and sarcopenia (P > 0.05). However, the association between CMI and sarcopenia was not consistent in some subgroups, and the effects of marital status, PIR, alcohol use, diabetes, and hypertension altered their associations (P < 0.05).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study involved 4,808 participants. A positive association between CMI and sarcopenia was observed. We also performed subgroup analyses and interaction tests. We found that the association between CMI and sarcopenia was similar in the subgroups of sex, age, race, education level, and smoking status. The positive correlation between CMI and sarcopenia was more pronounced in individuals with lower socioeconomic status, those who consumed alcohol, and those without diabetes or with hypertension. This study found that a high CMI was associated with an increased risk of developing sarcopenia, which is supported by previous studies [21]. Our findings are consistent with a recent study [22] showing that high CMI is associated with an increased risk of developing sarcopenia. However, our study not only conducted different subgroup analyses and applied various modeling methods but also added ROC curve analysis, which demonstrated the good predictive value of the model. In addition, a Korean study [23] found that WHtR predicted sarcopenia. However, the correlation between sarcopenia and TG/HDL-C ratio is controversial. A 2011–2014 NHANES survey study [24] found that a high TG/HDL-C ratio was significantly associated with reduced grip strength in older adults. Nevertheless, a study in China [25] showed that individuals with high TG/HDL-C ratios had a lower probability of developing sarcopenia. The reason for this may be the differences in age, sample proportion, race, and survey methodology between the two included samples.

Furthermore, we conducted subgroup analyses based on sex, age, and race. In this study, the association between CMI and sarcopenia was found to be more revelant in men, a result that is inconsistent with the results of a study conducted in Korea [26], which reported a higher risk of sarcopenia in women. The cause of this may be the difference in the age and race of the study population; the Korean study included Korean individuals aged 60 years and older, whereas the present study included individuals aged 20–59 years from different races. Of note, the present study found that the association between CMI and sarcopenia was more applicable in the alcohol-drinking population, which is generally consistent with previous findings. A survey of a Japanese adult population [27] observed a significant and stable positive correlation between alcohol consumption and muscle strength loss. A Chinese study [28] found that men who consumed >25 g of ethanol per day were three times more likely to have reduced muscle mass or grip strength than non-drinkers. There are also studies [29,30]suggesting that chronic excessive alcohol consumption may lead to dysbiosis of intestinal flora and autophagy-induced hyperammonemia. Leading to increased muscle proteolysis and decreased muscle protein synthesis through activation of muscle growth inhibitor, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), and developmental regulation and response to DNA damage 1 (REDD1). Chronic or excessive alcohol consumption is quite common in Western countries. In view of this, the implementation of interventions aimed at changing drinking habits can help to reduce the incidence of sarcopenia.

In this study, we observed a significant association between higher CMI and sarcopenia, which may be explained by several potential mechanisms. Firstly, the inflammatory response triggered by abnormal distribution and dysfunction of visceral adipose tissue may be the key factor [31]. An American study [32] showed that visceral fat accumulation is usually accompanied by elevated levels of inflammatory factors. This inflammatory environment may interfere with muscle metabolic processes and disrupt the balance between muscle protein synthesis and catabolism, promoting sarcopenia. Further, CMI-related metabolic abnormalities may affect neuromuscular transmission [33]. Impaired nerve signal transmission will reduce muscle contraction and motor coordination [34], which will lead to disuse atrophy of muscles in the long term and promote the progression of sarcopenia. In addition, high CMI levels are often accompanied by other risk factors, such as physical inactivity [35] and poor nutrition [36]. These factors interact with each other and together enhance the likelihood of an individual developing sarcopenia.

However, our study observed a stronger association between CMI and sarcopenia in patients without diabetes, which differs from some previous findings [[37], [38], [39]]. We have considered several potential explanations for this observation. One possibility is that our cross-sectional study design may not fully capture the dynamic relationship between diabetes and sarcopenia risk. This could be due to diabetic patients already exhibiting higher levels of inflammation and oxidative stress due to their condition [40], which might diminish the additional impact of CMI on sarcopenia risk. Consequently, CMI may play a more pronounced role in patients without diabetes who do not have the added metabolic stressors of diabetes. This result could also be influenced by confounding factors or interactions with additional variables not accounted for in our models. Furthermore, the specific definitions and thresholds used for diagnosing sarcopenia and diabetes might affect the observed associations. In our study, the sarcopenia diagnostic criteria established by the FNIH focused on appendicular lean mass index, which may interact differently with diabetes status compared to other definitions [41]. Therefore, we acknowledge the need for further research to clarify these relationships more clearly, particularly through longitudinal or intervention studies that can better explain the dynamic interactions between CMI and sarcopenia risk.

The present study has several major strengths. First, this study investigates the association between CMI and sarcopenia for the first time, providing new insights into the relationship between lipid metabolism and chronic disease. Second, the NHANES data used in this study is a nationally representative database with a large sample size and a rigorous multi-stage probability sampling design to ensure that the results are representative of the broader U.S. population. Third, three models were constructed, and subgroup analyses were performed in this study, which allowed for a more accurate assessment of the association and stability between CMI and sarcopenia. Finally, the acquisition of data related to the diagnosis and CMI of the patients with sarcopenia included in this study was based on DXA scans and laboratory tests, which minimized subjectivity and avoided recall bias. However, this study also has the following drawback: we recognized that the sarcopenia definition employed in this study, based solely on ALMBMI, may not fully capture the functional dimensions of this condition. Future investigations should consider incorporating additional functional metrics to achieve a more comprehensive understanding. Then, we did not have the means to include all covariates that influence the association of CMI with sarcopenia. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of this study limited our ability to determine a causal relationship between CMI and the prevalence of sarcopenia. Therefore, future studies need to conduct long-term randomized controlled trials to provide clearer evidence for establishing the relationship between CMI and the prevalence of sarcopenia.

Conclusions

This study revealed a significant positive correlation between CMI and sarcopenia using NHANES data, suggesting that CMI has the potential to serve as a new predictor of sarcopenia. Given the importance of early prevention of sarcopenia, and in light of the findings of this study, a comprehensive assessment of people with elevated CMI levels and targeted interventions, such as improving lipid metabolism disorders, changing drinking habits, strengthening nutritional support, and increasing physical activity and exercise, further research is needed to explore whether interventions targeting dyslipidemia and other related factors could influence sarcopenia risk.
