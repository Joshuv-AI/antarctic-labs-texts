Outsmarting NK surveillance: engineering stealth CAR T cells
Authors: Ana Karen Nunez Cortes, Rafet Basar
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Apodaca et al1 identified a rational engineering strategy to protect gene-edited allogeneic chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells from natural killer (NK) cell–mediated rejection by leveraging HLA-E single-chain trimer (ScT) expression.

Allogeneic off-the-shelf CAR T-cell therapies are highly promising in terms of rapid availability, scalability, and consistent product quality.2, 3, 4 However, clinical translation remains a major challenge because of fundamental immunologic barriers. Disruption of the T-cell receptor and major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules reduce graft-versus-host disease and host-versus-graft rejection, respectively,2 but the loss of MHC-I renders engineered cells vulnerable to NK cell–mediated clearance through the missing-self response.5 The innate immune barrier poses a challenge that must be resolved to reach the full potential of universal cell therapies.3,4 Recent strategies have also explored viral immune evasion mechanisms to enhance allogeneic CAR T-cell persistence,6 further highlighting the need for robust approaches to overcome immune rejection.

Apodaca et al developed a composite engineering approach to overcome the challenge posed by the innate immune barrier. Their strategy relies on the expression of an HLA-E ScT that presents the selected signal peptide (SP-1C: VMAPRTLIL), which selectively engages the inhibitory natural killer group 2A (NKG2A) receptor on NK cells while simultaneously minimizing activation through the natural killer group 2C (NKG2C) receptor.1 This model demonstrates that peptide selection is crucial to determine the balance between NK cell activation and inhibition and establishes SP-1C as an optimal ligand for the suppression of NK cell–mediated cytotoxicity.1,7

The authors demonstrated that HLA-E–mediated inhibition alone is insufficient to fully protect engineered cells from immune rejection by heterogeneous NK cell populations, because subsets that lacked NKG2A expression or those enriched in the adaptive NKG2C+ phenotypes remained capable of cytotoxicity and alloreactive rejection. Their approach required an added tier of engineering to eliminate the adhesion molecules CD54 and CD58, which are major components of immune synapse formation.8 With this combination of engineering, NK cell engagement was disrupted at a structural level, leading to significantly enhanced resistance to NK cell–mediated killing and enrichment of inhibitory receptor signaling1,8 (see figure).

Stealth CAR T cells engineered to evade NK surveillance. Allogeneic CAR T-cell gene editing leads to loss of MHC-I expression, thereby triggering NK cell activation via the missing-self response. HLA-E ScT presenting SP-1C peptide expression selectively engages inhibitory NKG2A receptors on NK cells, thereby decreasing activation. One step further, deletion of the adhesion molecules CD54 and CD58 disrupts immune synapse formation, which further limits NK cell engagement. In combination, these modifications strengthen the ability of engineered CAR T cells to evade NK cell–mediated clearance and exercise improved persistence and enhanced antitumor activity. In combination, these strategies present progress in the conceptual approach of designing off-the-shelf cellular therapies. A single immune evasion mechanism does not solidify protection from NK surveillance, whereas multilayered engineering of a signaling pathway and cell-cell interactions confers robust evasion.1,9 With an advanced engineering approach, the authors have reprogrammed the interaction between the host immune system and allogeneic engineered immune effector cells to avoid the issue of immune rejection.

The translational relevance of the strategy has been demonstrated through in vivo studies in humanized mouse models. Prolonged persistence and sustained tumor control were observed with universal anti-CD19 CAR T cells cells that expressed HLA-E SP-1C and lacked CD54/CD58 when compared with conventional gene-edited CAR T cells that were quickly eliminated in the presence of allogeneic NK cells. This is compelling evidence that overcoming NK cell–mediated rejection can directly translate into enhanced therapeutic efficacy.

Beyond these advances, there are still key areas to investigate. A critical consideration is that the heterogeneity of NK cell populations across patients will affect the durability of the approach. Cytomegalovirus seropositivity is often associated with the expansion of adaptive NKG2C+ NK cells and may weaken the efficacy of HLA-E–mediated inhibition. In addition, the long-term impact of multiplex gene editing on CAR T cell fitness, persistence, and safety requires further investigation.9 Although base editing reduces the risk for genotoxicity, the cumulative effects of multiple genetic modifications on cellular function are not yet fully understood. And lastly, NK cell–mediated rejection represents only 1 component of the host immune response, whereas additional mechanisms, including macrophage-mediated clearance and residual adaptive immunity, may also need to be addressed.

The authors have contributed to evolving the design principles of allogeneic cellular therapies. Future strategies will likely require active modulation of both the innate and adaptive immune responses, in addition to evasion of immune recognition. The framework established here, which combines inhibitory ligand engineering with targeted disruption of activating pathways, provides a modular and extensible approach that could be applied to other cell therapy platforms, including CAR-NK cells and stem cell–derived products.3,7

Apodaca et al provide a solution to overcoming NK cell–mediated rejection of allogeneic CAR T cells with a strategy rich in mechanistic underpinnings and translational relevance. These findings advance the field toward the development of truly universal, off-the-shelf cellular therapies and establish a blueprint for engineering immune-evasive, off-the-shelf cell therapy products.
