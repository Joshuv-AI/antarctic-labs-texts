Discovery of KC-1: a novel porcine dendritic cell-targeting peptide with potential applications in swine vaccine design
Authors: Bin Liu, Tian Xia, Chengjie Bian, Yanping Jiang, Wen Cui, Jiaxuan Li, Yijing Li, Li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDendritic cells (DCs) are key antigen-presenting cells essential for initiating and regulating immune responses. While DC targeting has proven to be an effective strategy for vaccine enhancement, and targeting peptides have been extensively utilized as efficient delivery tools in DC-targeted drug and vaccine development, there remains a notable scarcity of peptides specifically selected through porcine dendritic cell screening platforms. In this study, phage display biopanning was employed to identify a novel DC-targeting peptide, designated KC (KCCYPNQMAAFA). Systematic alanine-scanning mutagenesis identified the N-terminal hexapeptide KC-1 (KCCYPN) as the minimal functional epitope responsible for DC binding. In addition to DCs, KC-1 also demonstrated selective binding to bone marrow-derived dendritic cells(BM-DCs)and porcine alveolar macrophages (PAMs) but exhibited no interaction with intestinal porcine epithelial (IPI) cells, swine testis (ST) cells, or Vero cells. Further analysis revealed that KC-1 specifically binds to the N-terminal region (1-126 aa) of SLA-DRB1, which is a key domain of the MHC II β-chain involved in the formation of the peptide-binding groove. Using the Porcine Epidemic Diarrhea Virus (PEDV) S1 subunit as a model antigen, we further evaluated the immunomodulatory effects of KC-1 on DCs in vitro. The results demonstrated that KC-1-S1 significantly promoted DCs maturation and T-cell proliferation, accompanied by increased secretion of IL-4, IL-12, and IFN-γ. These findings indicate that KC-1-S1 enhances the overall immunogenicity of the antigen, triggering a robust and mixed immune response encompassing both Th1- and Th2-type cytokine profiles compared to controls. Collectively, this study characterizes the binding profile and preliminary immunomodulatory activity of the novel peptide KC-1 at the cellular level, providing basic experimental data and a reference for further development of porcine DC-targeted antigen delivery strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-026-05692-5.Keywords: Dendritic cell-targeting peptide, Swine Vaccine, SLA-DRB1, Phage display

Introduction

In recent years, the global swine industry has become a critical pillar for ensuring food security and driving rural economic development. China, in particular, has emerged as the world’s largest producer and consumer of pork, accounting for over half of the global annual output [1]. However, the intensification of farming practices has led to continuously increasing stocking densities, creating favorable conditions for the spread of major infectious pathogens [2]. Among swine populations, vaccination remains the most effective and sustainable strategy for controlling infectious diseases. To improve vaccine efficacy, durability, and broad-spectrum protection, researchers have explored various strategies. Among these, Dendritic cells (DCs)-targeted vaccine design has garnered increasing attention due to the crucial role of DCs in initiating and regulating immune responses, positioning this approach as a promising avenue for next-generation vaccine development [3].

DCs, as the most potent professional antigen-presenting cells in the immune system, are widely distributed at mucosal and cutaneous surfaces—the front lines of pathogen invasion—where they serve as central “immune sentinels” [4, 5]. Their core functions involve recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and danger signals via pattern recognition receptors (such as TLRs), efficiently capturing and processing antigens, and presenting them to naïve T cells via MHC class I and II molecules, thereby activating adaptive immune responses [6–11]. Mature DCs not only act as a bridge linking innate and adaptive immunity, but also coordinate the activity of B cells, NK cells, and other immune effectors through co-stimulatory molecules and cytokines, facilitating integrated humoral and cellular immunity [5]. Owing to this central immunological role, targeted delivery of antigens to DCs has become a key strategy to enhance vaccine performance. Current DC-targeting approaches typically employ antibodies, ligands, or targeting peptides specific to DC surface receptors to guide antigens, nucleic acids, or drugs to specific DC subsets [12–15].

Among these approaches, targeting peptides have gradually emerged as an important direction in DC-targeted vaccine design due to their unique advantages over antibodies and ligands. Peptides are small in size, structurally stable, low in immunogenicity, and capable of penetrating cell membranes. They are also easier to synthesize, modify (e.g., PEGylation or D-amino acid substitution), and scale up for production [16, 17]. Moreover, techniques such as phage display and receptor docking simulation allow for high-throughput and highly specific identification of functional peptides that bind to DC surface receptors, significantly simplifying the construction of antigen delivery systems [18]. Many of the identified targeting peptides have been validated to improve the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of vaccines. For instance, the mouse-derived DC-targeting peptide DCpep3 has been shown to enhance antigen uptake and antibody responses when used in a nanoparticle vaccine against porcine circovirus type 2 (PCV2) [19].The peptide hr-8, identified from the C-type lectin receptor DEC-205, specifically targets DCs via DEC-205 binding and promotes their maturation by upregulating the surface expression of MHC class II, CD80, and CD86, thereby enhancing antigen presentation and cross-priming of T cells [20]. Additionally, DP7-C, a cholesterol-modified peptide designed through computational optimization, exhibits dual functionality: it targets DCs while simultaneously enhancing their migration and lymph node homing, acting both as a delivery enhancer and an immunostimulatory adjuvant [21].

Despite the successful application of DC-targeting peptides in multiple species and their ability to enhance antigen uptake and immune responses, significant interspecies differences exist in the composition of DC subsets, expression of surface receptors, and antigen presentation pathways [22]. To address this critical need, this study first employed phage display technology to screen high-affinity linear peptides capable of specifically recognizing porcine DCs, and further analyzed their key amino acid motifs and binding sites on DC surface receptors. Based on these findings, the selected targeting peptides were fused with the S1 subunit of Porcine Epidemic Diarrhea Virus (PEDV). PEDV is a highly contagious enteric coronavirus that causes severe diarrhea, vomiting, and high mortality in neonatal piglets, leading to devastating economic losses in the global swine industry [23]. Since the S1 protein is the primary target for neutralizing antibodies, the fusion proteins’ effects on antigen presentation and DC activation were evaluated to assess their potential as a targeted vaccine strategy.

Collectively, these findings provide basic experimental evidence for porcine DC-targeted peptides and offer valuable references for further research on porcine DC-targeted vaccine development.

Materials and methods

Plasmids, bacterial strains, and cell lines

The pMD19-T and pMD19-T Simple cloning vectors were purchased from TaKaRa Biotechnology (Dalian, China). The recombinant plasmid pMD19-S1, containing the S1 gene of PEDV, and expression vectors (pET23a/TG1, pET23a-S1/TG1, and pCMV/TG1) were preserved by the Microbiology and Immunology Laboratory, College of Veterinary Medicine, Northeast Agricultural University (China). TG1 competent E. coli were purchased from TransGen Biotech (Beijing, China; Cat# CD101-01). The BHK, ST, and IPI cell lines were similarly maintained by our laboratory.

Animal ethics and care

Healthy, antibody-negative, 1-month-old female piglets (Large White crossbreed, 8–10 kg) purchased from the Acheng Experimental Practice Base of Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China). Animals were confirmed negative for PEDV, PRRSV, and PCV2 via RT-PCR and ELISA. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northeast Agricultural University (Approval No. NEAUEC0210337).

Primary cell isolation and culture

PBMCs and monocytes

PBMCs were isolated from 5-8-week-old piglets using Histopaque-1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; Cat# 10771-100mL) density gradient centrifugation at 800 × g for 25 min. red blood cells were removed using Lysis Buffer (Beyotime, China; Cat# C3702). Monocytes were obtained by plastic adherence in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, USA; Cat# 11875093) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS; Gibco; Cat# A5256701) for 6 h at 37 °C.

Mo-DC induction and maturation

Immature monocyte-derived dendritic cells (Mo-DCs) [24] were generated by culturing monocytes with 20 ng/mL recombinant porcine GM-CSF and 20 ng/mL pIL-4 (R&D Systems, USA; Cat# 711-PG and 654-P4). Fresh medium was partially replaced every 2 days. On day 4, immature Mo-DCs were harvested. For maturation, cells were stimulated with 1 µg/mL Lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Sigma-Aldrich; Cat# L2630) for 24 h.

Other primary cells

Porcine Alveolar Macrophages (PAMs) were obtained via lung lavage and centrifuged at 400 × g for 10 min. Bone Marrow-Derived DCs (BM-DCs) were generated from femoral bone marrow using the same cytokine cocktail. Rabbit Mo-DCs were induced using recombinant human GM-CSF and IL-4 (PeproTech, USA; Cat# 300-03 and 200-04).

Phage display biopanning

Peptides binding to porcine Mo-DCs were screened using the Ph.D.™-12 Phage Display Peptide Library Kit (New England Biolabs, USA; Cat# E8111L) through four rounds of affinity enrichment.

In the first round, 2 × 1011 pfu (Plaque Forming Unit) of the library were incubated with immature Mo-DCs (1 × 106/mL) at 4 °C for 30 min. To ensure exclusive specificity and eliminate clones with affinity for non-target cells, a rigorous subtractive biopanning procedure was implemented in rounds 2 to 4. Briefly, the amplified library from the previous round was pre-incubated with freshly isolated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs, 5 × 105/mL) from healthy piglets at 4 °C. The supernatant containing phages that did not bind to PBMCs was collected via centrifugation (800 × g, 3 min) and subsequently transferred to Mo-DCs for positive selection.

To enhance selection stringency, the subtractive incubation time was progressively reduced from 30 min (Round 2) to 20 min (Round 3) and finally 10 min (Round 4). Simultaneously, the concentration of Tween-20 in the washing buffer (PBST) was increased from 0.05% in the first round to 0.1%, 0.3%, and 0.5% in subsequent rounds.

Bound phages were eluted with 0.2 M Glycine-HCl (pH 2.2) containing 1 mg/mL BSA, followed by neutralization with Tris-HCl (pH 9.1). The titer of eluted phages was determined, and the phages were amplified and purified using the PEG/NaCl precipitation method for the next round of input. The binding specificity of candidate clones was validated by Phage ELISA, comparing their affinity for mature Mo-DCs against freshly isolated PBMCs. Individual positive clones were sequenced (Comate Bioscience, China) using the 96gIII primer. Selected peptides were synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) with N-terminal FITC or biotin modifications.

Binding and competition assays

Cell ELISA

Cells (105/well) were fixed with 0.03 mg/mL Poly-L-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat# P4707) and blocked with 2% BSA. Phage clones (1010 pfu/mL) were added, followed by detection with Mouse anti-M13 polyclonal antibody (LSBio, USA; Cat# LS-C149455; 1:1,500) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (ZSGB-Bio, China; Cat# ZB-2305; 1:4,000). A P/N ratio (OD450 of candidate / OD450 of negative M13 phage) ≥ 3 was defined as positive.

Competitive ELISA

Mo-DCs were pre-incubated with synthetic KC-1 peptide (0.1–100 µg/mL) at 4 °C for 1 h to saturate receptor sites, followed by the addition of phage clones (1010 pfu/mL). The inhibition rate (%) was calculated as: [1 - (OD450 with peptide / OD450 without peptide)] × 100%.

Competitive cell ELISA for alanine scanning

To identify the key functional residues of the KC peptide, a competitive cell ELISA was performed. Briefly, 96-well plates were pre-coated with 0.03 mg/mL Poly-L-lysine and seeded with porcine Mo-DCs (106 cells/well). After incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, cells were washed and then pre-incubated with various concentrations (1.5, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 40 µg/mL) of synthetic alanine-substituted KC variants for 1 h at 37 °C to saturate potential receptor sites. Subsequently, 0.05 µg/mL of biotinylated KC peptide (KC-BIOTIN) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 1 h. After washing, bound KC-BIOTIN was detected using HRP-conjugated Streptavidin (or the appropriate secondary antibody used in your study). Flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy

Peptide binding

106 Mo-DCs were incubated with 25 µg FITC-labeled peptides at 4 °C for 10 min. For microscopy, cells were stained with CD86-PE antibody (BD Pharmingen, USA; 1:100, Cat# 334098) and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Bio-Rad, USA).

Maturation markers

Mo-DCs were stained with PE-conjugated anti-porcine CD80 (Cat# 567240), CD86 (Cat# 569305), and MHC II (Cat# 569420) antibodies (all from BD Pharmingen, USA) at 1:100 dilution. Isotype-matched IgG controls were included to validate staining specificity. Data were acquired on a FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, USA).

Protein expression and western blot

Fusion proteins (KC-1-S1, NC-S1) were expressed in E. coli and purified via Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. For Western Blot, protein samples were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated with Mouse anti-His tag primary antibody (Beyotime; Cat# P2135; 1:2,000) in Primary Antibody Dilution Buffer (Beyotime; Cat# P0023), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Roche, USA).

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and receptor identification

Total membrane proteins were extracted using a membrane extraction kit (Abbkine, China; Cat# KTP3004). Biotinylated KC-1 peptide was conjugated to streptavidin-coated magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher; Cat# 88817) and incubated with membrane lysates at 4 °C overnight. Bound proteins were washed with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100, separated by SDS-PAGE, and visualized by silver staining (Sigma-Aldrich; Cat# 41105322). Specific bands were excised and analyzed via LC-MS/MS (Comate Bioscience, China) for protein identification (e.g., SLA-DRB1).

T-cell proliferation and cytokine analysis (MLR)

Mo-DCs treated with KC-1-S1, S1, NC-S1, or LPS were used as stimulator cells and co-cultured with allogeneic T lymphocytes (responder cells) at ratios of 1:1, 1:10, and 1:100. After 72 h, T-cell proliferation was measured using a CCK-8 kit (Beyotime, Cat# C0037). The levels of IFN-γ, IL-12, and IL-4 in the culture supernatants were determined using porcine-specific ELISA kits (R&D Systems, USA; Cat# EL985, NBP3-49793, and DY654, respectively) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA Extraction Kit (Feijie Biotech, China; Cat# 220011). cDNA was synthesized using the RT-SuperMix Kit (Toyobo, Japan; Cat# FSQ-301). Real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Roche; Cat# 4913914001) on a 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Relative mRNA levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method with β-actin as an internal control. Primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. All data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test. Statistical significance was defined asp < 0.01 or p < 0.05 as indicated in figure legends.

Results

Screening and identification of DCs-targeting peptides

Porcine monocytes were isolated using previously reported methods [25] and differentiated into DCs by stimulation with GM-CSF and IL-4, followed by maturation with LPS. The resulting cells exhibited typical dendritic cell characteristics in terms of morphology, surface markers (CD172a, MHC-II, CD80, CD86), and phagocytic activity (Supplementary Figure S1). To identify peptides capable of specifically binding to porcine DCs, a 12-mer phage display peptide library was employed for biopanning against porcine Mo-DCs. After four rounds of biopanning, targeted phages were enriched, and 204 monoclonal phage clones were randomly selected for sequencing, yielding 161 distinct peptide sequences (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Figure S2). The binding affinity of the 3 most frequent peptides (HS, KC, and SF) and randomly selected 13 peptides to porcine DCs was detected using phage ELISA (Supplementary Table 2). The results indicated that phages expressing HS, KC, and SF peptides exhibited stronger affinity for porcine Mo-DCs than other 13 peptides (Fig. 1A). Additionally, fluorescence microscopy and confocal laser scanning microscopy revealed that HS, KC, and SF peptides could bind to porcine Mo-DCs (Fig. 1B). While peripheral blood-derived Mo-DCs provided an accessible and robust model for initial peptide identification, their functional profile and receptor expression may not fully recapitulate those of tissue-resident DCs within the complex intestinal microenvironment. Given our primary focus on developing oral vaccines to induce mucosal immunity, it was imperative to validate whether these candidates maintain their targeting efficacy in situ. The intestinal mucosa represents the largest immune organ and a major inductive site for immune responses in swine; thus, we evaluated the binding affinity of the candidate peptides to porcine small intestinal tissues to assess their potential as carriers for targeted antigen delivery to the gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). As expected, these peptides demonstrated specific binding to DCs within piglet intestinal tissue sections (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, flow cytometry results showed that the targeting affinity of KC for porcine Mo-DCs was higher than that of the short peptide HS and SF (Fig. 1D). Based on the above results, we selected the KC peptide for subsequent experiments.

KCCYPN of the KC peptide are critical for targeted binding to porcine DCs

The amino acids involved in ligand-receptor recognition and binding that mediate ligand function are typically 5–8 or 2–3 in number [26]. To identify the amino acids responsible for the targeted binding function of the KC peptide with porcine Mo-DCs, this study used alanine scanning to mutate the amino acids of the targeting peptide KC one by one, followed by competitive cell ELISA experiments. Using this approach, we identified the key amino acid residues in the KC peptide responsible for binding to porcine Mo-DCs. The competitive ELISA results revealed that four specific amino acid residues (K1, Y4, P5, and N6) within the KC peptide constitute the essential binding sites for its targeted interaction with porcine Mo-DCs. In contrast, substitutions at positions C2, C3, Q7, M8, and F11 had minimal impact on binding affinity (Fig. 2A). Although simultaneous mutations at C2A and C3A did not significantly affect KC binding, the complete deletion of these two residues substantially reduced its binding to dendritic cells (DCs) (Fig. 2B left). Similarly, substitutions at Q7, M8, and F11 had no significant effect on binding affinity.

Notably, the truncated peptide KC-1 (KCCYPN), consisting of the N-terminal six amino acids of KC, exhibited stronger binding affinity to monocyte-derived dendritic cells (Mo-DCs) than the full-length KC peptide (Fig. 2B right). To exclude potential non-specific binding at high concentrations, the binding affinity of both peptides was further evaluated at low concentrations (1.5, 2.5, and 5 µg/mL). The results demonstrated that KC-1 exhibited stronger binding affinity to porcine dendritic cells than the full-length KC peptide at all tested concentrations (Fig. 2C). These findings collectively identify the N-terminal hexapeptide KC-1 (KCCYPN) as the functional core of the KC peptide responsible for selective and strong binding to porcine DCs.

KC-1 can bind to various immune cells

To evaluate the cell-specific binding ability of the targeting peptide KC-1, FITC-conjugated KC-1 peptide was incubated with various cell types, including porcine DCs, PAMs, porcine BM-DCs, rabbit Mo-DCs, IPI, ST cells, and Vero cells. Fluorescence microscopy revealed KC-1 binding to porcine Mo-DCs, PAMs, BM-DCs, and rabbit Mo-DCs, while no detectable fluorescence was observed in IPI, ST, or Vero cells (Fig. 3A). Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed the binding of KC-1 to porcine DCs, PAMs, porcine BM-DCs, and rabbit Mo-DCs (Fig. 3B). The specificity of the fluorescence shift in these professional APCs was validated by the absence of signal in non-target cells (IPI, ST, and Vero), which served as internal negative controls. Furthermore, this binding intensity was significantly higher than the baseline established by the non-specific control peptide (NC-FITC) in our preliminary screening (Fig. 1D), confirming that the observed shift was due to specific peptide-cell interaction rather than non-specific binding of the FITC fluorophore. These results indicate that KC-1 can bind to immune cells derived from different species, demonstrating its potential as a broadly applicable immune cells-targeting peptide.

SLA-DRB1 as the target protein of the KC-1

To identify the molecular target of KC-1, KC-1-Biotin was synthesized by introducing a lysine residue at the C-terminus of KC-1 for biotinylating, with an irrelevant sequence NC-Biotin serving as the negative control. HPLC analysis confirmed > 95% purity for both peptides (Fig. 4A), and ELISA demonstrated that biotinylating did not compromise KC-1’s specific binding to PAMs (Fig. 4B). Pull-down assays were performed by incubating PAM membrane proteins (Fig. 4C) with KC-1-Biotin, followed by capture using streptavidin-coated magnetic beads. SDS-PAGE and silver staining of the pull-down eluates revealed a distinct protein band between 25 and 40 kDa specifically enriched in the KC-1-Biotin group but absent in the negative control (Fig. 4D). This band was excised for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis, which identified 39 differentially expressed proteins, including 15 membrane proteins (Supplementary Table 3). Among the membrane proteins, the MHC class II β chain (SLA-DRB1) was identified with the highest confidence, while the MHC class I molecule (SLA-1) was likewise detected. Accordingly, SLA-DRB1 and SLA-1 were prioritized as candidate targets of KC-1 for validation. Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays demonstrated that KC-1 could interact with SLA-DRB1 and SLA-1 in PAMs and Mo-DCs, the interaction of KC-1 and SLA-DRB1 was effectively blocked by anti-SLA-DRB1 antibodies, however, anti-SLA-1 antibodies could not completely block the interaction of KC-1 and SLA-1 (Fig. 4E). Therefore, SLA-DRB1 was selected for subsequent experiments. Laser scanning confocal microscopy confirmed pronounced co-localization of KC-1 with SLA-DRB1 on the surface of PAMs and Mo-DCs (Fig. 4F), further validating their interaction. Collectively, these results confirm SLA-DRB1 was the primary target protein of the targeting peptide KC-1.

Determination of the binding domain of KC-1 to SLA-DRB1

To identify the binding domain of SLA-DRB1 that interacts with the KC-1, molecular docking analysis was performed using HPEPDOCK 2.0 software, resulting in 10 interaction models between KC-1 and SLA-DRB1. Among them, nine models predicted the binding domain of KC-1 to SLA-DRB1 to be between amino acids 38 and 111, while one model predicted the binding domain of the peptide KC-1 to SLA-DRB1 to be between amino acids 130 and 255 (Fig. 5A). Based on these predictions, SLA-DRB1 was divided into two fragments: SLA-DRB1a (amino acids 1–126) and SLA-DRB1b (amino acids 126–266) (Fig. 5A). IFA and Western blotting analyses showed that SLA-DRB1, SLA-DRB1a and SLA-DRB1b were successfully expressed (Fig. 5B and C). Co-IP assays demonstrated that KC-1 interacted with full-length SLA-DRB1 and the SLA-DRB1a (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results indicate that the region of SLA-DRB1 interacting with KC-1 is localized within residues 1–126.

The fusion expression of KC-1 with antigens promoted Porcine DCs maturation and T cell proliferation

To evaluate the immunomodulatory effect of the KC-1 on porcine DCs under antigenic stimulation, porcine DCs were incubated for 12 h with the peptide KC-1, PEDV S1 protein, the fusion proteins KC-1-S1, NC-S1, or LPS. qPCR analysis revealed that treatment with S1, NC-S1, KC-1-S1, and LPS significantly upregulated the expression of costimulatory molecules (CD40, CD80, CD86) and Toll-like receptors (TLR2, TLR6, TLR9), with the highest levels observed in the KC-1-S1 group (Fig. 6A–B). To further assess whether KC-1 fusion affects antigen presentation capacity, we prepared single-cell suspensions of piglet peripheral blood lymphocytes and conducted T cell proliferation assays. The results revealed that KC-1-S1 significantly enhanced T cell proliferation compared to other treatment groups (Fig. 6C), indicating that KC-1 fusion facilitates the ability of DCs to activate T cells. Additionally, ELISA analysis showed that KC-1-S1–treated Mo-DCs promoted the secretion of IL-4, IL-12, and IFN-γ (Fig. 6D), further demonstrating that the KC-1 enhances T cell differentiation. Taken together, KC-1 enhances the antigen-presenting ability of DCs and promotes DC-mediated T cell proliferation and differentiation.

Discussion

Antigen targeting to DCs has been recognized as an effective strategy to enhance vaccine efficacy [27–31]. Achieving efficient antigen delivery and precise recognition of DCs is critical for the development of DC-targeting vaccine strategies. Previous studies have demonstrated that screening ligands for DC surface receptors, generating specific antibodies against DC surface molecules, and developing DC-specific targeting peptides are all feasible approaches for antigen delivery [32–35]. However, each of these methods has certain limitations [36, 37]. Compared with antibody-based targeting strategies, short targeting peptides possess several advantages, including lower immunogenicity, improved tissue penetration, and relatively simple structural properties [18, 38, 39].

In the present study, a DC-targeting peptide designated KC, consisting of 12 amino acids, was identified using phage display technology. Protein–protein interactions are often mediated by a limited number of critical amino acid residues [40, 41]. In addition, shorter peptides may facilitate target recognition while maintaining favorable membrane permeability and low immunogenicity [42]. Therefore, alanine-scanning mutagenesis was performed to identify the functional residues within KC. The results demonstrated that the first six N-terminal amino acids constitute the key functional region responsible for DC binding. To further evaluate the specificity of KC-1, its affinity toward different cell types was examined. KC-1 also exhibited measurable affinity for macrophages, which may be attributed to the fact that both DCs and macrophages are professional APCs sharing partially overlapping surface molecular expression profiles. These findings suggest that KC-1 may interact with multiple professional APC populations, prompting further investigation into its molecular binding mechanism.

Because PAMs are readily obtainable, easily cultured, and possess representative APC-associated molecular characteristics [43, 44], they were selected for screening potential KC-1-interacting proteins using pull-down assays coupled with mass spectrometry. Among the identified candidates, SLA-DRB1, the β chain of MHC class II molecules, attracted particular attention because of its high-confidence identification in the MS results. The interaction between KC-1 and SLA-DRB1 was subsequently validated in porcine Mo-DCs. Since SLA-DRB1 is broadly expressed on professional APCs [11, 45], this finding provides a molecular explanation for the ability of KC-1 to bind multiple APC types. Although SLA-1 was also identified with relatively high confidence in the MS analysis, subsequent validation experiments did not provide sufficient evidence supporting a stable interaction with KC-1 under our experimental conditions. Therefore, the present study focused primarily on SLA-DRB1.It should also be noted that SLA-DRB1 is a highly polymorphic MHC class II molecule in pigs, and polymorphisms in MHC molecules have been reported to influence peptide-binding characteristics, structural dynamics, and antigen presentation properties [46, 47]. Therefore, whether KC-1 exhibits comparable binding efficiency across distinct SLA-DRB1 alleles remains to be determined.

Identifying the precise binding region between KC-1 and SLA-DRB1 is important for understanding the molecular basis underlying KC-1-mediated APC targeting. Previous reports have shown that short targeting peptides frequently recognize flexible or solvent-exposed regions of surface proteins without substantially disrupting their native biological functions [48, 49]. Accordingly, this study evaluated the binding capacity of KC-1 toward different structural domains of SLA-DRB1. Most prediction models suggested that KC-1 preferentially binds to the unstructured region encompassing amino acids 1–126 of SLA-DRB1. Experimental validation further confirmed that the binding site of KC-1 is localized within this region. Similar observations have been reported for other MHC-associated ligands that preferentially interact with unstructured or solvent-accessible domains [50, 51]. Since the identified binding region is located outside the predicted structured domain of SLA-DRB1, KC-1 binding may exert minimal interference with the native antigen-presentation functions of SLA-DRB1. However, this possibility still requires further functional verification.

Notably, KC-1 was also capable of binding rabbit Mo-DCs, consistent with our previous observations. Earlier studies identified a 12-mer peptide termed “DCpep” using human CD1a+DRbrightCD11cbrightDCs, and this peptide likewise exhibited varying degrees of affinity toward DCs from multiple species, including poultry, dogs, horses, and cats [52]. The cross-species binding activity of KC-1 may be associated with its recognition of structurally conserved and solvent-exposed regions of MHC class II molecules or other APC-associated surface proteins shared among mammalian species [53, 54]. These findings suggest that KC-1 may possess broader applicability beyond porcine vaccine platforms, similar to previously reported DC-targeting peptides [55, 56].

Although KC-1 itself lacks immunogenicity, the PEDV S1 protein is capable of inducing potent neutralizing antibody and cellular immune responses [57–63]. Therefore, KC-1 was fused with the PEDV S1 antigen to evaluate its immunomodulatory effects on porcine DCs. Compared with recombinant S1 protein alone, KC-1-S1-treated Mo-DCs exhibited significantly enhanced expression of DC maturation-associated surface molecules, indicating that KC-1-mediated targeting promotes antigen-stimulated maturation of porcine DCs. Upon antigen recognition, activated DCs migrate to lymphoid tissues, where they present antigens and secrete cytokines that regulate T-cell activation and differentiation [64–66]. Based on this rationale, a mixed lymphocyte proliferation assay was performed to evaluate T-cell proliferation mediated by recombinant protein-stimulated DCs.

Furthermore, Mo-DCs incubated with KC-1-S1 secreted elevated levels of IFN-γ, IL-12, and IL-4. The simultaneous upregulation of Th1-associated cytokines (IFN-γ and IL-12) and the Th2-associated cytokine IL-4 suggests that KC-1-mediated antigen delivery tends to induce broad T-cell activation rather than a strongly polarized helper T-cell response. These findings indicate that the KC-1 fusion strategy enhances antigen-driven adaptive immune activation. Similar observations have also been reported in previous studies demonstrating that DC-targeting peptide-mediated delivery of PEDV antigens can enhance cellular immune responses [60]. It should be noted that IL-13 has been reported to exhibit higher expression levels and greater responsiveness in porcine Th2 immune responses compared with IL-4, and may therefore serve as a more sensitive indicator of Th2-type immunity [67]. Therefore, future studies should consider incorporating IL-13 as an additional Th2-associated cytokine to further refine the evaluation of immune polarization induced by KC-1-mediated antigen delivery.

Although the present study demonstrated that KC-1 can effectively enhance antigen uptake and immune activation in porcine DCs in vitro, its ability to induce mucosal immune responses has not yet been demonstrated. In this study, the PEDV S1 protein was primarily used as a model antigen to validate the antigen-presentation-enhancing effect mediated by KC-1. Therefore, the current findings should mainly be interpreted as evidence supporting the APC-targeting and immunostimulatory properties of KC-1, rather than direct proof of pathogen-specific mucosal immune effects. Future studies incorporating appropriate in vivo immunization models as well as oral or mucosal delivery platforms will be necessary to further evaluate the potential application of KC-1 in mucosal immune induction.

In summary, this study identifies KC-1 as a novel APC-targeting peptide capable of interacting with SLA-DRB1 and enhancing antigen-induced activation of porcine DCs and T cells in vitro. These findings provide mechanistic insight into peptide-mediated DC targeting and offer molecular research references for the future development of precision DC-targeted vaccine strategies in pigs.
