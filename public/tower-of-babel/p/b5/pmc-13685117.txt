Field-based evaluation of hand–arm vibration (HAV) and heat exposure among grounds maintenance workers in the southeastern United States: a pilot study
Authors: Seunghyeon Yang, Nathan Chen, Claudiu T Lungu, Jonghwa Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this pilot study was to evaluate simultaneous exposure to hand-arm vibration (HAV) and heat among grounds maintenance workers.MethodTen groundskeepers employed at a southern US institution were recruited. Individual workers’ exposure to HAV and heat was monitored during their regular work shift for three days. HAV exposure was measured using tri-axial vibration dosimeters worn on both hands of the participants to obtain daily vibration exposure (A(8)) and vibration total value (ahv) for each power tool used. Daily HAV exposure dose was determined using ahv for all tools used and corresponding tool operation duration. Lifetime cumulative HAV exposure dose (ahv−lifetime) was determined based on ahv for all tools used and exposure history obtained from a questionnaire. Personal heat exposure was evaluated using a wearable device to collect estimated core body temperature (CBT). Environmental heat metrics, including heat index (HI) and wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT), were determined using a WBGT monitor. Environmental parameters, including ambient temperature, relative humidity, and solar radiation, were also measured using a portable weather station.ResultsMean A(8) values of the ten individual workers ranged from 0.8 to 3.3 m/s2 for the right hand and 0.7 to 2.9 m/s2 for the left hand. Tool-specific mean ahv ranged from 2.3 to 4.8 m/s2 for the right hand and 2.6 to 6.2 m/s2 for the left hand. The daily HAV exposure dose ranged from 6.2 to 23.7 m/s2 for the right hand and from 4.6 to 23.4 m/s2 for the left hand. The ahv−lifetime range was 1,128.5 to 172,333.0 and 1,102.4 to 161,462.6 h∙m/s2 for the right and left hands, respectively. The daily mean of estimated CBT of the individual workers ranged from 36.7 to 39.1 °C. The daily mean HI and WBGT values were from 20.7 to 48.9 °C and from 17.5 to 37.5 °C, respectively. The average ambient temperature, relative humidity, and solar radiation were 30.3 ± 4.1 °C, 59.9 ± 14.5%, and 217.8 ± 248.5 W/m2, respectively.ConclusionOne groundskeeper’s three-day average A(8) exceeded the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) action limit (AL) of 2.5 m/s2. During the three-day full-time shift, 19.0% of total work hours exceeded the upper normal CBT range of 38 °C, and 66.0 and 65.4% of total work hours were at or above the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA)’s proposed high heat trigger for HI (32.2 °C) and WBGT (26.7 °C for heavy workload), respectively. These findings indicate that continuous monitoring for heat strain is needed for outdoor workers whose jobs require a high workload especially under the southeastern US summer climate.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12995-026-00523-7.Keywords: Hand-arm vibration (HAV), Heat stress, Core body temperature (CBT), Wearable monitoring devices, Groundskeepers, Heat index (HI)

Background

Grounds maintenance workers regularly use power equipment to maintain outdoor environments, resulting in occupational exposure to hand-arm vibration (HAV). Employment of groundskeepers is increasing with an expected growth of 4% over the next 10 years, higher than the average for all occupations in the United States [1]. Hence, a growing number of groundskeepers, especially in the southern United States, are expected to be simultaneously exposed to HAV and heat stress. Although such outdoor workers face these occupational hazards, the literature on simultaneous field assessment of personal HAV and heat exposure using integrated monitoring approaches is limited.

Excessive exposure to HAV can induce hand-arm vibration syndrome (HAVS), a complex of peripheral vascular, musculoskeletal, and neurological disorders [2–5]. HAVS may become irreversible if it is not appropriately remedied at an early stage [6, 7]. Secondary Raynaud phenomenon, also known as vibration-induced white finger (VWF), is the best known vascular disease of HAVS. In the United States, the workplace prevalence of HAVS among power tool users was reported to range from 20 to 50%, while prevalence data for specific occupations is unavailable [8]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the only recent data available on the prevalence of HAVS in the US., indicating that there is a huge knowledge gap of the extent of hand-transmitted vibration (HTV) exposure and health status in the US workforce, including grounds maintenance workers. It is well documented that users of hand-held power tools including grass trimmers, brush cutters, and chainsaws, which landscaping and groundskeeping workers commonly use, are at high risk of HAVS [2, 9].

Both duration and magnitude of HAV exposure are known to be positively associated with HAVS [3, 10]. The conventional vibration measurement utilizes accelerometers that are mounted directly on the tools, which does not necessarily reflect personal vibration exposure [11–13]. More recently, hand adapters which an accelerometer is attached to or inserted in have been increasingly adopted by researchers to capture the true HTV at the interception between the hand and tool handle [11, 14–16]. The vibration dosimeter developed by SVANTEK, for example, uses a palm adapter which allows to measure acceleration values while users hold the tool handle throughout the work shift [17].

While there is no Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) standard for vibration exposure, guidelines for the measurement and evaluation of HAV are available, including the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 5349-1 and 5349-2: Mechanical vibration —Measurement and evaluation of human exposure to HTV [18, 19]. The American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH), American National Standards Institute (ANSI) S2.70-2006 (R2020), and the European Union (EU) Directive 2002/44/EC recommend the daily exposure action value at 2.5 m/s2 and the daily exposure limit value at 5 m/s2 [20–22].

Overexposure to heat can cause heat-related illnesses, including heat rash, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke, and it also may contribute to vascular or neurological disorders [23–27]. Heat risks are expected to increase as global temperatures are estimated to increase approximately 1.5 °C above the pre-industrial level between 2030 and 2052 [28, 29]. In occupational settings, wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) is a widely used heat stress index, designed to reflect the human body’s response to external heat by taking the effects of ambient air temperature, relative humidity, air movement, and radiation into account [30, 31]. The heat index (HI), which describes perceived heat based on ambient air temperature and relative humidity, is also commonly used [32, 33].

OSHA proposed a heat standard in 2024, in which two heat trigger levels using HI and WBGT were proposed, an initial heat trigger of a HI of 26.7 °C and a WBGT equal to the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) Recommended Alert Limit (RAL) and a high heat trigger of a HI of 32.2 °C and a WBGT equal to the NIOSH Recommended Exposure Limit (REL). NIOSH defines the metabolic rate of 350–465 watts (mean: 407.5 watts) for heavy workload. For occupations like groundskeepers whose job typically involve heavy workload, the initial heat trigger and high heat trigger in WBGT are calculated to be 23.1 and 26.7 °C, respectively, at 407.5 watts [31, 34].

However, those environmental indices do not account for individual physiological responses to heat. On the other hand, core body temperature (CBT) reflects the internal thermal state of the human body and is regularly maintained within a range of 37 ± 1 °C for normal bodily functions [31]. Thus, CBT can be considered a potential heat strain indicator to help prevent severe heat-related illnesses in particular. In recent years, wearable devices that provide estimated CBT have become popular as an alternative to the conventional CBT measurement methods, such as rectal, esophageal, pulmonary artery, and ingestible pill, whose use is generally limited during physically demanding tasks in occupational settings because of invasiveness and discomfort [35–37]. The SlateSafety (SS) wearable device, for example, estimates CBT in real time using proprietary algorithms based on heart rate measurement through the photoplethysmography (PPG) technique. This method provides a non-invasive approach for estimating CBT and convenient alternative to the conventional CBT measurement methods [38].

This pilot study aimed to simultaneously evaluate the groundskeepers’ personal exposure to HAV and heat using the vibration dosimeter and the wearable CBT device, under real-world occupational settings. Environmental heat exposure was also evaluated using the traditional HI and WBGT index. Because exposure to both HAV and heat has been associated with vascular health, the combined exposure data will allow further investigations into their potential combined effects on peripheral vascular health. Given the small study group of ten grounds maintenance workers, this pilot study was intended to provide preliminary exposure data by simultaneously assessing HAV and heat exposure in outdoor occupational environments.

Methods

Study population and monitoring

IRB approval was obtained prior to the study (UAB IRB #300012832). Ten groundskeepers who were employed at a higher education institution in Alabama were recruited. Inclusion criteria for the study were workers who regularly operated power tools outdoors and were over 18 years old. No additional exclusion criteria were applied. Research assistants observed each study participant and recorded detailed daily and hourly activities during the monitoring period. Study participants used grass trimmers, backpack blowers, riding blowers, an edger, seating mowers, a riding mower, hedgers, and a pole saw during grounds maintenance. The details of power tools that participants used are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. The groundskeepers typically involved intense arm and truck work, prolonged periods of standing, walking, and bending because of the use of power tools and equipment, which can be categorized as a heavy workload [22, 31]. One to three workers were grouped together and daily tasks of each group were assigned by the crew manager. The entire data collection was performed from June 2025 through September 2025.

Evaluation of HAV exposure

Each study participant was monitored for HAV for 3 separate days over a one-week period during their regular work shift from 6:30 AM to 3 PM, except for lunchtime (11 AM to 12 PM). The evaluation of HAV exposure followed our previous study based on ISO 5349-1: Mechanical vibration —Measurement and evaluation of human exposure to hand-transmitted vibration— Part 1: General requirements [19, 39]. The real-time vibration data were collected using tri-axial vibration dosimeters (SV103, Svantek SP. Z O. O., Warszawa, Poland). The participants were wearing the adapter of the vibration dosimeter that contained a tri-accelerometer on both palms and the meter unit on both arms for their entire work shift from 6:30 AM to 3 PM, except for dining and breaks. Most participants did not wear gloves during routine tasks, while several workers wore cloth or nitrile gloves. When gloves were used, the palm adapter was placed under the gloves to ensure the collection of the workers’ true exposure data. All data collected by vibration dosimeters were downloaded through Supervisor software version 1.8.21 (Svantek SP. Z O. O., Warszawa, Poland).

The pre- and post-calibrations of the vibration dosimeters were conducted every day at an acceleration of 10 m/s2 and frequencies at 79.58 and 159.2 Hz using a vibration calibrator (SV110, Svantek SP. Z O. O., Warszawa, Poland). The vibration dosimeter collected frequency-weighted root-mean-square (i.e., r.m.s.) acceleration values from the three axes (ahwx, ahwy, and ahwz) in one-third octave bands with center frequencies ranging from 0.8 to 1,600 Hz at one-second intervals. Vibration total value (ahv) was then computed using Eq. 1 [19]. Abnormally high ahv values exceeding 50 m/s2 were excluded from data analysis, consistent with our previous study [39].

Daily HAV exposure, A(8), which represents the standardized 8-hour equivalent frequency-weighted vibration magnitude for overall daily exposure, was calculated for each individual worker using Eq. 2 [19]. The A(8) data was compared to the ACGIH AL.

For the determination of tool vibration for each individual, the average vibration magnitude in ahv for each tool type was calculated as the time-weighted r.m.s. value for the corresponding operation duration of each tool. Evaluation of heat exposure

Heat strain was assessed using estimated CBT as a physiological proxy indicator in this study. Estimated CBT was obtained using the SS wearable monitor (BAND V2, SlateSafety, Georgia, US). The SS device was positioned on the study participants’ left triceps with direct skin contact, placed above the vibration dosimeter meter unit while avoiding tattoos or scars [38]. Estimated CBT was measured concurrently with the HAV exposure monitoring. Estimated CBT data were continuously collected at 10-second intervals throughout the monitoring period and were automatically uploaded in real-time to a web-based cloud platform for data storage and monitoring [38]. The estimated CBT data below 36 °C were considered outliers based on the ACGIH TLVs and Biological Exposure Indices (BEI)s guideline [22].

Environmental heat stress was assessed using the HI and WBGT index along with other environmental parameters. HI and WBGT index were obtained from a waterless WBGT monitor (QUESTemp 46, TSI, IL, US). Ambient temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), and solar radiation (W/m2) were measured using a portable weather station (MicroRX station and sensors, Onset HOBO, MA, US). Both the weather station and the waterless WBGT monitor were set up on a tripod at 1.2–1.5 m near the groundskeeper’s workplace throughout the monitoring period from 6:30 AM to 3 PM. HI and WBGT data were compared with the OSHA’s proposed heat standard to evaluate the heat stress to which groundskeepers were exposed during the work shift [34].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted to summarize HAV exposure data including A(8), ahv for each tool, and ahv−lifetime. The error of ahv was calculated by time-weighted r.m.s. values using Eq. 6.

The environmental heat parameter and metrics, including ambient temperature, relative humidity, solar radiation, HI, and WBGT and physiological heat response (i.e., CBT) were summarized by conducting descriptive statistics.

Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk tests were conducted for normality of the heat-related variables, including CBT, ambient air temperature, relative humidity, solar radiation, HI, and WBGT [41].

Correlation analyses were conducted using participant-day summary values to evaluate the association between CBT and environmental heat parameter/metrics. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to assess their linear associations [42]. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (rs) were additionally computed as part of a sensitivity analysis [42]. All statistical analyses were performed with SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, US). A two-sided α level of 0.05 was used.

Figure 1 shows a summary of HI and WBGT during the entire sampling period. The mean HI was 34.5 ± 5.4 °C with a range of 20.7–48.9 °C (Fig. 1.a). Based on OSHA’s proposed HI thresholds (initial heat trigger of 26.7 °C and high heat trigger of 32.2 °C), 90.9% and 66.0% of total work hours were at or above the initial and high triggers, respectively. The mean WBGT was 28.4 ± 3.6 °C with a range of 17.5–37.5 °C (Fig. 1.b). Using OSHA’s proposed WBGT threshold for heavy workloads at 407.5 watts (initial trigger of 23.1 °C and high heat trigger of 26.7 °C), 93.4% and 65.4% of study participants’ total work hours were at or above the initial and high triggers, respectively [31, 34]. The corresponding exposure durations that exceeded the initial and high trigger levels were 169.2 and 122.8 h for HI and 178.8 and 125.0 h for WBGT, respectively.

Discussion

Hand arm vibration (HAV) exposure

The mean daily vibration exposure, A(8), was 2.1 ± 0.6 m/s2 for the right hand and 2.1 ± 0.5 m/s2 for the left hand. The A(8) values were comparable between hand sides while all the workers were right-handed. The A(8) values of three participants (CBB, CBC, and CBJ) exceeded the ACGIH AL of 2.5 m/s2 on the right hand at least one day during the three-day monitoring period. The A(8) of four participants (CBB, CBC, CBG and CBJ) exceeded the AL on the left hand at least one day during the monitoring period. One participant (CBJ) exceeded the AL on all monitoring days and showed the highest three-day mean A(8), 3.0 ± 0.3 and 2.9 ± 0.1 m/s2 for the right and left hands, respectively. Daily HAV exposure was comparable between the right and left hands although all study participants were right-handed. Several workers exceeded the ACGIH AL of 2.5 m/s² during the monitoring period, but all workers remained below the ACGIH TLV of 5 m/s². The individual exposure results will further provide data to examine potential associations with relevant health outcomes.

The hedgers and grass trimmers had the highest mean ahv values exceeding 5 m/s2 among the eight types of power tools observed (Table 3). On the other hand, the backpack showed the lowest mean ahv at below 2.5 m/s2. These findings were comparable to our previous field study, which reported mean ahv values (right hand/ left hand) for hedgers (4.1/3.1), grass trimmers (5.1/5.0), and backpack blowers (2.4/1.4), respectively. The findings will help occupational safety and health professionals develop exposure assessment and control strategies to reduce HAV exposure.

With respect to each tool’s contribution (%) to the average daily HAV exposure dose (right hand/left hand), the grass trimmers showed the highest proportion (25.0/24.9), followed by the seating mowers (17.0/18.7), the hedgers (17.5/15.9), the pole saw (12.4/14.6), the edger (8.3/8.1), the backpack blowers (9.2/7.1), the riding mower (5.7/5.7), and the riding blowers (4.7/4.5) (Supplementary Table 3). In the previous study, the contribution of grass trimmers (22.0/22.3), riding mowers (18.2/19.2), and seating mowers (14.6/18.4) to the daily dose were on the top three while riding mowers’ contribution shown in the present study was among the least two (5.7/5.7) [39].

The wide range of vibration magnitudes was observed for most hand-held powered tools, except for the backpack blowers (Table 3). For example, hedger showed the range of mean ahv values from 3.8 to 20.3 m/s2, which was relatively wider than riding mower (2.7 to 3.2 m/s2) and riding blowers (2.7 to 3.6 m/s2). These results may imply that vibration magnitude of hand-held tools can vary depending on individual factors such as contact force and posture. The use of hand-held power tools may require further consideration in control strategies (e.g., training) to help reduce HAV exposure.

The daily HAV exposure dose range, 6.2–23.7 m/s2 and 4.6–23.4 m/s2 for the right and left hands, respectively, was larger than our previous study results, 0.5–10.3 m/s2 and 0.4–10.1 m/s2 for the right and left hands, respectively [39]. The cumulative lifetime HAV (ahv−lifetime) varied between study participants and hand sides across all tool types. Although mowers generally produced relatively lower daily vibration magnitudes (2.5 to 3.1 m/s2) than hand-held power tools (e.g., grass trimmers, edger, hedgers, and pole-saw: 3.7 to 6.5 m/s2), their longer operation durations contributed to higher mean ahv−lifetime: 9,829.7 and 11,792.5 m/s2 for seating mowers, and 3,155.8 and 3,313.6 m/s2 for riding mower for the right and left hands, respectively. Grass trimmers (14,570.1 and 14,567.9 m/s2 for the right and left hand, respectively) were the only hand-held tools estimated to generate higher ahv−lifetime values than seating mowers because of the longer exposure duration.

Heat exposure

The mean CBT was 37.7 ± 0.4 °C, and 19.0% of groundskeepers’ work hours exceeded 38.0 °C during the entire work shift. The highest CBT was observed in CBI: 38.1 ± 0.4 °C with 33.0% of total work hours exceeding 38.0 °C. The lowest CBT was observed in CBA and CBE: 37.3 ± 0.2 °C and 37.3 ± 0.1 °C with 0% of total work hours exceeding 38.0 °C. Most study participants experienced estimated CBT values above 38.0 °C during at least part of the three-day sampling period. These findings may suggest that physiological heat strain can vary between workers even under similar environmental heat conditions. Individual factors, such as work intensity, hydration status, and heat acclimatization, may have contributed to the variability [43, 44]. The individual CBT data will further assist in examining potential associations with vascular health, a common health outcome associated with heat exposure.

Groundskeepers in this study were exposed to a mean HI of 34.5 ± 5.4 °C and a mean WBGT of 28.4 ± 3.6 °C, exceeding the OSHA’s proposed high heat triggers. The proportion of study participants’ total work hours that exceeded the OSHA’s proposed high heat trigger in WBGT for heavy workload (26.7 °C at a metabolic rate of 407.5 W) was approximately 65.4% (125.0 h) [31, 34]. The results indicate the potential risk of heat-related illness among outdoor workers during summer in the southeastern US. The WBGT and HI values are comparable to the studies conducted among outdoor workers in the southeastern United States, including grounds maintenance workers in eastern North Carolina, where mean WBGT was 27.76 ± 3.05 °C for 104 days monitoring period in 2015 to 2016, and outdoor workers in Florida, where summer HI and WBGT averaged approximately 33.7 °C and 28.8 °C, respectively, from 2015 to 2017 [45, 46]. These findings suggest that outdoor workers across the southeastern region are routinely exposed to WBGT levels approaching or exceeding the recommended OSHA’s proposed heat triggers, especially during summer.

The weak Pearson and Spearman’s correlation coefficients (r and rs 0) and non-significant correlations (p > 0.05) observed between CBT and environmental heat variables (i.e., ambient temperature, relative humidity, radiant heat, HI, and WBGT) suggest that the environmental conditions may not directly reflect the individual physiological responses to heat exposure. These findings indicate that additional factors, such as individual characteristics, work intensity, hydration status, and acclimatization, are likely to have contributed to variability in CBT response under real-world conditions, as reported in the previous studies [43, 44]. Therefore, integration of real-time monitoring of individual physiological responses to heat into occupational heat-related risk management strategies will help reduce heat-related illness and injury.

Limitations

It should be noted that this pilot study was limited to providing preliminary data on HAV and heat exposure in the specific occupational environment. The application of the study findings to the general work population in the grounds maintenance industry should be considered carefully. First, this study has a small sample size (n = 10) with relatively homogeneous demographic characteristics, (i.e., all males, majority Hispanic, and right-hand dominance). These factors may limit the ability to explain findings from other groups with different demographic characteristics. In addition, the small sample size limited the statistical power of the correlation analyses, and careful interpretation of the results is required. Second, the three-day monitoring period may not be sufficient to capture long-term variability in HAV and heat exposure. In some cases, the error of ahv in r.m.s. was 0 for certain tools among certain participants. These results were simply because those participants did not use the specific tool(s) during our monitoring period. Third, the lifetime HAV exposure estimates were based on self-reported questionnaire data, which may cause recall bias despite the use of correlation factors. Fourth, although the wearable devices have been validated in several studies, thorough investigations under various environmental conditions are still needed. Fifth, the OSHA’s heat trigger levels in WBGT leave room for interpretation. We calculated the NIOSH REL and RAL using the NIOSH equations but there are no clear instructions on how work/rest schedules should be taken into consideration. In addition, the metabolic rate by workload differs across organizations. Lastly, comprehensive research, including large-scale cohorts and diverse occupational settings, is needed to establish more generalizable exposure data for both HAV and heat hazards.

Conclusion

One groundskeeper’s three-day mean A(8) exceeded the ACGIH’s AL. Although the riding mower and backpack blowers generated relatively lower vibration magnitudes than hand-held power tools, their longer operating durations contributed more to the lifetime cumulative HAV exposure dose than the other equipment, except for grass trimmers.

During the work shift, 19.0% of total work hours for eight participants exceeded the upper limit of normal CBT (38.0 °C). HI and WBGT were at or above the OSHA’s proposed high-heat trigger for heavy workload for approximately 66.0 and 65.4%, respectively, of the total monitored work period. No clear association found between estimated CBT and HI and WBGT values suggests CBT or other physiological monitoring in addition to the environmental heat indices may help better characterize the individual-level of heat strain.

Overall, these findings from this pilot study support the use of personal monitoring for HAV and heat exposures. Longitudinal follow-up and multi-site studies on health effects associated with HAV and heat exposure should be followed to further investigate the potential relationship between cumulative HAV and heat exposure and their long-term health effects, in particular vascular health.
