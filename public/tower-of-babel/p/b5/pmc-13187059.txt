The development and validation of Direct and Indirect Prejudice Scales (DIPSs)
Authors: Jiwon Shin, Hwajin Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs international migration continues to reshape societies worldwide, understanding public attitudes toward immigrants has become a critical issue in both academic and policy contexts. This study introduces and validates the Direct and Indirect Prejudice Scales (DIPSs), a psychometric instrument developed to measure prejudice among receiving society members, with a focus on Japan. Using a two-phase approach, we first conducted focus group interviews to identify direct and indirect expressions of prejudice. Insights from this qualitative phase informed the design of a structured questionnaire, which was administered to 770 Japanese nationals residing in the Tokyo metropolitan area. Factor analyses revealed two dimensions of direct prejudice— socio-economic exclusion and public exclusion—and three dimensions of indirect prejudice—defensive ethnocentrism, demand for assimilation, and minimization of differences. Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses confirmed the scale’s reliability and construct validity. While the scales was developed in a Japanese context, the findings reflect broader global dynamics, where prejudice increasingly manifests not only in overt discrimination but also in subtle, normalized behaviors. The DIPSs offers a robust, data-driven tool for assessing prejudice across cultural contexts and contributes to the growing field of intercultural and migration studies by enabling cross-national comparisons and informing inclusive policy design.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40675-4.Keywords: Prejudice, Foreign residents, Japan, Scale developmentSubject terms: Quality of life, Psychology and behaviour

Introduction

Globalization has reinforced prejudice and discrimination against migrants and foreign residents. As immigrant demographics become more diverse, the relationship between immigrants and receiving societies has also become more complex. In examining prejudice toward immigrants, it is essential to consider how specific migrant groups interact with the receiving society, because perceptions and experiences vary with migrants’ attributes and circumstances rather than nationality alone1. Previous research has shown that public attitudes toward immigrants vary across societies and are shaped by multiple contextual and individual factors2. However, in East Asian contexts such as Korea and Japan, policy infrastructures, institutional capacity, and public discourse have not fully adapted to increasing immigrant diversity3. Japan’s demographic landscape is undergoing rapid transformation, with the number of foreigners residing in Japan surging since 2000 and exceeding 3.5 million in 2024.

Accompanying the medium- to long-term increase in the foreign resident population, research on discrimination toward foreigners within Japanese receiving communities has grown in recent years3–6. These studies point to the visible and invisible ways foreigners are discriminated against in Japan, the need to adapt to Japanese culture, and the fact that Japanese society does not recognize foreign discrimination as a major problem.

Addressing these issues requires a comprehensive approach that considers the evolving nature of Japanese society4,7. However, the relevant research in Japan often conceptualizes Japanese and foreign residents as separate groups and proposes solutions for each independently, thereby failing to account for the interdependent relationships that shape everyday social interactions within the same society. This dual-track approach overlooks the complexity of the issue. While it may be useful for understanding each group in depth, it ignores how the attitudes of one group are fundamentally shaped by their interactions with the other.

Current research on foreigners living in Japan has only focused on the education and policies needed to help them integrate into Japanese society. For example, literature has identified and proposed ways to solve problems faced by foreigners, such as international marriages8,9 and employment for former international students10,11. Conversely, research on Japanese citizens themselves has focused on promoting intercultural communication competence among university students through intercultural collaborative learning12, foreign language classes, and addressing those who engage in overt discriminatory behavior, that is, openly discriminatory actions or statements7,13. Discussions on integrated solutions and policies that encompass both foreign residents and Japanese citizens—the parties directly involved in multicultural society—have yet to be sufficiently developed.

Previous research has gaps. The first is the need to delve deeper into the psychological attitudes that underpin intercultural relations, particularly how Japanese people perceive immigrants as part of the receiving society3,5. While current studies have primarily focused on public encounters with foreigners14, the increasing number of foreigners living in Japan will inevitably lead to more frequent interactions in daily life. This highlights the importance of Japanese people understanding foreigners’ daily lives and accepting them as integral members of society. To create a truly diverse society, it is necessary to address Japanese attitudes toward foreigners and their underlying prejudices.

The second issue is that intercultural understanding education for Japanese people has often focused solely on cultural differences, rendering prejudice and discrimination invisible. In Japan, intercultural understanding education or international education often frames “different cultures” as those outside Japan15. This framing can be misinterpreted as simply learning foreign languages or foreign cultures and, in some cases, risks developing into cultural racism—the assumption that Japanese culture is the norm16. Addressing prejudice and discrimination cannot be achieved solely by changing individual attitudes, and such programs risk remaining abstract if they avoid confronting these issues directly.

Against this background, prejudice-related attitudes often remain implicit and under-measured in both research and educational practice. This has resulted in a lack of objective data on how Japanese people view foreigners in specific situations, as well as limited policy and educational guidance for addressing these attitudes. To better understand the real world, it is essential to analyze Japanese prejudice and discrimination against foreigners using a highly valid and reliable scale. Such an analysis could lead to the development of educational content for Japanese citizens, enhancing their understanding of foreigners and making it even more critical to address these attitudes.

Because these scales to measure and evaluate prejudice toward foreigners were originally developed in Western contexts, researchers have found that they do not fully capture the nuances of Japanese intercultural communication styles and competencies17,18.

To this ends, the primary objective of this study was to create a robust scales to measure Japanese prejudice toward foreigners living in Japan. Once developed and validated, these scales can serve as a useful tools for assessing the extent of prejudice in Japan’s multicultural society. This, in turn, could facilitate the formulation of targeted policy proposals aimed at fostering greater intercultural understanding.

While previous research in Japan has examined prejudice toward specific groups, such as modern and old-fashioned prejudice toward Zainichi Koreans—ethnic Koreans who are permanent residents of Japan, many of whom are descendants of those who migrated during the Japanese colonial period (1910–1945) (e.g., Taka & Amemiya3— there remains a lack of conceptual frameworks that distinguish between direct and indirect prejudice in broader, contemporary contexts.

Given the changing landscape of immigration in Japan—where over half of foreign residents now come from East and Southeast Asian countries (e.g., China, Korea, and Vietnam), making visual or linguistic distinctions less apparent—there is a growing need to capture both overt and subtle forms of prejudice. In this study, we define and operationalize direct and indirect prejudice based on insights from focus group interviews (FGIs) and the lived experiences of foreign residents. This approach allows us to better reflect the complex, often implicit prejudice in the Japanese context.

Building on this foundation, the primary goal of the scale developed in this study is to measure the extent to which receiving community members (in this case, Japanese nationals) are aware of their prejudice.

Literature review

Stereotypes, prejudice and discrimination

In social psychology, social bias toward a group and its members is typically categorized into three forms: stereotypes, prejudice, and discrimination19. According to Dovidio et al.20, these can be distinguished as follows: (a) stereotypes are associations and beliefs about the characteristics of particular groups; (b) prejudice refers to individual-level attitudes directed toward groups and their members; and (c) discrimination refers to biased behaviors that create, maintain, or reinforce advantages for some groups over others. In a strict sense, prejudice and discrimination refer to attitudes and actions, respectively; however, under broader conceptual definitions, prejudice may also encompass discriminatory behaviors19. The term is used in this broader sense in the present study, except in cases of direct quotation.

Prejudice can be further classified according to its accessibility to introspection. Explicit prejudice refers to attitudes that are consciously accessible and can be directly reported, whereas implicit prejudice operates outside of conscious awareness and is not readily overt. Both forms can influence behavior, but implicit prejudice may be less recognizable to those who hold it.

Within the prejudice literature, McConahay21 distinguished between old-fashioned prejudice and modern prejudice as attitudinal constructs. Old-fashioned prejudice is characterized by explicit rejection and overt exclusion of minority groups, often linked to visible and direct discriminatory behaviors. With the decline in social acceptance of such overt prejudice, modern prejudice has emerged, reflecting subtle, symbolic, or indirect negative attitudes toward minorities. Modern prejudice may be expressed through microaggressions or resistance to policies aimed at promoting equality. It is often situationally dependent, can be misinterpreted as positive or neutral, and is less likely to be acknowledged by the person expressing it. In psychological scales, modern prejudice tends to show weaker correlations with related explicit attitude scales compared to old-fashioned prejudice, reflecting its more implicit and socially masked nature21.

In this study, we introduce direct and indirect prejudice as scale-oriented operational labels. While these terms partially overlap with legacy concepts (e.g., overt/covert, blatant/subtle), they are not interchangeable. Table 1 summarizes the approximate correspondence between widely used labels and our operationalization. We define direct prejudice as overt, explicitly exclusionary statements or behaviors and prescriptive norms (e.g., open rejection, explicit “should/should not” rules). This concept broadly overlaps with traditional notions of old-fashioned, overt, blatant, and explicit prejudice. In contrast, we use indirect prejudice to denote context-dependent, socially normative, or procedural signals that may be subtle, modern, covert, or implicitly communicated. Although these families of terms are related, they are not identical: the labels direct/indirect are specifically chosen to foreground scale considerations (behavioral anchors, item wording, scoring) and to reflect the Japanese context, where prejudice frequently manifests through socially sanctioned or ambiguous practices.

Contemporary research increasingly recognizes that discrimination is not limited to overt and explicit exclusion, but often occurs in subtle, everyday forms such as microaggressions—chronic, low-intensity insults that may be automatic or unconscious on the part of the perpetrator46. These include microassaults, microinsults, and microinvalidations, and are often difficult for targets to identify or confront due to their ambiguous and socially acceptable nature45,46.

In addition to interpersonal manifestations, discrimination is also understood as structurally embedded, encompassing systemic macroaggressions and forms of structural racism that operate through institutional norms and policies46. These multi-layered forms of bias result in cumulative and chronic harm to marginalized groups45.

As such, there is growing demand for multi-dimensional tools that can differentiate: (1) types of prejudice (e.g., direct/blatant vs. indirect/subtle); (2) levels of operation (interpersonal vs. structural); and (3) patterns of impact, referring to the consequences of prejudice not only for targets but also for those who enact it, ranging from acute high-severity manifestations to persistent low-severity orientations. The DIPSs responds to this need by integrating perspectives from both minority group perceptions and majority group orientations, capturing how prejudice is enacted and experienced in context-specific and culturally nuanced ways.

Japanese context and empirical motivation

While most research on prejudice has focused on issues in the Anglo-American world, some studies have also been conducted in Japan. For example, a steady stream of research has examined prejudice toward people with disabilities18 and Buraku discrimination—historical and ongoing prejudice against descendants of socially segregated communities that were part of Japan’s former caste system22—among other topics. Research on prejudice against foreign migrants in Japan initially focused on Zainichi Koreans6 and later expanded to include other foreign migrant groups3,4,23. These studies have highlighted conflicts arising from cultural differences and emphasized the strong expectation of assimilation within Japanese society.

With the increase in foreign immigrants to Japan since 2010, a growing number of Japanese studies have emphasized the need to reconsider how foreign residents are perceived. Segmented assimilation theory, which explores how socio-environmental factors shape immigrants’ adaptation pathways, highlights the complexity and diversity of immigrant experiences. From this perspective, the persistent tendency in Japanese society to adopt a simplistic view of immigrants is linked to the formation and reinforcement of prejudice. In this study, we adopt a broad operational definition of prejudice to include negative verbal expressions and reported discriminatory behaviors experienced by foreign immigrants in Japan. This approach allows us to capture a wide range of bias-related experiences while acknowledging that, conceptually, verbal expressions are attitudinal, and discriminatory acts are behavioral.

Scale development related to prejudice on intercultural relationships

We conceptualize DIPSs as measuring prejudice as an attitude and distinguish between direct and indirect forms. This section reviews relevant multicultural intergroup attitude scales to situate DIPSs and clarify its distinct contribution.

Many important scales have been developed for research on prejudice in the field of intercultural relations. For example, the Intercultural Development Inventory (IDI) has been developed to measure intercultural sensitivity through a five-factor model that includes denial, defense, minimization, acceptance, and adaptation24. Alternatively, the Intercultural Sensitivity Scale (ISS-15), developed by Wang & Zhou25, was based on IDI.

Research has also been conducted on the development of multicultural awareness and skills in specific settings. Examples include the Multicultural Ideology Scale by Stogianni et al.26, and the more detailed Multicultural Experience Assessment Scale (MExA), which distinguishes between multi-cultural exposure and multicultural interactions measured based on frequency, duration, and breadth. Rodil et al27. developed the United States Identity Scale (USIS) to measure the scale of identity in the multi-ethnic environment of the US. Although identity is conceptually distinct from prejudice, identity-related orientations can shape intergroup attitudes and thus provide contextual background for understanding prejudice formation. USIS is introduced here to delineate adjacent constructs in multicultural settings; however, the present study focuses specifically on prejudice as an attitude (direct and indirect), rather than identity. Attempts to develop intercultural communication scales tailored to the Japanese context began in the 2000s. While the development of Japanese-language scales began in the 1990 s, these scales were largely adaptations of Western psychological theories or direct translations of Western scales into Japanese328. Since the 2000 s, researchers have been developing intercultural communication scales specifically designed for the Japanese context to address these issues. These scales aim to better reflect Japanese social norms, communication patterns, and cultural values. In addition, recent studies have examined how direct and indirect contact with different cultures influences changes in attitudes towards foreigners, further informing the development of culturally relevant intercultural communication scales29–32. These diverse scales contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of intercultural adaptation by highlighting the importance of psychological characteristics, multicultural exposure, and social contact in shaping intercultural competence. In addition, they aim to reduce prejudice and discrimination in society by promoting a deeper awareness of intercultural dynamics and fostering more inclusive attitudes.

Komisarof and Leong33 underscore the critical role of immigrants’ sense of belonging within receiving societies and note that discrepancies between receiving society and immigrant expectations can generate intergroup tensions, particularly in relatively homogeneous contexts such as that of Japan, where national identity definitions strongly influence acceptance. The related construct of Social Markers of Acceptance (SMA) refers to socially constructed criteria—such as language proficiency and adherence to societal norms—that determine whether immigrants are perceived as members of the in-group34,35. Although SMA consistently exhibits ethnic and civic dimensions, the specific markers are culturally contingent36. In the Japanese context, Komisarof, Leong, and Lim37 demonstrated that heightened perceptions of threat increased the salience of both ethnic and civic markers across immigrant groups, revealing nuanced patterns in perceived status and contribution. Complementary evidence comes from Jassi and Safdar38, who applied the SMA framework to the Canadian context. Additionally, in Russia, Grigoryev and colleagues39,40 identified discrepancies between preferred and perceived immigrant adaptation strategies and demonstrated that multiculturalism ideology was a stronger predictor of acceptance of immigrant discrimination than other socio-political orientations, such as right-wing authoritarianism or social dominance orientation. Building on these findings, this study introduces the DIPSs, developed to capture enacted prejudice from the perspective of the receiving society, based on narratives shared by foreign residents in focus group interviews.

Methods

Research overview

Figure 1 summarizes the research procedure used in this study, based on Creswell41. The squares represent data collection and analysis. The ovals represent synthesis and interpretation. The arrows indicate the order in which the research was conducted.

Research flow.

The development of DIPSs proceeded in two stages (Fig. 1). In the first stage, conducted in Shin, Lim, and Shin6, we reviewed previous research on prejudice scales (April–September 2021), carried out focus group interviews (FGI) with mid- to long-term foreign residents in Japan (November 2021), and used the FGI results to develop candidate items (April 2022). For an overview of the FGI, as well as detailed findings and analyses, please refer to Shin, Lim, and Shin6. For transparency, the present manuscript also summarizes the interview sample, procedure, and analytic approach in the section “Questionnaires”.

Building on these findings, this study focuses on the second stage, in which we examined the content validity of the items with expert reviewers (May 2022) and then administered a questionnaire survey to a general sample of Japanese participants (June 2022).

Regarding the content validity of the items with expert reviewers, the proposed psychological items were examined by three experts in related fields: intercultural communication and geography. Then, a questionnaire survey was conducted among Japanese people in their 20 s to 40 s living in the Tokyo metropolitan area. The questionnaire’s validity and reliability were statistically analyzed and assessed. Psychological scales related to multicultural competence in Japanese participants were included in the questionnaire. The criterion-related validity of the scale developed in this study was tested using correlation analysis with existing scales.

Questionnaires

The questionnaire was developed based on a focus group interview (FGI). The FGI was conducted in November 2021 with 12 foreign migrants who had lived in Japan for more than 3 years. Interviews were conducted in-person in Japanese, covered direct and indirect prejudice in everyday contexts, and lasted 90 min. With consent, interviews were recorded, transcribed, anonymized, and thematically coded by two researchers. Themes were converted into an item pool and refined before pilot/validation. Full protocol details are in Shin, Lim & Shin5.

Despite being fluent in Japanese and familiar with Japanese culture, the FGI participants openly discussed experiences of prejudice as well as other uncomfortable situations they had encountered. Shin, Lim, and Shin6 found that prejudice toward foreign residents in Japan includes both direct and indirect forms. Direct prejudice refers to explicit and conscious exclusions, whereas indirect prejudice refers to implicit and unconscious exclusions. The distinction between direct and indirect prejudice was based on their relative visibility and the extent to which contextual factors shaped how prejudice was expressed by those holding such views.

According to Shin, Lim, and Shin6, immigrants in Japan experience both direct and indirect prejudice. Direct prejudice refers to explicit and conscious forms of exclusion. The codes identified in their study included institutional exclusion, social exclusion, and psychological exclusion. Indirect prejudice, on the other hand, refers to subtle forms of prejudice that may not be consciously recognized by those expressing them but nonetheless evoke unpleasant feelings in foreign residents during interpersonal communication.The codes associated with indirect prejudice involved feeling differentiated from Japanese people, being pressured to conform to Japanese-style communication, and the perceived need to understand other cultures. The direct and indirect prejudices of the FGI were very similar to the modern prejudices examined in previous studies. Participants interpreted their experiences as being caused by some Japanese people, or they interpreted them as behavior based on misunderstanding or without malicious intent. Therefore, even foreign residents who were directly affected felt uncomfortable in certain situations but accepted them as personal incidents. This means that prejudice against foreigners in Japan is subtle.

Based on the FGI results, a specification for scale development was prepared by defining two factors and their respective subcategories. After multiple discussions of the appropriateness of each item for each factor, 30 direct and 24 indirect prejudice items were developed. Prejudice can be divided into explicit prejudice, which corresponds to expressed behavior and emotions, and implicit prejudice, which corresponds to unconscious thoughts16. In this study, we sought to develop a psychological scale corresponding to explicit prejudice in order to measure how people think and behave in Japanese communication situations. (Table 2).

Participant recruitment

We surveyed 770 Japanese people in their 20 s and 40 s living in the Tokyo metropolitan area (Tokyo, Kanagawa, Chiba, and Saitama) in April and May 2022. The Tokyo Metropolitan Area was chosen because of its diverse population and high concentration of cross-cultural interactions. The survey took approximately 20 min to complete.

Participants were recruited through an online survey company in Japan, using a stratified sampling method based on age (20–40 years), gender, and region. Previous studies have shown that both the frequency of contact with foreign residents and xenophobic attitudes vary by age48. Japan’s foreign resident population has increased steadily since the 1990 s, with particularly rapid expansion after 200049. Given these demographic shifts, the present study targets individuals who entered the workforce during or after the 1990s—a generation that has spent most of its working life in an increasingly globalized social context. This age cohort was selected as the primary target population because it constitutes the core of Japan’s working-age demographic and is therefore more likely to engage in routine, everyday contact with foreign residents in workplace and community settings. Moreover, this aligns with the age range (20s to 40 s) of foreign nationals who participated in our preliminary FGIs, ensuring consistency between the perspectives of those experiencing prejudice and those potentially exhibiting it. Due to the survey’s system—which continues sampling until the target number of valid responses is achieved—traditional response rates could not be calculated. To ensure a balanced representation, we surveyed 387 men (108 in their 20 s, 123 in their 30 s, and 156 in their 40 s) and 383 women (104 in their 20 s, 121 in their 30 s, and 158 in their 40 s) according to their age and demographic composition. This study was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Kanda University of International Studies (approval number: #2022-4). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The purpose and significance of the research were explained at the beginning of the survey. Data confidentiality and anonymity were guaranteed, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without consequence.

Scale development and validation

Content validation

An expert conducted content validation of the questions to determine whether the items, created based on the FGI, measured direct and indirect prejudice in Japan. In April 2022, two experts in intercultural communication and one in geography were asked to verify the content validity of the items. We provided them with the definitions and subcategories described in the instructions and asked them to classify the factors and categories to which the 54 items belonged.

Thirty of the 54 items were found to be content-valid based on the researcher’s intended factors and the same categorization by the three experts. Two experts categorized the 15 items using the same factor as the researcher’s intended one. Of these, 11 questions were classified differently only by the Japanese experts. For example, the Japanese experts classified “Saying ‘you are like a Japanese’ to a foreigner is a compliment” and “Foreigners should communicate in Japanese while in Japan” as direct prejudice, while the researchers and foreign experts classified them as indirect prejudice. However, since these are invisible attitudes, it was judged that differences in understanding of foreigners living in Japan were reflected depending on nationality and experience. As a result, the factors were classified as those where the opinions of researchers and two experts agreed. Based on comments regarding ambiguous Japanese expressions and question clarity, the questions were revised and rewritten to improve content validity.

Eight questions for which the classifications of researchers and experts did not match were classified as indirect prejudice because they were behaviors that were difficult to visualize. For example, “Foreigners must produce better results than the Japanese to be promoted” was a question intended to identify direct prejudice, but it was classified as indirect prejudice. Conversely, “Foreigners with residence status are members of Japanese society” was classified as indirect prejudice, but experts classified it as direct prejudice. This seems to be related to the official aspect of residence status; however, researchers determined that this was more difficult to visualize than direct prejudice because it was an attitudinal aspect.

One question on the prioritization of Japanese citizens for COVID-19 vaccination (i.e., “Vaccinations [such as those for the new coronavirus] should be prioritized for Japanese people over foreigners”) was deleted after the experts pointed out that it was highly related to social situations and individual beliefs.

The finalized questionnaire included 19 items related to direct prejudice and 35 items related to indirect prejudice.

Factor analysis

Exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were conducted in this study. The survey data were randomly divided into two groups (Random A and Random B), and an exploratory factor analysis was conducted on the data from the Random A group to delete items and confirm the factor structure. An exploratory factor analysis with maximum likelihood and promax rotation was conducted using Random A (385 respondents).A confirmatory factor analysis was performed on the Random B data, and the final design was reconfirmed. Confirmatory factor analysis was performed using data from Random B (385 respondents). We used factor analysis to test the construct validity of our FGI-based measure of prejudice against foreigners.

In the initial EFA, 54 items assessing both direct and indirect prejudice were analyzed. The analysis revealed a three-factor structure, consistent with the classification derived from content validity. The first factor comprised 25 items reflecting indirect prejudice, the second factor included 17 items representing direct prejudice, and the third factor primarily consisted of 12 items related to indirect prejudice but showing relatively low factor loadings. Based on these findings, the study proceeded to perform separate factor analyses for direct and indirect prejudice, in accordance with the content validity framework.

Direct prejudice

First, using maximum likelihood and promax rotation, EFA (Exploratory Factor Analysis) was conducted on Random A (385 respondents). By checking the grouping of items and the scree plot and parallel analysis, we found that the 19 items of direct prejudice had a two-factor structure. Next, we analyzed the number of extracted factors, fixed at two. We then confirmed the factor structure by sequentially removing items with primary factor loadings of ≤ 0.40, as well as items exhibiting substantial cross-loadings—defined as loadings of ≥ 0.35 on non-primary factors. This resulted in five items for the first factor and nine items for the second. The correlation matrix between the factors was 0.610, confirming that the two factors had a strong positive correlation. Factor names were determined based on the item content. The first factor was named “socio-economic exclusion” because it showed social and psychological exclusion that favored the Japanese, including attitudes such as “foreigners should be paid less than the Japanese” and “it is desirable to give priority to serving Japanese people over foreigners.” The second factor was named “public exclusion” because it shows systematic exclusion in public situations, which included “foreigners should always carry their identification cards” and “foreigners’ property, including property in their home country, should be checked more strictly than that of Japanese people.”

Based on the results of the exploratory factor analysis, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis using Random B (385 respondents). We set the variance of each factor to 1 and estimated the model. In the confirmatory factor analysis with random B, 14 items showed adequate model fit (χ2 = 322.081, df = 76, p <0.001, CFI = 0.913, RMSEA = 0.092, SRMR = 0.075). Although the fit indices in this study were not high, we judged the model acceptable based on commonly cited guidelines (e.g., CFI above 0.90 and RMSEA and SRMR below 0.10; Kline, 201044). In addition, we did not place strong interpretive weight on the chi-square statistic because it is easily influenced by large sample sizes.

An examination of the mean and standard deviation for each item indicated that neither a ceiling effect nor a floor effect was present. These results are summarized in Table 3.

Indirect prejudice

This analysis of indirect prejudice is based on a study involving FGI and expert judgment, which examined 35 items: 27 content-validated items and eight categorized between direct and indirect prejudice. The same rigorous procedures used for assessing direct prejudice were applied to ensure a comprehensive understanding of the topic.

First, EFA was conducted with Random A (385 respondents) using maximum likelihood and Promax rotation. After reviewing the item groupings and scree plots and parallel analysis, a three-factor structure for indirect prejudice was identified. We then deleted items with primary factor loadings of ≤ 0.40, as well as items with cross-loadings of ≥ 0.35 on non-primary factors, to ensure that the final factors were the most significant and representative. We named the factors according to their content in order to provide a clear and informative framework for understanding this study. The first factor, “defensive ethnocentrism,” consists of 12 items that distinctly portray a sense of Japanese superiority and a clear boundary between Japanese and foreigners.

The second factor, “demand for assimilation to Japan,” comprises four items, including “Foreigners living in Japan should learn Japanese history,” and “In order to live in Japan, foreigners should have the same level of Japanese language proficiency as Japanese people.” The second factor was based on the premise that foreigners should behave like Japanese people and follow Japanese ways.

The third factor, “minimization of differences,” consists of seven items, including “Saying ‘your Japanese is good’ to a foreigner is a compliment,” and “You should always explain things to foreigners in simple Japanese.” The “minimization of differences” factor draws on Bennett’s42,43 Minimization of cultural difference, which explains how emphasizing similarities can lead people to see their own values as universal. While often intended as goodwill, it can reflect benevolent stereotyping—portraying out-groups positively while reinforcing their outsider status. Our FGI results showed that Japanese respondents viewed such remarks as considerate, whereas foreign participants perceived them as subtle exclusion. Its positive correlation with intercultural positivity measures highlights this ambivalence, linking the factor to both benevolent prejudice and, in some cases, aversive bias. In addition, the positive correlation coefficients of.334 for “defensive ethnocentrism” and.391 for “demand for assimilation to Japan” indicate that this is a pattern of behavior that expresses prejudice that is difficult to visualize.

To verify the validity of the results of the exploratory factor analysis, we conducted a confirmatory factor analysis using Random B (385 respondents). We fixed the variance of each factor at 1, as with direct prejudice, and estimated the model. The goodness of fit was χ2 = 669.896, df = 227, p <0.001, CFI = 0.887, RMSEA = 0.071, SRMR = 0.081. The CFI, which was used to evaluate the goodness of fit of a model, was below 0.90, indicating that the model required improvement. After reviewing the modified indices and standard coefficients, we deleted the items “The crimes of foreigners appearing in the media are only a small part of the whole” and “Foreigners who are proficient in Japanese should also be able to speak other languages (e.g., English, etc.).” As a result, the goodness of fit of the three-factor structure of the 21 items was improved (χ2 = 531.532, df = 186, p <0.001, CFI = 0.905, RMSEA = 0.070, SRMR = 0.071). We refrained from introducing error correlations or applying modification indices to improve model fit, in line with Kline’s (2016) view that hypotheses are the cornerstone of structural equation modeling. This approach upholds the principles of confirmatory factor analysis, maintaining minimal model adjustments and preserving item independence to ensure theoretical and analytical rigor.

All three factors showed significant positive correlations in the confirmatory factor analysis, with “defensive ethnocentrism” and “demand for assimilation to Japan” at 0.776, “defensive ethnocentrism” and “minimization of differences” at 0.452, and “demand for assimilation to Japan” and “minimization of differences” at 0.623. These correlations underscore the interconnectedness of the factors and the complexity of indirect prejudice.

Reliability and validity

Reliability and robustness

Cronbach’s reliability coefficients of direct prejudice were 0.898 for “socio-economic exclusion” and 0.869 for “public exclusion.” Both were above 0.80, confirming adequate reliability coefficients. Cronbach’s reliability coefficients of indirect prejudice were 0.922 for “defensive ethnocentrism,” 0.784 for “demand for assimilation,” and 0.691 for “minimization of differences.” All coefficients were above 0.60, confirming the reliability.

The final version of the DIPSs includes only positively worded items. Although negatively worded items were initially included, they were removed during factor analysis for several reasons. First, such items may introduce acquiescence-related artifacts and tend to capture response bias in addition to the intended construct. Second, Taka and Amemiya3 reported high correlations among negatively worded items in prejudice measures, indicating potential method bias. Based on these considerations, only positively worded items were retained in the final version of the DIPSs.

The results indicated a strong positive correlation between the subfactors of direct and indirect prejudice, suggesting considerable conceptual overlap. Furthermore, all direct and indirect prejudice subscales were positively correlated with old-fashioned racism, modern racism, and intercultural negativity. In contrast, direct and indirect prejudice were negatively correlated with attitudes toward foreigners and intercultural ambivalence, with all coefficients significant at the 5% level. Intercultural positivity showed a distinct pattern. Specifically, socio-economic exclusion, public exclusion, defensive ethnocentrism, and demand for assimilation into Japan exhibited significant negative correlations with intercultural positivity at the 5% level, and similar tendencies were observed across related scales. In contrast, minimization of differences demonstrated a positive correlation with intercultural positivity. This divergent pattern suggests that certain forms of prejudice may coexist with seemingly positive attitudes toward other cultures, highlighting the complex and sometimes ambivalent nature of intercultural perceptions.

Finally, we found that old-fashioned racism showed a stronger correlation with indirect prejudice than with direct prejudice. This contradicts our initial hypothesis. One possible interpretation is that, in Japan, strong ethnocentric norms and assimilationist expectations remain influential. For example, praise directed at foreigners for behaving “like Japanese” may be perceived positively by the majority, but as a subtle form of pressure by minorities (see Shin, Lim, & Shin, 20215).

Verification of validity using differential item functioning analysis

In this study, we conducted a differential item functioning (DIF) analysis to scrutinize the scales for measuring prejudice, ensuring that it functioned uniformly across genders and that each item measured the same content consistently. DIF analysis—a rigorous method to verify bias—was employed to examine potential discrepancies in item functionality between groups. Utilizing a multi-group confirmatory factor analysis, our DIF analysis meticulously examined the differences between groups based on the relationship between the latent and observed variables of the measurement tool, thereby validating the internal criteria.

For DIF analysis, CFA was conducted using all data (Random A and Random B) in a multi-group analysis. The extent to which the model with equal constraints on all path coefficients from the latent variables to the observed variables differed from the baseline model was used as the criterion for judgment.

Our analysis revealed no DIF items between male and female for the items measuring direct and indirect prejudice. The difference between measurement invariance (χ2 = 624.682, df = 152, p <0.001, CFI = 0.914, RMSEA = 0.064, SRMR = 0.075) and configural invariance (χ2 = 633.744, df = 167, p <0.001, CFI = 0.915, RMSEA = 0.060, SRMR = 0.076) for direct prejudice was 9.092, which was not significant at the 5% level. Furthermore, the individual fit of male and female was nearly identical, confirming the robustness and fairness of the scale developed in this study.

Although the difference between measurement invariance (χ2 = 1052.920, df = 372, p <0.001, CFI = 0.902, RMSEA = 0.049, SRMR = 0.072) and configural invariance (χ2 = 1087.335, df = 396, p <0.001, CFI = 0.900, RMSEA = 0.048, SRMR = 0.076) for indirect prejudice was larger (Δχ² = 34.415) than that observed for direct prejudice, this difference was not statistically significant between males and females at the 5% level. The results by gender confirmed that the model fit was lower for men. Discussion

Motivation and key conceptual findings from the DIPSs development

The motivation for developing the scale in this study is as follows. In Japan, where the number of foreign residents has been rapidly increasing in recent years, relatively few cross-sectional and comprehensive scales addressing prejudice exist. Consequently, discussions often focus on direct forms of prejudice, leaving indirect and subtler forms underexplored. However, in daily life, it is becoming increasingly difficult for both victims and perpetrators to identify prejudice, mainly when they are not overt. Therefore, there is a growing need for a scale that comprehensively captures both direct and indirect forms of prejudice.

The findings of this study can be contextualized through the results of FGIs conducted with long-term foreign residents in Japan5. A discrepancy was observed between the factor structure derived from the FGIs and that identified in the questionnaire survey. Notably, psychological exclusion, classified as direct prejudice in the FGIs, was not included in the direct prejudice factor in the survey results. This was because psychological exclusion was perceived to be more challenging to quantify than public exclusion and social exclusion. Meanwhile, the indirect prejudice items showed partial consistency with the FGI findings, leading to the assignment of new factor names. These results suggest that certain forms of psychological and social exclusion, initially categorized as direct prejudice in the FGIs, are challenging to conceptualize within a structured framework. This raises the possibility that distinguishing between direct and indirect prejudice may be difficult. Furthermore, as demonstrated by Shin, Lim, and Shin6, the study supports the idea that direct and indirect prejudice exist along a continuum rather than as separate constructs.

Validation and contextual insights from the DIPSs

This study verified the reliability and validity of the scale measuring prejudice against foreigners from multiple perspectives. The scale demonstrated high reliability coefficients, and its validity was assessed through several methods: content validity by experts, construct validity using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis, and criterion-related validity through correlation analysis with existing scales. Correlation analysis with existing scales confirmed that the scale measured content generally related to prejudice. However, results from the cross-cultural sensitivity scale varied. Notably,the positive correlation with the minimization of differences suggest that this scale may capture subtle and less visible forms of prejudice in Japanese society. Additionally, differential item functioning (DIF) analysis was conducted to ensure internal criterion-related validity, enhancing the scale’s accuracy in measuring both direct and indirect prejudice against foreigners in Japan.

Also, this study considers specific situations in which prejudice occur in Japan. While previous research has measured perceptions of foreigners38 focused on particular ethnic groups, such as Korean residents in Japan6, or examined the frequency of contact with foreigners39, these studies primarily explored relationships between prejudice and other factors. In contrast, this study identified concrete scenarios in which foreigners experience prejudice.

On the other hand, this study incorporates the perspectives of individuals living in Japan who are actively engaged in its multicultural society, ensuring that a wide range of voices are represented. The psychological scale was developed based on findings from focus group interviews (FGIs) with long-term foreign residents and later tested through a questionnaire survey of the Japanese population. This approach allowed for identifying behaviors perceived as prejudiced or discriminatory by foreign residents and Japanese residents. For example, a positive correlation was found between the minimization of differences and intercultural positivity. The latter refers to a tendency among Japanese people to differentiate foreigners from themselves, often in seemingly positive ways, such as saying, “Your Japanese is good” or “You sound Japanese.” While intended as compliments, foreign residents may perceive these remarks as microaggressions, reinforcing social boundaries. These findings highlight the potential for future development of intercultural education programs that foster greater mutual understanding. According to Brown19, new prejudices, including modern racism, have two sides. In other words, they are not simply negative attitudes, but rather a mixture of negative and positive elements. In some cases, the positive elements may be more prominent, which is a characteristic of new prejudices such as modern racism. In this context, we interpret that the aspect of minimizing differences by saying that one is good at the Japanese language showed a positive correlation with the perception that one actively accepts other cultures. Our research showed that Japanese people interpreted this indicator positively. However, the FGI revealed that foreigners perceived it as ambiguous exclusion and discrimination. This suggests that foreigners and Japanese people evaluate the same situation differently. Therefore, the DIPSs we propose is an indicator that can measure how the ambivalent characteristics of new prejudices, including modern racism as described by Brown, manifest themselves in practical situations.

Redefining prejudice with DIPSs

A long-standing debate in prejudice research concerns who is entitled to define what counts as prejudice—the targeted groups themselves, members of the potentially prejudiced majority, or third-party observers such as social scientists. Conventional scale development has typically proceeded from the majority side, by adapting existing items and adding new ones that reflect how prejudice is understood within the dominant group. In contrast, the present study deliberately began with focus group interviews with first-generation residents of immigrant background who had lived in Japan for at least three years, and used their accounts of everyday interactions as the primary basis for item generation. We adopted this target-centered starting point because it foregrounds the subtle, routine harms—ambiguous slights, exclusions, and invalidations—that foreigners actually experience in situation, and reduces the risk that a purely majority-defined framework will normalize structurally embedded harms or overlook socially masked forms of bias.

At the same time, we do not treat targets’ perceptions as infallible or exhaustive. The content emerging from focus groups was systematically filtered through theory and subjected to validation, and the DIPSs structure is explicitly designed to be extendable to majority-side applications in future work. In this sense, our approach positions targets’ perceptions as the starting point for defining the content domain of prejudice in the Japanese context, while recognizing the complementary value of majority perspectives and third-party scientific adjudication in refining constructs and evaluating their validity.

This study differs from conventional prejudice research in that it begins from the perspective of perceived prejudice (via FGIs with foreign residents) and develops indicators assessed by members of the receiving society. As a result, the DIPSs may capture both alignment and divergence between the experiences of minorities and the attitudes of majorities. The correlation results with existing scales, such as the stronger link between old-fashioned racism and indirect prejudice, suggest that our measure reflects culturally specific dynamics not fully accounted for in Western-based frameworks.

Although this study was conducted in the Japanese context, its three-factor model for indirect prejudice may have broader relevance and connects to the wider literature on modern prejudice. As Brown19 notes, new prejudices combine positive and negative elements, and our findings illustrate this through the differing interpretations of remarks such as “you are good at Japanese,” viewed positively by many Japanese respondents but as subtle exclusion by foreign participants. These results highlight the potential for cultural variation in interpreting indirect prejudice indicators. Such interpretive asymmetries likely occur in other societies, suggesting potential applicability beyond Japan, though cross-cultural validation is needed to confirm the model’s generalizability.

This study highlights a potential gap between how prejudice is experienced by minorities and how it is expressed by the majority population in multicultural contexts like Japan. Identifying this perceptual mismatch is a key contribution of the DIPSs. Further research is needed to investigate the factors underlying such divergence in intergroup perceptions.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the sample used for the quantitative survey was not nationally representative, as participants were recruited via an online panel with quota sampling. Second, the study focused on individuals aged 20–40, which limits the generalizability of the findings to older or younger populations whose formative experiences with diversity may differ. Third, while the DIPSs items were based on foreign residents’ narratives obtained through focus group interviews, the final scale measures enacted prejudice from the perspective of the receiving society. This perceptual-to-enacted transition, while analytically grounded, may not capture all nuances of foreign residents’ lived experiences. Fourth, because the final version of the DIPSs consists exclusively of positively worded items, scores may be influenced by acquiescence response tendencies, potentially inflating correlations with other self-report measures. Accordingly, the results of correlational validity analyses should be interpreted with caution. Future research should examine versions of the scale that include a balanced mix of positively and negatively worded items to further assess and address potential response bias. Lastly, as the DIPSs was developed specifically for the Japanese context, future studies are needed to assess its cross-cultural applicability and generalizability.

Conclusion

This study developed and evaluated DIPSs for Japanese, offering a comprehensive scale for measuring both direct and indirect forms of prejudice. Using standard psychometric validation procedures, we established the scale’s reliability and validity and developed an instrument suited to capturing both direct and indirect prejudice in contemporary Japanese society.

This study contributes to the development of the scale by distinguishing between direct and indirect forms of prejudice against foreigners in Japan. We confirmed that Japanese individuals exhibit a two-factor structure of direct prejudice and a three-factor structure of indirect prejudice against foreigners. The scales addresses an important gap in the existing research by incorporating both direct prejudices, such as public and socio-economic exclusion, and indirect prejudice, including ethnocentrism and the minimization of cultural differences. This dual focus enables a deeper understanding of Japanese individuals’ psychological processes toward foreigners, which often involve both nuanced and indirect forms of exclusion. A key finding of this study was that Japanese people may not recognize the actions or attitudes that foreign residents perceive as prejudice.

Additionally, by integrating insights from FGIs with quantitative analyses, this study provides a structured framework for understanding and addressing prejudice. It is crucial to continuously refine and adapt the scale to reflect societal changes and evolving dynamics of multicultural interactions. Future research should incorporate longitudinal studies to monitor the effectiveness of interventions based on this scale and explore how shifts in policy and education influence perceptions of prejudice over time. Integrating the perspectives of diverse foreign communities in Japan will further enhance the scale’s inclusivity and relevance.
