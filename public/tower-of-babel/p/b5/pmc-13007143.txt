Anifrolumab in systemic lupus erythematosus: real-world evidence from a Spanish multicentre cohort of 206 patients and literature review

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnifrolumab is approved for adults with moderate-to-severe active systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) based on Randomised clinical trials (RCTs). While randomised pivotal RCTs have demonstrated their efficacy and safety, real-world evidence (RWE) remains limited.ObjectivesTo describe the clinical characteristics, effectiveness and safety of anifrolumab in clinical practice and to assess findings from published observational studies.MethodsMulticentre study of patients with SLE classified according to EULAR/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 2019. Data were collected from medical records up to 30 April 2025. Variables included demographic characteristics, clinical and serological features, prior and concomitant therapies, disease activity indices including the SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K), SLE Disease Activity Score (SLE-DAS) and Physician Global Assessment (PGA), damage and disease control measures including the Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/ACR Damage Index, Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS) and Definitions of Remission in SLE (DORIS) remission and the association with adverse events (AEs). A review of published observational studies was also conducted.ResultsWe included 206 patients (183 women; mean age 44.6±12.6 years) from 54 Spanish centres. The most common indications for anifrolumab were cutaneous (61.7%), musculoskeletal (48.5%) and haematological (27.2%).A rapid and sustained improvement was observed in disease activity (SLEDAI-2K, SLE-DAS, PGA), LLDAS, DORIS remission, serological markers (anti-double-stranded DNA, C3/C4) and corticosteroid tapering. Organ damage remained stable. After a mean follow-up of 7.5±5.3 months, the most frequent AEs were herpes zoster (n= 5), respiratory infections (n= 6) and headache (n= 3). Twenty patients discontinued treatment. These results were consistent with published observational studies.ConclusionIn this large RWE cohort, anifrolumab demonstrated early and sustained clinical benefit, a favourable safety profile and a significant corticosteroid-sparing effect. These findings reinforce the evidence from CT and support the incorporation of anifrolumab into routine care for patients with SLE.

Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, heterogeneous autoimmune disease characterised by the production of autoantibodies, immune complex deposition and multisystem inflammation. It predominantly affects women of childbearing age and presents with a wide range of clinical manifestations, including cutaneous, musculoskeletal, haematological, renal and neuropsychiatric involvement. Despite advances in treatment, many patients continue to experience persistent disease activity, flares and irreversible organ damage, often requiring long-term immunosuppression and corticosteroids.13

A key pathogenic feature of SLE is the sustained activation of the type I interferon (IFN-I) pathway, which promotes dendritic cell maturation, B-cell survival and autoantibody production. Elevated expression of IFN-stimulated genes correlates with disease activity and severity, making this pathway a central therapeutic target.46

Anifrolumab is a fully human IgG1κ monoclonal antibody that binds to the type I interferon receptor subunit 1, thereby blocking the activity of all IFN-I (α, β and ω). It was approved as an add-on therapy for adults with moderate-to-severe, active autoantibody-positive SLE who have an inadequate response to standard treatment.7 8 Its efficacy and safety were demonstrated in the phase III Treatment of Uncontrolled Lupus via the Interferon Pathway (TULIP)-1 and TULIP-2 trials,9 10 which showed improvements in global disease activity, skin and joint manifestations, corticosteroid tapering and flare reduction.911

Randomised controlled trials (RCTs) are conducted under highly controlled conditions, with strict inclusion and exclusion criteria that often exclude patients with severe organ involvement, comorbidities or prior biologic exposure. As a result, a substantial proportion of patients seen in clinical practice may not meet eligibility criteria for these trials. This limits the validity of RCT findings and underscores the need for real-world evidence (RWE) to assess the effectiveness and safety of new therapies in more diverse patient populations.12

To date, five published studies1317 have reported clinical outcomes of anifrolumab in real-world settings. These studies have shown promising clinical benefits, including reduced disease activity and steroid use. However, all four reports are limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up durations and lack of control groups. Methodological heterogeneity and observational designs weaken causal inference. In addition to these early real-world studies, several observational cohorts have published their study protocols but have not reported clinical outcomes yet.18 19

This study aims to describe the clinical profile, effectiveness and safety of anifrolumab in a large cohort of patients with SLE treated in routine clinical practice across Spain. In addition, we provide a narrative review of the current literature on real-world outcomes associated with anifrolumab to contextualise our findings.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

The study was conducted across 54 tertiary care hospitals in Spain. The study includes adult patients (aged ≥18 years). All patients fulfilled the EULAR/American College of Rheumatology (ACR) 2019 classification criteria for SLE.20 The study period extended from June 2021 to 31 April 2025 coinciding with the early postapproval phase of anifrolumab in Spain.

Eligible patients had moderately to severely active, autoantibody-positive SLE and had initiated treatment with anifrolumab as part of routine clinical care. No exclusion criteria were applied beyond the absence of sufficient clinical data.

Study objectives

The main objective of this study was to describe changes in global disease activity and glucocorticoid tapering after anifrolumab initiation in routine clinical practice and to characterise the safety profile. Secondary objectives were to describe baseline patient characteristics and anifrolumab discontinuation.

Data collection

Data were retrospectively extracted from electronic medical records using a standardised case report form designed by the study steering committee. Treat-to-target outcomes were assessed according to established definitions of Lupus Low Disease Activity State (LLDAS)21 and DORIS remission.22

All data were reviewed and validated according to a predefined, consensus-based protocol. To ensure data accuracy, a double-entry verification process was performed independently by two investigators at each participating centre.

AEs and infections were identified from clinical charts and electronic medical records. For the purposes of this study, serious infections were defined as infections requiring hospitalisation or intravenous antimicrobial therapy, or leading to death or to permanent discontinuation of anifrolumab, in line with definitions commonly used in observational rheumatology studies.

Treatment exposure

All patients received anifrolumab at the approved dose of 300 mg administered intravenously every 4 weeks, in accordance with the European Medicines Agency label.7 Two patients with active lupus nephritis received an initial loading regimen of 900 mg every 4 weeks for 3 months, followed by the standard 300 mg dose. Concomitant treatment with glucocorticoids and/or immunosuppressants was permitted and recorded. No modifications to standard clinical practice were introduced by the study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise demographic, clinical and laboratory variables. Continuous variables were expressed as mean±SD for normally distributed data, or as median and IQR for non-normally distributed data. Qualitative variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages (%). For longitudinal outcomes, analyses were performed using a complete case approach (available cases at each visit), without data imputation. The number of patients contributing data at each time point was recorded and missingness of some data mainly reflected variability in routine clinical follow-up across centres. Paired-sample t-test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare continuous variables over different time points. Statistical significance was considered as a p value <0.05 in all the calculations.

Treatment continuation and discontinuation were summarised descriptively as proportions over follow-up, together with reasons for discontinuation and drug survival over time was additionally evaluated using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V.20.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA), and Stata V.16/SE (Stata, College Station, Texas, USA).

Literature review strategy

In parallel with the clinical data analysis, a narrative literature review was conducted to contextualise our findings within the current body of RWE on anifrolumab in SLE.

A structured search was performed in the following databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase and Scopus, covering the period from January 2021 (coinciding with the first regulatory approvals of anifrolumab) to April 2025. The search strategy included combinations of the following terms: “anifrolumab”, “systemic lupus erythematosus” OR “SLE”, “real-world” OR “real life” OR “observational” OR “registry”, “effectiveness” OR “safety” OR “outcomes”.

Only peer-reviewed articles published in English were considered. Protocols without results and preclinical studies were excluded from the synthesis. The final selection included studies reporting real-world clinical outcomes of anifrolumab in SLE, which were then summarised and compared with the present cohort.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 206 patients with SLE from 54 tertiary hospitals across Spain were included. The mean age at baseline was 44.6 ±12.6 years with a predominance of women (n =183; 88.8%). The mean disease duration prior to the initiation of anifrolumab was 10.4±9.35 years (range: 0–44 years).

At the time of anifrolumab initiation, the most frequent clinical manifestations were articular involvement (87.8%), cutaneous lesions (79.1%), haematological abnormalities (65.0%), oral ulcers (47.8%) and alopecia (43.7%). Less common but clinically relevant features included renal involvement (27.8%), serositis (21.8%), neuropsychiatric manifestations (13.6%) and gastrointestinal symptoms (8.3%) (table 1).

Previous and concomitant therapies

Before starting anifrolumab, patients had received a mean of 2.5±1.6 immunosuppressive agents (range: 0–8). The most frequently used therapies prior to anifrolumab, in addition to antimalarials, were belimumab (66.9%), mycophenolate mofetil (41.4%), azathioprine (41.4%), methylprednisolone boluses (30.4%), rituximab (29.1%) and methotrexate (29.1%) (table 1).

At baseline, 198 patients (96.1%) received anifrolumab in combination with other immunosuppressants, while eight patients (3.9%) received it as monotherapy. The most common concomitant treatments were oral glucocorticoids (82.3%), antimalarials (80.2%), mycophenolate mofetil (24.6%), methotrexate (17%) and azathioprine (11%).

All patients received anifrolumab at the approved dose of 300 mg intravenously every 4 weeks, except for two patients with renal involvement who received an initial loading regimen of 900 mg every 4 weeks for 3 months, followed by the standard 300 mg dose.

Clinical response

Overall, a rapid and sustained clinical improvement was observed across multiple domains. Disease activity scores showed marked reductions from the first month of treatment onwards, including SLE-DAS, SLEDAI-2K and PGA. Safety outcomes

After a mean follow-up of 7.5±5.3 months (range: 0–26), AEs were reported in 27 patients (13.1%). The overall incidence rate of AEs was 27.4 events per 100 patient-years (PY) (2.28 events per 100 patient-months (PM)). The most frequently observed relevant AEs were herpes zoster (n=5; 4.15/100 PY, 0.35/100 PM); headache, upper respiratory tract infections and pneumonia (n=3; 2.49/100 PY, 0.21/100 PM); arterial hypotension, hidradenitis suppurativa and vaginal candidiasis (n=2; 1.66/100 PY, 0.14/100 PM) (table 2).

Literature review and comparative analysis

To contextualise these findings, a narrative review of published real-world studies on anifrolumab in SLE was conducted. Most observational cohorts reported consistent clinical benefits across mucocutaneous, musculoskeletal and serological domains, with early improvements in disease activity scores and reductions in corticosteroid use. In addition, the safety profile observed in our cohort was comparable to that reported in previous studies, particularly regarding herpes zoster and mild infections, which remain the most frequent AEs. Discussion

This multicentre, real-world study provides robust evidence supporting the effectiveness and safety of anifrolumab in a large and diverse cohort of patients with SLE treated in routine clinical practice across Spain. With 206 patients from 54 hospitals (including tertiary centres, regional hospitals and general institutions), to our knowledge, this is the largest national series published to date. The geographic and institutional diversity of participating sites enhances the representativeness of the cohort and reflects the full spectrum of clinical practice and patient profiles in the Spanish healthcare system.

Our findings demonstrate a rapid and sustained reduction in disease activity across multiple domains, including musculoskeletal, mucocutaneous and haematological involvement. These improvements were consistently reflected in validated indices such as SLEDAI-2K, SLE-DAS and PGA, as well as in serological markers, with increases in complement levels and reductions in anti-dsDNA titres. These results are in line with those reported in the pivotal TULIP-1 and TULIP-2 trials, where anifrolumab showed superiority over placebo in achieving composite end points and organ-specific responses.911 However, unlike those trials, our cohort included patients with more complex disease phenotypes, including renal (27.8%) and neuropsychiatric (13.6%) involvement, as well as individuals with prior biologic exposure and comorbidities typically excluded from RCTs.12

One of the most clinically relevant findings of this study is the significant corticosteroid-sparing effect observed. The mean daily prednisone dose was reduced by >50% after only 3 months of treatment, with the most pronounced decline occurring within the first 6 months. The proportion of patients achieving a corticosteroid-sparing effect and maintaining disease control in our study aligns with data from pivotal trials such as TULIP-1 and TULIP-2,9 10 as well as with previous real-world reports13 15 and addresses a critical therapeutic goal in SLE management, given the well-documented long-term toxicity of glucocorticoids in these patients.13 Importantly, this reduction in corticosteroid use was achieved without compromising disease control or increasing organ damage, as evidenced by the stability of the SDI during follow-up. Furthermore, our cohort included a broader spectrum of disease severity and treatment histories, including patients with prior exposure to multiple biological and immunosuppressant agents, reflecting real-world complexity.

In our cohort, previous biologic exposure did not appear to markedly influence clinical response or anifrolumab treatment retention, suggesting that anifrolumab may be useful in both biologic-naïve and biologic-experienced patients. However, detailed analyses with larger samples and longer follow-up are warranted to provide more robust evidence for this subgroup finding.

The safety profile of anifrolumab in our cohort was favourable and consistent with clinical trial data until now. The most frequently reported AEs were mild-to-moderate infections, particularly herpes zoster, which occurred at rates comparable to those observed in the TULIP trials.911 Serious infections were infrequent and no new AEs or toxicity signals emerged.

Discontinuation rates in our series (9.7%) were slightly higher than those reported in clinical trials, likely due to the inclusion of patients with refractory disease or comorbidities not typically represented in randomised controlled settings. Importantly, no new reasons for discontinuation or withdrawal were identified.

When compared with other real-world studies (table 3), our results are highly concordant and expand on previous findings. The Toronto Lupus Programme cohort,13 one of the earliest real-world experiences with anifrolumab, reported significant reductions in disease activity indices and corticosteroid use after a median of 3 months. However, that study included only 25 patients and had limited follow-up. Similarly, the prospective case series by Martín-Torregrosa et al14 focused exclusively on patients with cutaneous lupus erythematosus (CLE), demonstrating rapid improvements in Cutaneous Lupus Erythematosus Disease Area and Severity Index Activity scores and patient-reported outcomes, but lacked systemic data and included only nine individuals.

More recently, the Italian multicentre cohort by Tani et al15 evaluated 26 patients with refractory SLE and confirmed short-term effectiveness of anifrolumab, with over half of the patients achieving remission and 80% low disease activity at 6 months. While these findings are encouraging, the study was limited by its modest sample size and heterogeneity in baseline treatments.

In the LOOPS registry,16 a Japanese registry including 45 patients with SLE, a drug retention rate of nearly nine out of 10 patients was observed after 26 weeks of treatment, almost 70% achieved LLDAS after 12 weeks and a significant reduction in glucocorticoid doses and Safety of Estrogens in Lupus National Assessment–SLE Disease Activity Index (SELENA-SLEDAI) score.

Viedma-Martínez et al17 conducted a retrospective observational study at a Spanish tertiary referral centre, evaluated the real-world use of anifrolumab in eight patients with CLE, most of whom had refractory disease. All patients showed clinical improvement and some achieved complete resolution of skin lesions.

In contrast, our cohort represents a broader and more representative population, with longer follow-up and more comprehensive data on organ involvement, corticosteroid tapering and safety outcomes. Notably, our study includes patients who would not have met inclusion criteria for RCTs, such as those with prior biologic failure or complex multisystem disease, thereby enhancing the external validity of our findings.

This study has several notable strengths. First, it includes a large, geographically and institutionally diverse cohort from across Spain, encompassing tertiary referral centres, regional hospitals and general care facilities. This diversity enhances the generalisability of our findings and reflects real-world clinical heterogeneity. Second, data were collected using a standardised case report form and validated by independent investigators, ensuring methodological consistency. Third, the inclusion of patients with severe and refractory disease, including those with renal and neuropsychiatric involvement, provides insights into populations often under-represented in clinical trials.

However, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective design may introduce selection and information biases and the absence of a control group limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Given the retrospective and uncontrolled design of this study, improvements in disease activity and corticosteroid reduction cannot be causally attributed to anifrolumab alone. The high rate of concomitant immunosuppressive cotherapy and the absence of a comparator group may have contributed to the observed outcomes; accordingly, our results should be interpreted as real-world associations rather than definitive evidence of a direct treatment effect. Follow-up duration was variable and relatively short in some cases, which may underestimate long-term outcomes or delayed AEs. Although the SDI remained stable during follow-up, the relatively short and heterogeneous observation period does not allow conclusions regarding long-term prevention of organ damage. SDI stability should be interpreted with caution and long-term studies are needed to clarify the impact of anifrolumab on damage accrual, particularly in terms of sustained prevention of damage over years of treatment. We did not apply a standardised flare definition, which is rarely implemented in a systematic way in routine clinical practice outside formal clinical trials, and the modest increase in glucocorticoid dose between months 6 and 12 should therefore be interpreted as a pragmatic reflection of clinical worsening in some patients rather than as a formally adjudicated flare rate. Additionally, patient-reported outcomes and IFN gene signature data were not systematically collected, which could have enriched the analysis and allowed for more personalised interpretations of treatment response.

In conclusion, in this large, multicentre real-world cohort of patients with SLE, anifrolumab demonstrated early and sustained clinical effectiveness across multiple disease domains, with a favourable safety profile and a marked corticosteroid-sparing effect. These findings are consistent with pivotal clinical trials and previously published observational studies, reinforcing the external validity of trial data in routine clinical practice. Therefore, anifrolumab represents a valuable therapeutic option for patients with refractory or difficult-to-treat SLE. Long-term follow-up and prospective studies are warranted to further assess its impact on organ damage accrual and long-standing outcomes.
