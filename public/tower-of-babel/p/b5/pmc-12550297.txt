Effects of two different doses of HIFT on physical function, cognitive performance, and quality of life in older adults with mild cognitive impairment: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Diego Fernando Afanador-Restrepo, Yulieth Rivas-Campo, Carlos Mario Basto-Cardona, David Alejandro Gonzalez-Bustamante, Agustin Aibar-Almazan, Yolanda Castellote-Caballero, Fidel Hita-Contreras, Eduardo Guzmán-Muñoz, Yeny Concha-Cisternas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/Objective: Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) compromises physical and mental function in older adults and increases the risk of dementia. High-Intensity Functional Training (HIFT) is a promising intervention, but the optimal dose-response for improving cognitive and physical outcomes in this population remains unclear.MethodsA randomized controlled trial was conducted with 224 older adults (≥65 years) allocated to three groups: high-dose HIFT (4 sessions of 60 min/week), low-dose HIFT (2 sessions of 45 min/week), and control (non-exercise activities). The intervention lasted 12 weeks. Primary outcomes included cognitive function (MAAS, MoCA, TMT A/B, VFAT, DSST), physical fitness (Senior Fitness Test, Tinetti), and quality of life (SF-36, PSQI). A repeated-measures ANOVA was used to examine the time × group interaction.ResultsSignificant time × group interactions were observed for MAAS (F = 20.50; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.005), MoCA (F = 17.40; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.034), TMT A (F = 38.60; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.098), and VFAT (F = 109.00; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.101), with greater improvements in both HIFT groups compared to control. For quality of life, significant interactions were found across all SF-36 dimensions, notably in Vitality (F = 76.20; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.095) and Emotional Role (F = 23.00; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.032). Physical fitness also improved, with significant effects in the 6-Minute Walk Test (F = 29.80; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.108) and 8-Foot Up-and-Go (F = 81.00; p < 0.001; η2G = 0.191).ConclusionBoth high- and low-dose HIFT programs improved cognitive, physical, and quality-of-life outcomes in older adults with MCI. The low-dose intervention achieved similar effects to the high-dose protocol, offering a time-efficient and scalable approach for implementation in clinical and community settings.Keywords: Aging, Cognitive dysfunction, Exercise therapy, High-intensity interval training, Mild cognitive impairment, Quality of life

Introduction

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is widely recognized as a transitional clinical stage between normal age-related cognitive decline and dementia, most commonly Alzheimer's disease.1 It is characterized by measurable deficits in one or more cognitive domains—such as memory, attention, and executive function—that exceed typical age-related changes but do not significantly interfere with daily activities.2 While some individuals with MCI remain stable or even revert to normal cognitive functioning, a substantial proportion progress to dementia, with conversion rates ranging from 15 % to 30 % annually.3, 4, 5 Although biological aging and accelerated brain aging are core contributors to MCI pathogenesis, increasing evidence highlights the role of modifiable lifestyle factors—including physical inactivity, poor dietary patterns, tobacco use, and excessive alcohol consumption—in both the onset and progression of cognitive decline.6,7

Given its rising prevalence and clinical implications, MCI has become a major global public health concern. According to the World Health Organization, approximately 47 million people worldwide were living with dementia in 2015, a figure projected to reach 152 million by 2050, with the majority of cases originating from low- and middle-income countries.8 Alzheimer's disease, responsible for 60–70 % of dementia cases, remains one of the leading causes of disability and dependency among older adults.8 Beyond cognitive deterioration, individuals with MCI frequently experience emotional and functional challenges, including increased risk of depressive symptoms, impaired gait and balance, reduced muscular strength, and diminished ability to perform daily tasks.9,10 These issues severely compromise autonomy and quality of life, underscoring the need for comprehensive and preventive approaches that address cognitive, emotional, and physical dimensions of health in this vulnerable population.

Physical exercise has emerged as one of the most effective non-pharmacological strategies for preventing or delaying cognitive decline in older adults.11 Beyond its well-established benefits for cardiovascular and musculoskeletal health, regular physical activity is associated with improvements in cognitive function, neuroplasticity, and emotional well-being.12,13 One of the key mechanisms proposed involves the upregulation of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a neurotrophin that supports neuronal survival, synaptogenesis, and neurogenesis in the adult brain.12 These neurobiological adaptations may enhance executive function, working memory, and processing speed—domains often impaired in individuals with MCI.12 Furthermore, exercise interventions have shown positive effects on sleep quality, depressive symptoms, and functional autonomy, thereby contributing to a better overall quality of life.14 Given its multidimensional benefits, physical activity represents a promising tool for maintaining cognitive health and delaying the onset of dementia.

Among the diverse modalities of exercise, High-Intensity Functional Training (HIFT) has gained attention for its potential to simultaneously target physical, cognitive, and neuromuscular functions in aging populations.15, 16, 17 HIFT is a multimodal approach that combines aerobic, resistance, balance, and functional movement exercises performed at relatively high intensities, often in a circuit format and adapted to individual capacities.15, 16, 17 This training modality not only enhances cardiorespiratory fitness, muscular strength, flexibility, and postural stability, but also engages cognitive processes such as attention, dual-tasking, and decision-making—features particularly relevant in individuals with MCI.15, 16, 17 Additionally, HIFT sessions are typically short and varied, which may improve adherence and motivation in older adults. Preliminary evidence suggests that HIFT can elicit improvements in global cognition, executive function, and verbal fluency, while also reducing depressive symptoms and fall risk.15, 16, 17 However, the optimal dose-response relationship of HIFT for maximizing cognitive and physical benefits in individuals with MCI remains unclear, and further research is needed to establish effective and sustainable protocols for this population. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of two different doses of HIFT on physical function, cognitive performance, and quality of life in older adults with MCI.

Materials and methods

Study design

This randomized controlled clinical trial (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT04638322) employed a pre–post intervention design.18 It was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Ethical approval was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Jaén (Code: 20231113/NOV.PRY1).

Participants

Participants were recruited through direct visits to community institutions where the study was presented. All eligible individuals received verbal and written information about the study and were allowed to voluntarily participate. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Participants were eligible if they were (1) aged ≥65 years; (2) community-dwelling; (3) classified as “low active” according to the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form (IPAQ-SF); and (4) able to walk independently with or without assistive devices. Exclusion criteria included (1) clinical diagnosis of major cardiovascular, pulmonary, or musculoskeletal conditions that contraindicated exercise participation; (2) severe uncorrected sensory deficits; (3) participation in any structured exercise program within the past three months; and (4) moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment, defined as a Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score <18.

Sample size estimation was performed using G∗Power 3.1 software, based on a two-sample comparison of mean changes in MoCA scores between intervention and control groups. The between-group difference in improvement (Δ = 1.5 points) and the corresponding standard deviation (SD = 2.2) were extracted from a previous randomized controlled trial in older adults with MCI.16 Assuming a 95 % confidence level (α = 0.05, two-tailed) and 80 % statistical power (β = 0.20), the required sample size was 99 participants in total (33 per group). To account for an anticipated 20 % dropout rate, as commonly reported in exercise interventions with this population, the final target sample size was 120 participants (40 per group).

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated allocation sequence (block randomization with a 1:1:1 ratio), created by a member of the research team who was not involved in participant recruitment, intervention delivery, or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was ensured through the use of sequentially numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened only after baseline assessments had been completed.

Due to the nature of the exercise intervention, neither participants nor fitness trainers could be blinded to group allocation. However, the outcome assessors responsible for administering the cognitive, physical, and quality-of-life tests remained blinded to group assignments throughout the trial. Data analysts were also blinded to group allocation until the completion of the primary statistical analyses.

Outcomes

Sociodemographic information, including age, sex, socioeconomic status, educational level, handedness, marital status, occupation, economic dependency, and frequency of tobacco and alcohol consumption, was collected through a self-administered questionnaire created using Google Forms. In addition, anthropometric measurements were obtained to calculate body mass index (BMI), using a SECA 813 digital scale (accuracy: 100 g; capacity: 200 kg) and a SECA 213 stadiometer (accuracy: 0.1 mm).

To assess mindfulness-related cognitive awareness, the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS) was administered. This validated instrument measures individuals' capacity for receptive attention to present-moment experiences in a non-judgmental manner.19 Cognitive performance was further evaluated using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), a widely recognized screening tool that examines various cognitive domains, including executive and visuospatial functions, memory, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, and orientation.20

Attention and executive functions were specifically assessed through the Trail Making Test, using both Part A (TMTA) and Part B (TMTB). TMTA evaluates psychomotor speed and visual attention, whereas TMTB focuses on cognitive flexibility and executive control. In both tests, shorter completion times indicate better cognitive performance.21

Verbal fluency was measured using the Animal Naming Test, in which participants were asked to name as many animals as possible within 1 min, excluding responses based on broad or subordinate categories.22 Processing speed was evaluated using the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST). This task required participants to match numbers with symbols according to a reference key, with performance scored based on the number of correct matches completed within a time limit of 90–120 s, up to a maximum of 60 points.23

To assess overall well-being, health-related quality of life was measured using the Short Form-36 Health Survey (SF-36), which consists of 36 items grouped into eight domains and two summary scores (physical and mental health).24 Additionally, sleep quality was measured using the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a 24-item instrument that generates a global score and seven component subscales. Higher PSQI scores indicate poorer sleep quality.25

Physical fitness and functional capacity were evaluated using the Senior Fitness Test (SFT),26 a battery that includes the arm curl and chair stand tests (for upper and lower body strength), the 6-min walk test (aerobic endurance), the chair sit-and-reach and back scratch tests (flexibility), and the 8-foot up-and-go test (agility and dynamic balance). Lastly, postural stability and fall risk were assessed using the Tinetti Performance-Oriented Mobility Assessment, which consists of two subscales: balance (maximum 16 points) and gait (maximum 12 points), for a total score of 28.27 Scores below 19 are indicative of a high risk of falling.

Interventions

Participants assigned to the high-dose group followed a supervised 12-week HIFT program, consisting of four sessions per week, each lasting 60 min. Each session was structured in three phases. The warm-up phase included 10 min of joint mobility exercises to prepare the body for high-intensity activity. This was followed by the main phase, lasting 35 min, which was divided into four high-intensity intervals performed at 80–85 % of maximum heart rate. The exercises in this phase included seated cycling simulations, wall push-ups, chair squats, and lateral lunges combined with ball throws. Each exercise was performed for 30 s at maximal safe speed, followed by 15 s of rest. Each interval lasted 5 min and was followed by 3 min of active recovery at 50–70 % of maximum heart rate.

The high-dose HIFT protocol (4 × 60-min/wk, 240 min/wk) was selected to align with the World Health Organization's recommendations for older adults (150–300 min/wk of moderate-to-vigorous activity) and to build on previous studies demonstrating that higher weekly exercise volumes yield improvements in cognition, function, and quality of life in older populations.15, 16, 17 The low-dose HIFT protocol (2 × 45-min/wk, 90 min/wk) was designed as a more feasible alternative that reflects common participation patterns in community-dwelling older adults, while still being consistent with evidence showing that even volumes below current recommendations may confer meaningful health benefits.12,14

To ensure progressive overload, the intensity and complexity of the exercises were systematically increased every three weeks. Progressive overload was applied by gradually increasing movement speed, repetitions, and reducing rest intervals. Complexity was enhanced by introducing progressive variations of functional tasks (e.g., sit-to-stand advancing to squats without chair support, wall push-ups advancing to push-ups against lower surfaces, bilateral ball throws progressing to unilateral throws with trunk rotation). Additional challenges included dual-task components, balance-demanding positions, and multi-joint movements. Exercise intensity was continuously monitored using Polar® heart rate sensors, targeting 80–85 % of maximum heart rate during high-intensity intervals and 50–70 % during active recovery. Trainers also recorded the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE, 6–20 scale) at the end of each session to complement objective monitoring and ensure participants remained within the prescribed intensity range.

Following the main exercise block, sessions concluded with a 15-min cool-down period consisting of static stretching and breathing control exercises to facilitate recovery. Throughout the intervention, heart rate was continuously monitored using wearable sensors to ensure adherence to intensity targets. All training sessions were supervised by qualified professionals, including physical education specialists, physiotherapists, and nursing staff, to ensure both safety and correct execution. Sessions were conducted in small groups of 6–10 participants, and although delivered in a group format, each exercise was individually adapted to the participants’ functional capacities to guarantee safety and effectiveness.

Participants assigned to the low-dose HIFT group followed the same exercise structure, progression plan, and supervision protocol as the high-dose group. However, the frequency and duration of sessions were reduced to two sessions per week, each lasting 45 min, over the same 12-week intervention period. This allowed for direct comparison of the effects of different training doses while maintaining consistency in content and delivery.

In contrast, the control group participated in a 12-week program of non-physical activities, which included three weekly sessions of 45 min each. These sessions involved occupational therapy-based manual activities such as painting and weaving, as well as short educational talks on the benefits of physical activity. Importantly, no structured exercise regimen was implemented for this group, allowing their participation to serve as a reference for non-exercise-based engagement.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 9.0 statistical software was used to conduct all statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all outcome variables and presented as mean and standard deviation. Normality of the data was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test, while homogeneity of variances and sphericity assumptions were verified with Levene's and Mauchly's tests, respectively. When the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. A two-way mixed ANOVA with repeated measures was performed to assess the interaction effect of time (pre vs. post) and group (control, low-dose HIFT, high-dose HIFT) for each outcome variable. When significant time × group interactions were found, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc tests were conducted to examine within-group (pre vs. post) and between-group (intervention vs. control) differences. Effect size for the interaction was quantified using the generalized eta squared (η2G). The η2G values were interpreted using the following thresholds: small (≥0.01), moderate (≥0.06), and large (≥0.14), based on established conventions. For pairwise comparisons, Cohen's d was computed to determine effect size (ES), with values of ≥0.2, ≥0.5, and ≥0.8 representing small, moderate, and large effects, respectively. Analyses were performed using a per-protocol approach, including only participants who completed both baseline and post-intervention assessments. Missing data were not imputed; participants with incomplete outcome data were excluded from the final analysis. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set for all statistical tests.

Results

A total of 245 older adults were assessed for eligibility, of whom 15 were excluded (12 did not meet inclusion criteria and 3 declined to participate). The remaining 230 participants were randomized into three groups: control (n = 78), low-dose HIFT (n = 74), and high-dose HIFT (n = 78). During follow-up, 6 participants were lost: four due to intercurrent infectious diseases (three respiratory infections and one urinary infection), including one from the control group, one from the low-dose HIFT group, and two from the high-dose HIFT group. In addition, two participants died from causes unrelated to the intervention, one in the control group and one in the high-dose HIFT group. The final analysis included 76 participants in the control group, 73 in the low-dose HIFT group, and 75 in the high-dose HIFT group (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flow diagram of participants through the trial.

No serious adverse events related to the intervention were reported. A few participants experienced mild and transient musculoskeletal discomfort (e.g., knee or lower back soreness) during the first sessions of the program. These events were resolved with exercise modifications and did not require medical attention. Importantly, no participants discontinued the intervention due to adverse events. Adherence to the intervention was high in both groups. The average attendance rate was 94.5 % in the high-dose HIFT group and 95.8 % in the low-dose HIFT group.

In terms of sex distribution, the control group was composed of 63.16 % women (n = 48) and 36.84 % men (n = 28). Similarly, the low-dose HIFT group included 61.64 % women (n = 45) and 38.36 % men (n = 28), while the high-dose HIFT group comprised 58.67 % women (n = 44) and 41.33 % men (n = 31). Regarding age, participants in the control group had a mean age of 76.74 years (SD = 6.90), those in the low-dose group averaged 75.59 years (SD = 6.54), and those in the high-dose group had a mean age of 77.76 years (SD = 7.39). As for body composition, the control group had a mean body mass index (BMI) of 27.78 kg/m2 (SD = 5.04), the low-dose group had a higher average BMI of 29.71 kg/m2 (SD = 5.92), and the high-dose group recorded a mean BMI of 28.26 kg/m2 (SD = 4.34).

Cognitive outcomes

Repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant time × group interactions for several cognitive outcomes, as detailed in Table 1. Statistically significant interactions were observed for MAAS (F = 20.50, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.005), MOCA (F = 17.40, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.034), TMT A (F = 38.60, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.098), and VFAT (F = 109.00, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.101), indicating greater improvements over time in the intervention groups compared to the control group. In contrast, no significant time × group interactions were found for TMT B (F = 0.16, p = 0.849, η2G = 0.001) and DSST (F = 0.97, p = 0.379, η2G = 0.006), suggesting similar changes across groups.

Physical fitness

Repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant time × group interactions for several physical fitness outcomes, as detailed in Table 3. Statistically significant interactions were observed for Chair Stand Test (F = 3.17, p = 0.044, η2G = 0.014), 6MWT (F = 29.80, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.108), CSR Test (F = 23.10, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.064), Back Scratch Test (F = 13.90, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.025), and 8UG (F = 81.00, p < 0.001, η2G = 0.191). In contrast, no significant time × group interaction was found for the Arm Curl Test (F = 1.25, p = 0.289, η2G = 0.006).

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial aimed to determine the optimal dose-response of HIFT in improving cognitive function, physical fitness, and quality of life in older adults with MCI. The principal findings indicate that both high-dose and low-dose HIFT protocols led to statistically significant improvements across cognitive, physical, and quality of life outcomes. Interestingly, the lower-dose protocol—consisting of shorter sessions with reduced weekly frequency—achieved benefits that were largely comparable to those of the more intensive protocol, suggesting that lower training volumes may suffice to elicit substantial health gains in this population.

These findings align with previous studies reporting that physical exercise exerts dose-dependent benefits in frail and cognitively impaired older adults.28, 29, 30, 31 However, the optimal dose necessary to achieve sustained cognitive benefits remains under investigation. Our results add to the growing evidence that meaningful improvements can be achieved even with weekly volumes lower than the 150 min usually recommended for moderate-intensity activity by WHO. Importantly, since HIFT is classified as vigorous-intensity exercise, the 90 min performed by the low-dose group still fall within the threshold of 75–150 min of vigorous activity recommended by WHO.32 Thus, our findings should be interpreted as complementary to the general health benefits of physical activity promoted by WHO guidelines, which are intended as broad public health recommendations rather than prescriptions for specific cognitive or physical outcomes. Indeed, the current data support the concept of a nonlinear dose–response curve, where moderate volumes of high-intensity exercise may yield maximal benefit with fewer sessions.33

The similar cognitive gains observed in both HIFT groups—particularly in global cognition, attentional control, and verbal fluency—may be partly explained by neurobiological mechanisms involving increased expression of BDNF. Exercise-induced BDNF upregulation has been consistently linked to neurogenesis, synaptic plasticity, and enhanced hippocampal and prefrontal connectivity, processes that are fundamental for executive and memory functions.34,35 Recent neuroimaging evidence also shows that high-intensity exercise can augment hippocampal volume, strengthen frontotemporal networks, and activate regions associated with episodic and spatial memory in older adults.36

In addition to cognitive benefits, both HIFT groups experienced improvements in physical functioning. Significant gains were observed in lower-body strength (Chair Stand Test), aerobic capacity (6MWT), flexibility, and agility (8UG), all of which are key determinants of autonomy in aging. Notably, improvements in 6MWT and 8UG were of large magnitude and were accompanied by meaningful gains in quality of life domains, particularly vitality, emotional role, and social functioning. These findings reinforce prior evidence that high-intensity exercise not only improves physical function but also contributes to enhanced psychosocial well-being and sleep quality.15,37

An important implication of this study is the demonstration that a reduced-dose HIFT protocol (2 × 45-min sessions per week) offers an efficient, less burdensome, and potentially more scalable alternative to higher-dose regimens. Shorter sessions may reduce perceived burden and logistical barriers, which could contribute to the high adherence observed in both intervention groups. While fatigue was not systematically measured, the consistent adherence rates and RPE monitoring suggest that the reduced-dose protocol was well tolerated. Furthermore, the lower-dose group performed equally—or in some domains, even better—than the high-dose group, suggesting that more is not always better when intensity is adequately controlled.

Nevertheless, some limitations should be acknowledged. The intervention period was limited to 12 weeks, precluding conclusions about the long-term sustainability of the observed effects. Additionally, the sample was geographically and demographically homogeneous, which may affect the external validity of the findings. The lack of blinding of participants and intervention staff, inherent to exercise trials, introduces a potential source of bias. Furthermore, some refinements were made to the selection and prioritization of secondary outcomes after trial registration, although all modifications were decided before data analysis and were based on feasibility and methodological considerations. Despite these constraints, this study provides robust head-to-head evidence comparing two distinct high-intensity exercise doses, offering valuable guidance for designing time-efficient interventions for older adults with MCI.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that both high- and low-dose HIFT protocols are effective in improving cognitive performance, physical fitness, and quality of life in older adults with mild cognitive impairment. Importantly, the reduced-dose protocol—characterized by lower frequency and shorter sessions—demonstrated comparable effectiveness to the higher-dose intervention, suggesting a more efficient and accessible approach to exercise prescription in this population. These findings highlight the potential of time-efficient, high-intensity interventions to produce clinically relevant benefits while minimizing participant burden and implementation costs. Future research should explore the long-term sustainability of these effects and examine their generalizability in more diverse populations and real-world community settings.
