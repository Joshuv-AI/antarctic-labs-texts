Benchmarking genomic variant calling tools in inbred mouse strains: recommendations and considerations
Authors: Alexis Garretson, Laura Blanco-Berdugo, Aleisha M G Roberts, Beth L Dumont, W Valdar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the growing affordability of whole-genome sequencing, variant identification has become an increasingly common task in modern genomics. In recent years, the number of software packages available for variant calling has rapidly increased. Understanding the benefits and drawbacks of different tools is important in setting leading practices. These considerations are especially crucial in model organism research, as many variant calling tools assume outbred genomes and implicit heterozygosity, conditions that do not apply to inbred laboratory models. Here, we present an analysis of multiple widely used variant calling tools and their performance in the simulated genomes of the C57BL/6J inbred laboratory mouse and 9 non-reference strains. Our findings reveal a tradeoff between the recall and precision of different tools. Balancing these considerations, we show that an optimal variant call set is obtained by using an ensemble approach focused on the intersection of variants reported by multiple callers. However, specific variant calling recommendations vary by strain and analytical goals. Further, we identify empirical filters for improving the performance of different variant calling tools, both for the discovery of rare variants and in the identification of strain polymorphisms. Overall, our simulation-based analysis provides best practices for calling and filtering genomic variants in inbred organisms, particularly laboratory mice.Keywords: variant calling, single nucleotide variants, genetic variation, mammalian genomes, short-read data, next-generation sequencing, simulation, WSB/EiJ, 129S1/SvImJ, A/J, CAST/EiJ, DBA/2J, NOD/ShiLtJ, NZO/HlLtJ, PWK/PhJ, SPRET/EiJ

Introduction

Despite their crucial importance for clinical research and basic biology, accurately identifying and calling single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from high-throughput sequencing data remains an imperfectly solved challenge. Sequencing errors, alignment artifacts, and biological variation can all give rise to false positive or false negative SNP calls (Li 2014; Krusche et al. 2019; Stoler and Nekrutenko 2021; Wang et al. 2023). These errors can have significant impacts on the interpretation and accuracy of research findings, as SNPs are critical inputs to many genomic analyses, including genome-wide association studies, evolutionary inferences, and population genetic studies. SNPs also have significant functional importance. SNPs have been causally linked to numerous diseases and phenotypic traits (Frazer et al. 2007; Wray et al. 2007; Altshuler et al. 2010; MacArthur et al. 2017) and can modify the efficacy of different therapeutic treatments in different individuals (Mackay et al. 2009; Alexandrov et al. 2013; Goddard et al. 2016; Lewis and Vassos 2020). The genomic distribution of SNPs also holds critical information about genome evolutionary history, including the genomic targets of natural selection and demographic events (Galtier et al. 2000; Schork et al. 2000; Emerson et al. 2001; Shakya et al. 2020).

Numerous software packages and bioinformatic pipelines have been developed for SNP calling from whole genome sequences (DePristo et al. 2011; Li 2011; Garrison and Marth 2012). These approaches have been at the forefront of scientific research in recent years, with availability and usage of tools varying over time (Fig. 1). Different variant calling tools employ diverse algorithms, statistical approaches, and filtering strategies (Table 1). These fundamental distinctions mean that different tools can produce different variant call sets from the same input sequencing data. While this point is tacitly recognized, limited guidance exists in the scientific literature to aid investigators in selecting the optimal variant calling tool or combination of tools in view of diverse analytical applications.

Consequently, researchers are often faced with uncertainty when selecting the most appropriate tool, with limited knowledge of the comparative strengths and weaknesses of competing approaches. Existing benchmark comparisons of variant calling tools use actual or simulated data from human genomes or other outbred organisms (O’Rawe et al. 2013; Pirooznia et al. 2014; Bian et al. 2018; Yao et al. 2020; Lefouili and Nam 2022). Additionally, the statistical models employed by many variant calling tools are developed and tested against outbred samples, and therefore rest on assumptions about levels of heterozygosity in diploid genomes (Pirooznia et al. 2014; Warden et al. 2014; Krusche et al. 2019; Barbitoff et al. 2022). Thus, current best practice workflows for detecting polymorphic SNPs may not be generalizable to researchers working in highly inbred or haploid model systems.

In this paper, we present a comprehensive analysis of 5 widely used variant calling tools (DeepVariant, GATK, Graphtyper, Mpileup, FreeBayes) and their performance in simulated genomes of the reference inbred laboratory mouse strain C57BL/6J (B6) and in 9 non-reference inbred strains with variable levels of genomic divergence from the reference: 129S1/SvImJ (129), A/J (AJ), CAST/EiJ (CAST), DBA/2J (DBA), NOD/ShiLtJ (NOD), NZO/HIltJ (NZO), PWK/PhJ (PWK), SPRET/EiJ (SPRET), and WSB/EiJ (WSB). We evaluate the accuracy and sensitivity of different variant calling approaches and provide benchmarks and practical guidance for variant calling using inbred genomes.

Materials and methods

Mutation and read generation

Mutations were simulated on the mouse reference genome (GRCm39) using simuG (v1.0.0; Yue and Liti 2019). Ten independent replicates of chr1 were generated, each containing 5,000 randomly placed fixed differences at a transition/transversion ratio of 1.5 compared to the reference genome (Fig. 2). simuG produced 2 key outputs: (i) a text file recording the genomic positions of the simulated SNPs and (ii) a FASTA file in which these SNPs were inserted at their respective locations.

Schematic of the experimental design. Boxes in light green indicate steps only performed for the non-B6 strains, while the boxes in orange indicate steps taken for just B6. All other aspects of the simulation protocol were identical for both reference and genetically diverse non-reference strains.

Next, InSilicoSeq (v1.5.3; Gourlé et al. 2019) was used to generate simulated Illumina paired-end 150 bp sequencing reads from the simuG FASTA file under an Illumina NovaSeq error model. Sequencing was simulated at a uniform coverage of 40× using the Lander–Waterman equation (Lander and Waterman 1988)

where C is the intended coverage, L is the read length, N is the number of reads, and G is the genome size. To simulate at coverage 40× for only chr1, we set G equal to the GRCm39 reference chr1 length (195,154,279 bp), estimating a requirement of 26.02 M 150 bp paired-end reads. To evaluate the impact of allele balance (AB) on SNP calling, the output FASTA files from simuG were combined with the unmodified reference chr1 FASTA. InSilicoSeq was then used to generate FASTQ files reflecting AB values ranging from 0.1 to 0.9 by proportionally mixing variant-supporting reads with reference reads. This process ensured that all resulting FASTQ files maintained a coverage of 40× across replicates and AB conditions. To further investigate the effects of coverage, the 40× coverage BAM files were randomly down-sampled to approximately 10× and 20× coverage using Picard (v2.23) DownsampleSam. This tool employs a probabilistic approach to retain the desired proportion of input reads, ensuring consistent coverage reduction while preserving read distribution characteristics.

For the 9 non-reference strains—129S1/SvImJ, A/J, CAST/EiJ, DBA/2J, NOD/ShiLtJ, NZO/HlLtJ, PWK/PhJ, SPRET/EiJ, and WSB/EiJ—mutations were simulated on each strain-specific assembly (Lilue et al. 2018, for accession numbers see Supplementary Table 1). This approach allowed us to capture both varying levels of divergence from the C57BL/6J reference and biologically realistic patterns of genomic variation. For each non-reference strain genome, we generated 10 replicates of 5,000 variants introduced into each chr1 sequence at 40× coverage. Despite slight differences in the chr1 genome length for the non-reference strains, we simulated the same number of reads as we did for B6, meaning that coverage values fluctuate slightly across strains. To determine the GRCm39 reference location of variants simulated on strain-specific assemblies, coordinate conversion was performed via Picard LiftOverVcf (v2.23) with chain files generated by the UCSC Genome Browser (Supplementary Table 1).

Variant calling

We aligned all simulated sequencing reads to the GRCm39 reference genome using BWA (0.7.17) (Li and Durbin 2009) and performed variant calling. Four variant calling tools (DeepVariant, FreeBayes, GATK, and Mpileup) were applied to B6 simulated data, and an additional tool, Graphtyper, was used for variant calling in the 9 non-reference strains. For all variant calling tools except Graphtyper, variants were called individually for each set of simulated reads. For Graphtyper, calling was performed jointly across all strains for each replicate (for example, jointly on all 9 strains for the simulation replicate produced under AB = 0.5 and 40× coverage). This distinction owes to the fact that Graphtyper is a pangenome approach that provides a comprehensive representation of genomic diversity across a population, rather than just capturing the genetic content of a single individual or strain (Eggertsson et al. 2017; Sibbesen et al. 2018).

Variant calling and filtering were accomplished using tool-dependent protocols (Table 3). Mpileup (executed under the command “bcftools call” in bcftools v 1.9-1) and FreeBayes (v 1.3.1) were run with default parameters. SNPs were extracted and subjected to the following post hoc filters using vcflib (v1.0.10): “QUAL > 1 & QUAL/AO > 10 & SAF > 0 & SAR > 0 & RPR > 1 & RPL > 1.” The DeepVariant call set was generated using the WGS model in DeepVariant (v 1.2.0), limiting calling to the simulated region with the flag “–regions chr1.” GATK (v 4.2.2.0) variant calling proceeded in multiple steps according to caller Best Practices. First, all read alignments to the GRCm39 reference were regrouped, and duplicated reads were marked using gatk MarkDuplicatesSpark. Resulting bams were recalibrated using gatk BaseRecalibrator and gatk ApplyBQSR. Next, SNPs were called using gatk HaplotypeCaller, limiting the call region to chr1 (-L chr1). We applied GATK-recommended hard filters: QD < 2.0 || FS > 20.0 || MQ < 40.0 || SOR > 3.0 || MQRankSum < −2.0 || MQRankSum > 4.0 || ReadPosRankSum < −3.0 || ReadPosRankSum > 3.0. Graphtyper variant calling was performed using default parameters, again limited to just chr1 (--region = chr1). Variants output by Graphtyper were filtered to exclude those meeting one or more of the following: ABHet < 17.5%, ABHom < 90%, QD < 6, QUAL score < 10, and AAScore < 0.5.

For all call sets, variants with a reference call (e.g. 0/0) were removed in order to prioritize variants that did not match the reference. We further exclude SNVs overlapping a deletion (“ALT = *”), as we exclude indels from our analysis. Any remaining multiallelic sites (i.e. sites with ≥3 possible alleles), were split into biallelic records using the command BCFtools norm -m -both. This step ensured that each individual SNV could be analyzed independently.

Call set evaluation

To evaluate the performance of each variant calling tool on our B6 simulation-based call sets, we annotated each SNP call as a false positive (i.e. called but absent from the simulated variants), false negative (i.e. present in the simulated data but not called by the tool), or true positive (i.e. called and present in the simulated data). Recall represents the proportion of the true positives (simulated variants) that were correctly identified (called by the variant caller). Recall measures the proportion of possible correct cases the model was able to identify.

Precision is the proportion of the predicted positive instances (i.e. called variants) that are truly positive and is a measure of the reliability of the positive predictions.

The F1 score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, summarizing the performance across both metrics to provide a single score to evaluate model performance.

Significance between tools and simulation parameters was assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests. The relationship between sequencing metrics and variant properties was evaluated using linear regression models. Because the relationship between AB and each variant calling performance metric appeared logarithmic, we applied a natural logarithm transformation to the AB variable. While we initially fit a mixed-effects model with replicate as a random effect, the variance estimates for the replicate-level random effect were near zero, indicating minimal variation attributable to this factor. Machine learning of optimal tool combination and filtration strategies

We used rpart (Therneau et al. 2023) via the tidymodels (Kuhn and Wickham 2023) package to generate a classification decision tree to determine the best combination of variant calling tools and to assess whether additional filtering could improve SNP calling performance. For this purpose, we designated 25% of all called variants as a testing set and the remaining 75% as a training set. We generated CART (classification and regression tree) decision trees by modeling the probability that a given variant in the simulation dataset was correctly called by a given variant calling tool.

To evaluate the performance of variant calling in the 9 non-B6 strains, we followed the above procedure, with the key modification of including tool performance in recovering known strain-specific polymorphisms (Keane et al. 2011). We also generated CART regression trees to evaluate the impact of post hoc variant filtration on tool performance. To this end, we built CART classification trees for each tool, AB, and strain combination using the following features: (i) the genotype of the called variant, (ii) whether the site was included in the set of known SNPs for that strain, (iii) the output quality score for the variant, (iv) the RepeatMasker family-level annotation (based on GRCm39 annotations), (v) whether the variant fell into a structurally variable region of that strain's genome (as annotated by Ferraj et al. 2023 for all strains excluding SPRET) and, if so, SV type (insertion or deletion) and SV length, and (vi) whether the variant fell into a set of genes known to be rapidly evolving and highly copy number variable in mice (e.g. vomeronasal receptors, olfactory genes, zinc-finger proteins) (Niimura et al. 2024).

Results

Precision and recall trade-offs in variant calling performance

We began by assessing the performance of 4 variant calling tools using simulated sequence reads derived from the mouse reference genome. This simulation design represents a “best case scenario,” as sequence divergence from the reference is restricted to sites of simulation-induced single-base mutations, minimizing possible variant calling artifacts due to read mis-mapping.

Recall, precision, and F1 scores varied significantly across variant calling tools and simulation conditions (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 2). As expected, performance metrics showed notable gains with increasing coverage and AB (Fig. 3). While this trend was consistently observed across all tools, the magnitude of the improvements in recall and precision varied by caller (Table 2, Fig. 3d). Overall, the performance of individual callers was highly consistent across replicates for each simulation condition (average variance: recall = 2.9 × 10−5, precision = 3.4 × 10−5, and F1 = 2.9 × 10−5, Fig. 3a–d), indicating that the specific locations of the simulated mutations had negligible effect on general performance of each variant calling tool.

Under the set of simulation conditions where variant calling is expected to be the most accurate (i.e. AB = 0.9 and coverage = 40×), precision performance across all tools approached the maximum of 100%. DeepVariant exhibited significantly lower precision than other callers under these ideal conditions, but the magnitude of this difference was slight (precision = 0.999 ± 0.0003). However, at lower coverage values, DeepVariant's reduced precision was counter-balanced by higher recall (0.995 ± 0.001; Mpileup = 0.993 ± 0.001; FreeBayes = 0.977 ± 0.001; GATK = 0.901 ± 0.003), revealing a tradeoff between these 2 performance indicators.

Ensemble approaches improve variant calling performance, particularly when coverage is low

To better understand the performance differences across variant calling tools, we next evaluated the intersections of variant call sets across tools at each coverage (10×, 20×, 40×) and at 3 representative AB levels (0.1, 0.5, and 0.9; Fig. 4a, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). We uncover nuanced patterns of agreement and divergence between callers that are not captured by overall precision and recall estimates. For example, even at high coverage, the majority of variants simulated at AB = 0.1 were missed by all callers (Supplementary Fig. 2). While performance improves as AB increases, a large share of simulated variants are still missed by all tools, especially at low coverage (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). On the other hand, the majority of false-positive calls is unique to a single tool, rather than shared across callers (Fig. 4a). However, considering only false-positive variants that are detected by multiple callers, most are shared between Mpileup and DeepVariant, suggesting common error profiles associated with these 2 tools (Fig. 4a). GATK recovered few unique true positives, but a relative excess of these unique true variant calls was recovered under low AB conditions compared to other callers. GATK may have unique performance gains for detecting rare, low AB variants, particularly in combination with other callers that recover a greater share of variants under these conditions (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

We next used a decision tree framework to find the optimal ordering and combination of variant calling tools to apply in pursuit of a high-quality variant call set (Fig. 4b–e). To make this discrimination, we relied on the importance values, which quantify how much each feature (in this case, each variant calling tool) contributes to correctly discriminating between true and false positives, with higher importance values indicating greater influence. At 10× coverage, importance values varied across simulated AB values, with no single tool consistently dominating across all conditions (Fig. 4e). Mpileup had high importance at lower AB but importance declined with increasing AB, whereas DeepVariant had intermediate importance at high and low AB but higher importance at intermediate AB (Fig. 4e). GATK had relatively low importance at low AB, but importance rose steadily with increasing AB (Fig. 4e). In contrast, at 20× coverage, importance scores stabilized across AB, with Mpileup having the highest importance across all conditions, followed by DeepVariant. GATK and FreeBayes were only retained in a small number of models with extreme AB (0.1 and 0.9) and contributed little to overall model performance (Fig. 4e). At a depth of 40×, all final trees had no supported splits, indicating that the most effective approach for maximizing the true positive rate was to use the union of all 4 variant calling tools (Supplementary Fig. 3).

For each simulation condition, we assembled a high-quality variant call set in accordance with the optimal decision tree. Strikingly, when AB > 0.25, there is little difference in the precision of these call sets across coverage values (Supplementary Fig. 4). This finding suggests that the reduced performance of variant calls on low coverage datasets can be overcome, at least to some extent, by using ensemble calling approaches that require support from multiple variant calling tools to exclude false-positive calls (Fig. 4, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). In contrast, recall performance exhibits strong dependence on both coverage and AB across these optimized variant call sets, with performance gains incurred for increasing values of both simulation variables (Supplementary Fig. 4). Similarly, when targeting low AB variants (AB < 0.25, such as rare somatic mutations) coverage can be critically important for confidently identifying true mutations.

Sample divergence from the reference genome is associated with high numbers of false positive SNP calls

We evaluated 5 variant calling tools (DeepVariant, GATK, Graphtyper, Mpileup, FreeBayes) across 9 genetically diverse inbred mouse strains—129, AJ, CAST, DBA, NOD, NZO, PWK, SPRET, and WSB—each with varying degrees of genetic divergence from the reference B6 strain (Fig. 5a and b). Each combination of strain, AB threshold, and variant calling tool was analyzed across 10 independent simulation replicates. Because mutations were simulated against strain-specific reference genomes, coordinates were first lifted from the original strain reference to the GRCm39 reference (see Methods). The proportion of successfully lifted-over variants was high for the 8 Mus musculus strains, ranging from an average of 96% in PWK to 98.2% in WSB (Fig. 5c), but low for the Mus spretus strain, SPRET (44.7%; range of 2,199 to 2,235 variants successfully lifted over across simulation replicates). A small number of variants (<0.6%) lifted over to other chromosomes (Supplementary Table 3), but the majority of failures did not have a clear cause. These failures may be due to the presence of assembly gaps, structural variants between mouse strains, or errors in the chain files used to convert between assemblies (Ormond et al. 2021). Overall, we observe poor variant calling performance in SPRET, with very low recall and precision across all simulation conditions (Fig. 5d–f). These outcomes presumably reflect the high genetic divergence of SPRET from the GRCm39 reference, with approximately 1 sequence variant every ∼100 bp (Dejager et al. 2009).

For all surveyed strains, variant call sets were dominated by false-positive calls and the resulting precision estimates were generally quite low (Fig. 5d–f, Supplementary Table 4). Overall, the classical laboratory strains have higher precision than CAST, PWK, and WSB (Fig. 5d), a trend mirrored in F1 scores (Fig. 5f). However, while the precision was low across all strains, the majority of simulated mutations were detected in each call set, with recall values close to 1 at intermediate and high AB (Fig. 5e). SPRET remains an exception, with uniformly low recall across simulated AB. For the majority of strains, Mpileup, DeepVariant, and GATK reach near-perfect recall for AB >0.3, whereas Graphtyper and FreeBayes do not reach similar recall performance until AB > 0.5 (Figure 5e). Taken together, our findings indicate that SNP variant calling in diverse inbred strains is challenged by large numbers of false-positive calls, even though most simulated variants are detected.

Variant calling performance declines as strain divergence from the reference sequence increases

We next adopted a modeling approach to evaluate the impact of AB and strain divergence from the mouse reference genome on simulated SNP calling performance. In models exploring the sources of variance in precision, model intercepts varied across variant callers, with the lowest precision intercept in Graphtyper and the highest in FreeBayes and GATK (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Table 5). (Note that, because AB is log-transformed in these models (see Methods), the intercept represents tool performance when AB is 1, and the focal strain is either identical to the reference (in models with chr1 SNP count modeled as a continuous variable) or 129 (in models with strain treated as a factor). However, as in the reference simulations, these trends in caller performance were not mirrored in recall, with DeepVariant having the highest intercept in our recall model (Fig. 6a). Intriguingly, models evaluating caller performance at known fixed sites between strains showed even more pronounced differences in precision and recall across callers (Fig. 6b). In particular, recall is markedly lower across all tools, with FreeBayes and GATK exhibiting especially poor performance by both recall and precision metrics (Fig. 6b). AB did not contribute uniformly to increasing model performance across variant calling tools, although the relative magnitude of the performance gains for each tool were similar for both precision and recall (Fig. 6a). Notably, FreeBayes and Graphtyper enjoy the largest performance gains with increasing AB. As expected, the AB of simulated mutations did not impact our ability to detect fixed strain-specific SNPs (Fig. 6b).

Unsurprisingly, increasing SNP divergence relative to the reference genome reduces precision for all variant calling tools. We observe the largest negative impact with GATK (Fig. 6a). Using 129 as a baseline (i.e. the strain with the fewest number of fixed SNPs compared to the reference; Fig. 5a, Supplementary Table 4), we find no significant difference in precision between 129 and A/J, NOD, or NZO, but model performance decreases for more divergent strains, with particularly steep losses for wild-derived strains CAST, PWK, WSB, and SPRET (Fig. 6c). In contrast, the increasing number of SNPs improved the recall for known sites (Fig. 6b). This observation is likely due, at least in part, to a pure statistical effect: when both the number of predicted positives and the actual positives increase, the model has more opportunities to recover true positives, even without a substantive improvement in discrimination. In favor of this interpretation, we find that as the number of SNPs increases, precision decreases, and the total size of the call set increases dramatically (Supplementary Fig. 5). These observations reflect a tendency of the variant calling tools to call a larger share of potential positions as variants, capturing many of the true positive SNPs but leaving an enrichment of false positive sites. Alternatively, increased recall with increased divergence to the reference could suggest that a higher number of true positives increases the chance that some fall within regions of the genome where their detection is more accurate, effectively boosting true positive recovery. Below, we test this latter hypothesis by assessing model performance under various variant filtration conditions, including genomic positions and sequence context.

Evaluating optimal tool combinations in genetically diverse inbred strains

As with the reference-based B6 simulations, we used a decision tree framework to find the optimal combination of variant calling tools to produce a high-confidence variant call set for each of our genetically diverse inbred strains. Independent trees were constructed for each strain and AB combination and for both known and simulated sites. In all strains except SPRET and across all AB values, GATK had the highest importance values (Fig. 7a). However, the relative importance of GATK compared to other callers varied with AB. In the M. m. domesticus inbred strains (i.e. excluding CAST and PWK), the importance of DeepVariant was relatively higher at intermediate AB (Fig. 4e). In CAST and PWK, removing known sites had greater importance in tree inference relative to other strains, in concordance with the higher number of fixed SNPs for those strains compared to the reference (Fig. 7a, Fig. 5b). Very few trees included Mpileup or Graphtyper, and the importance of these callers was low in those trees that did retain these variant call outputs (Fig. 7a).

All SPRET trees had no splits, indicating that no combination of tools sufficiently alters the probability of recovering true-positive variants enough to justify a split (see Supplementary Fig. 3). We suspect that this outcome reflects both the small number of simulated variants that were successfully lifted over from SPRET to the GRCm39 reference genome and the high number of false-positive calls recovered in this strain across all tools (Fig. 5).

Site-based filtration improves precision even in highly diverged samples

We next built CART classification trees to explore whether variant calling performance could be improved by applying genomic context or region-based filters. Trees aimed to delineate a post hoc filtering program maximizing the recovery of true positive SNPs for each simulated combination of tool, strain, and AB, guided by the following potential filters: (i) genotype call state, (ii) variant quality, (iii) whether the site was a known site, (iv) whether the variant resides in an annotated repeat and if so, repeat type, (v) whether the variant lies a strain-specific structurally variable region, and if so, the length and type of SV, and (vi) overlap with a list of genes known to be rapidly evolving and structurally dynamic in mice. We find that all trees incorporated at least 2 filters, indicating that the performance of all tools can be improved by application of location-based filters (average number of rules across trees = 5.75, Supplementary Fig. 3). Trees for non-domesticus strains tended to feature more bifurcation rules (average of 5.96 rules in PWK and 6.22 in CAST), and we report differences in the number of rules across tools, ranging from 3.24 rules in GATK to 7.2 rules in Mpileup (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Across the assessed potential filtering criteria, the feature with the highest average importance was the genotype state output by the variant calling tool (e.g. heterozygous [0/1] or homozygous [1/1] for the alternate allele, as well as nonstandard calls [1/0, 1|0, 0|1]). In alignment with our simulation workflow, all trees recommended retaining only 1/1 genotypes for known fixed sites, and 0/1 heterozygous calls for simulated sites. Variant quality and whether the location was a known site also carried high importance (Fig. 7b). The remaining filtering features exhibited variability in their importance across replicates under a given set of simulation conditions, indicating sensitivity to the random location of simulated SNPs. Thus, it could be important to consider whether there are known mutational processes or biases influencing the genomic context of variants expected in a given study cohort, as this could inform the selection of optimal location-based filters. Generally, however, excluding SNPs in repeats had higher importance than any SV-related filter or eliminating SNPs in blacklisted genes (Fig. 7b). This finding presumably reflects the larger number of bases annotated as repetitive DNA vs those within SVs or other blacklisted regions. The most common repeat family split across trees called for the removal of variants falling in low complexity, satellite, and simple repeats, as these regions are most enriched for false positives. The blacklisted gene list did not have high importance in our models, although a few blacklisted genes are present on chr1. Future work exploring this feature in whole genomes is likely necessary to fully assess the utility of this filter (Fig. 7b).

The application of these filters greatly increased the precision of the resulting call sets across all strains, eliminating a substantial proportion of the false positives and raising the precision of the naïve call sets (Fig. 5d) to values above 0.5 for all strains, even at low AB (Supplementary Fig. 6). However, this approach does reduce the recall considerably compared to the unfiltered call sets, particularly for extreme AB. These findings emphasize the tradeoff between precision and recall under experimental variant calling conditions. Post-filtration, the highest precision at low AB is achieved using the GATK call sets. However, for AB >0.5, the performance of DeepVariant and GATK is quite similar, with DeepVariant achieving higher precision in some conditions, and often with higher recall. Taken together, our findings indicate that straightforward filtration to exclude repetitive regions and strain-specific structural variation can improve the precision of variant calling without substantial reductions in recall for SNPs with intermediate AB.

Discussion

Variant calling must balance the detection of true variants while minimizing the recovery of false-positive calls. Different variant calling tools address this goal through the implementation of distinct algorithms, statistics, and advised filtering approaches, but the relative performance of different callers across different experimental settings is not fully understood. Here, we evaluate the overall performance and trade-offs among 5 popular variant calling tools using simulated data from inbred laboratory mouse strains. We find significant variation in the performance of our tested callers, including differences in recall, precision, and false positive rates across different simulated AB and coverage levels.

While depth of sequencing is known to impact SNP call rates (Kishikawa et al. 2019), we found that increasing depth had nonuniform effects on the performance of the evaluated callers. For example, at low coverage (10×), Mpileup had higher precision and recall than DeepVariant (Fig. 3b), but at high coverage (40×), DeepVariant had higher precision than Mpileup. Similarly, higher AB was associated with an increased probability of detection by all algorithms, but increasing AB had the largest effect on DeepVariant recall and the overall performance of FreeBayes (Table 2). These findings carry notable relevance for variant calling efforts in diverse laboratory mouse strains, where AB is expected to be 0.5 for heterozygous variants and 1 for variants fixed between inbred mouse strains. On the other hand, AB may be much lower than 0.5 in studies of somatic variation. Caller performance at low AB will have a large effect on the reliability of the resulting call sets and downstream biological inferences.

Our results suggest that the 2 haplotype-based variant calling approaches (GATK and FreeBayes) generally outperform alternative tools in precision but suffer a corresponding reduction in recall. This may reflect inherent properties of the callers themselves. For instance, the GATK HaplotypeCaller first flags potentially variable regions for local de novo reassembly. While this approach may improve accuracy, it may be overly stringent and miss a subset of true variants. While other surveyed tools have lower precision, the application of straightforward, targeted filters can improve precision to within the ranges seen for FreeBayes and GATK.

As has been suggested by other studies (Kim et al. 2014; Krøigård et al. 2016; Bian et al. 2018; Bergeron et al. 2022; Sendell-Price et al. 2023; Wooldridge et al. 2025), we find that an ensemble approach that relies on the integration of variants called by multiple variant calling tools produces superior results compared to any one tool on its own. However, our results also show that some false positives are shared across multiple callers (Fig. 4). Even in the reference strain, we recover some variants that are shared by 3 tools at low coverage (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). At intermediate coverage levels in simulated data from B6, a combination of Mpileup and DeepVariant (Fig. 4d) produces a high-quality call set with minimized false positives and the largest share of true positive SNPs. However, in the 40× coverage condition, most variants are recovered by all tools, and the lingering false positives detected by DeepVariant and Mpileup are likely counter-balanced by the recovery of unique true-positive calls introduced by those tools (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Thus, while our work exposes a number of generalizable patterns, there is no single selection or combination of variant calling tools that will provide a one-size-fits-all solution for variant calling across all experimental and analytical applications in inbred house mice. For example, FreeBayes output virtually no false positives in our simulated data (Fig. 3a), contradicting a previous study reporting large numbers of false-positive calls recovered by this caller in simulated human tumor datasets (Bian et al. 2018). Depending on the clinical or research question being pursued, FreeBayes may be the most appropriate tool despite its absence in our optimal recommendations. Alternatively, if recall is more important than precision for a given analysis, exclusive use of DeepVariant or Mpileup may be the most beneficial approach.

Our work also underscores the importance of using data-driven filtering approaches. Genotype and the variant quality score were consistently the strongest predictors of variant validity, but genomic context also matters. Notably, repetitive elements like low complexity, satellite, and simple repeats were enriched for false-positive variant calls, highlighting the utility of straightforward repeat masking. While filtration clearly boosts precision, especially in low AB scenarios, it also reduces recall, reinforcing an inherent tradeoff. Ultimately, our findings support a pragmatic approach: strategic use of genomic context, particularly repeats and known variant loci, can meaningfully improve variant calling precision without overly compromising recall, with varying magnitudes of importance depending on the types of variants being identified and strain genetic background.

Even after filtering, we recover a large number of persistent false positive SNP calls in divergent strains using Mpileup and Graphtyper. This finding likely points to deeper issues tied to cryptic strain-specific structural variation or alignment challenges that are not fully addressed by existing methods. In the case of Graphtyper, some of these performance losses may be related to the underlying graph approach implemented by the software. Graph aligners try to construct paths in the alignment graph, but if samples carry large, novel variants not in the graph (e.g. insertions, rearrangements, or divergent haplotypes), reads may outright fail to align, align to the wrong part of the graph, or align equally well to multiple, incorrect paths. These outcomes could lead to missed variants, false positives, and overall poor call quality (Sibbesen et al. 2018; Hickey et al. 2020; Kaye and Wasserman 2021; Rautiainen et al. 2023). While we do not consider inversions here, we note that inverted regions may be especially important to investigate as regions enriched for poor-quality calls in future applications of graph-based call sets. Further, our adoption of a graph approach may have been limited by our restricted focus on chr1. A small fraction of variants simulated in diverse strain genomes lifted over to other chromosomes on the reference genome (Supplementary Table 3), highlighting the presence of inter-chromosomal translocations that could impact graph construction and the ability to accurately call variants. Importantly, graph-based approaches show significant promise in detangling complex variants (Garrison et al. 2018; Rakocevic et al. 2019), and the performance of graph-based approaches for SNV calling may not reflect their advantages for SV calling or other types of variant discovery. Finally, graph-based approaches may be most powerful when applied to heterozygous or mixed-ancestry samples. Our reliance on inbred strains—most with relatively little divergence from the reference—may have obscured these advantages.

Recommendations and future directions

Together, our results lead us to a set of general recommendations for variant calling in inbred strain genomes. First, we emphasize the use of ensemble approaches that combine 2 or more tools that employ distinct statistical or algorithmic frameworks for variant discovery. Our results show that ensemble approaches consistently increase the recovery of true-positive variants and minimize the number of recovered false positives. Second, as divergence from the reference genome increases, application of sensible quality and sequence-context filters can minimize the number of false-positive calls. All callers output a measure of variant quality or confidence, with false positives enriched among low-quality calls. Further, excluding variants in regions of the genome known to be enriched for false positives—including variants located in repetitive or structurally complex regions, annotated SVs, or overlapping rapidly evolving gene families—can result in substantial gains in caller performance. These performance gains may be disproportionately impactful for samples with high divergence from the reference. While we relied on high-quality curated sets of SVs for strains included in our simulation study, such resources may not be available for all study organisms. In this case, SV call sets could be generated from available whole-genome data and used as a mask. Finally, across our simulations, the quality of variant calls was lowest in samples with the highest divergence from the reference genome (SPRET, PWK, and CAST), emphasizing the importance of reference selection and continued investment in generating lineage-specific references for accurate SNV detection.

Our findings are most immediately relevant to trio or pedigree studies of mutations in the mouse strains studied here. However, beyond this narrow application, our results carry broader relevance across multiple domains of biomedical research. Inbred model systems other than mice—including rats, guinea pigs, plants, and fruit flies—are routinely used in genetic studies. The variant calling challenges noted in our simulation results and suggested mitigation approaches are likely to translate to these systems. Moreover, the best practice strategies for regional filtration in non-reference strains may help guide decisions about variant filtration and prioritization in multiparent populations derived from strains included in our study, including recombinant inbred panels like the BXD (Ashbrook et al. 2021) and Collaborative Cross (Srivastava et al. 2017), and outbred models like Diversity Outbred mice (Churchill et al. 2012). Additionally, the observation of varying caller performance at low AB and identified filtering strategies to improve recovery of true positives under low AB conditions is especially relevant to studies of somatic mutations and cancer, where ABs can deviate substantially from germline expectations.

Here, we focused on default tool parameters and varied the number of simulated variants and the genetic background of the samples. This approach allowed for controlled comparison across tools but may not reflect tool performance under optimized parameter settings. Future work investigating the impact of alternative parameter settings, including parameters related to alignment quality or expected heterozygosity, will likely be informative. An additional limitation of this study is that we did not assess the computational time or resource costs associated with each tool. Runtime and memory usage are increasingly important considerations for scalability and sustainability and could inform tool selection in cases where multiple tools are expected to be comparable in performance. Additionally, while we focus on chr1 as broadly representative of mouse genome sequence variation, variant calling performance in specific genomic regions of interest may diverge from these generalized findings. In particular, structurally complex or repeat-rich loci such as the major histocompatibility complex, the Y chromosome, the defensin cluster, and sensory gene clusters may require uniquely tailored variant calling and filtration practices. Future work interrogating variant calling performance in such regions may be required to identify the analysis path for obtaining optimized variant call sets.

Conclusions

The utility of sequencing data depends on the availability and accuracy of tools to identify critical genomic features, including genetic variants. Through simulation, we show that selection of variant calling tools and filtration strategies can have a meaningful impact on the accuracy of reported variants and that there are precision and recall trade-offs across callers. Because different clinical and scientific questions impose distinct tolerances for recall, precision, or false positives, different callers may be most appropriate in different experimental contexts. Through extensive simulation of sequencing data from the C57BL/6J inbred mouse strain and 9 non-reference laboratory inbred strains, we systematically document differences in SNP calling performance across tools, strains, and conditions. We show that an optimized call set is obtained by using ensemble approaches and filtering based on positional genomic context, with a particular eye toward repetitive regions. We provide a thorough release of the optimized workflow for each of the simulated contexts and strains we surveyed as Supplementary material (Supplementary Table 5, and 10.6084/m9.figshare.29089805). As the number of variant calling tools available for processing next-generation sequencing data continues to proliferate, careful benchmark evaluations are needed to understand their benefits and drawbacks and to set leading practices for the clinic and research environments. Further, understanding the conditions where existing tools underperform also allows for targeted development to fill existing software gaps.
