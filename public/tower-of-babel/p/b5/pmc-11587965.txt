A case of central airway collapse following extubation attributable to an undiagnosed endotracheal tumor
Authors: Shalendra Singh, Manish Singh, Avinash Das
Published: 2024

Sir,

The liberation from mechanical ventilation (MV) in the intensive care unit is a critical step toward recovery. Here, we report a case who experienced central airway collapse following extubation, which was later found to be an undiagnosed endotracheal tumor (ET).

A 20-year-old 23-week primigravida frequently visited the hospital in the last year as a case of uncontrolled bronchial asthma. She got admitted with severe dyspnea with an oxygen saturation of 76% on room air. After the initial management failed for severe acute asthma, she required MV. She was ventilated with sedation for 24 h. Following a successful weaning trial, she underwent extubation. However, she quickly manifested respiratory distress with stridor, leading to urgent reintubation. A computed tomography (CT) scan of the chest revealed a well-defined intraluminal soft tissue density mass (13 × 15 × 12.5 mm) arising from the left posterior lateral tracheal wall proximal to the carina with bilateral lower lobe consolidation [Figure 1]. Further video bronchoscopic evaluation corroborated the presence of a pedunculated lesion situated 2 cm proximal to the carina.

An endotracheal mass originating from the tracheal wall proximal to the carina is visible in the CT scan image of the chest in the axial (a) and sagittal (b) orientations

To be liberated from MV assistance, our patient met all of the extubation criteria. Her arterial gas analysis was normal, and she was completely awake. Her rapid shallow breathing index was 70, indicating a strong likelihood that she could breathe on her own without assistance. She entered stridor as soon as she was extubated. When the vocal cords were purposefully inspected after reintubation, laryngoscopy revealed no abnormalities, indicating that a central cause of airway collapse must exist. Studies indicate that airway failure accounts for 31–45% of extubation failure cases, while non-airway failure is responsible for 50–69%.[1] Non-airway failure is marked by the inability to breathe without invasive MV, including conditions such as congestive heart failure, respiratory failure, and encephalopathy. Spontaneous breathing trials (SBTs), along with meticulous cardiac, diaphragmatic, pleural, and lung assessments using brain natriuretic peptide or ultrasound during SBT, are widely accepted tools for evaluating non-airway extubation failure. Airway failure encompasses upper airway causes such as laryngeal edema, vocal cord dysfunction, excessive dynamic airway collapse, tracheobronchomalacia, tracheal tumors, and lower airway causes such as excessive secretions.

After patients on MVs are extubated, central airway collapse contributes significantly, a factor that is often overlooked. Tracheomalacia is the most common cause of airway collapse. The anesthesiologist should consider physiological and pathological narrowing of the central airway as the primary diagnosis during reintubation.

Our patient presents with chronic cough and wheeze. These findings frequently result in an incorrect diagnosis of asthma. It is difficult to conclusively pinpoint ET as an explanation for MV. Pregnancy-related physiological changes frequently cause increased breathing.[2] The patient in this instance failed extubation after a year of incorrect asthma diagnosis, and it was discovered that ET, which encircled 70% of the tracheal lumen, was the cause of the asthma. Patients of ET on ventilators have high peak airway pressure, difficulty clearing secretions, and repeated unsuccessful weaning. Whenever patients experience reintubation due to stridor, bronchoscopy is the best course of action.[3] CT scan can also be employed to know the exact diagnosis.

ET is a rare and under-recognized contributing factor to respiratory compromise. A high degree of suspicion on behalf of the anesthesiologist would help to recognize and treat such causes of catastrophic extubation failure.
