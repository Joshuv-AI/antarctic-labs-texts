Refolding of the Alkali Denatured Bovine Beta-Lactoglobulin by the Synthetic ZnO Nanochaperone: A Useful Technique for Protein Renaturation
Authors: Ramkrishna Dalui, Subrata Sardar, Hasan Parvej, Shahnaz Begum, Falguni Mondal, Nayim Sepay, Umesh Chandra Halder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Proteins undergo unfolding and subsequent denaturation by the alternation of physical parameters like pH and temperature or by the addition of salts and denaturants. Protein denaturation and misfolding make it nonfunctional or trigger protein aggregation. Owing to their size, shape, and surface charges, nanoparticles act as artificial chaperones by providing the scaffold for the favorable conformation that prevents protein aggregation and refolds the protein into its native functional form. In the present study, the structure of the unfolded bovine beta-lactoglobulin in an alkaline medium has been investigated and compared with the native protein at physiological pH. Further, this alkali-unfolded beta-lactoglobulin was refolded to its native-like structure by the gradual addition of synthesized ZnO nanoparticles, thus acting as an artificial chaperone. Applying multispectroscopic, DLS, and electron microscopy studies, we have investigated the structures and hydrodynamic radii of the native, unfolded, and refolded proteins. The hydrodynamic radii of the refolded protein were nearly identical to those of the native protein. Therefore, the refolded beta-lactoglobulin demonstrated the structure that is close to the native form.

Introduction

In many food and drug industries, proteins are used as one of the important components. Their processing and stabilization in solutions have received much attention and have been extensively studied. , Proteins naturally tend to remain in their functional and soluble state under physiological conditions. However, they often have a propensity to aggregate in altered environmental conditions. Aggregation in vivo causes several neurodegenerative diseases and metabolic disorders. In such cases, aggregated proteins appear as thread-like species called amyloid fibrils. , Protein folding in vivo is a very complex process occurring in an environment crowded with numerous molecules and mediated by molecular chaperones. The molecular chaperones play important roles in controlling proteins’ folding/unfolding phenomenon and guiding them to attain native structures from partially folded states. By stabilizing the hydrophobic residues or balancing the internal charges, the molecular chaperones not only trap the initially formed unfolded protein structure but can also prevent the irreversible aggregation of proteins during their refolding. , Hence, in vitro studies of folding pathways to unravel the refolding mechanism are of great interest. Identification and characterization of the folding intermediates are the key steps to understanding folding, stabilization, and, hence, the function of a protein. Several techniques are employed for the renaturation of the proteins from their solutions. Among them are the methods of removal of the denaturants from the protein solution, controlling the different physical parameters like pH, temperature, or ionic strength of the solution, or the methods involving the addition of refolding agents to the denatured protein solutions. ,

Bovine beta-lactoglobulin (β-lg), a well-known globular whey protein in ruminant milk (MW 18.3 kDa), is one of the most extensively studied proteins. Bovine β-lg belongs to a class of important carrier proteins for oxidation-sensitive hydrophobic drugs and nutraceuticals, showing unique acidic pH resistivity and potential encapsulating properties. At elevated temperatures, the protein undergoes a conformational change with the exposure of its hydrophobic residues and the thiol (SH) group of the Cys121 residue. Therefore, β-lg has been considered as a model protein for the study of the aggregation and folding/unfolding phenomenon of proteins. Moreover, it is water-soluble and consists of nine antiparallel β-strands and one α-helix segment, where the hydrophobic chains are mainly buried.

Different methods, such as temperature, a combination of heat and pressure, high-intensity ultrasound, microwave radiation, and enzymatic hydrolysis can destroy or change such epitopes in β-lg. However, the pH variation of the medium has a profound influence on the structure of β-lg. At pH 3, bovine β-lg is known to undergo dimerization with slight alteration of the structure. Below pH 3.0, the dimer dissociates into monomers with the preservation of their native structure. Between pH 4.0 and 5.0, β-lg undergoes a dimer-to-octamer structural transition as measured by ORD. It is interesting to note that all the structural changes between pH 2.0 to pH 9.0 do not involve any appreciable changes in the native-like-β-barrel conformation of β-lg. But, above pH 9, β-lg undergoes an irreversible, base-induced unfolding with the global disruptions of both secondary and tertiary structures. Loosening of the globular structure in alkaline pH affects the accessibility of β-lg to tyrosinase-induced oxidation and subsequent cross-linking. This loss of native structure brought about by any external agency led to the formation of amorphous or fibril-like aggregates.

In recent years, great advances have been observed in the study of nanomaterials because of their unique properties and potential applications, i.e., biosensing, biolabeling, therapy, and tissue engineering. In this context, polymers, metallic nanoparticles (NPs), silica NPs, and self-assembled nanostructures , have been developed as synthetic chaperones. The porous nanoparticles have shown their efficacy in delivering biomolecules like proteins and plasmid DNA to their target cells owing to their large surface area, changeable pore size, and flexible surface modifications. Recently, multifunctional synthetic nanochaperones have been employed for peptide folding and intracellular delivery. Our group has reported that negatively charged gold NP stabilizes the monomeric structure of bovine β-lg, inhibiting the thermal unfolding and aggregation of β-lg. Thermally unfolded GFP at 100 °C was refolded by ZnO, as evidenced by fluorescence and DLS studies. In another investigation, thermally inactivated α-amylase was observed to regain 65% of its residual activity on incubation with unmodified TiO2 NPs. Magnetic iron (II and III) oxide NP help to refold thermally denatured cholesterol oxidase Surfactants also modulate the unfolding and refolding by their synergistic association with the protein. − Few NPs (like ZnO and gold and silver NPs) are less toxic and biocompatible and can be employed in several biomedical and pharmaceutical applications. Horse cytochrome C has been reported to refold from its alkaline transition state by ZnO NP. ZnO NP influences the structure and stability of alpha-lactalbumin. ,, However, limited investigations have been done to identify the alkaline unfolded states of bovine β-lg around pH 11–12, where the tyrosine residues of the protein remain deprotonated. At high pH (pH 11.0), the protein attains a deformed structure far from its native secondary and tertiary structure. Through the multispectroscopic study, we demonstrated here the interactions between ZnO NPs and bovine β-lg and how the alkali-unfolded bovine β-lg at pH 11.0 refolds to its native-like structure in the presence of ZnO NPs at the same pH. To the best of our knowledge, this is possibly the first report of refolding of alkali-induced denatured β-lg by ZnO NPs acting as an artificial nanochaperone.

Materials and Methods

Reagents and Chemicals

Sodium dihydrogen phosphate, zinc acetate, potassium hydroxide (KOH), and methanol were purchased from Merck (Mumbai, India). Acrylamide, bis­(acrylamide), N,N,N,N-tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED), ammonium persulfate (APS), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), bromophenol blue, glycerol, and coomassie brilliant blue were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Fluorescent probes 8-anilinonaphthalene-1-sulfonic acid (ANS) and Thioflavin T (Th T) were purchased from Sigma Chemical Co. (St. Louis, USA) and used without further purification. Other chemicals were of the highest purity available. All buffer solutions were filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter (Millipore, USA).

Isolation and Purification of Bovine Beta-Lactoglobulin (β-lg)

Bovine β-lg was isolated from cow milk following the method of Aschaffenburg and Drewry. The product was lyophilized and stored at 4 °C. For spectroscopic preparations, β-lg was weighed and dissolved in 5 mM Na-phosphate buffer, pH 7.4. Protein stock solutions were made using the same buffer. The pH was adjusted to 11.0 by the addition of small amount of dilute NaOH. The solutions’ pH was measured at room temperature with a digital pH meter. Using the extinction coefficient of β-lg (0.96 mg-1 mL–1 cm–1 at 280 nm), different concentrations of protein solutions were prepared by dissolving β-lg in Milli-Q water and measuring the O.D. at 280 nm.

Preparation of Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles (ZnO NPs)

Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) were synthesized in accordance with a previously established methodology. The glassware employed in this preparation underwent a thorough cleaning process using aqua regia, followed by rinsing with Milli-Q water and then drying in an oven. The objectives of this study were to synthesize and characterize ZnO NPs utilizing zinc acetate, potassium hydroxide (KOH), and methanol as chemical reagents.The synthesis procedure involved dissolving of 0.8977 g of KOH into 100 mL of methanol, which was then heated at 60 °C to obtain a homogeneous mixture. A solution of 2.107 g of zinc acetate dihydrate in 60 mL of deionized water was prepared, and the Zn-solution was added to the KOH solution and stirred vigorously at 60 °C for 90 min to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting solution was divided into three parts.

The initial portion of the synthesis served as a reference for evaluating the properties of the ZnO nanoparticles. The second and third portions underwent washing using a modified Meulekamp method, utilizing methanol as the dispersing agent, followed by centrifugation to eliminate the byproducts. This washing and redispersion process were conducted three times to obtain pure ZnO nanoparticles. To characterize the final product, the solvent was gradually removed while heating to 60 °C until a white, dry powder of ZnO nanoparticles was formed. A comprehensive analysis was performed using powder X-ray diffraction with a D8 Discover diffractometer to identify the crystal phases. Average crystalline sizes were estimated using Scherrer’s formula, and the purity of the nanoparticles was confirmed through washing and redispersion method thrice.

UV–Visible Characterization

The UV–visible spectroscopic characterization of ZnO NPs was conducted at room temperature. Optical absorption spectra from 200 to 600 nm were recorded with and without the β-lg sample using a Shimadzu-TCC 240 A UV–vis spectrophotometer with a spectral resolution of 2 nm. The ZnO NPs showed a characteristic signal at 365 nm, confirming their formation with a diameter of 30 ± 2 nm, further validated by XRD study. Additionally, the absorption band of ZnO NPs shifted notably in the presence of β-lg. The size was also confirmed through SEM and TEM studies.

Electrophoretic Analysis

Sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed under nonreducing conditions using a 15% acrylamide resolving gel according to Laemmli’s method. Samples of β-lg solution (2.5 mg mL-1) in 5 mM sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4 and pH 11.0, with and without ZnO NPs were electrophoresed separately under nonreducing conditions. The resulting solutions were filtered with a syringe filter with a 0.2 μm membrane filter. Aliquots of 20 μL β-lg solutions at pH 7.4 and pH 11.0 in the absence and presence and of ZnO NPs were loaded in the wells. Similarly, 30 μL of native β-lg solution (2.5 mg mL–1) was loaded in another well. In a separate lane molecular weight marker, specifically the PageRuler Prestained Protein Ladder (Thermo Scientific, Product #26616), with known weights of 10, 17, 25, 35, 40, 55, 70, 100, 130, and 180 kDa, run in parallel with the samples.

Electrophoretic separations were done by applying a maximum voltage of 100 V for 1 h. The gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue R-250 and destained in the usual way using a solution containing methanol and acetic acid.

UV–Visible Spectroscopic Study

In this study, a UV–visible JASCO spectrophotometer (Model no V-730, Serial no B184461798) equipped with JASCO Spectra Manager Software was utilized to acquire absorption spectra for monitoring the UV–visible spectral characteristics of the protein in both the absence and presence of ZnO nanoparticles at room temperature (25 °C). Alterations in the microenvironments surrounding the chromophores of the protein during its interaction with the nanoparticles are expected to result in changes to the UV–vis spectra of the protein. To facilitate this investigation, two PerkinElmer quartz cells with a path length of 1 cm were employed for reference and sample measurements. The intensity versus wavelength spectra for absorbance were recorded over a wavelength range of 200–600 nm. For reference purposes, a 5 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.4 was utilized. The concentrations of the β-lg sample solutions were maintained at 13 μM. The native β-lg displays an absorption peak at 280 nm, attributed to the presence of a tryptophan (Trp) chromophore. The UV–visible spectral patterns of native β-lg at pH 7.4, as well as the alkaline unfolded β-lg, were documented in the presence and absence of ZnO nanoparticles within the wavelength range of 220–400 nm.

Surface Area and Porosity Analysis (BET Isotherm)

The specific surface area is a critical parameter for characterizing porous materials, including nanoparticles, as it greatly affects their physical, chemical, and catalytic properties. Among the various analytical techniques, the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method is widely recognized as the standard approach for determining surface area, relying on nitrogen adsorption and desorption isotherms. Nitrogen gas adsorption and desorption measurements were conducted to ascertain the specific surface area of the samples, utilizing a Quantachrome Autosorb iQ2 surface area and porosity analyzer. Prior to the measurements, the samples were subjected to degassing under vacuum at 120 °C for a duration of 4 h to eliminate any adsorbed moisture and gases. The adsorption and desorption isotherms were subsequently recorded at liquid nitrogen temperature (77 K), employing high-purity nitrogen gas as the adsorbate.

1-Anilinonaphthalene-8-sulfonic Acid (ANS)-Fluorescence to Monitor the Hydrophobicity Changes of β-lg Samples

1-Anilinonaphthalene-8-sulfonic acid (ANS) is utilized to assess modifications in the surface hydrophobicity of proteins. The present study examined the structural transitions of β-lactoglobulin (β-lg) at an alkaline pH of 11.0, as well as the subsequent refolding process facilitated by the gradual incorporation of Zinc Oxide Nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), monitored through ANS fluorescence. A stock solution of ANS (1 mg mL–1) was prepared using Milli-Q water, and a 30 μM solution was added to each sample to ensure a 50 molar excess relative to the protein concentration. ANS fluorescence measurements were conducted with a Horiba fluorometer (Model: Fluoromax-4C), activating the samples at an excitation wavelength of 385 nm within a quartz cell of 1 cm path length. The emission and excitation slit widths were maintained at 5 nm. ANS fluorescence emission spectra were recorded in the range of 395 to 600 nm, with each spectrum subjected to blank correction. The data points presented reflect the average of triplicate measurements.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) Measurements

Small particles in solution undergo diffusion, which significantly influences the intensity of scattered light and results in observable fluctuations. Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) is employed to detect these fluctuations using an autocorrelator on a microsecond time scale, thereby facilitating the analysis of molecular distribution. Measurements were conducted with Zinc Oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) utilizing a Zetasizer Nano (Malvern Instruments, UK) to ascertain the average particle size at 25 °C and a pH of 7.4. The hydrodynamic radii of both native and partially unfolded β-lactoglobulin (β-lg) at pH 11.0 were determined separately. Furthermore, the hydrodynamic radius of refolded β-lg was measured by the subsequent addition of ZnO NPs solutions, which were illuminated with a 633 nm laser.

For the analysis, a 2-ml transparent rectangular quartz cuvette with a path length of 10 mm was employed, containing sample solutions of β-lg at a concentration of 20 μM, prepared at room temperature. The time-dependent autocorrelation function was obtained through 12 acquisitions for each run, with the reported data representing the mean of three consecutive analyses.

High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR TEM) imaging was performed utilizing a JEOL HRTEM-2011 (Tokyo, Japan) to analyze native β-lactoglobulin (β-lg) at pH 7.4, its denatured structure at pH 11.0, and the refolded β-lg following the incorporation of 25 μM ZnO nanoparticles. The samples underwent centrifugation, dilution, and were subsequently drop-cast onto carbon-coated copper grids. After a duration of 20 s, the droplets were carefully removed using filter paper, and the grids were stained with a 2% uranyl acetate solution. Finally, the grids were air-dried overnight in preparation for TEM imaging at a magnification of 12-30000.

Circular Dichroism (CD) Spectroscopy

To investigate the secondary structural changes induced by the addition of alkali to bovine β-lactoglobulin (β-lg) and the subsequent formation of its refolded structure through the addition of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), circular dichroism (CD) measurements were performed using a Jasco spectropolarimeter (J-815) at a controlled temperature of 20 °C. These measurements were conducted in the far-UV region (200–260 nm) utilizing rectangular cuvettes with path lengths of 1 mm and 10 mm. For all native and alkali-unfolded β-lg solutions, both in the presence and absence of ZnO NPs, the protein concentration was consistently maintained at 0.25 mg/mL for the far-UV CD analyses.

In the context of partially unfolded β-lg and those that were gradually refolded through the influence of ZnO NPs at elevated pH levels, the transitions from α-helical to random coil structures were systematically assessed. The results were expressed as mean residual ellipticity (MRE) in degrees cm2 dmol– 1, calculated according to the formula: MRE = θobs (mdeg)/(10 × n × C p × l), where θobs denotes the observed ellipticity in millidegrees, n represents the number of amino acid residues in a single subunit (162), l signifies the path length of the cuvette in centimeters, and C p indicates the molar concentration of proteins. All spectra were derived as averages of three scans, and the final spectrum resulted from subtracting the corresponding solvent spectrum. The far-UV CD curves were analyzed using the curve-fitting program CDNN 2.1 to determine the percentages of secondary structures present in β-lg under diverse experimental conditions.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM)

The physical characteristics, including the morphology and size of the synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs), were examined using Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) with a Hitachi S-4800 apparatus from Japan, operated at a voltage of 20 kV. Each solution was diluted with ethanol to form a film on carbon tape, which was subsequently placed under vacuum for evaporation. Finally, the dried samples were coated with gold prior to FE-SEM analysis.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) Measurements

The Amide I band (1600–1700 cm– 1) and Amide II band (1480–1570 cm– 1) serve as important indicators in FT-IR spectra for identifying the structure of β-lactoglobulin (β-lg) as it transitions between its native, unfolded, and refolded states, particularly in an alkaline environment (pH 11.0) influenced by zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs). In our study, we utilized a Spectrum 100 FT-IR spectrometer (PerkinElmer) to measure the infrared spectra of β-lg samples at room temperature (25 °C) with a resolution of 2 cm– 1. Each sample contained a concentration of 500 μM β-lg, and we recorded data within the range of 1500–1700 cm–1.

Results

UV–Vis Characterization

The optical characteristics of the ZnO NPs were investigated by UV–vis spectroscopy at room temperature, as depicted in Figure . The result revealed a pronounced absorption feature of the nanoparticles in the ultraviolet region, spanning from 200 to 400 nm. Remarkably, the absorption peak centered at around 365 nm, confirming the formation of ZnO NPs with a diameter of 30 ± 2 nm, as the characteristic signal of ZnO NPs was observed. This observation is consistent with the previous studies on the optical properties of ZnO NPs, emphasizing the importance of studying their characteristics for various applications in the field of nanotechnology. The UV–vis spectrum of the same sample solution remains unaltered even after 48h, indicating the stability of the colloidal ZnO NPs. Further, the size and shape of the synthesized ZnO NPs were confirmed by SEM and TEM studies.

Powder XRD Study

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern obtained from the sample indicates the presence of ZnO nanoparticles possessing well-defined crystalline characteristics. The XRD spectra displayed prominent diffraction peaks at 31, 34, 36, 47, 56, 62, 66, 67, and 68 degrees of 2θ. These peaks correspond to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), and (201) crystal planes, respectively (Figure ). The diffraction pattern matched well with the JCPDS file, indicating a hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO with a space group of P6_3mc. The lattice parameters were found to be a = b = 3.249 Å and c = 5.206 Å. From the powder XRD, the particle size of the ZnO NPs was found to be 30 ± 2 nm (from JCPDS file).

As shown in Figure , a detailed examination of these SEM images reveals a diverse array of nanoparticle formations, showing their unique and distinct morphologies. Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) Analysis

To emphasize the significance of surface area and porosity in the characterization of ZnO nanoparticles (NPs), particularly concerning their potential role as chaperones, the BET analysis was performed. These properties play a crucial role in determining how NPs interact with proteins, thereby influencing their binding capacity, stability, and overall biological activity. The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis is a method utilized to quantify the specific surface area of a material through nitrogen adsorption and desorption isotherms (Figure ). This technique provides valuable insights into material properties and is widely applied in various scientific and industrial fields. The analysis provides insights into the surface area, pore volume, and pore size characteristics of the nanochaperone. The N2 gas adsorption–desorption isotherms of ZnO NPs, observed across a P/P0 range from 0 to 1 (Figure a), indicate that at lower relative pressure, the amount of N2 adsorbed remains minimal, but it gradually increases as the pressure rises. This trend confirms the formation of a type IV isotherm with an H3-type hysteresis loop. The ZnO NPs exhibit a surface area of 24.14 m2/g, a pore volume of 0.036 cc/g, and a pore size of 27.86 Å (2.786 nm).

(a) Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) adsorption and desorption isotherm of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) and (b) pore size distribution graph of ZnO NPs in BET adsorption and desorption isotherm.

Protein Interaction Study by UV–Vis Spectroscopy

β-lg is known to demonstrate its distinct UV–vis spectra, which can be attributed to the presence of chromophores having an aromatic nucleus conjugated with groups of varying electronic effects. The native β-lg at pH 7.4 exhibits a characteristic band for proteins having tryptophan residues, showing a λmax at 280 nm, as depicted in Figure (curve black). Any change in the UV–vis spectrum of the native β-lg may indicate changes in its microenvironment surrounding the tryptophan chromophores, as reported in the literature. The findings also imply that the microenvironment of the chromophores of β-lg at pH 11.0 has been changed due to partial unfolding during the structural transition of β-lg in the alkaline medium and showing different UV–vis spectra (curve-red) of decreased intensity. The spectra of this partially unfolded β-lg complexed with ZnO NPs of varying amounts (25–75 μM) slowly regain the native-like β-lg structure. The ratio of protein–ZnO NPs used in this study was 1:2, 1:4, and 1:6. This clearly indicates the gradual stabilization of the unfolded β-lg structure by ZnO NPs, showing the chaperone-like activity of ZnO NPs. The resultant structural restoration of the protein is manifested in the UV–vis absorption spectra of β-lg.

Analysis of Secondary Structures by Circular Dichroism (CD)

The secondary structural aspects of the native unfolded and refolded β-lg were obtained by estimating the different secondary structural contents and monitoring the conformational transitions from far-UV CD spectra. The CD spectra of native β-lg at pH 7.4, unfolded and refolded β-lg samples at pH 11 in the absence and presence of varying amounts of ZnO NPs were recorded at room temperature and shown in Figure . The native β-lg at pH 7.4 presents a negative MRE in its far UV-CD spectrum (190–260 nm) in the wavelength region 215 nm-216 nm, indicating the prevalence of beta-sheet rich structure in β-lg (curve chocolate brown). In an alkaline medium, at pH 11, β-lg shows a changed CD spectrum with a much lower negative ellipticity in the wavelength region 206–207 nm and a blue shift of the signal (dark green curve). This large change in MRE value can be correlated with the structural transitions due to the unfolding of the protein in an alkaline medium. Secondary structural contents calculations indicate a much increase in beta-sheet and random structures with a decrease in alpha-helices structures (Table ). Due to partial unfolding at higher pH, the buried Cys121 residue of β-lg opens up and encourages the formation of disulfide-linked β-lg dimer, rich in β-sheet structure. The CD spectrum of the unfolded β-lg remains unaltered when the protein is allowed to refold on its own capacity at room temperature without the addition of any ZnO NPs. However, the CD signals begin to change and go back to the closer of the native β-lg signal during the gradual addition of ZnO NPs at pH 11.0. Gradual additions of ZnO NPs return the β-lg structure closer to that of the native protein (change from olive, blue, and cyan curves), as evident from the CD spectra (Figure ). Far-UV CD spectra revealed that the refolded β-lg has native-like secondary structures. The orange curve (having minimum ellipticity) represents the CD spectrum of ZnO NP only. Thus, the ZnO NP stabilizes the molten globule state of β-lg and acts as a nanochaperone in the refolding of alkaline unfolded β-lg.

Tertiary Structure Analysis via Near-UV CD

The near-UV CD spectrum of native β-lg, typically spanning wavelengths from 250 to 350 nm, offers valuable insights into the protein’s tertiary structure and the environment of its aromatic amino acids. This spectrum features prominent peaks, particularly at 293 nm (tryptophan), 285 nm (tyrosine), and 269 nm (phenylalanine), which indicates tertiary structure native β-lg. These peaks are sensitive to factors such as temperature and ligand binding, which can disrupt the protein’s tertiary structure. The tertiary structure of β-lg was evaluated using near-UV CD spectroscopy.

Native β-lg displayed characteristic near-UV CD signals, indicating a well-folded tertiary structure (Figure S1, see Supporting Information). However, exposure to alkaline pH = 11 resulted in a significant change in ellipticity, suggesting partial unfolding and disruption of the native tertiary structure. Incubation with ZnO NPs led to a partial recovery of the near-UV CD signals, indicating that these nanoparticles may aid in refolding or stabilizing the protein under denaturing conditions. These findings suggest that ZnO nanoparticles may interact with the protein in a manner that promotes the restoration of its tertiary structure, potentially through mechanisms that mimic the native folding environment.

Monitoring the Changes of Secondary Structures by Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

The analysis of the amide I band (1600–1700 cm–1) in the FTIR spectra provides valuable information about the secondary structures of proteins. In this study, the FTIR spectra of β-lg samples (native, at pH 7.4, and partially unfolded, at pH 11.0 in the absence and presence of ZnO NPs) in the D2O buffer are shown in Figure . As a beta-sheet-rich protein, native β-lg at pH 7.4 exhibited a characteristic peak (curve black) approximately at 1633 cm–1 in its amide I region (mainly due to CO str.), confirming the presence of intramolecular beta-sheet structures in native β-lg. On the other hand, the partially unfolded β-lg shows an abrupt change in FTIR spectra (curve red, Figure ) where the amide 1 band has been shifted approximately to 1638 cm–1 with a significant decrease in transmittance value due to secondary structural transitions at the elevated pH in the alkaline medium. Gradual additions of ZnO NPs to the unfolded β-lg solutions at pH 11.0 restore the amide I band closer to the native protein by the gradual increment of the transmittance values and the decrease of the CO str. frequency. Therefore, ZnO NPs conjugated β-lg displayed a strong tendency to refold the protein in situ. The ZnO NP stabilizes the unfolded protein by reducing its negative charge and acts as a molecular nanochaperone in the refolding process to reach the molten globule of β-lg. Thus, FTIR findings corroborate the earlier CD results of the protein in conjugation with ZnO NPs and also reveal the phenomenon of assisted protein folding.

SDS- PAGE Data Analysis

The SDS-PAGE technique was utilized to explore the intricate electrophoretic patterns of native β-lg at physiological pH 7.4, as well as β-lg samples subjected to an alkaline environment at pH 11.0, both in the absence and presence of zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs). In lanes 2 and 3, a distinct single band emerged under nonreducing conditions at pH 7.4, signifying the presence of native β-lg in its monomeric form. Notably, the data revealed a significant inhibition in the formation of oligomeric structures when ZnO NPs were present, highlighting the nanoparticles’ efficacy in stabilizing the unfolded β-lg structure through weak electrostatic interactions. This stabilization suggests that ZnO NPs function as molecular nanochaperones, facilitating the refolding of β-lg into a productive molten globule state.

Discussion

The knowledge of nanoparticle–protein interaction is important for the development of nanomedicine since the nanoparticles have the capacity to enter the biological system to work as delivery, diagnostic, and imaging tools for different illnesses. In the present study, we have investigated the influence of ZnO NPs on the unfolded β-in at alkaline pH. The de novo refolding of protein from the unfolded state to its native or native-like form is of utmost interest. When proteins are synthesized in vivo, the molecular chaperones are the key players in the proper folding and stabilization of the nascent polypeptide chain. Therefore, it is very necessary to design and synthesize artificial chaperones that can assist or promote the refolding of a denatured protein in vitro in order to prevent misfolding or the aggregation of that protein.

Multifunctional synthetic porous nanochaperones and hydrophobic microdomains surrounded by hydrophilic PEG chains have been found to promote the refolding of peptides and proteins. , To assist in the refolding of alkali-denatured bovine β-lg in vitro, we have synthesized and employed ZnO NPs. Because of their size, these nanoparticles provide a larger surface to the distressed client protein molecules. At pH 11.0, the protein beta-lactoglobulin transitions into a partially unfolded state due to its conformational changes. The uniformly distributed hydroxyl ion charge in the solution at pH 11 contributes to the unfolding of the protein in the absence of nanoparticles. When ZnO nanoparticles are introduced into this solution, the chemisorbed protons (H+) release from the particle surfaces, resulting in a negatively charged surface that contains partially bound oxygen atoms (ZnO–). This process causes the charge to concentrate on the surface of the ZnO nanoparticles, leading to anisotropic charge distribution in the solution. Consequently, as the concentration of nanoparticles increases, the charge density in the solvent diminishes due to the accumulation on the ZnO NPs surface. This reduction in charge density in the presence of ZnO nanoparticles enhances the refolding of the unfolded β-lg at high pH.

The SDS-PAGE experiment demonstrates unambiguously that incubation of β-lg in the alkaline medium at pH 11.0 led to the formation of oligomeric species (like trimer, tetramer, etc.) and higher aggregates (lane 4, Figure ) under nonreducing conditions. The formation of disulfide bonds by Cys121 of monomeric β-lg in an alkaline medium facilitates the formation of oligomeric structures. The hydrophobic interactions encourage the formation of different oligomers in the absence of ZnO NPs. The SDS-PAGE data analysis (Lanes 5, 6, and Lane 7, Figure ) also showed an inhibition in the formation of oligomeric structures in the presence of ZnO NPs, indicating the effectiveness of the nanoparticles in the stabilization of the unfolded protein by weak electrostatic interactions and acts as molecular nanochaperone in the refolding process of β-lg.

Thus, ZnO NPs displayed the ability to refold the β-lg molecule in a concentration-dependent manner, as evidenced by the gradual increase in ANS-fluorescence intensities and finally reaching the value close to that of native β-lg. The larger surface area of NPs assisted in the refolding of the protein.

The alkaline unfolded β-lg (pH 11.0) shows a changed CD spectrum with a large decrease in the ellipticity value and secondary structural contents compared to native β-lg. The CD spectrum of the unfolded β-lg remains unaltered when the protein is allowed to refold on its own capacity at room temperature without the addition of any ZnO. However, the CD signals begin to change and return to the closer of the native β-lg signal during the gradual addition of ZnO NPs at pH 11.0. Far-UV CD spectra also supported that the refolded β-lg has native-like secondary structures.

DLS measurements also indicate that the treatment of the unfolded β-lg with ZnO NPs at pH 11.0 led to a significant decrease in the hydrodynamic radii of β-lg. This reduction in hydrodynamic radii of the protein is due to the stabilization of the unfolded β-lg on the surface of the ZnO NPs. ZnO NPs thus assist the refolding of the alkali-denatured β-lg and thus act as molecular nanochaperones in this study.

Conclusions

The present work involves the synthesis and characterization of ZnO NPs using analytical techniques such as UV spectroscopy, XRD, and FESEM. Through our investigations, we have discovered that these nanoparticles possess a unique chaperone-like activity, which allows the alkali-unfolded β-lg to refold and then restore the protein structure. To the best of our knowledge, this is possibly the first report of refolding of alkali-denatured β-lg by ZnO NPs acting as artificial nanochaperone. One of the most fascinating aspects of our findings is that ZnO NPs retain their chaperone-like activity even at very high pH levels, making them an ideal candidate for use in various industrial and biomedical applications. The results of our study suggest that ZnO NPs hold great potential in the field of protein renaturation and stabilization, and their applications can lead to the development of new and improved therapeutic agents.
