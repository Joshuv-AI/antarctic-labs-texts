Introduction

The Ising model is a popular model, used in physics but also in computational science, econophysics, sociophysics and psychosociophysics [6, 25, 28, 29, 42]. The model is relatively simple, yet exhibits intriguing phase transitions, both first- and second order. To obtain such qualitative descriptions, we explore mean-field theory of a generalisation of the Ising model that extends beyond the spin values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document}-1 and 1 to include, for any \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\in \mathbb {N}$$\end{document}k∈N, the spin values generated by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$(-k+j)/k$$\end{document}(-k+j)/k, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$j=0,1,2,\ldots ,2k$$\end{document}j=0,1,2,…,2k. For example, with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k=1$$\end{document}k=1, we get \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\{-1,0,1\}$$\end{document}{-1,0,1}. This model is called the general-spin Ising model and has been introduced before in Rabe and Mardia [34], where the interest was mostly in estimating parameters, and even earlier in Suzuki [39], which defined an Ising model for multiple states in general and derived the correlation functions.

On the face of it, the general-spin Ising model seems related to the continuous XY model and a discretisation of the Heisenberg model. However, the general-spin Ising model shows very different behaviour than both the standard Heisenberg and XY model; the general-spin Ising model has a first-order phase transition while the standard Heisenberg and XY model do not, even in the mean-field limit [3, 27]. The Blume-Capel model, which includes the value 0 and has an additional term for the energy of non-zeros (crystal field), has been investigated as a general-spin model [10, 32, 36]. The interest there was mostly in the effect of the crystal field term on magnetisation. Variations of the Ising model, such as the Potts model, are similar to the general-spin Ising model in that they involve multiple states; however, they only contribute to the energy when neighbouring states are equal. [17]; here any product of fractional values can contribute to the energy. The general-spin Ising model is different from the above models because it can be defined on the integers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}$$\end{document}Z, and here we normalise the values by the maximum k, so that all values are between -1 and 1.

Applications of the general-spin Ising model in physics mostly involve systems with quantized spin values [10] and mixed spin cases to model different magnetic materials [2, 15, 16]. For example, in Ertaş et al. [19] a two-layer square lattice is used, where nodes within each layer represent one of the materials in thin film, and interactions within and between layers are modelled according to the Ising model with spin values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 2$$\end{document}±2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 1$$\end{document}±1, 0. However, our inspiration comes from the use of statistical physics models in sociology and psychology [41]. In sociophysics, for example, agents’ opinions on social networks (e.g., Facebook, Instagram) may span a range of ordered values, such as from extreme left to extreme right, including a neutral position [e.g. 9]. Similarly, in mathematical psychology, the Ising model is applied to phenomena such as attitudes [13] or major depression disorder [11], where node values can also vary along a spectrum, from negative to positive, again, including a neutral position. In both last two contexts, nodes are often measured by questionnaires, the most common of which are Likert scales. A typical Likert scale item asks respondents to rate their position on a 7-point scale ranging from ’strongly disagree’ to ’strongly agree’ [24]. Currently, visual analogue scale responses (sliders) are becoming popular [5, 23] which can also be covered by the general-spin Ising model with large k.

The Ising model is relevant to social sciences because it captures the interactions by the product of the variables. Known as the law of mass action [18], it can be found in research on human interactions, such as in opinion dynamics, e.g., the voter model [35, 38] and in epidemiology, e.g. in susceptible-infected-susceptible models [26], but also in research on intelligence [37], attitudes [14], and psychopathology [12, 40, 45].

Our interest is qualitative descriptions, and mean-field theory is appropriate for that purpose. Mean-field theory is correct in the thermodynamic limit (infinite graph size), but is still quite accurate when the mean degree of nearest neighbours is sufficiently high [21]. In physics this is often true, since the typical graph topology in physics is the d-dimensional lattice \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {Z}^d$$\end{document}Zd [e.g. 1, 20, 22]. Also in more modern applications of (variations of) the Ising model, versions of the d-dimensional lattice are used [19]. In social science applications, graph topologies often resemble a small world, and in neuroscience the topology often resembles a scale-free graph [4, 38], although sometimes the degree distribution resembles a Poisson distribution [9], suggesting an Erdös-Renyí random graph. In applications to psychopathology, the graph topology appears to be sparse and cannot be distinguished from an Erdös-Renyí random graph [8, 40].

After introducing the model and some notation in Sect. 2, we consider the mean field equations in Sect. 3. Then, in Section 4 we show that spontaneous magnetisation depends on an additional factor containing the number of categories \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2k+1$$\end{document}2k+1. In Sect. 5 we show with Monte Carlo simulations that the results of the Metropolis algorithm for the general-spin Ising model correspond well to the theoretical predictions, including dependence on system size and the degree of nodes.

Conclusion and discussion

The general-spin Ising model, a model with multiple valued spins, symmetric around zero between \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$-1$$\end{document}-1 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$+1$$\end{document}+1, is important not only as a theoretical framework, but also because of its applications–ranging from S-spin models in physics to polarisation dynamics in sociology and attitude modelling in psychology. In such applications, it is often valuable to understand the types of states and dynamics the model can exhibit, to assess its suitability for a given modelling context. Mean-field theory offers insights into these properties.

We obtained the Gibbs free energy for the mean field and minimised this to obtain the mean field solution. The properties of the general-spin mean field are similar to those of the regular Ising model, i.e. the emergence of spontaneous magnetisation via a second-order transition at low temperatures, and hysteresis. However, spontaneous magnetisation occurs at a shifted value compared to the regular Ising model, determined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r=(2k+1)/6k$$\end{document}r=(2k+1)/6k, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$2k+1$$\end{document}2k+1 denotes the number of categories of the general-spin Ising model. The phase diagram showed that temperatures need to be lower to obtain spontaneous magnetisation in the general-spin Ising model than for the regular Ising model. Additionally, the hysteresis effect is slightly different depending on the number of categories, but converges when taking the limit of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k\rightarrow \infty $$\end{document}k→∞. Monte Carlo simulations confirm the theoretical predictions derived from the mean field, and indicate the dependence on the system size and node degree.

Using these results allows for the application of the general-spin Ising model in different fields. In psychology, for example, this model is applicable in contexts where responses are measured using a visual analogue scale, typically implemented as a slider with around 100 response categories. These types of responses could then be modelled with a general-spin Ising model with many categories. Experiments should reveal whether the predictions from mean-field theory accurately reflect empirical data.
