Abscisic acid strongly promotes anthocyanin accumulation in grape cell suspensions (Vitis vinifera L. cv. ‘Gamay Fréaux’) independently of glucose concentration
Authors: Qian Tong, Junhua Kong, Xiaobo Xu, Yongjian Wang, Eric Gomès, Lina Wang, Péter Teszlák, Antonio Santiago, Gastón Alfredo Pizzio, Zhenchang Liang, José Tomás Matus, Zhanwu Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFruit metabolism is highly sensitive to environmental variations, with climate change disrupting processes that determine fruit composition and quality. In grapes, elevated temperatures increase sugar accumulation but reduce anthocyanin levels as co-occurring responses under heat stress, leading to imbalances that affect wine typicality. To explore sugar signaling and its interaction with abscisic acid (ABA) in regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis, we conducted a comprehensive transcriptomic and phenotypic analysis using a “Gamay Fréaux” (GT) cell suspension model. Treatments included ABA combined with varying glucose concentrations, glucose analogs, and hexokinase inhibitors.ResultsHigh glucose levels increased anthocyanin concentration by 13.2% compared to low glucose levels, whereas exogenous ABA induced far stronger increases of 108.4% and 94.4% under low- and high-glucose conditions, respectively, relative to ABA-free controls. Additionally, mannose contributed to sugar signaling, with sugar-induced anthocyanin accumulation dependent on hexokinase-mediated phosphorylation. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that while ABA strongly upregulated anthocyanin biosynthetic genes, the effect of glucose on these genes was modest. Notably, 3OM treatment induced upregulation of several structural genes but resulted in reduced anthocyanin accumulation, suggesting regulation primarily through post-transcriptional mechanisms and additional factors influencing post-translational control. By developing an open-source GT-transcriptome analysis tool and integrating it with anthocyanin quantification, we identified VviCIPK25 as a candidate gene whose expression correlates with anthocyanin accumulation in response ABA, and which may respond indirectly to sugar-induced phosphorylation status.ConclusionsThis study provides a comprehensive transcriptomic resource for understanding anthocyanin regulation in grape cell suspensions and identifies VviCIPK25 as a candidate gene for future functional studies. Our findings also highlight the importance of post-transcriptional regulation in anthocyanin biosynthesis, as transcriptional changes do not always predict phenotypic outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09539-x.

Introduction

The enological potential of grape berries depends largely on the balance of primary and secondary metabolites, particularly soluble sugars, organic acids, anthocyanins, and aromatic hydrocarbons [1]. Among these, soluble sugars and anthocyanins are key determinants of the organoleptic quality of grapes and wine [2, 3]. Berries with optimal sugar content and high anthocyanin concentrations are generally preferred for premium wine production [2]. However, global warming has been linked to excessive sugar accumulation and reduced anthocyanin content [4–6], creating an imbalance that threatens the sustainability of the wine industry [7, 8]. It is important to note that these are parallel responses to heat stress rather than evidence of a direct inhibitory effect of sugars on anthocyanin biosynthesis. Elucidating the mechanisms governing sugar–anthocyanin balance in grape berries is therefore critical for preserving fruit composition and wine quality.

Grape berry-derived cell suspensions have provided a simplified and controllable system that can be used to extrapolate and study the molecular processes in the fruit, such as sugar signaling and anthocyanin biosynthesis. The Vitis vinifera L. ‘Gamay Fréaux’ var. Teinturier (GT) cell suspension system has been extensively used as a model for studying secondary metabolism regulation in grapevine. Early studies developed a mathematical model linking cell growth, sucrose uptake, and anthocyanin production in GT cells, establishing that primary and secondary metabolism compete for precursors in a substrate concentration-dependent manner [9]. Subsequent work showed that sugars directly regulate polyphenol production in GT cells, with different sugars exerting distinct effects on anthocyanin accumulation [10]. More recently, the ABA-activated transcription factor VviNAC17 was shown to be a positive regulator of secondary metabolism in GT cells; its constitutive overexpression significantly upregulated phenylpropanoid and flavonoid pathway genes and enhanced anthocyanin accumulation [11]. Collectively, these studies validate GT cell suspensions as a robust platform for dissecting the regulatory mechanisms underlying anthocyanin biosynthesis, providing a strong foundation for the present investigation of ABA-sugar crosstalk.

In fact, while using GT cell suspensions, anthocyanin accumulation was markedly higher in media with high sucrose concentration (50 g L− 1) compared to those with low sucrose concentration (20 g L− 1) [12]. Similarly, anthocyanin synthesis in grape berry cultures is induced when the sucrose content exceeds 2% [13, 14]. Furthermore, exogenous sugars at sufficiently high concentrations promote anthocyanin synthesis in diverse tissues, including strawberry fruits [15], lily petals [16], petunia flowers [17], and radish hypocotyls [18]. At the whole-plant level, manipulating the source-to-sink ratiod anthocyanins, as exogenous ABA promotes anthocyanin accumulation under both low- and high-carbon conditions [19, 20]. However, the effect and underlying mechanisms of ABA–sugar crosstalk in regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis in grape cells remain elusive.

Previous studies have established that sugars regulate plant growth and development through pathways dependent on sugar metabolism [21, 22]. Later, research showed that even non-metabolizable sugar analogs can influence plant growth, development, and gene expression, indicating that sugars also act as signaling molecules independently of their metabolic roles [23, 24]. For instance, glucose analogs, such as mannose and 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), both phosphorylated by hexokinase (HXK) but not further metabolized, act as sugar signals that induce anthocyanin biosynthesis in intact grape berries and grape cell suspension cultures [25, 26]. In contrast, glucose analogs, such as 6-deoxyglucose (6-DG) and 3-O-methylglucose (3OM), which cannot be phosphorylated by HXK, do not promote anthocyanin accumulation in these systems [25, 26]. Similarly, HXK inhibitors, such as glucosamine hydrochloride (DGH), N-acetyl-glucosamine, or mannoheptulose, suppress anthocyanin accumulation in grape berries, even under high-sugar conditions [25–27]. Overall, these findings suggest that sugar signaling depends on the ability of sugars to be phosphorylated by HXK rather than on the phosphorylated products themselves [28]. Moreover, downstream metabolic processes following hexose phosphorylation appear unnecessary for sugar signaling. Despite this growing understanding, transcriptome remodeling in response to varying sugar concentrations, sugar analog, and HXK inhibitors, particularly in the context of ABA interaction, remains largely unexplored in grapevine.

A variety of sugar signaling components have been identified in plants, with HXK being the first recognized sugar sensor [29, 30]. The discovery that HXK phosphorylates hexoses as a key step in sugar signal sensing marked a major advance in understanding sugar perception and signaling in higher plants [31, 32]. Beyond hexose phosphorylation, HXK also exhibits protein kinase activity, allowing it to phosphorylate other proteins, thereby influencing their stability and potentially modulating anthocyanin accumulation [33]. In grapevine, an ASR (ABA-, stress-, and ripening-induced) protein, VviMSA, has also been shown to bind strongly to two sugar-responsive elements in the promoter of the sugar- and ABA-inducible hexose transporter gene VviHT1 [34, 35]. Similarly, overexpression of an ASR gene promoted strawberry fruit coloring [36]. Trehalose-6-phosphate (T6P) has also emerged as a key signaling molecule that promotes anthocyanin accumulation [37]. In kiwifruit, elevated carbon supply increased T6P content, which in turn enhanced anthocyanin accumulation by repressing MYB27, an inhibitory transcription factor via its SRE2 promoter element [38]. Additionally, sucrose non-fermenting related kinase 1 (SnRK1) has been identified in several plant species as a key sugar signaling regulator that modulates multiple aspects of plant growth and development [39]. For instance, SnRK1 has been shown to repress anthocyanin biosynthesis under carbon- and energy-depleting stress conditions by both transcriptionally downregulating MYB transcription factors and directly phosphorylating and dissociating the MBW complex in Arabidopsis [40]. In contrast to SnRK1, members of the SnRK2 subfamily are primarily involved in ABA signaling. For example, in strawberry, FaSnRK2.6 negatively regulates anthocyanin biosynthesis by phosphorylating the anthocyanin regulator FabHLH3, thereby suppressing FaUFGT expression [41]. While numerous studies have reported the roles of these signaling factors in modulating anthocyanin accumulation, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. In particular, it is still unclear whether additional key components participate in mediating the crosstalk between sugar and ABA signaling during the regulation of anthocyanin biosynthesis.

In this study, we used GT cell suspensions, which naturally accumulate anthocyanins, and subjected them to varying glucose concentrations in combination with exogenous ABA, glucose analogs, and HXK inhibitors [42]. By analyzing anthocyanin accumulation and whole-genome transcriptomic profiles at two culture stages (3 and 8 days), we employed this cell suspension system as a model to elucidate the molecular mechanisms of sugar signaling and its crosstalk with ABA in regulating anthocyanin biosynthesis, leading to the identification of key candidate genes involved in this regulatory network.

Materials and methods

Cell culture, treatments, and sampling

Cell suspension cultures of grape (Vitis vinifera L. cv. ‘Gamay Fréaux’ var. Teinturier hereafter referred as GT) were established following Decendit and Merillon [43] and maintained in modified Murashige and Skoog (MS) liquid medium as described by Kong et al. [44]. Cultures were grown in 250 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of MS liquid medium and sub-cultured every 10 days by transferring 10 mL of suspension into fresh medium. All cultures were maintained at 25 ± 1 °C under continuous fluorescent light (20 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) on an orbital shaker at 120 rpm.

The MS basal medium (without sugar) was supplemented with 250 mg L− 1 N-Z-Amine A (C7290, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), 0.5 mg L− 1 α-naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), 0.1 mg L− 1 kinetin, and a vitamin mix comprising 100 mg L− 1 myo-inositol, 1.0 mg L− 1 nicotinic acid, 1.0 mg L− 1 pantothenic acid, 0.01 mg L− 1 biotin, 1.0 mg L− 1 pyridoxine HCl, and 1.0 mg L− 1 thiamine HCl. It was observed that the most effective condition for GT cells was MS liquid medium containing 2% sucrose (0.058 M) [43, 44]. However, a later study showed that glucose or fructose in MS liquid medium could be even more effective than sucrose [14]; therefore, glucose was used here as the primary sugar. The experiments included eight treatments, comprising different glucose concentrations combined with or without exogenous ABA, two glucose analogs, and an HXK inhibitor. Specifically, 3OM is a non-metabolizable and non-phosphorylatable glucose analog [26], whereas mannose is non-metabolizable but phosphorylatable [25]. Furthermore, mannose (0.02–0.1 M) stimulated anthocyanin accumulation in grape cells in a concentration-dependent manner [31]. Therefore, we treated cells with mannose at the same concentration as glucose (both 0.029 M) to assess whether glucose analogs can elicit sugar signaling effects. Similarly, we used the same concentration for 3OM treatment (0.029 M 3OM plus 0.029 M glucose). The HXK inhibitor DGH reduces the enzyme’s hexose-phosphorylating activity, and at higher concentrations, also inhibits its protein kinase function, with a maximum inhibition of 55% observed at 0.1 M [27]. Therefore, we selected 0.1 M DGH for our experiments. To eliminate the confounding effect of osmotic potential differences between the high and low glucose treatments, mannitol was added to the low glucose medium. Specifically, 0.029 M mannitol was added to 0.029 M glucose to achieve a total solute concentration equivalent to that of 0.058 M glucose (the high glucose condition). Mannitol was chosen because it is a non-metabolizable osmotic agent that does not interfere with sugar signaling or anthocyanin metabolism in GT cells [45].

Glucose was used as the primary sugar in this study, with the normal concentration set at 0.058 M. A lower glucose concentration (0.029 M) was also prepared, corresponding to half of the standard level (0.058 M). Further, based on previous studies demonstrating effective induction of anthocyanin biosynthesis in grape cell suspensions [34, 46], we evaluated the effects of 10 µΜ ABA in combination with different glucose concentrations on anthocyanin biosynthesis. This concentration has been widely used in grape cell culture systems as it consistently activates ripening-related gene expression and anthocyanin accumulation without causing growth inhibition or stress artifacts. In summary, the eight treatments, prepared by supplementing the basic MS liquid medium (MS medium, M0221, Duchefa, Haarlem, The Netherlands) with the previously mentioned sugar and hormone combinations are as follows: I, 0.058 M glucose (high glucose); II, 0.029 M glucose with 0.029 M mannitol (low glucose); III, 0.029 M glucose (low glucose without mannitol); IV, 0.058 M glucose with 10 µΜ ABA ; V, 0.029 M glucose with 10 µΜ ABA; VI, 0.029 M glucose with 0.029 M 3OM; VII, 0.029 M glucose with 0.029 M mannose; and VIII, 0.058 M glucose with 0.1 M DGH.

All treatments were applied on the eighth day of standard cell culture. Each treatment included nine flasks, organized into three replicates of three flasks each. Cells were sampled at 3 and 8 days after treatment (DAT) using vacuum filtration. A portion (20%) of the cells was weighed for fresh weight and then dried at 65 °C until constant for dry weight determination. The remaining GT cells were quickly washed twice with sterile de-ionized water, vacuum-filtered, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen. Frozen cells were ground into a fine powder and stored at − 80 ℃ for analysis of sugar concentration, anthocyanin accumulation, and RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq). Additionally, 5 mL samples of culture medium were collected at each sampling point and stored at − 20 °C for sugar analysis.

Analysis of soluble sugars and anthocyanins

For soluble sugar analysis (sucrose, glucose, and fructose), 500 mg aliquots of cell powder were freeze-dried for 48 h and then extracted as described by Tong et al. [20]. The extracts and culture media were filtered through a 0.22 µΜ filter and analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) following the procedures of Tong et al. [20]. For anthocyanin analysis, 200 mg aliquots of cell powder were freeze-dried, extracted, and measured using HPLC as described by Hibert et al. [47] and Tong et al. [20].

Transcriptomic analysis using RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from GT cell suspensions using plant total RNA isolation kit (Biomarker Technologies, Beijing, China). Library construction and transcriptome sequencing were performed by Biomarker Technologies (https://www.biomarker.com.cn). Raw count data were preprocessed to remove low-quality sequences and adapters using Trimmomatic v.0.39 [48], yielding clean reads. Clean sequences were mapped to the grape reference genome PN40024 T2T.v5 [49] using STAR v.2.7.9a [50]. Raw counts were calculated using Feature Counts [51], based on T2T v5.1 annotation, available at Grapedia (https://grapedia.org/). Expression levels were quantified as TPM (transcripts per kilobase million) using RSEM v.1.3.1 (https://github.com/deweylab/RSEM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 R package v.1.34.0 [52] with cutoffs of an adjusted P-value (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05 and fold change > 2. The FDR was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. Gene annotations were obtained from the Grape Gene Catalogue [53] and supplemented via the AnnotationHub R package v.3.2.0 (https://bioconductor.org/packages/release/bioc/html/AnnotationHub.html). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed using TBtools v.1.098685 [54] and AgriGo v.2.0 [55].

Implementation of the ABA/Sugar-Cell gene expression atlas

A gene expression atlas explorer was developed to visualize the transcriptome dataset generated in this study. Results from the differential expression analysis (DEA) were integrated into an app on VitViz suite within PlantaeViz platform [56]. The app features a heatmap in which cell color represents log₂ fold change (logFC) for each experimental comparison, and dot size corresponds to the − log(values).

Data analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R software [57]. For comparisons involving more than two treatments, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For comparisons between two treatments, Student’s t-test was applied. A significance level of P < 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. All experiments were performed with three biological replicates, and data are presented as means ± standard error (SE).

Results

Effects of different glucose concentrations and exogenous ABA on anthocyanin accumulation in GT cells

Glucose was used as the primary sugar in this study, as previous studies showed that optimal concentrations of sucrose, glucose, and fructose promote anthocyanin accumulation in grape berries, with glucose and fructose being more effective than sucrose [14]. To investigate the effect of glucose concentration on anthocyanin accumulation in GT cells, we compared media containing 0.029 M and 0.058 M glucose, both adjusted to equal osmolarity with mannitol. Additionally, exogenous ABA was applied to examine its potential crosstalk with glucose. Cell growth and anthocyanin accumulation were differentially influenced by glucose concentration and ABA treatment at 3 and 8 DAT (Fig. 1).

Higher glucose concentrations did not significantly affect GT cell dry weight at either time point (Fig. 1B). In contrast, high glucose increased anthocyanin accumulation, showing an 13.2% increase at 8 DAT compared with low glucose, although no difference was observed at 3 DAT (Fig. 1C). ABA treatment markedly promoted anthocyanin accumulation under both glucose conditions, with increases of 173.6% (3 DAT) and 94.4% (8 DAT) under high glucose and 160.8% (3 DAT) and 108.4% (8 DAT) under low glucose (Fig. 1A, C). While exogenous ABA had no effect on cell growth at 3 DAT, it significantly inhibited growth by 8 DAT, reducing dry weight by 20.8% under high glucose and 28.5% under low glucose (Fig. 1B).

Soluble sugar concentration analysis in GT cells and culture medium under different treatments (Supplementary Fig. S1) showed that glucose was the predominant sugar in cells, followed by fructose, whereas sucrose was detected at the lowest levels (Supplementary Fig. S1A). ABA treatment significantly increased glucose + fructose levels at 3 DAT, by 26.1% under high glucose and 30.3% under low glucose, compared with untreated cells. However, by 8 DAT, intracellular glucose + fructose concentrations showed no significant differences across glucose or ABA treatments (Supplementary Fig. S1A). In the culture medium, glucose levels consistently exceeded fructose levels, whereas sucrose was undetectable (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Notably, at 8 DAT, glucose was completely depleted in the low-glucose treatment, whereas in the high-glucose treatment, glucose + fructose levels remained 467.2% higher than in the low-glucose treatment (Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Effects of glucose analogs on GT cell suspension cultures

To determine whether glucose regulates anthocyanin glucoside accumulation through metabolism or signaling, we applied two glucose analogs with distinct physiological properties: 3OM, a non-metabolizable and non-phosphorylatable analog used to assess glucose effects independent of metabolism [26], and mannose, a non-metabolizable but phosphorylatable analog used to evaluate the role of sugar signaling independent of metabolism [25].

The effects of glucose analogs on GT cell growth and anthocyanin accumulation are shown in Fig. 2. At 3 DAT, neither 3OM nor mannose significantly affected cell growth. By 8 DAT, however, 3OM significantly inhibited growth by 34.9%, whereas mannose-treated cells showed no significant difference compared with the high-glucose condition (Fig. 2B). Regarding anthocyanin accumulation, 3OM-treated cells exhibited significant reductions of 31.2% at 3 DAT and 54.0% at 8 DAT, with levels even lower than those under the 0.029 M glucose treatment. Trypan blue staining confirmed that the reduction in anthocyanin levels by 3OM was not attributable to cell death (Supplementary Fig. S2). In contrast, mannose-treated cells maintained anthocyanin levels comparable to high-glucose conditions (Fig. 2A, C). Intracellular sugar analysis revealed that GT cells contained predominantly glucose, followed by smaller amounts of fructose, with sucrose present at the lowest concentration (Supplementary Fig. S3A). In 3OM-treated cells, hexose concentrations were reduced by 50.8% at 3 DAT and 59.8% at 8 DAT, whereas mannose treatment had no significant effect (Supplementary Fig. S3A). At 3 DAT, the 3OM-treated medium retained a high hexose concentration (6.1 mg mL− 1), whereas the mannose-treated medium showed the lowest level (3.5 mg mL− 1). By 8 DAT, no residual hexose remained in the mannose-treated medium, and only fructose was detected in the 0.029 M glucose-treated medium. In contrast, the 3OM treatment retained 2.8 mg mL− 1 hexose in the medium (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Collectively, these results indicate that sugars play a role in signaling pathways that promote anthocyanin accumulation.

Effects of HXK inhibition on GT cell suspensions

To determine whether glucose-mediated sugar signaling depends on phosphorylation, we evaluated the effects of the HXK inhibitor DGH on anthocyanin production in GT cell suspensions. DGH treatment significantly affected both cell growth and anthocyanin accumulation (Fig. 3). Anthocyanin levels were reduced by 35.2% at 8 DAT, with no significant effect at 3 DAT (Fig. 3A, C). In contrast, cell growth was strongly inhibited, showing reductions of 40.6% at 3 DAT and 63.9% at 8 DAT (Fig. 3B). These results indicate that DGH not only suppressed cell proliferation but also reduced anthocyanin accumulation in GT cells.

DGH treatment significantly altered soluble sugar concentrations in both cells and culture medium (Supplementary Fig. S4). In DGH-treated cells, glucose was the predominant sugar (45.2 mg g− 1 dry weight (DW) at 3 DAT and 29.5 mg g− 1 DW at 8 DAT), followed by lower amounts of fructose (3.5 mg g− 1 DW at 3 DAT and 4.0 mg g− 1 DW at 8 DAT) and minimal sucrose (1.3 mg g− 1 DW at 3 DAT and 1.1 mg g− 1 DW at 8 DAT; Supplementary Fig. S4A). Compared with high-glucose (0.058 M glucose) conditions, hexose concentrations in DGH-treated cells were reduced by 67.2% at 3 DAT and 78.0% at 8 DAT (Supplementary Fig. S4A). Conversely, hexose concentrations in the culture medium were higher under DGH treatment than under high-glucose conditions, suggesting soluble sugars largely remained in the medium rather than being efficiently taken up or metabolized by the cells (Supplementary Fig. S4B).

Modulation of genes involved in anthocyanin synthesis and ABA- and sugar-mediated responses

To investigate how glucose concentration, ABA, and sugar signaling influence anthocyanin accumulation in GT cell suspensions, we performed RNA-seq on 60 cDNA libraries (three biological replicates) representing the eight treatments, each sampled at two time points. On average, 23.0 million reads per sample were obtained, with mapping rates of 82.5%–90.3% against the T2T PN40024 reference genome (v5.1 annotation). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed clear separation among treatments and between the two time points (Fig. 4A, B, showing the two different conducted experiments), while tight clustering of replicates confirmed dataset reliability for DEA. The numbers of DEGs varied across comparisons (Fig. 4C; Table 1), with generally higher number of downregulated genes in comparison to up-regulated. Under high-glucose treatment, independent of osmotic effects (Table 1, c1), 41 genes were upregulated and 466 downregulated at 3 DAT, whereas 13 were upregulated and 282 downregulated at 8 DAT (Fig. 4B). By contrast, high glucose combined with osmotic potential (Table 1, c2) yielded significantly fewer DEGs—only 5 downregulated at 3 DAT, whereas 84 upregulated with 157 downregulated at 8 DAT. Osmotic potential alone (Table 1, c3) induced more genes than the combined glucose–osmotic treatment at both time points.

Exogenous ABA combined with either low or high glucose (Table 1, c4 and c5) induced a greater number of DEGs at both time points compared with treatments without ABA (Table 1, c1, c2, and c3), indicating that ABA exerts a stronger influence on anthocyanin-related gene expression than glucose alone. Mannose and 3OM, two glucose analogs with distinct physiological properties, were included to further dissect glucose functions. While glucose contributes to both sugar signaling and sugar metabolism, the 3OM treatment (Table 1, c7) was specifically used to test whether glucose effects on anthocyanin accumulation depend primarily on signaling rather than metabolism. Conversely, mannose (Table 1, c8) was used to verify whether glucose effects on anthocyanin accumulation are independent of sugar signaling. Mannose treatment (Table 1, c8) resulted in 105 upregulated and 4 downregulated DEGs at 3 DAT but showed no differential expression at 8 DAT. In contrast, 3OM induced a greater number of DEGs at both time points compared to mannose. Furthermore, DGH (Table 1, c9) was applied to assess whether inhibition of HXK activity in glucose-containing MS medium suppresses anthocyanin accumulation. DGH elicited a strong transcriptional response, with 493 upregulated and 1021 downregulated DEGs at 3 DAT, whereas 738 upregulated and 921 downregulated DEGs at 8 DAT.

Discussion

High glucose concentration promotes anthocyanin accumulation via upregulation of structural genes in the biosynthetic pathway

The GT red cell suspension culture, which produces anthocyanins under light, is a widely used model for studying factors influencing anthocyanin biosynthesis [58]. Previous studies have also shown that osmotic potential influences anthocyanin accumulation in suspension cells [59]. To eliminate the confounding effect of osmotic stress, mannitol was added to the low-glucose medium, adjusting its osmotic potential to 0.058 M.

Glucose functions not only as a primary energy substrate but also as a versatile signaling molecule and carbon donor that supports plant cell growth. In culture medium, it promotes cell proliferation through three main mechanisms: (i) Energy supply, wherein glucose-derived ATP, generated via glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, fuels immediate energy demands for cell growth [60]; (ii) Signal transduction, wherein glucose acts as a signaling molecule that activates cell growth pathways, with the TOR kinase pathway integrating glucose status with light and hormonal cues to drive cell cycle progression and meristematic activity [61, 62]; and (iii) Biosynthetic precursors, where glucose metabolism provides carbon skeletons required for the synthesis of macromolecules essential for cell growth and development [63].

The observation that low glucose (0.029 M) reduced anthocyanin accumulation without affecting cell growth (Fig. 1B, C) is important for interpreting our results. This uncoupling of growth and secondary metabolism indicates that glucose specifically regulates anthocyanin biosynthesis rather than merely serving as a general nutrient limiting cell proliferation. If low glucose had also inhibited growth, it would have been difficult to distinguish whether the reduced anthocyanin accumulation was due to a direct effect on anthocyanin biosynthesis or an indirect consequence of reduced biomass production.

High glucose concentration markedly enhances anthocyanin biosynthesis and accumulation in GT cells, and that exogenous ABA further amplifies this effect under both high- and low-glucose conditions (Fig. 1). It should be noted that the modest effect of high glucose (13.2% increase) reached statistical significance in Fig. 1 (5-group comparison) but not in Fig. 2 (4-group comparison), likely reflecting both the weak effect size and differences in multiple comparison correction stringency. These findings align with previous observations in in vitro grape berry cultures [14, 26, 46], and with studies on fruiting cuttings of cv. ‘Cabernet Sauvignon’ subjected to varying carbon supply and ABA treatments [20]. In that study, a low source-to-sink ratio (two leaves) suppressed anthocyanin synthesis and significantly downregulated most structural and regulatory genes involved in the anthocyanin biosynthetic pathway.

Variations in grape anthocyanin content are typically accompanied by changes in the transcription of related structural genes and transcription factors. In GT cell suspensions, although high glucose increased anthocyanin levels, only a few anthocyanin-related structural genes (Vvi5GT and VviUFGT1) showed modest transcript upregulation (Fig. 4). This discrepancy may reflect regulation at the protein level rather than the transcriptome or fundamental differences between experimental systems. Several factors inherent to the GT cell suspension system may explain the limited transcriptional responses observed in our study. First, GT cells are maintained under continuous light, which constitutively activates anthocyanin biosynthetic genes and elevates their basal expression levels, thereby reducing the dynamic range for further transcriptional induction. Second, as a rapidly proliferating homogeneous cell population, GT cells continuously produce anthocyanins, and even under low-glucose conditions, some cells retain red coloration, indicating that anthocyanin biosynthesis is not fully inhibited [64]. This partial inhibition likely explains why transcript levels of anthocyanin-related genes showed only modest reductions under low glucose. Third, the constitutive expression in red cell lines such as GT may reflect permanent pathway activation, possibly due to epigenetic or genetic changes acquired during prolonged subculture [65]. Finally, unlike intact berries with differentiated tissues and complex inter-organ signaling, the homogeneous cell system lacks tissue-specific regulation and transport processes, which likely contributes to the stronger phenotypic effects of ABA observed in cell suspensions. Therefore, while the GT cell system is valuable for dissecting specific mechanisms, our findings, particularly the limited transcriptional responses, should be interpreted within the context of these system-specific limitations, and key conclusions require validation in more physiologically relevant systems such as in vitro berry cultures [14].

An apparent inconsistency was observed between the transcriptomic and phenotypic responses to 3OM treatment. While several anthocyanin biosynthetic genes were upregulated at the transcript level (Fig. 5B, c7), anthocyanin accumulation was significantly reduced (Fig. 2C). Even though structural genes are transcriptionally activated (possibly as a stress response or feedback mechanism), the lack of metabolic substrates (e.g., UDP-glucose for UFGT) and energy supply prevents anthocyanin accumulation. These observations highlight the importance of integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic data, as transcriptional changes alone do not always predict phenotypic outcomes.

In additional, ABA treatment induced a transient upregulation of VviMYBA1 and VviMYBA2 at 3 DAT, which returned to baseline by 8 DAT (Fig. 5B, c4, c5). This temporal pattern suggests that ABA triggers an early transcriptional activation of these master regulators, which then initiates downstream structural gene expression (e.g., VviUFGT1, VviGST4) and subsequent anthocyanin accumulation. The decline of MYBA transcript levels at 8 DAT, despite sustained high anthocyanin levels, may reflect feedback regulation or post-transcriptional mechanisms that maintain MYBA protein activity without requiring continuous high transcript abundance.

Sugar signaling-induced anthocyanin biosynthesis depends on hexose phosphorylation by HXK

Sugars act not only as energy sources but also as signaling molecules regulating plant growth and development [66, 67]. A key step in this pathway is the phosphorylation of hexoses by HXK, which enables them to participate in signal transduction [31, 68]. To elucidate the role of HXK in sugar signaling and differentiate metabolic from signaling effects, we examined the impact of glucose analogs with distinct biochemical properties on anthocyanin synthesis in GT cell suspensions.

Mannose, a hexose phosphorylatable by HXK, promoted anthocyanin accumulation, whereas 3OM, a non-phosphorylatable analog, had no such effect (Fig. 2). These results are consistent with previous findings in grape cells [25], grape berries [26], and other species such as strawberries [15]. Moreover, 29 mM 3OM was non-toxic to GT cells, in line with reports that 3OM is non-toxic at concentrations up to 100 mM in Arabidopsis and tomato cell cultures containing 52 mM sucrose or 166 mM glucose, respectively [69]. Taken together, these findings support the hypothesis that only sugars capable of hexose phosphorylation can serve as signaling molecules to induce anthocyanin accumulation.

Treatment with the HXK inhibitor DGH significantly suppressed both cell growth and anthocyanin accumulation. Soluble sugar levels in the culture medium were higher under DGH treatment than in controls, indicating impaired sugar uptake or metabolism. This effect likely reflects HXK inhibition, which disrupts both sugar utilization and signaling. Consistent with this, downregulated DEGs under DGH treatment were enriched for phosphorylation-related functions, indicating broader interference with phosphorylation-dependent signaling. Together, these findings reinforce the notion that hexose phosphorylation by HXK is essential for sugars to function as signaling molecules promoting anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Although different sugars had distinct effects on anthocyanin accumulation, with some significant changes in transcript levels of anthocyanin biosynthesis-related genes following 3OM or mannose treatment, the expressions of sugar signaling-related genes (VviTOR and VviSnRK1; Supplementary Fig. S8) remained largely unchanged across treatments. Several non-mutually exclusive mechanisms may explain this phenomenon. Firstly, sugar signaling may not operate primarily through transcriptional regulation of these genes, emphasizing the need to examine changes in phosphorylation status or post-translational modifications in GT cell suspensions under different treatments [70]. For instance, hexokinase (HXK) functions as a glucose sensor whose activity is modulated by protein-protein interactions and phosphorylation status rather than changes in transcript abundance [31, 71]. Similarly, SnRK1 activity is regulated by phosphorylation and allosteric modulation, and TOR kinase activity is controlled by post-translational mechanisms [60]. Second, the sugar signal may be transduced through metabolic intermediates (e.g., trehalose-6-phosphate, glucose-6-phosphate) that modulate enzyme activities directly without requiring transcriptional reprogramming. Third, post-transcriptional regulation, including mRNA stability, translation efficiency, protein degradation, and reversible phosphorylation of enzymes (e.g., PAL, UFGT), may play a dominant role in mediating sugar effects on anthocyanin accumulation. To directly test this hypothesis, future studies should measure the enzymatic activities of key anthocyanin biosynthetic enzymes, such as PAL and UFGT, under ABA and sugar treatments. Activity assays for these enzymes are well-established in grape and would provide direct evidence for post-translational regulation. Additionally, protein phosphorylation status or ubiquitination levels of these enzymes could be examined to identify specific post-translational modifications responsible for altered enzyme activity without corresponding changes in transcript abundance. Furthermore, given the physiological differences between GT cell suspension cultures and grape berries, it is essential to validate the role of sugar signaling in anthocyanin accumulation using in vitro berry culture systems. Such studies would provide a more comprehensive mechanistic understanding of how sugar signaling modulates anthocyanin biosynthesis, as previously suggested [14].

HXK inhibition by DGH enhances the ABA response, as evidenced by increased expression of VviPP2Cs and decreased expression of VviPYLs (Fig. 6), suggesting that HXK may act as a repressor of the ABA signaling pathway. Moreover, ABA is known to inhibit cell proliferation by repressing TOR activity through nuclear export of SnRK1 [72], implying that ABA-mediated growth suppression may operate via this mechanism. Although no significant transcriptional changes were observed for VviTOR, VviSnRK1a or VviSnRK1b under ABA treatment (Supplementary Fig. S8), potential alterations at the protein activity level cannot be excluded. Notably, Merino et al. [73] report that p-coumaric acid acts as an ABA antagonist by directly binding to PYL receptors, strongly supporting our hypothesis and suggesting a potential link between phenylpropanoid metabolism and ABA signaling.

The ABA associated marker, VviCIPK25, respond indirectly to sugar induction in grape

Among the six common candidate genes identified in both GT cell suspension and grapevine fruit cuttings, VviCIPK25 (Fig. 9), showed a strong positive correlation with total anthocyanin concentration (Fig. 9A; r = 0.68, P < 0.000001). In grape berries from fruit cuttings, VviCIPK25 transcript levels were downregulated under carbon limitation but upregulated by exogenous ABA application [20]. In GT cell suspensions, VviCIPK25 expression did not significantly differ between high- and low-glucose treatments; however, exogenous ABA markedly induced its expression under both conditions. Although mannose and DGH treatments modulated its expression, its lack of response to 3OM treatment does not support a direct role in sugar signaling (Fig. 9B). We therefore propose that VviCIPK25 functions primarily in ABA-mediated anthocyanin biosynthesis.

These findings show that VviCIPK25 functions primarily in ABA-mediated anthocyanin biosynthesis, with possible indirect crosstalk with sugar signaling pathways. Supporting this notion, the Arabidopsis cipk14 mutant exhibits reduced glucose sensitivity affecting seed germination [74], whereas MdCIPK22 enhances ABA-induced phosphorylation of MdAREB2 under abiotic stress in apple [75]. CIPK genes have also been shown to modulate ABA signaling in grape [76] and rice [77], contributing to stress tolerance. Interestingly, AtCIPK14 interacts with the ABA phosphatase ABI1 [78], suggesting a direct role in ABA-mediated signaling. Whether VviCIPK25 functions similarly in grape, particularly in the context of anthocyanin accumulation, remains to be determined. We acknowledge that the correlative nature of our data does not establish a regulatory function for VviCIPK25. Future studies using genetic approaches (e.g., overexpression, CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, or transient transformation) are needed to confirm its role in anthocyanin biosynthesis.

Limitation of the current study

Despite the insights described above, several aspects of this study warrant further investigation. Firstly, our findings indicate that exogenous ABA strongly enhances anthocyanin accumulation under both low- and high-glucose conditions in GT cells. However, because all ABA treatments were conducted in the presence of sugar (required for cell viability), we cannot completely exclude the possibility that ABA’s effect may be partially dependent on or influenced by the sugar background. Whether ABA can promote anthocyanin accumulation independently of sugar remains to be tested in other experimental systems. Second, the GT cell suspension system involves active cell proliferation, where carbon is partitioned between growth and anthocyanin production, which may confound the interpretation of sugar-specific effects. Nevertheless, the key finding that ABA promotes anthocyanin accumulation regardless of carbon supply has been validated in grape fruit cuttings [20]. The findings obtained with glucose analogs and the HXK inhibitor should be further validated in in vitro grape berry cultures to confirm the role of hexose phosphorylation in sugar signaling independently of carbon partitioning.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides a comprehensive transcriptomic analysis of anthocyanin regulation in grape cell suspensions under ABA and sugar-related treatments. High glucose concentrations promoted anthocyanin accumulation in GT cell suspensions, and ABA application further enhanced pigment levels under low-glucose conditions, compensating for reduced sugar availability. Sugars function as signaling molecules to induce anthocyanin biosynthesis, as evidenced by reduced anthocyanin levels under treatment with the HXK inhibitor DGH, highlighting the dependence of this process on hexose phosphorylation. Despite these physiological effects, transcript levels of key sugar signaling-related genes remained largely unchanged, suggesting that regulation occurs predominantly at the post-translational level and additional factors influencing post-translational control. Among the candidate genes, VviCIPK25 may serve as an ABA associated marker associated with anthocyanin accumulation.

We acknowledge that the correlative nature of our data does not establish a regulatory function for VviCIPK25, and future studies using genetic approaches (e.g., overexpression, CRISPR-Cas9 knockout, or transient transformation) are needed to confirm its role in anthocyanin biosynthesis. Similarly, since all ABA treatments were performed in the presence of sugar, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that ABA’s effect may be partially dependent on the sugar background. Validation in non-dividing systems such as in vitro grape berry culture assays would further strengthen the conclusions.
