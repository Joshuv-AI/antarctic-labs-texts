Insolation-driven northern Atlantic westerly wind patterns shaped the mid-Pleistocene transition in three phases
Authors: Xiaowen Quan, María F. Sánchez Goñi, Qiuzhen Yin, Stefanie Kaboth-Bahr, Paul Moal-Darrigade, Josue M. Polanco-Martinez, Zhipeng Wu, André Bahr, Emmanuelle Ducassou, Lucas J. Lourens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Mid-Pleistocene Transition ( ~ 1200–650 ka) marked a shift to 100-kyr glacial cycles, despite no significant change in insolation cyclicity. In this study, a high-resolution pollen record from the southwestern Iberian margin, combined with climate modeling, reveals a three-phase climate evolution linked to boreal summer insolation amplitude. In the early phase ( ~ 1200–930 ka), strong insolation minima shifted westerlies north, boosting ice accumulation, while maxima promoted melting, limiting ice growth. The middle phase ( ~ 930–790 ka) followed regoliths loss and featured weaker insolation extremes, leading to the southward migration of the westerlies, mega-droughts and greater ice buildup. In the late phase ( ~ 790–680 ka), stronger minima pushed westerlies north, aligning with longer, colder glacials. These findings suggest that changes in insolation amplitude drove shifts in westerly wind patterns, influencing hydroclimate and ice sheet development, and may have contributed to the transition to 100-kyr glacial cycles.

Introduction

The Mid-Pleistocene Transition (MPT, ~1200–650 ka) represents a fundamental shift in Quaternary climate, marking the transition from dominant 41-kyr glacial-interglacial cycles to more prolonged 100-kyr cycles, despite no change in the dominant insolation periodicities1,2. While the 41-kyr and 21-kyr astronomical cycles persisted, the emergence of 100-kyr ice age cycles suggests the involvement of additional mechanisms beyond orbital forcing1. Multiple hypotheses have been proposed to explain this shift, including ice-sheet dynamics, such as ice-albedo feedbacks3, north-westerly driven precipitation at the ice margins4 and the combined effect of CO2 and insolation5,6. However, the primary forcing behind this transition remains actively debated7. Additionally, the nature of the transition itself, whether gradual or abrupt, is still uncertain8. A recent study observes a weak variation in both total and mean seasonal insolation around 800 ka due to a simultaneous weakening of the variation of precession and obliquity, and proposes this change in the astronomical forcing as a potential candidate to explain the MPT9. This hypothesis and the associated mechanisms, in particular, changes in precipitation at the ice margins, remain however to be confirmed by proxy reconstructions and model simulations.

Here, we aim to reconstruct the evolution of precipitation in the mid-latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere across the MPT, and test the role of insolation amplitude changes in driving the climate reorganization. We first present a high-resolution and chronologically robust composite pollen record from the southwestern Iberian margin, IODP Sites U1386 (36°49.685’ N, 7°45.321’ W, 561 m depth) and U1385 (37°34.285’ N, 10°7.562’ W, 2578 m depth), directly compared with benthic foraminifera δ¹⁸O (δ18Ob) records from the same sites (Figs. S1-S2, Tab. S1, 'Methods'). The pollen assemblages reflect shifts in Mediterranean forest amount10 linked to winter precipitation and westerly wind dynamics11. Secondly, we combined these records with other paleoclimate datasets and LOVECLIM model simulations. We propose that changes in amplitude of insolation significantly influenced the position of the westerlies, a primary moisture source for Northern Hemisphere mid-to-high latitudes, and ice ablation, both potentially serving as key drivers of ice volume changes during the MPT.

Results

Southwestern Iberian vegetation and westerlies variability during the MPT

In the U1385 δ¹⁸Ob record, glacial-interglacial transitions were defined using the 3.8 ‰ threshold proposed by Hodell et al.12. The similar shape of the U1386 δ¹⁸Ob record allows us to define the transitions in the latter. The pollen record is a composite of Site U1385 (Marine Isotope Stage (MIS) 31 and MIS 19–16) and Site U1386 (MIS 36–32 and MIS 30–20) (Supplementary Figs. S3-S4, 'Methods'). Changes in the pollen percentage curves (Fig. S4a-c) do not parallel the fluctuations in the main pollen and total sporo-pollen concentrations, which oscillate from ~778 to 560,000 grains cm⁻³ (Supplementary Fig. S3d-e), indicating that the former reflect real shifts in forest amount and composition rather than changes in pollen production, transport and preservation (Supplementary Fig. S3, 'Methods'). We interpret changes in pollen assemblage (Supplementary Fig. S3, 'Methods') in terms of vegetation changes based on the modern relationship between pollen assemblages and the vegetation of the Tagus Basin10. Mediterranean forest pollen percentages below 20% indicate a landscape dominated by herbaceous formations such as grasslands or semi-deserts, whereas percentages above 40% reflect a Mediterranean forest–dominated landscape. Intermediate values (20–40%) correspond to open Mediterranean forest or woodland conditions. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) of the pollen data indicates that two variables explain 62.2% of the pollen record variability (Supplementary Fig. S5, 'Methods'). The scree plot and broken-stick model suggest that both axes capture significant environmental variability rather than random noise (Supplementary Fig. S6). Based on the present-day climatic requirements of the pollen assemblages identified by the PCA13, we infer that winter precipitation and summer temperature are the primary factors influencing vegetation changes through time (Supplementary Fig. S4, 'Methods'). This interpretation is further supported by the loading analysis (Supplementary Fig. S7), which shows that variability along Axis 1 is primarily associated with the contrast between Mediterranean forest (e.g., Quercus) and semi-desert taxa (e.g., Artemisia). Previous studies have demonstrated that expansions of Mediterranean forest are robust indicators of enhanced winter precipitation11, driven by variations in the intensity and latitudinal position of the north-westerly winds under the influence of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO)11. This interpretation is further supported by transient climate experiments with time-varying insolation and CO2, which show that interglacial forest dynamics are largely coupled to changes in winter precipitation14 and, consequently, to the position and strength of the westerlies.

Among the interglacial periods (Fig. 1), MIS 35 was a prolonged interglacial lasting ~50 kyr12, marked by the expansion of the Mediterranean forest, with forest pollen reaching ~23%, and minimal semi-desert vegetation. This suggests a warm and humid regional climate, further supported by the presence of Platycarya (see source data), a tropical-subtropical tree found in present-day southeastern Asian mountain forests15,16. In contrast, MIS 33 was short ( ~ 13 kyr) and featured by semi-desert expansion, reflecting limited regional warming and moisture availability. MIS 31 exhibited stronger Mediterranean forest expansion than MIS 35, aligning with lighter δ18Ob values. The forest during MIS 31 persisted ~35 kyr and peak at around 1080 ka. MIS 29 showed low Ericaceae percentages and high Taraxacum-type abundance (Supplementary Fig. S2), suggesting relatively low precipitation and cooler conditions. The Mediterranean forest showed a long-term decline from MIS 31 to MIS 20, partially deviating from the typical glacial (non-forested)–interglacial (forested) pattern. During MIS 25 and MIS 21, limited forest amount and Ericaceae expansion (Supplementary Fig. S2) suggest relatively high winter precipitation and cold conditions in southwestern Iberia. Pollen from Castanea, a temperate and summer drought intolerant tree17, was recorded at ~951 ka during MIS 25 (see source data). Weaker interglacials, such as MIS 23, showed minimal expansion of forest ( ~ 14% forest pollen) and heathlands, coeval with increased semi-desert, indicating a cold and dry regional interglacial. MIS 19 and MIS 17, in contrast, were longer and warmer interglacials, with forest pollen reaching 78% during MIS 17. During glacial periods, the Mediterranean forest contracted, while cold-/drought-adapted vegetation expanded, particularly during MIS 24, MIS 22, and MIS 20. The relative high abundance of Cupressaceae (Supplementary Fig. S3), a drought-resistant taxon in Europe18, further reflects harsh climate conditions during these intervals. Interestingly, MIS 28, characterized by high Taraxacum-type prevalence (Supplementary Fig. S3), exhibited interglacial-like characteristics, with greater forest amount than the preceding interglacial (MIS 29). Change-point detection approach highlights the emergence of a long-term three-phase climate pattern in the Mediterranean forest pollen percentages throughout the MPT (Supplementary Figs. S8-12, 'Methods'), associated with shifts in their dominant spectral signature (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. S13, 'Methods'). The PCA results, together with 95% confidence ellipses and cluster centroids, support the presence of a three-phase pattern in the pollen record (Supplementary Fig. S5). Samples from the middle phase MIS 24-20, concentrated on the left side (negative value) of Axis 1 associated with dry winters, while the early phase MIS 35-25 is mainly located on the lower value of the Axis 2 related to cold summers. The late phase MIS 19-17 clusters on the positive side of Axis 1 indicating wet winters (Supplementary Fig. S5). In the early phase ( ~ 1200–930 ka), Mediterranean forest pollen percentages averaged ~22%, reflecting intermediate forest expansion under the moderate influence of the westerlies. This was followed by a marked decline to an average of 15% during the middle phase ( ~ 930–790 ka), coinciding with substantial semi-desert expansion, suggesting a southward migration of the westerlies that reduced moisture supply to southwestern Iberia. Winter precipitation and westerly wind activity exhibited a dominant 100-kyr spectral signature, during 1200–1050 ka, though it may not be reliable as it falls outside the cone of influence (COI) (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. S13, 'Methods'). This cyclicity may represent a proto-100-kyr behavior rather than a fully established eccentricity cycle. Similar transient ∼100-kyr components have been reported in global δ¹⁸O stacks and regional paleoclimate records during 1.2–1.0 Ma, reflecting a non-linear response of large Northern Hemisphere ice sheets to the 41-kyr obliquity forcing1,19. From about 870 ka onward, a significant ~80-kyr cycle developed and continued through the end of the MPT. This periodicity likely reflects either double obliquity forcing or nonlinear interference between precession and obliquity cycles20. This signature feature weakened and became muted between 1050–870 ka, preceding both the onset and cessation of prolonged extreme drought events (930–790 ka). In the late phase, MIS 19–17 ( ~ 790–680 ka), forest amount rebounded to 30% on average, peaking during MIS 17, indicating the optimum position of the westerlies bringing winter precipitation and warmth to the southwestern Iberia21. The Mediterranean forest record exhibits significant ~40-kyr and ~20-kyr cyclicities at the end of MPT, along with millennial-scale variability at 5–8 kyrs22.

Discussion

Linking climate changes in Europe to North Atlantic Ocean dynamics

Thermal gradients between the sea surface temperature (SST) in the central North Atlantic at 41°N (IODP Site U1313) and the sub-surface temperatures in the southwestern Iberian margin at 37°N (IODP Site U1385) provide insights into the latitudinal shifts of thermocline water sourcing at Site U1385 (Fig. 2d)23. A high gradient, with values more negative than −8°C, signifies a subpolar source, whereas a low gradient indicates an increased subduction of warm mid-latitude waters. Over the long term, the thermal gradient, i.e. the position of the moisture source, exhibits a statistically significant inverse correlation (indicated by a negative coefficient) with Mediterranean forest abundance (Pearson’s correlation coefficient = −0.26 [95% CI: −0.34, −0.18], and, consequently, with winter precipitation (Supplementary Fig. S14). During MIS 35 to MIS 25, against a backdrop of strong amplitude of insolation (Fig. 2a)24, the southwestern Iberian climate remained relatively warm and humid, even during glacial stages MIS 30, MIS 28, and MIS 26, driven by persistent westerly influence. This interpretation is supported by multiple proxy records, including consistently high thermal gradients ( − 8 to −12 °C) (Fig. 2d), evidence of a relatively vigorous and active AMOC as inferred from δ¹³C at IODP Site U1385 (Fig. 2g)12, and sustained high SST in North Atlantic Ocean (Fig. 2c)25. This evidence indicates moisture transport to mid-to-high latitudes in the North Hemisphere, that may facilitate ice cap expansion in Greenland, Eurasia, and the Arctic. This long-term configuration was briefly disrupted during MIS 34 and MIS 32, leading to episodes of cooling and aridification in southwestern Iberia. These changes are consistent with a weakened AMOC, reduced SST, and diminished thermal gradients (~−2°C), which likely displaced the primary moisture source far south of Iberia.

During MIS 24 to MIS 20, under conditions of reduced amplitude of insolation (Fig. 2a), the climate in southwestern Iberia became markedly cold and arid, reflecting a diminished influence of the westerlies and a southward displacement of the moisture source far from Iberia, consistent with reduced thermal gradients of around −2°C. This phase also coincides with evidence for massive iceberg discharges in the North Atlantic, as indicated by the IRD records from ODP Site 983 and IODP 1314 (Fig. 2f)26,27. The concentration of IRD during this phase exceeds that of Heinrich Events28, when the polar front shifted to approximately 40°N29. Model simulations indicate that during Heinrich events, freshwater surges drive a northward expansion of the jet stream over the western Atlantic. This shift transports cold air into Europe and weakens wind-driven circulation in the Atlantic subpolar gyre30, resulting in North Atlantic cooling and reduced precipitation across Europe. Concurrently, the intertropical convergence zone (ITCZ) shifts south-eastward, causing increased aridity in the Sahel region30. The intensified IRD inputs to the North Atlantic during the middle phase of the MPT, together with evidence of extreme aridity in southwestern Iberia extending into northern Africa, suggests that the westerly wind belt contracted and migrated toward lower latitudes. MIS 24, MIS 22 and MIS 20 stand out as the driest and coldest glacials of the MPT, marking three episodes of pronounced mega-droughts in southwestern Iberia. Among them, MIS 22 had the extreme drought, persisting for approximately 32 kyrs from ~900 ka. Pollen records from southern Europe also documented that from around 900 ka, habitats became more open, and some water-demanding taxa, particularly hydrophilic tree taxa such as Cathaya, Taxodium-type, Cedrus, Tsuga, and Liquidambar, underwent marked decline, with some eventually becoming locally extinct (Supplementary Fig. S15)31–33. The subsequent glacial MIS 20 marked the third episode of severe cold and aridity event during the MPT, spanning ~40 kyrs (830–790 ka) associated with weakened AMOC, reduced SST, and frequent IRD deposition. Interestingly, during MIS 23 muted and short-lived warm and wet conditions in southwestern Iberia were coeval with reduced SST and weakened AMOC, which likely constrained evaporation, limiting moisture availability and precipitation. As a result, interglacial MIS 23 is considered part of the extended glacial cycle spanning MIS 24–22, which marks the onset of the first prolonged 100-kyr climate cycle. In contrast, warm and humid conditions in southwestern Iberia during MIS 21 were associated with reduced IRD flux, and strengthening of AMOC.

During the late MPT ( ~ 790–668 ka), the increasing amplitude of summer insolation changes coincided with a progressive recovery of the Mediterranean forest. This trend reflects the strong influence of westerlies, accompanied by substantial increases in winter precipitation and temperatures. These changes were likely driven by the northward migration of Atlantic moisture sources, which became closest to Iberia, with thermal gradients between −8°C and −6°C and enhanced AMOC activity, even during the glacial MIS 184.

Extreme drought events in the Northern Hemisphere mid-latitudes between 930 ka and 790 ka

Southwestern Iberia experienced a long-term trend of increasing aridity from MIS 31 to MIS 20, culminating in the mega-droughts recorded during the middle phase of the MPT (Fig. 3b). This scenario is associated with the proliferation of drought-resistant C4 plants in North Africa and the maximum dust accumulation in the Mediterranean Sea (Fig. 3d)34. The U1385-U1386 composite pollen record corroborates this trend, highlighting the presence of Neurada procumbens, a North African taxon indicative of extreme dryness (Supplementary Fig. S16)35. A statistically significant correlation between semi-desert pollen percentages and dust concentration from North Africa (Supplementary Fig. S17, 'Methods') also supports the occurrence of extreme arid periods across the Mediterranean region (Pearson’s coefficient = 0.30 [95% CI: 0.23, 0.36]. This aridity pattern is also recorded at Site U1313, showing an increased aeolian input sourced from North America into the North Atlantic from the accumulation rate of C26-alkan-1-ol (Fig. 3e)25,36. Chinese loess deposits documented unusually coarse and thick layers at around ~900 ka, suggesting enhanced aridification (Fig. 3f)37–39. Studies of ²³²Th and ²³⁰Th concentrations in hydrogenetic ferromanganese crusts from the Northern Pacific Gyre indicate increased phosphorus supply and enhanced biological carbon sequestration due to high aeolian input from Chinese loess between 940 ka and 800 ka40. These consistent findings suggest that widespread drought events in the mid-latitudes of the Northern Hemisphere were likely related to a reduction in the amplitude of insolation changes. Forcing and feedbacks explaining the three-phase climatic pattern during the MPT

A recent review7 highlights the potential role of Northern Hemisphere climatic changes in influencing the Southern Ocean and Antarctic climate during the MPT. Further, it suggests that the emergence of the 100-kyr cycles appears to be linked to precessional variability, modulated by eccentricity’s influence on Northern Hemisphere summer insolation intensity5,41,42. Thus, subtle regional shifts at high latitudes likely played a decisive role in driving the transition. Our data reveal a three-phase pattern of westerly-driven regional precipitation changes (Figs. 3b, 4) associated with varying amplitude of insolation (Fig. 3a), which in turn is modulated by eccentricity (Fig. 2a). This indicates the potential role of the changes in the intensity of insolation maxima and minima on the long-term evolution of the westerlies. To test this hypothesis, the results of eight time slices selected from an existing transient model simulation43,44 are analysed (Supplementary Fig. S18). One set of the time slices (117, 232, 324 and 399 ka BP) is characterized by contrasting insolation minima, and another set pair (128, 220, 313 and 410 ka BP) by contrasting insolation maxima. Comparison of the four time slices in each set shows that the north-westerlies are not sensitive to the intensity change of insolation maxima (Supplementary Fig. S18n-q), but they are sensitive to the intensity change of insolation minima (Supplementary Fig. S18j-m). A strong insolation minimum leads to both an intensification and a northward migration of the North Atlantic westerlies as compared to a weak insolation minimum. The calculation of the average minima of insolation for each of the three phases identified by the pollen and other paleoclimatic records also supports the three-phase pattern across the MPT, with average stronger minima in the early and late phases of the MPT (462.4 W/m2 and 450.6 W/m2, respectively), bracketing the weaker minima of the middle phase (464.8 W/m2, Fig. 2a).Fig. 4Schematics reconstruction of the temporal-spatial evolution of the three-phase transition between 1200 and 668 ka.a MIS 35-25. b MIS 24-20. c MIS 19-17. The early phase of MIS 35–MIS 25 ( ~ 1200–930 ka) was characterized by pronounced amplitude of insolation, with strong minima and intense maxima. According to the model results, the strong insolation minima during this phase could lead to stronger and more northward migration of the westerlies (Fig. 4a). Stronger insolation minima result in cooler temperature at the pole and a greater temperature gradient between the poles and the equator. This strong gradient intensifies heat transport from the tropics to the poles via the westerlies and the Gulf Stream45. The northward displacement of the westerlies enhanced precipitation, supplying moisture to high-latitude regions, and likely increasing Siberian River outflow, which facilitated the freshening of Arctic Ocean waters46. This freshening stimulated sea ice formation in the Atlantic and contributed to the expansion of Northern Hemisphere ice sheets47. Simultaneously, some moisture could be transported to mid-latitude southwestern Iberia, fostering wetter conditions and promoting the moderate to high expansion of Mediterranean forest. While increased precipitation facilitated ice accumulation, strong summer insolation maxima during this phase could lead to periodic ice melt, resulting in relatively stable and intermediate ice volume throughout this interval (Fig. 3h). This phase represents a precipitation-driven glacial expansion triggered by strong insolation minima, in which ice sheets were sustained by consistent moisture influx despite periodic ablation (Fig. 3h). In addition, the transition from MIS 27 to MIS 25 ( ~ 980 ka and 930 ka) towards the middle phase, experienced intensified cratonic erosion supported by exceptionally low εNd isotopic ratios36, that could trigger and promoted sustained ice sheet growth (ice-elevation feedback).

During the middle phase, spanning MIS 24 to MIS 20 ( ~ 930–790 ka), a reduced amplitude of insolation changes led to weaker insolation minima and maxima. The weakened insolation minima likely caused the westerlies to shift southward and weaken (Fig. 4b), thereby reducing moisture transport to higher latitudes. The combination of minimal Mediterranean forest amount and peak dust concentrations reaching the North Atlantic highlights the coldest and driest climatic conditions in the North Atlantic borderlands (Fig. 2b, Fig. 3e). Simultaneously, the weakened insolation maxima would have limited ice ablation, potentially fostering the growth of the ice sheet. The maximum ice expansion would lead to episodic massive iceberg discharges, reinforcing the Siberian High, intensifying the East Asian Winter Monsoon, and amplifying the Northern Hemisphere cooling and drying48.

Persistent and severe drought conditions had widespread effects on global climate dynamics. The increasingly open and sparsely vegetated landscapes likely enhanced surface albedo, reinforcing ice-albedo feedback mechanisms that further cooled the atmosphere and inhibited ice melting. Strengthened winter monsoonal winds accelerated aeolian deposition, facilitated dust transport to adjacent oceans and in the Mediterranean Sea (Fig. 3d-e)49,50. This intensification of dust flux coincided with increased carbon sequestration, particularly in the North Pacific Gyre40, further amplified by the weakened ocean thermohaline circulation between MIS 25 and 21 ( ~ 950–860 ka)51. The combined effects of increased chemical weathering, AMOC slowdown, enhanced aridity, and intensified dust deposition stimulated oceanic primary productivity, potentially leading to substantial atmospheric CO₂ drawdown at around 950 ka (Fig. 3g)52,53. This process amplified the carbon storage caused by the large reorganization of Southern Hemisphere processes during this period54,55. As atmospheric CO₂ levels declined, global cooling intensified, further reducing ice sheet ablation and promoting the onset of the first long and intense glacial period, starting from MIS 24 and culminating in the major glaciations of the MPT (MIS 22 and MIS 20) (Fig. 3h). As the ice sheets grew larger, they began to exhibit hysteresis: their stability no longer depended solely on current insolation levels but also on their prior growth history. Once established, large ice sheets could remain stable even during periods of increasing insolation that would previously have caused retreat1,5. This middle phase represents a temperature-driven glacial expansion originally forced by the synergy of regoliths loss and weak insolation amplitude.

During the late phase (MIS 19–MIS 17), the amplitude of boreal summer insolation increases, with both stronger minima and maxima, leading to the strengthening and northward migration of the north-westerlies, locating the moisture source close to Iberia (Fig. 4c), in a context of decreased iceberg discharges. Thus, at around 800 ka, cooling and aridification trends reversed in the North Atlantic, tropical western North Pacific, and Eurasia. Data and model simulations indicate that the thermal gradient progressively increased, associated with an intensification of the East Asian Summer Monsoon and the northward migration of the westerlies. This scenario is further supported by evidence of significant incision in the Seine Valley (northern France) at around 700 ka56. In this phase, insolation-driven increases in precipitation at the ice-sheet margins, together with hysteresis-induced stability, amplified ice-sheet growth and led to the establishment of the dominant 100,000-year glacial cycles after ~700 ka4,28, while westerly cyclicity simultaneously emerged in 80-kyr, 40-kyr, and notably, precessional bands (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig. S13).

This suite of processes may represent a unique period in the evolution of climate and glaciation over the past 2.6 million years, a period that was potentially forced by long-term variability of the amplitude in insolation. Our results support the hypothesis that a gradual rather than an abrupt change likely caused the Mid-Pleistocene Transition8. Furthermore, solving the MPT puzzle requires identifying climatic rhythms linked to precessional variability7, which governed ice sheet dynamics throughout the Pleistocene, and particularly deglaciations57. In our study, the emergence of a precessional cycle in the Mediterranean forest, and consequently in the north-westerlies, around 700 ka underscores the crucial role of Northern Hemisphere atmospheric processes in shaping the dominant 100-kyr cyclicity of the Late Pleistocene.

Methods

Regional setting

The IODP Sites U1386 and U1385, drilled in 2011 as part of the Mediterranean Outflow Expedition 339, are strategically located on the southwestern Iberian Margin58. Site U1385 is located off the Tagus and Sado River mouths and in front of the Arrabida mountain range. Site U1386 lies on the Faro Drift along the Alvares Cabral Moat, southeast of the margin and offshore from the Guadiana and Guadalquivir River mouths.

These sites experience direct influence from several major currents: the North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) at depth 1500 m to 4000 m, the Mediterranean Outflow Water (MOW) at intermediate levels (600 m-1500 m), and the surface Eastern North Atlantic Central Water (ENACW, 500 m-0 m), in addition to the Azores Current (AC) and the Portugal Current (PC)59,60. Notably, ENACW comprises both subtropical and subpolar branches, with the subtropical water occupying the upper layers and the subpolar branch lying below61,62.

The climate in this region is characterized by seasonal variability: in winter, it is influenced by North Atlantic pressure systems, bringing relatively warm and wet conditions, whereas in summer, the Azores subtropical high induces dry, hot weather63. Vegetation reflects this climatic pattern, with temperate Mediterranean forests in the middle elevation areas, including deciduous oaks, and sclerophyllous Mediterranean woodlands dominated by species such as evergreen oaks, Olea, Pistacia, Phillyrea, and Cistus at lower altitudes64. Additionally, Mediterranean shrubs like Ericaceae thrive in regions with higher humidity ( > 600 mm)65, while drought-tolerant taxa such as Cistus are prominent in drier areas13,65. The marshes surrounding the mouths of the Guadiana and Guadalquivir Rivers are dominated by grasses and shrubs of the Fabaceae, Poaceae, Cyperaceae, Lamiaceae and Amaranthaceae families, while a Mediterranean forest occupy the hilly landscape at the mouths of the Sado and Tagus Rivers13.

The pollen record for the entire MPT interval results from the combination of pollen records from Sites U1386 and U1385. The reliability of combining the two records is justified by: a) the vegetation of the both related hydrographic basins belong to the same biogeographical super province “Mediterranean-Iberoatlantic”66, b) previous study has shown that the Mediterranean forest pollen percentages during the MIS 12/MIS 11 transition overlap between the two sites67, and c) the pollen percentage values of the Mediterranean forest are similar, within the uncertainties of the pollen counting that is less than 10%, between the studied intervals at both sites (Supplementary Fig. S4f)68.

Benthic foraminifera δ¹⁸O analysis

Two species of mostly epifaunal-living benthic foraminifera, Planulina ariminensis and Cibicidoides ungerianus, were processed following the cleaning protocol of Kaboth et al.69. The interval 450-315 c-mcd was analyzed at the EPOC laboratory (France) using a Thermo Fisher Scientific MAT253 Isotope Ratio Mass Spectrometer (IRMS) coupled with a KIEL IV carbonate device and the uncertainties of the δ18O and δ13C measurements were ±0.10 ‰ and ±0.03 ‰, respectively. The interval 315-233 c-mcd was analyzed on a Gasbench 2 preparation device linked to a Delta V plus Advantage MS at VU Amsterdam as well as on a CARBO-KIEL preparation device linked to a Finnigan MAT253 mass spectrometer at Utrecht University. The results from the KIEL were calibrated using the international standard NBS-19 and the in-house standard NAXOS. Uncertainties for the δ13C and δ18O measurements are 0.03 ‰ and 0.08 ‰, respectively. Results from the Gasbench set-up were calibrated using the same international standard but another in-house standard, being IAEA-CO1. The uncertainties for the δ13C and δ18O measurements are 0.10 ‰ and 0.22 ‰, respectively. A total of 903 samples at an average resolution of 30 cm was measured.

Chronology

The chronology for Site U1386 was established by correlating its δ¹⁸Ob record with that of Site U138512, and the LR04 δ¹⁸Ob stack70 using Analyseries software71, after removing turbidite intervals. Originally, 40 tie points were selected, with a particular focus on deglacial intervals72. To further refine the isotopic stratigraphy, calcareous nannofossil analysis was performed in the interval 410-466 c-mcd. MIS 37 was identified at the base of the interval based on the temporary disappearance of large Gephyrocapsa forms dated to ~1240 ka72.

For the MPT interval, 17 out of 40 control points were selected. We further refined the MIS chronology by visually aligning the glacial and interglacial peaks in the U1386 δ¹⁸Ob record with those in the U1385 δ¹⁸Ob record (Supplementary Tab. S1, Supplementary Fig. S1). The values of the δ¹⁸Ob record range from 0.83 ‰ to 3.50 ‰, with an average sedimentation rate of ~50 cm/kyr. The amplitude of the δ¹⁸Ob signal at U1386 closely aligns with that of the LR04 stack70 and the U1385 record (Supplementary Fig. S1)12. However, some discrepancies, such as those observed during MIS 26, may be attributed to coarse-grained layers deposited under strong bottom currents. Despite these variations, the overall consistency supports the reliability of the age model.

Age uncertainty assessment

To evaluate the impact of age uncertainty on the pollen record, we quantified uncertainties in the age–depth model using a Monte Carlo tie-point optimization approach. Given that the LR04 benthic δ¹⁸O stack has reported age uncertainties of ~5 kyr throughout much of the Pleistocene70, and that tie-point selection is inherently subjective, we allowed individual tie-point ages to vary conservatively within ±10 kyr.

For each of 5000 Monte Carlo realizations, tie-point ages were randomly perturbed within this range, a new linear age-depth model was generated between tie points, and the misfit to LR04 was evaluated using the root mean square error (RMSE). Realizations that reduced the RMSE relative to the original tuning were retained. The resulting ensemble was used to quantify age uncertainty at each depth, with the median taken as the best-estimate age and the ensemble spread defining a 95% uncertainty envelope (Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Tab. S2). This approach refines existing tie points without introducing additional constraints and remains consistent with orbital-scale variability represented by the LR04 stack.

Pollen analysis

A total of 926 samples from IODP Site U1385 (432 samples) and IODP Site U1386 (494 samples) were analysed at every 4 cm and 10–30 cm, respectively, with each sample representing a 1–2 cm discrete sediment portion, resulting in an average temporal resolution of ~580 years. However, due to the lack of the availability of samples, the pollen record has coarse temporal resolution at the transitions of MIS 32-31, MIS 27-26, and MIS 20-19, and within MIS 34, MIS 32, MIS 21 and MIS 20. Each sediment sample (2.5-3 ml) was prepared following the standard palynological procedure at the University of Bordeaux73. At the beginning, each sample was sieved by 150 µm mesh to separate the coarse component and the finer fraction; and then using cold HCL successively at 10%, 25%, and 50% to remove carbonate in the finer element, and cold HF at 45% and 70% to eliminate the silica and silicates. The residue was sieved through the 10 µm mesh and then mounted on the microscope slide after fully mixing in glycerol to rotate the pollen grains.

Pollen identification was carried out under a Zeiss microscope at magnifications x400 and x1000 (oil immersion). Each sample comprised at least 17 taxa and a minimum pollen sum > 100 grains, excluding Pinus, aquatics and spores. As an over-represented genus in the marine sediment, Pinus pollen grains were not included in the main sum of terrestrial pollen to avoid artificial variations in other terrestrial taxa74,75. The pollen percentages of the different terrestrial taxa were calculated on the pollen main sum, while the Pinus percentages were based on the main sum plus Pinus. The spore and aquatic percentages were estimated from the total sum (pollen + spores + indeterminables + unknowns). The main pollen types, expressed as percentages based on the calculations above, are shown in Supplementary Fig. S3. The main pollen concentrations were calculated as the number of terrestrial pollen grains, excluding Pinus, per unit of sediment volume, while the total sporo-pollen concentrations were calculated based on the number of terrestrial and aquatic pollen grains, spores, indeterminables and unknowns per unit of sediment volume (Supplementary Fig. S4d-e). The uncertainties of the pollen percentage were calculated using exact Clopper-Pearson method, assuming a binomial proportion76 in R version 4.5.177.

All the main pollen types were grouped into different ecological groups based on their climatic requirements. There are four ecological groups: 1) The Mediterranean forest, principally consisting of Quercus deciduous-type, Quercus evergreen-type, Carpinus, Corylus, Fagus, Fraxinus excelsior-type (Supplementary Fig. S4a)64. 2) Mediterranean taxa group, mainly composed of Quercus evergreen-type, Cistus, Olea, Phillyrea and Pistacia, representing a seasonal climate with cool and wet winter and hot and dry summer (Supplementary Fig. S4a)78,79. 3) Semi-desert comprises Artemisia, Amaranthaceae and both Ephedra types, indicating dry and cold climate in winter (Supplementary Fig. S4b)79. 4) Heathland is formed by Ericaceae and Calluna, which indicate typical annual humid climate (Supplementary Fig. S4c)65.

Principal component analysis

To identify the primary climatic factors influencing pollen distribution, and consequently vegetation changes, we employed PCA to reduce the dimensionality of the pollen data (Supplementary Fig. S5-S7). This approach enables the extraction of key components from the complex multivariate data, thereby facilitating a clearer interpretation of the climatic drivers of vegetation dynamics. Seventeen pollen taxa with high abundance and clear ecological significance were selected for the analysis, since the rare taxa with very low percentages were excluded to reduce statistical noise and highlight the main climatic gradients.

The PCA was performed on the pollen percentage data that were centered but not scaled prior to ordination, in order to preserve the variance and ecological weighting of the dominant taxa (Supplementary Fig. S5). This ordination forms the basis for the PCA biplot and loading interpretation. The statistical significance of the extracted principal components was evaluated using the Broken-stick model, which compares observed eigenvalues against a random expectation (Supplementary Fig. S6). In addition, a loading plot for PC1 was generated to quantify the contributions of individual taxa to the primary compositional gradients (Supplementary Fig. S7). To assess differences among the three stratigraphic phases (early, middle, and late phases), we calculated 95% confidence ellipses and phase-wise cluster centroids in the PCA ordination space (Supplementary Fig. S5). These metrics characterize differences in the central tendency of pollen assemblages among phases, while explicitly acknowledging overlap inherent to high-resolution records. For time-series analysis (Fig. 3b), PC1 scores derived from the same PCA were subsequently standardized (z-scores) to facilitate comparison with other paleoclimate proxies. PCA analysis was carried out using package vegan80, and factoextra81(Kassambara and Mundt, 2020) in R version 4.5.177.

Time series preprocessing

The Mediterranean forest pollen percentage time series is irregularly spaced in time. To enable statistical analysis (wavelet, change-point, and correlation analyses), which requires regularly spaced observations82, the series was resampled to a constant time step of Δt = 580 years, corresponding to the arithmetic mean of the original age differences (Supplementary Fig. S8).

The selected interval avoids artificial oversampling, as the majority of consecutive age differences (660 out of 926) are shorter than 580 years (Supplementary Fig. S8). To test the sensitivity of the results to the choice of resampling interval, we also considered alternative time steps of 400 and 700 years. These produced regular time series and their spectra nearly indistinguishable from those obtained using the 580-year interval, and therefore are not shown in the main text (Supplementary Fig. S9). Interpolation was performed using four methods: linear, nearest-neighbor, cubic Hermite, and Akima splines. All methods yielded consistent outcomes, and comparative analyses demonstrated that interpolation choice does not materially affect the regular times series (Supplementary Fig. S9). Based on this comparison, the Akima spline method was selected for subsequent analyses because it provides smooth interpolation while minimizing artificial oscillations and preserving local variability83,84. This implementation was carried out using the R package akima85 in R version 4.5.177. It is widely used in paleoclimate time series of irregular time series and avoids artefacts associated with higher-order spline interpolation86.

Change-point analysis

To statistically define the major ecological transitions in the Mediterranean forest percentage record, we applied a structural break detection framework. A critical parameter in this analysis is the minimum segment size (h), which prevents over-segmentation and ensures that detected phases represent persistent climate regimes rather than short-term variability.

To objectively constrain h, we performed REDFIT spectral analysis87 on the original unevenly spaced pollen series using the R package dplR88. The analysis included 2000 Monte Carlo simulations, an oversampling factor (ofac = 4) and five Welch segments (n50 = 5) to obtain a smoothed consistent periodogram. This analysis identified a dominant quasi-periodicity of ~80-90 ka (Supplementary Fig. S10), consistent with orbital-scale variability. Based on this dominant timescale, the minimum segment length for change-point detection was constrained to 15-17% of the total record length (h = 0.15-0.17), ensuring that identified phases represent major climatic intervals.

Structural breaks were then estimated using multiple breakpoint methods89, implemented in the breakpoints() function of the R package strucchange90,91. The analysis was applied to the interpolated time series described above. We tested models with h = 0.15, 0.16, and 0.17 and m = 0 to 5 breakpoints for each h value, and selected the optimal solution by minimizing the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), which exhibited a clear minimum at m = 2 for all cases (Supplementary Fig. S11). Residual sum of squares (RSS) was also evaluated for all candidate models as a measure of goodness-of-fit, but was not used for model selection because it decreases monotonically with increasing model complexity and therefore favors overfitted solutions (Supplementary Fig. S11). Confidence intervals for breakpoint locations were estimated using the strucchange framework (Supplementary Fig. S12)90,91.

Spectral wavelet analysis

After resampling, the evenly spaced Mediterranean forest record was linearly detrended by subtracting a first-order polynomial fitted via least-squares method and subsequently standardized (zero mean, unit variance). This procedure removes low-frequency bias and facilitates comparability across scales. These preprocessing steps ensure that the variance detected in the wavelet transform reflects genuine oscillatory components rather than low-frequency trends or differences in amplitude82,92.

The Morlet wavelet is widely used in geophysical, climatological, and ecological applications82,93–96, as it provides an optimal balance between time and frequency localization93,95,97,98. Its Gaussian envelope ensures smooth spectral tapering, minimizing spectral leakage, while the sinusoidal carrier allows straightforward interpretation of both amplitude and phase. Furthermore, the Morlet wavelet has a simple and well-defined scale–frequency relationship (f ~ 1/s, where f is frequency and s is scale), which facilitates comparison with Fourier-based spectra and makes the identification of dominant periodicities more intuitive. This combination of properties makes it particularly suitable for detecting periodic/quasi-periodic oscillations and localized variability in natural records such as the Mediterranean forest pollen percentage time series21,96,99,100. Bias correction and normalization

The standard formulation of wavelet power is known to exhibit a bias toward smaller scales, as noted in several previous studies, and this bias is inherent to most existing implementations82,93,101,102. To mitigate this limitation, we applied the bias-corrected normalization proposed of Liu et al.,103 which rescales the wavelet power to produce an unbiased representation of variance across scales. This correction is implemented in the biwavelet R package73 available freely from CRAN, which builds upon the MATLAB routines developed by Grinsted et al.93. The framework proposed by Grinsted et al.93 and Gouhier et al.73 also refines the normalization of wavelet power relative to background spectra for both univariate and bivariate (cross-wavelet and coherence) analyses, thereby ensuring consistency and comparability between localized and global spectral measures.

Statistical significance and null models

To assess whether observed spectral features (i.e., time-scale points) are statistically significant (i.e., exceed what would be expected under a null hypothesis of background variability), Torrence & Compo82 derived the theoretical wavelet spectrum for red-noise processes, modeled as a first-order autoregressive (AR(1)) process. Under this null hypothesis/model, the expected wavelet power Pnull(s) and the associated confidence levels can be obtained from the chi-squared distribution with the appropriate degrees of freedom. We adopted an AR (1) process as the null hypothesis to represent the red-noise background of the climate system, following the rationale that many climatic time series exhibit persistence consistent with red noise87,104. Regions where the observed power Px (s,τ) exceeds the 95% confidence level of the corresponding null distribution are thus identified as statistically significant. To enhance the robustness of the significance assessment, we also applied Monte Carlo surrogate testing following the approach of Grinsted et al.93 implemented in the biwavelet R package73. This procedure generates synthetic AR (1) realizations to derive empirical confidence levels that complement the theoretical red-noise model. Regions exhibiting power exceeding the 95% confidence threshold were identified as significant, after accounting for the scale-dependent bias corrections proposed by Liu et al.103.

Edge effects and cone of influence

Due to the finite length of the time series, the wavelet transform becomes increasingly unreliable near the beginning and end of the record, where the support of the wavelet extends beyond the available data. To account for this, a cone of influence (COI) is defined as the region in the (s,τ) plane where edge effects reduce the effective wavelet power (variance) by a factor of e−2. Wavelet power within the COI is therefore potentially influenced by edge effects and should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Fig. S13)82.

Computational implementation

In our analysis, we employed the bias-corrected wavelet power Pxcorr (s, τ) and assessed significance at the 95% level against correspondingly corrected null distributions. All computations were performed using the R package biwavelet73, which implements the algorithms described by Grinsted et al.,93 Torrence & Compo82, and Liu et al.103. The smallest resolvable scale was set to s0 = 2Δt (1160 years; where Δt = 580 years, mean sampling of differences between consecutive ages of Mediterranean unevenly spaced time series), with a scale spacing parameter dj = 0.1, and the analysis was extended to a maximum scale smax = 196 kiloyears, sufficient to capture variability from orbital to millennial timescales.

Monte Carlo surrogate testing was carried out separately to assess robustness, with a fixed significance level of 0.95 and 1000 surrogate simulations. These computations were performed using the biwavelet R package73 in R version 4.5.177.

Correlation analyses

Correlation and cross-correlation analyses were performed to evaluate the temporal relationships between pollen and other paleoclimate records. All interpolation procedures followed the preprocessing strategy described above. To ensure statistical integrity and avoid biases associated with asymmetric interpolation, we adopted a proxy-specific resolution matching approach. Specifically, for the thermal gradient and Mediterranean forest analysis, the pollen record was interpolated onto the native temporal grid of the thermal gradient series (Δt = 1.0 kyr). For the dust flux (Ti/Al) and semi-desert vegetation analysis, both records were interpolated onto a common regularly spaced temporal grid with a resolution of Δt = 580 years.

Prior to analysis, the dust flux and semi-desert vegetation time series were linearly detrended using a least-squares fitting procedure (pracma package)105 to remove long-term trends and ensure stationarity. Zero-lag correlations were assessed using both Pearson and Spearman coefficients, which yielded consistent results. To capture lead-lag dynamics, cross-correlation functions (CCF) were conducted to identify optimal offsets. The sequences were aligned according to the identified lag, and the maximum Spearman rank correlation coefficients (r) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated via bootstrapping with 1,000 replicates (RVAideMemoire package)106. All statistical computations were performed in R version 4.5.177, and the results are presented in Supplementary Fig. S14 and Fig. S17.

Model simulations

To investigate the effect of Northern Hemisphere summer insolation (NHSI) amplitude on the North Atlantic westerlies, the Orb transient simulation of the last 800 ka, performed in Yin et al.43 and Wu et al.,44 was analyzed. The Orb experiment is performed using the model LOVECLIM1.3107,108, and only astronomical parameters24 vary with time, the greenhouse gases and ice sheets are fixed to their pre-industrial conditions. The 500-hPa winter (DJF) zonal wind at eight time slices are analyzed: 410, 399, 324, 313, 232, 220, 128 and 117 ka BP. At 410, 313, 220 and 128 ka BP, NHSI is maximum but with different intensities; while at 399, 324, 232 and 117 ka BP, NHSI is minimum but with different intensities (Supplementary Fig. S18).
