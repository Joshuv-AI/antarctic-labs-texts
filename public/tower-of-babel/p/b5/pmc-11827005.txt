Modified Iliac Oblique for Intraoperative Evaluation of Dual-Mobility Liner Malseating
Authors: Richard D. Murray, Charles W. Powell, Joseph R. Duff, Dillon L. Morrow, Kristopher C. Sanders
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The use of dual-mobility systems in both primary and revision total hip arthroplasty for patients with concern for instability has become increasingly popular. While these systems are an effective means for combating instability, they are not without unique complications, such as liner malseating. We describe an intraoperative technique to evaluate for malseating via fluoroscopy, allowing a surgeon to identify and correct malseating intraoperatively. Reducing the incidence of liner malseating in dual-mobility total hip replacement systems could reduce overall complication rates postoperatively and presumably improve their efficacy.

Introduction

Instability is a common and costly cause of revision total hip arthroplasty (THA). The use of dual-mobility systems to combat instability in both primary and revision settings has become increasingly popular over recent years. While dual-mobility systems have proven to be comparable, and even superior, to other techniques utilized to prevent instability, they are not without their unique complications. Malseating of dual-mobility liners within acetabular cups is one such complication and has been associated with issues such as corrosion and ongoing instability. With the use of appropriate surgical technique and intraoperative evaluation of liner seating within an acetabular cup under both direct visualization and fluoroscopically utilizing a modified iliac oblique view, we believe the incidence of malseating can be greatly reduced.

Surgical technique

Once surgical exposure of the acetabulum is achieved, the press-fit acetabular cup (PINNACLE Dual Mobility Liner System, Depuy Synthes) is placed in appropriate version and inclination [1]. The highly polished, lipped liner is inserted by hand, followed by automated impaction under fluoroscopic guidance (Fig. 1). Cutouts around the lip of the liner are used for visual confirmation of complete and symmetrical seating. We then fluoroscopically obtained a modified iliac oblique view of the acetabular components to reduce the incidence of malseating or incomplete seating of the lipped liner. Because ante-inclination is variable for each patient, the radiologist technician must vary the roll of the C-arm to obtain a perfect perpendicular view of the cup and liner, usually approximately 25 degrees of internal rotation (Fig. 2). The presence of malseating is then assessed by evaluating for step-off and incongruity at the interface between the cup and the inner liner. Discussion

Dislocation after THA is a common complication, leading to increased morbidity, reoperation rates, and cost. Recent data have shown instability to be responsible for greater than 20% of all primary THA revisions, with some series showing rates of revision due to instability as high as 50% [2,3]. Considering projections for all-cause revision THA to increase by 43%-70% from 2014 to 2030 and the recent increases in cost for revision THA, as much as 300%, it has become increasingly important for surgeons to minimize revision rates in patients undergoing THA [4,5]. In the setting of revision for instability, the use of dual-mobility THA has shown reduced subsequent dislocation and re-revision rates, comparable to upsizing of the femoral head component [6]. This effectiveness of dual-mobility constructs for instability has led to a substantial increase in their utilization [2].

Additionally, dual-mobility systems have even become an attractive option for primary THA in patients at elevated risk of instability, such as those with reduced spinopelvic mobility secondary to a stiff or fused lumbar spine [7]. Meta-analyses have shown dual-mobility constructs to effectively reduce dislocation rates in both primary and revision settings [8].

The dual-mobility implant provides an additional articulation compared to traditional THA implants. It is composed of a small ceramic or cobalt chrome femoral head that snap-fits into a polyethylene liner, forming one articulation. The outer polyethylene liner then articulates within a cobalt-chrome inner cup, forming an additional articulation.

This enhanced stability is explained by 2 principles: (1) a dual articulation increasing arc of motion before prosthetic neck impingement and (2) the larger head size of the articulating polyethylene component relative to the shell size increases the distance necessary for the femoral head to translate before dislocation, known as the jump distance [8].

Dual mobility, however, is not without potential complications and drawbacks. The increased modularity of these implants introduces 2 unique complications: liner malseating and intraprosthetic dislocation [9,10]. The incidence of malseated dual-mobility liners using postoperative radiographs has shown incidences as high as 16.4% in early studies and as low as 0.15% in more recent large-scale multicenter studies [[11], [12], [13]]. If the liner is left incompletely seated, complications such as instability and metal debris formation may ensue. Metal debris is due to increased micromotion at the modular metal-metal junction, leading to fretting corrosion, posing a risk for metallosis, adverse local soft-tissue response, and subsequent abductor injury [14].

Confirmation of complete seating is of the utmost importance. Intraoperative techniques to ensure appropriate seating have been described. Chalmers et al. [15] describe a 4-quadrant method of ensuring complete taper seating and appropriate seating of the cup. Their methods describe using an impactor around the periphery of the liner at the 12 o’clock, 9 o’clock, 6 o’clock, and 3 o’clock positions. During periphery impaction, attention is paid to the opposite quadrant to ensure that the liner is not toggling. Once confirmed that the liner is well seated, a final forceful impact is performed centrally. If, during this test, the liner toggles, then the liner should be removed, and the process repeated. They reported a 1.6% incidence of liner malseating using this technique.

Modular dual-mobility designs using elevated-lipped liners have proven especially difficult to implement with regards to radiographically assessing for appropriate liner seating. In one cadaveric study, 2 fellowship-trained, high-volume arthroplasty surgeons evaluated malseating in elevated and flush design dual-mobility liners using anteroposterior pelvis, anteroposterior hip, iliac-oblique, obturator-oblique, and a series of cross-table lateral (0, 15, and 30 degrees) radiographs. They found a significant difference in misdiagnosis of intentionally well-seated vs malseated liners between the elevated and flush liner design groups, 40% and 5%, respectively [16].

While many publications have described intraoperative techniques to reduce the incidence of malseating of dual-mobility liners, we offer a technique allowing the surgeon to evaluate for appropriate seating of the liner and the ability to correct malseating intraoperatively before completion of the case. This technique has been effectively utilized in over 80 cases by the primary surgeon and can be extrapolated for use with all modular dual-mobility implant systems.
