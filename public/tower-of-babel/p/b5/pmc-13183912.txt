Brain energetic landscapes shape state dysregulation in major depressive disorder: a morphological network controllability perspective
Authors: Jinpeng Niu, Jie Xia, Qingjin Liu, Yaohui He, Wei Li, Kangjia Chen, Xi Zhang, Jiang Qiu, Huafu Chen, Jiao Li, Wei Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aberrant dynamic shifts in brain states are a hallmark of cognitive and behavioral dysfunctions in major depressive disorder (MDD), yet the underlying mechanisms of these disturbances remain elusive. Leveraging network control theory of morphological networks, we characterized aberrant brain dynamics and energy deficits of MDD patients in two independent cohorts. MDD patients exhibited reduced dynamic stability, characterized by elevated intra-state transitions and diminished inter-state transitions, which were associated with impaired control energy. Region-specific deficits of energy regulation capacity were observed in key nodes of the default mode and limbic networks, including the posterior cingulate cortex and temporal pole, which correlated with cognition and clinical symptoms in MDD patients. MDD-related energy inefficiency was related to multiscale energy architectures at cellular, molecular, and biological levels, including mitochondrial morphologies and functions, energy metabolism pathways, and brain metabolic patterns. Additionally, we demonstrated an association between energy demands and cortical dynamics, indicating a disrupted energy-dependent neurophysiological activity in MDD patients. Together, these results identified the energetic fundamentals underlying pathological brain-state transitions in MDD patients. Identifying energy-vulnerable nodes from a controllability perspective may therefore provide valuable targets for restoring normative neural dynamics in MDD.

Introduction

Continuous neural dynamics underpin normal brain function by generating diverse activity states and complex inter-state transition rules [1, 2]. Notably, these fundamental dynamics are disrupted during neurological and psychiatric disorders, manifesting as profound cognitive and behavioral impairments [3, 4]. Major depressive disorder (MDD) is a common psychiatric disorder characterized by clinical symptoms and cognitive impairments [5, 6]. Emerging evidence indicates that disrupted dynamic transitions in MDD patients are associated with cognitive deficits and core clinical symptom severities, including emotional processing and cognitive control [7–9]. Characterizing aberrant brain dynamics in MDD patients may provide critical insights into their underlying pathophysiology.

Network Control Theory (NCT) provides a framework for modeling brain-state transitions under the constraints imposed by the structural architecture [10, 11]. Applications of NCT in MDD have identified structural and functional brain controllability deficits, which explain the capacity of specific regions and networks to mediate state transitions [8, 9, 12–14]. However, the mechanisms responsible for specific state transition dynamics remain unclear. By quantifying the control energy required to transition between brain states, NCT provides a profile of the energy landscape that reflects the dynamic brain repertoire [15, 16]. Control energy, defined as the minimum input needed to direct brain dynamics along an optimal trajectory [17], serves as a marker of energetic efficiency. It has been used to explain structurally supported executive function development [18], reveal energetic disruptions in epilepsy [19], and predict responses to external stimuli [20, 21]. Recent evidence links energy inefficiency to dysregulation of brain state in MDD patients [22], suggesting that NCT is a valuable tool for identifying biomarkers and guiding modulation of pathological brain dynamics.

However, recent NCT studies have focused on diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) derived networks, which have inherent limitations in accurately quantifying long-range connections [23]. Notably, the role of gray matter in controlling brain dynamics warrants emphasis, as it constitutes the primary energy-consuming substrate. White matter connectivity and gray matter volume make complementary contributions to network control, which together establish the foundation for regulating brain state transitions [24]. Individual-level morphological networks, such as the Morphometric INverse Divergence (MIND) and morphometric similarity networks (MSN), have been widely used to characterize the macroscale architectural organization of the human brain [25–27]. The MIND provides a more reliable, heritable, and biologically informed measure than MSN, showing enhanced sensitivity to both age-related changes and underlying gene co-expression [28]. MDD-related alterations in MIND showed systematic associations with structural connectivity, transcriptomic similarity, and neuro-receptor distributions [29]. Therefore, applying control theory to morphological networks bridges stable neuroanatomy with disrupted brain dynamics, offering a more comprehensive and biologically grounded understanding of depression. Moreover, conventional NCT approaches largely ignore the temporal organization of control energy. Recently proposed time-averaged control energy (TCE) captured the average control energy required to maintain and transition between functional brain states during the resting-state [30]. Using this parameter, Ceballos et al. reported that the brain maintains functional diversity with minimal energy cost [30]. Identifying MDD-related TCE patterns constrained by structural architecture, therefore, helps to determine the energy basis of state dysregulation.

As a purely statistical parameter, control energy requires validation against measurable energetic architectures to establish biological plausibility. Limited positron emission tomography studies have linked control energy to cerebral glucose and oxygen metabolism [19, 30], providing insights into its metabolic basis. Beyond biological energy reserves, all fundamental cerebral processes rely on cellular energy transformation [31]. Mitochondria control subcellular activities and provide energy essential for maintaining brain functions. Moreover, energy-related substrates are metabolized through interconnected pathways that generate energy and synthesize biomolecules essential for neuronal function [32]. Overall, mitochondrial properties, energy metabolic pathways, and macroscopic metabolic signatures characterize multiscale energetic architectures that collectively control brain dynamics.

This study, therefore, integrated network control theory with multiscale energetic architectures to determine whether pathological state transitions in MDD patients were associated with energetic deficits across cellular, molecular, and systemic biological systems. Specifically, we first identified altered brain-state transitions in MDD patients, based on their morphological network architecture. Then, we found energy-related evidence for these dysregulations, and linked energy inefficiency to cognitive deficits and clinical symptoms. Finally, we correlated atypical energy demands in MDD patients with mitochondrial phenotypes, energy metabolic pathways, macroscopic metabolic patterns, and neurophysiological oscillations. These observations helped us better understand how brain energetic landscapes affect pathological brain dynamics in MDD patients.

Methods

Participants

This study included two independent cohorts. MDD patients and demographics-matched healthy controls (HCs) in the discovery cohort were recruited from the Southwest University Brain Imaging Center. The replication cohort included outpatients from the Department of Psychiatry at the First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University and the nearby community. All MDD patients were diagnosed by experienced psychiatrists according to DSM-IV criteria. Depression and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) and 14-item Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), respectively. Exclusion criteria for MDD patients included major neurological or psychiatric comorbidities, structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities, or MRI contraindications. HCs were free from Axis I or II disorders and had no family history of psychiatric illness. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All study protocols were performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975 and were approved by the ethics committees of Southwest University (discovery cohort) and Shanxi Medical University (replication cohort).

MRI data acquisition and pre-processing

All MRI images in the discovery cohort were collected with a Siemens Trio 3.0 T scanner at the Southwest University Center for Brain Imaging. Acquisition parameters for structural T1-weighted MRI images were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 1900 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.52 ms, field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 256 × 256, flip angle = 9°, and voxel size =1 × 1 × 1 mm3. Diffusion MRI images were acquired using a diffusion-weighted, gradient-echo planar imaging (EPI) sequence: TR = 11,000 ms, TE = 98 ms, FOV = 256 × 256 mm2, matrix size = 128 × 128, voxel size =2 × 2 × 2mm3, slices = 60, one volume with b = 0 s/mm2, 30 non-collinear directions b = 1000 s/mm2). Resting state fMRI images were acquired using a gradient-echo type EPI sequence: TR/ TE = 2000/30 ms, matrix size = 64 × 64, flip angle = 90°, voxel size = 3.44 × 3.44 × 4 mm3, FOV = 220 × 220 mm2, axial slices = 32, and number of volumes = 242.

All MRI images in the replication cohort were acquired with a 3.0-T Trio Siemens System at the Shanxi Provincial People’s Hospital, Taiyuan, China. Structural MRI images were acquired using a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo T1-weighted sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2300 ms, TE = 2.95 ms, TI = 900 ms, flip angle = 9°, FOV = 225 × 240 mm2, 160 slices, thickness=1.2 mm. Diffusion-sensitizing gradients were applied along 12 non-collinear directions (b = 1000 s/mm2), together with a non-diffusion-weighted acquisition (b = 0 s/mm2). For each DTI scan, 45 contiguous axial slices were acquired with the following parameters: TR = 6000 ms, TE = 90 ms, flip angle = 90°, slice thickness = 3 mm, FOV = 240 mm × 240 mm2, matrix size =128 × 128, and voxel size=1.875 × 1.875 × 3 mm3. The resting-state images were acquired using an EPI sequence, and the acquisition parameters are as follows: TR = 2000 ms, TE = 40 ms, flip angle = 90°, matrix = 64 × 64, FOV = 240 × 240, thickness = 4 mm, axial slices = 32, and number of volumes = 212. The specific data pre-processes are provided in the supplementary materials.

Morphometric network construction

Diffusion MRI data were parcellated into Schaefer-400 cortical regions to reconstruct whole-brain structural networks. Connection strengths were measured using streamline counts, followed by binarization to represent the presence/absence of white matter pathways. For morphological networks, five T1-derived features, including cortical thickness, gray matter volume, surface area, mean curvature, and sulcal depth, were standardized and aggregated per region. Multivariate similarity between regions was quantified using the symmetric Kullback-Leibler divergence, yielding an individualized MIND network (400 × 400) [28]. To integrate multimodal information, the MIND network was thresholded by the binarized DTI network. Specifically, only edges that existed in the DTI network (indicating direct white matter connectivity) retained their original MIND similarity values (which reflect gray matter morphometric alignment). The resulting network concurrently captures both white matter tractography connections and the biological characteristics of gray matter.

Time-averaged control energy

Time-averaged control energy (TCE) was used to quantify the average control energy during dynamic transitions across brain states [30]. All cortical regions (N = 400) were assigned to seven canonical intrinsic networks [33]. At each time point, the network with the highest mean activity was identified as dominant, generating a temporal sequence of network dominance. BOLD activity was then averaged within each dominant state to create seven state-specific activity maps. Using an optimal control strategy, we simulated transitions between every pair of these network states and computed the required control energy for each transition (Supplementary Methods). Individual TCE maps were generated by averaging transition-specific energy maps across all transitions, with each regional value representing its cumulative energy cost during dynamic state transitions.

Case-control comparison analysis

Y represents the measure of regional controllability. The models incorporated a non-parametric smoothing function (f1) for age to account for potential nonlinear relationships without imposing predefined constraints. To control for the confounding effects of head size and motion, we incorporated total intracranial volume (TIV) and mean framewise displacement (mFD) as covariates in the model. Meanwhile, the case-control differences in TCE maintained high consistency irrespective of whether TIV and mFD were included as covariates in the model (Fig. S1). Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d values, and the false discovery rate (FDR) correction (P < 0.05) was used to adjust for multiple comparisons across regions.

At the network level, regions were aggregated into seven canonical Yeo-Krienen functional networks [33]. The same model was used to assess network-level differences in TCE. Here, network enrichment significance testing (NEST) was used to evaluate the specificity of brain-phenotype associations within intrinsic functional networks. Using participant-level permutations (1000 permutations per network), NEST generated a null distribution while controlling Type I errors. Enrichment scores quantified the relative strengths of within-network associations when compared to out-of-network effects. The P-values were evaluated based on 999 permutations, with FDR corrections used across networks [34].

Correlation analysis with cognitive terms

To establish an association between MDD-related abnormalities in TCE and cognitive terms from the NeuroSynth meta-analytic database [35]. Twenty-three cognitive terms were selected from the Neurosynth 50 meta-analytic topics (www.neurosynth.org/analyses/topics/v5-topics-50/). The overlap ratio was calculated by dividing the number of overlapping voxels between TCE-defined regions (showing a significant increase/decrease) and each cognitive term map by the total number of relevant voxels in those comparative maps.

Differential analysis of state transition probability and energy

To identify the relationship between TCE and MDD-specific state-switching profiles, we quantified two metrics across all intrinsic network pairs: transition probability (TP), which represents the likelihood of shifting between brain states [20], and transition energy (TE), which reflects the control energy required for state transitions under structural constraints.

We further incorporated a functional hierarchy framework, classifying networks as unimodal (visual and sensorimotor) or heteromodal (others), based on their functional specializations [36, 37]. This enabled evaluation of TP and TE dynamics not only within but also across hierarchical levels.

Correlation analysis with clinical profiles

To determine the specific and separable associations between MDD-related energetic deficits and depressive and anxiety symptoms, independent behavioral partial least squares (PLS) analyses were used to examine the multivariate relationship between TCE and symptom severity. In each model, TCE values from 400 cortical regions were used as predictors, with depression (HAMD) and anxiety (HAMA) scores as response variables, respectively. All variables were z-scored and adjusted for the same covariates used in case-control comparisons. Using singular value decomposition, PLS analyses identified latent components that maximized covariance between TCE patterns and symptom profiles. Statistical significance was assessed using permutation testing (1000 iterations), which shuffled symptom scores to create a null distribution of covariance. For significant components, bootstrapping evaluated the reliability and contributions of regional TCE loadings and clinical variables loadings, revealing distinct and shared neural control mechanisms responsible for depression and anxiety symptoms in MDD patients.

Multiple linear regression analysis

As a statistical measure quantifying the capacity to control brain-state transitions, aberrant TCE may indicate impaired efficiency or dysregulation of the foundational energy-metabolism architecture. We employed mitochondrial phenotypes [38], metabolic pathways, and metabolic resources [39] to reflect metabolic energy characteristics at the cellular, molecular, and biological levels, respectively. Multiple linear regression models were used to characterize the links between MDD-related TCE alterations and these multiscale brain energy architectures (Supplementary Methods). In each model, case-control differences in TCE were the dependent variables, and the corresponding energy landscape matrices were the predictor variables. The model goodness-of-fit was measured by the coefficient of determination (R2). Finally, the relative importance of each variable in explaining variance in case-control differences was quantified using the pmvd metrics from the relaimpo R package, v.2.2-7 [40].

Correlation analysis with neurophysiological oscillations

Brain state transitions require synchronized neural activity, which is intimately connected to the brain’s energy consumption profile [41]. Here, we examined the association between MDD-related energy dysregulation and cortical dynamics. Six-minute resting-state magnetoencephalography (MEG) data of 33 unrelated healthy younger adults (ages: 22–35 years) were obtained from the Human Connectome Project (HCP) S1200 release [42]. Complete acquisition protocols are documented in the HCP S1200 Release Manual. Following standard HCP pre-processing and spectral analysis (Supplementary Methods), vertex-level power data were parcellated into 400 cortical regions for six frequency bands: delta (2–4 Hz), theta (5–7 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (15–29 Hz), low gamma (30–59 Hz), and high gamma (60–90 Hz) [43, 44]. Pre-processed spectral maps were sourced from the neuromaps package [45]. To evaluate how oscillatory activity contributes to alterations in energy demand in MDD patients, a multiple linear regression model was fitted using spectral power across all six bands to predict case-control differences in TCE. Model fit was evaluated using R2, and predictor importance was quantified to determine the relative contribution of each frequency band.

Reproducibility analysis

The above MDD-related alterations in energy demands were validated in the replication cohort. Using the identical semiparametric generalized additive models in the discovery cohort, we calculated case-control differences in the energy demands at the whole brain and node levels in the validation set, while controlling for the effects of age, sex, TIV, and head motion. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d values, and the FDR correction (P < 0.05) was used to adjust for multiple comparisons across regions. To test the replicability of regional changes in energy demand, a spatial similarity analysis was used to compare d-value maps between the discovery and replication cohorts.

Moreover, given that antidepressants influence brain dynamics [7, 46], we stratified MDD patients into drug-use (n = 81, age: 38.94 ± 12.61 years, 52 females) and drug-naive (n = 76, age: 38.26 ± 12.61 years, 50 females) groups to assess the medication effect on energy demands specifically. Specifically, we evaluated differences in energy demands between drug-use and drug-naive groups and HCs, while also assessing how closely each group resembled the discovery cohort.

TCE was computed as the mean transition energy across all state pairs, representing regional energy demands for maintaining brain dynamics (Fig. 1D). NEST identified significant case-control differences predominantly within the default mode network (DMN), ventral attention network (VAN), and limbic network (LIM) (Fig. 2C, Table S2). In contrast, regions showing reduced TCE were linked to facial and emotional regulations (Fig. 2E).

Altered state transition patterns in MDD

Using NCT, we computed the TE between all pairwise network states. The resulting asymmetric TE matrix reflected direction-dependent energy costs for state transitions, shaped by structural topology (Fig. S2). TP analysis revealed that both MDD patients and HCs preferred low-energy intra-state transitions over costlier inter-state transitions (Fig. S3A).

Compared to HCs, MDD patients showed elevated TE for most transitions, especially for those originating from the limbic network (Fig. 3A), and exhibited increased intra-network TP alongside decreased inter-network TP (Fig. 3B). Linking MDD-related energy demands to multiscale energetic architectures

To identify systematic relationships between temporal control energy and energetic constraints, we linked our findings to tangible energetic architectures of the brain. Specifically, a multiple linear-regression model was constructed to examine the associations between MDD-related alterations in TCE and multiscale energy representations, including mitochondrial distributions and functions, energy metabolic pathways, and macroscale brain metabolic patterns. The mitochondrial phenotypes explained 27.63% of the variance in MDD-related alterations in the TCE (F(6, 393) = 25.01, P < 2.2e-16, adjusted R2 = 0.27). Among them, mitochondrial respiratory capacity (relative contribution = 48.1%, PFDR < 0.001) and tissue respiratory capacity (20.1%, PFDR = 0.006) exhibited higher relative importance for case-control differences in the model (Fig. 5A, Table S3). The energy pathways explained 18.87% of the variance in the MDD-related alterations in TCE (F(5, 394) = 18.33, P = 2.3e-16, adjusted R2 = 0.18), with tricarboxylic acid cycle (36.9%, PFDR = 0.025) and lactate (23.7%, PFDR = 0.028) exhibiting higher relative importance for predicting the case-control differences of the TCE (Fig. 5B, Table S4). Linking MDD-related energy demands to neurophysiological oscillations

Given the fundamental dependence of neurophysiology on energetic metabolism, we examined whether MDD-related energy demands correlated with cortical dynamics. Using MEG power spectra across six frequency bands, we found that neural oscillations explained 21.04% of the variance in MDD-related alterations in the TCE (F(6, 393) = 17.46, P < 2.2e-16, adjusted R2 = 0.20), with model predictions matching observed case-control differences (Fig. 6a). The alpha power (relative contribution = 23.8%, PFDR = 0.042) and theta power (37.1%, PFDR = 0.002) showed higher contributions in the model (Table S6). Reproducibility of MDD-related alterations in energy demands

We validated MDD-related alterations in energy demands using an independent cohort. Both MDD patients and HCs showed spatially heterogeneous energy-demand patterns, consistent with those in the discovery cohort. Higher demands were observed in the posterior cingulate, medial prefrontal, and temporal cortices, while specific limbic regions exhibited lower demands (Fig. S6A). Case-control analysis confirmed significantly higher whole-brain energy demands in MDD patients (Cohen’s d = 0.24, P = 0.03, Fig. S6B). Nodal-level analyses reproduced spatial patterns from the discovery cohort (R = 0.33, Pspin < 0.001), with consistent hyper-energetic demands in the medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortices and hypo-energetic demands in limbic regions (Fig. S6C).

Moreover, we found that the observed case-control differences in energy demands were robust and independent of medication status (Fig. S7). These results suggested robust, reproducible disease-specific disruptions in energetic constraints during brain-state transitions.

Discussion

Using NCT, this study investigated brain dynamics constrained by structural architecture to identify the energetic basis of pathological state transitions in MDD patients. Our findings revealed moderately replicable dysregulated network flexibilities in MDD patients, manifested as increased intra-state transitions and decreased inter-state transitions. Altered energy demands in regions such as the cingulate cortex and temporal pole were linked to deficits in memory and emotional processing, as well as to core clinical symptoms. Further analyses established associations between MDD-related energy inefficiencies and mitochondrial phenotypes, energy metabolic pathways, macroscale metabolic profiles, and cortical dynamics, showing that energetic dysregulation was responsible for impaired neurophysiological activity in MDD patients. This study established the energetic basis of aberrant state transitions in MDD patients, which advanced our understanding of MDD as a fundamental disorder of brain dynamics.

NCT has advanced our understanding of dysregulated state transitions in MDD [7, 8, 22]. Previous studies predominantly emphasized disruptions in structural connectivity; this topological perspective overlooked the fundamental roles of regional neuronal excitability, metabolic capacity, and local circuit properties [24]. By incorporating a morphological network, we highlighted the role of gray matter in aberrant brain dynamics in MDD. The results showed more intra-network transitions and fewer inter-network transitions in MDD patients, especially in DMN and LIM networks, which were potentially associated with disrupted memory and emotion regulations in depression [47–49]. Furthermore, MDD patients exhibited elevated intra-hierarchy transition probabilities consistent with impaired balance between functional integration and segregation during depression [50, 51]. Overall, these findings showed that brain state dysregulation was a fundamental pathological characteristic of MDD, manifested by dysregulated network flexibility and impaired hierarchical reconfiguration.

The human brain operates as a high-energy system, demanding constant metabolic support for cognitive and behavioral functions [31, 52, 53]. Using TCE, we quantified region-specific energetic demands governing spontaneous brain dynamics. At the whole-brain level, MDD patients showed elevated energy demands, indicating constrained dynamic repertoires and greater reliance on external control to sustain baseline neural activity [16, 54]. Regionally, MDD patients exhibited elevated energy demands in the prefrontal and posterior cingulate regions, with reduced energy demands in the temporal poles. As key nodes of the DMN and LIM, these regions exhibit structural, functional, and controllability abnormalities in MDD patients [55, 56]. These deficits are implicated in impaired cognitive processes such as memory and emotion regulation [6, 55, 57, 58]. In addition, identified energy demand profiles predicted the severity of depression and anxiety symptoms, consistent with prior evidence associating impaired brain controllability with clinical symptoms in MDD patients [9, 59].

As a statistical parameter, the neurobiological basis of control energy still needs to be fully elucidated. Our findings linked MDD-related energetic inefficiencies to multiscale energy architectures, including mitochondrial phenotypes, energy pathways, and brain metabolic profiles. Mitochondria regulate energy production through respiratory capacity, supporting neuronal function [60, 61], while mitochondrial dysfunction represents a key pathological component of MDD [62–64]. Genetically regulated metabolic pathways further modulate energy supply, with the TCA cycle serving a dual role in energy generation and biosynthetic support [65]. Lactate, a central metabolic intermediate, enters the TCA via pyruvate conversion [66], and its dysregulation in MDD has been linked to stress sensitivity and the risk of suicide [67–69]. Building on established relationships between control energy and cerebral glucose/oxygen metabolism [19, 70], our study linked brain state transition demands to glycolytic activity, providing a neurophysiological basis for control energy. Together, these results established MDD as a disorder of multiscale energy dysregulation, spanning molecular, cellular, and systems levels.

Neurophysiological processes, such as synaptic transmission and maintenance of membrane potential, are key energy-consuming processes that sustain neuronal excitability and function [32, 71]. Our results revealed concordant alterations in energy demands and neurophysiological oscillations across multiple temporal scales. These oscillatory rhythms reflected cyclic variations in neuronal excitability, and were a direct electrophysiological signature of cortical dynamics [44]. Electrophysiological evidence revealed that MDD patients showed distinct perturbations in alpha and theta rhythms, which were linked to affective dysregulation, greater depressive symptom severity, and a heightened risk of suicide [72, 73]. Consequently, energy inefficiency in MDD patients may have compromised neural excitability and function, resulting in pervasive cognitive and emotional deficits.

Several limitations of the present study warrant consideration. Similar to previous studies, our neural dynamics model relied on linear time-invariant assumptions [15, 16, 74, 75]. Brain dynamics are nonlinear, yet simplified models provide useful first-order approximations. In fact, they also outperform specific complex nonlinear frameworks in predicting macroscopic brain activity [3, 76, 77]. Future work incorporating nonlinear dynamics may more fully capture MDD-specific pathophysiological signatures [78, 79]. Second, the power spectral data and the multiscale energy architecture are publicly available as population-level rather than participant-specific data. As such, these preliminary cross-cohort findings warrant cautious interpretation and further validation using multimodal datasets in future studies. Finally, this study is limited to cortical control properties and does not encompass subcortical regions that are pivotal to depression pathophysiology. Multimodal integration of cortical and subcortical data is essential for a more comprehensive understanding of state-transition dysregulation and energy deficit in MDD.

Conclusion

This study identified the critical role of energetic dysregulation in shaping aberrant brain dynamics in MDD. We demonstrated elevated energetic demands and atypical temporal patterns of brain states in MDD patients, showing significant associations with cognitive impairments and clinical symptom severities. Furthermore, we linked MDD-related energy inefficiencies to mitochondrial phenotypes, energy metabolic pathways, macroscale metabolic patterns, and cortical dynamics. Collectively, these findings established network controllability-derived biomarkers of aberrant brain dynamics in MDD, elucidating the fundamental role of energetic dysregulation in disease pathogenesis.
