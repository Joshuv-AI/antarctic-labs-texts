BeanGPT: a domain-specific retrieval-augmented generation system for Phaseolus vulgaris research
Authors: Kiarash Mirkamandari, Mohsen Yoosefzadeh-Najafabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BeanGPT is a domain-specific retrieval augmented generation system designed to support research and breeding decisions in common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) by transforming natural language questions into citation-backed, verifiable answers. The platform integrates a large, curated corpus of legume-focused peer-reviewed literature with structured multi-year agronomic trial records collected across diverse environments, climate projections extending to 2090 under multiple emission scenarios, and standardized cultivar nomenclature to resolve naming inconsistencies across datasets and publications. BeanGPT combines semantic retrieval from a vector database with intent-based query routing and structured parameter extraction to direct questions to genetics, field performance analytics, or climate modules. To reduce errors that commonly occur in general-purpose language models, BeanGPT incorporates a genomic index that enables constant time membership lookup of gene and protein identifiers against authoritative resources, ensuring that molecular entities are either validated or clearly flagged as literature-derived. The system is implemented with a streaming web interface and an asynchronous backend that supports concurrent users and can generate interactive visualizations through automated Plotly code generation. Beta testing demonstrated strong retrieval relevance, low response latency, reliable gene verification, and high citation precision, indicating that domain-grounded RAG can improve accuracy and usability for Phaseolus vulgaris research.

Introduction

The integration of artificial intelligence into agricultural research has accelerated considerably in recent years, yet significant challenges remain in developing domain-specific tools that can effectively bridge the gap between vast scientific literature and practical breeding decisions [1]. Common bean (Phaseolus vulgaris L.) represents one of the most important legume crops globally, serving as a primary protein source for millions of people and contributing substantially to agricultural economies [2]. However, researchers and breeders working with this crop face considerable obstacles in navigating the extensive and rapidly growing body of scientific knowledge, which encompasses genomic data, agronomic trials, climate projections, and molecular biology research spanning multiple decades.

General-purpose large language models (LLMs), while demonstrating impressive capabilities across diverse tasks, exhibit significant limitations when applied to specialized scientific domains [3]. These models often lack access to domain-specific knowledge, produce outputs that cannot be verified against authoritative sources, and frequently generate hallucinations, plausible-sounding but factually incorrect statements [3]. Such limitations are particularly problematic in agricultural research, where decisions based on inaccurate information can have substantial economic and food security implications.

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as a promising approach [4, 5] to address these challenges by combining the generative capabilities of LLMs with targeted information retrieval from curated knowledge bases. RAG systems ground model outputs in retrieved evidence [6], thereby reducing hallucinations and enabling models to access information beyond their training data [7]. However, the effectiveness of RAG systems depends critically on the quality of the underlying knowledge base, the retrieval mechanism, and the integration of domain-specific validation methods.

This paper presents BeanGPT, a comprehensive AI system specifically designed for Phaseolus vulgaris research that addresses these challenges through several key innovations: (1) a curated corpus of over 300,000 research papers specific to legume, (2) integration of structured agronomic trial data and climate projections, (3) a genomic index with O(1) lookup of gene identifiers, (4) intelligent query routing based on semantic classification, and (5) dynamic visualization capabilities for data analysis. Unlike general-purpose conversational AI systems, BeanGPT is engineered to provide factually grounded, citation-backed responses that integrate diverse data types while maintaining computational efficiency through an asynchronous processing architecture [8].

The primary contributions of this work include: (1) Architecture and Design: A scalable, domain-specific RAG system that integrates literature retrieval, structured data querying, genomic validation, and response generation within a unified framework optimized for concurrent user requests through asynchronous processing, (2) Curated Knowledge Base: A comprehensive corpus encompassing 300,000 peer-reviewed publications on legumes, 4,000 agronomic trial records spanning 11 years, climate projections through 2090 [9], and standardized cultivar nomenclature, (3) Genomic Index: An efficient O(1) membership lookup system for gene identifiers against NCBI Gene and UniProt databases, with hierarchical fallback mechanisms to ensure factual accuracy, (4) Intelligent Query Processing: A semantic classification pipeline that routes queries to specialized modules (genetics, agronomic data, or climate prediction) using deterministic GPT-4o classification [10], and (5) Multi-Modal Output Generation: Integration of text generation with dynamic data visualization through automated Plotly code generation, enabling interactive exploration of trial data and trends. This system represents a significant advancement in domain-specific AI applications for agriculture, demonstrating how RAG architecture can be adapted to meet the specialized requirements of plant breeding and genomics research while maintaining computational efficiency and factual accuracy.

Materials and methods

System architecture and design philosophy

BeanGPT is architected as a modular, scalable system designed to handle concurrent user requests while maintaining low latency and high accuracy. The system comprises three primary layers: a presentation layer (frontend), an application layer (backend API), and a data layer (databases and knowledge sources). This separation of concern enables independent scaling of components and facilitates maintenance and feature additions.

Frontend architecture

The frontend is implemented using React, a component-based JavaScript library that enables efficient rendering and state management. The interface provides reusable components for handling diverse data types, including chat messages, interactive visualizations, citation displays, and user feedback forms. Component architecture follows modern web development best practices, with separation between presentational and container components to maximize code reusability.

The frontend communicates with the backend through Server-Sent Events (SSE) rather than traditional HTTP request-response patterns. SSE enables unidirectional, real-time data streaming from server to client over a single persistent HTTP connection. This approach is particularly well-suited for applications requiring live updates without bidirectional communication, such as streaming text generation from language models. SSE provides several advantages: automatic reconnection if the connection drops, efficient resource usage by eliminating repeated polling, and reduced network traffic compared to WebSocket implementations where full duplex communication is unnecessary.

The SSE implementation uses the browser’s native EventSource API with message handling structured as:

const eventSource = new EventSource(‘/api/stream’); eventSource.onmessage = (event) => { const data = JSON.parse(event.data) // Update UI with streamed response};

This architecture enables BeanGPT to provide responsive, real-time feedback as responses are generated, enhancing user experience, particularly for long-form responses that may require several seconds to complete.

Backend architecture

The backend is built using FastAPI, a modern Python web framework designed for high-performance asynchronous applications. FastAPI’s key advantage lies in its native support for asynchronous request handling through Python’s async/await syntax, which is crucial for applications that make multiple I/O-bound operations such as database queries, external API calls, and model inference. BeanGPT is currently hosted on AWS Lightsail at beangpt.ca using a $7/month instance (1 GB RAM, 2 vCPUs, 40 GB SSD, 2 TB transfer) with Ubuntu 22.04 LTS. The Lightsail instance handles the FastAPI and React Frontend, while the heavier workloads such as embedding generation, language model inference, and vector search are offloaded to OpenAI and Zilliz Cloud respectively.

When multiple users query BeanGPT simultaneously, the asynchronous architecture enables the server to handle concurrent requests efficiently. While waiting for I/O operations (e.g., vector database retrieval, LLM API calls) to complete, the event loop can process other requests, dramatically improving throughput compared to synchronous implementations. Benchmarks of similar FastAPI applications have demonstrated request-per-second (RPS) improvements of up to 651 RPS with asynchronous configurations compared to ~ 370 RPS for synchronous implementations [8].

The FastAPI application is structured around several specialized microservices that handle distinct functions: (1) Literature Retrieval Service: Interfaces with the Zilliz vector database to retrieve relevant research papers, (2) Agronomic Data Service: Queries structured trial data and climate projections, (3) Genomic Validation Service: Validates gene references against NCBI and UniProt databases, (4) Generation Service: Manages interactions with OpenAI’s GPT-4o models, and (5) Visualization Service: Generates executable Python code for creating interactive plots. Each microservice operates semi-independently but communicates through a central orchestration layer that coordinates the overall query processing workflow. This tiered structure allows components to be modified or replaced without affecting the entire system, facilitating iterative development and testing.

Data flow and integration

Data flows through BeanGPT via an orchestrated, end-to-end pipeline designed to minimize latency while preserving response accuracy, as illustrated in Fig. 1. User queries are first received through the React-based frontend and transmitted to the FastAPI backend using server-sent events (SSE), after which a GPT-4o model with Temperature = 0 performs intent classification, assigning each query to one of three categories: genetics, bean data, or climate prediction. Based on this classification, the system retrieves relevant information from the appropriate knowledge source, which may include a vector database, a structured SQL database, or integrated web search. The retrieved materials are then assembled into a consolidated context block of approximately 2,000 to 5,000 words, providing sufficient evidence for grounded response generation. For genetics-related queries, candidate gene references within this context are validated using an O(1) hash-map lookup system before generation. GPT-4o then produces the final response explicitly grounded in the assembled context, which is streamed back to the frontend in real time via SSE as it is generated.

When a query requests data visualization, the system additionally generates executable Plotly code, executes it securely on the server, and transmits the resulting chart object to the frontend for interactive rendering. This pipeline is optimized to handle the heterogeneous nature of biological data, from unstructured text in research papers to structured numerical data in trial records, while providing a unified interface to end users.

Corpus collection and data integration

The effectiveness of any RAG system depends critically on the quality, comprehensiveness, and organization of its underlying knowledge base. BeanGPT’s knowledge base comprises two complementary components: unstructured literature and structured agronomic datasets.

Unstructured literature retrieval

The literature corpus spans over 300,000 peer-reviewed research papers focused on legumes, with a primary focus on Phaseolus vulgaris. The inclusion of broader legume research ensures coverage of comparative genomic and agronomic studies that are directly relevant to common bean. This targeted scope ensures that retrieved context is relevant to bean research while maintaining manageable computational requirements for embedding generation and storage.

Web of science pipeline

The first data collection pipeline interfaces with the Web of Science Core Collection, a curated multidisciplinary citation database maintained by Clarivate that indexes peer-reviewed journals, conferences, and books across 250 research areas [11]. Web of Science was selected due to its rigorous editorial selection process, which evaluates publications against transparent quality criteria, ensuring that included sources meet high standards of scholarly rigor. The pipeline uses Selenium WebDriver, a browser automation tool, to programmatically navigate the Web of Science interface and download publications. Selenium is particularly well-suited for this task because Web of Science employs JavaScript-based rendering that is not accessible through simple HTTP requests. The automation workflow follows steps: (1) Initialize ChromeDriver with appropriate options (headless mode, user-agent specification), (2) Navigate to Web of Science and perform authenticated login to access search functionality and retrieve article metadata including DOIs, titles, authors, and abstracts; full text content was obtained exclusively through open-access sources and no paywalled content was downloaded, (3) Execute search queries targeting Phaseolus vulgaris across titles, abstracts, and keywords, (4) For each result, extract metadata (DOI, publication year, authors, journal) and download PDF/HTML full text when available, and (5) Store metadata in structured JSON format with schema:

{“doi”: “10.xxxx/xxxxx”, “title”: “…”, “authors”: [“Author A”, “Author B”], “year”: 2023, “journal”: “…”, “full_text”: “…”, “file_path”: “/path/to/pdf”}

The Selenium pipeline implements retry logic to handle transient network errors, and respects rate limiting to avoid overwhelming the Web of Science servers. BeautifulSoup, a Python HTML parsing library, is employed to extract structured information from downloaded HTML content.

PubMed pipeline

The second pipeline queries PubMed, the U.S. National Library of Medicine’s database of biomedical literature containing over 36 million citations [12]. PubMed provides programmatic access through E-utilities APIs, enabling efficient batch retrieval of citations and abstracts. The PubMed pipeline uses the following workflow: (1) Construct search queries using PubMed’s query language: “Phaseolus vulgaris“[All Fields], (2) Use ESearch API to retrieve PMIDs matching the query:

A critical challenge in historical literature collection is that older publications often lack digital full-text versions. To address this, the pipeline implements a fallback strategy: when full text is unavailable, abstracts are retrieved and indexed to ensure comprehensive temporal coverage of research from early studies through contemporary publications. Both pipelines maintain referential integrity by preserving DOIs, which serve as persistent identifiers enabling users to verify original sources. This citation infrastructure is essential for scientific applications where claims must be traceable to authoritative sources.

Structured agronomic and climate datasets

Beyond literature, BeanGPT integrates three structured datasets that enable quantitative analysis and evidence-based recommendations for breeding decisions.

Ontario bean trial data

This dataset comprises approximately 4,000 entries documenting variety performance trials conducted across six locations in Ontario, Canada, from 2011 to 2022. Trial locations include Elora, Woodstock, St. Thomas, Kippen, Granton, and Brussels, representing diverse soil types and microclimates within Ontario’s bean-growing region [24].

Variety Information: including Cultivar name, market class (e.g., navy, kidney, cranberry), breeder identification,

Agronomic Traits: such as Yield (kg/ha), seed weight (g/100 seeds), days to maturity, plant height (cm), lodging score, disease resistance ratings, and

Trial Metadata: including Location, year, replicate number, plot characteristics. The trial data enables BeanGPT to answer queries such as “What was the average yield of navy beans in Woodstock from 2015-2020?” or “Which varieties show consistent lodging resistance across all trial locations?”

Such questions require structured query capabilities beyond what can be achieved through text retrieval alone.

Climate projection data

Climate change represents a critical consideration for long-term breeding strategy. BeanGPT incorporates climate projections from the CMIP6 model ensemble spanning 1980–2090 for each trial location under three Representative Concentration Pathway (RCP) scenarios: RCP 2.6 (stringent mitigation), RCP 4.5 (intermediate), and RCP 8.5 (high emissions). RCP 8.5 has recently been questioned as an implausible emissions trajectory, and future versions of BeanGPT should incorporate updated climate scenarios as they become available.

For each location, decade, and RCP scenario, projections are organized into three categories:

Temperature Metrics: Maximum temperature (°C), minimum temperature (°C), and growing degree days, collectively describing the thermal environment experienced by bean crops across growth stages.

Precipitation Metrics: Total precipitation (mm) and precipitation during critical growth phases.

Extreme Events: Frequency of frost events, heat stress days (>30°C), and drought index, which captures the incidence of conditions known to cause yield loss or crops failure.

These projections enable breeders to assess whether varieties currently performing well will remain adapted under future climate conditions. For example, a query like “How will precipitation change in Brussels under RCP 4.5 by 2050-2060?” can inform decisions about selecting for drought tolerance traits.

Cultivar nomenclature database

A persistent challenge in breeding record-keeping is inconsistent naming conventions, where the same genetic material may be referred to by different names across time, locations, and institutions. BeanGPT addresses this through a curated database linking historical breeding codes, previous variety names, and current market names using the format “NewName(PreviousName)”.

For example, a variety might be recorded as:

Market Name: AC Compass

Previous Names: OAC Rex, OT94-42

Breeder Code: OT1994-042

This nomenclature standardization enables accurate retrieval of performance data and genetic information regardless of which naming convention users employ in their queries.

Vectorization and embedding strategy

The core of BeanGPT’s retrieval mechanism relies on transforming textual information into high-dimensional vector representations that capture semantic meaning. This section describes the embedding model selection, vectorization process, storage infrastructure, and retrieval methodology.

Embedding model selection

BeanGPT employs OpenAI’s text-embedding-3-large model to generate semantic representations, producing 1536-dimensional embedding vectors that are reduced from the model’s native 3072 dimensions using Matryoshka Representation Learning. This model was selected for its strong empirical performance, achieving 64.6% on the Massive Text Embedding Benchmark and 54.9% on the Multilingual Information Retrieval Across a Continuum of Languages benchmark, representing a substantial improvement over earlier models such as text-embedding-ada-002, which achieved 61.0% [13] and 31.4% [13] on the same benchmarks, respectively. Although BeanGPT primarily targets English-language research, the model’s robust multilingual capabilities ensure effective representation of Spanish and Portuguese literature that is common in Latin American bean research. The model also supports flexible dimensionality reduction to 1536, 1024, 512, or 256 dimensions with minimal loss in semantic fidelity, and BeanGPT adopts the 1536-dimensional configuration to reduce storage requirements by approximately 50% while retaining more than 95% of the model’s semantic capacity. In addition, the model supports input lengths of up to 8191 tokens [14], allowing efficient embedding of long abstracts and multi-paragraph scientific texts. Vector database storage and indexing

Embeddings in BeanGPT are stored in Zilliz, a fully managed vector database built on the open-source Milvus platform, selected for its strong balance of performance, scalability, and feature richness. Zilliz provides sub-10 ms search latency [16] for billion-scale datasets through optimized approximate nearest neighbor algorithms, and in the context of BeanGPT, the 300,000-vector index consistently achieves query latencies below 5 ms. The platform supports multiple indexing strategies, including HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World), IVF (Inverted File), and DiskANN, with BeanGPT specifically using HNSW configured with M = 16, which defines the number of connections per node, and efConstruction = 200, which controls search depth during index construction. In this approach, vectors are organized into a multi-layer graph where nodes represent embeddings and edges link nearby neighbors, yielding an approximate search complexity of O(logN), where N is the database size, and enabling efficient scaling as the corpus grows [17]. Zilliz also supports cosine similarity, inner product, and Euclidean distance, with BeanGPT relying on cosine similarity due to its scale invariance and favorable computational properties for high-dimensional semantic embeddings. BeanGPT uses k=8 based on empirical testing, which produces context blocks of 2000–5000 words. This range provides sufficient information to ground responses while remaining within GPT-4o’s context window (128,000 tokens) and minimizing latency.

Genomic index and O(1) validation

A critical challenge in applying LLMs to molecular biology is their tendency to hallucinate gene names and functions. Even when LLMs generate plausible-sounding gene identifiers, these may not correspond to actual genes or may conflate genes from different organisms. BeanGPT addresses this through a genomic index that enables O(1) membership lookup of gene identifiers against authoritative databases.

Data sources

NCBI gene database

The National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) maintains a comprehensive gene database with 16,326 species, including Phaseolus vulgaris [18]. Each gene entry contains: - Official gene symbol (e.g., Phvul.001G189200) - Synonyms and alternative names - Chromosomal location (chromosome 1, position 45,123,456) - Functional description - Cross-references to sequence databases, publications, and pathways NCBI Gene data for Phaseolus vulgaris encompasses several thousand annotated genes derived from the reference genome assembly (P. vulgaris v2.0, GenBank: GCF_000499845.2) [19].

UniProt protein database

UniProt provides curated protein sequence and functional information for proteins across all organisms. The UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB) contains two sections: (1) Swiss-Prot: Manually curated, high-quality annotations, and (2) TrEMBL: Automatically annotated entries from genomic and transcriptomic data [20]. For Phaseolus vulgaris, UniProt contains thousands of protein entries with sequence data, domain annotations, post-translational modifications, and functional characterizations.

Hierarchical validation logic

When BeanGPT generates a response that may contain gene references, these entities are subjected to a hierarchical validation process to ensure biological accuracy and appropriate citation. In the first stage, Tier 1 – NCBI Gene Validation, candidate gene names are extracted using pattern matching rules such as identifiers resembling Phvul.XXX or italicized gene symbols, after which the system queries internal gene_id_map and symbol_map resources; when a match is found, complete gene metadata are retrieved and incorporated into the response with a citation to NCBI. For candidates not resolved at this level, Tier 2 – UniProt Protein Validation is applied, whereby the system queries the UniProt database using gene names, protein names, and known synonyms, and if a valid match is identified, the corresponding protein information is added with proper UniProt citation. Finally, for genes that appear in the literature but are absent from primary databases, such as newly described loci or provisional gene designations, Tier 3 – Literature-Based Definition is invoked, in which GPT-4o Mini is used with Temperature = 0.3 and a constrained prompt to generate a definition grounded explicitly in the cited literature, ensuring transparency about the evidence source and preventing unsupported gene hallucination while maintaining coverage of emerging scientific findings:

Given the gene name “{gene_name}” appearing in context about Phaseolus vulgaris, provide a concise 2–3 sentence definition of this gene/protein based solely on information from the retrieved scientific literature If no reliable information exists, state “No validated information available.”

This tiered approach ensures that every gene mentioned in BeanGPT responses is either (1) validated against authoritative databases with explicit citations, or (2) clearly marked as preliminary/literature-only information. The system never allows unvalidated gene references to appear without qualification, thereby preventing hallucinations in this critical domain. The model returns a single-word classification, which directs subsequent processing. The temperature = 0 setting ensures deterministic output, meaning identical queries always receive the same classification. This determinism is critical for system reliability and debugging.

GPT-4o’s strong zero-shot classification capabilities, derived from its extensive pre-training on diverse text, enable accurate intent detection without requiring fine-tuning on domain-specific classification datasets. Empirical testing during development showed > 95% classification accuracy on manually labelled test queries.

With strict mode enabled, OpenAI’s function calling ensures 100% compliance with the specified JSON schema, automatically retrying generation if the output doesn’t match. This guarantees that extracted parameters are type-safe and conform to expected formats.

Web search integration

For queries that require up-to-date information not available in the internal knowledge base, such as “What are recent market trends for navy beans?”, BeanGPT integrates OpenAI’s web search functionality by reformulating the user query to optimize it for web retrieval, executing the search through OpenAI’s Responses API [21], retrieving and processing the top-ranked results, and synthesizing web-sourced information directly into the generated answer while explicitly distinguishing web-based citations (e.g., [Web-1], [Web-2]) from citations derived from the curated internal database (e.g., [1, 2]); this hybrid retrieval strategy combines the depth, reliability, and domain specificity of structured research knowledge with the timeliness of real-time web information, allowing BeanGPT to effectively address both foundational scientific questions and rapidly evolving topics such as market trends and industry dynamics.

Cross-validation and model training considerations

While BeanGPT does not train custom machine learning models and instead relies on pre-trained models from OpenAI, the principles of cross-validation inform its evaluation strategy by providing a structured framework for assessing generalization across diverse query types. In this context, evaluation for a RAG system such as BeanGPT proceeds by constructing a representative query set spanning genetics, agronomic trial data, and climate projections, followed by ground-truth annotation in which expert botanists and breeders provide reference answers. These queries are then partitioned into k folds, with k − 1 folds used to tune retrieval-related parameters such as top-k selection and similarity thresholds, and the remaining fold reserved for evaluation. Performance is quantified using metrics including precision, recall, F1 score, contextual relevancy, and faithfulness, after which system parameters are iteratively adjusted and the evaluation repeated across folds. This process, analogous to cross-validation in supervised learning, ensures that BeanGPT’s performance is robust across heterogeneous query formulations and domains, reducing the risk of overfitting to specific phrasing patterns encountered during system development.

Multi-model response generation strategy

BeanGPT employs a tiered multi-model architecture designed to balance response quality with computational efficiency, using GPT-4o as the primary generation model for producing final answers with a low temperature setting of 0.2 to maintain a strong balance between determinism and fluency while explicitly grounding outputs in retrieved contextual evidence and accurate citation, and GPT-4o Mini as a rapid evaluation model for lightweight but critical tasks such as pre-generation validation of contextual sufficiency and Tier-3 gene validation based on literature definitions, where its substantially lower latency and cost make it well suited for classification, verification, and extraction workflows; together, this structured division of labor optimizes overall system cost and responsiveness without compromising the depth, reliability, or scientific rigor required for complex domain-specific queries.

User feedback integration

To enable continuous improvement, BeanGPT incorporates an integrated user feedback mechanism in which users can rate each response on a five-point scale and optionally provide free-text comments, with all feedback stored in structured JSON format alongside the original query, generated response, retrieved context, and relevant metadata; aggregated analyses of this feedback guide ongoing system refinement by highlighting low-rated outputs that signal weaknesses in retrieval or generation, recurring comments that reveal missing features or data gaps, and consistently high-rated responses that validate effective interaction and reasoning patterns, thereby transforming BeanGPT from a static application into an adaptive system that evolves in response to user needs over time.

Information retrieval accuracy

In a test set of 100 diverse queries spanning genetics, agronomy, and climate topics, constructed by the authors and beta users to reflect realistic research questions across BeanGPT’s three modules. BeanGPT’s retrieval module successfully returned at least one highly relevant document in the top-8 results for 95% of queries (95% Confidence Interval, CI: 88.7–98.4%). In 90% of cases, the top-1 result was deemed relevant (95% CI: 82.4–95.1%) to answer the question. For example, for the query “drought tolerance QTL in common bean”, the top results included a 2013 genetics paper on drought QTL and a 2019 study on physiological traits under drought, both directly useful in framing the answer. The mean cosine similarity of retrieved relevant documents was 0.88 (on a [0,1] scale), indicating strong semantic matches.

Response time

End-to-end query response time averaged 4.8 s for typical questions (with an 8-document context and a few-sentence answer). More complex queries involving on-the-fly visualization or web search fallback averaged ~ 8–10 s due to the additional processing. The vector search itself is very fast (sub-100 ms for each query on the Zilliz cloud database), and the majority of latency comes from the LLM generating the answer (2–4 s) and any required API calls (e.g. NCBI gene lookup or Plotly image rendering). Through FastAPI’s async handling, the system achieved concurrent response handling with negligible slowdown up to 20 simultaneous users. These results suggest the system can support real-time interactive use in a research workshop or breeding team meeting setting. Users receive a streaming answer (tokens arriving progressively), so even longer answers begin appearing within ~ 2 s, improving the perceived responsiveness for the user.

Genomic verification success

We assessed the gene name verification pipeline on a set of 50 queries that required mentioning specific genes or markers (e.g. disease resistance genes, nutrient uptake genes). BeanGPT’s multi-tier gene check correctly recognized and verified 100% of the 120 gene mentions across those answers. Of these, 93% were found directly in the NCBI Gene database (hence annotated with the official gene info), and 7% (mainly newly reported genes from very recent publications) triggered the GPT-4o mini fallback. In all cases, the system provided a valid description or clarification for the gene, with no instances of an unchecked hallucinated gene name. This demonstrates that the O(1) membership lookup approach effectively filters the LLM output for unrecognized gene identifiers before they reach a final response. For instance, in an answer about nitrogen fixation genes, BeanGPT correctly identified NF-Y as a transcription factor in bean nodulation and cited the gene’s UniProt entry, whereas a generic model might have invented a plausible-sounding but nonexistent gene. The current evaluation focused on realistic research queries; future work will include more diverse testing with non-existent gene names and cross-species identifiers, alongside standardized benchmarking through frameworks such as RAGAS.

Citation precision

BeanGPT’s answers are accompanied by in-text citations. Manual evaluation of 50 answers found that 97% of citations pointed to a source that directly supported the associated claim (95% CI: 87.4–99.6%). This high precision is a byproduct of the RAG approach: because the context is drawn from relevant literature, the model typically quotes or closely paraphrases those sources. In a few cases (3%), a citation was only loosely related (e.g. pointing to a general background source rather than the specific fact); these will be addressed by further prompt tuning and expanding the context window to include more granular snippets. Importantly, no completely spurious references (fabricated titles or authors) were observed in the evaluated answers, a common issue in naive LLM responses, thanks to the curated reference retrieval.

User testing and feedback

We gathered informal feedback from beta users (including 2 breeders, 5 agronomy researchers, and 10 graduate students) who interacted with BeanGPT. Overall, the response was very positive: users appreciated the convenience of a single interface where they could ask questions in plain English and get answers with both data and literature support. Breeders reported that BeanGPT was especially useful in quickly retrieving information on disease resistance and ancestry of breeding lines. For example, instead of combing through papers, a breeder could ask “Which bean varieties carry the bc-3 gene for BCMV resistance?” and get an answer listing the cultivars and citing relevant breeding reports.

As a demonstration, we present a yield trend analysis case study that a breeder might perform. The system recognizes the need for a plot of yield vs. year. It filters the Ontario trial data for navy bean entries at Elora from 2011 to 2021 and computes the average yield per year. The GPT-4 model then generates Python code to plot this trend. The resulting line chart showed an upward trend in navy bean yield, from roughly 2.6 tons/ha in 2011 to about 3.0 tons/ha in 2021 (with some year-to-year fluctuations). BeanGPT’s textual answer described the trend, e.g.: “Navy bean yields in Elora have increased from ~ 2.6 t/ha in 2011 to ~ 3.0 t/ha in 2021, an improvement of about 15% over 10 years, likely due to genetic gains and agronomic improvements.” The answer cited trial reports confirming this yield improvement. For instance, a reference from the 2020 trial bulletin might be cited as evidence of recent high yields. This case illustrates how BeanGPT can quickly turn raw data into an interpretable insight. A breeder could further ask, “Which navy cultivar contributed most to yield gains?”, and BeanGPT could delve into cultivar-specific trends (perhaps identifying a high-performing variety that was introduced mid-decade).

Comparative analysis

BeanGPT vs. general-purpose LLM (GPT-4)

To compare BeanGPT against a general-purpose LLM, 150 queries covering genetics, agronomic data, and climate topics were run through both BeanGPT and GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613). On 50 genetics queries, GPT-4 provided gene identifiers without validation against authoritative databases, including fabricated allele names (Co-x, Co-y, Co-z), a hypothetical gene ID (Phvul.001G123456) presented without qualification, and cross-species misattribution of resistance loci, with at least 14 of 50 responses containing unvalidated or incorrectly attributed identifiers. BeanGPT produced 0 unvalidated gene mentions across the same queries, with all unrecognized identifiers explicitly flagged. For agronomic queries, GPT-4 returned only general knowledge in all 50 cases, while BeanGPT retrieved quantitative cultivar-level data directly from the Ontario trial dataset. For climate queries, GPT-4 provided only generic RCP descriptions with no location-specific projections, while BeanGPT returned available weather station data for Ontario trial locations. Overall, the results demonstrate a clear advantage of BeanGPT’s retrieval and genomic validation approach over general-purpose LLMs for common bean research.

BeanGPT vs. keyword search (PubMed, Google Scholar)

Compared to the keyword-driven search engines like PubMed or Google Scholar, BeanGPT enables semantic searching that identifies relevant publications even without the use of the same terms synthesizing the information acquired from different sources, rather than listing various documents. This reduces the burden on users who would need to otherwise manually read and integrate the findings from numerous papers.

BeanGPT vs. domain RAG (AgriGPT [22])

Finally, while both domain-specific RAG models answer with groundings, BeanGPT’s focus on Phaseolus vulgaris and incorporation of agronomic trial data and climate projections enable it to respond to quantitative performance and adaptation questions, which are beyond the capabilities of RAG models restricted to literature alone. Altogether, BeanGPT is a complimentary tool to existing databases and language models, optimized for accuracy, traceability, and support in common bean research.

Limitations

BeanGPT demonstrates several key innovations that advance the state of domain-specific AI applications in agriculture. By combining unstructured literature, structured trial data, climate projections, and genomic databases within a unified system, BeanGPT addresses the heterogeneous information needs of breeding programs. A hierarchical validation system with hash-map indexing represents a specialized approach to reducing hallucinations in specialized domains. By maintaining in-memory indices of authoritative databases, BeanGPT validates references in microseconds without the latency of database API calls. This architecture could be generalized to other domains requiring factual grounding in structured knowledge bases (e.g., chemical databases for drug discovery, taxonomic databases for ecology). The FastAPI backend with Server-Sent Events enables BeanGPT to serve multiple breeding programs simultaneously with minimal degradation in response time. This scalability is essential for institutional deployment where tens of researchers may query the system concurrently during critical decision-making periods (e.g., variety selection for upcoming growing season). Unlike many commercial AI systems that obscure source attribution, BeanGPT provides explicit citations to DOIs and database accessions, enabling users to verify claims and delve deeper into primary sources. This transparency is critical for scientific applications where traceability and reproducibility are paramount.

Despite its innovations, BeanGPT faces several limitations that warrant discussion. The system’s knowledge is bounded by its corpus. Recent publications (last 3–6 months) may not be indexed due to processing lag. Similarly, unpublished trial data or proprietary breeding records remain inaccessible unless explicitly integrated. Users must recognize that BeanGPT represents a snapshot of available knowledge at the time of corpus construction. While BeanGPT handles single-intent queries effectively, complex multi-step queries requiring reasoning across disparate information sources (e.g., “Identify varieties with high yield in Elora, drought tolerance genes, and adaptation to RCP 8.5 projections in 2050”) may exceed current capabilities. Such queries would benefit from agentic workflows where multiple specialized modules collaborate through iterative reasoning steps. Although BeanGPT grounds responses in evidence, interpreting contradictory findings across studies, assessing study quality, or providing nuanced context about experimental limitations remains challenging. The system may present conflicting results from different papers without deeply analyzing methodological differences that explain discrepancies. Expert human judgment remains essential for weighing evidence quality. While the embedding model supports multilingual text, the corpus is predominantly English-language publications. Important research published in Spanish, Portuguese, or French may be underrepresented, potentially biasing recommendations toward English-speaking research communities. Climate projections and trial data become outdated as new observations accumulate and models are refined. Maintaining currency requires establishing data update pipelines and version control mechanisms to track when information was last refreshed.

BeanGPT demonstrates a approach for how domain-specific AI can augment, but not replace, human expertise in agricultural research. By making specialized knowledge accessible through natural language queries, BeanGPT lowers barriers for researchers in resource-limited institutions who may lack access to proprietary databases or extensive library subscriptions. A breeder in a developing country can query the system to access the same knowledge base as researchers at well-funded universities. Systematic literature reviews that traditionally require weeks of manual screening and synthesis can be partially automated. Researchers can rapidly identify consensus findings, knowledge gaps, and relevant methodologies, freeing time for hypothesis generation and experimental design. Integrating trial performance data with genomic information and climate projections enables data-driven variety selection. Rather than relying solely on personal experience or limited local trials, breeders can query performance across multiple locations and years, identify climatically adapted varieties for projected future conditions, and prioritize traits based on quantitative evidence. BeanGPT serves as an educational resource for training students and early-career researchers. Complex concepts in plant genetics, quantitative genetics, or climate adaptation can be explored through interactive dialogue, with the system providing curated information tailored to the learner’s knowledge level.

Future perspective

Several promising avenues exist for extending BeanGPT’s capabilities. Implementing systems where specialized modules (literature retrieval, trial analysis, genetic analysis, economic modeling) collaborate through structured communication protocols could enable more sophisticated reasoning. For example, an agent coordinator could decompose complex queries into subtasks, delegate to specialists, and synthesize results. While BeanGPT currently uses general-purpose pre-trained models, fine-tuning on domain-specific instruction datasets (query-response pairs from bean research) could improve response quality, reduce hallucinations specific to the domain, and enable more natural integration of domain jargon. Extending BeanGPT to process images (e.g., disease symptoms, seed phenotypes, field photographs) and genomic sequences (FASTA files) would enable queries like “Identify this bean disease from the leaf symptoms shown in this image” or “Predict the likely function of this gene sequence”. Current RAG systems excel at information retrieval and synthesis but struggle with causal inference. Integrating causal discovery methods could enable BeanGPT to reason about interventions (e.g., “If I select for increased seed size, what pleiotropic effects on maturity and lodging should I expect?”). Maintaining user profiles that track research interests, preferred citation styles, and knowledge level could enable personalized responses. A molecular biologist would receive different explanations than a field agronomist, even for the same query. Enabling users to contribute annotations, corrections, or additional data could transform BeanGPT into a collaborative knowledge common similar to Wikipedia, but with AI-assisted curation and quality control. The architectural patterns developed for BeanGPT, curated corpora, genomic validation, structured data integration could be adapted to other crops. A generic framework for constructing crop-specific AI systems could accelerate knowledge access across the agricultural sector.

Deployment of AI systems in agriculture raises important ethical considerations. While BeanGPT aims to democratize knowledge access, digital divides in internet connectivity and AI literacy may create new inequities. Efforts to provide offline access modes, multilingual interfaces, and training programs are essential for equitable deployment. As breeding programs increasingly rely on AI-assisted decision-making, clarity is needed regarding accountability when AI recommendations lead to poor outcomes. Users must understand that BeanGPT provides information and synthesis, but ultimate decision responsibility rests with human breeders who must weigh additional contextual factors. The computational resources required for embedding generation, vector search, and LLM inference consume energy and contribute to carbon emissions. Optimizing system efficiency and considering renewable energy sources for hosting infrastructure align with sustainability goals of agricultural research.

Conclusion

BeanGPT represents a significant advancement in domain-specific artificial intelligence for agricultural research, demonstrating how Retrieval-Augmented Generation architectures can be tailored to meet the specialized needs of plant breeding and genomics. By integrating diverse knowledge sources, peer-reviewed literature, structured trial data, climate projections, and authoritative genomic databases, within a unified, efficient system, BeanGPT enables researchers and breeders to access and synthesize information that would otherwise require extensive manual effort. The system’s key innovations include an O(1) genomic validation mechanism that reduces hallucinated gene identifiers in responses, intelligent query routing that adapts retrieval and generation strategies to query intent, and an asynchronous architecture that maintains low latency under concurrent load. These technical contributions address fundamental challenges in applying large language models to scientific domains where factual accuracy and source transparency are non-negotiable requirements. Beyond its technical contributions, BeanGPT exemplifies a approach for democratizing specialized knowledge. By transforming the vast corpus of bean research into an accessible conversational interface, the system lowers barriers for researchers in resource-limited settings and accelerates evidence synthesis for breeding decisions. As climate change intensifies pressure on agricultural systems and breeding programs seek varieties adapted to specialized conditions, tools like BeanGPT that synthesize genomic, agronomic, and environmental data will become increasingly valuable. Future work will focus on extending the system’s reasoning capabilities through agentic workflows, incorporating multimodal data types (images, sequences), and refining evaluation frameworks to ensure robust performance across diverse query types. The architectural patterns and validation strategies developed for BeanGPT provide a framework for constructing domain-specific AI systems in other agricultural contexts, potentially accelerating knowledge access across the global agricultural research community. It should be noted that the evaluation presented here is preliminary, based on a small beta sample and large scale testing with independent annotators and standardized benchmarking is required to fully validate these findings. As artificial intelligence continues to reshape scientific practice, systems like BeanGPT demonstrate that thoughtful integration of AI with domain expertise, rather than wholesale replacement of human judgment, offers the most promising path toward enhancing agricultural research and supporting evidence-based breeding decisions that address global food security challenges.
