Pulsed directed energy deposition-arc technology for depositing stainless steel 309L: Microstructural, elements distribution, and mechanical characteristics
Authors: Madesh R, Barath Kumar M D, Bala Murali N, Nandhakumar S, Arivazhagan N, Manikandan M, Gokul Kumar K
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, additive manufacturing has increased in prominence as a primary method of manufacturing around the globe. Modern metal additive manufacturing presents an innovative approach to manufacturing complex structures for the aerospace, energy, and construction sectors, due to technological advancements. In the present study, the austenitic stainless steel 309L (SS-309L) thick wall component was manufactured employing pulsed current gas tungsten arc welding (PC-GTAW) WAAM technology. The metallurgical characteristics of the as-deposited SS-309L thick wall part were evaluated. The investigation of material characteristics in the manufactured areas encompasses a study of cross-sectional (CS) and transverse-sectional (TS) regional portions, specifically the upper, middle, and lower regions. The microstructures of the CS and TS planes showed austenite, columnar, and delta ferrite dendrites in the upper, middle, and lower regions, respectively. The CS sample reveals the greatest grain size at 159.4 μm, following the EBSD investigation. The average size of the grains across the TS is 127.09 μm. The thermal cycles in multi-layer production result in changes in grain size. The microhardness in the transverse sectional regions is higher than in the CS regions, measuring average values of 257 HV, 253 HV, and 251 HV for the upper, middle, and lower sections, respectively. The ultimate tensile strengths (UTS) in the cross-sectional parts are 656 MPa, whereas in the transverse regions it was 661 MPa for the topmost regions. This study investigates the correlation among the mechanical and metallurgical properties of wire arc additively fabricated SS-309L austenite steel material.Keywords: Additive manufacturing, WAAM, Welding, Microstructure, Mechanical properties

Introduction

A highly alluring new production technique, metal additive manufacturing (MAM) has several potential uses in the petrochemical, automotive, and aerospace industries, among other sectors [1]. One major benefit of AM is the reduction in manufacturing time and material waste. Advancements in additive printing technologies have enabled the direct production of finished objects. The MAM technology can be classified into three types based on its power source: (i) laser source, (ii) electron beam, and (iii) electric arc [2]. Using an electric arc as a heat source, the wire arc additive manufacturing (WAAM) process melts and deposits filler wire layer by layer to produce a net near-shape component [3]. WAAM is a specific type of additive manufacturing that effectively uses arc (gas metal arc, gas tungsten arc, or plasma arc) as a heating element and a filler wire as the input material [4]. This process involves adding the wire layer-by-layer under the controls of a CNC system and robotic system to create components. The WAAM technology has a considerable benefit due to its high deposition rate and inexpensive material and equipment costs [5]. The manufacture of aircraft landing gear ribs using WAAM technology is a recent accomplishment in WAAM technology, saving 78 % on raw material costs when compared to other standard subtractive machining methods [6]. Currently, the WAAM technique is widely used in the aerospace, automotive, and quick tooling sectors [7]. Nevertheless, the widespread use of WAAM parts in industrial domains has been impeded by some inevitable issues such as compositional segregation, porosity faults, inconsistencies among deposited layers, and inadequate mechanical qualities [1,8]. Various research has been conducted to enhance and address challenges in the manufacturing process of WAAM [1,9]. The findings suggested that achieving a satisfactory WAAM deposition necessitated the use of a high-quality arc power source, because the heat input and behaviour of the molten pool might be affected by the pulsed pattern of the electric arc in the WAAM method [10]. The stability and precision of the arc's pulsed amplitude are crucial elements in determining metallurgical flaws and microstructures [11]. The use of a pulsating GTAW process has the potential to enhance the materials filling method by minimising spatter, improving droplet detachment, and reducing heat input [12,13]. This is achieved by using an pulsing amplitude for the electric arc and utilising a filler wire motion as a consumable electrode. Several studies have indicated that the introduction of the pulsed GTAW technique into WAAM has distinct benefits. These advantages help in controlling the quality of the formed material and enhancing its mechanical properties [14,15].

Austenitic stainless steels (SS), which consist of austenite and ferrite phases, are extensively used in many sectors as a structural material because of its exceptional mechanical capabilities, welding qualities, processing properties, and resistance to corrosion [16,17]. Within a nuclear power plant, several crucial components, including the pressurizer, reactors pressure vessel (RPV), RPV sprayers, and steam turbines, are constructed using 309L austenitic stainless steel (SS) [18]. The efficient use of the distinct separate characteristics of this metal provides enhanced synergistic qualities that are beneficial for its intended application. 309 L stainless steel has exceptional corrosion resistance and includes a specific proportion of ferrite, which aids in minimising the likelihood of welding hot cracks [19]. The presence of welding hot cracks and secondary phases significantly affects the material properties and is well-known for increasing fracturing. The use of pulsed current technology is currently attracting considerable attention due to its unique ability to minimise heat input. PAW and laser-based cladding exhibit inferior production efficiency and limited dilution control capabilities as compared to conventional welding techniques such as GMAW and GTAW [20]. Nevertheless, by the utilization of pulsed GTAW technology, it is possible to achieve metallic bonding while simultaneously reducing dilution to a minimum. This method additionally aids in preventing the enlargement of microstructures and the creation of intermetallic substances in the heat-affected zone [11]. Furthermore, pulsed gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW) allows for the creation of near-net-shape components for effective manufacturing processes, owing to the distinctive properties of current pulsing during the solidification phase. This technique enables a more precise regulation of the heat input and more control over the behaviour and solidifying of the weld pool, effectively managing the development of cracks [13]. References [21,22] indicate that the presence of both ferrite and austenite phases in stainless steel may result in fractures at the border between both phases, which is often caused by incorrect processing conditions and high heat input. Nevertheless, the existing research on 309 L stainless steel has mostly concentrated on pulsed arc based WAAM techniques, overlooking the aspect of hot working in relation to 309 L stainless steel. Hence, it is important to investigate the microstructure and mechanical characteristics of stainless steel, as well as the measures to mitigate cracking throughout the manufacturing process. Motghare et al. [23] fabricated an SS309L wall using the WAAM technique. Microstructure characteristics indicate the presence of delta ferritic, austenitic, and sigma phases in different orientations. Across the wall, various areas showed suitable adherence among the layers, characterised by full fusion, absence of oxidation, and absence of undesired geometries. The specimens' tensile strength and yield strength vary depending on their orientation position. In their study, Tongming et al. [17] found a strong correlation between material chemistries and the susceptibility to stress corrosion cracking (SCC) in 309L and 308L claddings. The presence of Cr element dispersion and weaker δ-ferrite content in 309L compared to 308L leads to a decrease in Cr, which in turn results in weaker oxidation resistance and increased susceptibility to SCC in 309L compared to 308L.

The use of WAAM route is essential for industries that require critical components with SS-309L. Furthermore, the stainless steel used in various applications needs to be free of secondary phases and maintain the proper ferrite and austenite structure. While certain aspects of WAAM have been reported in the literature, there is a lack of thorough demonstration and understanding about the process stability, structural integrity, component morphology, microstructure, and mechanical properties of SS-309L fabricated by using pulsed WAAM technique. This study examined the behaviour of microstructure, phase formation, and mechanical properties in samples of travel and build directions at different regions. However, as far as we comprehend, there is currently lesser, or no research conducted on the pulsed mode wire arc additive manufacturing of thick-walled SS-309L. Therefore, this study focused on investigating the microstructural and mechanical properties in different sections of the SS-309L thick wall manufactured using the WAAM process. This study findings will provide fresh perspectives on the significance of manufacturing SS-309L components for chemical processing and nuclear power plant applications.

WAAM deposition

During deposition, SS309L wire was solidified onto the previously formed layer, and the molten pool was heated by a pulsed arc wave. The study aimed to optimize the process variables in order to develop a suitable weld layer for each deposition, building upon the authors' previous investigation [13,14]. Characterization process

The study was conducted to examine the microstructure and mechanical properties of the cross-sectional and transverse regions of the SS309L multi-layered thick-wall part in as-deposited condition. The microstructure and tensile specimens were collected and processed in accordance with ASTM standards, and Fig. 2a shows the interpretation of the microstructure and tensile at the top, middle, and bottom regions. The collected samples experienced grinding and polishing procedures with sandpapers of different grades ranging from 220 to 2000. In order to remove any visible scratches and create an extremely smooth surface, the sample was disc-polished using alumina and water mixed together. The electrolytic etching solution consists of 5 mg of oxalic acid combined with 15 ml of hydrochloric acid. At room temperature, the resulting solution is subjected to a direct current of 6 V for 30 s. A comprehensive examination of the microstructure has been conducted in multiple parts, including the lower, middle, and topmost regions, in order to assess the metallurgical properties. The examination was conducted using a computerised microscope, FE-SEM, and EBSD. The EBSD analysis was used to identify the phases and orientations of grains and grain boundaries in different locations of the as-deposited part. An indentation spacing of half a step was used in the microhardness evaluation, which involved applying 500 g-force for 15 s at regular intervals. The INSTRON machine was used to conduct the tensile test within normal ambient conditions at a predefined strain rate in accordance with ASTM E8M − 11 standard.

Results and discussions

Microstructural investigation

The microstructure of 309L SS manufactured using the WAAM process is depicted in Fig. 3, Fig. 4. The microstructure of SS 309L is mostly formed of face-centered cubic (FCC) austenite, together with a body-centered cubic (BBC) δ-ferrite phase. The formation of δ-ferrite predominantly occurred at the borders of austenite grains. Austenite is the thermodynamically secure phase that exists at temperatures exceeding the threshold of critical temperature [24]. SS 309L is composed of a substantial quantity of chromium (Cr) and nickel (Ni), which encourage the occurrence of austenite because of its strong connection to the FCC crystal structure. δ-ferrite is a phase in the BBC crystal structure that occurs at lower temperatures. The occurrence of δ-ferrite in SS 309L can be ascribed to variables such as the chemistry of the alloys and the pace at which it cools throughout the PCGTAW solidifying method. Chromium (Cr) and Molybdenum (Mo) tend to maintain the δ-ferrite phases. The layer-by-layer deposition method results in the development of both FCC and BCC phases due to variations in temperature gradient and solidification rate. The microstructure of the material is mostly determined by the pace at which it cools, and this has a significant impact on its physical qualities, including elasticity, hardness, strength, and resistant to corrosion [17]. Fig. 3c displays the microstructure of the sample, which exhibits a well-organized arrangement of columnar dendritic grains consisting primarily of austenite (FCC) and δ-ferrite (BCC) in a uniform spacing. The development of complete austenite solidification in this investigation was unanticipated, given its chemical makeup and temperature ranges. Although researchers anticipated a few deviations towards unstable solidification, the extent of austenite solidification was surprising.

The microstructure in different locations exhibited heterogeneity due to the transition from main δ-ferrite to secondary austenite. The WAAM-fabricated 309L material undergoes recrystallization when regulated temperature, resulting in a mixed-phase microstructure consisting of skeletal δ ferrite encased in γ austenite. The duplex microstructure is comparable in both the transverse-sectional part and the cross-sectional part, as depicted in Fig. 3, Fig. 4. The diverse microstructure in different parts of the layer can be attributed to the heat build-up and arc pressure that the melting pool undergoes during the method of depositing layer by layer [25].

The WAAM processed microstructure is composed of large grains with a dendrite-like austenitic structure. Ferrites are present within the dendrites as depicted in Fig. 3a-c and Fig. 4a-c. Nevertheless, the distribution of ferrite among the dendrites is not consistent. Some regions have a limited amount of delta ferrite that has separated in the borders, indicating that these portions solidified in the austenitic-ferritic (AF) phase. As depicted in Fig. 3a cross-sectional portion, the uppermost area is composed of AF grains. Regions that have solidified in the ferritic-austenitic (FA) mode include a higher proportion of ferrite, as shown in Fig. 3b and c. Similarly, in Fig. 4a, the transverse-sectional portion reveals that the grain size in this area is marginally less in comparison to the cross-sectional portion. The boundaries between layers frequently line at a right angle to the cross-sectional area, leading to a greater buildup of heat caused by substantial heat transfer in the transverse-sectional area. As evidenced in this experiment and corroborated by other studies [23,26,27], it is possible to observe both AF and FA phases in a various region of the thick wall. The solidifying process of the welding metal is mostly governed by the chemical substance, specifically the ratio of chromium equivalent (Creq) to nickel equivalent (Nieq) [27]. A greater Creq/Nieq often signifies that the weld metal consolidated in the FA mode, while the opposite applies for the AF mode [28].

Elemental analysis

A scanning electron microscopy (SEM)/energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) point analysis was conducted at different areas of the WAAMed SS309L part. The SEM/EDS analysis of cross-sectional and transverse-sectional samples is depicted in Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8. An examination utilising EDS was conducted both within the individual grains and at the interfaces between them. The high-resolution images (Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8) clearly show the existence of δ-ferrite and γ-austenite. The EDS spectrum in Fig. 5a-b (i) and (ii), and 7(a-b) (i) and (ii) indicates the existence of chromium, iron, nickel, and molybdenum in various concentrations in both the δ-ferrite and γ-austenite regions of the thick wall. This verifies the existence of various stages of ferrites at distinct sites. The γ-austenite region exhibits a small increase in the concentration of Ni and a tiny decrease in the concentration of Fe, as depicted in Fig. 5a-b (i) and (ii), and 7(a-b) (i) and (ii). The elemental evaluation findings for the matrix of δ-ferrite and γ-austenite may be seen in Fig. 5, Fig. 7. Decreasing the amount of nickel (Ni) can result in a decrease in the energy associated with stacking faults, which in turn increases the concentration of stress by promoting the movement of dislocations in a similar plane [29]. Fig. 5, Fig. 7 display a point scanning conducted along this grain border. The grain boundaries are adorned with chromium-rich forms, as depicted in Fig. 5a-b (i) and Fig. 7a-b (i). The presence of Cr reduction and Ni enhancement can be observed within the grain core regions, as depicted in Fig. 5a-b (ii) and Fig. 7a-b (ii). Nevertheless, Chromium is distributed throughout the γ, δ, and Cr23C6 elements. Delta ferrite exhibits a significant amount of chromium, with a weight percentage of 30 % in 309L [30]. To obtain the accurate bulk 309L austenite Cr content, it is necessary to incorporate the complete δ particles in Fig. 5, Fig. 7 and the adjacent γ matrix in Fig. 5, Fig. 7 while conducting the EDS analysis. The amount of metallic chemicals in the grain boundaries (i) is higher than in the grains interiors (ii). This illustrates that metallic elements with a smaller atomic number than iron become incorporated into diffusion pathways due to the heat produced during the process of layer-by-layer depositing [31]. The figures displayed in Fig. 5, Fig. 7 illustrate the Fe, Cr, and Ni spectra in 309L at the δ/γ boundary, as determined by EDS. Another characteristic that was found is the loss of Cr at certain boundaries between the δ and γ phases. The decrease of Cr in certain boundaries was a result of the negligible migration of Cr to the δ/γ borderline during the rapid cooling process, taking into account that ferrite had a significantly larger Cr content compared to the γ phase. Chromium is an element that promotes the production of ferrite in materials.

Therefore, a larger amount of Cr is beneficial for ferrite production. On the other hand, nickel (Ni) is an ingredient that promotes the creation of austenite in 309L stainless steel [27]. However, a higher amount of Ni has a detrimental effect on the durability of ferrite. The ferrite percentage is influenced by the heat input, as indicated by Bozeman et al. [27]. A higher heat input leads to a drop in the ferrite content. Recent research has shown that the arrangement of ferrite in austenite stainless steel is influenced by the ratio of Creq/Nieq, which determines the way Fe–Cr–Ni alloy solidifies [23]. However, in this study, the utilization of pulsed current-based WAAM technique results in a more equal distribution of Cr and Ni components in the 309L material. This even distribution is one of the factors contributing to the reduction in susceptibility to stress corrosion cracking. Pulsed current WAAM functions by minimising heat input and increasing cooling frequency [12,13]. This improvement resulted in a reduction in elements segregation, making the SS 309L thick wall more prone to cracking at normal temperatures. Consequently, the development of cracks during the process of solidification can be limited or minimised.

Furthermore, the process of mapping the elements' area was carried out in both CS and TS parts, as shown in Fig. 6, Fig. 8. The EDS analysis reveals that the main components with higher weight percentages are Iron (Fe), Cr, and Ni. This suggests that the movement of Cr and Ni has occurred during the solidification process. The substantial difference in composition between the CS and TS parts is confirmed by EDS area mapping, as shown in Fig. 6, Fig. 8. However, there is a significant difference in the weight percentage of elements found using EDS spectrum analysis obtained from cross section and transverse section, as shown in Fig. 5 (i), (ii) and 7(i), (ii). The results acquired exhibit a high level of consistency with elemental area mapping. Furthermore, the analysis revealed the presence of substantial quantities of Ni, molybdenum (Mo), and Cr in the precipitants, as depicted in Fig. 8. EDS area mapping research revealed that the grain boundaries exhibited elevated concentrations of Cr and Mo in comparison to the grain interiors. The temperature variations during the deposition process caused changes in the thermal and physicochemical characteristics, such as hardness, corrosion resistance, and density, of the molten metal. This, in turn, resulted in fluctuations in the alloying components [32].

EBSD analysis

The study utilized electron backscattered diffraction (EBSD) to examine the changes in crystallographic texture and grain boundary character of the samples generated using WAAM. Fig. 9, Fig. 10 displays the EBSD findings of 309L stainless steel manufactured using the WAAM technique. The majority of the 309L SS is composed of γ austinite and columnar crystals that are oriented perpendicular to the direction of arc travel and heat dissipation. These crystals have a preferential orientation along the [001] axis, as shown in Fig. 9. The EBSD results of CS and TS regions are shown in Fig. 9, Fig. 10, (g), respectively. The rise in grain size in the WAAM deposited SS-309L samples can be attributed to the creation of solid solution during manufacturing [17]. Fig. 9a-h and 10(a-h) depict the influence on the development of the microstructure in both CS and TS areas. The figure clearly shows that the various parts of the deposited sample display uneven distribution of bands and do not have a clear grain orientation. The grain size data for the various locations may be apparent in Fig. 9, Fig. 10g. The cross-sectional region, which experiences the longest length of deposition heat cycling, shows a noticeable increase in grain size, with the largest grain measuring 159.4 μm. The mean grain size in the transverse-sectional region is determined to be 127.09 μm. The transverse-sectional region undergoes a shorter time of thermal cycling compared to the cross-sectional region, leading to a less pronounced grain coarsening impact.

The grain boundary character (GBC) of the SS-309L manufactured using the WAAM process is illustrated in Fig. 9, Fig. 10b. At elevated temperatures, the presence of numerous nucleation sites leads to the development of a sub grain structure in the TS regions. This promotes the production of lower angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) within the dense material. The coalescence of grains in the WAAM manufacturing process occurs due to the diffusion of the Fe–Ni–Cr substrate across the borders of δ and γ grains, which is facilitated by the presence of precipitate components in the liquid stage of the WAAM deposition. The cross-section samples exhibited the greatest percentage of high angle grain boundaries (HAGBs), accounting for 38.3 % of the total, while the fraction of LAGBs was the smallest, at 61.7 %. Transverse section analysis revealed a rise in LAGBs and a decline in HAGBs. In the cross-section, the majority of HAGBs were observed at 53.2 %, while LAGBs accounted for 47.8 %. This phenomenon was ascribed to the elimination of dislocated dipoles. The transformation of HAGB to LAGB in WAAM is made easier by the low stack fault energy [33]. The main catalyst for this change is the displacement of grain boundaries caused by diffusion during the melting process [15].

Fig. 9, Fig. 10h shows the distribution of misorientation angles for the SS 309L deposited using the WAAM process. WAAM deposition exhibits an augmentation in the proportion of low angle grain boundaries during grain growth, as depicted in Fig. 9, Fig. 10b. A spectrum with a range of 2°–15° indicates the presence of LAGBs, while angles greater than 15° indicate HAGBs. Furthermore, the presence of boundaries at a misorientation angle of 60° indicates the existence of a coincidence site lattice (CSL), while the cross-section sample barriers at about 20.2° signify the presence of a CSL. The growth of grain boundary texture in WAAM deposition is influenced by an increase in the percentage of peaks at roughly a 60° misorientation, as depicted in Fig. 9, Fig. 10h. Nevertheless, it was evident that the rate of Ni diffusion and the growth of grains declined when the misorientation in transverse section reached 17.4°. The misorientation angles of 20.2° and 17.4° indicate a higher level of mass transfer after diffusing over the borders of grains due to the outermost energy, form, and size of the atoms following deposition. Furthermore, it indicates that the surface diffusion is the predominant factor during the initial phases of WAAM deposition. Atoms exhibit a tendency to move from a flat surface to a curved surface along the grain boundary due to the greater chemical stability of the flat surface compared to the concave portion [34].

As shown in Fig. 9, Fig. 10d the Kernel average misorientation (KAM) reveals a reduction in the magnitude of grains distortion in the transverse section, as well as a drop in density of dislocations. The reason for this is that when the temperature increases, the rate at which defects move and dislocations ascend also increases. This results in a much lower dislocation density compared to the cross section. At the traverse section, the KAM exhibits a larger green area when subjected to a low strain rate of 0.01 s–1. However, as the strain rate grows to 10 s − 1, the KAM is mostly characterised by a blue region. In addition, the minor grain size observed in this case suggests that dynamic recrystallization (DRX) has not occurred during deformation. Consequently, there is a decrease in interior fractures, as well as in the components indicated by LAGBs. The rise in HAGBs may also be seen in the graphs displaying the distribution of misorientations at grain boundaries.

Fig. 9, Fig. 10e depict the pole figures of the TS and CS regions. The deposited sample of SS-309L in the WAAM process has a face-centered cubic (FCC) structure. The pole density reaches a maximum of 6.1 in the CS and 4.7 in the TS. In addition, the texture intensity is less prominent in the transverse section, as depicted in Fig. 10(b–e). Furthermore, the pole figures of the WAAM samples clearly illustrate the significant texture change from the normal direction (ND) to the transverse direction (TD) and rolling direction (RD). The textural modification in discussion is primarily driven by the ongoing merging of sub-grains and a partial rotation of grains, which occur due to compaction and diffusion during the melting process [35].

The EBSD phase map of the WAAM SS-309L is shown in Fig. 9, Fig. 10c. The EDS investigation verified the concentration of alloying elements in the interfaces between cells during the deposition process. In order to have a more comprehensive comprehension of this phenomenon, the EBSD phase maps were conducted. The phase map depicts FCC austenite in a green colour and BCC ferrite in a red colour. Furthermore, it was noted that the austenite pools had a high concentration along the grain boundary in both the transverse and cross-sectional areas. The ODF analysis offers comprehensive data on the crystalline orientations of each of the grains in a specific specimen. Accurately forecasting anisotropic mechanical qualities, such as strength or ductility, is essential as they can vary based on the position of the grains. Fig. 9, Fig. 10f display the Orientation Distribution Function (ODF) at the transverse and cross section regions. The acquired textures exhibit a high degree of similarity, with a little improvement in precision evident in the cross-section sample as the grain size expands. The intensity of the transverse section is 9.7, while the intensity in the cross section is 15.3. Another distinction lies in the fact that the intensities across the transverse section are spread in a more uniform manner compared to the cross section.

Microhardness

The hardness value significantly increases from the bottom to the topmost regions, according to microhardness observation, as depicted in Fig. 11a, b. The thick wall of WAAMed SS309L has an average hardness of 254 HV, 251 HV and 247 HV in cross-sectional regions and 257 HV, 251 HV and 253 HV were obtained in the transverse sectional regions of upper, middle and lower zones as illustrated in Fig. 11c. The fluctuations in value of hardness obtained between the various regions are a result of the microstructural non-homogeneity that arises from the development of a thick wall during the WAAM process. The hardness values are reduced by the lower ferrite in the austenitic matrix. There is a correlation between the presence of delta ferrite and the level of hardness. It implies that as the amount of delta ferrite grows, the hardness also increases. The complex thermal history during layer-by-layer deposition is responsible for the gradual change in microstructural properties along the CS and TS regions. Along the cross-sections, the hardness measurements were made at three specific locations (top, middle, and bottom) on the as-deposited WAAM part. In the SS309L topmost region, the greater average hardness was 254 HV and 257 HV of cross and transverse sections respectively. The decreased hardness values have been found between the middle and bottom zones of the developed parts, in both sections.

When metals have smaller grain sizes at the microscopic level their hardness and strength can rise significantly due to the Hall-Petch connection [37]. This phenomenon arises due to the intrinsically elevated concentration of grain borders, where fractures have the potential to get immobilized. However, when the grain size of metal falls below a crucial size of approximately 10 nm, the material's strength may drop due to the significant increase in the fraction of grain boundaries. This allows the grains to shift easily in relation to one another other. The phenomenon of reduced hardness due to increased grain size is referred to as the reversed Hall-Petch Phenomenon [36]. However, it can be used since the materials have the ability to undergo aberrant (super-plastic) stretching, allowing for expansions of over 1000 % before experiencing failures.

The increased microhardness value observed in the top layer is attributed to the presence of a greater amount of finely dispersed ferrite. The presence of ferrite and austenite directly influences the general tendency of reduced hardness in the lower and middle regions. The microhardness values exhibited considerable variation among the layers, which correlates with the research findings of Pranav Nikam et al. [38]. The transverse direction microhardness value was slightly higher than cross-sectional regions due to its higher ferrite concentration and smaller austenite grain placements. The layers developed through additive manufacturing comprise the distorted austenitic phase. This is a significant factor that contributes to the observed increase in hardness. The orientation correlations with the γ-matrix varied across various austenitic morphologies at different places. The distorted austenitic grains, clearly seen in the kernel average misorientation images, can also contribute to the increased hardness of the microstructure.

The average micro-tensile values obtained from the CS and TS are displayed in Fig. 12a, b. For the topmost section in the transverse direction, the UTS and YS values are 661 ± 3.09 and 355 ± 5.31 MPa, respectively. The tensile values varied in both regions cross and transverse sections because of the temperature gradient. The mean percentage of elongation (EL) for the topmost regions is 42 % and 41 %, TS and CS respectively. The growth of fractured points take place within the austenite structure, leading to enhanced ductility or percentage elongation of the component. The difference in the tensile property of the WAAM processed structure is mostly attributed to the heterogeneous microstructural changes that develop as a result of non-uniform heating and cooling cycles that prevail during fabrication [39]. The development of microstructure is responsible for variations in tensile characteristics. The bottom of the thick wall in the cross-sectional and middle of the transverse direction exhibited reduced tensile characteristics due to the formation of a dendritic microstructure. An increase in plastic deformation resistance could be associated with dendritic boundary formation. The topmost regions of the transverse section indicated a positive correlation between larger grain size and increased material strength. The application of pulsed current WAAM can greatly minimise the susceptibility to cracking tendency in the as-deposited SS 309L part. Therefore, the tensile strength has been greater at the topmost regions of the transverse section.

The SS-309L thick wall undergoes a layer-by-layer production process, which leads to observable changes in its microstructure. The discrepancies in the microstructure at different elevations might be responsible for the disparities in hardness and tensile characteristics. The decrease in heating frequency and the increase in particle size lead to changes in hardness and tensile characteristics distributes. Similarly, Nagasai et al. [24] investigated the microstructure and mechanical properties of duplex stainless steel in the top and bottom regions. Similar results were observed, with upper regions of the part having better mechanical and microstructural properties. A slower rate of cooling and a longer period of time for the solid-state phase transformation were found to be responsible for the substantial increase in the percentage of austenite that occurred from the bottom to the top region. The higher level of hardness is attributed to the existence of more delicate dendrites in the upper region, as illustrated by the microstructural features presented in Fig. 3, Fig. 4. The density of the smaller δ ferrite dendrites decreases in each subsequent layer, leading to a slight reduction in both hardness and tensile characteristics. Reheating can cause ferrite grains to undergo modifications that lead to a reduction in strength in the middle and bottom layers. The repetitive procedure of alternating temperatures utilized in the multi-layer deposition resulted in the development of very fine δ ferrite, which later contributed to an enhanced hardness.

Conclusions

The SS-309L alloy has been successfully manufactured using the pulsed mode GTAW based WAAM technique. In this study, the microstructure, and mechanical properties of an as deposited 309L part were explored in detail. The conclusions can be summarized as follows.

•The absence of any flaws or lack of fusion was observed during the layer-by-layer deposition process of the developed thick wall part.

•The presence of delta-ferrite and an austenitic matrix was clearly shown in the as-deposited WAAM specimens, while the absence of any additional secondary phases was also highlighted in the SEM analysis.
