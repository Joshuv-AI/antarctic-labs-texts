Compliance with existing standards: overview comparing Cochrane with non-Cochrane living systematic reviews
Authors: Melanie M. Golob, Andrea Leinberger-Jabari, Brenna Tilka Loufek, Jonathan Livingstone-Banks, Nia W. Roberts, David Nunan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLiving systematic reviews (LSRs) have been published with increasing frequency since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic, but there has been little formal evaluation of their methodological consistency and compliance with best-practice standards. The objective of this overview was to evaluate the extent and nature of current LSRs in health research, as well as the degree to which they acknowledge and adhere to existing methodological standards.MethodsWe searched MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, PROSPERO, Scopus, Epistemonikos, and TRIPdatabase from inception to 24 June 2022. We ran a restricted update search for LSRs published 1 January 2025 to 9 January 2026 to see if conclusions would be similar. We extracted data in duplicate. The primary outcome was the number and proportion of LSRs that conform to existing living standards: criteria and guidance for their need and conduct as published by Cochrane. Secondary outcomes assessed reliability and other reporting aspects. Outcomes were assessed according to explicit author reporting for each review.ResultsFinal analysis included all 23 Cochrane LSRs at the time and a randomly selected 23 non-Cochrane LSRs (among a total of 113). Ten (43%) Cochrane LSRs clearly met the three need criteria for living mode. None of the non-Cochrane LSRs met all three criteria. Cochrane LSRs were also more likely than non-Cochrane LSRs to adhere to Cochrane methodological guidance for the conduct of LSRs, with six (26%) of 23 Cochrane reviews meeting all guidance items. None of the non-Cochrane reviews met all guidance items. In the restricted update (10 LSRs included: five Cochrane and five non-Cochrane), Cochrane LSRs were still more likely to adhere to the three need criteria (four of five, 80%) and five methodological guidance items (three of five, 60%) compared to non-Cochrane LSRs (one of five, or 20% for criteria and guidance adherence). Ten (22%) of all 46 included LSRs were cited by clinical practice guidelines, according to PubMed.ConclusionsThis overview highlights a lack of methodological consistency among LSRs, potentially stemming from a lack of awareness of Cochrane’s LSR standards. One criterion to become living is that the topic is a priority for decision-making; however, there is little evidence that LSRs are cited frequently in clinical practice guidelines. Establishing clear, objective LSR standards would help to support both Cochrane and non-Cochrane authors and improve evidence-based health care. Background

Health-related research dominates worldwide publication output, with over 700,000 articles published in the field in 2020 and publications continuing to trend upward [1]. The COVID-19 pandemic was a major driver of increased publications in health research [2]; one publisher saw a 63% increase from the previous year in health and medicine article submissions [3]. Systematic reviews (SRs), often considered to be the gold standard in evidence synthesis, are a way to understand this flood of research in a more meaningful way. However, SRs cannot always keep pace; one study found that 7% of SRs were already outdated by the time of publication [4]. If a review is not continually updated, it is only a matter of time before it lags behind new research.

To remedy the issue of reviews rapidly becoming out of date, Cochrane established the concept of “living” SRs (LSRs), whereby relevant new evidence is incorporated into an existing SR on a continual basis [5–7]. Existing living guidance is based on the same core concepts as traditional SRs, but Cochrane has led the movement toward LSRs, publishing standards for production and publication and launching a pilot program in 2019 [8]. As Cochrane is often seen as setting the standard for rigorous methods, other groups outside of Cochrane typically follow their lead; though it has not been studied if Cochrane or non-Cochrane LSRs adhere to Cochrane’s LSR standards, Cochrane LSRs may differ in what exactly it means to be “living” across reviews [9]. A scoping review on conducting, reporting, publishing, and appraising LSRs found that there was ample guidance on conducting and publishing, but that there are major gaps in guidance for reporting and appraising LSRs. Notably, some journals provide LSR reporting guidance within their instructions for authors, which are not indexed and would thus not be discoverable via database searches [10–12].

Cochrane’s LSR standards include three criteria for their need and five guidance items for conduct (detailed in the Outcomes heading of the Methods section). Criteria are specific to starting a new LSR or transitioning a topic to living mode, while guidance details LSR conduct [8]. It is unclear, however, if these living standards are being followed by groups synthesizing evidence, or if they have been suitably defined and operationalized. The primary objective of this study was to determine the proportion of LSRs that conform to established living criteria and guidance for conduct, and thus the extent to which they are “alive” according to current standards. Secondary objectives included assessment of methodological and reporting parameters, providing information to inform ongoing developments of LSRs. As Cochrane established living standards, we compared Cochrane with non-Cochrane LSRs. Critically examining publications identified as LSRs will inform methods and best practices.

Methods

This review was prospectively registered in June 2022 and followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) statement, a checklist specific for overviews of SRs [13, 14].

Data sources and search strategy

We included all existing published LSRs from inception to 24 June 2022, based on a refined search within the following databases: MEDLINE(vidSP)[1946-], Embase(OvidSP)[1974-], Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews(Cochrane Library, Wiley)[Issue 6 of 12, June 2022], PROSPERO (Scopus[Elsevier]), Epistemonikos, and TRIPdatabase [15]. In addition, a limited interim search was conducted in July 2025 in the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews to identify newly published Cochrane LSRs since the original search; this search was restricted in scope and is presented descriptively. We subsequently ran a restricted update search on 9 January 2026 to identify LSRs published between 1 January 2025 and 9 January 2026, to assess whether inclusion of more recent LSRs would result in similar conclusions. Where applicable, overlap between the 2025 interim search and the 2026 update was identified and managed to avoid double-counting.

Results were exported and de-duplicated using EndNote 20; additional duplicates were identified manually. The search in PROSPERO was limited to review protocols in the following categories: Review Published, Completed, Being Updated. Protocols were retrieved in order to assess volume of anticipated LSRs but not assessed for compliance with LSR standards. Details of the search strategy are provided in Supplementary file 1.

We ran an initial test search, including synonyms of the term “living” (using terms such as “continu*” or “updat*”), but as no meaningful additional studies were found, only the term “living” was used as a qualifier for LSRs in the final search.

Eligibility criteria

We included LSRs addressing a medical or health-related question, with the requirement that the review must identify using a “living” methodology or approach in the title or abstract, even if the review was the final update or had transitioned out of living mode. If the review had ceased or transitioned out of living status but did not mention living, it was not included. The term systematic review was defined according to criteria published in the Cochrane handbook. Only the most recent version of the review that included the term living at the time of the search was included. Reviews were included regardless of language or date of publication. Records were excluded if not identified as an SR, or the “living” approach was not specified in the title or abstract (see Supplementary file 1 for terms used).

Selection process

We screened for eligibility in two stages: title/abstract and full text. All records in the original search were screened in duplicate at each stage using Covidence. One reviewer (MMG) screened every record, and duplicate screening was split between two other authors (ALJ, BTL). Discrepancies in screening or data extraction were resolved by discussion or via consultation with a third author (JLB).

Records excluded at full text eligibility assessment were documented with reasons. As living standards were established by Cochrane, all Cochrane LSRs were included and compared to an equal number of randomly selected non-Cochrane LSRs from among records that met eligibility criteria to ensure a representative sample. Random selection was performed by numbering eligible studies alphabetically, generating 23 random numbers using Google random number generator, and identifying the 23 corresponding numbers from the list to select LSRs for the overview. The updated search followed this same process, except that a random 20% sample was independently screened by two authors (ALJ, BTL).

Data extraction and management

As with screening, one reviewer (MMG) extracted data from all studies, and duplicate data extraction was split between two other authors (ALJ, BTL). We extracted data from each LSR based on pre-determined fields (see Supplementary file 2 for data extraction form), using information from the published review only. We extracted data from the most recent LSR version. Publication date collected was the date published online, if provided. Any discrepancies between reviewers were adjudicated by consultation with a third reviewer (JLB). Data extraction for records included from the update search followed the same process.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome was the number and proportion of LSRs that conform to living standards (criteria and guidance) for their need and conduct as published by Cochrane, in “Guidance for the Production and Publication of Cochrane Living Systematic Reviews” [8]. Although these standards were created for Cochrane authors, they were, at the time, the only published standards for living evidence and therefore set the threshold for what should be considered “living.”

Three criteria for which an LSR is appropriate (all three must be met):The review question is a particular priority for decision-making,There is an important level of uncertainty in the existing evidence, andThere is likely to be emerging evidence that will impact on the conclusions of the LSR.

All outcomes were assessed according to explicit author reporting for each review. This choice was made in order to limit subjective judgments in favor of objective outcomes. Synonyms of terms were used to assess compliance, but value judgments were not applied as this would have required subject-level expertise for each topic.

Number of versions, involvement of key stakeholders, and how many report using the LSR process are also indicators of the reliability of the LSR and how up to date it is, thus affecting how useful it is for informing healthcare policy decisions and ultimately health outcomes. As such, the number of articles citing each included LSR was also analyzed. PubMed was used for number cited by, as it has “practice guideline” available as a filter to refine the overall number cited by; Google Scholar was also used for comparison, as it captures publications outside of academic journals as well. The first living version was used for citations, as this version was more likely cited in other publications and guidelines due to time since publication. It can also be extrapolated that if an LSR was used as the basis to inform a guideline, it would continue to do so throughout its lifetime, and thus examining citations linked to each LSR would not provide additional information; however, this is an assumption.

Data analysis

Characteristics of included LSRs were aggregated and displayed quantitatively in both tabular and visual formats using Microsoft Excel and Power BI. Recurring methodological criteria across LSRs were identified, including frequency of recurrence and integration as a result of methodological guidance. Subgroup analyses were conducted, grouping Cochrane vs non-Cochrane reviews to examine evidence of difference in the above measures. See Supplementary file 3 for differences between protocol and review.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in this study.

Results

A total of 2826 records were identified. Following title/abstract screening and removal of duplicates, 846 full texts were screened for inclusion (see Supplementary file 4 for excluded records); 136 publications were eligible for inclusion following full-text screening: 23 Cochrane LSRs and 113 non-Cochrane LSRs. In order to compare an equal number of Cochrane to non-Cochrane LSRs, 23 non-Cochrane LSRs were randomly selected. A final list of 46 reviews was included for analysis, 23 Cochrane and 23 non-Cochrane (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1PRISMA 2020 flow diagram for systematic reviews

First authors were predominantly based in high-income countries (78%, 36 of 46); no authors from low-income countries were represented in the selected LSRs. European countries represented 48% (22 of 46) of first authors. In the majority of Cochrane reviews (70%, 16 of 23), funding came from multiple sources (e.g., a combination of organizational/infrastructure and project/grant funding), while non-Cochrane funding was from a single source, either organizational/infrastructure or project/grant funding (61%, 14 of 23). All Cochrane reviews were consistent in using the term “living systematic review” to describe their publication, whereas non-Cochrane reviews varied in their terminology, using other synonyms in six of 23 reviews (26%). There was a correlation between more recent publication dates and a larger total number of living versions; this correlation was more pronounced in non-Cochrane LSRs (see Supplementary file 7). This association was likely strongly impacted by the increased volume of LSRs during the COVID-19 pandemic (see Supplementary file 8 for visual representation of LSRs by year published). Among all 677 SRs screened (including protocols and preprints) that identified as living, the vast majority (47%, 317 of 677) concerned SARS-CoV-2 or COVID-19. In comparison, the 2nd most frequent topic was cancer, with 36 records (5%) (see Supplementary file 9).

Living standards

The degree to which included LSRs were updated according to their own pre-defined methods was clearly reported in nine Cochrane reviews (39%), compared to four non-Cochrane reviews (17%). In the restricted update in 2026, this was reported in four of five Cochrane reviews (80%) and one of five non-Cochrane reviews (20%), representing changes of + 41% and + 3%, respectively.

Less than a quarter of LSRs met all three criteria to be considered living; those that did were Cochrane LSRs (ten of 23, or 43%, compared to zero non-Cochrane). In the 2026 restricted update, three of five Cochrane reviews (60%) and one of five non-Cochrane reviews (20%) met these criteria, representing changes of + 17% and + 20%, respectively. In the 2025 interim search (Cochrane only; n = 14), six reviews (43%) met these criteria.

Even fewer met all five living guidance items, and all those were again Cochrane LSRs (six of 23, or 26%, compared to zero non-Cochrane; see Fig. 2). Cochrane reviews predominantly searched monthly (43%, ten of 23), though this was likely due to the Cochrane LSR pilot program specifying monthly searches; in the restricted update, monthly searches still predominated for Cochrane (three of five, or 60%). The non-Cochrane review search frequency was fairly evenly distributed from daily to annual searches; however, almost 40% (nine of 23) did not report search frequency. See Supplementary file 10 for search frequency.

The majority of published versions were living; three reviews did not start out as living and transitioned into living mode from a static version. No reviews were identified that transitioned out of living mode and back again. Only four of 46 (9%) reviews were identified as having ceased living mode; one of these reviews only refers to this fact in the article title as “concluded” but does not mention anywhere else in the text it had ceased living.

Less than half of the reviews stated they followed guidance for conducting or reporting LSRs (43% and 48%, or 20 and 22 of 46 LSRs, respectively). Guidance cited for conducting LSRs was primarily Cochrane’s “Guidance for the production and publication of Cochrane living systematic reviews” (55%, 11 of 20); this guidance was even referenced in one non-Cochrane LSR. Almost all (95%, 21 of 22) reviews that referenced reporting guidance did not use guidance that accounted for LSRs (e.g., PRISMA versions not specific to LSRs); only one review (non-Cochrane) referenced LSR-relevant reporting guidance (PRISMA 2020); this differed in the newer studies included in the restricted update, with eight of 10 LSRs referencing reporting guidance, six specific to LSRs.

Methodological assessments

Almost all Cochrane reviews (87%, 20 of 23) used GRADE to assess certainty of evidence, compared to 61% (14 of 23) of non-Cochrane reviews. The remaining nine non-Cochrane reviews did not evaluate certainty; one evaluated strength of evidence using the AHRQ Methods Guide for Comparative Effectiveness Reviews.

For LSRs that rated strength/certainty of evidence (76%, 35 of 46), almost half of those (46%, 16 of 35) only had one version (no updates) of the LSR, and thus a change in certainty for any outcomes could not be determined from the previous version. For the remaining LSRs (54%, 19 of 35), the stated change was distributed fairly evenly between increase in certainty, decrease in certainty, or certainty did not change (see Table 3). Software tools to assist with the SR process were mentioned in all but seven LSRs. Eighteen LSRs (39%) with multiple living versions referenced one or more software tools. Tools most frequently mentioned were RevMan, GRADEpro, Covidence, and literature auto-alerts, mentioned in 26, 14, 10, and 10 LSRs, respectively (see Supplementary file 11, or Supplementary file 12 for full breakdown). Cochrane LSRs mentioned 14 different software tools a total of 76 times across 23 studies, compared with 13 different software tools in non-Cochrane studies a total of 33 times across the same number of studies. Software tools were used for a range of tasks, from search and screen to data extraction and analysis. However, tools that used automation assistance (e.g., literature auto-alerts, study classifiers) to reduce workload were used exclusively for the search (35%, 16 of 46) and screen (33%, 15 of 46) phases.

The first living versions of the 46 LSRs were referenced by 6577 sources according to Google Scholar, as compared with 2105 sources according to PubMed using the “cited by” feature. Ten (seven Cochrane, three non-Cochrane, 22% total) of the LSRs were cited by 12 clinical practice guidelines (CPGs), nine of which were unique (see Supplementary file 13) [64–72]. However, four of the CPGs that cited LSRs (67%) did not cite the most up-to-date LSR even when one was available (see Supplementary file 14 for volume of LSRs cited by CPGs). In three CPGs, the LSR was used as a source to locate relevant RCTs, and not a source of evidence itself. Only one guideline was almost entirely based on the evidence published in the LSR. In the others, the LSR was just one of many systematic reviews or meta-analyses referenced.

Seven total reviews (three Cochrane, four non-Cochrane) could be considered “zombie” reviews, defined as inactive LSRs: only one living version but more than two years old. Twenty-two LSRs (48%) had one living version; 15 of those only had one version in total, meaning zero updates had been published, living or otherwise. See Table 3 for all outcomes.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

The majority of LSRs included in this overview did not meet all living standards, indicating they may not be truly “alive.” Cochrane LSRs were more likely to meet current standards, which is reasonable considering they were created by Cochrane. However, it should be noted that whether or not an LSR meets all living standards does not reflect on quality from a methods perspective. Despite not meeting standards, almost a quarter of all LSRs were cited by CPGs. Cochrane reviews were consistently cited more frequently than non-Cochrane reviews by all publications and specifically by CPGs, indicating Cochrane reviews have a more established pathway for informing guidelines.

Search frequency ranged from daily to annually, but this was likely skewed for Cochrane reviews by Cochrane’s pilot program, which specified monthly searches. LSR topics that mainly involved COVID-19 or SARS-CoV-2 predominantly conducted daily or weekly searches, which was appropriate given the evolving evidence during the pandemic. Guidance for conducting and reporting LSRs was not consistently cited or employed according to this overview; however, during the course of this overview, a PRISMA-LSR extension was developed, which is why the newer studies included in the restricted update were more likely to reference LSR-relevant reporting guidance [73].

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this overview of LSRs lies in its evaluation of LSR standards during a period of rapid proliferation due to the COVID-19 pandemic. While other reviews have examined characteristics of LSRs, this review goes beyond characteristics to look at methodological parameters and reproducibility, which provides a more complete picture to help inform the living approach. SRs are frequently used as the basis for downstream evidence products such as health technology assessments and clinical practice guidelines, so findings of this overview can be translated to other types of syntheses.

A limitation of this overview is that LSRs are continually being published. Updates conducted in 2025 and 2026 identified additional Cochrane LSRs, with generally higher proportions meeting living review standards (both criteria and guidance items) compared with the original 2022 search. Though the numbers of recently published studies provided a relatively small sample size, these findings suggest a potential improvement in adherence to living standards within the Cochrane community over time. However, in the absence of clearly coordinated efforts to implement Cochrane’s LSR standards, these changes may reflect gradual uptake rather than systematic improvement.

Compliance with existing standards

It should be noted that although these LSR standards exist, that does not necessarily mean they are automatically followed. There are limitations in assessing compliance with Cochrane’s living standards due to the ambiguity of the criteria and guidance items (e.g., an LSR stating the prevalence of a disease does not mean it is a priority for decision-making). And these LSR standards do not appear to be commonly agreed upon outside of Cochrane, unlike quality standards for SRs. This overview is one way to audit these issues so they can be addressed.

Other limitations include only using a selection of all eligible non-Cochrane LSRs, as the sample may not be representative and reflect true differences. Using only the search term “living” may not have captured all reviews intended to be continually updated, but without limiting the search in some way, there would be no meaningful way to find all studies that are continually updated. Extracting data from only the most recent LSR version and not including protocols or supplementary materials may have resulted in lower perceived adherence to standards than was actually the case. Using explicit author reporting for outcomes limits evaluation of methodological quality.

Presentation of living parameters

LSRs were least likely to identify as “living” in the title only (10 of 46 LSRs). This is unsurprising on the part of Cochrane reviews, as it is explicitly stated in Cochrane living guidance not to mention living in the review title. BMJ’s author guidance, on the other hand, explicitly says to include the term “living” in the study title. Other publishers, including the journal Systematic Reviews and F1000Research, have created their own LSR reporting guidance. The number of publications that considered themselves “living” compared with the number that met living standards differed considerably, indicating groups are not aware of the guidance, do not follow it, or both.

Versions and changes were not always easy to follow, as protocols, publications, and updates oftentimes had different titles or authors; authors did not typically state what had changed from the previous version (e.g., conclusions, interventions, setting). This poses a particular challenge when LSR authors do not have a way of linking publications across the same or different publishers. Readers can try to link versions using the protocol number if provided with a unique identifier and pre-registered (such as those provided with PROSPERO registration), but this is not common among all protocols. It has been suggested that blockchain technology to link related reviews may be a possible solution to this issue with LSRs [74–76].

Comparison with existing literature

There have been multiple studies examining LSR methods [77–80]. One study specifically highlighted the need for development in reporting and appraising LSRs [78]. The Luo et al. paper included 64 LSRs, of which 24 overlapped with LSRs included in this overview [79]. Three of the 24 overlapping LSRs were randomly selected to replicate via AMSTAR 2 to check for agreement. Replicated quality assessments agreed in overall quality for each of the three selected LSRs [79]. See Supplementary file 15 for comparison. It primarily focused on methodological and reporting quality rather than compliance to best-practice guidance as was the case with this overview, but it arrived at a similar conclusion: there is room for improvement [79].

Another study looked at conduct, reporting, and currency of Cochrane COVID-19 LSRs, and found that there are ongoing challenges for all these aspects [9]. There are also concerns about methods for publishing LSRs, including editorial processes and platform limitations, as there can be issues at the organizational and infrastructure levels [9].

A comparable study to this overview included an evaluation of LSR quality using AMSTAR 2, but the authors did not provide specific LSRs evaluated for AMSTAR 2 in their review, so could not be compared for quality assessments. Similar to the findings in this overview, they found that roughly half of LSRs have not published any updates, as did a study from 2021 by Zheng et al. looking at LSR characteristics. This overview of LSRs is unique in examining compliance with existing living criteria and guidance.

Implications of the overview findings

Frequently, SRs are the basis of other evidence syntheses, such as clinical practice guidelines and health technology assessments. If LSRs are informing policy and healthcare practice, there needs to be commonly agreed upon criteria and a threshold which must be met to qualify evidence syntheses as living. This review shows there is a lack of standardization for conducting and reporting LSRs, particularly among non-Cochrane LSRs, which could impact their purported role as reliable and trustworthy sources of current evidence to inform healthcare practice and policy when urgency and uncertainty are high. Without this standardization, the trust placed in LSRs is partially undermined, reducing their effectiveness in informing healthcare policy and potentially having a deleterious effect on health outcomes. Future research should coordinate groups involved in living evidence synthesis to discuss necessary components to be considered living, and potentially reach a consensus on terminology and guidance, including whether the term “living” is actually appropriate given current inconsistencies in application.

Conclusions

Most LSR authors do not adhere to Cochrane living standards, even Cochrane LSR authors, though this is improving. Living parameters are inconsistently reported, indicating a lack of awareness for living standards. Changes in certainty across versions should be highlighted, as uncertainty is a key LSR criterion, but this was not always done. Cochrane’s living standards can be interpreted subjectively, making compliance challenging. LSRs are not consistently referenced in clinical practice guidelines to inform care; they may potentially inform policy, but it is not clear they are being used for decision-making. As evidence synthesis researchers look for ways to keep pace with the increasing flow of publications, providing a clear, structured, and objective process for living standards would add value to the field of evidence-based health care.
