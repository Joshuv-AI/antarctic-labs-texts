Segmental duplications and supernumerary chromosomes drive antifungal drug resistance in Candida auris
Authors: Aswathy Narayanan, Sameer Joshi, Ritika Harchand, Rajendra Prasad, Shivaprakash M Rudramurthy, Koodali T Nishant, Kaustuv Sanyal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida auris, a World Health Organisation–listed critical priority fungal pathogen, causes frequent multidrug-resistant outbreaks worldwide. While point mutations underlying antifungal resistance are well characterised, the contribution of structural genomic variation to antifungal responses remains poorly defined. Here, we integrate whole-genome sequencing, a genome-wide copy number variation (CNV) screen, electrophoretic karyotyping, and mutation-accumulation analyses to investigate the role of structural variation in antifungal susceptibility across a clinical cohort of C. auris isolates. We identify recurrent CNV hotspots, with segmental duplications representing a predominant mode of genome variation. Small duplications encompassing ERG11 arise at high frequency and frequently co-occur with drug resistance-associated ERG11 mutations, collectively enhancing azole resistance. In addition, large centromere-inclusive duplications of chromosome 1 generate supernumerary chromosomes, leading to paradoxical growth and reduced susceptibility to caspofungin, an echinocandin. At the population level, structural variants frequently arise in parallel to FKS1 mutations, suggesting multiple genetic mechanisms underlying reduced drug susceptibility. Together, our findings establish segmental duplication as a major, non-mutational driver of antifungal resistance, highlighting the need to consider structural genomic variation in both resistance surveillance and clinical susceptibility testing.Subject terms: Antifungal agents, Fungal genomics, Antimicrobial resistance, Pathogens

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is an escalating clinical and public health threat driven by the widespread use of antimicrobial compounds across clinical, agricultural, and environmental settings1. Fungal infections, affecting more than 6.5 million individuals annually, are a medically important but often less-recognised component of the AMR crisis worldwide2. Invasive Candida infections, including candidemia, result in mortality rates exceeding 60% in some patient cohorts2. Treatment options remain limited due to the small number of antifungal drug classes and the rising incidence of antifungal drug resistance (AFDR). Among emerging fungal pathogens, Candida (Candidozyma) auris has rapidly attained global significance as a multidrug-resistant threat and is designated by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as a critical-priority pathogen. Its clinical success reflects a combination of adaptive traits, including thermotolerance, aggregation, environmental persistence, and both intrinsic and acquired antifungal resistance3.

Reduced antifungal susceptibility in fungi encompasses multiple microbe–drug interaction phenotypes. These include growth at high minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) exhibiting drug resistance or drug tolerance, in which a drug-susceptible strain survives at drug concentrations above MIC, and heteroresistance, a condition of drug resistance driven by a small subpopulation of cells4. Echinocandins, which target the β−1,3-glucan synthase complex, are first-line agents used to treat C. auris infections. While echinocandin resistance commonly involves point mutations in the FKS1 alleles, C. auris clinical isolates also frequently exhibit a different phenomenon, paradoxical growth5, often referred to as the Eagle effect, where growth re-emerges at higher drug concentrations despite apparent susceptibility at lower concentrations of the same drug6. The stable point mutation-driven echinocandin resistance frequency is relatively low, and the conventional MIC-based breakpoints do not capture the supra-MIC growth patterns, which can directly contribute to treatment failure4.

In addition to classical single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)-mediated mechanisms, many primarily asexual fungal pathogens exploit karyotype plasticity, resulting in alterations in chromosome number and structure to enhance survival under environmental or drug stress7. The events involving breaking and rejoining of DNA fragments result in structural variants, causing changes in gene copy numbers and location8. Segmental duplication (SD) events, a major class of structural variants, arise from replication errors, template switching, or recombination-based repair processes, and exhibit substantial architectural karyotype diversity, ranging from tandem gene duplications to the formation of large duplicated chromosomal segments or entirely novel chromosomes9–13. In fungi, SDs frequently lead to gene copy number variations (CNVs) that modulate gene dosage, buffer deleterious alleles, and enable rapid adaptive responses to antifungal exposure14. Although C. auris possesses a highly plastic genome15, CNV-associated antifungal adaptation has been documented mainly through laboratory evolution studies16,17. The prevalence, diversity, and functional consequences of SDs in clinical isolates remain largely unexplored, in part due to the transient and reversible nature of these structural changes.

Here, we address this critical knowledge gap by integrating clinical genomics, long-read sequencing, electrophoretic karyotyping, and mutation-accumulation experiments to demonstrate that C. auris adapts to major antifungal drug classes via distinct SD-based structural variation strategies: small adaptive SDs centred on the ERG11 genomic neighbourhood that enhance azole resistance through a combination of mutation and dosage effect and large, centromere-inclusive SDs that persist as supernumerary chromosomes and contribute to a reduced drug susceptibility via the Eagle effect during echinocandin stress. Together, these findings reveal a previously underappreciated structural basis of antifungal adaptation in C. auris and provide a mechanistic framework for understanding how genome plasticity facilitates survival of this global pathogen under antifungal pressure, in conjunction with point mutations.

Results

CNV hotspots in chromosomes 1 and 3 of the C. auris genome

To evaluate whether large-scale structural variation contributes to differences in antifungal susceptibility among South Asian clade 1 isolates, we performed a genome-wide CNV screen on a cohort of 79 Indian clinical isolates (Supplementary Table 1) collected between 2014 and 2021, with 60 isolates obtained from blood samples (Fig. 1a, b). These isolates displayed broad variability in azole susceptibility, whereas echinocandin MICs remained comparatively narrow. The amphotericin B MIC values centered around 2 µg/mL, with moderate variability (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 1). CNVs were identified using whole-genome sequencing data analysed with YMAP18 for gross chromosomal changes and Control-FREEC19 for breakpoint resolution (Supplementary Data 1 and 2). A pan-genome CNV plot covering all patterns that emerged across clinical isolates is shown (Fig. 1c). To prioritise biologically relevant CNVs, we considered the frequency of their occurrence across isolates and the size of the duplicated segment. As expected, the most prominent hotspots mapped to the rDNA locus on chromosome 5 (chr5) and chr6, a region with well-documented copy-number plasticity20, and to several subtelomeric regions across chromosomes, which are similarly known to be dynamic in Candida species21, including in C. auris22. Beyond these established plastic regions, a set of CNVs, corresponding to loci containing non-LTR retrotransposon fragments, appeared significant, which were associated with localised CNV peaks in the genome-wide plot (Fig. 1c). Notably, two recurrent CNV loci fell outside these known dynamic regions located on chr3.

To assess whether these CNVs extend beyond clinical settings, we analysed publicly available genomes of environmental C. auris isolates from the Andaman Islands and from stored apples23,24. SDs with ERG11 consistent with C3T2 are present in multiple azole-resistant isolates but absent in a drug-susceptible salt marsh isolate (Supplementary Fig. 1c, Supplementary Data 2). Chr1 SDs are not observed in environmental isolates, suggesting lower prevalence or stability.

Across clinical and environmental isolates, chr3 SDs are the dominant CNV event (47% of isolates) and consistently focus on the ERG11 locus. (+)ERG11 SDs are also reported in experimentally evolved azole-resistant strains of clade 216. We reanalysed the publicly available WGS data for these strains (F30 and FC17 in Supplementary Fig. 1d) by adding CEN coordinates. These SDs are found to be centromere-inclusive, carrying ERG11 ((+)CEN (+)ERG11), like C3T3 revealed by our screen. Considering the frequency of occurrence, we decided to investigate the two CNV hotspots identified on chr3. The breakpoints of the (+)ERG11 SDs vary across isolates (Fig. 2a), consistent with earlier reports of ERG11 CNVs with heterogeneous boundaries25. Some isolates exhibit single-unit duplications, while others show multi-step nested amplification profiles (Fig. 2b), likely resulting from sequential duplication cycles, displaying a stair-step pattern, like the events reported in C. albicans previously26. To understand the pattern of duplication associated with regions spanning ERG11, we performed long-read sequencing of S61, which displays a stair-step pattern. The assembly of the long reads revealed a complex rearrangement involving the two hotspot loci located on chr3 in strains harbouring the C3T1 pattern. A duplication event with a breakpoint in THR1 results in amplification of a segment including HMS1, ERG11, and THR1. A second tandem duplication further increases the ERG11 copy number to four. A duplicated, inverted copy of the chr3 locus 1 is present between the two tandem copies of locus 2 (Fig. 2c). Analysing the coordinates of SDs across isolates, we observed a higher breakpoint density in three regions: THR1, B9J08_001445, and the intergenic region between HMS1 and CFL4. Analysis of the region spanning CFL4–HMS1–ERG11–THR1–SAM51–GYP6 revealed multiple poly-A tracts of varying lengths and frequent short tandem repeat pairs (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Combined with antifungal selection pressure, these sequence features likely contribute to the recurrent emergence and positive selection of (+)ERG11 SDs. Comparative genomics with other Candida species where ERG11 CNVs occur revealed conserved synteny in the ERG11 locus and shared breakpoint-associated genes between C. parapsilosis and C. auris. However, cross-species synteny analysis identified recurrent synteny breaks mapping to the ERG11 neighbourhood with breakpoints distinguishing C. albicans and C. parapsilosis from C. auris (Supplementary Fig. 2b), hinting at the presence of structurally flexible chromosomal regions. Multiple fragile sites can not only account for the diverse breakpoints but also explain the ERG11 copy numbers observed in the clinical isolate collection. As the ERG11 copy number predicted by the CNV screen was confirmed by the S61 genome assembly, we examined a subset of isolates and confirmed diverse copy numbers using qPCR (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

Resistance to azoles occurs through alterations in the drug target mediated by point mutations, increased drug efflux, or increased copy numbers of the drug target, ERG1127. As the structural changes observed in the screen consistently encompassed ERG11, we next evaluated how CNVs interact with known resistance-associated mutations in ERG11. Variant calling identified two major azole resistance-associated SNPs, Y132F and K143R, in the clade 1 clinical isolate cohort, both known to impair azole binding to Erg11p in C. albicans28. Y132F predominated in our cohort of South Asian clade 1 isolates (Fig. 2d). The two mutations are mutually exclusive in the dataset, except S6, which lacks any ERG11 mutation. We also examined the relationship between CNVs resulting from SDs and resistance-associated point mutations in ERG11. Mapping SDs to the SNP background of strains reveals that C3T1 is the most frequently occurring SD pattern among ERG11Y132F-carrying strains (Fig. 2e), with occasional C3T2 type. In contrast, ERG11K143R-carrying strains predominantly possess the C3T2 pattern (Fig. 2f). A positive correlation of SNPs and SDs with the MIC50 values suggests that Y132F is associated with high fluconazole MICs, and the co-occurrence of Y132F and ERG11 amplification significantly increases resistance to fluconazole and voriconazole but not to any other drugs tested (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 2d–h). No significant combinatorial action was observed for K143R with ERG11 amplification. However, the low number of K143R strains in the cohort limits interpretation. To investigate the role of increased ERG11 copy number in azole resistance, we studied ERG11 expression in two strains under drug treatment. Isolate S3 harbours the mutation Y132F and no additional ERG11 copies. Isolate S61 harbours the same mutation and four copies of the ERG11 gene. Under drug-untreated conditions, compared with S3, S61 exhibited higher basal levels of ERG11 transcripts, which were further increased upon treatment with 64 µg/mL, the tentative breakpoint for fluconazole, and returned to basal levels at 256 µg/mL. The transcript levels were not significantly altered in S3 at any drug concentration (Fig. 2h).

In summary, the CNV screen of C. auris clade 1 clinical isolate cohort presented here suggests that (a) CNVs are not randomly distributed in the C. auris genome, rather they are concentrated in specific regions indicative of structural flexibility, (b) there are CNVs present in common between the clinical and environmental isolates (c) (+)ERG11 SDs, the most frequently occurring event, have diverse breakpoints and copy numbers, and (d) (+)ERG11 SDs are associated with increased azole resistance in strains carrying ERG11 mutations.

Centromere-inclusive duplicated segments of chromosome 1 exist as supernumerary chromosomes

In the CNV screen, we identified a subset of clade 1 isolates carrying centromere (CEN)-inclusive segmental duplications (Supplementary Fig. 3a). As a chromosome with two functional centromeres often generates dicentric instability, we hypothesised that these SDs would result in altered karyotypes. To test this, we performed electrophoretic karyotyping using clamped homogeneous electrical field (CHEF) electrophoresis across the clade 1 isolate collection. The resulting profiles revealed pronounced karyotype heterogeneity, consistent with the high karyotype plasticity previously reported in C. auris15. Strikingly, each of the four isolates carrying CEN-inclusive SDs also harboured an additional chromosome, smaller than the native chromosome set (Fig. 3a).

To characterise these additional chromosomes, we generated a pan-karyotype profile by integrating CHEF data with available clade 1 genome assemblies. The seven conserved chromosomes (core chromosomes) constitute the baseline karyotype. In contrast, smaller variably present non-native chromosomes were defined as supernumerary chromosomes (SNCs) (Fig. 3b). Read-depth analyses matched the sizes of the duplicated regions in three SNC-containing ((+)SNC) strains. A ~300 kb-long SNC was detected in S4 and S32, while S28 carried a ~550 kb-long SNC (Fig. 3c). Their concordance with CHEF band sizes supports that CEN-inclusive SDs exist as independent chromosomes. In S23, however, the CHEF band (~230 kb) did not match the predicted SD length, implying additional rearrangements contributed to the generation of the SNC in this isolate.

As chromosome stability relies on centromere integrity, we confirmed CEN duplication events by quantifying the CEN1 copy number in (+)SNC strains using quantitative PCR with CEN4 as the control. All three isolates with chr1 SDs carried approximately double CEN1 copies compared to the (-)SNC controls (Supplementary Fig. 3b). We then asked whether centromere retention is a general feature of stabilised SDs. A tandem CEN-inclusive SD, reported previously in the clade 2 type-strain B1122022, was verified by PCR across the unique rearrangement junction (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Further analysis is required to determine whether they also exist as separate chromosomes.

As telomere acquisition is essential for the long-term maintenance of acentric fragments as independent chromosomes, we assessed whether SNCs had acquired telomeres. Using ONT read mapping to infer telomere-SD junctions, we identified unique TEL-SD junctions in all (+)SNC isolates but not in the reference genome (Supplementary Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 2). Thus, CEN-inclusive SDs can be stabilised as SNCs through telomere acquisition, consistent with centric fragment stabilisation in C. glabrata12.

Whether such SNCs become stably integrated into the core genome or are lost over time during clonal propagation remains unknown. To explore their fate across generations, we serially passaged a (+)SNC strain (S4 harbouring a ~300 kb SNC) for ~1500 generations in YPD at 30 °C under a single colony bottleneck regime across 12 independent mutation-accumulation (MA) lines (lines A-L) in the absence of antifungal drug pressure (Fig. 3d). Karyotype analysis of the MA lines at bottleneck 1 showed that all lines initially retained the SNC. Three lines at bottleneck-50 had lost the SNC, and one line acquired a novel ~500 kb SNC. At bottleneck-100, this novel SNC was also lost, leaving five (-)SNC lines (Fig. 3e).

Whole-genome sequencing of the parental strain and ten evolved lines confirmed these karyotypic changes: at the 100th bottleneck, (+)SNC lines retained the original SD, whereas (-)SNC lines had lost the SD (Supplementary Fig. 3e). We also confirmed the loss of CEN1 duplication in the terminal line 100-A, which had lost the SNC (Supplementary Fig. 3f). Sequencing of the bottleneck-50 intermediate carrying the ~500 kb chromosome revealed the original ~300 kb duplication from chr1 (Supplementary Fig. 3e, g). The size discrepancy between CHEF and CNV data may arise from recombination between the SNC and its homologous native chromosome, chr1, which transfers subtelomeric DNA and increases the SNC length without altering the CNV read depth.

In summary, CEN-inclusive SDs originating from chr1 acquire TELs to form stable SNCs in C. auris clinical isolates. They can be stably maintained, occasionally lost during propagation, or transiently rearranged. Their persistence in a C. auris cell likely depends on the retention of centromeres, the acquisition of telomeres, the genomic context, and additional factors that maintain genome stability.

A novel small supernumerary chromosome drives echinocandin tolerance via the Eagle effect

Aneuploidy can alter diverse phenotypes in yeasts, including drug resistance, thermotolerance, and carbon utilisation29. To assess the physiological implications of SDs existing as SNCs, we leveraged mutation-accumulation (MA) lines derived from a common parental strain, comprising both (+)SNC and (−)SNC derivatives with minimal background variation. We performed a dilution-based phenotypic screen across antifungals, chemical stresses, and temperatures.

Among all stresses tested, a growth advantage was observed exclusively in the presence of caspofungin (CPF). (+)SNC MA lines exhibited enhanced growth compared to (−)SNC lines (Fig. 4a), indicating reduced CPF susceptibility. In addition, (+)SNC lines displayed slower growth at elevated temperature and under SDS stress, suggesting a context-dependent fitness cost associated with maintaining an additional chromosome. We further tested two additional drugs of the echinocandin class, micafungin and anidulafungin, and observed a similar susceptibility shift, pointing to a pan-echinocandin effect (Fig. 4b). In intermediate MA lines, SNC loss restored CPF susceptibility, confirming a direct association between the presence of SNC and reduced echinocandin susceptibility (Fig. 4c).

Reduced CPF susceptibility in C. auris may reflect mutation-driven resistance or the Eagle effect, wherein growth paradoxically improves at supra-MIC drug levels while remaining suppressed at lower levels5,30. To discriminate between these outcomes, we monitored CPF dose–response profiles of four MA line representatives: the parental (+)SNC strain (P), a terminal (−)SNC line (100-A), and two (+)SNC lines (100-B and 50-E). Unlike 100-A, strains P, 100-B and 50-E exhibited pronounced growth recovery at high CPF concentrations, a hallmark of the Eagle effect, while remaining inhibited at low concentrations (Fig. 4d). This phenomenon was confirmed in liquid and solid media at both 30 °C and 37 °C, respectively (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 4a). Scanning electron microscopy revealed dramatic CPF-induced morphological alterations in Eagle effect-positive cells: the cells are large and spherical, unlike the uniformly elongated cells that are untreated. The population appeared heterogeneous, comprising cells with variable surface textures and sizes, and was often arranged in chains, suggestive of cell separation defects (Fig. 4f).

The Eagle effect is known to be a manifestation of intrinsic compensatory pathways, such as the calcineurin and high osmolarity glycerol (HOG) pathways, resulting in cell wall modifications, including increased chitin content31,32 (Fig. 5a). We first examined the cell wall ultrastructure of a (+)SNC strain S4 using transmission electron microscopy. Untreated, healthy cells exhibited a compact, well-organised cell wall composed of two distinct layers: an inner dense layer and an outer fibrillar layer. In contrast, CPF-treated cells displaying the Eagle effect showed a less-defined bilayer organisation, accompanied by a noticeable thickening of the cell wall. These structural alterations suggest stress-induced remodelling of the cell wall under high CPF treatment (Fig. 5b). These observations also indicate that the SNC-driven Eagle effect is accompanied by cell wall changes previously reported in Candida sp.33. Calcofluor staining further indicated increased chitin and clumping associated with impaired cell separation (Supplementary Fig. 4b). The Eagle effect can also be abrogated using chemicals that target various steps in these compensatory pathways (Fig. 5a). Checkerboard assays demonstrated that inhibitors of calcineurin (FK506), chitin synthase (nikkomycin Z) and Hsp90 (geldanamycin) each abrogated the Eagle effect and synergised with CPF (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 4c). In addition, we also tested if the genes linked to calcineurin pathway are differentially expressed during the Eagle effect. Out of the regulators (HSP90, CRZ1, and CMP1) and effectors (CHS2, CHS3, and CHS8) (Fig. 5d), HSP90 was upregulated in cells grown at 32 µg/mL (Fig. 5e), a drug concentration that induces the Eagle effect. Together, these data indicate that SNC-mediated paradoxical growth involves canonical Hsp90–calcineurin pathways and cell-wall remodelling.

The SD on chr1 leading to SNC formation in the strain S4 includes 147 genes (Supplementary data 3). A Gene Ontology analysis of the duplicated genes revealed the presence of genes involved in response to chemical (GO ID: 42221), signal transduction (GO ID: 7165), and cell wall organization (GO ID: 71555). We studied the expression patterns of a set of selected genes from these GO categories. Genes including a homolog of C. albicans CHS1 (chitin synthase), CLA4 (Serine/threonine specific protein kinase), and TPK1 (cAMP-dependent protein kinase catalytic subunit), which are associated with responses to cell wall perturbing agents in C. albicans34,35 were upregulated in the SNC-harbouring strain, consistent with gene dosage effects arising from segmental aneuploidy (Fig. 5f). We also detected the upregulation of PLC1, involved in calcium ion homeostasis36, and potentially regulating the calcineurin pathway exhibiting the Eagle effect. The expression of these genes, however, was not further altered by the drug treatment.

Finally, to assess the clinical relevance of the SNC-linked Eagle effect, we screened CPF responses in the clade 1 clinical cohort. Four phenotypic classes were observed: resistance, susceptibility, trailing growth, and the Eagle effect (Supplementary Fig. 4d). These responses were confirmed in a few isolates using growth assays and E-strips (Supplementary Fig. 4e, f). While FKS1 hotspot mutations (S639F/P; F635L/Y)37 accounted for resistance in 10 isolates (Supplementary Table 3), most isolates exhibiting the Eagle effect lacked FKS1 mutations. We further confirmed the presence of FKS1 mutations in the cohort by Sanger sequencing. The integration of CNV profiles and drug responses revealed that although the SNC-driven Eagle effect is relatively infrequent, it enables survival under drug pressure in clinical isolates. No FKS2 hotspot mutations were detected in the isolates. This highlights diverse, non-mutational mechanisms contributing to reduced echinocandin susceptibility in C. auris.

Microevolution of antifungal resistance shaped by the interplay of CNVs and SNPs within clade 1

To assess how CNVs and resistance-associated SNPs shape population structure within clade 1, we constructed a phylogeny of the cohort of South Asian clinical isolates, alongside isolates from the Andaman Islands and stored apples, using genome-wide biallelic SNPs (Fig. 6a). We also mapped different events discussed in this study onto the tree to understand their interplay. This ensemble illustrates microevolutionary patterns within clade 1, which comprises three previously defined subclades (1a-1c)22. Most isolates harboured the ERG11 mutation Y132F, whereas those with K143R formed a distinct subcluster that included environmental isolates and the reference strain B11205. The presence of environmental isolates in this subcluster suggests a shared lineage between clinical and non-nosocomial reservoirs.

Mapping SD patterns onto the phylogeny revealed distinct evolutionary trajectories of different SD types. Isolates with the C3T1 chr3 SD pattern formed a relatively cohesive cluster, suggesting that they likely expanded from a common ancestor. In contrast, the C3T2 pattern was distributed across multiple branches, indicating independent or recurrent events. A single isolate carried the C3T3 configuration and remained separate from other (+)SD strains. Among Andaman isolates, those with the C3T2 pattern grouped with clinical isolates, showing similar azole resistance profiles. In contrast, the azole-susceptible isolate, lacking SDs, clustered with (−)SD clinical isolates. Across this clinical cohort of clade 1 isolates, locus 2 on chr3 is occasionally found alone (pattern C3T2), whereas locus 1 is never observed without locus 2, indicating a strong linkage between the two loci. These observations also suggest that locus 1 is secondary to the positive selection acting on locus 2 due to ERG11 amplification. However, the possibility of conversion from C3T1 to C3T2 following the loss of the first duplication in the former, or a shared mutational mechanism, cannot be ruled out without further investigation.

As chr1 SDs were associated with reduced caspofungin susceptibility, we next evaluated echinocandin-linked genomic variation within the phylogeny. (+)SNC isolates and FKS1 mutants were sparse compared to the events leading to azole resistance, consistent with overall higher echinocandin efficacy. The presence of two genetically related strains carrying a ~300 kb-long SNC derived from the C1T2 group suggests that they inherited from a recent common ancestor, followed by subsequent SNP diversification. In contrast, the C1T1-associated SNC occurred on a different phylogenetic background and within the K143R subcluster, indicating at least two independent origins of chr1 SDs in clade 1. Among FKS1 mutations associated with echinocandin resistance, two genetically related strains harbour S639F, while a subcluster exists for the mutation F635L. There were no strains that harbour both FKS1 mutations and SNCs. Additionally, we detected coexistence of ERG11 mutation, ERG11 SD, and FKS1 mutation in three clinical isolates. Out of these, two isolates with a point mutation Y132F in the ERG11 gene and SDs co-occurring with a point mutation S639F in the FKS1 gene exhibit elevated MIC50 values for azoles and echinocandins, consistent with the emergence of multidrug resistance.

Together, these data support multiple evolutionary routes toward reduced antifungal susceptibility within clade 1, with ERG11-containing chr3 duplications showing signatures of lineage expansion, whereas chr1 duplications arise less frequently and on divergent genetic backgrounds.

Discussion

Structural alterations of the genome enable fungal pathogens to survive and evolve under antifungal drug stress. While azole and echinocandin resistance in fungal pathogens has primarily been attributed to point mutations in a limited set of genes, such as ERG11 and FKS1, accumulating evidence suggests that genome plasticity also plays a significant role7. By analysing a cohort of clade 1 isolates of the multidrug-resistant pathogen C. auris, we show that copy-number variation arising from segmental duplications contributes to antifungal adaptation and shapes microevolution within a clade, a relevant aspect given that outbreaks are typically clade-restricted38. Our findings reveal that C. auris utilises structural genome variation at two independent hotspots to adapt to the two major classes of antifungal drugs. At a high frequency, segmental duplications in chr3 that encompass ERG11—often combined with resistance-associated SNPs—increase clinical azole resistance. Independently, centromere-inclusive duplications on chr1 form supernumerary chromosomes (SNCs) that enable paradoxical growth termed as the Eagle effect in echinocandin concentrations above the MIC, likely through calcineurin-mediated stress responses (Fig. 6b). These CNVs operate in parallel with point mutations in ERG11 and FKS1 at the population level, generating a multi-layered landscape of reduced susceptibility (Fig. 6c). Phylogenetic analyses reveal vital clues to the clonal expansion of ERG11 CNVs within clade 1, distribution of FKS1 mutations, the emergence of multidrug resistance, and possible transmission of isolates between clinical and environmental niches.

Across fungi, mutational landscapes beyond SNPs are increasingly being revealed, reflecting the recurrent exploitation of structural genome plasticity under drug pressure. CNVs represent a dynamic source of genome diversity that contributes to AFDR across pathogenic Candida species39–41.

ERG11 amplification events, both tandem and segmental, have been documented in multiple Candida species and shown to occur with significant structural diversity25,42,43. Genomic expansion leading to tandem copies of ERG11 carrying the Y132F and S154F substitutions represents a major azole resistance mechanism in C. tropicalis clinical isolates. In fluconazole-resistant C. parapsilosis clinical isolates, ERG11 CNVs exhibit high diversity, occurring as segmental duplications and short-range amplifications, similar to those observed in C. auris isolates described in this study. Together, these observations suggest that amplification of ERG11 is a convergent adaptive strategy employed by multiple Candida species to enhance azole resistance, often acting in concert with resistance-associated point mutations. SNCs have been extensively characterised in plant fungal pathogens, such as Zymoseptoria tritici, Magnaporthe oryzae, and Fusarium oxysporum, where they contribute to virulence and rapid adaptive evolution44–47. In contrast, SNC-mediated modulation of antifungal susceptibility in human fungal pathogens remains sparsely reported. Here, we provide evidence for an SNC-mediated chromosomal basis for the Eagle effect - a longstanding but mechanistically unresolved phenomenon described across bacteria and fungi. The SNCs responsible for the Eagle effect identified in this study represent centromere-inclusive SDs that acquire telomeres in the process, paralleling the stabilisation of centric fragments in Candida glabrata12, engineered dicentrics in Saccharomyces cerevisiae48, and experimentally evolved strains of C. auris13. Generation of SNCs, therefore, constitutes a naturally accessible mode of genome innovation that enhances drug tolerance. The mitotic instability observed in SNCs compared to the core chromosomes aligns with the higher loss rate of accessory chromosomes reported previously in other fungal species12,13,44. Although whole-chromosome duplications have been reported in C. auris17,49, none could be detected in our screen. However, the possibility that whole-chromosome duplications act as intermediates that undergo DNA sequence attrition, generating centric fragments as SNC precursors, cannot be ruled out. The structural variant-driven drug resistance landscape coexists and may be influenced by point mutations. In our dataset, all strains with the SDs harbour either of the two ERG11 point mutations, suggesting that the selective advantage conferred by the point mutation likely contributes to the selection of gene amplification.

Our study identified ERG11 mutation, gene amplification, and FKS1 mutation in the same strain, revealing a complex landscape driven by multiple events underlying multidrug resistance in C. auris. From a clinical standpoint, the persistence and spread of (+)ERG11 SD-harbouring strains underscore the inefficacy of azole-based antifungals against C. auris clade 1 clinical isolates. Additionally, the instability of SNCs suggests that reduced echinocandin susceptibility, manifesting as the Eagle effect, may be transient and reversible, thereby challenging the binary classification of susceptible versus resistant isolates50. Routine MIC testing may, therefore, underestimate in vivo survival potential. The Eagle effect, while recognised in bacteria6, has uncertain clinical consequences in fungi51; yet in vivo studies suggest that supra-MIC growth can affect therapeutic outcomes52,53. Given that caspofungin plasma concentrations can fluctuate and, at times, exceed typical MIC thresholds54, SNC-mediated growth at high drug levels may impact treatment success. Inhibition of calcineurin and Hsp90 has shown promise against multiple fungal pathogens55. Our observations support the potential of such combinatorial regimens to suppress drug tolerance behaviours, such as the Eagle effect, that evade standard diagnostics.

A priority for future work is to define the driver(s) within the duplicated segments that mediate these responses. Studying the effects of aneuploidy and drug exposure on the transcript and protein levels of duplicated genes may help determine whether reduced drug susceptibility is driven by a single or multiple genes. While the Eagle effect likely reflects multiple stress-response pathways, results of this study pinpoint a specific genomic region that confers this phenotype. In particular, we observed that the expression of Hsp90 is upregulated during the Eagle effect; Hsp90 is implicated in echinocandin resistance by activating calcineurin in pathogenic fungi56. The fact that multiple stress-related genes are upregulated in an SNC-containing isolate suggests that such fungal cells may be primed for the Eagle effect by the aneuploidy itself. Similarly, collateral gene amplification surrounding ERG11 may confer additional fitness benefits to a fungal pathogen. Further characterisation, including long-read sequencing and genome assembly of clinical isolates with varying ERG11 copy numbers, is required to determine whether multiple rearrangement patterns result in (+)ERG11 SDs. The association and impact of CNV locus 1 on locus 2 in C3T1 also requires further investigation. The recurrence of segmental duplications at conserved ERG11 neighbourhoods across species suggests biased selection of fragility hotspots under drug selection. Understanding why these regions are predisposed to SDs will require systematic mapping of conserved structural features across fungal genomes. There are two features common in chr1 and chr3 SDs: (a) they occur on chromosomes that harbour clusters of non-LTR retrotransposons, (b) their proximity to the centromeres in two acrocentric chromosomes—the ERG11 neighbourhood is located approximately 40 kb away from CEN3, and the SNC breakpoints are approximately 50 kb away from CEN1. Extending similar analyses to the clinical isolate collections of other geographical clades will be useful for understanding the role of structural variation in AFDR, as well as its genomic and chromosomal context. It will also add to our understanding of clade-specific evolution.

Collectively, this study illustrates how a haploid global human fungal pathogen adapts under drug pressure by leveraging genome plasticity rather than sexual recombination. Incorporating CNV and structural variant profiling into antifungal surveillance, together with tolerance-focused phenotyping, may be the need of the hour for anticipating the ongoing evolution of multidrug resistance. Furthermore, combining frontline antifungals with inhibitors of compensatory stress pathways may offer a more durable therapeutic strategy against multidrug-resistant global fungal pathogens, such as C. auris.

Methods

Strains, growth media, reagents, and culture conditions

The clinical isolates, MIC, and sample details were obtained from the National Culture Collection of Pathogenic Fungi (NCCPF) maintained at the Post Graduate Institute of Medical Education and Research (PGIMER), Chandigarh, India. The clade status of the strains was identified as reported previously57. All strains were grown in a medium consisting of yeast extract (1%), peptone (2%), and dextrose (2%), supplemented with 100 mg/L uridine. Solid media were prepared by adding 2% agar. The stocks are stored at −80 °C, in 15% glycerol. Growth under different stress agents was tested in YPD supplemented with caspofungin (CPF) diacetate (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: SML0425), micafungin (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: SML2268), anidulafungin (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: SML2288), sodium dodecyl sulphate (Himedia, Cat No: GRM886), and amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: A4888 for dilution spotting, and Y0000005 for AFST), fluconazole (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: F8929-100MG), voriconazole (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: PHR1892-1G), itraconazole (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: I6657-100MG), and posaconazole (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat No: SML2287-25MG). MICTM strips of CPF (Himedia, Cat No: EM119) were used to test the antifungal susceptibility. All the oligonucleotides used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 4.

Whole genome sequencing

Short-read sequencing

Genomic DNA was isolated using the glass bead method. DNA concentrations were determined using Qubit™ dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Catalog: Q32853, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The readings were taken in a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Short-read sequencing was performed using either the Illumina or PacBio Onso system.

For Illumina sequencing, the genomic DNA library was prepared using the NEB Next Ultra DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) after fragmenting 1 µg of genomic DNA with the Bioruptor to generate ~300 bp long fragments. The library quality and quantity were checked using the Bioanalyser and Qubit, respectively.

For Onso sequencing, library preparation was performed using QIASeq FX DNA Library Preparation protocol (Cat#180475) by following the manufacturer’s instructions using 50 ng of Qubit quantified DNA, which was enzymatically fragmented, end-repaired, and A-tailed in a one-tube reaction using the FX Enzyme Mix provided in the QIASeq FX DNA kit. The end-repaired and adenylated fragments were subjected to adapter ligation, whereby an index-incorporated Illumina adapter was ligated to generate a sequencing library. The library was subjected to 6 cycles of Indexing-PCR (Initial Denaturation at 98 °C for 2 min, followed by cycling (98 °C for 20 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s) and final extension at 72 °C for 1 min) to enrich the adapter-tagged fragments. Finally, the amplified library was purified using Cambrian Bioworks CamSelect NGS Beads (CBWD010) and Normalised using the Qiaseq Normaliser Kit, followed by a library quality control check. Illumina-compatible sequencing libraries were quantified using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), and their fragment size distribution was analysed on an Agilent 4150 TapeStation. Illumina-compatible libraries were converted to Onso-compatible libraries by Onso Library Conversion PCR.

Short-read sequencing was done at Clevergene Biocorp Pvt., Ltd., Bengaluru, India, and Genotypic Technology Pvt. Ltd, Bangalore, India.

Long-read sequencing and assembly

High-molecular-weight DNA from the three clinical isolates harbouring SNCs was extracted using a commercially available kit (NEB Monarch Genomic DNA Isolation Kit, T3010S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The DNA concentration was measured using the Qubit dsDNA quantification Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Q32851) in a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer.

Sequencing was performed on the Oxford Nanopore MinION platform (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxfordshire, UK, MN33891) using the ligation sequencing kit (SQK-LSK109) and native barcoding kit (EXPNBD104). The sample was sequenced on the MK1B MinION (MIN-101B) device with a FLO-MIN106 (FAW21612) (SpotON R9.4) flow cell using MinKNOW release 20.10.3. Raw nanopore reads were subjected to base-calling using Guppy (v6.5.7) with the parameters --flowcell FLO-MIN106 --kit SQK-LSK109 --barcode_kits EXP-NBD104.

For strain S61, barcodes from SQK-NBD114.96 kit (Oxford Nanopore Technology, UK) and native sequencing adapter using NEBNext ® Quick Ligation Module (E6056, New England Biolabs, MA, USA) were used. The library was quantified using Qubit and sequenced on Nanopore PromethION sequencer (PromethION24 and Data Acquisition Unit, ONT, Oxford, UK) using PromethION flow cell (FLO-PRO114-M). For genome assembly, the raw reads were processed using Porechop (Galaxy Version 0.2.4+galaxy1) and assembled using Flye, a de novo assembler (Galaxy Version 2.9.6+galaxy0). The assembly was then polished with Illumina reads using Pilon (Galaxy Version 1.20.1).

Copy number analysis

YMAP

The Illumina sequencing raw reads were mapped to the clade 1 genome assembly B11205 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCA_016772135.1) using YMAP (Yeast Mapping Analysis Pipeline)18. C. auris is a haploid species, so the baseline ploidy was 1. Corrections were applied for chromosome-end bias.

Control-FREEC

All WGS datasets were processed to detect genome-wide copy number variations (CNVs). The quality of the raw reads from 79 clinical isolates was checked using FastQC tool (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). Reads were filtered for the presence of Illumina adapters using Trimmomatic (version 0.39)58. The processed reads were aligned to the B11205 genome using Bowtie2 (version 2.3.5.1)59. The aligned reads in SAM format were compressed to BAM format using Samtools (version 1.10). Picard tools (https://github.com/broadinstitute/picard) were used to remove duplicate reads from the BAM files. These reads were further sorted and indexed using Samtools (version 2.3.5.1). Control-FREEC was used to detect genome-wide CNVs using these sorted BAM files as input, with telocentromeric parameter as zero and ploidy as 1 in the config file19. The statistical significance of the CNVs was analysed using an R script from the Control-FREEC toolkit. CNVs failing the statistical significance criteria (p > 0.05, Wilcoxon rank sum test) were filtered out. A subset of the CNVs was validated using read coverage analysis. We partitioned the C. auris B11205 genome into 250 bp bins and calculated the number of reads in each bin. These read counts were divided by the median read count for the genome to obtain normalised read counts for each bin. These normalised read counts were compared with the CNVs detected with Control-FREEC for validation. The plotting was done using R.

To generate the pan-CNV frequency map, we partitioned the B11205 genome into 1 bp bins, and a total of 1026 CNVs from the 79 isolates (Supplementary Data 2) were counted in these bins. Black and blue dots indicate centromeric and rDNA loci, respectively. For generating the SD distribution map, the SD events were extracted from the Control-FREEC CNV calls (Supplementary Data 2) and filtered to retain only copy-number gains. CNV size was computed as the difference between the annotated start and end coordinates. To visualise the genomic distribution of SD lengths, the chromosomes of the B11205 reference genome were concatenated linearly based on their sizes, and each SD was plotted as a vertical bar positioned at its genomic start coordinate, with bar height proportional to SD length (in kilobases). Chromosome boundaries were drawn using a cumulative offset derived from chromosome-specific maximum coordinates to ensure accurate delimitation. Isolate S10, which exhibits unusually large genome–wide duplication artefacts, was excluded from these plots to avoid skewing the size distribution. All visualisation was performed in R (v4.x) using ggplot2, and plots were exported using ggsave to preserve width and resolution.

From the CNV list for all the isolates, we extracted the breakpoints of CNVs near the ERG11 locus. Coordinates of short tandem repeats (STR) of length 10 bp and above were extracted using Unipro UGENE (v36.0), and poly-A tracts were detected using Tandem Repeat Finder (https://tandem.bu.edu/). Midpoints of STR regions and poly-A tracts were used for mapping to the annotated ORFs along with the CNV breakpoints.

Telomere detection using unique junction analysis

To detect TEL sequences specifically associated with SNCs, we concatenated native chr1 telomeric repeats to the ends of chr1 SD sequences. We used these DNA sequence constructs as queries against the local databases of Oxford Nanopore reads. A non-duplicated chromosomal region of equivalent length was used as a control (Supplementary Table 2). The unique junctions in the three (+) SNC isolates were identified by aligning the sequences of these junctions with the local Nanopore read database generated using BLAST (version 2.9.0). B11205 genome assembly (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/assembly/GCA_016772135.1) was used as a reference for the analysis.

Chromosomal plug preparation and Electrophoretic karyotyping

Overnight cultures from single colonies were used as inoculum for secondary cultures, which were grown till OD600 = 0.9. Approximately 3 OD cells were used for chromosomal plug preparation, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Bio-Rad), using Cleancut agarose (0.6%), lyticase enzyme, and Proteinase K. The chromosomes embedded in the agarose plugs were separated on a 1.0% agarose gel (Bio-Rad) using 0.5X TBE as the running buffer. The run protocol is as follows: 60–60 s switch, 6 V/cm, 120° over 8 h at 12 °C, followed by 90–150 s switch, 6 V/cm, 120° over 18 h at 12 °C. The run was performed in the CHEF-DR III system (Bio-Rad). The gel was stained with ethidium bromide post-run, and the bands were visualised using the Gel documentation system (Bio-Rad).

Mutation-accumulation (MA) lines

To calculate the growth rate and average generation time, (+)SNC strain S4 was streaked onto a plate to form single colonies. At different time points starting from 24 h, colonies were suspended in sterile water, and the number of cells was counted using a haemocytometer. Specific growth rate (r) was calculated as ln (number of cells)/time, and generation time was calculated as ln (2)/r. These parameters were used to calculate the number of generations. Single colonies from the parental strain were used to establish parallel evolving lines. The lines were passaged every 48 h at 30 °C on solid yeast extract-peptone-dextrose (YPD) media by picking one single colony each time, thus introducing population bottlenecks. For the intermediate stocks at every 10th bottleneck, half of the colony was used to make a small patch, which was frozen as the glycerol stock. The other half of the colony was used for the subsequent bottleneck.

qPCR

Genomic DNA was isolated from the strains using the glass-bead method. The concentration and purity of the extracted DNA were determined using a Nanodrop (ND-1000 spectrophotometer). Approximately two ng of DNA was used for the qPCR reactions. Primers specific to CEN1 and CEN4 (listed in Supplementary Table 4) and SensiFAST SYBR No ROX kit were used for the reactions. The program used the following steps: 94 °C for 2 min, 94 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s for 40 cycles. Three biological replicates, each with three technical replicates, were used for each reaction. The relative copy number was calculated using the formula:

2∨ (∆Ct CEN1(calibrator-sample))/2∨ (∆Ct CEN4 (calibrator-sample)), where the (−)SNC strain S3 was taken as the calibrator strain, and the (+)SNC strains were considered the samples.

For determining ERG11 copy number,

2∨ (∆Ct ERG11(calibrator-sample))/2∨ (∆Ct CEN4 (calibrator-sample)), where the (−)SD strain S52 was taken as the calibrator strain, and the (+)SD strains were considered the samples.

Growth kinetics

The growth assays were performed in 96-well plates in YPD with different concentrations of the indicated drug. Two-fold serial dilutions of the drugs were inoculated with approximately 105 cells from log-phase cultures. The plate was incubated in Varioskan Lux (Thermo Scientific) at 30 °C for 48 h with continuous shaking at 180 rpm. The optical density at 600 nm was measured every 60 min. The data were collected and visualised using SkanIt software version 5. The graphs were drawn using GraphPad Prism.

Antifungal susceptibility assays

The MICs of the clinical isolates shown in Supplementary Table 1 were determined using the CLSI protocol and RPMI-1640 medium. The Eagle effect was characterised using the same protocol and YPD medium. The heat maps based on growth were drawn using SRplot60.

Dilution-spot assay

Strains were grown to log phase, and an approximately equal number of cells was suspended in sterile water. The cell number was adjusted to 107 and was diluted tenfold. Approximately 3 μL of different dilutions was spotted on YPD plates supplemented with various chemical agents (as indicated in the figure legends). The plates were incubated at 30 °C and imaged.

Antifungal testing using Checkerboard assays

Effects of different drug combinations were tested using checkerboard assays. Briefly, different dilutions of CPF were prepared first in wells of different rows in 96-well plates. Next, dilutions of the second drug (FK506, geldanamycin, and nikkomycin Z) were prepared in wells of columns. The control sets with only CPF and only the second drug are given in the first row and the last column, respectively. The plates were incubated for 48 h at 37 °C to score for the Eagle effect.

Phylogenetic analysis and genome-level comparison

Along with the WGS data of 79 clinical isolates, publicly available WGS datasets of some environmental strains isolated from the Andaman Islands and stored apples were used for phylogenetic analysis. WGS reads from representative isolates from different subclades of clade 1 (subclade 1a-B8441, subclade 1b-B13916, 1c-B11205) were used for inferring the subclade structure. The genome assemblies used are GCA_016772135.1 (for the strain B11205) and GCA_016772235.1 (for the strain B13916). The genome assembly of B8841 (Genbank assembly: GCA_002759435.2), a drug-susceptible isolate, was used as a reference genome, and the WGS reads were processed to obtain BAM files as described above (CNV analysis using Control-FREEC). FreeBayes was used to call genome-wide variants (version 1.3.2)61. Only biallelic SNPs were extracted from total variants using bcftools (version 1.10.2). The VCF file was converted to a Phylip file using the vcf2phylip Python package. A maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree was built using raxmlGUI 2.062, with 1000 bootstrap replicates using the TVM + I + G4 model. The phylogenetic tree was visualised using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/).

The genome-level synteny analyses were done using Easyfig (version 2.2.5)63.

Gene Ontology analysis

The duplicated genes on the SNC were mapped to their homologs in C. albicans using information from the Candida Genome Database. All genes except 7 could be mapped to C. albicans homologs. The gene list was then analysed using the GO tools available at the Candida Genome Database. For CGD GO analysis, the homolog gene list was used as input for the GO Slim mapper. The GO Biological Process was analysed to identify associated GO terms.

Quantitative real-time PCR and analysis

For measuring ERG11 transcript levels, strains with only mutations or mutation-amplification were grown overnight. Overnight cultures were used to inoculate fresh medium at an initial OD600 of 0.1. Once the cultures reached the early log phase (OD600 of 0.4–0.5), different fluconazole concentrations were added to the samples. The drug-treated and untreated cultures were allowed to grow for 3 h before harvesting the cells. Untreated samples served as controls. For the Eagle effect, a (-)SNC strain, 100-A, was used as the control. (+) SNC strain, the parent line, was grown in the presence and absence of CPF (32 µg/mL). Total RNA was extracted from the cells using an RNA purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog no. K0731) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA samples were subjected to electrophoresis on 2% agarose gels to assess integrity. The quality of the preparations was confirmed using absorbance readings at 230, 260, and 280 nm. RNAs with 260:280 and 260:230 ratios of 2 or higher were used in RT-qPCR assays. The samples were treated with DNase to remove contaminating genomic DNA. Removal of genomic DNA was confirmed using PCR of DNase-treated samples, with the untreated samples serving as controls. cDNA was synthesized from the treated samples using RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog no: K1622), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each qPCR assay with samples treated or untreated with the antifungal drug was performed in triplicate on the Bio-Rad Real-time PCR machine. The housekeeping gene EFB1 was used as a reference to assess ERG11 expression. LSC2 was used as the housekeeping gene for the Eagle effect studies. The specificity of the qPCR products was evaluated by melt curve analysis.

Calcofluor staining and fluorescence microscopy

Calcofluor white stain (CFW, Fluka, 1 g/L) was freshly prepared before use by mixing an equal volume of the stain with 10% (w/v) potassium hydroxide. The cells were stained in the dark for 20 min and then washed with 1X PBS to remove the excess stain. The cells were imaged using Zeiss LSM 880 AxioObserver with an excitation wavelength of 405 nm and an emission wavelength of 446 nm. The images were processed using ImageJ 1.52p. The fluorescence intensity was measured within rectangular ROIs of equal area placed across the stained cell wall in the raw images to ensure that changes in total cell size did not affect the measurement.

Electron microscopy

For scanning electron microscopy, cells grown in the presence of different CPF concentrations were fixed overnight in a solution of 4% formaldehyde and 1% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer. The cells were then washed with the buffer, treated with 1% osmium tetroxide, and washed again in cacodylate buffer. The cells were then subjected to dehydration through a series of ethanol washes before being critical point dried and mounted on aluminium stubs. This step was followed by sputter coating with gold using a Pelco SC-7 Sputter coater. The samples were imaged with a scanning electron microscope (The MERLIN Compact VP).

For transmission electron microscopy, cells grown in the presence of different CPF concentrations were fixed overnight in a solution of 4% formaldehyde, 1% glutaraldehyde, and 0.02% calcium chloride in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer. The cells were then postfixed with 1% osmium tetroxide and 1% potassium ferricyanide and stained with 1% uranyl acetate. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded series of ethanol solutions and embedded in Spurr’s resin. Sections of thickness 60 nm were cut using an ultramicrotome (RMC PowerTome Ultramicrotome PC) and placed on copper grids and stained with uranyl acetate and Reynolds solution. They were then imaged using a Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN transmission electron microscope.

Statistics and reproducibility

Electrophoretic karyotyping of the 79 clinical isolates shown in Fig. 3a was performed once as a screening experiment. All isolates identified as carrying a supernumerary chromosome were subsequently re-analysed by independent CHEF experiments, confirming the presence and size of the additional chromosome. Mutation-accumulation bottleneck experiments (Fig. 3e) were performed using independent lineages, and each lineage was analysed once by CHEF at the indicated bottleneck intervals. The E-strip assays and confirmation of the SNC-driven Eagle effect on solid media were performed twice with similar results.
