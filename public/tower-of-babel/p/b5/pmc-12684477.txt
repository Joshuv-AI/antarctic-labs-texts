Real‐World Observations in Patients With Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy Treated With Mavacamten: Evidence of Favorable Disease Modification
Authors: Milind Y Desai, Andrew Gaballa, Yuichiro Okushi, Mohammed Abusafia, Shada Jadam, Katy Rutkowski, Susan Ospina, Sanaa Sultana, Maran Thamilarasan, Craig Asher, Zoran B Popovic, Bo Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMavacamten is commercially approved for use in symptomatic patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. This study evaluated its real‐world impact on symptoms, echocardiographic changes, and the need for septal reduction therapy in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy treated at a multihospital referral center.MethodsWe included 244 patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (mean age, 64 years; 57% women, 21% with atrial fibrillation, 51% New York Heart Association class III, 82% on β‐blockers) treated with mavacamten for ≥3 months (171 for ≥12 months). Serial clinical and echocardiographic assessments were performed.ResultsNew York Heart Association class distribution improved over time: at 3 months 21% class I, 61% class II, and 18% class III; at 12 months 36% class I, 57% class II, and 8% class III. After a mean of 1.02 years, significant improvements were observed in resting and post‐Valsalva left ventricular outflow tract gradients (−31±33 and −57±46 mm Hg), interventricular septal thickness (−1.7±4 mm), left ventricular mass index (−9.5±32 g/m2), left atrial volume index (−12±13 mL/m2), E/e′ (−5.5±6.4), left ventricular global longitudinal strain (1.04%±3.1%), and various left atrial and right ventricular strain measures (all P<0.05). Temporary interruption occurred in 8 patients (3.2%), and 3 (1.2%) discontinued mavacamten permanently. There were 2 noncardiac deaths, 7 heart failure admissions, 14 cases of new‐onset atrial fibrillation, and 1 patient required septal reduction therapy. At the last visit, most patients were on 5 mg (49%) or 10 mg (26%) mavacamten.ConclusionsIn this real‐world cohort, mavacamten treatment was associated with sustained symptomatic and echocardiographic improvements, suggesting a favorable disease‐modifying impact with minimal need for septal reduction therapy.Keywords: echocardiography, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, mavacamten, real world, remodelingSubject Categories: Echocardiography

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is a relatively common genetic cardiovascular condition inherited in an autosomal dominant manner. 1 , 2 The prevalence of HCM ranges from 1:200 to 1:500, with approximately two‐thirds of patients presenting with the obstructive HCM phenotype (oHCM). Many patients with HCM experience progressive symptom exacerbation, including exertional dyspnea, syncope due to left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction, diastolic dysfunction, heart failure, atrial and ventricular arrhythmias, and an increased risk of sudden cardiac death. 1 , 2 Until recently, the pharmacologic management of oHCM relied on β‐blockers, non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and disopyramide for symptom relief; however, their use in HCM is largely based on limited data, with variable efficacy and an inability to target the underlying pathophysiology of HCM. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 For patients with persistent symptoms despite medical therapy, septal reduction therapy (SRT), has been the mainstay of intervention. 1 , 2 Although effective, these procedures are not universally available, carry procedural risks, may not be appropriate for all patients, and are recommended to be performed at experienced centers. 1 , 2 In addressing these limitations, efforts have focused on therapies that directly target sarcomeric hypercontractility.

In previous studies of symptomatic patients with oHCM, mavacamten, a selective allosteric and reversible cardiac myosin inhibitor, has demonstrated significant and sustained improvement in LVOT obstruction, symptoms, quality of life, biomarkers, and exercise performance, regardless of sex or genotype status. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 These favorable findings have resulted in the approval of mavacamten for clinical use in multiple countries around the world and updated guidelines that recommend mavacamten as a key therapeutic option in managing symptomatic patients with oHCM. 1 , 2 In the United States, it was approved under the Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) program because of potential concerns related to left ventricular (LV) systolic dysfunction. In addition to symptomatic improvement, prior reports have demonstrated favorable effects on diastolic function and cardiac remodeling, including improvement in LV strain, left atrial (LA) size, and function. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Recently, data on real‐world experience using mavacamten have suggested a favorable clinical experience with a low rate of LV systolic dysfunction and logistic/operational feasibility. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 However, real‐world data on sustained clinical efficacy, including need for SRT, and the impact of mavacamten on cardiac remodeling are lacking. In this study, we sought to assess real‐world symptom improvement, safety, and serial echocardiographic changes in symptomatic patients with oHCM treated with commercial mavacamten under the REMS program.

METHODS

Study Population

The authors will not make the data, methods used in the analysis, or materials used to conduct the research available to any researcher for purposes of reproducing the results or replicating the procedure. This was a prospective observational study of consecutive adult (aged ≥18 years) patients evaluated at various Cleveland Clinic facilities in the United States (Ohio and Florida) and subsequently initiated on mavacamten for treatment of persistently symptomatic oHCM. All patients signed informed consent to be enrolled in the REMS program. The diagnosis of oHCM was made by experienced cardiologists based on typical features (LV wall thickness ≥15 mm occurring in absence of any other disease responsible for LV hypertrophy or ≥13 mm, along with the presence of dynamic LVOT gradient ≥30 mm Hg). 1 , 2 Eligible patients with at least a 3‐month clinical and echocardiographic follow‐up were included. The following patients were excluded if they: (1) had nonobstructive HCM, (2) had LV ejection fraction (LVEF) <55% at baseline, (3) were patients with an alternative diagnosis (eg, subaortic membrane, concomitant moderate or worse aortic stenosis, or amyloidosis), and (4) were patients unwilling to participate in the REMS program. Electronic medical records were reviewed for baseline and follow‐up clinical characteristics. Appropriate institutional review board approval was obtained.

Echocardiography

All patients underwent comprehensive echocardiograms at baseline, according to current guidelines. 25 , 26 , 27 LVEF was measured using the Simpson biplane technique. Characteristic late‐peaking resting and post‐Valsalva LVOT gradients were measured, with care taken to avoid contamination of LVOT waveform by mitral regurgitation. Follow‐up echocardiograms (measuring LVEF, resting and Valsalva LVOT gradients) were performed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks following initiation of mavacamten and subsequently repeated every 3 months, as stipulated by the REMS program. These were performed at various Cleveland Clinic facilities or local physician offices/hospitals (according to patients' preference) based on a prespecified limited protocol, per guidelines. 25 , 26 Images of echocardiograms performed outside of the Cleveland Clinic were electronically transmitted for physician review. Every echocardiogram was viewed by the prescribing Cleveland Clinic HCM provider to confirm LVEF and LVOT gradient measurements before the next mavacamten prescription. Dose titration followed the REMS protocol, 28 incorporating symptom evaluation and echocardiographic parameters (LVEF, resting, and post‐Valsalva LVOT gradients) to optimize therapy.

Additionally, detailed offline echocardiographic analyses were performed at baseline 3 to 6 months and 12 months in a blinded manner (Y.O. and A.G.), and the following variables were collected: maximal basal ventricular interventricular wall thickness, LV mass index, septal E/e′, LV global longitudinal strain (LV‐GLS), tricuspid regurgitant velocity, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, LA volume index (LAVI), LA strain (reservoir, conduit, and contraction), and right ventricular (RV) free‐wall and 4‐chamber strain. LA, LV, and RV global longitudinal strain assessment was performed offline using vendor‐neutral TOMTEC postprocessing software (TOMTEC‐ARENA TTA2; Philips Healthcare) and according to the recommendations of the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging and American Society of Echocardiography task force for deformation imaging. 29 After allowing TOMTEC software to automatically trace the region of interest, manual correction was performed if necessary. For the assessment of LA strain components of conduit, reservoir, and contraction strain, we used the nonforeshortened apical 4‐chamber view. A single beat was used to assess LA strain in all patients. In patients with AF at the time of echocardiography, LA contraction strain was not measured. In such patients, conduit and reservoir strain values were numerically the same, with a negative reported conduit strain and a positive reported reservoir strain. For LV global longitudinal strain assessment, nonforeshortened apical (4‐, 3‐, and 2‐chamber) views were used. RV free‐wall and 4‐chamber strain were measured on 4‐chamber view. The 0 reference was set at end‐diastole (ie, R‐R gating) for strain analysis. 29 All strain values are reported as positive.

Drug Dispensing, Delivery, and Dose Titration

Following a clinical decision by the REMS‐certified cardiologist, the patient was enrolled in the REMS program. Subsequently, the prescription was entered into the electronic medical record and routed to the specialty pharmacy where drug–drug interaction and safety assessment was performed. Following that, the prior authorization process was initiated, and a discussion with the patient about potential out‐of‐pocket expenses occurred. Once safety and feasibility were ascertained, the drug was dispensed at a starting dose of 5 mg. The first echocardiogram was scheduled 30 days after the patient ingested the first dose. Subsequent dose titration was performed, based on symptom evaluation and echocardiographic measurements (LVEF, resting and Valsalva LVOT gradients) according to REMS protocol. 30

Outcomes

Follow‐up data were manually extracted from electronic medical records. The follow‐up period spanned from the initiation of mavacamten to either the occurrence of an event or the last follow‐up date. The New York Heart Association (NYHA) class at 3, 6, and 12 months, and the need for SRT were documented. These data were gathered through an in‐person or virtual visit conducted by an experienced Cleveland Clinic HCM provider. Need for permanent discontinuation/temporary interruption of mavacamten, per REMS protocol, were recorded. 30 Adverse events like drug intolerance, reduced LVEF, heart failure hospitalization, new‐onset AF, and death were recorded.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis included all patients who had baseline and follow‐up comprehensive LV and RV function analyses. Continuous variables are expressed as mean±SD and/or median and compared using the Student t test or analysis of variance for parametric variables. For nonparametric variables and small sample sizes (n<15), Mann‐Whitney or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used. Categorical data are expressed as percentage frequency and compared using the χ2 test. Comparison of changes from baseline to 12 months in various echocardiographic parameters within the same patient were analyzed using a paired t test. For the changes in various echocardiographic variables over time, effect size was measured using Cohen d for paired t test using the following formula: (mean difference)/(standard deviation of the differences). The Cohen d value of 0.2 represented a small effect, 0.5 a medium effect, and 0.8 a large effect. Correlation between continuous variables was measured using the Pearson correlation coefficient. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0; Armonk, NY) was used for statistical analysis. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Between May 2022 and August 2024, out of 280 patients with oHCM who had an outpatient visit for shared decision‐making to initiate mavacamten, 244 were initiated on commercial mavacamten and took it for at least 3 months. Of these, 224 patients (92%) initiated commercial mavacamten after clinical evaluation and decision‐making, whereas 20 patients (8%) completed the long‐term extension arm of the VALOR‐HCM (A Study to Evaluate Mavacamten in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive HCM Who Are Eligible for Septal Reduction Therapy) trial 31 and transitioned to commercial mavacamten. Of the 36 patients not included in the study, 14 elected not to initiate mavacamten after shared decision‐making, 11 did not follow‐up or chose to follow‐up locally, 5 stopped due to drug intolerance like dizziness (not associated with reduction in LVEF), 4 patients were within the 3‐month window, and 3 chose to undergo myectomy before starting mavacamten. The baseline characteristics of the remaining 244 patients are reported in Table 1. The mean age of the patients was 64±14 years, with 139 (57%) women, and the average time for mavacamten initiation following an HCM diagnosis was 4.5±5 years. Ninety‐two patients (38%) had mavacamten initiated within a year of the initial diagnosis of HCM, 77 (32%) within 2 to 5 years, and 75 (30%) >5 years of diagnosis of HCM. At baseline, 199 patients (82%) were on β‐blockers, 59 (24%) on non‐dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and 12 (5%) on disopyramide.

Geographic Distribution and Pharmacy Logistics

The geographic distribution of the study population was as follows: (1) 160 from Ohio, (2) 29 from Florida, (3) 14 from Pennsylvania, (4) 8 from Indiana, (5) 6 each from Michigan and New York, (6) 5 from West Virginia, (7) 4 from Kentucky, (8) 3 from Illinois, and (9) 1 each from Alaska, Georgia, Missouri, New Jersey, New Mexico, Tennessee, Virginia, District of Columbia and United States Virgin Islands. The following specialty pharmacies managed the drug dispensation: Cleveland Clinic (n=151, 62%), Accredo (n=32, 13%), CVS (n=36, 15%), Optum (n=9, 4%), Theracom (n=10, 4%), Walgreens (n=2, 1%), Carelon (2, 1%), Charlton (1, 0.4%), and Crossroads by McKesson (n=1, 0.4%). The median copay (data available in 195 patients) was $10 (interquartile range, 0–12). Of these, 155 (79%) had a monthly copay of 0 and 27 (25%) had a monthly copay of $10. The average duration from treatment prescription to actual first dose taken was 37±25 days. In 56 (23%) patients, due to concerns about potential drug–drug interactions, the pharmacist recommended that the following drugs be discontinued before initiating mavacamten and substituted for appropriate alternatives without any significant clinical impact on care: 53 patients on proton pump inhibitors and 3 patients on fluoxetine.

Symptomatic Improvement During Follow‐Up

The mean duration of mavacamten exposure was 1.02±0.4 years. All patients were on at least 3 months of mavacamten, whereas 232 completed 6 months, and 171 patients completed 12 months of mavacamten therapy. A progressively higher proportion of patients became asymptomatic/minimally symptomatic over the duration of therapy (P<0.01). At 3 months 52 (21%) patients were in NYHA class I, 148 (61%) were in class II, and 44 (18%) were in class III, whereas at 6 months 67 (29%) were in class I, 142 (61%) were in class II, and 23 (10%) were in class III. This further improved at 12 months, where 61 (36%) patients were in class I, 97 (57%) were in class II, and 13 (8%) were in class III. At 4 weeks, out of 150 patients, 27 (18%) patients were in class 1, 81 (54%) in class II, and 42 (28%) in class III. At 8 weeks, out of 141 patients, 34 (24%) patients were in class I, 79 (56%) in class II, and 27 (19%) in class III. Similarly, at 12 weeks, out of 136 patients, 42 (31%) were in class I, 82 (60%) were in class II, and 11 (6%) were in class III.

Adverse Effects

Eight (3.2%) patients needed temporary interruption of mavacamten due to LVEF reduction between 30% and 50%. Mavacamten was reinitiated at a lower dose per REMS protocol with no significant adverse effects. An additional 3 (1.2%) patients needed permanent discontinuation of mavacamten (1 due to diagnosis of metastatic cancer, 1 due to lack of improvement and subsequent referral for SRT, and 1 due to LVEF <30% in the setting of new‐onset AF).

There were 7 (3%) admissions for heart failure, and 14 (6%) patients presented with new‐onset AF (without a prior documented episode in the past history), whereas 1 patient underwent surgical myectomy, and 4 (2%) patients had an appropriate defibrillator discharge. There were 2 noncardiac deaths, as described previously. 20 One was a 75‐year‐old woman with severely symptomatic oHCM who was turned down for myectomy due to significant comorbidities (including nonalcoholic liver cirrhosis) and had a prior unsuccessful alcohol ablation. She was initiated on mavacamten after discussion of risk and benefits with gastroenterology, the pharmacy, and the patient. While on mavacamten, she demonstrated a significant improvement in LVOT gradient and cardiac symptoms with normal LVEF. After 215 days, she required a readmission for hepatic decompensation, and mavacamten was discontinued. She subsequently died from complications of liver cirrhosis. The second patient, a 69‐year‐old woman, was admitted with acute chronic diastolic heart failure and was subsequently diagnosed with oHCM. She was deemed not to be an optimal surgical candidate due to comorbidities (including advanced obstructive respiratory illness), and the patient underwent alcohol septal ablation. However, the patient was readmitted with recurrent heart failure and severely elevated residual LVOT gradient, and a decision was made to initiate mavacamten therapy, which was continued for 55 days with preserved LVEF and normalization of LVOT gradients. However, she had a stroke (not due to AF) and was subsequently transferred to hospice due to no significant neurologic recovery, followed by death.

Echocardiographic Changes During Follow‐Up

The serial changes in various echocardiographic parameters from baseline to 3, 6, and 12 months are shown in Table 2 and Figures 1 and 2. The mean change in LVEF from baseline to last follow‐up was −5±7% (P<0.001). At baseline, 100% of patients had Valsalva LVOT gradients ≥30 mm Hg, and 173 (71%) had ≥50 mm Hg. After initiation of mavacamten therapy, Valsalva LVOT gradient ≥30 mm Hg was observed in 81 (33%) patients at 3 months, 54 (23%) patients at 6 months, and 32 (19%) patients at 12 months. Similarly, after initiation of mavacamten therapy, Valsalva LVOT gradient ≥50 mm Hg was observed in 51 (21%) patients at 3 months, 35 (15%) patients at 6 months, and 12 (7%) patients at 12 months. In addition to significant and sustained improvements in resting and post‐Valsalva LVOT gradients (−31±33 and −57±46 mm Hg, respectively), there were significant and sustained improvements in mean interventricular septal thickness (−1.7±4 mm), LV mass index (−9.5±32 g/m2), LAVI (−12±13 mL/m2), and E/e′ (−5.5±6.4) (all P<0.01) from baseline to 12 months (all P<0.001). In addition, there were significant and sustained improvements in LV‐GLS (1.04±3.1%), LA reservoir strain (3.7±9.3%), LA conduit strain (2.0±7.0%), LA contraction strain (1.9±6.0%), RV 4‐chamber strain (1.2±4.7%), and RV free‐wall strain (1.9±9.8%) from baseline to 12 months of follow‐up (all P<0.05).

Subgroup Analyses

The distribution of various echocardiographic parameters, separated on the basis of NYHA class at baseline and 12‐month follow‐up, are shown in Table 4. At baseline, various echocardiographic parameters were expectedly improved in the 13 patients who were in NYHA class 1, because they had a longer exposure due to transitioning from long‐term extension of the VALOR‐HCM trial to commercial mavacamten. However, at 12 months, the various echocardiographic features, including LVOT gradients and strain, were similar across various NYHA classes. Out of 229 symptomatic patients at baseline, 125 (55%) patients reported an improvement in at least 1 NYHA class, whereas 14 (6%) additional patients reported an improvement in at least 2 NYHA classes, and 90 (39%) patients reported no change in symptoms. We further analyzed the echocardiographic data in the 90 patients (mean age, 63±14 years, 50 [56%] women, 21 [23%] with AF, 77 [86%] on β‐blockers at baseline) who reported no symptomatic improvement during follow‐up. DISCUSSION

These real‐world data from a large multihospital tertiary care center describe the logistics of implementation and longer‐term follow‐up of symptomatic patients with oHCM who were initiated on commercial mavacamten and prospectively followed under the REMS program. It demonstrates that in addition to a significant and sustained improvement in LVOT gradients and symptoms, there was a significant and sustained improvement in diastolic function and various strain parameters, suggesting favorable cardiac remodeling. The effect size for improvement in LVOT gradients and markers of diastolic function was large, whereas it was moderate for improvements in various LV and LA strain parameters. The findings were similar to the data from prior clinical trials that demonstrated the efficacy of mavacamten in reducing LVOT obstruction, improving NYHA functional class, and enhancing quality of life in patients with symptomatic oHCM. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 In addition, mavacamten was well tolerated and demonstrated a favorable safety profile, similar to previous observations from clinical trials and other real‐world data. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 At last follow‐up, only 1 patient required SRT due to symptomatic oHCM despite being treated with mavacamten for at least 3 months. This suggests that vast majority of patients prefer medical therapy over invasive options. The therapeutic efficacy was achieved with the vast majority of patients requiring the lowest doses of mavacamten (93%), and only 7% patients on the highest (15 mg) dose. Additionally, 24% and 22% of patients were able to reduce or discontinue background HCM therapy, respectively. To report the complete experience, we did not exclude patients who had transitioned from the prior clinical trial (VALOR‐HCM 10 ) to commercial mavacamten.

We make additional important observations in various subgroups. Although LVOT gradients, LV wall thickness, LV mass index, and LV‐GLS improved, there were no significant improvements in other echocardiographic parameters in the subgroup of 11 patients who required mavacamten interruption or discontinuation. Approximately 4 out of 10 patients reported no significant improvement in their NYHA class at last follow‐up. However, despite a perceived lack of symptomatic improvement, there were significant improvements in LVOT gradients; LV mass index; E/e′; and LV, RV, and LA strain, similar to the overall study sample. This likely suggests 2 potential scenarios: (1) limitations of symptom assessment using physician/nurse practitioner ascertained NYHA class or (2) symptoms arising from other causes such as deconditioning, excess β‐blockade, diastolic dysfunction, or subendocardial ischemia. We also demonstrate that 5% of patients who were initiated on mavacamten due to persistent symptoms after failed prior SRT also demonstrated significant improvement in LVOT gradients, with no patients demonstrating residual obstructive physiology and 83% demonstrating symptomatic improvement. The baseline characteristics of the current real‐world study sample were different from the clinical trial patients, including more older patients, a greater proportion of women, and a higher proportion of β‐blocker use. 7 , 10 , 32 There was a higher proportion of patients with advanced symptoms (NYHA class III) as compared with the Clinical Study to Evaluate Mavacamten (MYK‐461) in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (EXPLORER‐HCM) 7 and Aficamten vs Placebo in Adults With Symptomatic Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (SEQUOIA‐HCM trials), 32 but similar to VALOR‐HCM, 10 likely reflecting the practice pattern at our tertiary care center. The prevalence of AF in the current study sample was similar to published literature. 1 , 2

Previous studies have highlighted the safety and efficacy of mavacamten in real‐world clinical settings. Recently, Desai et al 22 reported >6000 symptomatic patients with oHCM from the REMS program over 22 months, showing effective mavacamten dosing at 5 to 10 mg, significant Valsalva (v)LVOT reduction, minimal drug–drug interactions, and low rates of LVEF <50% requiring interruption, supporting the efficacy and safety of mavacamten. Abdelfattah et al 23 monitored 23 patients for 4 to 6 months, showing improvements in NYHA classification and LVOT gradients, with minimal, nonsignificant reductions in LVEF. Similarly, Desai et al 20 followed 150 patients for up to 571 days, recording improvements in NYHA class and Valsalva LVOT, with slight reductions in LVEF. As per the REMS data provided, most patients were administered a daily dose of either 5 or 10 mg. Reza et al 21 tracked 96 patients over 36 weeks, noting improvements in NYHA class and LVOT gradient. LVEF rarely fell <50%. By week 24, about 80% of patients were on a 5‐ or 10‐mg dose. Furthermore, an 18‐month prospective observational study with 66 participants concluded that the efficacy of mavacamten was consistent between White and non‐White patients. 24 However, none of these reports performed a detailed analysis of echocardiograms, including strain assessments.

The echocardiographic analysis in the current study highlights favorable structural and functional ventricular changes associated with mavacamten therapy. Although LVEF decreased modestly, the reduction was consistent with mavacamten mechanism of action as a cardiac myosin inhibitor, similar to the findings from the clinical trials 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 and other reports of real‐world experience. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 Similarly, although tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion reduced from baseline to 12 months (but still remained better than normal), RV strain values improved significantly. Additionally, there were significant reductions in maximal interventricular septal thickness and LV mass index, indicating regression of myocardial hypertrophy. These findings suggest disease modification beyond hemodynamic effects, thereby supporting long‐term structural remodeling by reducing hypercontractility and structural cardiac irregularities. Additionally, the changes in various echocardiographic parameters, especially LV mass index, E/e′, LV‐GLS, and LA strain, demonstrated significant internal correlation. Another small report of 61 patients with HCM suggested improvement in many echocardiographic parameters, including diastology, similar to the current study. 33 However, the authors showed stability of LV‐GLS, rather than improvement. These subtle differences could potentially be explained by the smaller sample size, need for a longer duration of follow‐up, and differences in study sample characteristics.

In HCM, prior observational data have suggested that LV‐GLS is associated with histopathologic changes, in vitro myocardial performance, myocardial fibrosis, with LV‐GLS better reflective of degree and distribution of hypertrophy as opposed to genotype. 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Additionally, identification of abnormal LV‐GLS at baseline may provide long‐term incremental prognostic value in HCM by identifying a phenotype that has a higher risk for cardiac events (eg, massively hypertrophied left ventricle or a higher fibrosis burden). 38 , 39 In patients with oHCM, LV function is influenced by LVOT obstruction, subendocardial ischemia, diastolic dysfunction, and progressive myocardial fibrosis. Hence, it makes intuitive sense that hemodynamic load caused by LVOT obstruction, which is significantly improved following use of mavacamten, 10 , 11 , 12 , 17 is likely important in improving LV‐GLS. In addition, long‐term exposure to mavacamten has also been shown to improve LV mass index and LV wall thickness, all of which likely to help improve LV‐GLS. 8 , 13 Additionally, the current study also demonstrates enhancement of regional RV myocardial function without impairment of longitudinal contraction, reinforcing the disease‐modifying effects of mavacamten beyond LV function.

Additionally, LA enlargement and mechanical dysfunction enhance thromboembolic risk 40 and are associated with adverse outcomes in HCM. 41 , 42 , 43 Prior retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated improved LA dimensions and diastolic function in obstructive HCM after myectomy 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 In patients with oHCM patients, LA mechanical function is influenced by increased LA afterload due to LVOT obstruction, atrial myopathy itself, and accompanying AF. With improvement in LVOT obstruction and mitral regurgitation, it potentially makes intuitive sense that LA hemodynamics would improve in the setting of mavacamten‐induced favorable cardiac remodeling. Although improvements in ventricular and atrial strain suggest disease modification at the sarcomeric level, some questions linger: (1) Should disease modifying treatment be initiated earlier and continued for a longer period of time to observe continued improvement in markers of favorable remodeling? (2) Could sensitive markers of strain and volumetric indices be used to identify patients who are at a higher risk for developing LV systolic dysfunction or AF? (3) Would the current findings translate to nonobstructive HCM? (4) Does disease modification using medical therapy translate into improved longer‐term outcomes or reduced risk of AF and sudden death?

The current findings should also be put in perspective of surgical myectomy, which is also highly effective in relieving symptoms in patients with oHCM. In previous smaller retrospective reports, authors have reported no improvement or even worsening of LV mechanical dysfunction after surgical myectomy, despite an improvement in various other parameters (eg, LA strain and exercise capacity), with a potential association with worse mortality. 49 , 50 In a recent prospective study in patients undergoing surgical myectomy, despite successful relief of LVOT obstruction and symptomatic and overall improvement in various echocardiographic parameters, there was a lack of improvement in LV‐GLS at 12 months of follow‐up, which was likely a result of a mechanically created left bundle‐branch block. 51

Limitations

Although this is relatively large prospective real‐world evidence for mavacamten in oHCM, it is a single‐arm study with no placebo control. However, the findings are similar to prior data from randomized placebo‐controlled trials. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Future studies with larger, diverse cohorts and much longer longitudinal follow‐up data are needed to confirm these findings, including ascertaining prognostic implications, along with focusing on understanding pathophysiological mechanisms and optimizing patient management strategies. This study primarily involved White patients diagnosed with oHCM who were treated at a high‐volume center. Thus, the findings may not apply broadly to other types, such as nonobstructive HCM. Approximately 13% were not included in the analysis for various reasons, which may have impacted on the results of the real‐world experience. Cardiac magnetic resonance data were not uniformly available and hence not reported.

CONCLUSIONS

In this real‐world study of symptomatic patients with oHCM, treatment with mavacamten was associated with serial and sustained improvements in symptoms, hemodynamics, and ventricular and left atrial strain, suggesting a favorable disease‐modifying impact on myocardial remodeling and function with a sustained avoidance of SRT. Whether these favorable disease‐modifying effects would translate into improved longer‐term outcomes remains to be confirmed.
