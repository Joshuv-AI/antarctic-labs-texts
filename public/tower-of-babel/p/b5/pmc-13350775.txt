The Impact of a Single Bout of Antagonist Stretching on Agonist Muscle Performance and Range of Motion: A Systematic review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Andreas Konrad, Konstantin Warneke, Stanislav D. Siegel, Megan Squires, Ethan Lawson, Javier Gene-Morales, David G. Behm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStretching is widely used to increase the range of motion (ROM) of a joint and has been shown to impact both stretched and non-stretched limbs.ObjectiveGiven conflicting findings regarding the acute effects of antagonist muscle stretching on agonist performance (e.g., strength, power) and ROM, this meta-analysis aims to clarify these issues and identify moderating factors.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 and ethical publishing guidance, the protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251014186). MEDLINE/PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus were searched from inception to April 2025. Eligibility followed PICOS criteria: healthy participants; unloaded static, dynamic or proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) stretching of an antagonist; passive control; agonist performance or ROM outcomes; randomised or controlled pre–post trials. Risk of bias was assessed with the PEDro scale. Where > 2 studies were available, pooled effects were calculated with a robust-variance-estimation random-effects model (robumeta R package); otherwise, results were described qualitatively. Effect sizes (SMD) were interpreted as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.49), moderate (0.5–0.79) or large (≥ 0.8). Moderator, subgroup, and sensitivity analyses examined stretch duration and stretching technique. Certainty of evidence was graded with GRADE.ResultsNine eligible trials involving 302 healthy participants met the criteria. Antagonist stretching produced a trivial, non-significant improvement in agonist performance (SMD = 0.22, 95% CI − 0.10–0.56, p = 0.14; low certainty). Moderator analyses showed no influence of stretch duration per bout (< 120 s vs ≥ 120 s) or stretching type (static, dynamic, PNF). Qualitative synthesis for ROM parameters suggested weak evidence for a small agonist ROM increase. Funnel-plot inspection revealed no clear publication-bias pattern; Egger’s test was not applied (< 10 studies). Findings are constrained by small sample sizes, moderate heterogeneity and predominantly fair-quality trials, yielding low overall certainty.ConclusionCurrent evidence indicates no clinically meaningful acute effect of antagonist stretching on agonist performance or ROM, irrespective of stretch duration or technique. Larger, high-quality trials are required to establish whether antagonist stretching offers practical benefits.

Introduction

Stretching is an ubiquitous and popular exercise that can acutely and chronically increase joint range of motion (ROM) of the stretched limb [1–4] and has acute effects on non-stretched joints (global effects) [5, 6]. Prior to the new millennium, static stretching (SS) was often proposed to improve performance (e.g., sprint speed, jump height, strength) since forces could be exerted over a greater joint ROM, in addition to less resistance to movement from more compliant musculotendinous tissues [7–9]. However, over the prior 2–3 decades, acute sessions of prolonged SS (> 60 s per muscle group) have been reported in many reviews to impair subsequent performance (e.g., force, torque, power, balance, muscle activation) [2, 7, 8, 10, 11]. These performance deficits, however, were reduced to trivial effects when the SS was combined with dynamic stretching and activities [12–15]. The possibility of SS-induced performance deficits has led many practitioners to shift to other types of stretching during warm-ups, such as dynamic or antagonist muscle stretches [9, 16–18].

Whereas co-activation of antagonist and agonist muscle activity is crucial for movement coordination and control [19–22], inappropriate timing or magnitude of antagonist muscle co-contractile activity can impair agonist muscle performance. It is also proposed that antagonist muscle activity can induce reciprocal inhibition of the agonist muscle, impairing agonist muscle activation [23–25]. Etnyre and Abraham [26] reported extensive antagonist-to-agonist muscle co-activation cross-talk. Furthermore, inaccurate timing or magnitude of antagonist contractions or excessive antagonist muscle stiffness can add resistance to the intended movement [27–29] by inducing a braking effect on the agonist muscle [21, 30, 31]. Since stretching has been shown to reduce muscle stiffness [32, 33], the braking effect on the agonist of a stiff antagonist muscle might be reduced with appropriate antagonist stretching prescriptions.

There are conflicting examples of both the ineffectiveness and effectiveness of antagonist stretching on agonist performance in the literature. A number of studies report no changes in agonist ROM [34] or performance following antagonist stretching [35–39], whereas others report an increase in agonist performance [31, 35, 40–46]. Studies monitoring electromyography (EMG) with 90-s [31], 120-s [35], and 225–240-s [37] of antagonist SS have also reported both no significant decrease in agonist EMG amplitudes, whereas 40-s SS of pectoralis major and shoulder adductors increased latissimus dorsi and biceps brachii EMG activity [41, 44, 46] as well as performance improvements. Since only 40-s of antagonist SS on a specific muscle group has shown agonist EMG increases, these results might be muscle-, exercise-, or SS duration-specific.

In summary, given the conflicts in the literature, a narrative review might not be able to discern whether there would be overall mean agonist performance improvements versus no change following antagonist SS. Hence, a more quantitative meta-analytical approach might reveal either significant general antagonist acute (single session or bout) stretch-induced changes or also provide specific subgroup factors (i.e., stretch duration or stretching technique) that could affect the outcomes (e.g., ROM, strength, power, endurance).

Methods

The systematic literature search was conducted in adherence with the most recent PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) 2020 guidelines. Accordingly, considering ethical publishing guidelines for review articles [47], the study was pre-registered in the PROSPERO database (registration number: CRD420251014186).

Methodological Study Quality and Risk Of Bias

The Physiotherapy Evidence Database Scale (PEDro) was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies [51, 52]. The quality was independently evaluated by two authors (SS, DB). If no agreement could be found, a third examiner made the decision (AK).

Data Processing and Statistics

The meta-analytical calculations were performed using the robust variance estimation (RVE) model introduced by Fisher and Tipton [53] when > 2 studies were found in the search. Otherwise, a qualitative approach to reporting the findings was applied. The robumeta R-Package is recommended to pool standardized mean differences (SMDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for heteroscedastic data and to account for multiple study outcomes from controlled study designs. Effect sizes were calculated based on means and standard deviations, which were extracted from two independently working authors into an Excel sheet and were double-checked by two further authors. The RVE analysis accounts for intra- and interstudy heterogeneity via τ2 and Ω2, respectively. Pooled effects were interpreted following common classification guidelines with 0 ≤ ES < 0.2 = trivial, 0.2 ≤ ES < 0.5 = small, 0.5 ≤ ES < 0.8 = moderate, and ES ≥ 0.8 = large [54]. Meta-analytical calculations were performed for all included studies to provide omnibus analyses, while subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed to reduce methodological heterogeneity and improve the validity of the results. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses were conducted, with subgroups defined according to stretch duration (< 120 s vs. ≥ 120 s) and stretching technique (static, dynamic, PNF). The risk of bias was evaluated using modified funnel plots as introduced by Fernandez-Castilla et al. [55] to account for multiple study outcomes. Only if the included study number reached n ≥ 10 studies, the Egger’s test was performed to underscore the validity of the subjective observation of the funnel plots [55]. Moderating variables were stretch duration as well as type of stretching.

Certainty of Evidence

In adherence to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) working group criteria, the certainty of evidence of the extracted effects was either up- or downgraded, starting from high quality of evidence under the assumption of randomized controlled trials. For instance, inconsistencies of results, indirectness of evidence, imprecision, a risk of publication bias, or low-quality studies led to a downgrade, while a dose–response gradient in results or large effects could upgrade the certainty.

Qualitative Study Results on Performance

Conclusions were drawn from a total of 179 participants (141 males/24 females/14 sex not specified), of whom 120 were at least recreationally active, and for 59 participants, their activity level was not specified. Out of the seven studies, three applied stretching to the hamstrings, two to the quadriceps, and one each to the dorsiflexors, plantar flexors, and shoulder adductors and extensors, respectively. The stretching exercise was performed with the static stretch technique in six studies and one study each with the dynamic or PNF method. While the dynamic exercises were movements throughout the full ROM of the target joint [59], the PNF method was applied as a classical contract-relax method [46]. The duration of the stretching bouts ranged from 30 to 600 s within those seven studies. Out of the total 32 outcome parameters, five were isometric measures, while 24 were isokinetic measures (angular velocity varying between 60°/sec to 240°/sec in both eccentric and concentric modes), two were maximum repetitions for leg extensions, and one was peak power during a free-weight bench-throw.

Qualitative Study Results on ROM

Thomas et al. [34] performed either 8 × 30 s of knee extensors stretching and tested ROM with the passive straight leg raise test, or 8 × 30 s of knee flexors stretching and performed a knee extension ROM test. Although there was a tendency in both studies for an increase in ROM compared to the control condition (passive straight leg raise: intervention condition + 1.8%; control condition 0%; knee extension ROM: intervention condition + 5.2%; control condition 0.7%), the studies' statistical tests reported no significant change when comparing pre- to post-test.

Wakefield et al. [42] performed a 3 × 30 s stretch protocol on the hip extensors and tested for hip flexion ROM in 15 participants. Again, the statistical results showed no significant effect for the antagonist stretching group on agonist ROM; the intervention group showed an increased ROM of 1.84%, while the control group showed a decrease in ROM (− 1.73%).

Discussion

The purpose of this systematic review with meta-analysis was to synthesize the evidence on the effects of antagonist muscle stretching on both agonist muscle performance and ROM. With only seven eligible studies, our meta-analysis showed no significant impact on agonist performance following a stretching exercise of the antagonist muscle compared to a control condition (ES = 0.22; P = 0.14) without any impact of moderators, i.e., stretching duration or type. Since only two studies were included for the effects of antagonist stretching on agonist ROM, no quantitative effect pooling was performed. Results were evaluated qualitatively, indicating weak evidence for agonist ROM increase.

Although we did not observe significant effects of antagonist stretching on agonist performance, several mechanisms could potentially explain such effects. It is well-established that inappropriate timing or an excessive extent of antagonist co-contraction can negatively impact agonist performance by providing a "braking effect," ultimately resisting the intended movement and limiting maximal force or power output [27–31, 60, 61]. Co-contraction, defined as the simultaneous activation of agonist and antagonist muscles [62, 63], can impair the capacity of the agonist to fully express force.

Given this, it has been hypothesized that SS of the antagonist might reduce co-contractile neuromuscular activity [64] and decrease antagonist musculotendinous unit stiffness or compliance [64, 65], thereby lessening the antagonist’s counteractive resistance and potentially enhancing agonist force production. However, our meta-analysis showed no significant agonist performance improvements following antagonist stretching.

This lack of effect may partly be due to the relative size and strength differences between the agonist and antagonist muscles. In particular, if the antagonist is considerably weaker than the agonist, reductions in its activation may not meaningfully influence agonist output. Moreover, while prolonged SS of a target (agonist) muscle has been shown to impair force, power, and neuromuscular activation [1, 8, 10, 11, 15], it remains unclear whether similar durations of antagonist stretching can consistently reduce the co-contractile resistance without inducing detrimental effects on overall neuromuscular function. Concerning our subgroup analyses, we did not detect a significant difference between ≥ 120 s stretching bouts (95%CI: -0.006 – 0.63) to < 120 s per bout (95% CI: -0.15 – 0.12), although the effect size of ≥ 120 s stretching was at least small (ES = 0.31 – i.e., indicating an increase in agonist performance) and close to significant (P = 0.05), while the effect size of < 120 s was trivial (ES = -0.01; P = 0.73). Since the ≥ 120 s stretching subgroup narrowly missed the threshold for statistical significance, further investigation is warranted to clarify this finding.

Thus, while theoretical mechanisms support a possible beneficial role of antagonist stretching, the current evidence remains inconclusive, and factors such as muscle group characteristics, stretching protocol specifics, and participant training status likely modulate the outcomes. Additionally, only seven studies are available on the effects of antagonist stretching on agonist performance, and this might not be enough to draw a strong conclusion on the topic.

It has to be noted that eight further studies [31, 38, 41, 43, 66–69] were excluded from the initial analysis due to missing pre- to post-test comparisons as a lack of design quality [49, 50]. By including those studies in the analysis with the goal to increase the statistical power, a significant overall small magnitude effect was found with ES = 0.32, 0.10–0.53 95% CI, p = 0.007 with negligible to small study heterogeneity (τ2 ≤ 0.21) (Fig. 4). As no pre-to-post-test-controlled conditions can be pooled, this analysis was performed on the post-comparisons of all (also for those studies that provided pre-post comparisons). The risk of publication bias was indicated in this analysis. However, caution should be taken not to overemphasize these results as the design quality of the added studies has been debated [49, 50]. Future studies should take these design limitations into account.

Concerning ROM, the current findings suggest that stretching an antagonist muscle group may have the potential to influence agonist ROM. However, the evidence remains limited (only two studies) and should be interpreted cautiously. Briefly, Thomas et al. [34] showed non-significant improvements in agonist passive straight leg raise (+ 1.8%) and knee extension ROM (+ 5.2%). Similarly, Wakefield et al. [42] reported a small agonist but non-significant ROM increase (+ 1.84%) following antagonist stretching. A plausible mechanism for the small observed increases in ROM following antagonist stretching could be a general systemic or "global" warm-up effect induced by the stretch itself [70]. Furthermore, if the antagonist was stretched to the point of discomfort, global increases in stretch or pain tolerance [5, 71] could contribute to agonist extensibility. Such mechanisms would not be surprising, as a previous review has reported acute non-local changes in non-stretched joint ROM and performance following stretching interventions [5, 72].

Given that only two studies have specifically investigated the acute effects of antagonist stretching on agonist ROM, these findings should not be overrated. The current evidence is not sufficient to draw firm conclusions, and further research is necessary to better understand the potential mechanisms and practical relevance of this phenomenon.

Limitations

This review has several important limitations. First, the number of available studies was very limited, with only seven studies included for agonist performance and just two for ROM, restricting the generalizability of the findings. Second, the included studies showed considerable heterogeneity in terms of muscles targeted, stretching protocols, and outcome measures, making direct comparisons difficult, and preventing subgroup analyses or meta-regressions. Third, the exploratory analysis indicated a risk of publication bias, suggesting that the true effects might be overestimated. Lastly, to increase statistical power, studies with lower methodological quality were included in a secondary analysis, which reduces the validity of those results and warrants cautious interpretation.

Conclusion

In summary, the present systematic review with meta-analysis found no significant effects of antagonist stretching on agonist performance compared to control conditions. Similarly, no moderating effects of stretch duration or technique were detected. Although no statistically significant difference was found (P = 0.05), the ≥ 120 s stretching subgroup showed a small, near-significant effect, suggesting the need for further investigation. While theoretical mechanisms suggest that antagonist stretching could reduce co-contractile resistance and potentially enhance performance, the current evidence remains inconclusive. Additionally, evidence for improvements in agonist ROM following antagonist stretching is very limited, based on only two studies showing non-significant trends toward ROM increases. While an exploratory analysis including lower-quality studies indicated a small but significant effect, the risk of bias and methodological limitations warrant caution. Overall, the current evidence is insufficient to draw firm conclusions, and further high-quality research is needed to clarify the potential benefits of antagonist stretching on both performance and flexibility outcomes.
