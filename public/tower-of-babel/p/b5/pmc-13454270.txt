Safety and efficacy of faecal microbiota transplantation in Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Vlada O. Chernova, Rita WY Ng, Liuyue Yang, Margaret Ip, Silvia Corcione, Nicolas Benech, Joffrey van Prehn, Ed J. Kuijper, M. Fiorella Contarino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota may influence Parkinson’s disease’s (PD) pathophysiology. Faecal Microbiota Transplantation (FMT) may therefore improve symptoms. We reviewed five randomized controlled trials (RCT), three cohort studies, one case series, and two single case reports (total of 236 subjects). FMT methods varied (donor selection, FMT preparation method, pre-treatment, administration). Mild, self-limiting gastrointestinal symptoms were the most common adverse events (AEs). Constipation improved consistently after FMT. Motor and non-motor outcomes were heterogeneous, with improvements mainly reported in uncontrolled studies. One RCT demonstrated motor improvement at 8-12 weeks, another only after one year, while others showed no significant changes. In most RCTs, changes fell within or just beyond the minimal clinically important differences (MCID). Five studies reported taxonomic shifts, most consistently increased within the phylum Firmicutes. Large, well-designed RCTs with optimized FMT protocol for patient selection, FMT preparation and delivery, and donor selection are needed to determine whether FMT can affect PD symptoms or disease progression.

Introduction

Parkinson’s Disease (PD) is a chronic, progressive neurodegenerative disorder, characterised by motor symptoms such as resting tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia, as well as a wide range of non-motor symptoms, including constipation1. A key pathological hallmark is the aggregation of misfolded neuronal alpha-synuclein (α-syn) in Lewy bodies and neurites2. As current treatments, such as levodopa and dopamine agonists, only provide symptomatic management, there is an urgent need for interventions that can delay, halt, or reverse disease progression3,4

An expanding body of research implicates that the gut microbiota plays an important role in the pathophysiology of PD5. Although the mechanism underlying the “gut-brain axis” remain incompletely understood, the aggregation of α-syn within the intestinal tract and its associated alterations in gut microbiota composition suggest that interventions on the gut microbiome may hold therapeutic potential6–8. Evidence from animal studies further demonstrates that gut microbiota derived from PD patients can induce neuroinflammation, α-syn accumulation, neurodegeneration, and the development of neurological deficit9–11. In addition, the gut microbiota composition may also affect the metabolism of levodopa with different mechanisms, such as the production of tyrosine decarboxylase or dopamine dehydroxylase by commensal intestinal bacterial species12,13.

Faecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is an ultimate treatment for replacing the host microbiota with that of a healthy donor. FMT is proven effective for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection and is included in international clinical guidelines14,15. It has also been studied in various neurological diseases16, with the first PD case report published in 201917. However, the effects of FMT for motor and non-motor symptoms of PD remain controversial18,19 and most evidence derives from open-label studies.

This review examines the current evidence on FMT in PD concerning safety, efficacy, and microbiome changes, and analyses the data from the perspective of recommendations for future studies in this field.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Of 730 studies imported for screening, 379 unique records were screened and assessed for eligibility. Eleven studies were included: five RCTs, three cohort studies, one case series, and two case reports (Figure S1). Study characteristics were summarized in Table 1. Sample sizes varied widely, ranging from single-patient case reports to RCTs with up to 54 participants, for a total of 236 subjects. FMT protocols varied across studies in donor selection, FMT preparation, administration route, treatment schedule, and placebo intervention. Follow-up durations ranged from 4 weeks to 12 months. All five RCTs18–22 were rated as level 2 evidence, defined as individual randomized trials with low risk of bias23. Patients (Table S3) had a mean age ranging from 49 to 75 years, with most in Hoehn & Yahr stage 1–3. Constipation was reported in all five RCTs, in two cohort studies, one case series and one case report. Baseline Wexner scores fell in the mild range in two RCTs18,19, and in the moderate range in one RCT22 and uncontrolled studies17,24,25. Levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) ranged from 150 mg/day to 1100 mg/day, with most participants maintaining stable regimens throughout the study period. In four RCTs, assessments were performed in standardized OFF-state at all time points18,19,21,22, and in one also in standardized ON-state18, while the remaining RCT and all uncontrolled studies performed assessments only in a non-standardized ON-medication state20.

Ongoing clinical trials

As of September 2025, three clinical trials are ongoing to assess the efficacy and safety of FMT in Parkinson’s disease, two of which primarily target constipation symptoms. These include two randomized studies conducted in China, one administrating FMT via oral capsules and another allowing multiple administration routes, and third Phase I safety study in U.S.A. investigating colonoscopic FMT following vancomycin pretreatment (Table S1).

Risk of bias and quality assessment

The overall risk of bias across all studies was 40% “low risk” and 60% ‘some concerns’ (Fig. 1). One manuscript was scored “some concerns” for measurement of the outcome because motor assessments were performed only in the ON-medication state without standardized timing, introducing potential variability in symptom severity across different evaluations20. Among uncontrolled studies, one met 9/10 criteria24, and the other three met 8/10 criteria25,26,28; all were therefore rated as good quality (Table S5). Common limitations included limited standardization or documentation of ON/OFF state assessments24–26,28. Two studies had selectively incomplete follow-up, due to participant withdrawal or partial outcome reporting (criterion 8)26,28, and one study did not include a statistical analysis25.

Safety and adverse events

Safety data were summarized for both FMT and placebo groups, where applicable (Table S4).

FMT was generally well tolerated, with most AEs classified as mild and self-limiting. AE rates varied widely across studies (11–88% in RCTs), reflecting heterogeneity in study design, follow-up duration, and possibly reporting strategy, which may hamper direct comparison of absolute risks. No treatment-related SAEs were reported. Gastrointestinal symptoms were the most frequently observed AEs (Table S4) and differed between FMT and control groups in three RCTs: 53% vs 7%18, 59% vs 25%19 and 33.3% vs 20%22. In uncontrolled studies, AEs were mostly mild gastrointestinal symptoms. Only two studies reported a single SAE each (worsening of motor symptoms28 and vasovagal event25), considered unlikely to be FMT related.

Neurological outcomes

Motor symptoms

Motor function (Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) or Movement Disorder Society–Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) part III) was the most consistently assessed outcome (Table 2, Figs. 2c, d and S2c, d). Two RCTs demonstrated significant between-group differences19,22. Wang et al. reported a significant improvement in FMT group vs placebo in the OFF-state at both 8 weeks (−8.27 vs −1.13; P < 0.01, Δ MDS-UPDRS −11.28, clinically relevant) and 12 weeks (−8.80 vs −0.07; P < 0.01, Δ MDS-UPDRS −10.64, clinically relevant), while placebo significantly improved only at 4 weeks (−3.47 P < 0.01, Δ MDS-UPDRS −4.16, clinically relevant)22. Bruggeman et al.19, reported greater improvement in the treatment group in the OFF-state only 12 months after FMT (−5.8 vs −2.7; P = 0.02, clinically relevant), while the placebo group, which had shown earlier clinically relevant and more favourable changes, deteriorated again at 12 months. Scheperjans et al. found no difference at 6 or 12 months18. Cheng et al20. found no between-group difference, but the placebo group improved significantly at weeks 4 and 8 (Δ −3.85 and −5.15; P < 0.001, P < 0.01, clinically relevant). Across RCTs, changes more often remained within or slightly below the MCID threshold, whereas uncontrolled studies reported larger improvements, generally exceeding the MCID (Figs. 2d and S2d).

Activities of daily living (ADL)

The only RCT showing a significant between-group difference in ADL was Wang et al22. showing improvement of MDS-UPDRS part II at weeks 4, 8, and 12 (Δ MDS-UPDRSII −5.27 and −4.4; P < 0.01, clinically relevant), while the placebo group showed a very small non-significant improvement. Three other RCTs showed modest, non-significant changes, with results remaining within MCID (Figs. 2a and S2a). In contrast, uncontrolled studies showed more pronounced effects (Figs. 2b and S2b), including one study24 reporting significant improvement at 12 weeks, and a single case27 noting clinically relevant ADL improvement at one week post-FMT, sustained at three months.

Motor complications

Motor complications (MDS-UPDRS part IV) were reported in three RCTs18–20 and one open-label study28, all showing no significant or clinically relevant change. One study20 reported a trend toward improvement in the FMT group compared to placebo at week 12 (P = 0.09). In contrast, a single case27 showed marked improvement at one week and three months post-FMT (Figs. 2e, f, and S2e, f).

Non-motor symptoms and cognitive function

Non-motor symptoms were assessed using diverse subjective and objective scales, with heterogeneous findings (Table 2).

Regarding cognition, one RCT20 reported significant improvements in Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) at four weeks and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) at 12 weeks in the FMT group compared to placebo; however, the placebo group also improved significantly from baseline at all time points. Other RCTs reported no significant between-group differences. Cognitive data were not reported in the uncontrolled studies.

Wang et al. found significant between-group improvements in Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) (8 and 12 weeks; P < 0.01, P < 0.001), anxiety (4, 8, and 12 weeks; P < 0.01; P < 0.01; P < 0.05), depression (8 and 12 weeks; P < 0.05), and sleep quality (4 and 8 weeks; P < 0.01, P < 0.05)22. Bruggeman et al19. reported a significant worsening of fatigue at 12 months in the FMT group compared to placebo. Scheperjans et al18. observed transient improvement in anxiety at one month in the FMT group, but worsening in NMSS scores at 12 months. Cheng et al20. found significant between-group improvements in MDS-UPDRS I scores at weeks 4 and 12, but no effects on anxiety or depression. DuPont et al21. reported subjective improvements in patient-reported symptoms.

In contrast, uncontrolled studies reported more favourable non-motor outcomes. One cohort study26 reported significant short-term improvements in sleep, depression, anxiety, and quality of life. One cohort study and one case series24,25found subjective non-motor improvements, and a single case report27 described improvement in MDS-UPDRS I.

Gastrointestinal outcomes

FMT had effects on improvement of constipation and gastrointestinal motility in four RCTs but not in the study of Scheperjans18, where FMT was administered by colonoscopy (Table 2). Wang et al. reported significant between-group improvements in complete bowel movements and stool consistency in the FMT group compared with placebo, whereas Wexner scores improved in the placebo group22. Bruggeman et al19. reported improved colonic transit time in the FMT group and worsening in placebo, though Wexner scores remained stable. Scheperjans et al18. reported modest Wexner score improvements in both groups, with greater improvement in placebo at 12 months and more frequent laxative use in the FMT group. Cheng et al20. found significant between-group improvements across several subjective gastrointestinal scales, though placebo also improved. DuPont et al21. reported objective motility improvements and subjective constipation relief.

Among three uncontrolled studies24,25,28 assessing gastrointestinal symptoms, two reported consistent improvements in constipation, including Segal et al. using colonoscopic administration; similar improvements were also noted in two case reports17,27.

Microbiota outcomes

Four of the five RCTs reported gut microbiome analysis before and after FMT (Table 3)18,20–22. Increases in β-diversity compared to baseline were reported in three studies18,21,22, though this effect diminished by 12 months18. No significant α-diversity changes were reported in two studies18,21. All four RCTs noted taxonomic shifts post-FMT, most notably increased genera within the phylum Firmicutes. Discussion

In this review we analysed the existing evidence on FMT for PD following a strict systematic approach and keeping a focus on the methodology. Furthermore, we elaborated the published results from the perspective of clinical relevance by processing them in relation to MCID.

Based on the analysed results, we extrapolate some important considerations that may inform future trial designs.

As reported in studies on other indications, FMT was also safe in PD patients: AEs were mostly mild, transient, and gastrointestinal in nature, with no treatment-related SAEs.

The most consistent reported benefit was improvement of gastrointestinal functions, as determined both by subjective and objective measures17,19,21,22,25. Previous studies showed that gut microbiota can influence intestinal motility through multiple pathways, including the production of short-chain fatty acids, regulation of bile acid metabolism, and modulation of enteric serotonergic signalling29–31. Interestingly, the improvement in gastrointestinal symptoms in patients with PD persisted up to 12 months after FMT, suggesting a sustained physiological effect rather than a transient placebo response. This is particularly relevant considering that most included patients had high baseline constipation scores.

Constipation improved in all five RCTs (with effects reported up to 12 months)18–22 and was also reported to improve in two cohort studies24,28, one case series25, and one case report17. In contrast, neurological outcomes were heterogeneous, with no significant motor effects in three out of five RCTs18,20,21.

Because no trial assessed correlations between individual changes in constipation and other PD symptoms, and because gastrointestinal improvements were also seen in placebo arms18,20,22, the hypothesis of a potential favourable effect of the improvement of constipation on the motor symptoms could not be verified, but a substantial influence appears unlikely.

Neurological outcomes showed great variability. The large improvements in motor and non-motor domains reported in uncontrolled studies were rarely observed in the RCTs. This is possibly explained by a strong placebo effect, as previously reported in interventional studies in PD32. The fact that all uncontrolled studies assessed motor scores in the ON-medication state further limits the interpretability due to variable medication effects.

The observed increase in genera within the phylum Firmicutes, including Faecalibacterium and Blautia, in PD patients after FMT aligns with previous findings of a depletion of gut bacteria from the Lachnospiraceae family and Faecalibacterium genus in PD. This pattern is associated with a pro-inflammatory state33, as also reported in other neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases34. These taxa are among the major butyrate-producing bacteria widely present in the healthy gut microbiota35. Short-Chain Fatty Acids (SCFAs) such as acetate, butyrate and propionate, are the main products of bacterial fermentation of dietary fibers and starches in the colon and promote gut health and immune regulation. Recent comparative analyses have identified reductions in both SCFA- producing bacteria—such as Roseburia intestinalis, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and Eubacterium rectale—and SCFA synthesis pathways in patients with PD36. Higher stool butyrate levels in PD patients have been associated with a later age at disease onset, suggesting a potential protective role37. Interestingly, two of the studies reported associations between the increased abundance of these bacterial families after treatment and improved clinical outcomes20,24.

There was a considerable methodological heterogeneity across the included studies. The donor’s strains engraftment and microbiological success of FMT were poorly described. An insufficient or unsuccessful engraftment might at least partially explain negative results, as shown in other indications38.

In only one case report, pre-FMT antibiotic treatment with vancomycin orally was given to the recipients27. Vancomycin is considered to increase the engraftment of donor FMT bacterial strains, since it reduces the load of Gram-positive bacteria in the recipients, preparing a niche for the beneficial donor Gram-positive anaerobes that are thought to have a key role in PD. The importance of antibiotic pretreatment for an increase in donor strain engraftment has been analysed for various FMT indications and correlated with clinical response to FMT32,44. Vancomycin orally is non-absorbed and has an excellent safety profile, and should be considered in future trials.

The optimal administration route of FMT in PD has not yet been established. The enema-based regimen showed no lasting clinical or microbiome effects in one cohort study28. Although FMT capsules are commonly used for patients with multiple recurrent C. difficile infections, their efficacy is less consistent, and there is variability due to non-standardized production process41, which can influence bacterial viability of the faecal transplant, especially of anaerobes42. Fresh or frozen FMT suspensions are therefore recommended for studies exploring new indications such as PD, as they ensure optimal transplantation with viable microorganisms. Unfortunately, fresh or frozen suspensions were only used in three of five RCTs. Colonoscopic delivery primarily targets the colonic microbiota, whereas administration via the upper intestinal tract also influences the small-intestinal microbiota. Therefore, gastro-duodenal or naso-jejunal routes may be preferable.

The RCTs reporting the most favourable motor outcomes used different delivery strategies.

Colonoscopic fresh FMT showed short-term benefit22, while nasojejunal delivery of frozen material showed delayed improvement19. In contrast, capsule-based FMT did not yield significant motor effects despite gastrointestinal improvement20,21, as well as colonoscopic delivery of frozen material18. Notably, more favourable results in a case series25 and a cohort study26 were observed following colonoscopic administration of fresh or frozen FMT.

On the other hand, delivery to the upper gastrointestinal tract (e.g., nasojejunal route) may influence the small-intestinal microbiota, potentially affecting levodopa metabolism and vagal α-synuclein pathology; however, this hypothesis requires confirmation.

Although capsule-based FMT has demonstrated efficacy comparable to colonoscopic FMT for recurrent C. difficile infection, when adjusted protocols with repeated administration are used, its effectiveness in other conditions remains unclear43 and may depend on the capsule composition, the amount of capsules and frequency of administration, viability of (anaerobic) bacteria, microbiome diversity and the presence or absence of specific taxa, or aspects related to microbiome functionality.

Interestingly, the placebo groups in RCTs often displayed significant improvements with respect to baseline. In one RCT19, the placebo group exhibited a sustained improvement up to six months, even exceeding that of the treatment arm. RCTs used different placebo interventions, namely placebo capsules, transplantation with saline, and autologous FMT, for which a symptomatic effect cannot be excluded. Another factor could be the used pretreatment, including bowel lavage, which was similar in placebo and treated groups in RCTs, and in the uncontrolled series. A substantial benefit in the placebo group with respect to baseline might have contributed to reducing the significance of between-groups differences after treatment.

The different donor and recipient microbiota compositions were not targeted in the reviewed studies. An increasing number of reports on other FMT indications suggest that the microbiome composition of the donor, including the virome composition (bacteriophages), influences clinical success44. Data from large cohort and case-control studies, including de novo PD patients associated with multi-omics approaches, integrating metagenomics, metabolomics, and immune profiling, may elucidate microbiome-host interactions and biomarkers of FMT response in PD patients. Future studies should consider using suspensions from a limited number of carefully selected and well-characterised donors to assess potential donor effects. The process of matching donor flora with recipient flora in specific regions of the intestinal tract has been analysed in mice models with clear effects on metabolism and immune function45. Profiling of levodopa pharmacokinetics and specific analysis of abundance and functional pathways of bacterial species implicated in levodopa metabolism (tyrosine decarboxylase from Enterococcus faecalis, Lactobacillus spp., and dopamine dehydroxylase from certain Eggerthella lenta strains) may provide new therapeutic options13,39,46.

Ultimately, tailored treatments of specific donor FMTs to PD patients with specific microbiome characteristics could be pursued.

Donor heterogeneity may partly explain variability in outcomes across RCTs. Different strategies were used across the five RCTs: Wang et al. used a single young donor22, Scheperjans et al. used two selected “nondysbiotic” donors18, Bruggeman et al. combined material from 17 donors19, Cheng used multiple individual donors without pooling20, and DuPont used pooled samples from multiple donors21. The two studies showing the strongest clinical effects19,22, represent opposite donor strategies (single vs pooled), which makes it difficult to draw conclusions as to which might be the best strategy.

Across the five RCTs, donor microbiome composition was poorly characterized, and only one study explicitly defined donor microbiota as non-dysbiotic18. Literature and guidelines suggest that large donor pools21 may obscure donor-specific effects and introduce inter-donor microbial competition38,47–50. In addition, using many donors19,21 prevents identification of specific donor effects. In contrast, case series more often use single or a few donors, enabling per-donor analysis.

Unfortunately, none of the studies systematically evaluated donor–recipient matching or donor-specific responses, further limiting interpretation.

The lack of standardized motor evaluations in the practically defined OFF state, as in the unblinded studies reported here, is known to introduce great variability in outcomes. In addition, future trials should focus on levodopa response by keeping the LEDD constant across the follow-up in order to monitor potential changes in effect, or even perform standardized levodopa challenges at fixed times, and possibly measure serum and faecal levodopa metabolites before and after FMT to clarify microbial contributions to pharmacokinetics.

In the two RCTs demonstrating significant but time-limited improvements in motor outcomes following FMT, these were time-limited. In the study by Wang et al., fresh FMT delivered via colonoscopy led to significant reductions in MDS-UPDRS III scores at 8 and 12 weeks compared with placebo, indicating a robust short-term benefit22, but long-term follow-up was not reported. In contrast, Bruggeman et al. reported a significant between-group difference in OFF-state MDS-UPDRS III scores only at 12 months, arguing in favour of a delayed effect19. This highlights the importance of long-term follow-up and rigorously controlled designs in trials involving microbiota-based interventions.

PD is a chronic, progressive disease, and therefore, a single FMT might not be sufficient to provoke a durable and significant response. Even if a sizable symptomatic improvement after one FMT is lacking, repeated FMTs might still have an effect on disease progression, an aspect that was not explored in the published studies and should be addressed in future study designs. To this aim, studies designed to provide multiple subsequent FMTs across a longer period of time are needed.

Although in some studies an effort was made to select specific patient populations (e.g. patients with dysbiosis18 or patients with constipation17,21,24,25), treatment effects were never directly compared across different patient subgroups. Across studies, participants were typically in their sixth decade of life, with a mean disease duration of 3–7 years and Hoehn & Yahr stages I–III, indicating mild-to-moderate PD (Table S3). Most cohorts included a predominance of males and cognitively preserved individuals (mean MoCA > 25), while advanced disease stages, dementia, and major comorbidities were consistently excluded. This relatively homogeneous population may limit generalizability to more advanced or cognitively impaired patients. In future studies, stratification based on clinical phenotype may help identify patients most likely to benefit from FMT for motor or non-motor symptoms. For example, patients with severe motor complications may benefit from a reduction in the abundance of bacteria metabolizing levodopa, whereas those at an earlier disease stage or with specific genetic profiles might be the best candidates for a putative disease-modifying effect.

Interestingly, ongoing studies are focusing on PD patients with constipation: these patients, or those with a “body-first” phenotype characterized by early autonomic involvement, may indeed be the most likely responders given the peripheral pathophysiology of their disease; furthermore, in consideration of the generally positive outcomes on gastrointestinal symptoms, constipation may even be considered primarily a target for FMT.

FMT is safe and beneficial for gastrointestinal symptoms in PD. However, at this stage, there is no convincing evidence that FMT produces a clinically relevant motor or non-motor symptomatic improvement on a group basis, at least in the short to medium term. Results from one RCT seem to suggest that the effect might be delayed, but this aspect needs confirmation. Large RCTs with long follow-up, carefully selected and limited number of donors to analyse donor effect, antibiotic pretreatment of recipients and multiple FMT administration via the upper gastrointestinal route could provide stronger evidence to determine whether FMT can impact not only the symptomatology but also the clinical trajectory of Parkinson’s disease, at least in specific PD patient populations.

We included RCTs, cohort studies, case series and case reports on patients with Parkinson’s disease of all ages treated with FMT that reported motor and/or non-motor outcomes and published in English. Medians and interquartile ranges were converted to means and standard deviations assuming normality58. Adverse events (AEs) and serious AEs (SAEs) were extracted and graded per Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE—Table S4)59. Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB2 tool for RCTs60 and a modified Joanna Briggs Institute checklist for case series61, rated as Good (8-10), Fair (5-7), or Poor(0–4) (Table S5).

The methods of the ongoing trials were analysed from the perspective of supporting the design of future studies.
