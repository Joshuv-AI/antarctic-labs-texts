Sustainable synthesis of mono-, bi- and tri-metallic nanoparticles using endophytic fungi: characterization and different applications
Authors: Abdulrahman Hasib, Mahmoud H. Sultan, Hussein H. El-sheikh, Mahmoud H. Hendy, Amr H. Hashem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endophytic fungi represent an emerging and promising biological platform for the sustainable biosynthesis of nanoparticles within the rapidly developing field of green nanotechnology. Conventional physicochemical methods for nanoparticle production often require toxic chemicals, high energy consumption, and complex processing conditions, which raise environmental and biomedical safety concerns. In contrast, fungal endophyte-mediated synthesis provides an eco-friendly, cost-effective, and scalable alternative for generating metal and metal oxide nanoparticles. Endophytic fungi inhabit internal plant tissues without causing disease and possess diverse metabolic pathways capable of reducing metal ions into stable nanoparticles through enzymatic and biochemical processes. Various biomolecules produced by these fungi, including reductases, proteins, phenolics, flavonoids, and other secondary metabolites, act simultaneously as reducing and stabilizing agents during nanoparticle formation. As a result, nanoparticles synthesized via endophytic fungi often exhibit controlled size and morphology and improved stability. Recent studies have demonstrated the ability of different fungal endophytes to biosynthesize monometallic nanoparticles, such as silver, gold, zinc oxide, iron oxide, copper oxide, and magnesium oxide, as well as more complex bimetallic and trimetallic nanostructures. These biologically synthesized nanoparticles display remarkable functional properties that enable their applications in diverse fields. In biomedicine, they show potent antimicrobial, anticancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities. In agriculture, they contribute to plant growth promotion, disease management, nano-fertilization, and induction of systemic resistance in crops. Furthermore, these nanomaterials demonstrate significant potential in environmental remediation, including heavy-metal removal, pollutant degradation, and wastewater treatment. This review highlights the diversity and ecological roles of fungal endophytes, their mechanisms in nanoparticle biosynthesis, the characterization of produced nanomaterials, and their advanced applications. The integration of endophytic fungi with nanotechnology offers a sustainable pathway for developing multifunctional nanomaterials capable of addressing critical challenges in medicine, agriculture, and environmental management.

Introduction

Endophytic fungi-mediated nanoparticle biosynthesis is a niche domain of green nanotechnology with the potential to replace the dependence on the traditional physicochemical routes of NP synthesis.1,2 These traditional approaches are typically associated with toxic chemicals, high energy input, and the generation of hazardous waste, which present not only environmental threats but also difficulties when used in delicate fields, such as biomedicine.3,4 By comparison, biological methods, particularly those mediated by microorganisms, such as fungi, are more sustainable, economic, and environmentally friendly.5–7 Endophytic fungi are microorganisms that naturally inhabit the internal tissues of plants, where they establish a symbiotic relationship with their hosts, with no evidence of causing damage.8,9 This special biological relationship provides them with numerous biochemical pathways and metabolic processes, which make them efficient “biofactories” to produce different metal nanoparticles (MNPs) and metal oxides. The size of these particles generally lies between 1 and 100 nm.5,10,11 The endophytic fungi possess inherent benefits of nanoparticle synthesis that are manifested in various ways. Fungi are highly tolerant to high amounts of metal ions, a precondition to produce nanoparticles effectively.3,12 In addition, the fungi can excrete several extracellular redox-active proteins and enzymes (e.g., nitrate reductases) as well as various secondary metabolites, such as alkaloids and phenols, terpenes, and flavonoids.7,13 These biomolecules have a dual role: they function as effective reducing agents, re-converting metal ions to the initial nanoparticle state, as well as being coating and stabilizing agents that prevent the aggregation of freshly formed nanoparticles and retain them in a stable condition over time.1,12 As a result, these processes can happen inside and outside the fungal cells in the extracellular media, giving some flexibility for controlled synthesis.14 The fungal approach to the synthesis of metal nanoparticles is receiving much attention due to the increasing necessity for environmentally friendly and non-toxic technologies. Studies in this area aim to utilize the biological action of these fungi to fabricate nanoparticles with precisely desired properties (such as size, shape and surface), which are imperative to improve their performance for specific applications.4,7 This green method not only addresses the environmental impacts of traditional strategies but also gives rise to nanoparticles with improved biocompatibility, which will find significant use in various fields, including medicine, agriculture, and environmental purification.14,15

Beyond these fundamental advantages, the intricate processes underlying endophytic fungi-mediated nanoparticle biosynthesis can allow unparalleled control over some vital physicochemical properties. Fungi have several types of enzymes, including oxidoreductases, such as nitrate reductase, and, through this, they can biosynthesize metal in their metallic form (Ag or Au) from their ionic state (Ag+ or Au3+).16,17 This bio-reduction may be further aided by the stabilizing effect of the secreted proteins and secondary metabolites acting as capping agents, which influence the particle size and shape and prevent agglomeration.18 For example, it has been reported that diverse fungi strains can synthesize nanoparticles in several shapes, such as spherical or rod-shaped, and some others in a triangular shape.19,20 The potential of fungi to grow in diverse harsh environments, as well as the feasibility for large-scale production, contribute to the scalability and cost-effectiveness of this green synthesis method.21 The existence of fungi in extreme biotopes and their easy large-scale cultivation could, in addition, make this green synthesis procedure more scalable and commercially viable. Furthermore, the innate bioactivity of fungal metabolites may confer additional therapeutic or functional attributes to the produced nanoparticles, resulting in significantly improved antimicrobial, antioxidant and anticancer actions, thus rendering them valuable for applications in biomedical science or sustainable agriculture.22–25 For instance, biosynthesized silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs) have been found to exhibit potent antibacterial activity on multidrug-resistant pathogens,26 while zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) show application for enhancing plant growth and metabolic processes.27 These breakthroughs position endophytic fungi as key biorefineries for the new generation of nanobiotechnology, paving the way to advanced materials with customized properties aimed at addressing different industrial and environmental problems.

This review aims to highlight the role of fungal endophytes as sustainable biofactories for the green biosynthesis of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, with a focus on mono-, bi-, and tri-metallic nanoparticle synthesis via endophytic fungi. It summarizes the mechanisms involved in endophyte-mediated nanoparticle synthesis and the factors influencing their physicochemical properties. Additionally, the review discusses the diverse applications of these biosynthesized nanoparticles in biomedicine, agriculture, and environmental applications.

Diversity and ecology of fungal endophytes

Fungal endophytes are a cosmopolitan and ecologically important assemblage of microorganisms that inhabit internal plant tissues without causing overt signs of disease.28 It is now believed there are more than 3 million distinct fungal endophytes worldwide, but, to date, less than 150 000 have been formally named, which illustrates the hidden resource of this microbial kingdom.29 They are diverse across the primary fungal phyla, Ascomycota being predominant, and differ markedly with regard to host plant, tissue type (roots, stems, leaves), geographical location, and environmental conditions.30,31 From an ecological standpoint, endophytes contribute significantly to both plant fitness and ecosystem function. They increase host tolerance to abiotic stresses, like drought, salinity and heavy metal toxicity, as well as confer robust resistance against biotic challenges, such as herbivory, bacterial pathogens and competing fungi, via induced systemic resistance (ISR) and direct antagonism.32,33 They can have potential far beyond ecological roles, extending into biotechnological uses, acting as prolific natural sources of bioactive secondary metabolites that are of interest for pharmaceutical purposes (e.g., anticancer drugs as analogs of taxol, antibiotics, or immunosuppressants) and also as eco-friendly and sustainable bioinoculants in agriculture, allowing reduced reliance on chemical fertilizers and pesticides.34–36

Definition and classification of fungal endophytes

Fungal endophytes are defined as a group of fungi that colonise interior, living plant tissues in an asymptomatic manner, i.e., without having any immediate overt appearance of disease-producing symptoms in their host plants.15,34,37 This definition distinguishes them from pathogens, which induce measurable damage to the host, and from epiphytes (organisms that live on plant surfaces).10 Endophytes have traditionally been ignored or treated as latent pathogens, but current investigations emphasize that they are found in many plants and can form various symbiotic relationships.15,33 Their interactions can provide several advantages to the host, such as increased nutrient absorption, resistance against biotic and abiotic stress (pathogens, drought), or potentially production of secondary metabolites useful for medicinal or agricultural applications.38,39 Some fungal endophytes also facilitate maintaining optimal levels of plant hormones, including auxins and gibberellins, thereby enhancing plant tolerance against environmental stress.40

Molecular approaches, such as 18S and 16S rRNA sequencing, have advanced the identification and classification of these endophytes beyond the conventional morphological methods.11,41 Also, fungal endophytes can be categorized according to their host preference, colonization strategy, ecological roles and functional traits.42 The most popular ones classify them into the following 4 major classes (Table 1): the phylogenetic, transmission and ecological approach is the most widely accepted classification system for endophytic fungi.43–45

Classification of endophytic fungi

Class 1 endophytes are systemic within their hosts and transmitted vertically through seeds. They are comprised primarily of Clavicipitaceous fungi. Members of this genus (and more generally, related genera) produce alkaloids that serve as strong defensive compounds against herbivore- and abiotic-stresses, thereby providing a strong fitness advantage to the host plant.46

Class 2 endophytes are non-Clavicipitaceous fungi that also infect above-ground tissues but are spread horizontally via environmental spores. These fungi have a wider host range and are often associated with many types of plants and contribute to stress tolerance, likely by producing secondary metabolites and the induction of systemic resistance.47

Class 3 endophytes are non-Clavicipitaceous fungi that mainly infect root tissues. This group comprises dark septate endophytes (DSE) and other root-associated fungi that have an important role in nutrient uptake, especially when growing on nutrient-poor soils.49

Class 4 endophytes are also non-Clavicipitaceous but narrower, in that they occupy defined microhabitats on the plant, such as the plant seed or a specialized structure, and, therefore, may be limited both spatially and temporally. Such classification is all the more imperative as it denotes their broad ecological diversity and, at the same time, level of integration with their host plant.47,50

Relation between endophytes and their host plant

The relation between fungal endophytes and their host plants is a complex dynamic interaction of mutual advantage and sophisticated molecular crosstalk. This is an excellent paradigm of mutualism, facilitating a continuum from commensalism to mutualism.15 These interactions are complex and sophisticated, providing plants with benefits, particularly when they are subject to biotic and abiotic stresses. The endophytes find a stable environment and nutrients in the partner, while the plant is supplied with resistance and growth advantage, as represented in Fig. 1.36 This association develops during a recognition phase, where the two partners release chemicals that recognize each other and allow colonization, while preventing host defense responses.51 Once in the so-called endo-sphere (the inner environment of the plant tissue), the endophytes start a permanent metabolic conversation with their host. They frequently stimulate plant growth by increasing nutrient uptake (especially phosphates and nitrogen), synthesizing phytohormones, such as auxins and gibberellins, and altering the plant's stress response pathways.33,52 The plant, in turn, provides the endophyte with a protected environment and fixed carbon through photosynthesis. This reciprocity is not a fixed process, however; it is environment dependent and can sit anywhere on the continuum from mutualism to commensalism, or even the mild end of parasitism, springing from being able to exploit resources in different conditions.53 The stability of this mutualistic relationship is achieved by highly effective regulatory machinery controlled by the plant, which can discriminate beneficial vs. poor-performing endophytes, commending some for developing in symbiosis and penalizing co-acting strains, keeping the association benevolent.51 This co-evolutionary association has developed into intricate interactions in which individual strains of endophyte exhibit host preference as well as tissue-specific colonization patterns, contributing to the general resilience and adaptability of the holobiont, i.e., the whole plant-endophyte system as a functional unit.54,55 Endophytes increase the potential of hosts to absorb nutrients, like phosphorus (P) and nitrogen (N), and improve the tolerance of plants to different environmental stresses, for example, drought, salinity, heavy metal exposure and extreme temperature, by alterations in plant physiology. This comes with heightened relations between the host and endophyte, producing a range of bioactive compounds, i.e., antibiotics, lytic enzymes and volatile organic compounds, which minimize pathogen/herbivore attack.

Ecological significance

Fungal endophytes are important members of terrestrial and aquatic environments, as they play a key role in plant biodiversity and ecosystem functionality.56 They are a key participant in nutrient cycling, plant community processes, and response to environmental change. They can affect plant successional dynamics, and they can contribute to the ecological competence of their hosts under competition.57,58 In addition, they are essential for improving plant health, environmental stress tolerance, and ecosystem sustainability.59 These microorganisms form mutualistic symbiosis with their host plants and enhance the tolerance to abiotic stresses, such as drought, salt and heavy metals, by including the production of stress-response metabolites and modulating plant growth hormones, including auxins and gibberellins.60 Moreover, fungal endophytes also combat biotic stress by the production of bioactive compounds, such as alkaloids, phenolic acids and terpenoids, that can suppress pathogens and repel herbivores.42 They further help to improve nutrient cycling by making important nutrients, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, more readily available for plants, enabling them to grow in nutrient-depleted environments.52 By inducing the growth of plants, in turn leading to increased fixation of carbon in biomass, they are also heavily implicated in global carbon uptake.61 The environmental adaptabilities of fungal endophytes reinforce their potential as candidates for sustainable applications in agriculture, such as natural biopesticides and biofertilizers, as well as in phytoremediation strategies, thereby facilitating ecosystem restoration/remediation, increasing sustainability and enhancing climate preparedness and resilience.62,63

Fungal endophytes-mediated biosynthesis of nanoparticles

Endophytic fungi stand out as an important bioresource for eco-friendly and sustainable biosynthesis of a plethora of metal-based nanoparticles (monometallic, bimetallic, and trimetallic).17,64 This myco-synthesis method presents unique advantages of cost-effectiveness, low energy consumption, and avoidance of toxic chemicals, thereby contributing to the advancement of green nanotechnology as an integral part.65,66 Endophytic fungi are asymptomatically colonized within plant tissues and produce various biomolecules, like enzymes (e.g., reductases, oxidoreductases), proteins, carbohydrates, and secondary metabolites, which influence the growth of nanoparticles by acting as reducing and stabilizing agents during their formation.67 These biological units induce the metamorphosis of metal ions to their elemental or oxide nanoparticle forms under mild conditions, typically at room temperature and pressure.66 The resulting nanoparticles are typically well-controlled in properties (size, crystallinity, and stability) and thus readily suitable for diverse applications, including biomedicine, agriculture, and environmental remediation.68 The particular biomolecules that are present, their concentrations, and the conditions under which they react impose limits on the size, shape and overall features of the nanoparticles synthesized.67

Biosynthetic metabolic pathways

Biosynthesis of nanoparticles using endophytic fungi – mechanistic insights nanoparticle biosynthesis by endophytic fungi is mediated through a complex interaction of enzymatic reduction, electron transfer pathways, and metabolites. Such a bottom-up strategy is regulated by biomolecules, which are secreted as reducing agents, capping agents and stabilizers, that transform metal ions into stable nanoparticles with controllable physicochemical properties.69 Knowledge of the mechanisms that control growth is crucial for the generation of nanomaterials with improved yield, size distribution and functional properties.

Enzymatic reduction mechanism

Extracellular enzymes play a fundamental role in metal ion reduction through fungal systems. Endophytic fungi produce a variety of reductases, such as nitrate reductase, NADH-reductase and other oxidoreductases that help to donate electrons to the metal ions.17,70 As an example, nitrate reductase has been discovered to play a critical role in the synthesis of silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs), since it promotes the reduction of Ag+ ions into elemental silver (Ag0).17 The general response includes cofactor oxidation, including NADH or NADPH donating electrons right into metal ions, which ultimately results in their reduction as well as nucleation into nanoparticles.70 Environmental factors, including pH, temperature and substrate concentration, are known to regulate the activity of most enzymes, ultimately affecting the rate of NP formation.69 Also, the selectivity of this process depends on these enzymes, which allows the synthesis of specific metal or metal oxide (depending on the fungal species and metabolic conditions).5 As an example of metal oxide nanoparticles that are produced by fungi, we indicate that Aspergillus terreus employs its enzymatic machinery to generate cobalt oxide, copper oxide and zinc oxide.71

Electron-transfer pathway

In terms of electron transfer, two main modules are proposed for fungal-mediated synthesis of nanoparticles, including direct enzymatic catalysis and indirect mechanisms from secondary metabolites. In the direct pathway, electrons flow directly from the active site to the metal ion in a rapid and thermodynamically favorable process that is usually charged by short-lived nucleation.17 Indirect pathways involve small-molecular-weight metabolites, such as quinones, flavonoids and phenolic compounds, which act as electron shuttles.69,70 They can either directly reduce metal ions or regenerate reduced cofactors that then take part in enzymatic reduction.64 Electron transfer efficiency is a key parameter influencing the size and morphology of the nanoparticles. High electron-transfer rates are generally associated with a high nucleation rate, which leads to the generation of smaller nanoparticles, whereas slower electron-transfer rates can favor growth over nucleation, leading to larger particles.69 The latter pathways are better regulated by certain fungal endophytes as they have strong metabolic networks and a greater ability to withstand metal stress, which often stimulates high levels of reducing equivalents and electron carriers.1,71

Metabolite-mediated stabilization

After being reduced, the nanoparticles that are formed are in an unstable state and thus tend to aggregate. Endophytic fungi avoid this by secreting capping and stabilizing agents, which are mainly proteins, polysaccharides and organic acids.64 These biomolecules adsorb on the nanoparticle surface, forming one or more layers of functional groups, giving rise to steric and electrostatic stabilization.70 Proteins, especially, serve as dual-functioning reducing and capping agents, as certain amino acid residues, such as cysteine, lysine, and arginine, interact with the metal surface.17 The structure and functionality of the capping layer have a huge impact on the biocompatibility and functional characteristics of the nanoparticles. For example, protein-capped Ag NPs produced by Neocosmospora solani have greater stability, low toxicity and suitability for biomedical applications.64 Likewise, polysaccharides produced by fungi such as Penicillium crustosum support magnesium oxide nanoparticle stability, which leads to a uniform dispersion and avoids their aggregation.71 It has also been proven that the precise form of the capping layer is dependent on the species and culture conditions, resulting in specific surface functionalities.5

Integration of mechanisms for controlled synthesis

This cooperation between enzymatic reduction, electron transfer and metabolite-mediated stabilization allows the endophytic fungi to synthesize nanoparticles with precise control over size, shape, and stability.69,70 Such an integrated mechanism can differ from other biological abstraction methods (e.g. bacterial or plant-based syntheses), which may not show a high level of extracellular enzyme activity and/or metabolic consistency.10,64 Fungi have the capacity to secrete high amounts of reducing and stabilizing agents into the culture medium, making downstream processing streamlined and highly scalable.1 In addition to metabolic flexibility, endophytic fungi produce nanoparticles in many metallic forms, including monometallic, bimetallic and metal oxide particles.5Fusarium oxysporum, for instance, is capable of producing stable Ag-NPs with significant antibacterial efficacy due to the metabolic capabilities at its disposal to assist in the reduction and stabilization process.71 An in-depth understanding of these mechanisms lays the groundwork for improving fungal nano-factories that could be optimized in pilot studies and ultimately applied for scaling-up processes as an environmentally friendly and cost-effective alternative strategy to traditional chemical synthesis approaches.69,70

Comparative biological synthesis approaches

Of all the biological entities available for such applications, each endophytic fungi, bacteria, plants, and algae has its advantages and limitations for nanoparticle synthesis. These biological approaches minimize waste generation and lower energy consumption, in keeping with the principles of green chemistry. The bacterial generation of nanoparticles is a well-investigated biological method. Bacteria, similar to fungi, include enzymes that may reduce metal ions. Nonetheless, a significant difference generally exists in the location of NP synthesis, with bacterial techniques often leading to intracellular NP creation.72 This needs extra processes, such as cell disruption, to collect the NPs, which increases process complexity and cost. Furthermore, bacterial systems may increase the possibility of endotoxin contamination, a problem for biomedical applications.69 Despite these obstacles, bacteria have successfully been employed for NP production. For example, Cytobacillus firmus, isolated from Terminalia arjuna, has been employed for the green production of silver nanoparticles (Ag NPs).72 Plants are involved in biological synthesis approaches. The utilization of plant extract for the synthesis of NPs is referred to as phytosynthesis. Phytosynthesis is known to be very straightforward because it uses the various phytochemical components such as polyphenols, flavonoids, and terpenoids that act as reducing and capping agents.73 Nevertheless, a primary limitation of using biological sources, such as plant extracts, is the variability in phytochemicals, which depends on various factors, including geography, climatic conditions, maturity of the plant itself, and the plant component used. This variability can result in variation in the size, shape, and yield of the NPs. Although the use of plant extracts is known to be very straightforward, problems may arise when trying to scale up due to inherent biological limitations.69 Apart from plants, microalgae and macroalgae also participate in the biosynthesis of NPs. Algae contain numerous biomolecules, such as proteins, polysaccharides, and pigments, that can help reduce the metal salts into NPs. The benefit of algae is rapid growth and suitability for culturing in bioreactors. This implies the possibility of scaling up to mass production. However, similar to plants, the specific composition of reducing agents may vary depending on the algae type, growing conditions, and method of extraction, affecting the characteristics of the NPs produced.71

Compared to other biological organisms, endophytic fungi have unique advantages. They produce metabolites via external synthesis (extracellular), reducing purification problems associated with bacterial internal synthesis (intracellular).69 Due to the stability and flexibility of their metabolism, constant production of NPs is possible as opposed to varying plant-based methods. Secretions by fungi possess good stability and biocompatibility, which makes them very applicable in medicine and industries.74 Many types of metal and metal oxide NPs have been successfully produced from endophytic fungi. Examples include Au, Ag, ZnO, Fe, selenium, and MnO NPs.5 Many applications have been realized after the production of these particles; for instance, in medicine (e.g., anticancer agents23 and antimicrobials75,76), agricultural use for enhancing plant growth,27 and environmental remediation for methylene blue degradation.77 Production of monometallic, bimetallic, and trimetallic particles enhances their applicability potential.78

Metal nanomaterials appear to change in size and shape due to either functional molecules or environmental conditions.79 Temperature, pH, incubation time, aeration, salt concentration, redox conditions, mixing ratio, and irradiation have all been studied as ways to improve the reaction conditions for nanoparticle synthesis.80 Chemical and physical factors determine the size and shape of NPs. Key factors in the synthesis of nanoparticles include the reaction mixture's pH, temperature, and ideal metal ion concentration. Temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and duration of the substrate exposure are examples of scheming parameters that may have an impact on the rate of intracellular nanoparticle formation and, consequently, the size of the NPs.81

Biosynthesis of bimetallic nanoparticles using fungal endophytes

Bimetallic nanoparticles (BMNPs), in which two different metal elements are simultaneously present, often show enhanced physicochemical properties compared to their respective mono-metallic counterparts, enabled by the synergistic or bifunctional effects.117–119 The fungal extracts are combined with two aqueous metal precursor salt solutions to create multimetallic nanoparticles (MMNPs), such as bimetallic nanoparticles (BMNPs). Competitive reduction occurs when two metal ions with different reduction potentials are present. Higher reduction potential metal ions undergo rapid reduction, followed by lower reduction potential metal ions.120 The difficulty is in balancing both reduction kinetics and concentrations of both metal ions to achieve core–shell structures or the desired alloy.121 As an example, the endophytic fungus Clonostachys rosea ZMS36, isolated from Anemarrhena asphodeloides, has been used to sustainably biosynthesize bimetallic Ag–CuO nanoparticles that exhibit promising properties for biomedical applications.117 Moreover, a biological synthesis of zinc oxide–magnesium oxide nanocomposites (ZnO–MgO NCs) using Sarocladium kiliense PV248633 has also been reported. These synthesized ZnO–MgO nanocomposites had an average particle size of approximately 35 nm and exhibited selective anticancer activity against MCF-7 breast cancer cells (IC50 = 78.1 µg mL−1) with significantly lower cytotoxicity towards normal WI-38 cells (IC50 = 218.7 µg mL−1). They also displayed considerable dose-dependent antibacterial and antibiofilm activity, especially against Acinetobacter baumannii and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, exceeding those of cefepime at times, whereas their combination with cefepime further improved antimicrobial activity.122 Furthermore, Fe–Pd bimetallic nanoparticles have been shown to exhibit high catalytic activity.121 The interactions of these two metal components are responsible for the unique electronic, catalytic and optical properties exhibited by BMNPs.118 Still nascent, the use of endophytic fungi for the synthesis of BMNPs enables sustainable biosynthesis, ensuring a green and biocompatible approach to producing advanced materials with multiple desired functions.117,118

Biosynthesis of trimetallic nanoparticles using fungal endophytes

Trimetallic nanoparticles (TMNPs) are composed of three diverse metallic elements. Their production is more challenging in comparison to bimetallic nanoparticles because the reductive as well as nucleation rates of three individual metal precursors must be controlled.123,124 The biosynthesis of TMNPs through biological agents is an emerging field, and reports are limited, especially with endophytic fungi. Nevertheless, the possible advantages are significant because TMNPs have the potential to exhibit more complex synergistic properties that can give rise to new applications in catalysis, sensing, and medicine.125 The reports show examples of plant extracts that can be used to synthesize Au/ZnO/Ag trimeric metal NPs, demonstrating the interest in this growing field.126 Although the previously discussed literature does not specifically mention trimetallic synthesis using endophytic fungi, the underlying principles of reduction and stabilization via fungal biomolecules could be assumed to scale to more complex systems, but would require additional optimization.67,125,126 The wide enzymatic and metabolic variance of endophytic fungi makes them ideal candidates for advancing this complex biosynthesis.127 In particular, these mechanisms of biosynthesis include the secondary metabolites and various cellular components of endophytic fungi. These include enzymes, such as nitrate reductase, capable of reducing metal ions to their nanoscale metallic state.128 Alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, and phenols are some of the other biomolecules that act as both reducing and capping agents to stabilize the formed nanoparticles and inhibit agglomeration.82,129 The extracellular metabolites produced by the fungi make them attractive biofactories for further downstream process engineering, as this simplifies purification of the nanoparticles.17,84,128

Characterization of monometallic nanoparticles

The properties of nanoparticles composed of single-metal elements are dependent on the intrinsic properties of the metal they are made of and also on several extrinsic parameters, such as particle size, shape and surface chemistry.143,144 Mono-metal nanoparticles are one of the least investigated subsets of inorganic nanoparticles.145 Compared with alloy nanoparticles, monometallic nanoparticles have a clear and accurate composition. In this manner, it is easier to predict their in vivo/vitro behavior and toxicity, as well as measure the purity of the final drug formulation.143,145,146 This part tentatively categorizes single-metal compounds, such as Ag NPs, Au NPs, ZnO NPs, iron oxide nanoparticles (FeO NPs), and some other monometallic nanoparticles.147

Ag NPs have been one of the most intensively investigated monometallic NPs because of their outstanding antimicrobial, antiviral and antiproliferative activity.148–150 They also have different shapes, as shown in Fig. 2. Ag NPs exhibit diverse morphologies depending on the synthesis conditions, including spherical, rod-shaped, triangular, cubic, and star-like structures. These variations in shape are influenced by factors such as the reducing agents, stabilizing biomolecules from fungal endophytes, and environmental parameters during the nanoparticle synthesis process.151 Ag NPs have a typical SPR peak in the UV-visible (UV-vis) spectrum, which is known to be around 400–500 nm.152 This SPR peak results from a coherent oscillation of the conduction electrons by an external light and is very sensitive to the size, shape, concentration and aggregation state of the nanoparticle.152,153

Au NPs are widely revered for their superb biocompatibility, chemical stability and unique optical/electronic properties, which can be sensitively tailored by adjusting the size and morphology.156,157 Like Ag NPs, Au NPs exhibit localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), which is responsible for their ruby-red color in colloidal suspensions.158 The LSPR peak can be exactly adjusted by changing the size and morphology of particles, which is very suitable for colorimetric assays and optical sensing. The procedures used for the synthesis of Au NPs are similar to those for Ag NPs and include physical, chemical and biological methods.156,158

ZnO NPs are semiconductor nanostructures, well-known for their photocatalytic, antibacterial and antifungal activities.159 Their properties depend very much on their morphology and size.159,160 ZnO NPs are commonly synthesized NPs through green synthesis processes.

Owing to the outstanding properties of the plentiful varieties of iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs), they have garnered popularity in various applications, facilitating advancements in high-technology systems based on nanoscale materials.161,162 The IONPs exist in numerous forms based on their different phases, such as magnetite, hematite, maghemite and wustite, and 6 different morphologies are represented in Fig. 3. However, the major types of IONPs exploited are γ-Fe2O3, α-Fe2O3 and Fe3O4, due to their distinctive characteristics and physicochemical capabilities.163,164 Interestingly, the existence of nanoscale IONPs bestows unique qualities to the materials, such as excellent magnetic, electrical and optical properties, which are responsible for quantum size phenomena.165 It was found that the smaller the IONPs, the more their superparamagnetic behavior improved, in contrast to the ferromagnetic behavior, which diminishes.162 Interestingly, the 10–20 nm size of the IONPs indicates good superparamagnetic characteristics of Fe3O4 and γ-Fe2O3 and, consequently, substantial performance in many applications.165,166

Characterization of bimetallic and trimetallic nanoparticles

Bimetallic nanoparticles consist of two distinct metallic elements integrated into a single nanoscale structure, the size of which can range from 1 to 100 nm. They may present different architectures, such as random or ordered alloys, core–shell, cluster-in-cluster, or more complex heterostructures, based on atomic size and bond strength, surface energy and synthesis conditions.168,169 These provide parallel evidence for the fact that multicomponent (or bimetallic) nanostructures offer a variety of synergistic effects, such as electronic ligancy (modified charge transfer at an interface), lattice strain, and cooperative or bifunctional/enhanced coordination chemistries, all of which lead to new optical, magnetic, catalytic and biological properties that are not realizable using monometallic nanoparticles.169,170 In particular, noble-metal BNPs (Au, Ag, Pt, and Pd) and their hybrids (e.g., Au–Ag, Au–Pt, Au–Pd, and Ag–Pt) have been widely studied in a wide range of applications, ranging from catalysis to plasmons to biomedicine, because they combine strong surface plasmon resonance with superior catalytic or chemical stability.168,170 Transition-metal hybrids (e.g., Fe–Pd, Cu–Fe, Ni–Co or Fe–Ni) have been extensively used in environmental remediation, electro-catalysis, magnetic data storage, and water and wastewater treatment, demonstrating the advantages of having a dual metal for improving redox activity, adsorption capacity and magnetic separability.171,172 It is possible to prepare BNPs by a variety of physical, chemical and biological methods, such as co-reduction or seed-mediated growth methods, microemulsions, sol–gel and hydrothermal processes, and laser ablation. More recently, greener synthesis approaches have been developed using plant extracts or microorganisms as both reducing agents and capping agents with less toxic reagents and improved biocompatibility.173–175 However, the performance of BNPs is highly dependent on their particle size, chemical composition, and internal elemental distribution; therefore, their characterization by X-ray diffraction analysis, electron microscopy with energy-dispersive analyses, UV-vis spectroscopy, and other surface/structural characterization techniques is vital to discriminate genuine alloys from core–shell/segregated structures, which, in turn, largely dictate the light absorption and associated photocatalytic activity and antibacterial effects.171

Trimetallic nanoparticles can be defined as multi-metallic nanostructures formed by the inclusion of three different metals or metal oxides in the same particle (usually <100 nm). They are designed to take advantage of synergistic effects, surpassing the performances of mono- and bi-metallic systems in catalysis, sensing, and biomedicine.78,176 Compared with monometallic and bimetallic nanomaterials, trimetallic nanoparticles maintain higher levels of catalytic activity and selectivity. They have also found unique applications in biomedical, sensing, antimicrobial, active food packaging, and catalysis.177 The superior physicochemical, optical, magnetic and catalytic properties emanate from synergistic electronic effects, lattice strain and multiple reactive metal–metal interfaces that raise the active site density, stability and tunable surface chemistry.178 Standard architectures are (random or ordered) alloys, core–shell and multi-shell structures, Janus and yolk–shell morphologies, and nanocomposites in which the ETH on oxide carbon supports a trimetallic core (e.g., Ag–Au–Pd or Au–Pt–Ru) for enhanced dispersion/stability.179–181 Synthetic pathways include classic chemical reduction, precipitation and microwave or thermal routes, as well as greener bio-fabrication approaches employing plant extracts, fungi, bacteria or biopolymers that serve as reducing and capping agents in the reactions, thereby minimizing toxic reagents while giving access to functional surface ligands.178,181,182

Furthermore, they can be used in environmental applications, where they contribute to pollutant removal, wastewater treatment, and bioremediation processes. Biomedical applications of the nano-derivatives obtained using endophytic fungi

Nanoparticles derived from endophytic fungi bear great potential for biomedicine because of their excellent physical, chemical, and biological properties and low cost, along with easy, low-energy-consumption production.64,74 They are commonly used as antimicrobial, anticancer, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents, advanced drug release systems, and bioimaging devices and diagnostic tools.

Antimicrobial activity

The global issue of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) constitutes one of the most urgent concerns in contemporary medicine and public health systems worldwide.183,184 In the last two decades, the creation and proliferation of multidrug-resistant organisms (MDROs) have escalated at an unparalleled pace, resulting in a significant public health catastrophe.185,186 Early alerts on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) from the 1940s have evolved into a contemporary crisis, with resistant infections resulting in approximately 1.27 million fatalities per year and impacting healthcare systems globally.187,188 As a result, one of the most important relevant biomedicine applications of fungal endophyte-engineered nanoparticles is their strong antimicrobial activity, which can be used to tackle the increasing global problem of resistant pathogens.189,190 Biosynthesized Ag NPs have been significantly effective as antibacterial agents against multidrug-resistant bacteria such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Staphylococcus aureus.191 For example, Ag NPs produced by the endophytic fungus Penicillium sclerotiorum exhibit notable antibacterial efficacy against antibiotic-resistant uropathogenic E. coli. Likewise, Ag NPs produced from Penicillium cinnamopurpureum showed antibacterial activity,83 and Neocosmospora solani endophyte-induced Ag NPs also had antibacterial activity.23 The mode of action frequently includes disruption of bacterial cell membranes, resulting in cell death.192 Apart from Ag NPs, ZnO-NPs produced by the endophytic Xylaria arbuscula have also demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus) and Gram-negative (Pseudomonas aeruginosa) bacteria.90 Also, copper oxide nanoparticles (CuO NPs) isolated from Aspergillus terreus have exhibited antibacterial activity.193 The activities are generally improved by coating these nanoparticles with a biocompatible polymer, such as chitosan, which increases their stability and enhances the targeted effects.194 Chitosan-capped Ag NPs synthesized by using Curvularia kusanoi and Amesia atrobrunnea exhibited significantly improved antifungal activity against phytopathogenic fungi such as Fusarium oxysporum.192,194 These findings open the door for endophytic fungi as a new source of nanomedicines to control infectious diseases in human health and agriculture. The microbicidal and antifungal activities of endophytic nano-derivatives act through different, usually synergic, mechanisms that exert a greater efficacy than their bulk counterparts. Biosynthesized nanoparticles, such as Ag NPs and ZnO NPs, in particular, have strong activity against different types of pathogens, since they disrupt the microbial cell membrane using electrostatic interaction or physical penetration.186

Fig. 5 illustrates the antimicrobial mechanisms of biosynthesized nanoparticles (NPs) produced by fungal endophytes against microbial cells. Initially, nanoparticles interact with the microbial cell wall and membrane through electrostatic attraction, leading to surface adhesion and physical penetration of the membrane, which disrupts membrane integrity and causes leakage of intracellular contents. After entering the cell, the nanoparticles induce intracellular oxidative stress by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as hydroxyl radicals, which damage essential biomolecules, including lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins and ribosomes (protein oxidation), and DNA (genetic damage). In addition, nanoparticles interfere with cellular metabolism by binding to thiol (–SH) groups of enzymes, leading to enzyme inhibition and disruption of the electron transport chain, which reduces cellular energy production and ultimately results in microbial cell death. The diagram also highlights that endophyte-mediated nanoparticle synthesis produces surface-functionalized nanoparticles coated with fungal biomolecules, which enhances their stability, bioavailability, and antimicrobial efficiency, while potentially maintaining lower toxicity toward human cells, enabling simultaneous multi-target antimicrobial action on membranes, DNA, and metabolic pathways.195,196

Anticancer activity

Fungal endophyte-mediated nanoparticles are gaining tremendous attention for their anticancer and cytotoxic properties based on their effects on different human cancer cell lines in vitro.73,74,197 For instance, Ag NPs bio-inspired by the fungal endophyte Colletotrichum gloeosporioides from Oroxylum indicum showed marked cytotoxic and antiproliferative effects on human breast cancer cells (MCF-7).197 Other studies demonstrated that Ag NPs of Penicillium oxalicum had in vitro antitumor activity towards human cervical carcinoma (HeLa) cells.13 The CuO NPs synthesized from endophyte fungus Aspergillus terreus showed potent anti-cancer activity on human lung cancer cell lines (A549) and human breast cancer cell lines (MCF-7). Likewise, Alternaria tenuissima-mediated ZnO-NPs exhibited significant anticancer activity.198 Furthermore, both zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) and Au NPs can be biosynthesised by the endophytic fungus F. chlamydosporum MW341592.1, which was isolated from healthy Eucalyptus sideroxylon leaves. These nanoparticles have the ability to lower the quantity of Caco-2 human intestinal cancer cells and HCT116 human colon cancer cells.75 This class of nanoparticles can kill tumor cells by apoptosis, inhibit cell proliferation, and serve as a cytotoxic entity, providing a new strategy for cancer treatment.13,64,199 The processes usually involve transformations of ROS (reactive oxygen species), which could also cause oxidative stress and DNA damage in cancer cells, but present lower levels of harm to normal cells.13,74 The specific binding of these nanoparticles to cancer cells marks them as potential candidates for the design of new nanomedicines.

Endophyte-based nanoparticles have been shown to possess significant anticancer and cytotoxic activities using complex mechanisms, thereby selectively damaging cancerous cells with minimal side effects on normal tissue (Fig. 6). These nanoparticles, most of which are made of silver, gold or zinc oxide, kill cancer cells by free radicals or reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS overcome antioxidant systems in the interior of cancer cells and have a cytotoxic effect, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and activation of apoptotic pathways.200 They are able to selectively adhere to cancerous cell membranes, which have different surface charge distributions and receptor expression patterns than normal cells, thus enabling targeted delivery and uptake.201 Upon entry into cancer cells, these nano-derivatives modulate the DNA replication, cell cycle progression and main proliferation and survival related signaling pathways, such as the PI3K/Akt/HIF-1α.202

Antioxidant anti-inflammatory effects

Endophytic fungi are reported to produce a wide range of bioactive metabolites with antioxidant properties, and these attributes are retained or even enhanced in the biosynthesized nanoparticles.24,203 Ag NPs of endophytic fungi, including Phlebia sp., showed pronounced antioxidant activity, which is important in scavenging free radicals and preventing diseases associated with oxidative stress.22 Ag NPs from Penicillium oxalicum and Cladosporium perangustum also exhibit antioxidative activity.204 The antioxidant properties of these nanoparticles are often linked to the presence of secondary metabolites, such as phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which play the role of both reducing and capping agents during synthesis.205 Besides antioxidant properties, certain nanoparticles can also exhibit anti-inflammatory effects that are useful in the treatment of chronic inflammatory conditions.73 Further studies of the properties of these compounds could provide new therapeutics for multiple oxidative stress and inflammatory disease states.

The antioxidative and anti-inflammatory activities of the endophyte-nano-derivatives are believed to modulate oxidative stress, as well as inflammatory signaling pathways through multi-directional pathways. As a consequence of these mechanisms, the nanoparticles, especially those prepared with zinc oxide and other kinds of metal oxides, exhibit excellent free radical scavenging activity owing to their high surface area per volume capacity and the bioactive phytochemicals derived from the fungal synthesis process, which act as natural capping agents.206 The phytochemicals scavenge reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, reducing oxidative damage and chronic inflammation, thus preventing cellular components from the deleterious effects of oxidative stress.207 In addition, nano-derivatives of these herbal drugs are able to upregulate intracellular antioxidant defense systems by activating the activity of enzymes, like superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GP). As anti-inflammatory agents, they are known to modulate key inflammatory mediators, including inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 production, and downregulate enzyme activity, such as COX-2 and iNOS, which are responsible for promoting inflammatory reaction.208 Increased levels of nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), cytokines, prostaglandins, and microRNAs within the inflammatory microenvironment cause aberrant DNA mutations, DNA methylation, angiogenesis, and altered cell proliferation, senescence, and apoptosis.209 Chemokines and cytokines are examples of soluble mediators that are essential to the inflammatory process and have the ability to control both tumorigenic and healthy cells. The tumor microenvironment is rich in pro-inflammatory mediators, such as proteases, cytokines, chemokines, and other inflammatory factors. TNF-α, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-17, IL-12, IL-23, IL-10, and transforming growth factor-β are just a few of the many cytokines that work together to shape the tumor microenvironment and frequently encourage tumor growth.210 Notably, metal nanoparticles can modify the relationship between systemic inflammation and local immune responses and suppress these inflammatory reactions in cancer.211 The fungal metabolites linked to these nanoparticles frequently display intrinsic anti-inflammatory activity, promoting a synergistic effect with potential for therapeutic applications in chronic inflammatory diseases associated with oxidative stress, such as neurodegenerative disorders and cardiovascular diseases.212

Drug delivery, bioimaging, diagnostic and biosensing applications

Nanoparticles have attractive sizes and surfaces that mark them as promising carriers for drug delivery with the ability to target and control the release of therapeutic agents.10,64,91 For instance, nanoparticles may be designed to deliver drugs to a targeted region in the body, which limits systemic toxicity while delivering a superior therapeutic effect.189 The biocompatible and biodegradable nature of biogenic nanoparticles, along with the ease with which their surface can be functionalized using targeting ligands, readily qualifies them for advanced drug-delivery applications.74 Also, those nanoparticles play important roles as imaging and diagnosis probes owing to their tunable optical, magnetic and electrical properties that enable improved contrast and detection sensitivity.213 Endophytic fungi can produce diverse metallic and metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs), such as gold, silver, iron oxide and manganese oxide NPs, with established imaging properties.19 For instance, magnetic nanoparticles, such as iron oxide produced by endophytic fungi, can be used as MRI contrast agents.214 Although there are no direct instances of fungal endophyte-derived nanoparticles that have been specifically utilized for bioimaging in the published literature, the formation of numerous different types of metal nanoparticles by these organisms provides a sound platform upon which to base future applications in this area.5,214 Moreover, the potential for controlling particle size and morphology during biosynthesis also provides possibilities for tuning their optical and magnetic properties to specific diagnostic techniques.7 The application of endophytic fungi in nanoscience is not limited to this; their use as biosensors in environmental monitoring is also being pursued.215 Unlike many other species of microorganisms, fungal endophytes have special biochemical capabilities that can be used to identify specific analytes. The high surface area and sensitivity of nanoparticles are combined to produce superior biosensors when combined in such systems.216 Endophyte fungus-mediated biosensors are seen as viable tools to detect pollutants in the environment, with optical and electrochemical methods of detection.71

Agricultural applications of endophytic fungi nano-derivatives

Engineered endophytic fungi-derived nanoparticles provide sustainable alternatives for multiple agricultural concerns, such as promoting plant growth, protecting crops from pathogens, and improving soil health.217 They also provide a means of reducing dependence on chemical pesticides, encouraging more sustainable food production.

Plant growth promotion and induction of systemic resistance

Endophytic fungi have been recognized for their capacity of enhancing plant growth through several mechanisms, including phytohormones, enhancement of nutrient uptake and stress resistance (Fig. 7).27,218,219 This growth-enhancing ability can be further increased when combined with nanoparticles. Nanoparticles, especially endophyte-synthesized nanoparticles, are used as nano-fertilizers or signaling molecules that induce various physiological processes in plants.220 For example, biogenic nanoparticles have the potential to increase nutrient use efficiency, provide photosynthetic enhancement, and protect plants from different abiotic stresses, such as drought, salinity and nutritional deficiency.65 It has been shown in different research studies that nanoparticles and FTNS (such as mycogenic) help to alleviate environmental stress in crops and improve secondary metabolite synthesis, leading to enhanced productivity. These nanoparticles have the potential to be used to enhance plant health and crop yield, representing a sustainable alternative to conventional fertilizers.65,91 Also endophytes fungi could trigger systemic resistance in the host plant against different pathogens and pests.221 NPs produced by these endophytes can additionally reinforce this defense armament. Through crosstalk with plant cells, these nanoparticles could induce immunity or prime plants' immune responses, thus conferring resistance against biotic stresses.8 This systemic resistance induction is an important aspect of integrated pest management and provides a natural, safe, environmentally friendly method for protecting crops. Although the literature declares that endophytes are involved in boosting plant resistance, supplementation with biosynthesized nanoparticles can augment these protection effects on plants, which induces plants to become more resistant against diseases.65

Overview of plant growth promotion and the induction of systemic resistance.

Biocontrol of phytopathogens

Endophytic fungi have been found to be natural antagonists of many plant pathogens and have the ability to synthesize antimicrobial nanoparticles; therefore, they are good candidates for biocontrol agents.217 Nanoparticles synthesized from fungal endophytes, including Ag NPs, ZnO-NPs and CuO-NPs, have direct antifungal activity against phytopathogenic fungi.194,222 For instance, chitosan-capped Ag-NPs synthesized from Curvularia kusanoi exhibited significant antifungal efficacy against Fusarium oxysporum.192 Likewise, ZnO-NPs fabricated from Xylaria arbuscula were a potent fungicide against Fusarium oxysporum.90 Manganese oxide nanoparticles (MnO NPs) biosynthesized from endophytic Trichoderma virens have also been assessed as anti-phytopathogenic agents.19 This coupled action of the natural antagonism of endophyte with fungicidal synergy between nanoparticles and endophytic secretion strategies makes it a promising solution for controlling fungal infections in agriculture, reducing the demand for chemical fungicides that may be harmful to both plant health and the environment.217,222

Nano-fertilizers, soil health and seed-priming enhancement

Traditional fertilizers generally possess low nutrient use efficiency and cause environmental pollution. Nano-fertilizers, such as endophytic fungal-mediated biosynthesized nano-fertilizers, can serve as a potential alternative source of nutrients for crops in a much more accessible and efficient way.220 These nanoparticles may improve the supply of nutrients, reduce nutrient dissipation and increase uptake by crops, promoting growth and increasing fertilizer utilization efficiency in agriculture.65 In addition to the provision of nutrients, these nanoparticles, when they come in direct contact with soil, can also help restore and promote soil health by positively interacting with the existing microflora in the rhizosphere region and altering soil physiochemical properties.223 Application of mycogenic nanoparticles in plants has been demonstrated to protect the plant against abiotic stress and promote production of secondary metabolites, both of which may ultimately contribute toward enhancing soil fertility and the resilience of crops.224 Moreover, the use of fungal endophyte-based nanoparticles for seed priming could significantly increase the seed germination and early seedling vigor.65 When used on seeds, nanoparticles may be able to induce penetration of the seed coat, triggering metabolism and water uptake while providing early protection from pathogens. This increased germination and establishment can result in healthier, more robust plants that have a higher chance of survival and increased productivity.225 Although no direct research on fungal endophyte-derived nanoparticles for seed priming was mentioned, nanoparticulate material may fall under the larger category of adding nano-related compounds to seeds for enhancements.65 The positive influence of endophytes on plant establishment, combined with the unique attributes of nanoparticles, implies a formidable synergy that could enhance agricultural productivity from the outset.

The disadvantage of nanoparticles in agriculture is that they are directly applied to ecosystems in pesticides, fertilizers, and soil amendments, which can cause contamination.226 Al poisoning mainly prevents the division of root cells, resulting in damaged, swollen, and stunted roots with poor water absorption.227 Studies show that Al2O3 NPs may stimulate plant growth, despite the fact that Al toxicity is well known. It has been demonstrated that metal oxide nanoparticles (such as TiO2, ZnO, and Ag NPs) disturb vital soil microbial communities.228 The buildup of NPs in soil can impair nutrient cycling,229 interfere with vital ecological processes,230 and ultimately reduce soil fertility and carbon sequestration capacity. Nevertheless, the majority of research shows that using nanoparticles has many advantages. It should be noted, though, that the research is still in its early stages and only offers preliminary findings obtained under controlled circumstances, which are frequently validated for particular plants, soil types, and climates.231 Furthermore, there are still restrictions and knowledge gaps regarding the use of NPs in agriculture and their long-term effects on the environment (soil, plants, water, and air).

Environmental applications

Heavy-metal removal and degradation of organic pollutants

Fungal endophyte-derivatized NPs have recently been identified as potential candidates to combat various environmental challenges, such as pollution, and facilitate eco-friendly material development.73,232 Soil and water bodies are the most important environments that are seriously polluted with heavy metals. Endophytic fungi have the capacity to tolerate and even store heavy metals, and the nanoparticles that they biosynthesize may be quite useful in remediation.74 Metal nanoparticles, for example, iron or manganese particles, are able to interact and adsorb heavy metal ions from contaminated water or soil, thereby reducing their concentration in the water phase, or convert them into less noxious forms.214 The high surface areas of the NPs contribute to their high pollutant adsorption. This bioremediation system represents an environmentally acceptable and economical process for the decontamination of polluted sites. For example, chitin and glucans are cell-wall polysaccharides of certain fungi that have functional groups with which heavy metal ions can form complexes, leading to a form of environmental clean-up called myco-remediation.66 The particular structure and the powerful properties of endophytic fungi-type compounds are important for these purposes.189 Furthermore, fungal endophytes produce a variety of enzymes that can degrade complex organics, and such enzymatic activities may be exploited and improved using nanotechnology.73 Nanoparticles are being biosynthesized as catalysts or carriers for these enzymes and exhibit enhanced stability and efficiency when it comes to the degradation of organic pollutants present in polluted water and soil.232

Wastewater treatment and catalysis processes

The high surface area and reactivity of nanoparticles make them effective for wastewater treatment and filtration.73 Fungal endophyte-based nanoparticles, particularly magnetic NPs, may well find applications in the treatment of wastewater by eliminating diverse pollutants, such as heavy metals, organic dyes and microbial pathogens.233 Nanomagnetic particles, in particular, enable simple recovery of treated water under an applied external magnetic field, which makes the filtration process convenient.234 In an eco-friendly procedure, these nanoparticles are prepared through endophytic fungi, which coincides with the goal of developing sustainable and environmentally friendly wastewater treatment technologies.235 Green catalysis activity, including benign catalysts green functional little is the horizon of fungal endophyte synthesized nanoparticles. Several of these biosynthesized metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, including Zn, Cu, and Fe, also possess catalytic activity.90,236 These nanomaterials can be used as catalysts in many reactions with better efficiency and less waste than conventional catalysts. Moreover, photocatalysis, i.e., the use of light energy to facilitate chemical reactions, is a major application.232 Certain fungal endophyte nanoparticles can act as photocatalysts for applications, like water purification and the degradation of pollutants.233

Pathways that can employ nanotechnology for remediation of wastewater include adsorption and biosorption, nanofiltration, photocatalysis, disinfection and pathological control, and so forth.

Adsorption and biosorption are exothermic processes and surface phenomena in which the transfer of a phase (molecule or ion in either the liquid or the gaseous bulk), referred to as an adsorbate, occurs from either liquid or gaseous phase onto a solid, sometimes a liquid surface, called the adsorbent, to form a monolayer on the surface via physicochemical or chemical interactions under certain conditions.237 Biosorption is adsorption whereby biological material, such as certain bacteria, algae, or fungi, is considered as an adsorbent owing to the characteristic property of the adsorbent to adsorb and accumulate heavy metals from even highly dilute aqueous solutions through chemically or biologically mediated pathways, or spontaneously (without consumption of ATP) physicochemical processes.238 In their study, Yang et al. explored the biosorption of chromium (Cr) (VI) from artificial wastewater using aerobic granular sludge. Similar biosorption experiments were performed by Ding et al. on alginate-immobilized Aspergillus niger microsphere (AAM) biosorbent to remove thorium (Th) ions, especially Th(iv), from radioactive wastewater. AAM showed superior biosorption performance at pH 6 and 40 °C, having a capacity of 303.95 mg g−1. Rapid enrichment of Th(iv) was attained within 100 min.239

Photocatalysis is among the advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) that involve the in situ production of extremely reactive oxidants, with the help of the Fenton reagent, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), ultraviolet light, ozone (O3), or a catalyst. Hydroxyl radicals formed in these reactions have the potential to oxidize stubborn organic compounds.240 The behavior of nanomaterials differs from that of bulk material owing to their superior mechanical, electrical, magnetic, and optical properties, and, hence, nanomaterials as a photocatalyst have attracted extensive attention from many researchers in recent years.241 Photocatalysis is a surface phenomenon that involves five basic mechanisms:242 Diffusion of the reactants/pollutants to the surface of the photocatalyst; adsorption of the reactants/pollutants on the surface of the photocatalyst; reaction of the adsorbed reactants/pollutants; desorption of the products from the surface; and removal/diffusion of the products from the interface.

Biosafety of endophytic fungi-mediated metal nanoparticles

Metal nanoparticles synthesized by endophytic fungi have attracted considerable attention as eco-friendly nanomaterials with promising applications in medicine, agriculture, environmental remediation, and drug delivery.106,243 Unlike conventional chemical synthesis methods, endophytic fungi-mediated biosynthesis utilizes fungal metabolites, enzymes, proteins, polysaccharides, and secondary metabolites as reducing and stabilizing agents, resulting in nanoparticles coated with biologically derived capping molecules. These naturally occurring surface biomolecules can significantly influence nanoparticle biocompatibility, stability, cellular uptake, and toxicity.64 Consequently, increasing research efforts have focused on evaluating the biosafety of fungal-derived nanoparticles to ensure their safe application in biological systems. Current evidence suggests that the biosafety of fungal-synthesized metal nanoparticles depends on several factors, including particle size, morphology, surface charge, concentration, metal composition, exposure route, and the nature of fungal biomolecules associated with the nanoparticle surface.74 Studies have shown that biologically synthesized silver, gold, zinc oxide, selenium, iron oxide, and copper oxide nanoparticles generally exhibit lower cytotoxicity than their chemically synthesized counterparts due to enhanced colloidal stability and reduced release of toxic metal ions.244–247 The presence of fungal proteins, amino acids, phenolics, polysaccharides, and other metabolites on nanoparticle surfaces can mitigate direct interactions with cell membranes and reduce oxidative damage, thereby improving biocompatibility.247

Nevertheless, fungal-mediated nanoparticles are not inherently free from toxicity. Excessive concentrations may induce reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, DNA damage, inflammation, and apoptosis in mammalian cells. These adverse effects are primarily associated with nanoparticle accumulation within tissues and the release of metal ions, rather than the fungal capping agents themselves.248 Therefore, comprehensive biosafety assessments are essential before biomedical or environmental applications. In vitro studies commonly evaluate cytotoxicity using normal mammalian cell lines, hemolysis assays, genotoxicity analyses, and oxidative stress biomarkers, whereas in vivo investigations assess biodistribution, organ accumulation, immunotoxicity, and long-term physiological effects.249 Recent reports indicate that fungal-derived nanoparticles often demonstrate selective toxicity toward microbial pathogens and cancer cells while exhibiting minimal toxicity toward normal cells at therapeutic concentrations, suggesting a favorable therapeutic index.250,251 Furthermore, biosynthesized nanoparticles have shown reduced hemolytic activity and lower inflammatory responses compared with chemically produced nanomaterials.252

From an environmental perspective, the release of fungal-derived nanoparticles into ecosystems may still influence soil microorganisms, aquatic organisms, and plant communities, particularly at elevated concentrations. However, the biodegradable organic corona surrounding these nanoparticles may enhance environmental compatibility and reduce persistence compared with conventionally synthesized nanomaterials.253 Despite encouraging findings, standardized protocols for biosafety evaluation remain limited, and the long-term effects of chronic exposure are not yet fully understood. Therefore, future studies should integrate advanced toxicogenomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and ecotoxicological approaches to comprehensively assess the safety of fungal endophyte-mediated nanoparticles. Such investigations are crucial for establishing regulatory frameworks and ensuring the sustainable development of nanoparticle-based technologies. Therefore, current evidence supports the notion that endophytic fungi-derived metal nanoparticles possess enhanced biocompatibility and reduced toxicity compared with many chemically synthesized counterparts; however, their safety must be verified through rigorous, case-specific toxicological evaluations before large-scale application.

Nano-toxicological limitations of fungal endophytes NPs

The most important limitation of the model is that the formation of a dynamic and heterogeneous biomolecular corona during myco-synthesis fundamentally affects NP stability, biological identity, functionality, and, inevitably, their toxicological footprint.254 In contrast to chemically synthesized NPs with well-defined surface ligands, biogenic NPs are occluded with complex mixtures of proteins and secondary metabolites that can differentially regulate cellular uptake mechanisms and immune responses in somewhat unpredictable ways, with the net result that the cytotoxicity observed is very difficult to standardize.64 Endophytic pathways of synthetic Ag-NPs and Au-NPs are also much more complex in terms of nanotoxicology due to possible oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in non-target organisms. Extensive studies relating to the interaction between biogenic conditions with susceptible microbes emphasize the potential for high biological activities from biogenic Ag-NPs yet raise concerns about their exposure through routes such as aspiration, skin permeation and direct consumption, inducing serious health hazards via biochemical insults or perturbations as well as physiological effects.255 Due to the high surface-to-volume ratio of these nanomaterials, it is easy to release metal ions from the Ag-NPs (e.g. Ag+), and these can induce mitochondrial dysfunction in mammalian cells as well as DNA damage and apoptosis.256 Specifically, biogenic NPs can impair barrier paracellular junctions in either epithelial or endothelial tissues, but various mechanisms may be at play, and the specific interactions between biogenic NPs and intercellular junctions are still poorly defined for fungi-mediated variants.257 Additionally, the long-term effects are an emerging environmental concern; these have been studied using model organisms such as Caenorhabditis elegans to assess both the short- and long-term impacts of inorganic NPs, including neurotoxicity and reproductive deficits that are not apparent in shorter acute toxic in vitro assays.258

The potential and frequently ignored limitation of NPs is as a “Trojan horse”, which refers to promoting the uptake of other environmental toxins or solubilizing toxic metabolites released by M. ansarii into cells. For example, nanoparticles can change the bioavailability of co-pollutants, with more toxicant uptake into cells than in their absence. For example, the nature of the residual capping agents from organisms such as Neocosmospora solani or Calonectria eucalypti may also have their own bioactivity and provide a synergistic effect with the metallic core to produce a composite toxicological profile that cannot be easily deconvoluted.23,259 Furthermore, the fact that various endophytic strains produce different capping layers (for example, Ag-NPs produced by Fusarium species compared to Aspergillus species have distinct protein corona compositions and may lead to strain-specific toxicological variations) makes it very difficult to set uniform safety guidelines.260,261 The use of metal oxide NPs, such as zinc oxide or magnesium oxide, synthesized by endophytes as a beneficial application, can adversely affect non-target soil microorganisms and have lasting implications on the diversity of the microbial community, with negative effects on soil fertility; this will further alter crop health due to minor alterations in natural ecosystems.27,262 Furthermore, the presence of these substances in the ecosystem and their possible bioaccumulation within the food chain requires rigorous ecotoxicological assessments, which are presently lacking for many of the fungi-mediated NP formulations.263,264

Lastly, batch-to-batch variability in size, shape and surface chemistry resulting from the scalability of endophytic NP production directly affects toxicological outcomes. Polydisperse populations with varying degrees of aggregation arise from inconsistent synthesis conditions, changing the hydrodynamic diameter and subsequent biological interactions.27 The non-reproducibility of such data presents a major hurdle to clinical translation and regulatory approval, as consistent toxicological profiling relies on identical physical material properties.74,265

Key challenges and future prospects

Since they can function as live bio-factories for the synthesis of diverse metallic and metal oxide nanoparticles, endophytic fungi represent a new paradigm in green nanotechnology, providing renewable resources to produce NPs without using high-energy-intensive physical or chemical methods. Nonetheless, there are formidable technical, biological, and regulatory challenges that impede the translation of laboratory-scale synthesis into industrial applications. At the same time, the specific characteristics of these biogenic nanoparticles suggest promising future applications in nanomedicine, agriculture, and environmental remediation, as long as ongoing technical bottlenecks regarding scalability, standardization and toxicological concerns are overcome.

Despite the impressive progress in fungal endophyte mediated nanoparticles (NPs) for biomedical, agricultural, environmental and industrial applications, there are still several major challenges that require concentrated future research. The principal challenge of standardization and scaling in green synthesis is a question to address, because the biological variability of microbial endophytes can cause variations in NP size, morphology, stability and yield, and ultimately hinder large-scale industrial application. This scaling concern can affect the overall viability of large-scale programmes. Moreover, monitoring and regulating nanoparticle production on a larger scale becomes progressively more challenging. Variations in growth conditions, availability of nutrients, and fungal metabolism can lead to inconsistent final products. Strict characterization procedures are necessary to achieve batch-to-batch reproducibility and quality control. Moreover, although their biocompatibility is considered a benefit, evaluating the long-term environmental destiny and ecotoxicological effects of the magic-sized clusters highlights the need for reliable risk assessment to avoid unexpected adverse effects. Addressing these challenges will lead to future advances, such as the establishment of high-throughput screening platforms for the isolation of new endophytic strains that may have better capabilities in NP synthesis and unique functionalities. More attention should also be paid to genetic modification of endophytes to increase the efficiency of their NP production and to tailor NPs for specific applications, e.g., tailoring surface chemistry for improved drug delivery or catalysis. The combination of AI/ML tools with high-throughput synthetic chemistry can streamline the search for optimal synthesis conditions and predict NP performance, allowing smarter design and application strategies. Ultimately, prospective research will develop strong regulatory frameworks for the safe and sustainable marketing of these promising eco-friendly nanotechnologies, realizing their potential for a greener economy and improved global health.

Conclusion

Endophytic fungi have emerged as highly promising biological platforms for the green synthesis of nanoparticles due to their metabolic diversity, adaptability to various environments, and ability to produce a wide range of bioactive metabolites. Their unique symbiotic relationship with host plants enables them to function as natural biofactories capable of converting metal ions into stable nanoscale materials through enzymatic and biochemical mechanisms. In comparison with conventional chemical and physical methods, fungal-mediated nanoparticle biosynthesis offers several advantages, including reduced environmental toxicity, lower energy consumption, improved biocompatibility, and cost-effective production processes. The biosynthesized nanoparticles exhibit diverse physicochemical properties, such as tunable size, morphology, surface chemistry, and stability, which are largely influenced by fungal metabolites acting as reducing and capping agents. These unique properties enable their application across multiple sectors. In the biomedical field, fungal-derived nanoparticles demonstrate strong antimicrobial, anticancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory activities, highlighting their potential as novel therapeutic agents and drug-delivery systems. In agriculture, they contribute to sustainable crop management by enhancing plant growth, inducing systemic resistance, controlling phytopathogens, and improving nutrient utilization through nano-fertilizers. Additionally, their environmental applications include heavy-metal removal, degradation of organic pollutants, wastewater treatment, and catalytic processes for environmental remediation. Despite these significant advances, further research is required to optimize synthesis conditions, improve reproducibility, and evaluate long-term environmental and biological safety. The integration of mycology, nanotechnology, and biotechnology will play a crucial role in advancing this field. Therefore, endophytic fungi-mediated nanoparticle synthesis represents a sustainable and innovative approach for developing advanced nanomaterials capable of addressing global challenges in health, agriculture, and environmental sustainability.
