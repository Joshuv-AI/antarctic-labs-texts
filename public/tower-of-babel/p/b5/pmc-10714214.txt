Anterior Opening-Wedge High Tibial Osteotomy in the Setting of Genu Recurvatum
Authors: Harkirat Jawanda, Christopher M. Brusalis, Sachin Allahabadi, Benjamin Kerzner, Daniel J. Kaplan, Felicitas Allende, Obianuju A. Obioha, Zeeshan A. Khan, Robert F. LaPrade, Jorge Chahla
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Patients with pathologic genu recurvatum may present with complaints regarding anterior knee pain, instability, loss of range of motion, or locking episodes of the knee. Symptomatic genu recurvatum refractory to conservative measures may require surgical treatment. In this Technical Note, the authors describe a surgical technique involving an anterior opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy for a patient with symptomatic genu recurvatum from a knee hyperextension injury resulting in posterior soft-tissue laxity. The surgical technique aims to correct the limb sagittal alignment and provide stability to the knee joint, with the ultimate goal of improving patient outcomes.

Genu recurvatum is characterized by excessive knee extension beyond 5°.1 It can occur either congenitally or from various acquired conditions, including trauma, cerebral palsy, cerebral vascular accidents, poliomyelitis, and physeal arrest, or hyperextension sprains.2, 3, 4, 5 Recurvatum can be classified anatomically as a purely osseous deformity from epiphyseal injury, purely capsuloligamentous secondary to tissue stretching on hyperextension, or as a mixed condition with both osseous and soft-tissue contributors.6

Patients with pathologic genu recurvatum may present with anterior knee pain, symptomatic instability, loss of range of motion, or locking episodes of the knee.1,7 Nonoperative measures, including rehabilitation1 and antihyperextension knee braces,8,9 may be effective for some patients; however, surgical correction may be necessary for those with ongoing symptoms. For patients presenting with symptomatic capsuloligamentous knee hyperextension, different surgical techniques have been described, including posterior capsular plication,10 bone block capsular retensioning,11 and a variety of tibial osteotomies.12, 13, 14

In this Technical Note, we describe a surgical technique of anterior opening-wedge high tibial osteotomy (AOWHTO) for the treatment of genu recurvatum secondary to posterior soft-tissue laxity caused by a knee hyperextension sprain. The surgical technique aims to correct sagittal limb alignment and provide stability to the knee joint, with the ultimate goal of improving patient outcomes.

Preoperative Evaluation and Surgical Decision-Making

The examination should begin with evaluation of a patient’s gait followed by a focused analysis of the knee. The heel height test is performed on both the operative (Fig 1A) and contralateral lower extremity (Fig 1B). While stabilizing the distal thigh, the great toe is lifted vertically. The vertical distance between the posterior aspect of the patient’s heel and the examination table is measured, and a side-to-side difference in cm is measured.15Fig 1Preoperative evaluation of heel height. Weight-bearing knee anteroposterior (Fig 2A), lateral, and sunrise radiographs are obtained to evaluate for any osseous abnormalities and the posterior tibial slope (Fig 2B). In addition, long-limb mechanical-axis radiographs are obtained to assess for coronal malalignment. Magnetic resonance imaging is obtained to evaluate the integrity of the anterior and posterior cruciate ligaments as well as evaluate for other concomitant intra-articular pathology (i.e. cartilage and meniscal lesions) (Fig 2C). Surgical Technique (With Video Illustration)

Patient Positioning and Examination Under Anesthesia

The patient is positioned supine on a standard operating table and administered general endotracheal anesthesia. A padded tourniquet is placed on the proximal thigh of the operative extremity. The operative thigh is secured with a commercial leg holder and the contralateral leg is secured with a sequential compression device. After the extremity is prepared and draped in the regular standard fashion, an Alvarado knee positioner boot and platform is placed. This knee positioner will allow for the knee to sit fixated in a variety of positions during the procedure. A large C-arm is brought from the contralateral side of the operative extremity. Preoperative intravenous antibiotics are administered before surgical incision.

Surgical Approach

The surgical technique is depicted in Video 1. To begin, after limb exanguination, a 10-cm longitudinal skin incision centered over the tibial tubercle is made with complete patellar tendon exposure (Fig 3A). Sharp dissection is carried down through subcutaneous tissues, preserving thick tissue flaps medially (Fig 3B and C) and laterally (Fig 3D) to expose the patellar tendon. A subperiosteal dissection is performed posteromedially under the superficial medial collateral ligament and popliteus musculature. Care is taken to preserve a flap of soft tissue, which can easily be repaired at the end of the surgical procedure and will cover the medial sided osteotomy plate. A radiolucent retractor is placed underneath the soft-tissue sleeve to protect neurovascular structures in the posterior aspect of the knee. Within the lateral aspect of the surgical incision, the anterior compartment musculature of the leg is dissected subperiosteally off of the proximal tibia to provide broad exposure to the anterior surface of the proximal tibia.Fig 3Initial soft-tissue dissection around the right knee. Next, 2 guide pins are placed sequentially from anterior-to-posterior on each side of the patellar tendon (Fig 4B). The guidepins are passed proximal to the superior margin of the tibial tubercle and aimed toward the posterior cruciate ligament facet. Placement is confirmed with intraoperative fluoroscopy after insertion of both the medial guide pin (Fig 4A) and the lateral guide pin (Fig 4C). After placement of an osteotomy guide to create a straight line on the anterior surface of the tibia, the osteotomy is initiated with a sagittal saw, first on the medial aspect of the tibia, then on the lateral aspect (Fig 5A). The osteotomy is performed posterior to the patellar tendon, retracting it as necessary. With liberal irrigation to minimize thermal-induced bone injury, the oscillating saw traverses approximately 75% of the sagittal width of the proximal tibia. A straight osteotome is then used to advance the osteotomy to approximately 1-cm anterior to the posterior tibial cortex (Fig 5B). Calibrated tines are placed into the osteotomy site to distract the anterior tibial cortex (Fig 5C). Distraction is titrated to correct the side-to-side heel height difference assessed on preoperative examination. A spreader device is left in place for approximately 5 minutes to allow for subsequent stress relaxation of the posterior cortical hinge (Fig 5D).Fig 4Placement of guide pins along the anterior tibia of the right knee. Tricortical iliac crest allograft bone wedges are then tailored to match the shape of the opening wedges on the medial and lateral aspect of the proximal tibia and inserted with a bone tamp (Fig 6A). A lateral radiograph is taken to confirm the ostetomy gap in addition to the integrity of the posterior cortex (Fig 6B). A 6-hole plate designed for proximal tibial osteotomies (NewClip Technics; Nantes, France) is fixed to maintain the opening wedge on the medial aspect of the proximal tibia. The plate is secured with two 4.5-mm locking screws and four 4.5-mm non-locking screws (Fig 6C). On the lateral side, a 6-hole plate (Variax2 Navicular-cuneiform; Stryker, Portage, MI) is fixed with 4 screws aimed to interdigitate with screws from the medial plate (Fig 6D). Repeat clinical evaluation under anesthesia demonstrates appropriate correction of the previous discrepancy in heel height between lower limbs. The torniquet is released, and the wound is closed in a layered fashion, taking care to provide adequate fascial tissue coverage of both plates.Fig 6Placement of tricortical bone wedges and plate placement of the right knee. Postoperative Protocol

At the conclusion of surgery, the patient is placed in a hinged-knee brace locked in full extension. Immediate postoperative radiographs (Fig 7 A-C) are obtained to assess the interval change in the tibial posterior slope (Fig 7 B and C). The patient remains nonweight-bearing with crutches for 6 weeks postoperatively, during which self-guided knee range-of-motion exercises are performed to progressive knee flexion from 90° up to 135°. At 6 weeks' postoperatively, patients initiate formal physical therapy to gradually regain lower-limb strength. Patients graduate to performing activities with greater resistance and are counseled to begin high-impact activities, such as running, once they obtain appropriate limb strength and at a minimum of 6 months' postoperatively.Fig 7Postoperative radiographs of the right knee. Discussion

The presented surgical technique demonstrates a reproducible, step-by-step approach to an AOWHTO for the treatment of genu recurvatum secondary to suspected soft-tissue laxity. AOWHTO has been shown to be a reliable surgical option for symptomatic genu recurvatum of various etiologies resulting in increased posterior tibial slope, reduced hyperextension, and improved postoperative outcomes.19

Treatment of genu recurvatum often falls into 3 broad categories, including nonoperative treatment, osseus surgical management, or soft-tissue release.6 A recent systematic review by Dean et al.19 highlighted the different surgical treatment options for symptomatic genu recurvatum excluding cases due to polio, cerebrovascular accidents, cerebral palsy, or surgery-induced recurvatum. This systematic review included 6 studies, of which 3 performed an isolated proximal tibial osteotomy technique, 2 performed a proximal tibial osteotomy with the addition of progressive distraction using the Ilizarov technique, and one performed isolated soft-tissue retensioning of the posterior capsule. All 5 studies that used proximal tibial osteotomies resulted in an increased posterior tibial slope and a majority of patients in each study were found to have improved postoperative outcome scores.19

Several iterations of the AOWHTO exist. The osteotomy may be performed proximal or distal to the tibial tubercle, and with or without detachment of the tibial tubercle. There may be clinical implications of this difference in osteotomy location. In a series of 27 AOWHTOs, Moroni et al.20 reported that patients who had an osteotomy performed proximal to the tibial tubercle, as demonstrated in the present technique, were more likely to have a favorable clinical outcome compared to those who had the osteotomy performed distal to the tibial tubercle at an average follow-up of 14.5 years. However, it should be noted that there is a potential risk of postoperative patella baja developing if the tubercle is not removed and placed in a position to avoid this. Conversely, leaving the tubercle in-situ avoids the risk of tubercle non-union, and minimizes fracture risk.

Due to the rarity of the condition and the variety of techniques used, the current literature is sparse on outcomes specific for osseus correction in the setting of genu recurvatum due to nonosseus abnormalities. In one case series, van Raaij and de Waal Malefijt14 analyzed 24 knees with idiopathic genu recurvatum after undergoing AOWHTO proximal to the tibial tubercle for anterior knee pain. The mean preoperative posterior tibial slope was 11.7° ± 3.5° with a resultant mean postoperative posterior tibial slope of 21.1° ± 5.7°. At a mean follow-up of 7.4 years, no cases of nonunion or deep infection were identified. Clinical outcomes included a mean Hospital for Special Surgery Score of 90.3 (range, 70.5-99.5), mean Knee Society Score of 94.6 (range, 70-100) for function, and 87.7 (range, 47-100) for pain. Overall, AOWHTO appears to be a promising technique for treatment of genu recurvatum, but further prospective studies are necessary to delineate appropriate surgical indications, optimize surgical technique, and determine long-term clinical effectiveness.
