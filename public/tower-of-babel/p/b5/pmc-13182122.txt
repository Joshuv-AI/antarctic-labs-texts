Muscle strength, neuromuscular function, and functional measures across stages of chronic kidney disease
Authors: Nicholas A Coker, Michael J Germain, Talat A Ikizler, Allen Cornelius, Elizabeth E Evans, Elizabeth O’Neill, Christopher D Mette, Feng Sha, Sarah Friedman, Samuel A Headley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIndividuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD) exhibit impairments to skeletal muscle function when compared to age-matched healthy controls. The current study was designed to compare muscle strength, functional measures, and the levels of some biomarkers across the stages of CKD compared to age-matched healthy controls (HC).MethodsThirty-nine individuals with either CKD stage 3 (n = 10, age = 69.7 ± 9.95), stage 4 (n = 10, age = 66.5 ± 6.95), or stage 5 (n = 9, age = 58.7 ± 13.01) or age-matched HC (n = 10, age = 62.3 ± 10.4) were recruited to participate in this study. After obtaining approval from a physician, participants came to the lab on two occasions. The first lab visit consisted of the completion of an informed consent form, a health screening form, and a medical history questionnaire. Anthropometric measurements were then taken, as well as a blood sample via venipuncture. Next, a familiarization trial with maximum voluntary isometric contractions (MVIC) was performed, in addition to the six-minute walk test (6MWT), and the short physical performance battery test (SPPB). Participants visited the lab for the second time after a minimum of 72 h had passed. During this visit, three MVICs were performed. Force was recorded using a load cell. Concentrations of uremic toxins indoxyl sulfate (IS) and p-cresyl sulfate (pCS), along with inflammatory markers interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-⍺) were analyzed using commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays per the manufacturer’s instructions.ResultsUremic toxins IS and pCS, as well as the inflammatory marker IL-6 all increased significantly from HC to CKD stage 3, to CKD stage 4, and to CKD stage 5. TNF-⍺ increased significantly from HC to CKD stage 3, but not between the CKD groups. SPPB, MVIC, and 6MWT performance decreased significantly between HC to CKD stage 3, to CKD stage 4. No significant change was found in SPPB, MVIC, and 6MWT from CKD stage 4 to CKD stage 5.ConclusionAs CKD progresses, indices of muscular strength and functional measures decline compared to HC but the biomarkers, uremic toxins and markers of inflammation increase. These changes in biomarkers are associated with worsened skeletal muscle function. ‘.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Neuromuscular function, Physical function, Uremic toxins, Inflammation

Background

The development of chronic kidney disease (CKD) results in the accumulation of uremic toxins (e.g. p-cresyl sulfate, indoxyl sulfate) and markers of chronic systemic inflammation (interleukin 6, tumor necrosis factor-alpha) [1, 2]. Over time, these toxins may produce changes to mitochondrial bioenergetics and upregulation of proteolytic pathways, resulting in changes to skeletal muscle function (e.g., reduced endurance capacity) and mass [3–5]. Indeed, previous research has observed that individuals with CKD display reduced muscle mass (due to reduced protein synthesis and enhanced protein degradation), reduced strength, and reduced functional performance compared to age-matched controls [4, 6]. The reduced physical function has been linked to poor clinical outcomes in this population [7].

While the causes of declines in muscular performance in individuals with CKD are multifaceted, mounting evidence suggests that changes to neuromuscular systems likely play a role. Previous research has suggested that aging results in motoneuron loss [8, 9] and compression of motor unit recruitment towards a lower range of forces [10]. It is therefore possible that age-related changes to motoneurons are exacerbated with the progression of CKD, combining to negatively influence force production and functional performance. Indeed, previous research has also indicated that the presence of hyperkalemia in CKD patients is strongly related to depolarization of motoneuronal axons at rest, which may contribute to reduced motoneuron excitability [11]. Previous research has also indicated that individuals with CKD display significantly lower compound muscle action potential amplitudes compared to control subjects, indicating a potentially lower number of motor units in CKD individuals [12]. This is also caused by disease-related losses in Type II muscle fiber area observed in CKD patients [13]. Additionally, previous research has indicated that the early-phase rate of force development is related to differences in functional performance metrics in individuals with CKD, but not healthy age-matched controls [14]. Taken together, this would seem to indicate that some individuals with CKD display reduced motoneuron excitability as a result of hyperkalemia and/or motoneuron loss, leading to impaired force production capacity and functional performance. However, to date, few studies have evaluated changes to motor unit physiology in individuals with CKD compared to healthy controls.

Additionally, alterations to skeletal muscle mitochondrial bioenergetics in non-dialysis dependent CKD (NDD) patients are related to six-minute walk test performance highlighting the fact that abnormalities in skeletal muscle endurance precede other changes in muscle function [4, 15]. Progression of the disease results in impairment in other skeletal muscle parameters that impact the quality of life of CKD patients with many becoming frail with all of the attendant negative consequences [4]. Previous research has indicated that individuals with end-stage kidney failure display hallmark indicators of muscle damage, including Z-disc degeneration and myofilament loss which were not present in healthy controls [16].

Since this negative progression in muscle function across the different stages of the disease has not been well documented, the current preliminary study was designed to compare skeletal muscle function, neuromuscular function, performance on functional measures, and the levels of some key biomarkers associated with skeletal muscle function between age-matched healthy controls and individuals with stage 3b to 5 CKD. We also assessed the presence of fatigue across the disease spectrum, as assessed with the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy (FACIT) questionnaire [17]. We hypothesized that as CKD progresses, all tested variables would decline and be at their worst at stage 5.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-nine males and females between 18 and 85 years, diagnosed with CKD stages 3b-5 (GFR, 44 − 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2) were recruited from a local nephrology practice and associated dialysis centers for this study. One of the coinvestigators (EE), reviewed the patient list at the nephrology practice and approached individuals who met the inclusion criteria for the study when they came for their nephrology appointment. If the patient indicated an interest in participating in the study, EE would then approach the patient’s nephrologist for their approval. Once the nephrologist approved, EE would then consent the patient. The final sample consisted of individuals with CKD stage 3b (CKD3), CKD stage 4 (CKD4), and CKD stage 5 (CKD5) (See Table 1 for descriptive information on the sample.). To qualify for inclusion in this study, participants were also required to be ambulatory with or without the use of an assistive device. Exclusion criteria included a history of acute kidney injury, uncontrolled cardiovascular or musculoskeletal problems, or having a pacemaker or implantable cardioverter defibrillator. Additionally, 10 individuals without a history of CKD or other diseases were recruited to serve as healthy age-matched controls (HC). These individuals were asked to complete the PAR-Q before they could participate.

Physical characteristics of the participants

Procedures

Participants were asked to report to the Human Performance Laboratory on two occasions, separated by at least 72 h. The environmental temperature in the laboratory was kept at 22 °C. The first visit consisted of the completion of the informed consent form, along with health screening, and medical history questionnaires. Following the completion of these forms, anthropometric and body composition assessments were done. Participants then completed the FACIT questionnaire before having a venous blood sample taken. Participants were then given a familiarization trial of maximal isometric assessment as well as submaximal isometric trapezoid contractions of knee extensors. These trials were followed by functional performance testing; short physical performance battery (SPPB) and the six-minute walk test (6MWT). The second visit consisted of a maximal isometric assessment and the assessment of motor unit firing characteristics of the knee extensors on the right leg (See Fig. 1).

Flow diagram depicting the overview of the study protocol

Body composition

When participants arrived at the laboratory, height, and weight were recorded using a stadiometer and calibrated scale (Detecto, Webb City, Missouri). Additionally, body composition was assessed using a standing bioelectrical impedance analysis device (BC-418, Tanita, Tokyo, Japan). Participants completed the assessment wearing clothes but were asked to remove socks and shoes. A one-pound clothing allowance was added for each participant. Percentage of body fat and BMI were recorded from the bioelectrical impedance analysis.

Blood sampling

Participants were asked to come for testing after an overnight fast (at least 8 h). Following the assessment of body composition, participants rested for at least 5 min before a venous blood sample was drawn into a vacutainer (EDTA Monoject Vacutainer, Coviden, Mansfield, MA) from an antecubital vein by a trained researcher. This blood sample was then spun for 15 min in a refrigerated centrifuge at approximately 4 °C at 3000 rpm. The plasma samples were aliquoted into cryotubes and stored at -80 degrees C until they were analyzed. Some samples were sent to Vanderbilt Medical Center for the analysis of the uremic toxins; PCS and IS. The concentrations of IS and PCS in the plasma were assessed by reverse-phase HPLC. In summary, 0.24 M sodium octanoate was added to plasma as a competitor to replace the non-covalent binding of PCS and IS to albumin. After 5 min incubating at room temperature, the plasma was deproteinized by adding 4 parts of cold acetone to 1 part of plasma. The mixture was then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 20 min at 4oC and the supernatant was measured by HPLC (Waters 1525 binary HPLC system coupled with 2475 fluorescence detector) [18].

The inflammatory markers, IL6, and TNF alpha were analyzed from plasma samples using commercial ELISA kits (R & D systems, Minneapolis, MN). The assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines with all control values falling within the acceptable ranges.

Isometric testing

Maximal voluntary isometric contraction (MVIC)

Knee extension was the only isometric test. Participants were seated in a custom-built dynamometer with their ankles secured to a load cell (Interface SSM-AJ500, Interface, Scottsdale, AZ) via a self-adhesive strap. The knee angle was standardized for all participants at 110 degrees of hip flexion and 70 degrees of knee flexion, and participants were secured to the dynamometer at the hips and torso via self-adhesive straps. Participants completed a warm-up consisting of progressive isometric contractions at 25, 50, and 75% of self-perceived maximum before performing maximal isometric contractions. Participants then completed three maximal isometric contractions separated by a rest period of one minute. During each attempt, real-time visual feedback was provided to participants on a projection screen approximately 1 m in front of the participant, and verbal encouragement was provided throughout the trial. The MVIC was recorded as the highest 500ms epoch during a single repetition and used as a measure of maximal strength as well as for the determination of relative force output to be used during submaximal isometric trapezoid contractions.

Submaximal isometric trapezoid contractions

Following the completion of MVIC testing, participants completed three repetitions each of submaximal isometric trapezoid contractions at 30% and 70% MVIC. For the contractions at 30% MVIC, participants were asked to remain still for 5 s at the start of the contraction before linearly increasing their force output at a rate of 10% per second until reaching 30% MVIC, holding the force output for 12 s, and then linearly decreasing at a rate of 10% per second until reaching their baseline force output again. For contractions at 70% MVIC, participants were asked to linearly increase isometric force output at a rate of 7%/sec until reaching 70% MVIC, holding the force output for five seconds, and decreasing output at a rate of 7%/sec back to baseline while receiving real-time visual feedback on a television monitor. Surface electromyography was recorded from the vastus lateralis during all submaximal trapezoid contractions using a four-pin surface array (Delsys Galileo, Delsys Inc., Natick, MA USA).

Surface electromyography

Before the start of MVIC testing, a four-pin surface array was placed at 2/3 of the distance between the anterior superior iliac spine and the lateral border of the patella. A mark was made at this site with indelible ink, the skin was shaved with a medical razor, dead skin cells and other cellular debris were removed with hypoallergenic tape, and the skin was cleaned with a single-use isopropyl alcohol wipe before electrode placement. Following confirmation of proper placement, electrodes were secured to the surface of the skin with hypoallergenic tape. After the completion of MVIC testing, a signal quality check was performed by having the participants sustain a submaximal isometric contraction at 25% MVIC and, if necessary, additional skin preparation procedures were performed to improve the quality of collected signals (i.e. signal-noise-ratio > 3.0, baseline noise < 0.5 uV RMS, line interference < 1.0 uV RMS). Surface EMG signals were collected during the performance of all submaximal isometric contractions to evaluate motor unit firing characteristics via signal decomposition with the Precision Decomposition III algorithm [19].

Signal processing

Surface EMG signal recordings were imported into Neuromap (Delsys Inc., Natick, MA USA) and decomposed into their constituent motor unit action potential trains (MUAPTs). The three isometric contractions at each intensity were manually inspected by an experienced investigator for visible countermovement, inability to maintain the target force, or excessive unsteadiness in the force signal, and contractions deemed to be of insufficient quality were not analyzed. Of all remaining contractions, the steadiest 2s portion of each contraction during the isometric hold at the target intensity was identified as the section with the lowest coefficient of variation, and within the steadiest portion of a given contraction, each active motor unit was characterized according to its recruitment threshold, mean firing rate and average action potential amplitude. Linear slope coefficients and y-intercepts of the mean firing rate vs. recruitment threshold regression and action potential amplitude vs. recruitment threshold regression were then generated and used for analysis. To be included in the analysis, a given contraction needed to have a minimum of five motor units decomposed with > 90% accuracy and a recruitment threshold range of at least 10% MVIC for the 30% contractions and at least 20% MVIC for the 70% contractions.

From maximal voluntary isometric contractions, the average baseline torque was calculated over a one-second interval before the onset of contraction. Contractions were visually inspected for the presence of a countermovement as well as excessive variability in baseline force, and contractions were discarded if baseline conditions were deemed unacceptable (e.g., the presence of countermovement or pretension). The onset of force production was assessed as the instant in time in which force output exceeded 5 standard deviations of the average baseline force. The rate of force development was calculated as the slope of the force-time curve at three time points relative to the timing of force onset: 50 ms (RFD50), 100 ms (RFD100), and 200 ms (RFD200). Additionally, force steadiness was calculated at each intensity used for submaximal isometric contractions as the coefficient of variation for the isometric force output during the steady hold at the target force once the participant was able to match the target force. This was determined via visual inspection from an experienced investigator by visualizing force over time in a standardized window and assessing the time to match the desired force output as the point at which the participant was within 3% of the target force for a minimum of one second. If a given contraction was not deemed to have met this criterion, it was not used for the calculation of force steadiness. For 30% contractions, force steadiness was calculated over a 5-second window, while a 2-second window was used for the 70% contractions.

Functional testing

Following the completion of isometric familiarization, participants completed functional testing using the SPPB and the 6MWT. SPPB and 6MWT have previously been shown to be valid and reliable for the assessment of physical capacities of older adults [20, 21] For the SPPB, participants completed three performance tests. The first test consisted of balancing for a minimum of ten seconds in three separate positions: with the feet beside each other, in a semi-tandem position, and a full-tandem position. Prior to the start of the test, participants were instructed that once the test began, they were able to use their arms to maintain balance, but could not take a step or reach out to touch anything around them or the test would be considered completed. Following the completion of the three balance tests, participants completed an assessment of their usual gait speed over a 4 m distance. A laser timing system was used (RM 501, ArenaGear, McKinney, Texas). Participants were instructed to stand behind the starting line and walk at their usual pace “as if walking down the street.” Two trials were performed, and the average of the two times was recorded. After the completion of the usual gait speed test, participants completed a five-time sit-to-stand test from a chair of standard height (43 cm height) without handrails. Participants began this test in a seated position with their arms across their chest and were instructed to fully stand up and return to the chair five times as quickly as possible. Timing began as soon as the participant stood up out of the chair on the first repetition and ended upon returning to the seat following the fifth repetition.

For the 6MWT, a 60 ft (18.3 m) walkway was marked with small cones, and participants were instructed to walk as many laps as possible in 6 min. Chairs were placed at either end of the walkway as well as in the middle to allow participants to rest if needed. Investigators provided timing updates every 2 min of the test but did not provide updates regarding the participants’ performance during the test. At the end of the timing window, investigators marked the point on the walkway where participants stopped and the distance was measured back to the previous cone and added to the total distance.

Statistical analysis

All data was entered into an Excel spreadsheet and checked for accuracy. Descriptive statistics were calculated (see Tables 2 and 3) and all assumptions for analyses were met. To examine the hypothesized decreases in muscle performance, functional ability, and increases in biomarkers (uremic toxins and markers of inflammation) between healthy controls and across the stages of CKD, the variable of CKD stage was converted into three planned contrast variables – one compared mean differences of the various outcome variables between healthy participants to those in Stage 3b, one compared participants in Stage 3b to Stage 4, and one compared participants in Stage 4 to Stage 5. These three planned contrasts were then used as predictors of the outcome variables in a linear model analysis using the lm function in R [22]. This approach allowed an examination of overall differences across all stages (significant model fit) as well as specific differences between each successive stage of CKD (significant coefficients for individual predictors). Effect sizes of differences between stages were calculated using Cohen’s d. [22]

Results

Muscle performance parameters

Results of this study indicate significant differencesbetween CKD stages for MVIC (F(3,34) = 3.88, p =.02). Analysis indicated that MVIC was significantly higher in HC than in CKD3 (p=.01), and significantly higher in CKD3 than in CKD4 (p =.03). However, no significant difference was observed between CKD4 and CKD5 for MVIC (p=.21).

There were also asignificant differences between CKD stages for RFD50 (F(3,34) = 2.36, p =.03). Analysis indicated that RFD50 was significantly higher in CKD3 than in CKD4 (p =.005). However, no significant differences were observed between HC and CKD3 or CKD4 and CKD5 (ps > 0.05). There were no significant differences for RFD100 (F(3,34) = 2.51, p =.07) or RFD200 (F(3,34) = 2.14, p =.11).

Results also indicated that there were no significant differences between stages for force steadiness at 30% MVIC (F(3,34) = 1.39, p =.26) or 70% MVIC (F(3,34) = 0.97, p =.42).

Functional performance assessments

Results of this study indicated significant differences between stages for SPPB scores (F(3,35) = 4.55, p =.008). Analysis indicated that significantly higher SPPB scores were observed in HC compared to CKD3 (p =.001), CKD3 compared to CKD4 (p =.01), and CKD4 compared to CKD5 (p=.03). There were also significant differences between stages on 6MWT performance (F(3,35) = 6.16, p =.002). Analysis indicated that 6MWT distance was significantly higher in HC than in CKD3 (p <.001) and CKD3 compared to CKD4 (p <.001) (See Table 2).

Blood parameters

The uremic toxins IS, PCS, and the inflammatory marker IL6 all increased significantly from HC to CKD3 to CKD4 to CKD5 (all ps < 0.05). TNF only showed a significant increase between HC and CKD3 (p =.03; See Table 3). Cohen’s d effect sizes [23] for significant differences between CKD stages were moderate to large (ranging between 0.38 and 1.33).

FACIT-F

Analysis of the FACIT-F data indicated a similar pattern to the other variables with a significant decrease in the FACIT-F score from HC (50.6 土 1.3) to CKD 3 (38.2 土 11.1; p <.001)) to CKD 4 (29.3 土 14.0; p <.001) with no additional reduction with CKD 5 (30.8 土 11.7; p >.05).

Motor unit firing characteristics

Due to multiple participants within each group not satisfying the criteria for inclusion in the motor unit decomposition analysis, a separate subgroup analysis was performed for all variables related to motor unit firing characteristics whereby individuals were classified as either HC or CKD. For analyses performed at 30% MVIC, the HC group consisted of six individuals while the CKD group consisted of seven individuals. Among these individuals, 120 motor units of 247 identified were excluded from analysis for not meeting quality criteria, leaving a final set of 127 motor units. For analyses performed at 70% MVIC, the HC group consisted of 6 individuals, while the CKD group consisted of 16 individuals. Among these individuals, 136 motor units of 378 identified were excluded from analysis for not meeting quality criteria, leaving a final set of 242 motor units. Results of this analysis indicated that the y-intercept of the mean firing rate vs. recruitment threshold regression at 70% MVIC was significantly greater in HC compared to CKD (p =.03; d = 1.10). However, all other comparisons were not significantly different (ps > 0.05).

Discussion

The main findings of our study indicate that as CKD progresses, skeletal muscle function, neuromuscular function, performance on functional measures, and the levels of some key biomarkers associated with muscle function worsen. In addition, the perception of fatigue, as assessed by the FACIT questionnaire, also increased in alignment with the objective deterioration in muscle function. Individuals in the later stages of the disease displayed marked deficits in isometric force production, rate of force development, and functional performance compared to healthy age-matched controls. Our results indicate that differences in muscle functional performance may be partially due to disease-related changes in muscle strength and power.

The findings from the current study generally aligned with previous research investigating comparisons in force and functional performance between individuals with CKD and healthy controls [14] as well as comparisons between individuals with CKD on hemodialysis and those not on hemodialysis [24]. Gollie and colleagues [14] observed that maximal isometric force was significantly lower in a group of individuals with CKD stages 3b to 4 when compared to healthy individuals. Additionally, Gollie et al. [14] noted that RFD50 was significantly correlated with sit-to-stand time in individuals with CKD but not in healthy controls. The current study also observed that these deficits are present between different stages of CKD, with CKD4 displaying significantly lower MVIC force than CKD3. These results were similar to those observed for both functional performance tests. Taken together, our results would seem to indicate that individuals with CKD experience changes to muscle performance that are independent of age-related changes, which may partially explain differences in functional performance as assessed via the SPPB and six-minute walk test. The differences that were observed were more marked as the disease progresses.

To our knowledge, no previous research has investigated differences in force steadiness comparing individuals with CKD to healthy controls or across stages of CKD. However, previous research has suggested that force steadiness decreases with age [25, 26]. One possible explanation is that age-related changes to motor unit firing characteristics may result in reduced muscular coordination due to motor neuron losses [27], and these changes are exacerbated in individuals with CKD. Indeed, it has been postulated that one of the causes of enhanced fatigability in individuals with CKD is neuromuscular impairments [28]. While preliminary, this is partially supported by the significant differences in the y-intercept of the mean firing rate vs. recruitment threshold at 70% MVIC in the current study. This difference would seem to indicate that healthy, age-matched individuals tend to adopt a recruitment strategy that is more dependent on increases in rate coding rather than motor unit recruitment at higher contraction intensities. Previous research has indicated that older adults display motor unit compression, primarily relying on lower-threshold motor units that fire at a faster rate, while younger adults recruit across a broader range of exercise intensities [10]. The difference in the y-intercept observed between groups in this current study, in addition to no differences observed in the corresponding slope, would seem to indicate that at higher isometric force productions, individuals with CKD display lower motor unit firing rates compared to healthy age-matched controls. While speculative, this could be an indication that individuals with CKD meet force demand by recruiting motor units across a broader range of forces that fire at lower frequencies than healthy individuals. As stated above, hyperkalemia is a common observation in CKD, which may result in reduced motoneuron excitability at rest [11]. Additionally, motoneuron loss due to aging may be exacerbated in individuals with CKD, resulting in a smaller population of less excitable motor units compared to healthy individuals. This would force a compensatory change in recruitment strategy whereby a larger proportion of the motor pool is recruited to meet a submaximal task demand due to lower rate coding within each motor unit. This would seem to be further supported by the lack of difference in contractions performed at 30% MVIC, which would seem to suggest that these differences are primarily the result of alterations to high-threshold motor units.

The levels of uremic toxins (PCS and IS), both increased as the stage of CKD progressed. It was interesting to note that, unlike some other variables that we assessed, the levels of both of these toxins were higher from stage 5 compared to stage 4. With most other variables, there was no difference between stages 4 and 5. However, this difference in the levels of these uremic toxins was not associated with corresponding further deterioration in skeletal muscle function as assessed by strength or functional parameters. There is evidence to suggest that high levels of uremic toxins adversely affect mitochondrial function which then negatively impacts skeletal muscle function [3–5]. The impairment is initially observed in skeletal muscle endurance capacity [15]. Our results generally support these previous findings.

The FACIT scores in the CKD patients were progressively lower than HC as the disease progressed, although they were not different between CKD4 and CKD5. The presence of lower FACIT scores indicates the perception of the presence of more fatigue. It has already been established that fatigue increases as the disease progresses, so this finding is not new [17, 29]. However, not all researchers have reported this finding in CKD patients [30]. Furthermore, 76% of the sample of CKD patients had FACIT scores < 44. This is in keeping with what other researchers have found when using < 44 as the cut-off score for the presence of fatigue [17].

Our study was limited by the small sample size, but we recognize that this was a preliminary study. Furthermore, we had difficulty acquiring the motor unit data in certain participants within the CKD group, leading to higher attrition and the need to perform an analysis on the CKD subgroup rather than individual group analysis. Therefore, our observations regarding motor unit activation across the stages of CKD are preliminary. While every effort was taken to improve signal decomposition quality through standardizing procedures, performing additional preparation of the skin when necessary, and thoroughly familiarizing all participants with proper procedures related to submaximal contractions, it is possible that excessive adipose tissue (mean % fat was > 30 for CKD groups), and/or reductions in action potential amplitude in this population, present additional challenges to the successful decomposition of motor units in individuals with CKD. We also acknowledge that we should have monitored the dietary intake for all participants prior to testing and analyzed it for protein and total calories. These limitations should be addressed in future research. Future research should also consider inclusion of various potentially relevant covariates and moderators (e.g., BMI, physical activity, comorbidities) that may help explain some of the differences among CKD stages and the processes associated with these differences.

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate that individuals with CKD display reduced isometric force and early rate of force development, which may be exacerbated by disease progression. Major decrements in muscle performance seem to occur early in the clinical stages of the disease but then seem to stabilize between stages 4 to 5. Admittedly, our study is preliminary in nature with a small sample size but our results warrant further investigation with larger numbers. Future research should further investigate specific mechanisms underlying the cause of disease-related strength and power losses, as well as potential interventions targeted at improving these measures on improvements in functional outcomes in individuals with CKD.
