Application of reinforced U-shaped suture technique in laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy: an analysis of 165 consecutive cases
Authors: Yuanjie Sun, Tingshuo Zhao, Yutao Wu, Zhigang Wei, Jie Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePostoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) remains a major postoperative complication of laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy (LDP) that severely impacts surgical safety and patient prognosis. This study was designed to investigate the clinical efficacy of the reinforced U-shaped suture technique in reducing the incidence of POPF after LDP, and to identify independent risk factors for POPF to provide evidence-based references for clinical practice.MethodsData were collected from 165 patients who underwent LDP between 2018 and February 2025 in our institution. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the reinforcement technique employed for pancreatic stump closure: Group A: non-reinforced U-shaped suture with 113 patients; Group B: reinforced U-shaped suture with 52 patients. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance baseline covariates between the two groups. Clinical data of all patients were collected, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to screen independent risk factors for POPF, and to compare the clinical outcomes of the two groups.ResultsUsing multivariate analysis, pancreatic stump reinforcement technique, including our method, and other well-known POPF risk factors were independently assessed. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified intraoperative bleeding (≥ 345.00 mL) (OR = 8.08, 95% CI: 2.41–27.11, p < 0.001) and non reinforced U-shaped suture (Group A, Group B OR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.65, p = 0.009) as independent risk factors for POPF. Additionally, pancreatic duct diameter ≥ 3.00 mm (OR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.39, p < 0.001) and hard pancreatic texture (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.68, p = 0.011) were identified as independent protective factors for POPF. Compared with Group A, Group B had a significantly lower incidence of clinically relevant grade B/C POPF, and presented more favorable perioperative outcomes including shorter hospital stay and lower hospitalization costs.ConclusionThe reinforced U-shaped suture technique is a feasible and cost-effective pancreatic stump closure method for LDP, which is associated with a lower incidence of clinically relevant grade B/C POPF and more favorable perioperative outcomes. Its proposed dual technical characteristics of staple-line mechanical stabilization and indirect pancreatic duct compression/sealing may contribute to the reduction of POPF risk, and the technique holds particular clinical value for high-risk populations awaiting further prospective validation.

Introduction

Distal Pancreatectomy (DP), initially proposed by Billroth in 1884, involves the resection of pancreatic tissue to the left of the mesenteric-portal venous axis, which encompasses the distal pancreas. This procedure is a standard treatment for both benign and malignant diseases affecting the pancreatic body and tail. However, the application of DP is severely hindered by the high rates of postoperative complications and mortality [1–3]. In recent years, improvements in surgical techniques and perioperative management have substantially reduced mortality rates; yet, the incidence of postoperative pancreatic fistula (POPF) remains distressingly high, varying from approximately 10% to 40% [4, 5]. Moreover, POPF could trigger a cascade of additional complications, such as intra-abdominal fluid accumulation, delayed gastric emptying (DGE), postoperative hemorrhage, abdominal infections, and sepsis, which can severely compromise patients’ quality of life and even result in death. Consequently, identifying effective strategies to mitigate the risk of POPF following DP is essential for enhancing patient outcomes and informing clinical practice.

Management of the pancreatic stump is pivotal in the prevention of POPF and represents the sole controllable factor in this context [6]. Various techniques for sealing the pancreatic stump have been documented in the literature, encompassing hand-sewn sutures, stapler closure, fibrin glue application, and serosal patching with the use of the falciform ligament, stomach, or jejunum. However, none of these approaches have demonstrated a significant reduction in the incidence of POPF, and the optimal method for pancreatic stump closure continues to be a matter of debate [7–14].

Although the 2020 Miami International Evidence-Based Guidelines on Minimally Invasive Pancreatic Surgery recommended reinforcing the pancreatic stump after stapler closure, the specific materials and methods for reinforcement have not yet been clearly defined [15]. Nowadays, some studies, based on the use of a stapler for handling the pancreatic remnant, further reinforce the pancreatic remnant through manual suturing and non-autologous reinforcers [16, 17]. In our center, hand-sewn sutures with a stapler are initially performed for sealing the pancreatic stump in LDP, and then the reinforcements with different hand-sewn techniques are applied to reduce the POPF. In this retrospective study, we compared the incidence of POPF in different suture reinforcement techniques for pancreatic stump closure with aims to find out the best way to pancreatic stump closure.

Methods

Data source and grouping

This study collected data from 165 patients who underwent LDP at the Department of Biliary and Pancreatic Surgery, First Hospital of Shanxi Medical University, between 2018 and February 2025. The inclusion criteria are patients who underwent LDP for various reasons and have complete clinical data. The corresponding exclusion criteria include patients who did not receive further reinforcement of the remnant and those who lack necessary clinical data that could influence the diagnosis of POPF. Patients were divided into two groups according to the method of pancreatic stump suture reinforcement: Group A: non-reinforced U-shaped suture with 113 patients; Group B: reinforced U-shaped suture with 52 patients.

The collected data covered three aspects: preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative. Preoperative data included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, drinking history, past medical history (e.g., diabetes and hypertension), American Society of Anesthesiology (ASA) classification, pancreatic duct diameter (main pancreatic duct, MPD; a diameter ≥ 3.00 mm was defined as pancreatic duct dilatation [18]) and pancreatic texture. Intraoperative data encompassed intraoperative blood loss and spleen preservation status. Postoperative data involved amylase levels in abdominal drainage fluid on postoperative day 3 and beyond, postoperative treatment and nursing measures, as well as the occurrence, type, and severity of postoperative complications (graded by the Clavien-Dindo classification system), total hospital stay, postoperative hospital stay, total hospitalization cost, 30-day readmission rate, and 30-day mortality.

Surgical procedure

The surgery was performed by experienced pancreatic surgeons using a five-port technique. An observation port was placed approximately 1 cm below the umbilicus, and pneumoperitoneum was established. Two 12-mm main operating ports were inserted at the lateral edges of the left and right rectus abdominis muscles near the midclavicular line as working ports, while two 5-mm auxiliary ports were inserted below the xiphoid process (on the right side) and 5 cm below the costal margin at the anterior axillary line on the left side. After laparoscopic exploration of the abdominal cavity, the gastrocolic ligament was incised to enter the lesser sac, freeing the lower margin of the pancreas and lowering the transverse mesocolon. The Toldt’s fascia plane behind the pancreas was then dissected and expanded. Dissection continued along the upper margin of the pancreas to expose the splenic artery and create a posterior pancreatic tunnel. The pancreas was transected with a stapler, followed by reinforcement of the pancreatic stump using different suture techniques. Notably, gentle prefiring compression of the pancreatic parenchyma has been reported to reduce the incidence of POPF after DP [19]. Consistent with this finding, the pancreatic parenchyma at the planned resection site was slowly compressed with forceps and the stapler for 10.00 min prior to transection. After firing the stapler, the jaws were not released immediately but remained closed for an additional 10.00 min to ensure adequate hemostasis and tissue adaptation. Specifically, purple KANGDI QRM® cartridges with a staple height of 3.50 mm were uniformly used for pancreatic transection in all cases of this study. The decision to preserve the splenic artery, vein, and spleen depended on the surgical approach. Finally, the specimen was extracted through an extended umbilical observation port, and at least one 18-Fr rubber drain was placed near the pancreatic stump.

Patients were divided into two groups based on the method of suture reinforcement after stapler transection of the pancreas: Group A: Following stapler transection, the pancreatic stump was reinforced using conventional interrupted or continuous suturing using 4 − 0 Prolene (Ethicon, San Lorenzo, Puerto Rico, USA). The needle entry and exit points were positioned within normal pancreatic tissue approximately 1.00–2.00 mm medial to the staple line. For interrupted sutures, a stitch interval of approximately 5.00–7.00 mm was maintained; for continuous suturing, a comparable interval was followed in a parallel fashion. The primary objective of this suturing was to achieve simple serosal imbrication or mild appositional reinforcement of the stump. No specific attempt was made to target the main pancreatic duct. Suture tension was adjusted to achieve adequate tissue approximation while avoiding excessive tightening that could lead to tissue cutting or necrosis. Group B: After stapler transection, the pancreatic stump was reinforced with U-shaped sutures using 4 − 0 Prolene (Fig. 1).The relevant surgical videos can be found in Video. In both groups, the needle entry and exit points during suturing were precisely positioned in the normal pancreatic parenchyma medial to the stapler line, approximately 1.00–2.00 mm away from the staples. This approach was intended to avoid direct suturing through the fragile staple line or the cartridge itself, thereby minimizing the risk of staple line disruption and pancreatic parenchymal injury. No specific surgical technique was prioritized; the selection of pancreatic stump closure method was solely at the discretion of the operating surgeon intraoperatively.

Definition and grading of postoperative complications

Pancreatic fistula is defined as an abnormal connection between the pancreatic ductal epithelium and other epithelial surfaces, containing enzyme-rich fluid originating from the pancreas. The diagnostic criteria established by the International Study Group of Pancreatic Surgery (ISGPS) in 2016 were adopted: the concentration of amylase in any amount of postoperative drainage fluid on or after postoperative day 3 must be more than three times the upper limit of normal serum amylase levels, and it must be accompanied by relevant clinical manifestations [20]. The 2016 criteria also revised the grading of pancreatic fistulas, eliminating “Grade A pancreatic fistula” and redefining it as a “biochemical leak” (BL). Previous studies have shown that Grade A pancreatic fistulas do not significantly differ in prognosis from cases without pancreatic fistula [21, 22].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis in this study was conducted using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Baseline covariates were balanced by the method of propensity score matching (PSM) using R v4.4.0. We compared categorical data using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to identify independent risk factors associated with POPF. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The cutoff for age (52 years) and intraoperative blood loss (345.00 mL) were determined based on the mean values calculated from the entire cohort of 165 patients in this study. This approach provides an objective and data-driven method to dichotomize these continuous variables into “high” and “low” groups for comparative risk analysis. In this study, the cutoff value of 24 for BMI classification was selected, which is in line with the relevant domestic health standards. Moreover, it meets the requirement of accurately assessing the risks of overweight and obesity for the research objects, who are the general adult population in China.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 165 patients undergoing LDP were retrospectively analyzed, stratified into two groups based on pancreatic stump closure techniques: Group A (non reinforced U-shaped suture, n = 113) and Group B (reinforced U-shaped suture, n = 52). PSM was subsequently performed to balance baseline covariates, yielding a matched cohort of 125 patients (Group A, n = 77; Group B, n = 48). In both the pre- and post-PSM cohorts, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of baseline demographic and clinical characteristics (all p > 0.05), including gender, BMI, prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, smoking and drinking habits, splenectomy status, pancreatic duct diameter, pancreatic texture, pathological type, ASA score, and age (Table 1). Intraoperative blood loss ≥ 345.00 mL was less frequent in Group B than in Group A in the pre-PSM analysis (17.31% vs. 30.09%, p = 0.082), though this difference did not reach statistical significance and was completely balanced after PSM (18.75% vs. 24.68%, p = 0.440).

Univariate and multivariate analyses of risk factors for grade B/C POPF after PSM

Univariate analysis of the post-PSM cohort identified multiple variables significantly associated with the development of grade B/C POPF (Table 2). Intraoperative bleeding ≥ 345.00 mL was a strong risk factor (OR = 6.27, 95% CI: 2.52–15.64, p < 0.001), with patients experiencing excessive bleeding having a markedly higher incidence of POPF. Pancreatic duct diameter ≥ 3.00 mm (OR = 0.22, 95% CI:0.09–0.53, p < 0.001) and pancreatic texture of hard consistency (OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.23–1.20, p = 0.126) were associated with a lower POPF risk, though the latter was not statistically significant. Most importantly, the reinforced U-shaped suture technique (Group B) was significantly protective against grade B/C POPF compared to the non-reinforced approach (Group A) (OR = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.13–0.85, p = 0.021). In contrast, gender, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, splenectomy, age, ASA score, pathological type, drinking, and smoking status showed no significant correlation with POPF occurrence in the univariate analysis (all p > 0.05).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to adjust for confounding variables, and the results confirmed four independent factors associated with grade B/C POPF in the post-PSM cohort (Table 2). Intraoperative bleeding ≥ 345.00 mL was the most prominent independent risk factor (OR = 8.08, 95% CI: 2.41–27.11, p < 0.001). Non-reinforced U-shaped suture (Group A) was verified as another independent risk factor, while the reinforced U-shaped suture technique (Group B) served as an independent protective factor (OR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.65, p = 0.009), which was associated with an 82.00% reduction in the odds of grade B/C POPF in the multivariate regression model after adjusting for confounding variables. Additionally, pancreatic duct diameter ≥ 3.00 mm (OR = 0.11, 95% CI: 0.03–0.39, p < 0.001) and hard pancreatic texture (OR = 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.68, p = 0.011) were identified as independent protective factors for POPF. Variables including gender, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, splenectomy, age, ASA score, pathological type, drinking, and smoking lost their statistical significance in the multivariate model (all p > 0.05), indicating they were not independent predictors of grade B/C POPF after LDP.

Postoperative clinical outcomes

Postoperative outcomes of the two groups in the post-PSM cohort were compared, and the results demonstrated that Group B (reinforced U-shaped suture) had more favorable perioperative outcomes than Group A (non-reinforced U-shaped suture) (Table 3). Group B exhibited shorter median total hospital stay (18.00 days vs. 21.00 days) and postoperative hospital stay (9.50 days vs. 12.00 days), as well as a lower median total hospitalization cost (69,121.00 vs. 75,647.00 Chinese Yuan, CNY), reflecting the economic and clinical benefits of the reinforced U-shaped suture technique. All POPF cases in Group A occurred within 7 days postoperatively in 84.62% of patients and after 7 days in 15.38% of patients, while Group B had no POPF events occurring after 7 days postoperatively. The Clavien-Dindo classification of complications showed that Group B had a numerically lower proportion of grade ≥ III complications (2.08% vs. 5.19%), and no reoperations were required in Group B, versus 2 cases (2.60%) of reoperation in Group A. There was no 30-day mortality in Group B, while Group A had 1 case (1.30%) of 30-day mortality. Additionally, the 30-day readmission rate was slightly lower in Group B (10.42%) than in Group A (11.69%). Collectively, these findings confirm that the reinforced U-shaped suture technique is associated with a lower risk of POPF and more favorable overall postoperative safety and clinical outcomes, though the potential influence of other unmeasured factors cannot be completely excluded.

Discussion

The management of pancreatic stump closure remains a critical determinant of POPF following DP. Our retrospective analysis of 165 consecutive LDP cases, with PSM to balance baseline covariates, provides robust evidence that the reinforced U-shaped suture technique exerts a significant protective effect against clinically relevant grade B/C POPF. Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified intraoperative bleeding ≥ 345.00 mL (OR = 8.08, 95% CI: 2.41–27.11, p < 0.001) as the most potent independent risk factor for POPF, and non-reinforced U-shaped suture (Group A) as another key independent risk factor (Group B OR = 0.18, 95% CI: 0.05–0.65, p = 0.009).

These findings confirm that the reinforced U-shaped suture technique is associated with an 82.00% reduction in the odds of grade B/C POPF in the multivariate regression model compared with non-reinforced suture methods. The reinforced U-shaped suture technique has two proposed dual technical characteristics that may address the core pathophysiological mechanisms of POPF formation after LDP. First, the U-shaped configuration distributes mechanical tension uniformly across the staple line, reducing dislocation risks in patients with thick or friable pancreatic parenchyma—a common challenge highlighted in prior studies [23–25]. Second, the U-shaped suture configuration may indirectly compress or seal the main pancreatic duct (MPD) and pancreatic parenchymal ducts at the stump site through uniform tension distribution on the staple line, which may address a critical limitation of stapler-only closure, where incomplete MPD sealing often precipitates leakage [26–28]. This aligns with reports emphasizing the importance of MPD management in POPF prevention [29, 30]. The staple-line stabilization effect of the reinforced U-shaped suture technique is similar to that of bioabsorbable mesh-reinforced staplers in clinical practice [31, 32], and this study does not provide direct evidence of mechanistic equivalence between the two techniques; yet reinforced U-shaped suture offers a cost-effective alternative without reliance on specialized materials.

Beyond the protective effect of stump reinforcement, our findings reaffirm that intraoperative blood loss ≥ 345.00 mL is a significant independent risk factor for POPF [33]. This aligns with established literature linking substantial hemorrhage to pancreatic parenchymal trauma, impaired healing, and elevated postoperative morbidity [34]. Consequently, our results emphasize the imperative of integrating meticulous hemostasis with pancreatic stump reinforcement as a dual strategy to mitigate the risk of POPF risk in LDP, particularly in high-risk cases characterized by soft pancreatic texture or a small pancreatic duct diameter (< 3.00 mm)—both of which were confirmed as independent risk factors associated with increased POPF risk in our multivariate analysis [23, 25, 35].

The clinical benefits associated with the reinforced U-shaped suture technique extend beyond POPF reduction, encompassing improved perioperative outcomes and economic efficiency. In the propensity score-matched cohort, Group B (reinforced suture) demonstrated a shorter median total hospital stay (18.00 vs. 21.00 days), a shorter postoperative stay (9.50 vs. 12.00 days), and lower median total hospitalization costs (69,121 vs. 75,647 CNY) compared to Group A (non-reinforced). Furthermore, the reinforced U-shaped suture group had numerically lower rates of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III complications, 30-day mortality, and reoperation, which may be associated with the surgical technique, but cannot exclude the potential influence of the learning curve of the surgical team and the optimization of perioperative management over the study period. Unlike methods utilizing bioabsorbable mesh-reinforced staplers or other specialized devices that increase cost, this technique employs standard 4 − 0 Prolene sutures, representing a cost-effective reinforcement strategy suitable for broad clinical adoption [8, 16].

This study has several limitations that contextualize the findings and inform future research. First, its single-center, retrospective design introduces potential selection bias and may limit generalizability to institutions with differing practices or patient demographics. Second, the smaller sample size in Group B (n = 48 post-PSM) compared to Group A may reduce statistical power for detecting differences in rarer outcomes. Third, unmeasured confounders, such as precise staple line tension or surgeon experience, were not systematically recorded and could influence results. Fourth, the study did not perform formal mediation or interaction analysis to explore the potential causal link or synergistic effect between intraoperative bleeding and surgical suture technique, which limits the ability to confirm the direct causal effect of the reinforced U-shaped suture technique on POPF reduction. Fifth, the potential biases caused by the learning curve of surgeons and the evolution of perioperative management over the 7-year study period cannot be fully eliminated by PSM, which may affect the interpretation of differences in clinically significant endpoints such as severe complications and 30-day mortality between the two groups. Sixth, long-term outcomes, including pancreatic exocrine or endocrine function, were not assessed and warrant investigation to confirm the technique’s long-term safety.

Notwithstanding these limitations, our study is strengthened by the use of PSM to balance key baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between groups, effectively controlling for known POPF risk factors like age, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, pancreatic texture, and duct diameter. Furthermore, we rigorously applied the 2016 ISGPS criteria for POPF diagnosis, excluding biochemical leaks (formerly Grade A) and focusing on clinically relevant grade B/C POPF, thereby ensuring our results reflect meaningful clinical endpoints. The standardization of the surgical procedure by an experienced team also minimizes technical variability, enhancing the reliability of the comparative analysis.

Future research should prioritize prospective, multicenter studies with larger cohorts to validate the efficacy and generalizability of this technique. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing the reinforced U-shaped suture against other established methods (e.g., bioabsorbable stapler reinforcement, serosal patching) are needed to define the optimal stump management strategy in LDP. Subgroup analyses focusing on high-risk populations (e.g., soft pancreas, small duct, high BMI) would clarify the specific scenarios conferring greatest benefit. Finally, long-term follow-up studies are essential to evaluate impacts on pancreatic function and late complications, ensuring the technique’s sustained safety and value.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the reinforced U-shaped suture technique is a feasible and cost-effective strategy associated with a lower incidence of clinically relevant grade B/C POPF and more favorable perioperative outcomes in patients undergoing LDP. Its proposed dual technical characteristics of staple-line mechanical stabilization and indirect pancreatic duct compression/sealing may contribute to the reduction of POPF risk, and this theoretical mechanism awaits further validation by prospective studies with objective intraoperative documentation. The potential influence of learning curve and era-related effects on clinical endpoints cannot be completely excluded, and future prospective randomized controlled trials with standardized surgical and perioperative management protocols are needed to verify the true effect of the technique. This technique holds particular clinical value for high-risk LDP populations, and integrating it with meticulous hemostasis may form an effective dual strategy to mitigate POPF risk in clinical practice.
