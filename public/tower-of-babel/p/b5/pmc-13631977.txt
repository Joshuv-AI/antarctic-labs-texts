Impact of social determinants of health on cervical cancer stage at presentation among women in Zambia: a cross-sectional single-center epidemiological study
Authors: Graciela M Nogueras-Gonzalez, Mehmet Enes Inam, Susan Msadabwe, Kamaria L Lee, Bernadette Njala, Dyness Sakala, Mwando Chitula, Jane Montealegre, Susan K Peterson, Elizabeth Chiao, Lilie L Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe impact of social determinants of health (SDOH) on cervical cancer stage in Zambia remains poorly understood and understudied.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, we administered the Accountable Health Communities Health-Related Social Needs Screening Tool, modified for Zambian culture, to newly diagnosed cervical cancer patients at the Cancer Diseases Hospital (CDH) in Lusaka, Zambia, between June 2022 and March 2025. The primary aim of the study was to determine the relationship between sociodemographic factors, SDOH domains, and advanced cervical cancer stage (FIGO stage III/IV) at presentation to CDH. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression models were performed to determine associations.ResultsThere were 259 survey respondents, with a median age of 50 years (range: 29–78); 47.1% of them were diagnosed with advanced cervical cancer, 55.2% were living with HIV, and most (79.1%) had completed primary (grades 1–7) or secondary (grades 8–12) education. In unadjusted analyses, lack of knowledge about cervical cancer risks and symptoms, rural residence, lower educational attainment, cooking outside the home, and staying in the hospital during treatment were associated with advanced FIGO stage at diagnosis. In contrast, lack of knowledge about cervical cancer risks and symptoms, a measure of health literacy, remained independently associated with advanced cervical cancer stage in the adjusted model (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.03–3.11; p = 0.040).ConclusionsAlthough additional studies are needed to understand how SDOH contribute specifically to access to cervical cancer prevention and diagnostic services in low-and middle income countries, we found that lack of awareness about the risks and symptoms of cervical cancer is one SDOH associated with advanced cancer stage at presentation. Our study therefore provides a basis for future development of educational strategies to increase awareness of the importance of screening and early diagnosis and reduce late-stage cervical cancer presentations in these settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16315-5.Keywords: Social determinants of health, Neoplasm staging, Uterine cervical neoplasms, Epidemiology, Cross-sectional studies, Health services research, Early detection of cancer, Health literacy, Zambia

Novelty and impact

We observed a significant association between awareness of cervical cancer risks and symptoms and FIGO stage at diagnosis. This finding provides new insights into how disparities in knowledge and health literacy, as well as other important social determinants of health, may contribute to later-stage presentation of cervical cancer, an area that has been insufficiently examined. By explicitly linking cervical cancer awareness to disease stage, our study addresses a critical epidemiologic gap and strengthens the empirical basis for developing targeted, stage-conscious interventions for low- and middle-income countries. These results offer relevant evidence to inform the design of equitable health education and screening strategies aimed at reducing late-stage cervical cancer diagnoses.

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDOH) encompass non-medical factors that impact health, including marital status, educational level, neighborhood disadvantage, social support, income status, and health literacy [1]. These SDOH factors shape individuals’ ability to recognize symptoms, navigate healthcare systems, and access timely and appropriate care. In cancer care, adverse SDOH have been linked to obstacles in accessing timely care, resulting in advanced-stage diagnoses and poorer outcomes [2]. Evidence from high-income countries such as the U.K. and Australia demonstrate that socioeconomic disadvantage, limited education, and constrained access to healthcare contribute to advanced cancer stage at diagnosis and impact cancer outcomes [3–5]. However, the pathways through which SDOH influence cancer outcomes may differ substantially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where structural barriers and resource constraints are more pronounced.

Cervical cancer is one of the most preventable and treatable cancers, yet it remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality globally [6]. The cervical cancer burden is disproportionately concentrated in LMICs, where cervical cancer screening coverage, early detection, and timely treatment are limited. In LMICs such as Zambia, SDOH-related barriers, including low health literacy, poverty, transportation challenges, and fragmented referral systems, affect diagnostic and treatment delays across the cancer care continuum [7–9]. Zambia bears some of the highest cervical cancer incidence and mortality rates in Southern Africa and worldwide [10]. Despite national screening efforts, many Zambian women continue to present with advanced disease, underscoring persistent gaps in early diagnosis and access to care and highlighting the urgent need for targeted research and policy interventions that enhance cancer care delivery in resource-limited settings [11–13].

Although prior studies in LMICs have described individual barriers to cervical cancer screening or treatment, [14, 15] critical gaps remain. Specifically, limited empirical evidence from Zambia and similar LMIC settings quantitatively examines how multiple, co-occurring SDOH domains collectively influence stage at cervical cancer diagnosis. Existing literature often focuses on single factors (e.g., screening uptake or travel distance) or post-diagnosis outcomes, rather than on the upstream social and structural conditions that shape delays before diagnosis. Moreover, few studies have applied standardized, multidimensional SDOH measurement tools to evaluate their relationship with advanced-stage disease at initial presentation. As a result, it remains unclear which SDOH domains most strongly contribute to late-stage cervical cancer diagnosis in Zambia, where access to cancer care is centralized in the capital, Lusaka, and oncology services are geographically limited.

To address this gap, we conducted a cross-sectional study aiming to understand the relationship between SDOH domains and advanced cervical cancer stage (International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics [FIGO] stage III/IV) at presentation to the Cancer Diseases Hospital (CDH) in Lusaka, Zambia. CDH is the country’s sole oncology referral center, receiving patients from all 10 Zambian provinces. We conceptualized SDOH influences on cervical cancer stage at diagnosis using an integrated framework drawing on the Social Determinants of Health model [16] and the Three Delays Framework [17]. Within this framework, SDOH such as economic stability, education access and quality, healthcare access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context may contribute to: (1) delays in the decision to seek care due to limited symptom recognition or competing socioeconomic priorities; (2) delays in reaching appropriate healthcare facilities due to transportation, financial constraints, or geographic distance; and (3) delays in receiving diagnostic and referral services within the health system. Women presenting for care with newly diagnosed cervical cancer were enrolled and completed a culturally adapted health-related social needs survey, the Accountable Health Communities (AHC) Health-Related Social Needs (HRSN) Screening Tool [18, 19]. The long-term goal of our study is to inform targeted interventions and policies to reduce diagnostic and treatment delays and improve equity in cancer care delivery in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and study population

We conducted a single-center, observational, cross-sectional study of patients with newly diagnosed cervical cancer who presented for treatment at CDH between June 2022 and March 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Eligible patients were over 18 years old; had a Karnofsky performance status score ≥ 70; had a hemoglobin level more than 9 g/dL; and, if living with HIV, were on a stable antiretroviral therapy regimen for at least 6 months and have viral suppression. Exclusion criteria included prior receipt of chemotherapy, use of any medications that could affect the immune system (other than antiretroviral therapy), evidence of hydronephrosis, presence of para-aortic lymphadenopathy, or presence of pelvic side wall disease, as these factors could impact ability to complete chemoradiotherapy.

Data collection

Data on demographic (age, marital status, and province of residence) and clinical (HIV status and cancer stage at diagnosis) characteristics were extracted from participants’ medical records. We used a survey adapted from the AHC HRSN Screening Tool, which was originally developed by the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services [18–21]. The survey was translated by a Zambian professional translator into the 4 most commonly spoken local languages (Tonga, Nyanga, Lozi, and Bemba) and subsequently back-translated into English to ensure linguistic consistency with the original instrument. The translated versions were reviewed by local study staff familiar with the clinical and cultural context to ensure the clarity and appropriateness of the wording. No formal pilot testing or psychometric validation of the adapted survey was conducted.

After participant consent was obtained, data were collected through in-person interviews conducted in the patient’s preferred language. Each interview lasted approximately 25 to 35 min and was administered by a trained research coordinator. A research electronic data capture project (REDCap) database was designed for patient-reported data registration and extracted clinical data [22, 23].

Measures

The primary endpoint of the study was cancer stage at presentation, defined as cancer of the cervix uteri of 2018 FIGO stage III/IV [24–26]. The modified survey assessed SDOH domains, including the core domains of housing instability (living situation or housing characteristics), food insecurity, transportation problems, and utility help needs, but omitted the interpersonal safety questions to tailor the survey to Zambia’s cultural and ethical context. Questions in the supplemental domain of the HRSN screening tool were also included, including questions on financial strain, employment, family and community support, education (primary [grades 1–7], secondary [grades 8–12], and tertiary [university/technical/vocational college]), physical activity, substance use, mental health, self-reported health literacy and knowledge about cervical cancer, self-reported prior cervical cancer screening, and disabilities. For self-reported knowledge of cervical cancer, women were asked whether, prior to diagnosis, they had heard of cervical cancer risk factors or symptoms; those responding affirmatively were asked to briefly describe what they knew. Among women reporting prior cervical cancer screening, we further assessed whether they had been counseled on symptoms to watch for, and, if so, to state which symptoms they were taught.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the University of Zambia’s Biomedical Ethics Research Committee and The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center’s Institutional Review Board.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population overall and by cancer stage at presentation. For bivariate analyses, chi-square or Fisher exact tests were performed as appropriate for categorical variables, and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for continuous variables. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Logistic regression models were used to examine the associations of cancer stage with SDOH domains and sociodemographic variables. For the adjusted (multivariate) logistic regression model, we included variables from the unadjusted (univariate) logistic regression models with an alpha level less than 0.10. Because this study was exploratory and observational, an alpha level less than 0.10 was used to identify suggestive associations for hypothesis generation, while an alpha level less than 0.05 denoted statistically significant findings. Missing data were handled using a complete-case analysis approach. Any variable with missing values greater than or equal to 25% missingness were excluded from the adjusted (multivariate) modeling to minimize potential bias and model instability. For variables included in the final adjusted model, missingness was low and assumed to be random given that data collection occurred during structured in-person interviews and clinical data abstraction. No imputation procedures were performed due to the modest sample size and limited auxiliary variables available to support robust imputation models. As a result, residual bias due to missing data cannot be entirely excluded and is acknowledged as a limitation of this analysis. All analyses were conducted using Stata/MP 18.0 for Windows (College Station, Texas).

Results

A total of 259 women with newly diagnosed cervical cancer presented for care at CDH. All participants completed the HRSN survey at the time of their initial presentation.

Overall, 47.1% of them women (47.1%) presented with advanced-stage (FIGO stage III/IV) disease, while 52.9% of the women presented with early-stage (FIGO stage I/II) disease. The respondents’ median age was 50 years (range: 29–78). Slightly more than half of respondents were aged 50 years or older (52.9%), while 47.1% were younger than 50. Most patients (79.1%) had completed primary or secondary education (analogous to elementary school or high school in the U.S.), 13.6% had no education, and 7.4% had completed tertiary education. More than half (55.4%) lived in urban areas (i.e., in a town). Regarding marital status, 60.6% of patients were married, and 39.4% were single, separated, divorced, or widowed. Patients traveled to CDH from all 10 provinces of Zambia, but mainly from Lusaka (29.3%), Copperbelt (17.8%), Southern (16.2%), Eastern (13.5%), and Central (11.2%) provinces; the other 5 provinces each contributed less than 3% of the participating patients. Of the 259 patients surveyed, 55.2% were women living with HIV (WLHIV), 38.8% had prior cervical cancer screening, and 34.4% reported having prior knowledge about their cervical cancer risks and symptoms.

Bivariate analyses showed that age distribution did not differ significantly between the subgroups with early-stage and advanced-stage disease at presentation (p = 0.661). In addition, no significant differences in marital status or province of residence were observed by cancer stage at presentation. Among patients with tertiary education, significantly more had early-stage rather than late-stage disease (10.3% vs. 4.1%; p = 0.057), and a greater proportion of early-stage patients lived in urban areas than in rural areas (61.0% vs. 49.2%; p = 0.056). A significantly higher percentage of patients diagnosed with early-stage disease reported prior knowledge of cervical cancer risks and symptoms compared to advanced-stage patients (41.6% vs. 26.2%, p = 0.009).

HIV status did not differ significantly by FIGO stage at diagnosis (p = 0.555; Table 1). Supplemental Table 1 presents sociodemographic characteristics stratified by HIV status, showing that WLHIV were younger (p < 0.001), more likely to live in urban areas (p = 0.001), and more likely to report prior cervical cancer screening (p = 0.005) and knowledge of cervical cancer risks and symptoms (p = 0.004) compared with HIV-negative women.

In the final adjusted (multivariate) model, after adjustment for education level, residence (urban vs. rural), the place where the participants cooked, and the place where participants stayed while receiving treatment (relative in Lusaka vs. hospital), only lack of knowledge of cervical cancer symptoms and risks was significantly associated with higher odds of having advanced-stage cervical cancer at presentation (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.03–3.11, p = 0.040). Cooking inside the home, an indicator of stable housing, was significantly associated with advanced disease stage in the unadjusted model but not remain significant in the adjusted model (Table 3).

Formal tests for effect modification were not conducted; however, stratified descriptive analyses by HIV status (Supplemental Table 1) demonstrated differences in age distribution, urban residence, screening history, and cervical cancer knowledge between WLHIV and HIV-negative women.

Supplemental Table 2 presents unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models examining associations between advanced stage at presentation and clinically relevant sociodemographic, clinical, and SDOH variables, including age, HIV status, and prior cervical cancer screening, which were evaluated regardless of univariable statistical significance due to their clinical relevance. In adjusted analyses, age was not significantly associated with advanced-stage disease (adjusted OR [aOR] = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.96–1.01; p = 0.300). HIV status was also not associated with stage at diagnosis after adjustment, with WLHIV having similar odds of advanced-stage disease as HIV-negative women (aOR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.57–1.72; p = 0.985). Similarly, prior cervical cancer screening was not significantly associated with stage at presentation in adjusted models (aOR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.19–1.91; p = 0.388). Educational level and place of residence showed marginal associations in unadjusted analyses but were not independently associated with advanced-stage disease after adjustment (aOR = 1.82, 95% CI: 0.99–3.35; p = 0.054).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of women with cervical cancer presenting to the CDH in Lusaka, Zambia, we found that a lack of knowledge about cervical cancer was associated with a higher risk of presentation with advanced-stage (FIGO stage III/IV) disease. Nearly half of the participants were diagnosed with advanced-stage disease, and more than 60% reported lacking prior knowledge about cervical cancer risks and symptoms. These findings underscore the critical role of awareness (or lack of awareness) of cervical cancer risk and symptoms and health literacy in influencing timely care-seeking behavior.

Our results are consistent with those of Lombe et al.,17 who applied the Three Delays Framework to cancer care in sub-Saharan Africa. Their systematic review demonstrated that delays in seeking, reaching, and receiving quality care are often driven by interconnected barriers, with lack of awareness and low health literacy being dominant contributors to delayed care. Of note, our study primarily captured SDOH factors related to seeking and accessing treatment. Although our survey did not assess system-level barriers, our findings suggest that even women with higher education or urban residence may experience late-stage diagnosis due to uniformly low screening rates and limited access to early detection services; notably, only 39% of participants reported having undergone cervical cancer screening prior to diagnosis.

In our cohort, most of the core SDOH domains—including housing stability, food insecurity, transportation access, and utility help needs—were not significantly associated with advanced-stage diagnosis in adjusted models. The strong association between lack of knowledge, a measure of health literacy, and advanced-stage disease suggests that informational and behavioral factors may play a more immediate role than structural SDOH in this context. Our results may also be explained by observations by Lombe et al. that low health literacy, fear, and reliance on traditional medicine are key contributors to delays in seeking care [17].

Our findings on cervical cancer awareness (or lack of knowledge of risk and symptoms) align with those of previous studies conducted in Zambia and other LMICs. Although cervical cancer screening is available at no-cost in Zambia, coverage remains low. For example, data from the 2021 Zambia Population-Based HIV Impact Assessment survey showed that only 22.2% of women aged 35 to 49 had undergone cervical cancer screening [27] Nyambe et al. [28] reported that only 36.8% of participants (including women and men) were aware of cervical cancer symptoms; similarly, just 38.8% of women in our cohort had undergone screening [29]. Lack of awareness of cervical cancer screening has been cited in other studies as the primary reason for not undergoing screening among women in South Africa and Nigeria [30, 31].

Importantly, while all women are at risk of cervical cancer, WLHIV face a significantly higher risk—approximately 6 times greater than that of HIV-negative women [32, 33]. A study by Mukosha et al. [34] found that HIV status impacts cervical cancer awareness and screening rates; among WLHIV in Lusaka, 62.4% were aware of cervical cancer screening, and 36.5% had been screened, suggesting that WLHIV may be more informed and proactive about screening [35]. Although WLHIV in our cohort demonstrated higher levels of knowledge about cervical cancer, this did not translate into earlier presentation; these women remained equally likely to be diagnosed at a late stage. These differences suggest potential heterogeneity in the pathways leading to delayed diagnosis that warrant further investigation in larger studies.

The strengths of our study include its cross-sectional design, which allowed for a contemporaneous assessment of sociodemographic factors, SDOH, and cervical cancer stage at presentation among women receiving care at the national oncology referral center in Zambia. We leveraged a standardized, validated survey instrument to assess multiple SDOH domains (e.g., economic stability, education access and quality, healthcare access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context), administered through in-person interviews, to capture a broad range of social and structural factors relevant to cancer care access. The survey was translated into multiple local languages and implemented by trained local staff at CDH to enhance its cultural appropriateness and participant engagement.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional nature of the of the study precludes causal inference and limits the ability to establish temporal relationships between SDOH factors—particularly knowledge of cervical cancer risks and symptoms—and stage at diagnosis. Health literacy may reflect accumulated exposure to the health care system through prior visits, counseling, or screening experiences, rather than exist entirely before the diagnostic process. Second, although the survey instrument was adapted for the Zambian context, it was originally developed in a high-income setting, and formal pilot testing or psychometric validation of the adapted tool in this population was not conducted. As such, measurement validity concerns remain, particularly for constructs such as health literacy and housing stability, which may not be fully captured by single survey items. The relatively modest sample size may have limited our power to detect moderate effects in multivariable analyses and restricted the ability to formally assess effect modification. Additionally, our sample only included patients who presented to CDH with a cervical cancer diagnosis. Although our findings may not represent all women in Zambia with cervical cancer, the study site of CDH provided the largest possible cohort of women presenting for treatment, with patients from all 10 provinces [27]. Selection bias may have been introduced, as enrollment was conducted during clinical visits and limited to women who spoke one of the 4 most common languages in Zambia. Although adjustment for education was used to reduce confounding, education may not fully capture functional health literacy, access to health information, or broader structural disadvantage. Consequently, residual confounding related to unmeasured socioeconomic, behavioral, and health system factors, including referral delays, misdiagnosis, or health system navigation barriers, cannot be excluded. Finally, self-reported measures are subject to recall and reporting bias, which may have affected survey responses related to prior screening, cancer knowledge, and social needs.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of women with cervical cancer in Zambia, lack of knowledge about the risk and symptoms of cervical cancer was associated with higher odds of presenting with advanced-stage (FIGO stage III/IV) disease, after other SDOH domains were adjusted for. While these findings do not demonstrate that awareness directly altered health-seeking behavior or access to care, knowledge may act as a marker of broader educational or structural advantage.

To build on this and the prior work by Lombe et al., [17] future studies are needed to evaluate the interaction between system-level barriers such as misdiagnosis, poor referral coordination, limited infrastructure, and individual-level determinants. Population-based interventions aimed at improving knowledge should be evaluated in tandem with efforts to expand screening access and strengthen referral systems to meaningfully improve cervical cancer outcomes in Zambia.
