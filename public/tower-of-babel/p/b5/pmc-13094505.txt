Targeting nucleotide metabolism and epigenetic regulation to overcome the differentiation blockade in AML
Authors: Shinichiro Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDifferentiation blockade is a central pathogenic hallmark of acute myeloid leukemia (AML). While all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) achieves curative differentiation in acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), this success has not extended to other AML subtypes. Recently, inhibitors targeting nucleotide metabolism—such as cytarabine, dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) inhibitors, and DNA hypomethylating agents—have emerged as promising candidates to overcome this therapeutic barrier. These compounds promote myeloid maturation through mechanisms involving cell-cycle arrest, epigenetic reprogramming, and replication stress–activated signaling. Preclinical and early clinical evidence suggests that targeting nucleotide metabolism may induce partial differentiation of leukemic blasts, providing a metabolic and epigenetic avenue for therapy beyond APL. This mini-review summarizes current understanding of how metabolic inhibition restores differentiation in AML, focusing on representative agents and their mechanistic and translational implications. Targeting de novo nucleotide biosynthesis may offer a metabolic and epigenetic route to expand differentiation-based strategies beyond APL.Keywords: AML, Differentiation therapy, De novo nucleotide biosynthesis, DHODH inhibitors, Hypomethylating agents

Introduction

Differentiation therapy with all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA) revolutionized the treatment of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL), a subtype of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) defined by the PML–RARA fusion gene. ATRA induces terminal granulocytic maturation, effectively reversing the differentiation block that drives APL pathogenesis. This approach yields high cure rates and relatively few adverse effects compared with cytotoxic chemotherapy. However, similar success has not been achieved in other AML subtypes, in which cells remain refractory to ATRA, underscoring the need for alternative differentiation-inducing agents. Differentiation blockade is now recognized as a central biological hallmark of AML [1], reflecting the inability of leukemic progenitors to undergo terminal myeloid maturation despite preserved proliferative capacity. Beyond genetic mutations, accumulating evidence indicates that disrupted transcriptional programs, epigenetic dysregulation, and metabolic constraints collectively enforce this arrested state [2,3]. In particular, interactions between chromatin regulation, lineage-defining transcription factors, and cellular metabolism create a permissive environment that maintains leukemic self-renewal while suppressing differentiation [4].

Recent studies highlight inhibitors of nucleotide metabolism as potential tools to reprogram leukemic cells toward maturation [5,6]. Agents targeting de novo nucleotide synthesis—such as cytarabine (Ara-C), dihydroorotate dehydrogenase (DHODH) inhibitors, and hypomethylating agents (azacytidine, decitabine)—induce differentiation through distinct but convergent mechanisms. Perturbations in nucleotide availability or dNTP pool balance can induce replication stress and activate checkpoint signaling pathways that promote myeloid differentiation in AML models, supporting the concept that metabolic stress linked to nucleotide depletion may bridge proliferation control and differentiation induction [7,8]. This mini-review summarizes the current knowledge of how targeting nucleotide biosynthesis restores differentiation and discusses its translational implications for AML therapy. The expanding therapeutic landscape of AML—including targeted therapies such as FLT3 and IDH inhibitors and combination regimens incorporating hypomethylating agents—highlights the importance of understanding how metabolic interventions integrate with epigenetic and signaling pathways [9]. Placing nucleotide metabolism within this broader context may clarify how differentiation-oriented strategies can be rationally combined and translated into clinical benefit beyond APL. Given the rapid expansion of differentiation-oriented therapies, including targeted inhibitors and metabolic interventions [10], a conceptual synthesis focusing on nucleotide metabolism as a unifying mechanism is timely and may help guide rational combination strategies. Rather than providing a comprehensive catalog, this review aims to synthesize emerging evidence into a concept-driven framework centered on replication stress.

Role of targeted therapies in differentiation-oriented AML treatment

Recent advances in targeted therapy have further reinforced the concept that differentiation can be therapeutically restored in molecularly defined subsets of AML. Inhibitors of mutant IDH1 and IDH2 reduce the accumulation of the oncometabolite 2-hydroxyglutarate, leading to reversal of epigenetic repression and induction of myeloid maturation programs [11,12]. Likewise, FLT3 inhibitors not only suppress aberrant proliferative signaling but may also promote differentiation [13], particularly in combination with hypomethylating agents or venetoclax-based regimens [9]. Emerging therapeutic strategies, including menin inhibitors targeting KMT2A-rearranged or NPM1-mutated AML, further illustrate that modulation of transcriptional and epigenetic networks can release differentiation blockade [14]. Together, these developments underscore a broader paradigm in which metabolic, epigenetic, and signaling-directed therapies converge to reprogram leukemic cell fate. Within this evolving framework, targeting nucleotide metabolism may represent a complementary strategy to reinforce differentiation responses and enhance the durability of clinical benefit.

Cytidine analogs: cytarabine as a prototype of differentiation-linked chemotherapy

Here, cytarabine is discussed as a historical and mechanistic prototype illustrating how perturbation of nucleotide metabolism can influence differentiation. The 2022 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) recommendations recognize cytarabine as a core component of standard AML therapy [9]. As a nucleoside analog, it is phosphorylated to Ara-CTP and incorporated into DNA, causing chain termination and inhibition of DNA synthesis. At low doses, Ara-C promotes differentiation rather than apoptosis in AML models [15]. Wang et al [16] showed that low-dose Ara-C induces p21CIP1-dependent differentiation in U937 cells, whereas loss of p21CIP1 redirects cells to apoptosis, acting as a molecular switch. Tomic et al [17] demonstrated that Ara-C induces differentiation via ATR/Chk1 activation, similar to pyrimidine synthesis inhibitors such as AICAr and DHODH inhibitors, as discussed in the following section. These findings suggest that Ara-C–induced differentiation and pyrimidine depletion share a Chk1-dependent stress pathway. Cellular stress signals—such as oxidative and genotoxic stress—are important mediators of fate decisions in hematopoietic stem cells [18]. Replication stress caused by nucleotide depletion can similarly trigger differentiation programs (Fig. 1).

De novo nucleotide biosynthesis inhibitors: DHODH, IMPDH, and related targets

AML cells rely on rapid DNA synthesis for proliferation [19]. Inhibition of nucleotide biosynthesis disrupts replication and induces S-phase arrest [20] (Fig. 1). Behbehani et al. showed that chemotherapy sensitivity among AML subtypes correlates with differences in leukemia stem cell cycling, as Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3)-internal tandem duplication (ITD) AMLs, which are characterized by aberrant oncogenic signaling and poor prognosis [21], display reduced S-phase fractions and are unresponsive to chemotherapy [22]. 5,10-Dideazatetrahydrofolic acid (DDATHF), a purine synthesis inhibitor, induces HL-60 differentiation by depleting GTP; guanine nucleosides abolish this effect, while adenosine rescues proliferation only [23]. DHODH, a key enzyme in de novo pyrimidine biosynthesis, has emerged as a promising therapeutic target. DHODH inhibition by brequinar, leflunomide, or teriflunomide depletes pyrimidine pools, induces S-phase arrest, and promotes AML differentiation [24]. These findings highlight DHODH inhibition as a valuable strategy to exploit cancer-specific metabolic reprogramming and synthetic lethal vulnerabilities, particularly in combination therapies. Dembitz et al [8] confirmed that DHODH inhibitor brequinar and 4-carboxamide ribonucleoside (AICAr) had similar effects on differentiation markers and S-phase arrest. They found that these effects were induced by disrupting pyrimidine biosynthesis, leading to replication stress, Chk1 activation and subsequent engagement of the DNA-damage response pathway.

In the purine pathway, IMPDH inhibition (e.g., gnidilatimonoein, myricetin) causes GTP depletion–induced differentiation reversible by guanosine [[25], [26], [27]]. Likewise, 6-benzylthioinosine (6BT) promotes AML differentiation via ATP depletion and ATR/Chk1 activation, independent of p53 [28]. Together, these agents demonstrate that nucleotide depletion–induced replication stress acts as a universal differentiation trigger.

DNA hypomethylating agents: azacytidine and decitabine as epigenetic differentiation therapies

DNA methylation maintains hematopoietic stem cell identity, and hypermethylation silences tumor suppressors and differentiation factors such as C/EBPα [29]. DNA demethylating agents such as azacytidine and decitabine, both nucleoside analogs that inhibit DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) activity, have been developed to reverse these epigenetic abnormalities and are now established therapeutic agents for AML and myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) [30]. By inhibiting DNMT, these cytidine analogs cause global DNA hypomethylation, leading to reactivation of silenced tumor suppressor and differentiation-related genes such as PU.1, C/EBPα, and RUNX1 [31]. This hypomethylation results in reactivation of differentiation programs rather than direct cytotoxicity.

Azacytidine promotes myeloid differentiation in AML and high-risk MDS by demethylating the upstream regulatory element of PU.1, restoring its transcriptional activity and activating downstream targets essential for granulocytic and monocytic maturation [32]. These effects are enhanced by cytokines such as GM-CSF and IL-3, suggesting cooperation between epigenetic and signaling pathways [33,34]. Decitabine similarly upregulates CD11b, CD14, CD15 and transcription factors CEBPα and RUNX1 in AML lines [25]. At sub-cytotoxic concentrations, decitabine induces G0/G1 arrest and activation of transcription factors (CEBPα, RUNX1) without extensive apoptosis [26]. These observations support the concept that DNA hypomethylation, rather than direct cytotoxicity, is responsible for the differentiation-inducing effect of hypomethylating agents. Moreover, combination therapy with ATRA, HDAC inhibitors, or agents such as a MYB inhibitor (Celastrol) and anti-miR-155 (AM155) synergistically enhances PU.1 expression and differentiation, overcoming both epigenetic and post-transcriptional repression [35].

In clinical settings, azacytidine and decitabine are widely used for older AML patients and those with high-risk MDS. Although their efficacy has traditionally been attributed to tumor suppressor reactivation and apoptosis induction, increasing evidence supports an additional mechanism involving partial re-differentiation of leukemic blasts into more mature myeloid cells [36]. In patient samples, treatment with these agents has been associated with hematologic improvement and partial morphologic normalization of bone marrow, accompanied by transcriptional signatures consistent with myeloid differentiation [37]. These findings suggest that the clinical benefits of hypomethylating agents may, at least in part, derive from their differentiation-promoting activities.

Translational perspectives

Despite clear mechanistic evidence that nucleotide metabolism inhibitors can induce leukemic differentiation, their translation into consistent clinical benefit remains challenging. Clinical studies, summarized in Table 1, have evaluated hypomethylating agents (HMAs) such as azacytidine and decitabine alone or in combination with other agents. These studies demonstrate hematologic improvement and modest survival benefits, particularly in older AML and high-risk MDS patients [[38], [39], [40]]; however, complete remissions are relatively infrequent [41,42], suggesting that differentiation alone is insufficient for durable disease control. This highlights the need to better define which subsets of patients exhibit a differentiation-dominant response and how this can be therapeutically exploited.

Recent translational studies have begun to identify molecular predictors of differentiation responsiveness. Epigenetic signatures—such as global methylation patterns—although not sufficient to distinguish patients who will progress [43]—indicate that subsets of specific CpGs or altered DNA-methylation profiles at diagnosis may be useful as a biomarker for predicting azacytidine response and survival [43,44]. Metabolic features, including nucleotide-pool imbalance or DHODH dependency [7,45], have also emerged as potential biomarkers of response. Integrating these biomarkers with transcriptomic or metabolomic profiling could enable prospective identification of patients most likely to benefit from metabolic differentiation therapy.

Another important translational direction involves combination strategies designed to convert partial differentiation into durable remission. Clinical studies are exploring HMAs combined with venetoclax [46], FLT3 inhibitors [47], or immune checkpoint modulators [48], and several trials have demonstrated encouraging, though variable, clinical efficacy. Likewise, combining DHODH inhibitors with standard chemotherapy or targeted agents has shown promising preclinical activity [49]; however, current clinical studies are mostly in early phases (Phase I/Ib) (NCT04609826, NCT03404726, NCT05246384), focusing on safety, tolerability, and preliminary evidence of differentiation, with limited data yet available on durable remission. From a developmental standpoint, the major challenge is to balance cytostatic and differentiation-promoting effects while avoiding myelosuppression or metabolic toxicity. Optimal dosing schedules, identifying biomarkers of on-target differentiation, and dynamically monitoring myeloid maturation in patient samples are likely to be critical for the safe and effective clinical translation of these approaches. Replication stress may represent a unifying mechanism linking metabolic perturbation to differentiation induction across multiple therapeutic classes.

Conclusions

Collectively, these observations support the view that de novo nucleotide biosynthesis inhibitors, including hypomethylating agents, represent a promising yet underappreciated class of differentiation-inducing therapies in AML. Further studies are needed to define optimal combinations, biomarker-guided patient selection, and strategies to translate differentiation responses into durable clinical benefit. By integrating metabolic, epigenetic, and transcriptional control, such approaches may expand the reach of differentiation therapy beyond APL to a broader spectrum of AML subtypes. This conceptual perspective may provide a framework for integrating metabolic and epigenetic therapies in future clinical development.
