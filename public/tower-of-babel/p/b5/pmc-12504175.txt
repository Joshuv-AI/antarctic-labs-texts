Splicing factor Sf3b1 facilitates maintenance of neuronal dendrites by modulating mitochondrial health
Authors: Wei-Chia Tsao, Yi-Chun Huang, Hsin-Ho Sung, Chi-Hung Lin, Hwei-Jan Hsu, Hsiu-Fen Lin, Cheng-Ting Chien
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The intricate process of dendritic arborization is essential for forming functional neural circuits, and many of the underlying molecular and cellular mechanisms have been uncovered. However, how they are linked to regulate dendritic arborization in neurons remains further exploration. Through genetic screening, we identify the splicing factor Sf3b1 as functioning cell-autonomously in neuronal dendrite growth and maintenance. Our transcriptomic analysis links Sf3b1-regulated alternative splicing to modulation of metabolic pathways, and we assess altered splicing patterns for several mitochondria-related genes. Importantly, Sf3b1 knockdown in neurons results in dramatic mitochondrial fragmentation and specific reductions in mitochondrial counts and ATP levels in dendrites, revealing a pivotal role for Sf3b1 in modulating the energy supply necessary for dendritic arborization. Additionally, a genetic rescue experiment uncovered mitophagy-modulating molecules that effectively restored the mitochondrial health and dendritic arborization of Sf3b1-depeted neurons. Our study establishes a previously unrecognized connection between RNA splicing and mitochondrial demand in differentiating neurons, providing insights into bioenergetic requirements for dendritic growth and maintenance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05860-0.

Introduction

Dendritic morphogenesis involves growth and branching that leads to characteristic arborization patterns crucial for neuronal sensing and signal receipt. Many neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases have been linked to changes in dendrite morphology [1]. As an extension of cell bodies, dendrites share many cellular organelles, trafficking systems, and cytoskeletal structures with the cell body, but display certain modifications for their distinctive tubular branching patterns. For example, the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a continuous structure extending to the dendritic process while the Golgi complex and mitochondria are isolated entities in the process [2]. In neurons, extensive growth of the dendritic processes demands considerable resources from the cell body. The building of cytoskeletal structures, as well as transportation of organelles and vesicles, is highly active in growing and branching dendritic arbors [3]. Neurons also undergo nuclear RNA splicing to generate alternative mRNA species, thereby generating specialized protein isoforms catering to their intricate morphology and distinctive functional needs [4].

The spliceosome, a dynamic ribonucleoprotein complex, undergoes orderly stepwise rearrangements throughout the splicing process. It catalyzes conversion of pre-mRNA into mature mRNA, thereby playing an essential role in temporal and tissue-specific regulation of gene expression [5]. Although it is generally assumed that spliceosome dysfunction leads to ubiquitous splicing defects, individual components may be linked to certain pathological phenotypes [6, 7]. For example, inhibiting the function of U1 small nuclear ribonucleoproteins (U1 snRNPs) enhances levels of amyloid precursor proteins, linking U1 snRNP-mediated splicing dysfunction to Alzheimer’s disease pathogenesis [8]. An analysis of the RNA expression profiles in the brain of patients suffering Alzheimer’s disease revealed dysregulated RNA processing and accumulations of aberrantly spliced mRNAs [9]. Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)-associated mutations in the Fused in Sarcoma (FUS) protein can interact with U1 snRNA in the cytoplasm, leading to impaired snRNP biogenesis and neuronal toxicity [10]. Dipeptide repeats in C9ORF72 bind U2 snRNPs in motor neurons differentiated from patient-derived pluripotent stem cells, promoting retention of U2 snRNPs in the cytoplasm. U2-dependent exon splicing is significantly negatively affected in C9ORF72–mutated brain tissue [11]. Mutant U2 snRNA causes ataxia in mice, which display extensive neurodegeneration and aberrant RNA splicing in the cerebellum where the U2 snRNA is needed [12]. Although all of these studies link specific spliceosome components to neurodegenerative disease, how spliceosomal components influence processes of cell differentiation such as dendritic arborization is still unclear.

Splicing factor 3b Subunit 1 (SF3b1) is an essential component of the SF3b complex in U2 snRNPs, with these latter being critical for spliceosome assembly and playing a key role in identifying the intronic branch point adenosine and selecting the 3’ acceptor sequences [13]. In adult mouse brains, Sf3b1 expression is elevated in the subventricular zone of the lateral ventricle wall, the neurogenic site for adult stem cells, emphasizing its significant role in neurogenesis [14]. Notably, the sf3b1b460 zebrafish mutant displays specific developmental defects during embryogenesis, with reduced gene activity of this hypomorphic allele affecting pre-mRNA processing of transcription factor genes and disrupting the formation of neural crest-derived pigment cells [15]. Human SF3B1 regulates the pattern of alternative mRNA splicing events. For instance, in SF3B1 knockdown cells, alternatively spliced variants were generated that included a diminished level of the exon 7-included mRNA of survival of motor neuron 2 (SMN2). Inclusion of exon 7 is essential to generate functional SMN proteins, and lack of it causes spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) [16].

Several studies have provided evidence for SF3b1 being involved in neurite formation or maintenance. For instance, the splicing factor RBM17 forms a protein complex with U2SURP and CHERP that interacts with SF3b1-hosting U2 snRNPs to modulate alternative splicing. RBM17 is vital for maintenance and survival of Purkinje cells, with loss of RBM17 gene activity in Purkinje cells leading to ataxia and neurodegeneration [17]. Polyglutamine-binding protein 1 (PQBP1) affects the association of Sf3b1 with certain mRNAs and alters their splicing patterns, causing defects in neuronal projections and the dendritic outgrowth of mouse neurons [18]. Additionally, SR-related protein of 100 kDa (nSR100/Srrm4) interacts with various proteins, including Sf3b1, to regulate exon inclusion, thereby impacting neurogenesis and neuritogenesis [19, 20]. Collectively, these studies support a role for Sf3b1 in splicing regulation, which is crucial for neurite development and neuronal function. However, it remains unclear how Sf3b1 impacts neurite growth and maintenance by regulating the splicing of mRNA targets.

At the cellular level, it has been suggested that Sf3b1 regulates mitochondrial energy metabolism. Notably, heterozygous somatic mutations in Sf3B1 have been linked to the occurrence of ring sideroblasts (RS) in bone marrow. RS are characterized by dysfunctional iron-rich mitochondria that encircle the nucleus, serving as an indicator of abnormal iron metabolism in myelodysplastic syndromes [21]. The Sf3B1 mutation severely impacts RNA splicing of genes central to iron homeostasis, heme synthesis, and mitochondrial iron transport, directly contributing to the abnormal mitochondrial iron accumulation observed in MDS-RS [22]. A metabolic shift from mitochondrial to fructose metabolism in the hypoxic heart requires an SF3B1-mediated switch of ketohexokinase isoforms. The depletion of SF3B1 impairs this adaptive mechanism, leading to pathological cardiac hypertrophy [23]. Homozygous Sf3b1 mutations in Caenorhabditis elegans downregulate transcripts related to mitochondrial function and reduced cellular energy supply, resulting in developmental arrest, whereas transcripts involved in mRNA processing are upregulated as a compensatory response to disrupted splicing [24].

Both the development and maturation of neurites are regulated metabolically and require an energy supply, necessitating the localization of mitochondria in the dendritic processes [25, 26]. Dendritic branching in hippocampal neurons is adversely affected in mice in which cytoplasmic polyadenylation element binding protein 1 (CPEB1) has been knocked out due to mitochondrial dysfunction and decreased ATP levels. CPEB1 regulates mitochondrial ATP production by controlling mRNA translation of NDUFV2, a key component of electron transport chain complex I [27]. In developing mouse Purkinje cells, mitochondrial localization within dendrites is essential for dendritic growth. Elevating ATP levels by activating ATP-phosphocreatine exchange via creatine kinase offsets reduced mitochondrial availability, thereby supporting dendritic growth [28]. In addition, the importance of splicing factors for neuronal energy dynamics is becoming increasingly clear. RNA profiling of the brains of patients suffering multiple sclerosis revealed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) responsible for RNA metabolism, neuronal maintenance, axonal health, and mitochondrial function. More than 80% of those differentially expressed transcripts were regulated by hnRNP A1, dysfunction of which also promotes altered splicing and reduced neurite length, indicating that neurodegeneration can be caused by accumulations of altered mRNA species over time [29]. Similar to Sf3b1, mutations in LUC7L2 have been associated with MDS prognoses. LUC7L2 is crucial in balancing glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) by regulating mRNA splicing of the glycolytic enzyme PFKM and the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11, highlighting a link between RNA processing and energy homeostasis [30, 31].

Here, we establish both genetic and functional links between Sf3b1 and mitochondrial dynamics in class IV dendritic arborization (C4da) neurons. We employed Mosaic Analysis with a Repressible Cell Marker (MARCM) clones [32] to screen for molecules required in C4da neuronal morphogenesis, allowing us to identify Sf3b1 as a cell-autonomous regulator in this process. Sf3b1 is vital at the later stage of larval development, i.e., when dendrites are scaling their growth, indicating a critical demand for energy to support dynamic dendrite growth. The pathways revealed by our transcriptomic analysis support the observation that Sf3b1 plays a significant role in neuronal metabolism during dendritic growth. Knockdown of Sf3b1 resulted in a reduction in the population of mitochondria localized in dendritic processes, thereby reducing energy levels. Remarkably, we found that reintroducing mitophagy-related molecules into the Sf3b1 knockdown neurons improved mitochondrial health, rejuvenated energy homeostasis, and restored dendritic arborization.

Results

Sf3b1 cell-autonomously regulates dendrite morphogenesis of C3da and C4da neurons

In a forward screen of MARCM clones to identify mutants of glycosylation pathways in which the dendritic morphology of ddaC neurons are defective, we observed that the lectin-24DbMI11191 line displayed a strong growth defect (Fig. S1A, middle). Attempts to rescue that dendritic defect by expressing lectin-24Db in the lectin-24DbMI11191 MARCM clones did not restore normal dendritic morphology (Fig. S1A, right). Moreover, the dendrites of two lectin-24DbRNAi lines driven by ppk-GAL4 revealed no morphological defects (Fig. S1B). Furthermore, transheterozygous mutants generated by crossing the lectin-24DbMI1119 allele to the Df(2L)BSC165 deficiency line also revealed that the lectin-24Db locus did not elicit morphological defects in dendrites (Fig. S1C). Therefore, we concluded that the lectin-24DbMI11191 allele is unlikely to be responsible for the morphological defects of dendrites observed in the lectin-24DbMI11191 MARCM clone.

To identify the mutation in the chromosome of lectin-24DbMI11191 that causes the observed dendritic defects, we performed complementation lethality tests using DrosDel deficiency stocks [33] covering various segments of the Drosophila 2L chromosome. In this screen, we identified three DrosDel lines—Df(2L)ED94, Df(2L)ED49, and Df(2L)ED62—that failed to complement lectin-24DbMI11191 and corresponded to a deleted region encompassing seven annotated genes (Fig. 1A and Fig. S1D). Therefore, we employed ppk-GAL4 to drive RNAi lines targeting each gene individually and detected that only Sf3b1 (ppk > Sf3b1RNAi) knockdown impaired the morphology of dendritic arbors, thus phenocopying the lectin-24DbMI11191 phenotype (Fig. 2A). The effectiveness of Sf3b1RNAi in depleting Sf3b1 mRNA expression was confirmed by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) (Fig. S1E). Sequencing of the Sf3b1 genomic region in lectin-24DbMI11191 uncovered a 3.3 Kb deletion that removed most of the Sf3b1 coding region, indicating that lectin-24DbMI11191 is a null allele (Fig. 1A). Sf3b1mt MARCM clones displayed strikingly defective dendritic arborization patterns of ddaC neurons, with a reduction in their terminal branches (Fig. 1B and 1C). MARCM clones for class III ddaF neurons also presented lower numbers of spike-like protrusions (Fig. 1G and 1H). In contrast, class I (ddaD and ddaE) and class II (ddaB) da neurons of the Sf3b1mt MARCM clones appeared normal (Fig. S2A). Full restoration of dendritic arbors in both C4da (Fig. 1D and 1F) and C3da (Fig. 1I and 1J) neurons was achieved upon expression of the UAS-Sf3b1 transgene, evidencing a cell-autonomous role for Sf3b1. Moreover, expression of human SF3B1 in the Sf3b1mt MARCM clones compensated for the dendritic defects (Fig. 1E and 1F), supporting functional conservation between the human and Drosophila homologs in shaping dendritic morphology.

Sf3b1 is required for late-stage dendritic arborization

C4da dendrites undergo a dynamic developmental process. Drosophila dendritic branches fill their arborization fields within the hemisegmental space at 72 h after egg laying (AEL), and continue refining their arbors through active branch addition and removal while scaling their growth to match increasing body size [34, 35]. Therefore, we examined the dendritic morphology of Sf3b1RNAi neurons at 72, 90, and 120 h AEL to understand the impact of Sf3b1 knockdown on dendritic development. Normal dendritic arbors were observed at 72 and 90 h AEL in the Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons (Fig. 2, S2C and S2D). However, severely reduced branching was observed at 120 h AEL, with much fewer branches than at either 72 or 90 h AEL (Fig. 2A and 2B). The Sf3b1mt MARCM clones show a decrease in dendrite terminal numbers at 90 h AEL but not at 72 h AEL (Fig. S2E and S2F). Thus, the Sf3b1RNAi knockdown is effective at the stage of 120 h AEL. To investigate how Sf3b1RNAi affects dendritic branching, we monitored terminal branch dynamics over a 3-h period, starting from 105 to 108 h AEL (Fig. 2C). We classified changes in terminal branches as either “emerge”, “vanish”, “extend”, or “retract” (see definitions in Material & Methods). We detected significant decreases among Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons in populations of both the “emerge” and “extend” categories, whereas those of the “vanish” and “retract” did not differ from the ppk-GAL4 control (Fig. 2D). At 72 and 90 h AEL, terminal branch dynamics were unaltered by Sf3b1 knockdown, apart from a notable decline in the “extend” category at 90 h AEL (Fig. S3). These results indicate that the lack of Sf3b1 activity in C4da neurons causes dendritic branching defects mainly in emanating new branches and elongating existing ones, which could contribute to the dramatic reduction in dendritic branches in Sf3b1RNAi neurons at 120 h AEL.

Sf3b1 is crucial for RNA splicing and expression levels

To gain a comprehensive insight into the function of Sf3b1, we performed RNA sequencing on Sf3b1RNAi knockdown samples driven by the ubiquitous driver da-GAL4, using a da-GAL4 driver control group for comparative purposes (Fig. 3A). First, we employed replicate Multivariate Analysis of Transcript Splicing (rMATS) [36] to detect differential expression of alternatively spliced mRNAs between the two groups. Our analysis revealed 2228 significant alternative splicing events, which were distributed across 1118 genes. Our rMATS analysis uncovered 761 skipped-exon (SE) events, representing the most frequent alternative splicing event category, followed by 443 retained introns (RI), 412 mutually exclusive exons (MXE), 308 alternative 3’ splicing sites (A3SS), and 304 alternative 5’ splicing sites (A5SS) (Fig. 3B). Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the respective genes indicated that Sf3b1RNAi knockdown affects terms related to cell junctions and synapses. To gain more insight into altered intron splicing events after the Sf3b1RNAi knockdown, we utilized Compact Idiosyncratic Gapped Alignment Report (CIGAR) strings and aligned the reads to the reference genome of Drosophila melanogaster. The intron splicing variations were classified as intron retention or exon extension at 5’ or 3’ spicing sites, which revealed that Sf3b1RNAi knockdown induces splicing variations in 185 genes. GO analysis of those genes uncovered terms relating to neuronal projection and morphogenesis (Fig. 3C). To better understand the specific splicing variations, we selected some representative genes for analysis by RT-PCR. To examine SE events, we chose Optic atrophy 1 (Opa1) and Sf3b3 (Fig. 4A), with this latter encoding two transcripts, Sf3b3-RA and Sf3b3-RC. Only Sf3b3-RC includes exon 6, which introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in a truncated protein of 469 amino acids (aa), whereas exon 6 is skipped in Sf3b3-RA, generating a full-length protein of 1,227 aa [37]. RT-PCR revealed that Sf3b1RNAi knockdown elicited more exon 6 skipping events. Opa1 encodes five annotated transcripts, with exon 4 being present in Opa1-RA and Opa1-RC. Again, RT-PCR showed that Sf3b1RNAi knockdown promoted exon 4 skipping events. We examined TFAM and preli-like (prel) as examples of RI events (Fig. 4B). Two TFAM transcripts, TFAM-RA and TFAM-RB, have been annotated to date, with TFAM-RB retaining the intron between exons 1 and 2, resulting in a 27-aa insertion in its mitochondrial targeting sequence [38]. Knockdown of Sf3b1 increased signal of the larger transcript, confirming TFAM-RB upregulation. Similarly, we observed a greater frequency of intron retention events for prel transcripts upon Sf3b1RNAi knockdown, as confirmed by RT-PCR. We examined A5SS events in like-AP180 (lap) and Glutathione peroxidase homolog with thioredoxin peroxidase activity (Gtpx) (Fig. 4C). The lap-RG and lap-RI transcripts have a longer exon 10 than the lap-RA, lap-RC, lap-RD, lap-RE, and lap-RF transcripts, with Sf3b1RNAi knockdown upregulating transcripts containing the longer exon. The Gtpx-RA isoform has a short exon 1, whereas exon 1 of Gtpx-RC is longer as it includes an additional start codon. We observed that Sf3b1RNAi knockdown enhanced the signal of the larger Gtpx-RC transcript relative to control. Finally, for A3SS events, we analyzed hulk (hlk) and misshapen (msn) (Fig. 4D). Sf3b1RNAi knockdown upregulated the hlk-RG transcript that contains a longer final exon than its hlk-RA, hlk-RL, and hlk-RM counterparts, resulting in an alternative reading frame that generates a protein with a distinct C-terminus. The msn-RF, msn-RG, msn-RC, and msn-RD transcripts have a shorter exon 13, and their levels were reduced by Sf3b1RNAi knockdown. Genes associated with mitochondrial function are downregulated by Sf3b1RNAi knockdown

The mRNAs displaying altered splicing patterns in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons may exhibit differential stabilities, altered expression levels, or further influence the expression of downstream genes. Following Sf3b1RNAi knockdown, 321 upregulated and 408 downregulated DEGs were identified (Fig. 5A-C). Among them, 40 of the downregulated and 34 of the upregulated DEGs overlap with the 1,258 genes we identified from our rMATS and intron splicing variant analyses (Fig. 3), indicating that splicing alterations impact transcript levels of only a small proportion of the genes. Interestingly, GO analysis of the upregulated DEGs revealed that they are involved in DNA damage and repair pathways (Fig. 5A), whereas the downregulated DEGs are associated with mitochondria and metabolic homeostasis (Fig. 5B). Notably, these mitochondria-linked genes, indicated as darker dots in the volcano plot (Fig. 5C) and listed in Fig. S4A, are predominantly found in the downregulated category, emphasizing their significant suppression. We adopted the gene set enrichment analysis tool PANGEA [39] to examine these downregulated mitochondria-linked genes in terms of related pathways and networks, which revealed gene clusters encoding mitochondrial, ribosomal, and oxidative-reduction processes, as well as components of the TCA cycle and respiratory electron transport (Fig. 5D). We also overlapped these DEGs with genes involved in mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy (Fig. S4B). Interestingly, almost all DEG genes in mitochondria biogenesis (31/32) were downregulated. Sf3b1 is required for normal mitochondrial morphology and maintenance of ATP levels

To further elucidate the defects in Sf3b1 mutant neurons, we leveraged a series of cellular markers to examine their cytoskeletal structures and organelles. Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons displayed normal distribution patterns, i.e., indistinguishable from control neurons, of microtubules (labeled by Jupiter-RFP) and actin (labeled by Lifeact) (Fig. S5A and S5B). Moreover, patterns of YFP-Rab5 (to label endosomes) and Mannosidase II (ManII) (to label Golgi complex) signal were equivalent between Sf3b1RNAi neurons and controls (Fig. S5C, S5D). In contrast, we detected differences in ER morphology (labeled by KDEL-GFP) and mitochondria (labeled by Dendra2) between Sf3b1 knockdown and control neurons. ER membranes can adopt different morphologies, primarily interconnected tubules or membranous sheets, with structural shifts between these forms playing a crucial role in regulating metabolism [40]. We found that sheets made up 12.32% of the ER of control neurons, whereas they accounted for significantly more (18.04%) in Sf3b1RNAi neurons (Fig. S5E).

By labeling mitochondria with Dendra2, we observed severe fragmentation of mitochondria following Sf3b1RNAi knockdown. This is in contrast to the tubular structure of mitochondria appeared predominantly in control neurons (Fig. 6A). Fragmented mitochondria are typically associated with dysfunction like impaired ATP production or with stress increases. However, mitochondrial fragmentation, in the context of dynamic fission and fusion, also plays essential physiological roles in maintaining mitochondrial quality control, enhancing metabolic flexibility, and allowing rapid adaptation to cellular stress by segregating damaged mitochondria for selective degradation [41]. We then quantified the length and the area of mitochondria and showed considerable reductions in both parameters in the knockdown neurons relative to controls (Fig. 6B). Since Sf3b1RNAi knockdown caused a significant reduction in neuronal dendrites (Fig. 2A and 2B), we examined mitochondria in the dendritic processes of those neurons. We detected a punctate pattern of Dendra2-labeled mitochondria throughout all arbors of wild-type dendrites. However, these punctate signals, reflective of mitochondrial density, were greatly reduced in the Sf3b1RNAi knockdown dendrites (Fig. 6C, S6A and S6B). Consistent with the dendritic growth defect only being detected at 120 h AEL in knockdown neurons (Fig. 2A and 2B), mitochondrial density was also reduced in those neurons at 120 h AEL (Fig. 6D). Widely spaced mitochondria in dendrites provide the major energy supply that supports dendritic growth [42]. We first validated the iATPSnFR1.0 probe for assessing ATP levels [43] in C4da neurons. By comparing control and Sf3b1RNAi neurons, the iATPSnFR1.0:mRuby3 ratio was significantly reduced by Sf3b1RNAi knockdown (Fig. 6E, 6F and S6C). With treatment of 1 mM Antimycin A that inhibits mitochondrial electron transfer, both iATPSnFR1.0:mRuby3 ratios in control and Sf3b1RNAi neurons were continuously reduced, and the differences between them were gradually narrowed, showing statistical significance at 5 and 10 min, and no significance at 20 and 30 min after treatment. This analysis, consistent with a previous report that oxidative phosphorylation is the major source to produce ATP in C4da neurons [44]. Knockdown of Sf3b1 partially compromises the ATP level, but the remaining level could be further reduced by inhibiting the electron transfer chain, suggesting that oxidative phosphorylation is still a prominent pathway to generate ATP in Sf3b1RNAi neurons. We then examined reactive oxygen species (ROS) produced through oxidative phosphorylation by expressing the ROS sensor transgene mito-roGFP2 in C4da neurons [45]. Indeed, Sf3b1RNAi knockdown produced higher levels of ROS than in control neurons (Fig. S6D). We then measured mRuby3 and iATPSnFR1.0 fluorescence intensities within the dendritic processes at 72, 90, and 120 h AEL (Fig. 6G). The mean mRuby3 signal intensities were comparable between control and Sf3b1RNAi neurons at all three timepoints, but mean iATPSnFR1.0 signal intensities were significantly reduced in Sf3b1RNAi neurons only at 120 h AEL, resulting in a significant reduction in the iATPSnFR1.0:mRuby3 ratio (Fig. 6H, S6E). Overexpression of AMPKαCA, Pink1, or park restores dendritic arborization and mitochondrial density in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons

Neurons primarily rely on oxidative phosphorylation by mitochondria for ATP production due to limited glycolysis. To meet the high energy demand of neurons, mitochondrial content and quality must be finely tuned. Consequently, we assessed molecules regulating mitochondrial function and promoting neurite outgrowth to determine if they can counteract the reduction in dendrite terminals caused by Sf3b1 dysfunction. Specifically, we examined the following genes: Buffy that encodes a Bcl-2 family protein [46]; Lim3 that encodes a homeobox protein regulating redox processes, ATP metabolism, and mitochondrial function [47, 48]; PPP1R15, a phosphatase regulatory subunit, promotes the dephosphorylation of eIF2α, alleviating translational repression under stress conditions [44]; mitochondrial assembly regulatory factor (Marf) encoding a dynamin-family GTPase functioning in mitochondrial fusion [49, 50]; nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferase (Nmnat) that chaperones mitochondria in maintaining C4da dendritic arbors [51, 52]; AMPKα encoding the α Subunit of the 5'adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase complex [53, 54]; and PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (Pink1) and parkin (park) that encode E3 ubiquitin ligases [55, 56]. These latter two genes function in the signaling pathway that promotes mitophagy [57].

Overexpression of each of these genes individually in control neurons did not affect dendritic morphology or terminal branch numbers (Fig. S7A and S7B). Next, we investigated if the observed increase in mitochondrial density in dendrites was related to changes in somatic mitochondria. Overexpression of AMPKαCA or park in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons had no significant effect on mitochondrial length or size when compared to controls (Fig. S8C and S8D). Pink1 overexpression reduced mitochondrial length in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons, as also observed for Pink1 overexpression in control neurons. However, surprisingly, Nmnat overexpression resulted in notable aggregation of mitochondria in the cell bodies of control neurons, which was not assessed in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons (Fig. S8A). Thus, the increase in mitochondrial density in dendrites of Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons and restoration of their dendritic growth by AMPKα (AMPKαT184D), Pink1, park, or Nmnat is unrelated to mitochondrial morphology in the cell body.

The AMPK-Pink1/parkin mitophagy axis enhances mitochondrial turnover in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons

AMPKα, Pink1, and park are key factors involved in mediating mitophagy, ensuring a healthy turnover of mitochondria is sustained within the cell [58–60]. To assess if normal mitochondrial turnover is affected by Sf3b1RNAi knockdown, we employed MitoTimer, a mitochondria-localized DsRed mutant protein that shifts fluorescence from green to red over time [61]. By comparing control and Sf3b1RNAi neurons, we found that Sf3b1 knockdown led to a higher red:green ratio in terms of MitoTimer expression, which is indicative of a relative increase in aged mitochondria (Fig. 8A). This increase was detected in both cell bodies and dendritic processes, albeit it was more pronounced in the former (Fig. 8B). When we examined the individual red and green fluorescence signal intensities, the Sf3b1 knockdown neurons presented increases in both channels, indicative of impaired mitochondrial degradation [62] (Fig. S9). We also observed solid red punctate signals representing damaged mitochondria awaiting removal, levels of which were increased in the Sf3b1RNAi neurons (Fig. 8C). Based on these observations, we propose that while Sf3b1 is required for the normal morphology and distribution of mitochondria in neurons (Fig. 6), it plays a positive role in regulating mitophagy. Importantly, overexpressing AMPKαCA, Pink1, or park in the Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons suppressed the MitoTimer red:green ratio in both their cell bodies and dendrites, though the change in dendrites induced by AMPKαCA overexpression was not significant (Fig. 8A and 8B). These results indicate that enhanced mitophagy in Sf3b1RNAi knockdown neurons is insufficient to remove defective mitochondria. Discussion

Dendritic morphogenesis, which is governed by a diverse range of molecular pathways, is a crucial step in neuronal development, leading to the establishment of neuronal connectivity and function. Herein, we have elucidated a role for the core spliceosome component Sf3b1 in neurite growth, particularly for neurons that possess complex dendritic arbors and that exhibit dynamic terminal branching. By knockdown Sf3b1 in C4da neurons, the regular cytoskeletal structure of dendrites, composed of actin filaments and microtubules, as well as their complement of organelles such as endosomes and Golgi complexes, remain intact. Thus, a reduction of Sf3b1 splicing activity does not lead to catastrophic neuronal damage, but instead induces specific alterations Such as a bias toward sheet-like versus tubular ER structures and pronounced fragmentation of mitochondrial tubular connections. We further showed a temporal correlation of mitochondria reduction and ATP level reduction in dendrites and branch reduction at 120 h AEL by Sf3b1RNAi knockdown. Finally, we have demonstrated herein that promoting mitophagy can partially reverse the effects of reduced mitochondrial density, thereby restoring the dendritic branches of Sf3b1 RNAi knockdown neurons. Thus, we have established a critical link between mRNA splicing and mitochondrial integrity during the energy-demanding task of dendritic branching in postmitotic neurons.

Our RT-PCR analysis of several alternative splicing events supports that Sf3b1 knockdown may significantly impact mitochondrial function, autophagy, and neuronal signaling. One notable finding concerns Opa1, a protein essential for the integrity of mitochondrial cristae. Upon Sf3b1 knockdown, transcripts that included exon 4 of Opa1 were greatly reduced relative to control (Fig. 4A). Opa1 exon 4 encodes a peptide of 39 aa located between a dynamin domain and a predicted N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), and it includes several proteolytic cleavage sites crucial for regulatory modifications [63–65]. TFAM is critical for mtDNA transcription and replication. In Sf3b1 knockdown neurons, intron retention to generate the TFAM-RB transcript results in a 27-aa insertion within its MTS [38], potentially affecting its targeting to mitochondria. Sf3b1 knockdown also increased the retention of a non-canonical intron in the 5'untranslated region (UTR) of prel, which encodes a mitochondrial protein that maintains mitochondrial structural integrity and the ATP level, as well as dendritic growth of C4da neurons [44, 46]. Thus, the retained intron in the 5'UTR might impact protein translation. The various Gtpx transcripts encode proteins of distinct subcellular localizations, with Gtpx-PA predicted to be cytosolic and Gtpx-PD likely targeting mitochondria, with both transcripts potentially suppressing ferroptosis [66, 67]. Sf3b1 knockdown reduced levels of Gtpx-RA, whereas Gtpx-RD remained unchanged, which might indirectly affect mitochondrial function. We also detected changes in mRNA splicing of Sf3b3, another component of the SF3b complex. Recent studies have linked Sf3b3 to autophagy regulation in cancer cells [68]. The altered splicing of Sf3b3 transcripts following Sf3b1 knockdown suggests a potential mechanism by which Sf3b3 may influence neuronal autophagy. These altered splicing events may affect different aspects of mitochondrial integrity and function, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and disintegration in neurons.

While the significance of RNA splicing in axonal dynamics has been highlighted previously [69], its role in dendritic development is equally critical and warrants further attention. Our transcript profiling of splicing variants identified genes involved in neuronal projection and morphogenesis (Fig. 3C). Morphological analysis shows that Sf3b1 knockdown in C4da neurons caused reductions in emanations and extensions of their branches at 105 h AEL (Fig. 2C and 2D), which could contribute to the oversimplification of their dendritic arbors, although branch retraction and elimination might also contribute to arbor simplification at later stages Such as 120 h AEL or in MARCM mutant clones with complete depletion in MARCM mutant clones (Fig. S2E and S2F). Alternative splicing enables diverse isoforms to be generated from a single gene, allowing neurons to fine-tune their dendritic structures in response to developmental signals and environmental changes. For example, MEC-3, a LIM homeodomain transcription factor in C. elegans, is modulated through alternative splicing, with deletion of the long isoform of MEC-3 specifically reducing dendritic complexity [70]. The isoform shift from pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) to PKM1 mediated by RNA-binding motif protein 4 (RBM4) enhances mitochondrial respiration, thereby promoting neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth [71]. Furthermore, the microtubule-severing protein katanin p60-like 1 (kat-60L1) exists in both short and long isoforms, which are differentially expressed during development and play a crucial role in regulating the dendritic morphology of C4da neurons [72]. Moreover, the cell adhesion molecule Neuroglian (Nrg) is present in two isoforms having distinct intracellular domains due to alternative splicing. Nrg180 is expressed exclusively in neurons, whereas Nrg167 is only present in the epithelial cells that contact them. Disrupting splicing patterns is known to cause aberrant dendrite aggregation [73], highlighting the critical role of regulated RNA splicing in dendrite arborization. During the development of dendritic arbors, alternative splicing can generate thousands of different isoforms of Down syndrome cell adhesion molecule 1 (Dscam1). Dendrites that emanate from the same neuron and that express the same set of Dscam1 isoforms avoid each other, thereby directing an appropriate spread of dendrites across the innervation field [74].

Neurons maintain constant levels of ATP, as shown by the constant iATPSnFR1.0:mRuby3 ratios (Fig. 6H and S6E). Significant declines of the integrated intensity (Fig. S6E) and the iATPSnFR1.0:mRuby3 ratio (Fig. 6H) in Sf3b1RNAi neurons were detected at 120 h AEL, reflecting a breakdown of energy homeostasis. This timing correlates with the dendritic branching defect (Fig. 2B) and the reduction of dendritic mitochondria numbers (Fig. 6D) in Sf3b1RNAi neurons, suggesting a correlative relationship among these phenotypes. In analysis of RNA sequence data, Sf3b1RNAi downregulates gene expressions in many mitochondrial pathways involved ATP production (Fig. 5D), providing a link to the downregulation of the ATP level in Sf3b1RNAi neurons. We also analyzed the altered splicing patterns of four genes highly related to mitochondrial functions and integrity (Fig. 4A and 4B). Thus, our and other studies strongly support the critical role of mitochondria in maintaining local ATP pools in dendrites [42, 75–77]. It is noted that mitochondrial defects in neurons might induce dendritic growth defect through other pathways. As an example, Prel is required for maintaining mitochondrial structural integrity and the ATP level, as well as dendritic growth of C4da neurons [46]. Overexpression or lack of Prel causes dendritic defects by altered protein translation activity, and the dendritic defect could be alleviated by overexpression of PPP1R15, a protein involved in the regulation of protein synthesis and stress response [44]. However, PPP1R15 overexpression failed to rescue dendritic induced by Sf3b1 knockdown (Fig. 7A and 7B). Application of acute stresses to cultured neurons induces rod-like actin structure formation, which was suggested to mediate the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP in neurites. However, the pattern of actin distribution is largely normal in dendrites of Sf3b1RNAi neurons (Fig. S5B). Thus, our study suggests that depletion of Sf3b1 expression impacts mRNA splicing and levels of genes that are crucial in multiple mitochondrial pathways (Fig. 5).

We observed an accumulation of aged mitochondria with Sf3b1 knockdown. Overexpression of AMPKαCA, Pink1, or park significantly reduced the overall MitoTimer red:green ratio, indicating enhanced mitochondrial quality control in the Sf3b1RNAi neurons (Fig. 8A and 8B). However, the number of pure red puncta remained high (Fig. 8C), representing severely damaged mitochondria that could not be degraded with enhanced mitophagy. The fact that Sf3b1 knockdown compromises multiple mitochondrial pathways (Fig. 5D), making full rescue challenging. These pathways include TCA cycle, electron transport chain, and oxidative phosphorylation. Consistently, we found that the level of ROS that could be produced through oxidative phosphorylation was also increased in Sf3b1 knockdown neurons (Fig. S6D). Elevated levels of ROS could further damage mitochondria that requires efficient removal through mitophagy. However, increased mitochondrial damage without a corresponding increase in mitochondrial clearance (pure red puncta) complicates full restoration of mitochondrial homeostasis [78]. Chronic exercise-induced mitophagy substantially enhanced mitochondrial structure and function in the Drosophila heart muscle, despite some residual red puncta that could not be completely removed [79]. This observation aligns closely with our findings, underscoring that enhancement of mitophagy can indeed yield meaningful phenotypic improvements in dendritic arborization, even when complete mitochondrial clearance is not fully achieved.

Specifically, overexpression of Pink1 or park reduced the accumulations of aged or damaged mitochondria in both the soma and dendrites (Fig. 8), consistent with their well-established roles in mitophagy within neuronal cell bodies [80, 81] and their involvement in local axonal mitophagy [82]. Pink1 and park-encoded Parkin mediate localized clearance of axonal mitochondria, effectively preventing the spread of oxidative damage throughout a neuron during transport. Additionally, Pink1 mRNA is associated with mitochondria through synaptojanin 2, allowing it to travel to distal processes and undergo local translation, thereby enabling Pink1- and Parkin-mediated distal mitophagy [83]. Beyond enhancing mitophagy, Pink1 also promotes mitochondrial biogenesis by phosphorylating parkin interacting substrate (PARIS), which subsequently leads to its Parkin-mediated ubiquitination and degradation. This process alleviates PGC-1α inhibition, thereby stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis [84]. Moreover, Pink1 and Parkin have been shown to facilitate localized translation of mRNAs encoding nuclear-encoded respiratory chain complexes in neurites [85]. This effect on biogenesis could contribute to the increased mitochondrial density observed along dendrites when Pink1 or park was overexpressed in Sf3b1 RNAi knockdown neurons (Fig. 7C).

Recent research on myelodysplastic syndromes (MDS) has also consistently revealed a potential role for Sf3b1 in regulating mitophagy. The mutations of SF3B1 observed in MDS patients have been linked to altered expression of several autophagy-related genes [22]. Similarly, mutations in U2AF1, another core spliceosome component linked to MDS, disrupt autophagy and promote malignant transformation [86]. Furthermore, mitophagy is essential for maintaining dendritic structure and functionality, as demonstrated by a study indicating that deficiency in the mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase Mul1 leads to reduced dendritic arborization [87]. Further supporting this link, a recent study identified GPR50 as a neuronal mitophagy receptor essential for maturation. Loss of GPR50 results in the accumulation of aged, dysfunctional mitochondria, accompanied by elevated mitochondrial ROS, reduced ATP levels, and simplified dendritic branching [88]. Altogether, these findings reinforce the idea that disruptions in mitophagy, which are associated with abnormal protein accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction, have been linked to various neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative diseases [89].

Fly strains and transgenic lines

Fly stocks and crosses were maintained at 25 °C on standard cornmeal medium. The human SF3B1 plasmid was obtained from Addgene. Following gel extraction and purification, the amplified products were cloned into the pENTR™/D-TOPO™ entry vector, followed by recombination into the pTGW Gateway™ vector with LR Clonase II enzyme mix. The open reading frame of Drosophila Sf3b1 was amplified from w1118 using primers containing NotI and KpnI restriction sites. The amplified product was subcloned into the pUAST-attB vector using the same restriction enzymes. These constructs were confirmed by sequencing before being injected into w1118 Drosophila embryos. To generate the UASp-mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0 line, the cyto-iATPSnFR1.0 plasmid was obtained from Addgene. The iATPSnFR1.0 sensor was amplified with primers bearing Not1 and BamH1 restriction enzyme sites and subcloned into the UASp-attB vector to create the UASp-iATPSnFR1.0 construct. The Ruby3 fragment was amplified from pKanCMV-mClover3-mRuby3 using primers containing a NotI site. The fragment was then inserted upstream of iATPSnFR1.0 in the UASp-iATPSnFR1.0 construct to generate the UASp-mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0 construct. The final plasmid was injected into embryos carrying attP40 on chromosome 2 and attP2 on chromosome 3 (performed by WellGenetics Inc, Taiwan).

MARCM clone generation and analysis

To generate Sf3b1 MARCM clones, virgin females of GAL45−40 UAS-Venus:pm, SOP-FLP#42; tubP-GAL80 FRT40A [97] stock were crossed with FRT40A, FRT40A Sf3b1mt, or FRT40A Sf3b1mt UAS-Sf3b1 males. Larvae at 72 h, 90 h AEL, and third instar wandering stages expressing UAS-Venus:pm were imaged (Carl Zeiss LSM 900; 20 ×/0.8 NA; 488 nm, 20%, 500–535 nm, 800 V; pinhole 80 μm).

Imaging and quantification of the dendrite terminals of neurons

Visualization of neuron morphology was performed essentially as described previously [98]. In brief, single larvae at the desired developmental stage were positioned dorsal side up on a glass slide in mounting medium (90% glycerol and 2.5% DABCO), and then covered with a coverslip, with gentle compression from tail to head to expel internal tissues and organs. Labeled neurons were imaged (LSM 710; 20 ×/0.8 NA; 561 nm, 20%, 565–625 nm, 800 V; pinhole 120 μm). Assessments of dendrite endpoints were carried out by capturing images of one or two neurons in the dorsal A5-A7 region of the body. Total numbers of terminal dendritic branches in C4da neurons were quantified using Determ [96], with a probability setting of 0.9 and a mask alpha value of 0.7. Manual correction was applied as needed. For class I and class III da neurons, dendrite endpoints of the entire neuron were counted manually.

Dendritic branch dynamics

Adult female flies were allowed to lay eggs and transferred to fresh tubes every 3 h. After hatching, larvae were collected at 72, 90, and 105 h AEL for live imaging of the C4da neuron dendrites in the dorsal A5-A7 segments using a method adapted from Li et al. [99]. Selected larvae were chilled in ice-cold water for 1 min, then dried on lens paper, before being placed on double-sided tape (3 M Removable Repositionable Tape 665) affixed to a glass slide. A space was created by attaching three layers of double-sided tape on both sides of the larvae before covering with a coverslip. Imaging was performed on a LSM 710 (20 ×/0.8 NA; 561 nm, 2% power, 880 V gain; pinhole: 140 μm). After initial imaging, larvae were returned to standard medium at 25 °C for 3 h before a second imaging session. Confocal images were maximally projected for comparison between sessions. Only well-imaged areas corresponding between sessions were selected for analysis. Individual terminal branches were tracked freehand and measured from the terminal to the first intersection using Fiji software. Increases or decreases in length of > 0.2 μm were classified as"extend"or"retract", respectively. Separately,"emerge"represented the appearance of a new terminal branch in the second imaging session (3 h later) that had not been present in the initial image, whereas"vanish"indicated complete disappearance of a branch that had initially been present. Visual representations of changes in terminal branch length were created using Adobe Photoshop 2021, with different colors indicating various dynamics. Percentages of dendrite dynamics were calculated by dividing the number of terminal branches exhibiting different behaviors (emerge, vanish, extend, and retract) within the 3-h period by the total number of analyzed branches.

Mitochondrial morphology and distribution

Mitochondria in C4da neuron soma were visualized using Dendra2 (LSM 710; 100 ×/1.40 NA oil; 488 nm, 25%, 490–560 nm, 830 V; pinhole: 80 μm). The mean length and area of soma mitochondria was determined using the Fiji Mitochondria Analyzer plugin [100], which provided output measurements after processing 2D slice images with adaptive thresholding and despeckling, on a per-cell basis. To analyze mitochondrial density in C4da dendrites, neurons were imaged (LSM 710; 20 ×/0.8 NA; sequential excitation: 488 nm, 25%, 800 V; 561 nm, 2%, 850 V; pinhole: 100 µm). Dendra2-labeled mitochondrial puncta were manually counted along CD4-tdTom-labeled dendritic processes. Mitochondrial density was calculated by dividing the total puncta count by the cumulative length of major processes emanating from the cell body of C4da neurons [101]. Dendrite lengths were quantified through filament tracing in Imaris 9.1.

Analyses of marker expression and distribution

To visualize microtubules and actin in C4da neurons, we expressed UAS-Jupiter-mCherry (with ppk-GAL4 > mCD8-GFP) and LifeAct-GFP (with ppk-GAL4 > CD4-tdTom). Imaging used a LSM 710 (20 ×/0.8 NA), exciting Jupiter (488 nm: 3%, 800 V; 561 nm: 7%, 750 V) and LifeAct (488 nm: 1.8%, 800 V; 561 nm: 4.5%, 750 V); pinhole: 90 µm. For Jupiter-mCherry quantification, a segmented line (25-pixel width) was drawn along the axon, soma, and a major dendritic process using the mCD8-GFP signal. Background noise in the mCherry channel was reduced with a 30-pixel rolling ball radius, and gray values along the line were measured with Fiji's plot profile plugin. Gray values above 50 were used to delineate the soma region. For LifeAct-GFP distribution analysis, acquired images were processed as previously described [101, 102]. Background was reduced (rolling ball radius: 50 pixels), followed by 32-bit conversion and smoothing. LifeAct-GFP images were default thresholded, and ratio images normalizing LifeAct signals to CD4-tdTom expression were generated using the RatioPlus plugin with a rainbow LUT (from NucMed plugin) scaled from 0.10 to 2.00. Quantification of LifeAct-GFP along dendritic branches was performed by tracing regions of interest (ROI) in ratio images using Fiji's plot profile plugin (line width = 8 pixels). ROIs were selected along the major and terminal branches of each neuron, and the average relative intensity of LifeAct-GFP for each neuron was calculated. Fluorescence imaging used a LSM 710 with oil objectives (100 ×/1.40 NA: Rab5, KDEL; 63 ×/1.40 NA: ManII), exciting Rab5 (488 nm, 2%, pinhole 110 μm), KDEL (488 nm, 20%, pinhole 83 μm), and ManII (561 nm, 6.5%, pinhole 95 μm), all at 800 V gain. We analyzed fluorescence signals from ManII and Rab5-Venus in dendritic segments by following methods described previously [99, 103]. The signals were transformed into mask images using a Moment threshold and then processed using the Analyze Particles function in Fiji to identify each punctum, applying parameters of 0.0–1.0 for'circularity'and 3–200 pixels for'particle size'. We quantified the numbers of Golgi complexes labeled by ManII and Rab5-labeled endosomes in the primary and secondary dendrites of C4da neurons. The ER of C4da neuronal soma was labeled with GFP-KDEL and analyzed by means of trainable Weka segmentation in Fiji [104]. Tubule and sheet domains were converted into separate mask images using a Moment threshold. The areas of these domains were then measured individually.

RT-PCR

RNA was isolated from first instar larvae utilizing TRIzol™ reagent and subsequently transferred into a miRNeasy Mini Kit and DNase I as per the manufacturer’s instructions. Synthesis of the cDNA was conducted by employing SuperScript™ IV VILO™ Master Mix, and PCR amplification was executed using DreamTaq Green PCR Master Mix. The PCR reactions were carried out using the primer pairs listed in the resource list.

RNA sequencing and analysis

RNA sequencing was performed on first instar Drosophila larvae of control (da-GAL4 crossed with w1118, duplicate samples) and Sf3b1 knockdown (da-GAL4 > UAS-Sf3b1RNAi, triplicate samples) lines. The Sf3b1RNAi TRiP line (located at Chromosome III) was backcrossed with the w1118; TM3ser/TM6b stock for several generations to generate a w1118; Sf3b1RNAi homozygous line. RNA was extracted using TRIzol™, Purified using a miRNeasy Mini Kit, treated with DNase I, and then Libraries were prepared with a TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit. Quality was checked using An Agilent HS DNA Bioanalyzer. Sequencing was performed by the Genomics Core Facility of the Institute of Molecular Biology, Academia Sinica, as 75-basepair paired-end reads on An Illumina NextSeq 500 High output platform at an average read depth of 50 million reads per sample. The RNA-seq data are publicly accessible at 10.5281/zenodo.13772179. The RNA-seq reads were aligned to the Ensembl reference genome BDGP 6.28.99 of Drosophila melanogaster using STAR [105], and abundance was calculated using RSEM [106]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using edgeR, applying a False Discovery Rate (FDR) of < 0.05 and a log2 fold change threshold of |log2FC|> 1 [107]. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs was conducted using clusterProfiler 4.12 in Rstudio [108]. The top three significant pathways were selected based on an adjusted p-value for cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF), and biological process (BP), and outputs were visualized using GraphPad Prism v.9. Volcano plots, generated in RStudio, were used to visualize significantly upregulated genes (in red), significantly downregulated genes (in blue), and mitochondria-related genes (as black dots), identified from the MitoMax database [109]. We also investigated alternative splicing events using rMATS [36]. Significant alternative splicing events were defined based on a FDR < 0.05 and an absolute change in Percent Spliced In |ΔPSI|> 0.1. We developed an algorithm to identify splicing variations by analyzing CIGAR strings from sequencing data, focusing on sequences with a mapping quality (MAPQ) above 10. The algorithm starts by loading the Drosophila melanogaster reference genome BDGP 6.28.99 and gene annotations from FASTA and GTF files. It parses CIGAR strings to identify operations such as matches (M), insertions (I), deletions (D), and skipped regions (N). Upon encountering (N), the algorithm segments the reads and checks them against exon boundaries. Reads entirely within exons are categorized as normal splicing events, whereas those spanning exon–intron boundaries are categorized as 5'exon extension or 3'exon extension events. Reads located entirely before the exon in question are classified as intronic events. The frequency of splice variants for each gene is determined by the ratio of non-canonical transcript occurrences (intron, 3'extension, and 5'extension) to the total transcript count per gene. Genes exhibiting a splice variant frequency exceeding 0.2, coupled with a FDR < 0.05 upon Sf3b1 knockdown, are denoted as exhibiting significant splicing variation. To identify genes related to"mitophagy,"we queried FlyBase for relevant GO terms, the KEGG pathway (map04137), Reactome pathways (R-DME-5205647, R-DME-8934903, R-DME-5205685), and included two curated datasets [110, 111], resulting in a total of 198 genes. For"mitochondrial biogenesis,"we similarly utilized FlyBase GO terms, Reactome pathways (R-DME-1592230, R-DME-2151201), and mitochondrial electron transport chain biogenesis genes from Zhang et al. [112], obtaining a total of 399 genes.

Imaging and signaling analysis for the mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0, MitoTimer and redox sensor

Control (ppk-GAL4; mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0 cross to lacZ; mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0) and Sf3b1 knockdown larvae (ppk-GAL4; mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0cross to mRuby3-iATPSnFR1.0; Sf3b1RNAi) at 120 h AEL were dissected by cutting larvae at the anterior tip, inversion, and removing fat bodies in ice-cold hemolymph-like 3 (HL3) medium. After dissection, larvae were incubated at 25 °C in fresh HL3 media containing either 1 mM Antimycin A (from 100 mM stock in ethanol; 10 µL/mL HL3) or ethanol alone (10 µL/mL HL3) as vehicle control for various duration, then mounted on slides for imaging. ATP levels in dendrites were quantified in vivo at different developmental stage without dissection. Larvae were imaged on a LSM 900 (40 ×/1.20 W water objective), sequentially exciting at 488 nm (soma 15%, dendrites 12%) and 561 nm (soma 0.3%, dendrites 4%), detected by Airyscan GaAsP-PMT (488 nm: 720 V; 561 nm: soma 685 V, dendrites 650 V) with a 125 μm pinhole. ATP quantifications were performed using a custom macro adapted from GitHub (https://github.com/eggerbo/Drosophila_brain) [113]. Composite images were summed projected and straightened along segmental lines (width: 55 pixels for soma; 25 pixels for dendrites). The RFP channel (mRuby3) was aligned to the GFP channel (iATPSnFR1.0) using MultiStackReg. Binary masks were generated from the RFP channel by mean threshold, background subtraction (radius = 40 pixels), and watershed segmentation (minimum particle size = 3 pixels). GFP:RFP ratios and integrated intensities within these masks were calculated using a customized Blob Ratiometric analysis, both in individual soma and dendritic ROIs. Neuronal soma visualization was performed according to Kardash et al. [114]. Images were straightened (width: 55 pixels), background-subtracted (rolling ball radius = 100 pixels), converted to 32-bit, and smoothed. A Li threshold was applied to the GFP channel (background pixels set to NaN). GFP:RFP ratiometric images were generated with the RatioPlus plugin and psuedo-colored using a rainbow LUT. Expression of ppk > Mitotimer in soma and proximal dendrites was imaged (LSM 900, 63 ×/1.40 NA oil), sequentially excited (488 nm, 1.8%; 561 nm, 0.2%) and detected by Airyscan GaAsP-PMT (488 nm: 730 V; 561 nm: 600 V; digital gain: 0.4), pinhole 90 µm. Images were processed by Sum-slice projection, converted to 8-bit, and segmented using the adaptiveThr plugin [115] (mean intensity calculated over 16-pixel blocks minus 17) to generate masks. For each ROI, the red:green ratio was analyzed using the Blob Ratiometric plugin with default background subtraction. ROIs for mitochondria in soma and proximal dendrites were manually separated. Integral intensities for both red and green channels were measured for each ROI, and the mean integral intensities were calculated separately for soma and dendritic ROIs across control and Sf3b1RNAi neurons. ROI with pure red puncta (signal exceeding the mean red integral intensities in the soma of both control and Sf3b1RNAi neurons and a red:green ratio > 1.1) were excluded from ratiometric analysis. Counts of red puncta were performed manually. For visualization, ratio images were pseudo-colored using rainbow LUT. For recording the redox sensor mito-roGFP2, C4da neurons were imaged (LSM 900, 40 ×/1.20 W), sequentially excited with 405 and 488 nm excitation (700 V), and emissions at 500 and 542 nm (GaAsP-PMT, 36 µm pinhole) were collected. Sum-projected images were split, and aligned via MultiStackReg (405 nm reference), and binary masks generated using Otsu threshold, background subtraction (radius = 40 pixels), and watershed segmentation (minimum size = 3 pixels).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted with GraphPad Prism9, utilizing unpaired Student’s t-tests. Results are displayed as scatter plots with bars, in which each dot represents the measurement of an independent biological sample. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Significance levels are denoted as follows: ns (not significant) for p > 0.05; * for p < 0.05; ** for p < 0.01; *** for p < 0.001; and **** for p < 0.0001.
