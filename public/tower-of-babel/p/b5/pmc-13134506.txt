CALR/HIF‐1α Positive Feedback Loop Drives CALR Upregulation to Promote EMT‐Mediated Bladder Cancer Progression via ROS/AKT Axis
Authors: Xintao Tian, Yue Liu, Zhiyuan Mi, Ye Liang, Zhao Zhang, Xiaocheng Ma, Huiqing Jia, Yuxuan Cao, Zhijuan Liang, Shaonan Yang, Guofeng Ma, Haitao Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCALR mutation is a key driver of myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) and has become a critical biomarker in clinical diagnostics and therapy. However, its function and mechanisms in solid tumors, particularly bladder cancer (BLCA), remain unclear. This study demonstrates that CALR expression is significantly elevated in BLCA, closely associated with poor patient prognosis, and serves as a critical factor promoting tumor progression. Further investigation reveals that the high expression of CALR stems from a novel positive feedback loop with HIF‐1α in the tumor microenvironment: CALR stabilizes HIF‐1α protein by von Hippel–Lindau (VHL), while HIF‐1α transcriptionally upregulates CALR expression, thereby self‐sustaining its high expression levels in BLCA. Mechanistically, CALR promotes the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) process by inducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation and activating the AKT signaling pathway, ultimately driving EMT‐associated tumor progression. Finally, we identify the natural small‐molecule compound—Sinapine as a direct inhibitor of CALR for the first time. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments confirmed that targeted inhibition of CALR effectively suppresses BLCA growth. This study not only elucidates the mechanism by which CALR maintains high expression through the CALR/HIF‐1α positive feedback loop and promotes malignant progression in BLCA but also provides a theoretical foundation for its potential use as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.Keywords: bladder cancer, CALR, EMT, HIF‐1α, ROS, tumor progression

Introduction

Bladder cancer (BLCA) ranks as the ninth most common cancer globally, with approximately 570,000 new cases and over 200,000 deaths annually [1]. Based on disease stage, about 70% of cases are classified as non‐muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC), while the remaining 30% are muscle‐invasive bladder cancer (MIBC) [2]. Current treatment strategies for BLCA include surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy [3]. However, despite these available modalities, clinical management remains challenging due to chemotherapy resistance, immune checkpoint blockade resistance, disease recurrence, and metastasis [4, 5, 6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets to improve outcomes for BLCA patients.

CALR is an endoplasmic reticulum (ER)‐resident chaperone protein that contributes to proper protein folding and calcium ion homeostasis [7]. In myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN), CALR mutations are well‐established drivers of disease, and the level of mutant CALR protein in patient plasma correlates with mutation burden [8, 9, 10]. Although the pathogenic mechanisms and clinical significance of CALR in MPN have been extensively studied, its expression patterns, molecular functions, and clinical relevance in solid tumors, such as BLCA, remain to be systematically elucidated. Beyond its ER‐related roles, CALR is involved in processes including cell adhesion and calcium signaling. CALR dysregulation may disrupt Ca2+ homeostasis [7], promote reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and consequently influence tumor‐related pathways [11, 12, 13]. Studies have reported that dysregulated CALR expression was associated with tumor progression and lymph node metastasis in cancers such as esophageal carcinoma and nasopharyngeal carcinoma [14, 15], further underscoring the need for research into its role in BLCA.

Our study reveals that CALR is highly expressed in BLCA and correlates with poor prognosis. We uncover a positive feedback loop wherein HIF‐1α transcriptionally activates CALR, which in turn stabilizes HIF‐1α by von Hippel–Lindau (VHL), potentially explaining CALR overexpression in BLCA. Mechanistically, CALR induces ROS accumulation to activate AKT signaling, driving EMT and reversing Gemcitabine resistance. Importantly, we identify Sinapine as a compound that targets CALR and inhibits BLCA progression, supporting CALR's role as a promising therapeutic target.

Materials and Methods

Bioinformatics Analysis and Patient Specimens

The mRNA expression data for BLCA patients were retrieved from the TCGA database (https://tcga‐data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/). The survival information is obtained from https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/. All of these data were analyzed using R software (version 4.3.4). For the real‐world cohort, a total of 8 fresh paired tumor samples were collected from patients undergoing radical cystectomy at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University and were subsequently utilized for Western blot and RT‐qPCR. A total of 130 BLCA tissue samples collected from patients undergoing radical cystectomy at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University were utilized for Immunohistochemical staining. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Informed consent was obtained from the participants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Qingdao University Affiliated Hospital (QYFYWZLL29831).

Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining

Tissue was harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through ethanol. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) under microwave heating treatment. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2 for 15 min, followed by incubation with 10% normal serum to reduce nonspecific binding. Primary antibodies diluted in PBS were applied overnight at 4°C, then detected with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies. DAB substrate was used for chromogenic visualization, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

For human BLCA tissue samples, this study incorporated tumor tissue specimens from 130 patients who underwent radical cystectomy at the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University. Among these, 90 samples were used to construct tissue microarrays, and 40 samples were processed into tissue sections. All cases completed scheduled follow‐up with the endpoint set to January 2025.

Immunoreactive score was obtained as a product of multiplication between the percentage of positively stained area (graded 0–4: 0 = < 5%, 1 = 5%–25%, 2 = 26%–50%, 3 = 51%–75%, 4 = > 75%) and target protein expression levels (graded 0–3: 0 = negative, 1 = weak yellow, 2 = moderate yellow, 3 = strong brown). Patients were dichotomized into high‐expression (n = 80) and low‐expression (n = 50) groups based on the median IHC score. Kaplan–Meier curves with log‐rank tests were employed to compare overall survival (OS) differences between cohorts. All cases were reviewed by two uropathologists.

Animal Experiments

For the BBN (N‐butyl‐N‐(4‐hydroxybutyl)nitrosamine)‐induced orthotopic BLCA model, male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were housed under specific pathogen‐free (SPF) conditions and acclimated for 3 days before receiving drinking water containing 0.05% BBN (3817‐11‐6, MACKLIN, China) or control (BBN‐free) for 16 weeks. The bladder index was calculated as (bladder weight/body weight) × 100%.

For the xenograft model, male BALB/c nude mice or male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks) were maintained in SPF conditions and acclimated for 3 days prior to subcutaneous injection of T24 (5 × 106 cells/mouse) to BALB/c nude mice or MB49 (1 × 106 cells/mouse) to C57BL/6 mice, mixed 1:1 with Matrigel. After experiments, euthanasia was performed to quantify tumor weight and volume. Tumor volume was measured as (length × width2)/2.

All mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the affiliated hospital of Qingdao University (AHQU‐MAL20230505TXT).

Hematoxylin–Eosin (H&E) Staining

Paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through ethanol. Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, followed by differentiation in 1% HCl‐ethanol and bluing in 0.2% ammonia solution. Cytoplasmic components were counterstained with eosin Y. Sections were dehydrated through ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Reagents, Antibodies, and Drugs

The reagents, antibodies, and drugs used in this study are shown in Table S1.

Cell Culture and Treatment

The human BLCA cell lines T24, J82, 5637, and the mouse BLCA cell line MB49 were purchased from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in DMEM with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a standard humidified environment. For hypoxia treatment, cells were cultured in an InvivO2 400 hypoxia workstation (Ruskin Technologies) with 1% O2 for 24 h. For the treatment of inhibitors or drugs, the inhibitors or drugs were directly added to the medium at the indicated concentrations and incubated for the specified durations (Table S1). For transfection, CALR small interfering RNA (siCALR#1, siCALR#2) was synthesized and purchased from GenePharma. According to the manufacturer's instructions, siRNA was transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). The sequences of siRNA and transfection details are shown in Table S1.

Western Blot

Proteins were extracted using SDS sample buffer, and the protein concentration was measured using the BCA protein assay kit. The proteins were boiled at 95°C for 5 min. Subsequently, the denatured proteins were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate‐polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 1.5 h, and primary antibodies (1:1000) were incubated overnight at 4°C. After washing the membrane three times, it was incubated with a secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. The bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Millipore). The experimental results were normalized to β‐actin or GAPDH expression levels.

Real‐Time Quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using AG RNAex Pro reagent. We used a spectrophotometer to measure the RNA concentration at 260 nm. The Evo M‐MLV RT mixed kit was used to reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA. All reactions are as follows: Denaturation (95°C 10s), annealing and elongation (60°C 30s) for 40 cycles. The expression level was normalized to β‐actin.

Immunoprecipitation (IP) and Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

For IP, after lysing the cells with IP lysis buffer, centrifuge the lysate and collect the supernatant. Incubate the supernatant with target protein‐specific antibodies and protein A & G‐conjugated magnetic beads overnight at 4°C with rotation. Subsequently, wash the beads three times rigorously using pre‐chilled lysis buffer. Add 1× loading buffer and boil the mixture at 95°C for 10 min to elute the protein complexes. Finally, separate the samples via SDS‐PAGE, transfer the proteins to a membrane, and detect the expression of the target protein by Western blot analysis.

For CHIP, crosslinking the cellular protein DNA complexes with 1% formaldehyde, then sonicating the chromatin. Chromatin fragments were incubated with anti‐HIF‐1α antibody or IgG overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, the complexes were separated using protein A & G‐conjugated magnetic beads. Subsequently, reversing cross‐linking was performed, and the protein was digested using proteinase K, and the purified DNA was analyzed by qRCR.

Functional Experiments

Transwell Assay

Transwell assays were performed using 24‐well Transwell chambers (8 μm, Corning). For the invasion assay, the upper chamber membrane was pre‐coated with Matrigel. Cells were collected and resuspended in serum‐free medium, and 5 × 104 cells were seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with complete medium containing 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation, non‐migratory cells in the upper chamber were carefully cleaned with a cotton swab. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and then stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Finally, images were captured under a microscope.

Wound Healing Assay

The cells were scraped with a sterile pipette tip after being seeded into a 6‐well plate, and they continued to be cultured in serum‐free medium for 24 h. Afterward, the cells were stained with Calcein‐AM. Images were captured using a microscope at 0 and 24 h.

Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) Assay

To assess cell proliferation ability, BLCA cells were seeded into a 96‐well plate (1,000 cells/well). Following the manufacturer's instructions, 10 ul of CCK8 solution was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) Determination

For the determination of cellular ROS, the ROS detection kit was used. According to the manufacturer's instructions, DCFH‐DA was diluted (1:1000) in serum‐free medium and used to incubate the cells at 37°C for 20 min. The cells were washed three times with medium. An electron microscope was used to capture images.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were seeded into a 24‐well plate with slides, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X‐100, blocked with 5% BSA, and incubated overnight at 4°C with the corresponding primary antibody. A fluorescent dye‐conjugated secondary antibody was incubated at room temperature for 1 h. The cell nucleus was stained with DAPI staining solution, and electron microscopy was used for imaging.

‌Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability ‌(Darts) Assay

After lysing the cells using BeyoLytic Mammalian Active Protein Extraction Reagent, the supernatant was collected via centrifugation and co‐incubated with the target small compounds overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, the mixture was digested with gradient concentrations of protease for 10–15 min, and the reaction was terminated by adding 0.5 M EDTA (pH 8.0). The samples were mixed with 5× loading buffer, boiled at 95°C for 20 min to denature proteins, and finally analyzed for target proteins via Western blot.

Construction of Gemcitabine‐Resistant BLCA Cell Line

Gemcitabine‐resistant cell line (GC‐T24) is derived from the human BLCA cell line T24. Previously detailed, DMEM with 10% FBS was used to culture the cells, and the cells were treated with 0.5 μg/mL Gemcitabine. The drug concentration was gradually increased.

Flow Cytometry

For the determination of apoptosis, an Annexin V‐FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit was used. Following the manufacturer's instructions, cells were collected by trypsin without EDTA, incubated at room temperature for 30 min with 2.5 μL of Annexin V/PI, and detected using a flow cytometer.

Statistical Analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 and compared using student t‐test or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The significance of overall survival (OS), disease‐specific survival (DSS), and progression‐free interval (PFI) differences between groups was compared using the Log‐rank (Mantel‐Cox) test. P‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

CALR Is Upregulated and Associated With Poor Prognosis in BLCA

Initially, we analyzed CALR expression across various cancers in the TCGA database, revealing notable upregulation in numerous solid tumors, including BLCA (Figure 1A,B). Subsequently, we found a correlation between high expression of CALR and poor overall survival (OS), disease‐specific survival (DSS), and progression‐free interval (PFI) in patients (Figure 1C, Figure S1A–F). The IHC results showed that patients with high CALR expression had a worse prognosis, which was consistent with the TCGA database (Figure 1D). Moreover, analysis of 8 pairs of BLCA samples demonstrated that the CALR expression was higher in tumors than in normal tissues, further supporting our initial observations (Figure 1E,F). To elucidate the functional relevance of CALR in BLCA, we established an orthotopic model by administering 0.05% BBN to mice over 16 weeks (Figure S2A,B). IHC staining of bladder tissue sections from the mice demonstrated elevated CALR expression in BBN‐induced bladder tumors compared to normal tissues (Figure S2C). Meanwhile, CALR knockdown led to a significant reduction in tumor volume and weight in vivo (Figure 1G,H). These results implicated a tumor‐promoting role for CALR. CALR Can Be Upregulated by HIF‐1α and Participates in a Positive Feedback Loop With HIF‐1α in BLCA

HIF‐1α Activates CALR Transcription by Directly Binding to the Promoter in BLCA

To investigate the causes of the upregulation of CALR in BLCA, we performed GSEA enrichment analysis on BLCA samples from the TCGA database. The results showed that hypoxia‐related pathways were significantly enriched in the CALR high‐expression group (Figure S3A). Meanwhile, we observed that both CALR protein and mRNA levels were upregulated under hypoxic conditions (Figure S3B). These findings suggested that the hypoxia pathway may be a critical upstream signaling pathway of CALR. There was increasing evidence that hypoxia in the tumor microenvironment served as a key driver of BLCA progression [16]. HIF‐1α, a key factor in hypoxia, played a pivotal role in regulating the hypoxia adaptive response of BLCA [17]. Then, we found a significant positive correlation between CALR and HIF‐1α by the GEPIA database (Figure 2A). Additionally, we observed elevated levels of HIF‐1α protein in tumors compared to normal tissues (Figure S3C). Furthermore, patients with the lower HIF‐1α expression group exhibited significantly improved OS compared to the high group (Figure 2B). Existing evidence consistently indicates that HIF‐1α exhibits marked instability and reduced transcriptional activity under normoxic conditions [18, 19]. To explore the functional interaction between HIF‐1α and CALR in BLCA, we established an in vitro tumor‐hypoxic microenvironment by exposing T24, J82, and 5637 cells to 1% O2 for 24 h. Previous studies have suggested that the HIF family can directly modulate the expression of downstream target genes by binding to hypoxia response elements (HREs) in gene promoters [20]. To investigate whether HIF‐1α directly regulates CALR transcription, we conducted experiments involving siHIF‐1α transfection, which resulted in a significant downregulation of CALR expression (Figure 2C). Similarly, the HIF‐1α inhibitor LW6 reversed the hypoxia‐induced effects on CALR expression (Figure 2D). Subsequently, we predicted potential HIF‐1α binding sites in the CALR promoter region (Region 1: −227 bp to −219 bp; Region 2: −924 bp to −919 bp) and performed ChIP assays. The results indicated direct binding of HIF‐1α to the CALR promoter, with stronger binding observed in region 2 (Figure 2E). These results suggested that HIF‐1α can directly bind to the CALR promoter region, thereby activating CALR transcription.

CALR Can Stabilize HIF‐1α by Inhibiting the Ubiquitination Degradation of HIF‐1α by VHL

HIF‐1α regulated CALR expression by binding to the promoter. At the same time, our investigation revealed that CALR knockdown resulted in decreased HIF‐1α expression (Figure 2F), suggesting that CALR exerted potential regulatory effects on HIF‐1α. Previous research has demonstrated that HIF‐1α primarily undergoes degradation via the Ubiquitin‐Proteasome System (UPS) [21]. Therefore, we speculated that CALR regulated the expression of HIF by modulating ubiquitination. To verify this hypothesis, we assessed HIF‐1α half‐life using Cycloheximide (CHX) and observed that CALR knockdown reduced the stability of HIF‐1α (Figure 2G). Subsequently, IP analysis revealed an interaction between CALR and HIF‐1α (Figure 2H). Finally, we found that CALR knockdown could enhance HIF‐1α ubiquitination levels (Figure 2I).

Next， we explore the specific molecular mechanism by which CALR knockdown promotes the ubiquitin‐mediated degradation of HIF‐1α. It is well‐known that in the canonical degradation pathway, the E3 ubiquitin ligase VHL recognizes hydroxylated HIF‐1α and mediates its ubiquitination, leading to proteasomal degradation [22, 23]. Therefore, we first investigated whether CALR regulated HIF‐1α ubiquitination via VHL. The results showed that CALR knockdown significantly reduced HIF‐1α protein levels but did not affect VHL expression (Figure 3A). Therefore, we suggested that CALR might influence HIF‐1α degradation by modulating the VHL‐HIF‐1α interaction rather than VHL expression. To test this hypothesis, immunoprecipitation (IP) assays revealed that CALR knockdown markedly enhanced the binding between VHL and HIF‐1α (Figure 3B, Figure S3D). To further confirm the specificity of this interaction, we employed VH298 (a specific inhibitor of the VHL: HIF‐1α protein–protein interaction). Preliminary experiments indicated that VH298 induced HIF‐1α accumulation in BLCA cells in a dose‐dependent manner, with a more pronounced effect at 3 h compared to 6 h (Figure 3C, Figure S3E−G). Therefore, 100 μM VH298 for 3 h was selected for subsequent experiments. Crucially, VH298 treatment effectively reversed the enhanced VHL‐HIF‐1α binding induced by CALR knockdown and restored HIF‐1α protein levels (Figure 3D,E). Subsequently, IP assays further demonstrated that VH298 also reversed the increase in HIF‐1α ubiquitination levels caused by CALR knockdown (Figure 3F). In conclusion, our findings indicate that CALR inhibits the ubiquitin‐dependent degradation of HIF‐1α by suppressing its interaction with VHL.

CALR Can Promote the Malignant Progression of BLCA by Regulating EMT

To investigate the role of elevated CALR expression in driving the malignant advancement of BLCA, we performed the GSEA enrichment analysis. The result revealed enrichment of tumor‐related pathways (e.g., EMT, glycolysis, hypoxia) in the high‐expression group (Figure 4A,B). EMT is a pivotal process in tumor progression, with previous studies underscoring its involvement in tumor initiation, progression, and metastasis [24]. Our results revealed that CALR knockdown markedly suppressed the migratory and invasive capabilities of BLCA cell lines (Figure 4C−E). Subsequently, we found that both the protein and gene expression levels of N‐cadherin and Vimentin were down‐regulated in the siCALR group. Furthermore, we observed a decrease in proliferation capacity and PCNA expression in the siCALR group (Figure 4F−H, Figure S4A). These results provided solid evidence that CALR contributed to the malignant progression of BLCA by regulating EMT.

CALR Affects EMT Through ROS/AKT Axis

CALR Affects EMT Through AKT Pathway

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the impact of CALR on EMT, we initially performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis, revealing a significant association between CALR and the AKT signaling pathway (Figure S4B). Given the established link between the AKT signaling pathway and EMT in existing literature [25, 26], we guess that CALR may modulate EMT via the AKT signaling pathway in BLCA. To validate this hypothesis, we assessed the levels of p‐AKT, N‐cadherin, and Vimentin after CALR knockdown, observing a reduction in their expression (Figure 5A, Figure S4C). Subsequently, we used SC79, an AKT agonist, to elucidate the involvement of AKT in CALR‐mediated regulation of N‐cadherin and Vimentin. Our results demonstrated that SC79 could reverse the impact of CALR knockdown on N‐cadherin and Vimentin expression (Figure 5B, Figure S4D). Collectively, these results support that CALR modulates EMT through the AKT pathway.

CALR Affects EMT via ROS‐Mediated AKT Pathway

Subsequently, our focus shifted to investigating the mechanism by which CALR knockdown triggers the activation of the AKT signaling pathway. CALR is a calcium transporter. Calcium is a crucial second messenger that can facilitate ROS generation in cellular processes. Numerous studies have illustrated that excessive influx of Ca2+ into cells results in the accumulation of intracellular ROS, subsequently initiating downstream signaling cascades such as AKT/mTOR and EGFR pathways [27, 28]. Moreover, research has suggested that ROS plays a role in regulating EMT [29]. Thus, we sought to determine whether CALR knockdown influences ROS levels, thereby activating the AKT pathway and impacting EMT in BLCA. Our experimental findings revealed a reduction in ROS accumulation in BLCA cell lines following CALR knockdown (Figure 5C). To further investigate the role of ROS in EMT through the AKT pathway, we utilized hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) to induce ROS production. Our results demonstrated that H2O2 activation led to AKT activation and increased expression of N‐cadherin and Vimentin (Figure 5D), indicating that ROS can modulate the AKT pathway and influence EMT. Subsequently, our results showed that the addition of H2O2 reversed the AKT phosphorylation and EMT alterations induced by CALR knockdown (Figure 5E, Figure S4E). These findings emphasize that CALR knockdown impacts EMT through the ROS‐mediated AKT pathway.

Sinapine Can Directly Target CALR and Inhibit the Progression of BLCA

Limited research has been conducted on small‐molecule drugs targeting the CALR protein. To address this gap, we utilized virtual screening to identify natural product‐derived small molecules with the potential to bind directly to CALR. Among the top six candidates ranked by binding affinity, Lindleyin, Retinyl acetate, Avanafil, Sinapine, Orotidine 5′‐monophosphate, and Aurantio‐obtusin β‐D‐glucoside were selected (Figure 6A,B). Notably, Retinyl acetate and Sinapine have previously demonstrated anti‐tumor effects [30, 31]. Thus, we evaluated their ability to target CALR and inhibit the progression of BLCA. Firstly, the CCK‐8 assay revealed a concentration‐dependent inhibition of BLCA cell proliferation by Sinapine, while Retinyl acetate showed no significant effect (Figure S5A−C). Western blot analysis further demonstrated that Sinapine significantly suppressed CALR expression in BLCA cells, whereas Retinyl acetate had no effect (Figure 6C). These results suggest that Sinapine may inhibit BLCA cells by targeting CALR. Next, Molecular docking between Sinapine and CALR confirmed two hydrophobic interactions, two hydrogen bonds, and two salt bridges, with a binding energy of −7.71 kcal/mol, indicating strong binding efficiency (Figure 6D). Darts revealed that Sinapine protected CALR from proteolytic degradation, confirming their direct interaction (Figure 6E). These results collectively suggest that Sinapine holds promise as a therapeutic strategy for BLCA by targeting CALR.

To assess the antitumor efficacy and drug toxicity in vivo, subcutaneous xenograft models were established in mice. The treatment group received intraperitoneal injections of Sinapine at a dose of 40 mg/kg every two days. Sinapine demonstrated a significant inhibition of tumor growth (Figure 7A, Figure S5D). Importantly, there were no notable effects on body weight, histomorphology of vital organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney), or hepatic and renal function parameters (Figure 7C−E, Figure S5E−H). IHC staining and Western blot analysis revealed a marked decrease in CALR protein expression levels within tumor tissues of the Sinapine‐treated group (Figure 7G, Figure S5I,J). These results suggest that Sinapine may exert its antitumor effects by suppressing CALR expression while maintaining a favorable safety profile.

CALR Knockdown Reverses EMT‐Associated Gemcitabine Resistance

EMT is known as contributing to tumor drug resistance [32, 33]. Our study has demonstrated that CALR represents a promising therapeutic target in BLCA and plays a role in EMT. Currently, Gemcitabine remains the first‐line treatment for MIBC. To investigate the role of CALR in Gemcitabine resistance, we assessed whether reducing CALR levels could reverse EMT‐mediated resistance to Gemcitabine. Initially, we developed a Gemcitabine‐resistant BLCA cell line termed GC‐T24 and assessed the expression of CALR and EMT‐related proteins in this model. Our analysis revealed significantly elevated levels of N‐cadherin and Vimentin in GC‐T24 compared to the parental T24 cell line (Figure 8A,B). Subsequently, we observed that CALR knockdown enhanced the sensitivity of GC‐T24 cells to Gemcitabine treatment (Figure 8C−E). In conclusion, our data support a role for CALR in modulating Gemcitabine resistance in BLCA through its impact on EMT processes.

Discussion

Tumor microenvironment (TME) is an important factor driving tumor progression [34]. TME is composed of multiple components. Hypoxia is a key characteristic of the TME and significantly promotes tumor progression by HIFs. HIFs play a pivotal role in regulating the hypoxia adaptive response of BLCA [17]. As crucial transcription factors, HIFs bind to HREs in the promoter regions of target genes, thereby enhancing their transcriptional activity. Studies have shown that hypoxia‐induced HIF‐1α can promote the maintenance of cancer stem cell properties and the process of EMT [35]. Furthermore, Katarzyna B. Leszczynska et al. demonstrated that hypoxia‐induced p53 regulated apoptosis and radiosensitivity through the AKT signaling pathway [36]. Mao et al. further elucidated that HIF‐1α influenced chemotherapy resistance in BLCA by modulating autophagy [37]. In this study, we reveal for the first time that HIF‐1α directly binds to the HRE in the CALR promoter region, leading to the upregulation of CALR expression in BLCA. However, the direct regulatory mechanisms of HIF‐1α on downstream signaling pathways in BLCA warrant further investigation. On the other hand, as an oxygen‐sensitive transcription factor, the protein stability of HIF‐1α is tightly regulated. Under normoxic conditions, HIF‐1α is rapidly degraded, primarily via the ubiquitin‐proteasome system (UPS), with a short half‐life typically ranging from several minutes to tens of minutes [21, 38]. Our findings demonstrate that CALR inhibits the ubiquitination‐mediated degradation of HIF‐1α by VHL, thereby enhancing its protein stability. This interaction establishes a positive feedback loop between CALR and HIF‐1α, thereby maintaining the high expression level of CALR in BLCA. High expression of CALR increases the mortality of patients with BLCA.

Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying BLCA is crucial for enhancing clinical treatment outcomes and patient prognosis. Our investigation revealed that CALR was significantly upregulated in BLCA and functions as an oncogene impacting patient prognosis. Through in vitro and in vivo experiments, we demonstrated that CALR promoted BLCA progression, particularly through the induction of EMT, a critical process in tumor advancement observed across various solid malignancies [35, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Notably, our study unveiled that CALR knockdown suppressed EMT by inhibiting the AKT pathway, with the AKT agonist SC79 capable of reversing this effect. Furthermore, our findings align with existing literature linking EMT to treatment resistance, as evidenced by our establishment of a Gemcitabine‐resistant BLCA cell line exhibiting elevated CALR and EMT expression compared to the sensitive counterpart [43, 44, 45, 46]. Remarkably, downregulation of CALR partially restored Gemcitabine sensitivity in the resistant cell line.

Targeting CALR in oncology is an underexplored therapeutic avenue. Our research has discovered Sinapine, a natural alkaloid found in cruciferous plants, could be a new inhibitor of CALR in BLCA. Sinapine functions by binding directly to CALR, reducing its protein levels, and effectively impeding BLCA progression by halting cell proliferation. These results not only expand the therapeutic landscape of CALR‐targeted treatment but also present Sinapine as a promising strategy for precise therapy in BLCA.

Our study revealed a significant association between aberrant CALR expression and the occurrence and progression of BLCA, with elevated CALR levels correlating with a poorer prognosis. We found that HIF‐1α promoted the transcription of CALR by binding to the promoter. Conversely, CALR upregulation stabilized the expression of HIF‐α by inhibiting ubiquitination. The positive feedback loop between CALR and HIF‐1α could drive sustained high expression of CALR in BLCA. Furthermore, high expression of CALR could promote EMT in BLCA through the ROS/AKT axis, thereby promoting tumor progression (Figure 9). Additionally, we discovered and validated that Sinapine directly targeted CALR and inhibited the progression of BLCA in vitro and in vivo. In conclusion, our research provided insights into the reasons, functions, and mechanisms of high expression of CALR in BLCA, and provided a solid theoretical basis for the treatment of BLCA.
