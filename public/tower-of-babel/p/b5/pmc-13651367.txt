Investigation of the effects of mouthwashes on the surface properties of CAD/CAM blocks and 3D-printed resins
Authors: Büşra Gül Güvenç, Ezgi Atamer Durmuş, Ceren Tezer Aşkın, Numan Aydın, Berkay Güvenç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe aim of this study was to investigate the effects of mouthwashes with different formulations on the surface roughness, microhardness, and wettability properties of restorations fabricated from 3D-printed resins and CAD/CAM blocks.Materials and methodsA total of 120 specimens (n = 8) were prepared using SLA (Permanent Crown, Formlabs), DLP (Crowntec, Saremco) 3D printing resins, and a resin-based CAD/CAM composite block material (Tetric CAD). Finishing and polishing procedures were performed using a two-step diamond-impregnated polishing system. Surface roughness (Ra) was measured using confocal microscopy, microhardness was evaluated by the Vickers hardness test (VHN), and wettability was determined by measuring the contact angle using the sessile drop method. Following baseline measurements, the specimens were divided into five groups according to the immersion solution: alcohol-containing mouthwash, alcohol-free mouthwash, whitening mouthwash, hydrogen peroxide-containing whitening mouthwash, and a control group (artificial saliva). Surface roughness, microhardness, and wettability measurements were repeated after 24 h and 1 week of exposure.ResultsTime significantly influenced surface roughness, contact angle, and microhardness (p < 0.001). Across all materials, exposure to mouthwashes resulted in increased surface roughness and decreased contact angle and microhardness values. The increase in surface roughness was most pronounced within the first 24 h, and significant differences were observed among all time points (p < 0.001). Material type significantly affected all outcome variables (p < 0.001), with the 3D-printed resin materials exhibiting greater surface roughness than the CAD/CAM block material. Mouthwash type significantly affected surface roughness (p = 0.001) and contact angle (p < 0.001), whereas no significant effect was observed on microhardness (p = 0.117). Among the tested mouthwashes, whitening formulations induced the greatest changes in surface roughness and contact angle.ConclusionsUnder the tested in vitro conditions, exposure to mouthwashes affected the surface properties of the evaluated restorative materials over time. These changes were more pronounced in the 3D-printed resin materials and whitening mouthwash groups.Clinical relevanceMouthwash composition may produce material-dependent differences in the surface behavior of restorative materials. Therefore, the possible interaction between restorative material type and mouthwash composition should be considered.

Introduction

The integration of intraoral scanners and CAD/CAM systems into dentistry has initiated a substantial transformation in clinical practice [1]. CAD/CAM technology enables the highly precise fabrication of various types of restorations, including inlays, onlays, veneers, partial crowns, and full crowns [2]. These technologies facilitate the rapid production of highly accurate dental restorations through both subtractive and additive manufacturing methods [3].

The majority of CAD/CAM systems operate based on the milling of prefabricated block materials. Owing to their polymerization under high temperature and pressure conditions, these materials may exhibit reduced residual monomer release, improved biocompatibility, and enhanced optical properties [4]. However, subtractive manufacturing methods are associated with several limitations, including high equipment costs, material waste, and the requirement for expensive milling units. In this context, 3D printing technologies have emerged as a promising alternative due to their cost-effectiveness and design flexibility. Three-dimensional printing systems enable the fabrication of highly precise restorations with complex geometries while reducing material waste, shortening production time, and lowering manufacturing costs [5]. Photopolymerization-based 3D printing systems, such as Digital Light Processing (DLP) and Stereolithography (SLA), provide high-resolution fabrication through the layer-by-layer polymerization of light-sensitive resins [6].

The long-term success of restorative dental treatments depends not only on the manufacturing technique employed but also on the influence of intraoral chemical and physiological conditions. In the oral environment, restorative materials are continuously exposed to environmental factors such as saliva, food and beverages, temperature fluctuations, and oral hygiene products. Factors including the chemical composition of food and beverages and the composition of saliva may directly affect the mechanical durability, surface integrity, and esthetic properties of these materials [7]. In addition, oral care products routinely used in daily oral hygiene practices may induce alterations in resin composite surfaces, such as increased surface roughness, reduced microhardness, and discoloration [8, 9]. In particular, the composition and active ingredients of mouthwashes may play a significant role in the physicochemical properties of dental restorative materials.

Mouthwashes are pharmaceutical solutions containing various components such as chlorhexidine gluconate, ethanol (alcohol), essential oils, humectants, flavoring agents, colorants, and stabilizers. In addition, formulations may include anti-inflammatory, anesthetic, antibacterial, antifungal, antihistaminic, and remineralizing agents. These over-the-counter products are widely used worldwide for the control of halitosis, gingivitis, and periodontitis, as well as for the prevention of dental caries. Alcohol commonly serves as a solvent and preservative agent in these solutions and may be present in varying concentrations depending on the brand, while some formulations may be alcohol-free [10]. Furthermore, certain formulations may contain low concentrations of hydrogen peroxide or other bleaching agents. Agents such as hydrogen peroxide and carbamide peroxide are frequently preferred because of their whitening effects. In recent years, numerous whitening mouthwashes claiming to provide rapid whitening effects have been introduced to the market. Moreover, due to the effects of their chemical constituents, these mouthwashes have been reported to increase the surface roughness of composite materials [11].

The degradation of composite materials generally begins with water absorption and progresses depending on the structure of the polymer matrix [12]. Alcohol, water, and acidic components present in mouthwashes may cause dissolution within the resin phase and induce ester hydrolysis, leading to degradation of the polymer matrix structure. This process may be associated with reductions in microhardness and alterations in surface properties [9, 13]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the long-term use of alcohol-based mouthwashes may lead to surface degradation, deterioration of optical properties, and discoloration in composite materials [14, 15]. Therefore, the selection of mouthwashes should be made by considering the type of restorative material used, its chemical resistance, and its esthetic integrity.

Restorative materials must be capable of withstanding intraoral masticatory forces while maintaining their surface microhardness. Reduced surface hardness may render the material more susceptible to wear and deformation, which may subsequently result in increased surface roughness and a greater tendency toward fracture over time [16]. Such surface degradation may lead to consequences including plaque accumulation, discoloration, loss of durability, and reduced biological stability [17].

While surface hardness reflects the mechanical strength of a material, another important property determining biological interactions is wettability. High contact angles indicate low wettability or high hydrophobicity, whereas low contact angles indicate high wettability or high hydrophilicity [18, 19]. Wettability is an important characteristic that determines the interaction of restoration surfaces with saliva and oral fluids. This property may influence microbial adhesion to the surface, plaque accumulation, and biofilm formation [20].

Previous studies have separately investigated the effects of mouthwashes on restorative materials or the surface properties of digital restorative materials [14, 21, 22]. However, based on the current literature, studies simultaneously comparing DLP- and SLA-based 3D-printed resins with resin-based CAD/CAM block materials in terms of surface roughness, microhardness, and wettability following exposure to mouthwashes with different formulations appear to be limited. The combined evaluation of these parameters may provide insights not only into the morphological changes occurring at the material surface but also into whether these changes are associated with the mechanical resistance and wettability behavior of the material. Thus, it may be possible to determine whether mouthwash-induced surface degradation is limited to a unidimensional change in surface roughness or represents a more comprehensive material response accompanied by microhardness loss and alterations in wettability. The combined evaluation of these three parameters may provide more comprehensive information regarding the surface integrity and mechanical stability of these materials. The aim of this study was to investigate the effects of mouthwashes with different formulations on the surface properties of restorations fabricated from 3D-printed resins and CAD/CAM blocks. The null hypothesis of the study was that mouthwashes with different formulations would not have a significant effect on the surface roughness, wettability, or surface microhardness of 3D-printed resins and CAD/CAM blocks.

Materials and methods

In this study, restorative materials fabricated from 3D-printed resins and CAD/CAM blocks, as well as mouthwashes with different formulations, were used. A DLP-based permanent restorative resin (Crowntec; Saremco Dental AG, Switzerland), an SLA-based permanent restorative resin (Permanent Crown; Formlabs, USA), and a resin-based CAD/CAM composite block material (Tetric CAD; Ivoclar Vivadent AG, Schaan, Liechtenstein) were included in the study. Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power software (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on a three-factor analysis of variance, a statistical power of 80% (1–β = 0.80) and a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05) were adopted. The large effect size used for the power analysis (f = 0.40) was determined according to values reported in previous in vitro studies with similar experimental designs in the field of dental materials [23–25]. Accordingly, the minimum sample size was determined as n = 40 for each material group, and a total of 120 specimens were prepared.

Specimen preparation

Specimens fabricated using the additive manufacturing method were produced with two different 3D printing techniques. The specimens produced with the DLP-type printer were fabricated using Crowntec permanent restorative resin in an Asiga MAX UV 3D printer (Asiga, Australia). The specimens produced with the SLA-type printer were fabricated using Permanent Crown resin in a Formlabs Form 3B+ 3D printer (Formlabs, USA). For both manufacturing techniques, the specimens were designed with dimensions of 7 × 12 × 2 mm, using a layer thickness of 50 μm and a build angle of 90°.

The post-polymerization process of the specimens produced using the DLP-based 3D printer (Asiga MAX UV, Asiga, Australia) was carried out in a post-curing unit (Labolight DOU, GC, Japan) for 10 min. The Permanent Crown resin specimens produced using the SLA-based 3D printer were post-cured in a Form Cure unit (Formlabs, MA, USA) for a total of 40 min, with each surface cured for 20 min, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations. Residual resin on the specimens was cleaned with 99% isopropyl alcohol for 3 min. The Tetric CAD specimens, representing the CAD/CAM composite block material, were sectioned under water cooling using a precision cutting device (MICROCUT 202, Metkon, Turkey) to dimensions of 7 × 12 × 2 mm, matching those of the 3D-printed specimens.

To minimize operator-related variability, all specimen preparation, polishing, immersion, and measurement procedures were performed by a single investigator using the same standardized protocol and device settings for all specimens. Surface polishing of all specimens was performed using a two-step diamond-impregnated polishing system (Twist Dia; Kuraray Noritake Dental, Japan). The polishing procedure was carried out under dry working conditions and with light pressure, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Standardization was ensured in all specimens by maintaining the same polishing system, application sequence, rotational speed range, application time, and light pressure throughout the procedures. In the first step, Twist Dia Coarse polishers were applied at 8,000–10,000 rpm for 15 s, followed by the use of Twist Dia Fine polishers at 8,000–10,000 rpm for 15 s in the second step. After completion of the polishing procedure, the specimens were stored in distilled water for 24 h prior to baseline measurements.

Microhardness (VHN), surface roughness (Ra), and contact angle measurements

To ensure standardized and comparable baseline, 1-day, and 7-day measurements, reference markings were created on the non-measured surfaces of the specimens. All measurements were performed using the same surface regions for each specimen and were obtained from areas close to the center of the specimens. This approach was adopted to preserve the comparability of the data obtained at different time points.

Vickers microhardness measurements were performed using a microhardness testing device (HMV-G; Shimadzu, Japan). Before the measurements, the accuracy of the device was checked using a reference hardness block. All measurements were performed using the same device, the same load, the same loading time, and the same measurement protocol. Measurements were obtained from three different points on each specimen under a load of 100 g with a loading duration of 15 s, and the arithmetic mean of the obtained values was used for statistical analysis. The measurement points were selected at a sufficient distance from previous hardness measurement indentations and specimen edges to avoid any influence.

Surface roughness measurements were carried out using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Smartproof 5; Zeiss, Germany). Before the measurements, the device was prepared for data acquisition by performing its built-in initial calibration procedure. A C Epiplan-Apochromat 20× objective lens was used during the measurements. Images obtained from three different surface areas of each specimen were analyzed using ConfoMap ST software (version 9.3.10494; Zeiss, Germany), and the arithmetic mean of the Ra values was recorded. Measurement standardization was ensured by using the same objective lens, scanning protocol, and analysis settings for all specimens.

To evaluate wettability, contact angle measurements were performed using a contact angle goniometer equipped with an image capture system (Attension Theta Lite Tensiometer, Biolin Scientific, USA). Before the measurements, the contact angle measurement system was calibrated using the calibration materials provided by the manufacturer. To ensure measurement standardization, the specimens were placed in a completely horizontal position during measurement, and distilled water at room temperature was used as the same test liquid. The water droplet volume was fixed at 4 µL using the device’s syringe dispenser, and the droplet was applied perpendicular to the specimen surface. After the droplet contacted the surface, a 10-second image recording was acquired by the device; using the images obtained during this period, the right and left contact angles of the droplet, defined as the angle formed at the liquid–solid interface, were measured with the device’s built-in analysis software, and the mean contact angle value was calculated. The same measurement procedure was repeated in three different regions of each specimen, and the mean of the obtained values was used for statistical analysis.

Immersion protocol

Specimens belonging to each material group were numbered and randomly assigned into five subgroups of equal size using a random number table. The specimens were subsequently immersed in the corresponding mouthwash solutions. All specimens were placed horizontally at the bottom of separate containers containing the corresponding mouthwash solutions, ensuring that the specimen surfaces were completely immersed in the solution. Each specimen was stored in a separate closed container containing 3 mL of the corresponding solution at 37 °C in an incubator, and the solutions were renewed every 24 h. Measurements related to surface properties were repeated after exposure periods of 1 day (24 h) and 7 days (168 h).

According to accelerated aging models reported in the literature, 12 h of continuous immersion in mouthwash solutions corresponds approximately to one year of clinical use when based on twice-daily use for 1 min each time, whereas 24 h of immersion represents approximately two years of clinical use. Accordingly, the 24-hour and 168-hour immersion periods applied in the present study were considered to represent approximately 2 years and 14–15 years of clinical exposure, respectively [26, 27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality assumption of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Surface roughness, microhardness, and contact angle values were analyzed using mixed-design repeated measures ANOVA, in which time was considered the within-subject factor, while material type and mouthwash type were considered between-subject factors. Pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Surface roughness

According to the mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA results, time, material type, and mouthwash type had significant effects on surface roughness (p ≤ 0.001). Examination of the effect sizes indicated that time, material type, and mouthwash type had large effects on surface roughness. In addition, the time × material and time × mouthwash interactions were found to be significant (p < 0.001). These interactions indicate that the time-dependent increase in surface roughness did not occur to the same extent across all materials and mouthwash groups. In contrast, the three-way time × material × mouthwash interaction and the material × mouthwash interaction were not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

When the effects of mouthwashes with different formulations on the surface roughness, wettability, and surface hardness of 3D-printed resins and CAD/CAM materials were evaluated, the null hypothesis proposing that these mouthwashes would not affect restorative materials was rejected.

In the oral environment, restorative materials are continuously exposed to numerous external factors, particularly mechanical loading, chemical agents, and biological influences. These interactions may lead to adverse effects such as increased surface roughness, reduced surface gloss, and discoloration associated with degradation of the polymer matrix [28]. Although mouthwashes, which are widely used as part of daily oral hygiene practices, are effective in reducing microbial load, their composition and duration of use may also cause undesirable effects on the surface integrity and physical properties of restorative materials [29, 30]. Therefore, evaluating the chemical interactions between mouthwashes and restorative materials may provide important insights into the surface integrity, durability, and esthetic performance of these materials.

Surface roughness is an important surface parameter that affects the esthetic properties of restorations and has been associated with susceptibility to biofilm accumulation in the literature. However, bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation are multifactorial processes that are not solely dependent on surface roughness but may also be influenced by the chemical composition of the material and other surface properties [31, 32]. Previous studies have reported that increased surface roughness may facilitate bacterial attachment. A systematic review identified an Ra value of approximately 0.2 μm as a reference threshold for bacterial plaque retention and suggested that bacterial accumulation may increase on surfaces exceeding this value [32]. In addition, roughness values within the range of 0.25–0.5 μm have been reported to be perceptible to patients by tongue sensation [33]. In the present study, surface roughness values in the Tetric CAD Block group generally remained below this threshold at all time points, whereas the Ra values of the 3D-printed resin materials (Crowntec and Permanent Crown) exceeded the 0.2 μm threshold following 1- and 7-day exposure periods. The increase in surface roughness was particularly more pronounced during the initial 24-hour period. This early-stage alteration may be associated with solvent interactions and contraction stresses occurring at the resin matrix–filler interface due to alcohol- and phosphate-based agents during the initial phase of mouthwash exposure [27, 34]. The subsequent reduction in the rate of increase over time may be explained by the material reaching a partial equilibrium state.

Whitening mouthwashes may contain components such as sodium hexametaphosphate and tetrasodium pyrophosphate. These components have been reported to exert their effects by desorbing surface molecules due to their strong binding affinity [35, 36]. This mechanism may induce microstructural alterations within the resin matrix or at the resin–filler interface, potentially leading to an increase in surface roughness. Consistent with this mechanism, the highest roughness values in the present study were observed in the whitening mouthwash group, followed by the alcohol-containing mouthwash group. This finding supports the notion that whitening and alcohol-containing formulations may induce more pronounced alterations in surface morphology. The increase in roughness observed in the alcohol-containing mouthwash group is also consistent with reports suggesting that ethanol and essential oils may partially dissolve the polymer network and weaken the integrity of the resin matrix [37, 38].

Conflicting results have been reported regarding the effects of hydrogen peroxide-containing whitening products on the surface roughness of composite resins. While some studies have reported a significant increase, others have found no meaningful change [39–41]. In the present study, a statistically significant increase in surface roughness was observed in the hydrogen peroxide-containing mouthwash group. However, this increase remained more limited compared with some of the other mouthwash groups. This finding suggests that hydrogen peroxide-containing formulations may influence surface roughness, although the extent of this effect may vary depending on the material used and the composition of the mouthwash.

Acids, fluoride, and various auxiliary components present in alcohol-free mouthwashes may induce alterations in the polymeric matrix. In addition, hydrolysis of the ester groups within dimethacrylate monomers may weaken the integrity of the polymer network. This process may contribute to deterioration of the surface morphology and an increase in surface roughness [9, 37]. Therefore, the observed increase in surface roughness may be considered a potential surface alteration with implications for biofilm accumulation and staining; however, these findings should be interpreted within the limitations of accelerated laboratory conditions created by continuous immersion.

In the present study, Tetric CAD exhibited lower surface roughness values compared with the 3D-printed resins. This result may be related to its high filler content and industrially polymerized CAD/CAM block structure [42]. Indeed, composites with higher filler content and a more homogeneous polymer network have been reported to exhibit lower surface roughness values [43].

In contrast, differences were also observed among the 3D-printed resins. The higher roughness values observed in Formlabs Permanent Crown compared with Crowntec may not be explained solely by their classification as resin-based materials. Printing orientation, layer thickness, light exposure duration, printing resolution, and the positioning of support structures may all influence surface topography [44, 45]. In addition, variations in post-polymerization duration, light intensity, and curing devices may alter the degree of polymerization and the amount of residual monomer [46]. These factors may contribute to differences in the susceptibility of 3D-printed resins to surface degradation following exposure to mouthwashes. Therefore, the differences in roughness observed among the 3D-printed resins may be associated with printing parameters, the layer-by-layer manufacturing process, and post-polymerization protocols.

Surface wettability is evaluated by the contact angle formed between a liquid droplet and a solid surface, and this measurement reflects the hydrophilic/hydrophobic character of the material. An increase in contact angle indicates reduced wettability and is also important in terms of bacterial adhesion processes because of its relationship with surface free energy [47, 48]. Contact angle values may be influenced not only by the chemical composition of the material but also by variables such as surface topography, surface energy, the surface tension of the liquid, and the extent of liquid–solid interactions [49]. In addition, hydrophobic materials have been reported to exhibit greater color stability and lower susceptibility to staining compared with hydrophilic materials [50].

In an in vitro study conducted by Abass et al., changes in the surface roughness and contact angle of 3D-printed resin materials exposed to mouthwashes were investigated. According to the results of the study, immersion in mouthwashes resulted in a decrease in contact angle values. The authors further suggested that the increase in surface roughness observed after immersion may have contributed to the reduction in contact angle values [21]. Similarly, Pogorzelski et al. demonstrated that exposure of PMMA surfaces to commercial mouthwashes decreased the contact angle and rendered the surfaces more hydrophilic; this effect was suggested to be associated with chemical and morphological alterations occurring on the surface [51].

Similarly, in the present study, a parallel trend was observed between the increase in surface roughness and the decrease in contact angle following exposure to mouthwashes. In particular, the observation of the highest roughness values together with the lowest contact angle values in the whitening mouthwash group is consistent with the existing literature. Higher surface roughness values were observed in the 3D-printed resin materials. Compared to the baseline values, the lower contact angle values detected in these materials suggest that alterations in surface morphology may influence wettability behavior. However, it should also be considered that contact angle is not determined solely by surface roughness, but may additionally be influenced by multiple factors such as surface chemistry, surface free energy, polymer degradation, and filler particle exposure.

Although the literature regarding contact angle values of resin-based CAD/CAM materials remains limited, the similarity between our baseline values and those reported in studies describing contact angles of approximately 80° for manually polished CAD/CAM restoratives suggests that the obtained measurements are consistent with the literature [52]. The lower contact angle values observed in Tetric CAD may be explained by its high filler content, nanohybrid structure, and more homogeneous polymer network achieved through industrial polymerization. Indeed, previous studies have reported that nanocomposites with high filler loading exhibit smoother surfaces and consequently lower contact angle values [53]. In addition, the presence of hydrophilic monomers such as TEGDMA within the resin matrix may increase wettability [54]. These characteristics may contribute to the wettability profile of the material, which is characterized by lower contact angle values. The lower surface roughness observed in Tetric CAD may suggest that this material exhibits a more favorable profile in terms of surface integrity and color stability. Previous studies have reported that smoother surfaces may reduce the retention of staining agents and support color stability [55, 56]. The absence of a significant difference in contact angle values between Crowntec and Permanent Crown suggests that these two 3D-printed resins exhibit similar wettability behavior. Nevertheless, consistent with the literature, wettability values in 3D-printed resins with lower filler content and greater microstructural heterogeneity have been reported to show more variable behavior over time and following chemical exposure [57, 58].

Previous studies have reported that more hydrophilic surfaces, characterized by lower contact angle values, may promote the adsorption of salivary proteins and thereby influence early bacterial adhesion [59]. Yuan et al. suggested that lower contact angle values in polymer-based restorative materials may be associated with increased Streptococcus mutans adhesion [59]. Similarly, although the literature indicates a potential relationship between wettability and biofilm adhesion, this relationship may vary depending on factors such as material type, surface roughness, and oral environmental conditions [60, 61].

Nevertheless, biofilm formation is not a single-factor process that can be explained solely by wettability. Multiple factors, including surface roughness, surface free energy, the chemical composition of the material, filler particle characteristics, and surface morphology, may contribute to this process [59]. When considered together with the existing literature, the increase in surface roughness and decrease in contact angle observed in the present study may suggest potential implications of these surface alterations for microbial adhesion and biofilm formation. However, since biofilm formation was not directly evaluated in this study, such interpretations should be considered indirect inferences. Future studies directly assessing microbial adhesion may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the biological implications of these surface changes.

The time-dependent decrease observed in microhardness appears to be associated with the effects of chemical exposure on the mechanical surface stability of the materials. In particular, the fact that the major change occurred within the first 24 h suggests that chemical agents may induce more pronounced mechanical alterations on the material surface during the early stage of exposure. Similarly, studies reporting significant reductions in microhardness following short-term chemical exposure support this finding [62]. Previous studies have also reported that both alcohol-containing and alcohol-free mouthwashes may lead to reductions in the microhardness of resin-based materials [63, 64].

The more pronounced decrease in microhardness observed in resin-based 3D-printed materials may be associated with microstructural differences related to material composition and manufacturing method. The significant effect of material type on microhardness has also been demonstrated in the literature [62]. In addition, previous studies have reported that the microhardness values of polymer-based CAD/CAM materials may also change following exposure to chemical agents [22]. Therefore, it may be expected that materials produced using different fabrication techniques—such as industrially pre-polymerized blocks and layer-by-layer manufactured 3D-printed materials—could exhibit different mechanical responses to environmental factors such as chemical agents.

In the present study, the higher microhardness values observed in Tetric CAD, a resin-based CAD/CAM block material, compared with DLP- and SLA-based 3D-printed resins may suggest that the manufacturing method plays a determining role in polymer network integrity and initial mechanical stability. The more homogeneous and highly cross-linked polymer network structure of industrially pre-polymerized CAD/CAM block materials may contribute to maintaining surface hardness more stably following exposure to chemical agents. Indeed, surface microhardness has been emphasized in the literature as an important parameter reflecting the surface stability of restorative materials [63, 65].

Although a significant time-dependent decrease in microhardness values was observed in all mouthwash groups, the absence of a significant difference among the mouthwash types in terms of microhardness change may suggest that alterations in mechanical surface properties are more closely related to the general effect of chemical exposure rather than to a specific mouthwash formulation. Consistent with this finding, recent studies have also reported that mouthwash type may not produce a significant difference in microhardness alteration and that material type may play a more determining role in this process [22, 62].Consequently, microhardness changes appear to vary according to material type and time, suggesting that the manufacturing method may influence mechanical stability.These findings suggest that the surface durability of 3D-printed resin materials following exposure to oral care products may require more detailed evaluation. However, it should be taken into consideration that the present findings are based on in vitro accelerated aging conditions created by continuous immersion and do not directly represent actual clinical exposure. Therefore, the results should be supported by long-term clinical studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the study was conducted under in vitro conditions, and the dynamic nature of the oral environment could not be fully simulated. In clinical conditions, restorative materials are simultaneously exposed to numerous biological and physical factors, including salivary flow, pellicle formation, pH fluctuations, biofilm accumulation, temperature variations, and mechanical loading. Therefore, the obtained findings should not be directly generalized to clinical conditions.

According to accelerated aging models reported in the literature, continuous immersion protocols have been used to simulate the long-term chemical exposure of restorative materials [66]. However, these durations should not be interpreted as direct equivalents of actual clinical usage periods, but rather as accelerated chemical aging models under standardized laboratory conditions. In the clinical setting, exposure to mouthwashes is intermittent and influenced by multiple factors such as saliva, thermal fluctuations, and mechanical loading. In the present study, the 1-day and 7-day immersion periods were selected to comparatively evaluate the short- and long-term chemical effects of mouthwashes on restorative materials. However, the absence of intermediate time points, such as 48–72 h, constitutes a methodological limitation with respect to assessing the intermediate progression of time-dependent changes. Although continuous immersion models are widely used in the literature within the framework of accelerated aging approaches, these durations should not be considered direct equivalents of real clinical use periods [67]. In particular, the 1-week immersion period was selected as a model to investigate long-term surface degradation tendencies, in line with similar experimental aging protocols reported in the literature [14].

In addition, thermomechanical aging methods such as thermal cycling and chewing simulation were not applied in the present study. However, in the oral environment, restorative materials are continuously exposed to thermal fluctuations and repetitive occlusal forces. These factors may contribute to stress formation between the resin matrix and filler particles, the development of microcracks, filler loss, and acceleration of surface degradation. Therefore, the absence of thermal cycling and mechanical wear simulation should be considered an important limitation, particularly when interpreting the long-term clinical implications of the findings. Furthermore, processes such as toothbrushing and mechanical wear may also influence the surface properties of materials. Therefore, the combined effects of chemical exposure and thermomechanical stresses could not be evaluated in the present study.

Although the polishing procedures were standardized, the inability to completely eliminate operator-dependent variation and the absence of a formal operator calibration or pilot repeatability assessment should be considered among the limitations of this study. Similarly, surface roughness and contact angle measurements were obtained only from specific regions of the materials and may not fully represent the entire surface heterogeneity. Wettability assessment was performed using only distilled water. However, under clinical conditions, salivary proteins and pellicle formation may alter surface energy and contact angle values. Therefore, direct correlation of the contact angle findings with clinical biofilm dynamics is limited.

In the present study, only one resin-based CAD/CAM block material and two different 3D-printed resins were evaluated. Accordingly, the obtained results cannot be generalized to all CAD/CAM and 3D-printed materials with different filler contents, resin matrix compositions, and manufacturing protocols. Further clinical studies are needed to validate these findings under intraoral conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, exposure to different mouthwashes was found to affect the surface properties of the restorative materials evaluated over time. Following mouthwash exposure, an overall increase in surface roughness and decreases in microhardness and contact angle values were observed. These effects were more pronounced in the tested 3D-printed resin materials than in the CAD/CAM block material, while the whitening mouthwash groups exhibited greater changes in surface roughness.
