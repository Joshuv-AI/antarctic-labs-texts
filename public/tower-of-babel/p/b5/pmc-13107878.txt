Multicenter peritumoral radiomics in oncology: advances, challenges, and future directions
Authors: Jie Li, Junting Wei, Ruixin Pan, Dianpei Ma, Zongyu Xie, Zhizhen Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The peritumoral region harbors critical microenvironmental heterogeneity information and has long been a focal point in radiomics research. This article systematically reviews the progress of multicenter peritumoral radiomics in oncology, with emphasis on its clinical and biological applications. Studies indicate that peritumoral radiomics may demonstrate value in tumor diagnosis/differential diagnosis, molecular subtyping identification, prediction of clinicopathological indicators, prognostic risk assessment, and treatment response evaluation. Multicenter validation shows that incorporating peritumoral features can enhance model performance for certain tasks, with peritumoral characteristics potentially outperforming intratumoral features in specific scenarios. At the biological mechanism level, peritumoral radiomics exhibits associations with genomics and immune microenvironment events, offering novel perspectives for non-invasive tumor biology analysis. However, the field still faces challenges including limited model generalizability, standardization and reproducibility issues, demands for interpretability, ambiguous peritumoral region definitions, and multi-omics integration barriers - these limitations necessitate cautious interpretation of current findings. Future breakthroughs may rely on prospective multicenter studies, standardized protocols, explainable AI techniques, biologically-driven optimization of peritumoral definitions, and innovative multi-omics fusion algorithms. To promote clinical translation, it is necessary to enhance model robustness and interpretability while deepening research on the association with tumor biological mechanisms.

Introduction

The peritumoral region refers to the interface between the tumor mass and healthy tissue, which appears normal on imaging but exhibits cellular heterogeneity, vascular abnormalities, and molecular alterations at the microscopic level [1]. This region is both the frontier of tumor invasion and a crucial component of the Tumor Microenvironment (TME), which is involved in the processes of tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion. TME is a dynamic ecosystem formed during tumorigenesis and development, consisting of tumor cells, surrounding stromal cells, extracellular matrix, vascular system, signaling molecules, metabolites, and other components [2]. The various cells within it are not passive bystanders in tumor development; they may play critical roles in the pathogenesis of cancer and represent promising therapeutic targets [3]. In the early stages, limited by technological capabilities and scientific development, research primarily focused on the tumor tissue itself, often overlooking the peritumoral region. By 2015, Jean-Michel Lemée et al. [4] pointed out that 90% of glioblastoma recurrences occurred in peritumoral brain regions containing specific tumor-promoting cellular components, highlighting the necessity to expand the scope of research. In recent years, breast cancer studies have explored the association between peritumoral features-such as edema, vascular indices, and Apparent Diffusion Coefficient (ADC) ratios-and prognosis [5], proposing that the 2–20 mm zone around the tumor could serve as a critical research target area. From neglect and discovery to research, this journey reflects a leap from pathological observation to molecular mechanism analysis, and further to the empowerment of imaging technology, indicating that the peritumoral region may provide new targets for precision medicine. Therefore, accurately identifying tumor-related factors in the peritumoral region is crucial.

Conventional pathology relies on invasive tissue sampling and provides a limited, spatially restricted assessment of tumor heterogeneity. And this heterogeneity may lead to heterogeneous distribution of genetically distinct tumor cell subpopulations between and within disease sites, which in turn leads to different clinical treatment and prognostic outcomes for cancer patients [6]. Medical imaging can reflect the heterogeneity of tumors and peritumoral regions through radiomics, a non-invasive method, serving as a medium and bridge for the quantitative analysis of the TME. The concept of radiomics was first proposed by Lambin in 2012 [7]. In essence, it is an interdisciplinary approach that employs computer technology to extract high-throughput quantitative features from medical images, combined with statistical and machine learning methods, to reveal the biological characteristics of diseases. Its core lies in transforming visual images into mineable numerical data to assist in diagnosis, prognosis prediction, and treatment decision-making [8]. Early studies primarily focused on intratumoral lesions, yielding encouraging yet preliminary results [9, 10]. However, we cannot overlook the peritumoral region as a crucial component of the TME, which also contains abundant biological information that may contribute to the precision diagnosis and treatment of tumors. Building on intratumoral research, scholars are increasingly incorporating peritumoral regions into radiomics analysis, leading to a sustained rise in the popularity of peritumoral radiomics studies [11, 12]. Evidence suggests that in certain scenarios, peritumoral models may offer superior predictive value compared to intratumoral models [13], while combined models often demonstrate more pronounced advantages. With the continuous advancement of research and scientific technology, radiomics is increasingly integrating with multi-omics data such as genomics and transcriptomics to build more comprehensive models [14–17], providing multi-scale decision support for precision patient treatment.

The clinical challenges posed by tumor heterogeneity highlight the urgent need to develop radiomics-based decision support systems and to deeply explore the associations between imaging features and molecular biological events. Although peritumoral radiomics has shown potential in oncology, single-center studies often suffer from limited sample sizes and variations in equipment parameters, leading to insufficient model generalizability. Multicenter validation, through cross-institutional data integration, provides higher-level evidence to support the biological interpretability and clinical translation of these models. This review primarily examines multicenter studies on peritumoral radiomics, delving into multidimensional application scenarios, with a focus on summarizing research progress in clinical practice and biological mechanisms. Finally, it analyzes the current scientific challenges and translational bottlenecks in the field, proposing feasible breakthrough pathways in line with cutting-edge technological trends to offer directional guidance for future research.

We conducted a literature search on PubMed using the following search terms: ‘peritumoral radiomics‘[Title/Abstract] AND (neoplasm OR cancer OR neoplasia OR tumor OR malignancy), retrieving 141 eligible articles. The last search date was April 27, 2025. Among these 141 articles, we manually screened and identified 48 studies related to multicenter peritumoral radiomics and 9 studies on the use of peritumoral radiomics to predict tumor biological mechanisms (including 3 multicenter peritumoral radiomics studies).

Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

The differentiation between benign and malignant tumors is a critical step in the diagnostic and treatment process, significantly influencing treatment selection, efficacy evaluation, and patient quality of life. Although intratumoral radiomics has made progress in tumor differentiation, with its predictive capability supported by multiple studies [57, 58], a key research question remains whether peritumoral regions can provide additional predictive value to optimize existing models. Several multicenter studies have explored this issue. Wang et al. [18] developed a nomogram integrating deep learning, intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics, along with clinical predictors for the preoperative differentiation of borderline ovarian tumors from stage I epithelial ovarian cancer. External validation demonstrated that the combined model (AUC = 0.819) outperformed both the clinical model (AUC = 0.708) and the radiomics model (AUC = 0.670) with statistical significance (P-values of 0.047 and 0.015, respectively). Zeng et al. [19] similarly reported that peritumoral radiomics can improve the classification efficacy of pure ground-glass pulmonary nodules, while a study on parotid gland tumors demonstrated that a model combining clinical, intratumoral, and 2 mm peritumoral radiomics achieved an AUC of 0.749 in external testing, outperforming single models [21]. In breast cancer imaging, a fusion model incorporating intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics with deep learning for Dynamic Contrast Enhanced (DCE) Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) lesion differentiation achieved an AUC of 0.921 in external validation, outperforming single-region approaches and radiologist assessments [20]. Zhang et al. [22] further demonstrated that a nomogram combining intra- and peritumoral radiomics features with clinical factors could assist in characterizing Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) 4 breast lesions, with an external test AUC of 0.904.

These studies suggest that in specific clinical scenarios, models integrating multi-source data may outperform single-method approaches. However, it should be noted that current evidence primarily comes from retrospective studies with varying methodologies. A common finding across these studies is that peritumoral information may provide incremental value to traditional methods. Particularly noteworthy is the need for future prospective multicenter studies comparing physician diagnostic performance to confirm the actual clinical utility of these models.

Molecular subtypes

Molecular subtypes of tumors categorize cancers with similar biological behaviors and clinical outcomes into distinct subtypes based on multidimensional molecular characteristics, thereby revealing tumor heterogeneity. For example, breast cancer is classified into subtypes such as Luminal A, Luminal B, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2)-positive, and triple-negative, while gastric cancer can be divided into four categories: Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)-positive, microsatellite instability, genomically stable, and chromosomally unstable. In the diagnosis and treatment of tumors, molecular subtypes not only guide precise therapeutic decisions and optimize prognostic evaluation systems but also drive the development of novel treatment strategies and enhance diagnostic accuracy [59, 60].

Braman, N et al. [28] were the first to investigate the correlation between peritumoral radiomics features and tumor biology in HER2-positive breast cancer, evaluating the pathological response to preoperative targeted therapy. Their study found that features extracted from the 9–12 mm peritumoral region demonstrated better predictive performance than intratumoral features alone. A classifier combining both feature types achieved an AUC of 0.89 in identifying the HER2-enriched subtype, indicating its potential value in guiding treatment. Subsequently, Luo et al. [23] were the first to utilize intratumoral and peritumoral ultrasound radiomics features for preoperative classification of HER2-positive, HER2-low, and HER2-zero breast cancer. The clinical-radiomics model achieved an AUC of 0.86 in the external test set. In another multicenter study, Wang et al. [25] developed a preoperative weighted combination model for predicting HER2 status based on automated breast volume scanning data. The model achieved a relatively low AUC (0.70) in the external test set, which may be attributed to the binary outcome design, limited peritumoral sampling, and small sample size. These cases highlight the necessity of further exploring peritumoral microenvironment features and integrating multimodal data. Peritumoral radiomics has also been applied to other tumor types. For example, a Computed Tomography (CT)-based study combined intratumoral and peritumoral features to preoperatively predict poorly differentiated invasive lung adenocarcinoma presenting as subsolid or solid lesions [24]. The nomogram incorporating clinical and radiomics features achieved an AUC of 0.887 in external validation. Further studies on cribriform structures in lung adenocarcinoma [26] and molecular subtypes of invasive breast cancer [27] also demonstrated that combined models can enhance predictive performance, aiding clinicians in formulating treatment and monitoring strategies.

In summary, the integrated radiomics model demonstrates potential in non-invasively predicting tumor biomarker status. In certain studies (such as Braman et al.‘s research [28]), peritumoral features have shown superiority over intratumoral features, suggesting an interaction between peritumoral imaging characteristics and the TME—for example, the spatial heterogeneity of peritumoral lymphocyte infiltration in HER2-positive breast cancer may correlate with targeted therapy response, further highlighting the role of the peritumoral region in tumor heterogeneity.

Clinicopathological indicators

Clinicopathological indicators not only aid in tumor diagnosis and classification but also serve as a central basis for the development of therapeutic strategies for tumor patients. However, traditional histopathological assessment of these indicators relies on postoperative specimen analysis, which is invasive and fails to capture the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of tumors [61]. Radiomics offers the possibility of non-invasive preoperative prediction, better guiding clinicians in selecting appropriate treatment options. The invasiveness of lung adenocarcinoma is a crucial characteristic of its biological behavior, directly influencing treatment strategies and prognosis evaluation. Researchers have achieved promising predictive performance in forecasting the invasiveness of lung adenocarcinoma based on multicenter radiomics [37, 41, 42]. It is noteworthy that Shang et al. aimed to develop and validate a habitat model combining intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics features to predict the invasiveness of lung adenocarcinoma through chest CT and habitat analysis [45]. The results showed that this habitat model helps predict the invasiveness of lung adenocarcinoma, and combining tumor subregions with overall information can improve predictive performance, providing a radiomics habitat analysis approach for subsequent research. Researchers have found that radiomics combined with clinical independent risk factors to construct a joint model can demonstrate better predictive performance in preoperative microvascular invasion of hepatocellular carcinoma [38], parametrial invasion in early-stage cervical adenocarcinoma and adenosquamous carcinoma [40], preoperative airspace spread in stage IA non-small cell lung cancer [43], pathological grading of soft tissue sarcoma [44], lymphovascular invasion in breast cancer [46], and histological grading of soft tissue sarcoma [47]. However, in some studies, the predictive performance of the joint model did not show this result. Yang et al. [39] aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics models based on MRI in predicting deep myometrial invasion of early-stage endometrioid adenocarcinoma, finding that a 5 mm peritumoral expansion was the optimal distance. They constructed a fusion model using ensemble and stacking algorithms, demonstrating performance comparable to that of radiomics models. Li et al. [32] constructed a model to predict preoperative nuclear grading in patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma based on intratumoral and peritumoral features. They found that the radiomics model combining intratumoral and peritumoral (5 mm) features outperformed clinical and nomogram models, achieving an AUC of 0.90 in the external test set. In predicting tumor deposits in rectal cancer patients [34], researchers discovered that the nomogram model built on clinical and radiomics features did not surpass the combined intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics model in predictive performance for tumor deposits, with AUCs of 0.870 and 0.874, respectively, in the external test set. Although the nomogram model constructed by Chen et al. [35] by combining intratumoral and peritumoral 9 mm radiomics features with clinical independent predictors can effectively predict lymph node status and overall survival in patients with non-small cell lung cancer, its predictive performance was not superior to that of the radiomics model in the external test set (0.66 and 0.67 in the external test set). Furthermore, in a study utilizing peritumoral radiomics based on multiparametric MRI for preoperative prediction of extracapsular extension in prostate cancer [48], peritumoral features significantly outperformed intratumoral features in both internal and external validation sets: T2-Weighted Imaging (T2WI): AUC 0.674 vs. 0.554, P < 0.05; ADC: 0.652 vs. 0.546, P < 0.05; external test set: 0.682 vs. 0.556.

Current research indicates that peritumoral radiomics, by integrating heterogeneity information from the tumor margin and surrounding microenvironment, demonstrates significant potential in predicting tumor aggressiveness and pathological grading. It is worth noting that some studies show that fusion models do not significantly outperform single models [32, 34, 35, 39], while peritumoral models exhibit superiority over intratumoral models [48]. Future research should further explore more efficient feature selection algorithms and incorporate the biological characteristics of tumors.

Survival prediction

Despite the continuous advancements in medical technology in recent years, the survival rate of cancer patients has not significantly improved [62]. Therefore, research on cancer prognosis is of great significance for guiding personalized treatment and improving patient outcomes. Peritumoral radiomics quantifies the microenvironmental features at the tumor edge and non-invasively captures biological processes that influence prognosis to construct prognostic models. The study by Sui et al. [29] concluded that a radiomics model incorporating both intratumoral and peritumoral (especially the 2 mm region) features is the optimal model for predicting overall survival in hepatocellular carcinoma, and combining clinical parameters can further enhance predictive performance. A CT-based radiomics study [31] analyzed intratumoral, peritumoral, and combined features to predict the prognosis of osteosarcoma patients. It found that in the external test set, the C-indices for predicting progression-free survival and overall survival were 0.814 and 0.764 for the intratumoral and peritumoral models, respectively, demonstrating the effectiveness of combined radiomics features in prognostic prediction for osteosarcoma patients. The researchers also developed nomogram models integrating multiple features to achieve survival prediction across various tumor types, including clear cell renal cell carcinoma [32], esophageal cancer [33], rectal cancer [34, 36], pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [30], and non-small cell lung cancer [35]. Among these, the multi-scale deep learning radiomics nomogram developed by Gu et al. [30] outperformed the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system in the training set and two external test sets, suggesting that peritumoral 15 mm features may reflect distant micrometastasis information, while deep learning can capture spatiotemporal evolutionary patterns overlooked by traditional staging.

Overall, these studies have preliminarily demonstrated the incremental value of the peritumoral region in predicting the prognosis of tumor patients. Future research could incorporate peritumoral radiomics features, integrating deep learning and habitat analysis to design and construct predictive models. Additionally, comparisons with existing evaluation systems should be made to highlight their clinical application value and advance the progress of personalized patient treatment.

Recurrence prediction

Radiomics can also predict patient prognosis by forecasting tumor recurrence, and researchers are utilizing tools like radiomics to shift from “passively responding to recurrence” to “actively preventing recurrence” [63, 64]. A study utilized CT-based radiomics of both intratumoral and peritumoral regions in parotid pleomorphic adenomas, combined with nine machine learning algorithms, to differentiate between intact and non-intact capsules. Areas with capsule rupture often exhibit tumor cell infiltration along perineural spaces, and such subclinical invasion is the primary cause of postoperative recurrence [49]. The results showed that the model combining intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics features with linear discriminant analysis had a higher AUC in the validation set (0.860) compared to the model based solely on intratumoral features. Li et al. aimed to develop a preoperative model for predicting the risk of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma recurrence based on CT radiomics features and clinical parameters [50]. The results showed that the radiomics-clinical nomogram performed optimally in predicting 1-year recurrence (validation set AUC of 0.764) and 2-year recurrence (validation set AUC of 0.773). These studies confirm that radiomics can serve as a powerful tool for predicting recurrence in various diseases. However, most current radiomics studies on tumor recurrence prediction are single-center studies, with insufficient generalizability and standardization of the models. Predictive models that perform well in single-center studies may not necessarily achieve satisfactory results in external validation. In the future, multicenter studies and open databases can be utilized to further explore the immense clinical potential of peritumoral radiomics.

Metastasis risk prediction

Tumor metastasis is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, directly impacting patient survival rates. The number, location, and dissemination patterns of metastatic lesions are crucial parameters in tumor grading and staging systems. Researchers are attempting to predict tumor metastasis through radiomics, aiming to achieve early intervention and timely treatment.

Wang et al. [51] investigated the potential of intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics features based on T2WI MRI in preoperatively predicting extrapelvic peritoneal metastasis in patients with epithelial ovarian cancer. They found that the integrated model achieved a significantly higher AUC in the external test set compared to the intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics models (0.843, 0.750, and 0.789, respectively), and that peritumoral radiomics provided more information about metastasis than intratumoral radiomics. Lymph node metastasis serves as an “early warning signal” of tumor biological behavior, acting as both a barometer for cancer progression and a basis for personalized treatment decisions. In a study predicting axillary lymph node metastasis in breast cancer patients using intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics features based on contrast-enhanced Mammography (MG) [52], researchers developed a nomogram by combining intratumoral and 10 mm peritumoral radiomics features, tumor diameter, progesterone receptor status, HER2 status, and contrast-enhanced MG-reported lymph node status. The nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.732 in the external test set, and radiologists effectively improved diagnostic accuracy by incorporating this nomogram to guide treatment strategies for breast cancer lymph node metastasis. In a similar study, Lin et al. [53] developed a predictive model based on radiomics features from intratumoral regions and the 10 mm peritumoral regions, which achieved an AUC of 0.7567 in the external test set, outperforming the Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) model. Shi et al. [54] were the first to explore different peritumoral distances in lymph node prediction for cervical cancer. They found that the optimal dilation distances differed between contrast-enhanced T1-Weighted Imaging (T1WI) and T2WI, and developed a nomogram combining multi-regional radiomics features, MRI-reported lymph node status, and tumor diameter, which achieved an AUC of 0.804 in the external test set. In the prediction of lymph node status in rectal cancer, Li et al. [36] constructed a nomogram model combining clinical and radiomics scores, which showed higher AUC in both the training and validation sets compared to the clinical model. However, no significant difference was observed in the external validation cohort (0.72 vs. 0.76, P = 0.164). It was concluded that the clinical-radiomics nomogram has good predictive efficacy for preoperative lymph node status in rectal cancer.

Currently, in the prediction of tumor metastasis, thanks to the unique biological mechanisms of the peritumoral region (which is often enriched with metastasis-related microenvironmental features such as angiogenesis, immune evasion, and stromal remodeling), peritumoral radiomics demonstrates distinctive performance advantages beyond traditional incremental value. For instance, peritumoral features outperform intratumoral features in predicting extraperitoneal metastasis in ovarian cancer patients [51], and in cervical cancer lymph node prediction, the 1 mm and 3 mm peritumoral regions are the optimal expansion distances for contrast-enhanced T1WI and T2WI, respectively [54]. In summary, the unique performance of peritumoral radiomics warrants attention and further exploration by researchers.

Treatment response prediction

Tumor response assessment runs through the entire cycle of disease management, with its value not only reflected in survival benefits but also in optimizing medical resource allocation and improving patients’ quality of life. With the introduction of the concept of radiomics, researchers have begun to explore the potential value of this technology in predicting tumor treatment responses.

Pathological Complete Response (pCR) refers to the absence of residual invasive cancer cells in the pathological examination of surgical specimens after neoadjuvant therapy. In the past, assessment often required comprehensive microscopic analysis of tumor components combined with imaging findings or endoscopic techniques for comprehensive judgment. Hu et al. [13] evaluated the predictive value of CT-based intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics features for pCR in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy. They found that a combined model incorporating 7 intratumoral features and 6 peritumoral features demonstrated good predictive performance in the external test set (AUC = 0.852). Notably, the intratumoral and peritumoral models showed similar performance, suggesting that the peritumoral region may contain radiomics information relevant to treatment response. A subsequent study [11] developed a tool for predicting pCR to neoadjuvant chemotherapy and immunotherapy based on MRI radiomics features, finding that it could provide significant assistance in formulating treatment plans for head and neck squamous cell carcinoma. Neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy efficacy prediction aims to achieve precise intervention in tumor diagnosis and treatment by screening sensitive populations through biomarkers, dynamically adjusting treatment plans, and quantifying prognostic risks. A study [55] employed a CT-based intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics strategy for preoperative prediction of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy efficacy in esophageal cancer. The results showed that incorporating peritumoral features could enhance predictive performance, providing new insights for improving treatment outcomes. In a study predicting the efficacy of the first transarterial chemoembolization treatment for hepatocellular carcinoma patients [56], the clinical-intratumoral-peritumoral model combining tumor growth patterns with intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics demonstrated superior predictive performance compared to single models. Braman et al. [28] utilized radiomics features from breast MRI to predict the response to HER2-targeted neoadjuvant therapy, finding that classifiers incorporating both peritumoral and intratumoral features showed correlations with targeted therapy response in two validation cohorts.

Overall, existing studies suggest that combining intratumoral and peritumoral features with clinical factors shows potential in predicting pCR and the efficacy of chemoradiotherapy. However, it is important to recognize that most current research has limitations, such as small sample sizes and a lack of external validation. Future studies need to further explore other aspects of tumor treatment response prediction, such as treatment mechanisms and intervention timing, while validating these findings through more rigorously designed multicenter studies.

Peritumoral radiomics in tumor biological mechanisms

The core value of peritumoral radiomics extends beyond aiding clinical decision-making to include providing non-invasive insights into the molecular basis of tumor biological behavior. By quantitatively extracting the heterogeneity features of the peritumoral region, researchers aim to establish its correlation with the dynamic evolution of the TME, molecular pathway regulation, and intercellular interactions, offering new perspectives for uncovering the mechanisms of tumor invasion, metastasis, and drug resistance. Zhang et al. utilized four types of two-dimensional convolutional neural networks to extract deep learning features from intratumoral and peritumoral regions in MRI sequences (T2WI, T1WI, and contrast-enhanced T1WI) to assess the identification of telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter mutations in glioblastoma patients [66]. The model constructed using stacked fusion achieved an AUC value of 0.902 in the test set. It is noteworthy that in the T2WI, enhanced T1WI, and T1WI sequences, the optimal AUC values of 0.823, 0.753, and 0.768 were achieved for peritumoral regions of 2 mm, 8 mm, and 10 mm, respectively. This suggests that the optimal peritumoral distance may vary depending on the imaging sequence but requires validation through multi-institutional studies. Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) is a key target for immune checkpoint blockade therapy in breast cancer. Wu et al. [67] developed a combined model based on preoperative DCE-MRI radiomics features, which achieved an AUC value of 0.853 in the test set, marking a significant step forward in non-invasive PD-L1 assessment and guiding precision immunotherapy for breast cancer. For ovarian cancer, a nomogram model integrating radiomics biomarkers and clinical factors achieved AUC values of 0.781 and 0.824 in predicting homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) scores and status, respectively. However, the lack of external validation limits its clinical translation [68]. The Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR) mutation status provides the basis for personalized treatment of patients, while conventional EGFR identification requires biopsy and genetic testing. Shang et al. extracted radiomics features from intratumoral CT and seven peritumoral regions to construct a model for predicting EGFR mutation status in lung adenocarcinoma [69]. The intratumoral combined with peritumoral 4 mm radiomics model achieved an AUC of 0.701 in the external test set. In another similar study [71], the EGFR prediction model constructed by combining radiomics with clinical features achieved an AUC of 0.826 in the validation set. The Vascularized Encapsulating Tumor Clusters (VETC) are formed by tumor cell clusters enveloped by endothelial cells, enabling cancer cells to enter the bloodstream in clusters for metastasis. In their study predicting VETC patterns in preoperative hepatocellular carcinoma, Yu et al. [70] found that the peritumoral model (validation set AUC of 0.972) demonstrated significantly superior predictive performance compared to the intratumoral model (validation set AUC of 0.919, P = 0.044), promoting the application of peritumoral radiomics in predicting VETC status. The immune score quantifies the tumor immune microenvironment by assessing the density of Cluster of Differentiation 3 positive (CD3-positive) and Cytotoxic T Lymphocytes (CTLs) in the tumor core and invasive margin. Researchers extracted radiomics features from the hepatobiliary phase images of contrast-enhanced MRI, and ultimately constructed a clinical-radiomics combined nomogram model that demonstrated optimal predictive performance (validation set AUC of 0.926), providing a new tool for predicting the immune score of hepatocellular carcinoma [72].

These studies have preliminarily explored the potential of peritumoral radiomics in tumor biological mechanisms, but several limitations still need to be noted. For example, the radiomics workflow involves multiple steps that may introduce variability, potentially affecting the reproducibility of results. Future research should incorporate peritumoral regions in a more standardized manner to improve model performance and explore its broader applications in tumor biology.

Challenges and future directions

In summary, peritumoral radiomics has broad applications in the field of oncology, yet most studies remain at the theoretical level and are rarely utilized in clinical practice [73]. Exploratory research on the peritumoral region is often conducted in conjunction with intratumoral analysis, reflecting the ongoing challenges in radiomics, including generalizability, prospective validation, standardization and robustness, interpretability and visualization, definition of the peritumoral region, and multi-omics integration. In response, we summarize the challenges encountered during the research process and propose future directions (Fig. 2).

Model generalization capability verification strategy

Generalizability refers to the ability of a model to maintain stability and accuracy across different devices, populations, and healthcare scenarios, and is crucial for radiomics research, serving as a “litmus test” for research reliability. The main reason for the low acceptance of radiomics research is the lack of reliable and robust external validation, which limits the generalization and clinical translation of research results [74]. A recurring challenge is that models that perform excellently in single-center studies may experience significant performance degradation in external environments, sometimes even failing to complete basic prediction tasks. This highlights the need for researchers to prioritize generalization ability during the experimental design phase.

We reviewed the literature and found that currently, multicenter studies account for only a small proportion of peritumoral radiomics research (48/141). However, it is encouraging to see that an increasing number of studies are focusing on improving model generalizability. Tables 1 and 2 summarize some multicenter peritumoral radiomics studies. By leveraging data from large-scale multicenter cohorts or open databases, such as The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA), the Lung Image Database Consortium (LIDC), and the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI), the reliability of developed models can be validated [75, 76]. As demonstrated by Hong et al., gene set enrichment and immune infiltration analyses based on transcriptomic TCIA and TCIA breast cancer datasets were employed to investigate the biological significance of axillary lymph node-related radiomics scores. Significant differences in eight RNA-based immune markers were observed between the low-score and high-score groups [77]. Large-scale multicenter cohorts require close collaboration and coordination among medical centers. Incorporating multicenter data from different countries, ethnicities, regions, and healthcare levels will more effectively validate the model’s generalization capability. Currently, large-scale data platforms represented by cBioPortal have been established, enabling researchers to perform multi-omics data visualization and interactive analysis [78–80]. Looking ahead, an increasing number of multicenter studies will bring more achievements to the field of radiomics.

The current retrospective study could further enhance the model’s generalization ability and clinical reliability through a prospective design. Although models built from retrospective studies generally achieve good performance, they are often limited by issues such as single-center data, small sample sizes, and data heterogeneity (e.g., variations in scanning parameters). The authors also explicitly pointed out these limitations and the fact that they were not prospective studies, which would result in insufficient generalization ability of the model, emphasizing the need for future supplementary studies to obtain higher-level evidence [23, 37, 50]. As the Ying team [81] mentioned in their review of breast cancer radiomics, future prospective studies should evaluate the classification accuracy and efficacy prediction performance of models using gold-standard outcomes. In summary, while retrospective analyses lay the foundation for research, prospective designs are the key pathway to addressing the current limitations of radiomics studies. Future research must recognize that even prospective studies may face implementation challenges when incorporating prospective design elements, and continuous validation remains necessary.

Standardization and reproducibility

Images from different devices, scanning protocols, reconstruction algorithms, imaging centers, and even different scanning time points may vary [82]. For example, one study systematically evaluated the robustness of radiomics features across different CT systems by using one photon-counting detector CT and four dual-energy CTs to repeatedly scan a texture phantom containing 28 different materials at three dose levels, with each scan performed twice. The results showed that the reproducibility of radiomics features across different CT systems was low, and there was significant variability in feature values between systems [83].

Another study evaluated the reproducibility of radiomics features in CT images of the same patient by varying dose levels, slice thicknesses, convolution kernels, and reconstruction algorithm settings, finding that only about 11% of the features remained stable across different parameters [84]. These differences can lead to variations in the intensity scale and texture characteristics of the images. If unprocessed radiomics features are directly input into classifiers such as machine learning models, these technical variations can interfere with the model’s ability to learn the true biological signals related to diseases, resulting in significant generalization bias during external validation or practical application. Therefore, the core objective of radiomics feature standardization is to minimize systematic differences in feature values caused by non-biological factors, ensuring comparability of imaging data from different sources and guaranteeing that subsequent feature selection, model training, and statistical analysis are based on genuine biological variations. In addition to the systematic differences addressed by standardization, there is also a significant amount of randomness and uncertainty in the process of acquiring and processing imaging data. Region of Interest (ROI) segmentation is one of the largest sources of variation, as the boundaries of ROIs drawn by different physicians or even the same physician at different times may differ. Automated segmentation algorithms also introduce uncertainty [85]. At this point, redundant features can be eliminated by calculating the consistency of the same feature values extracted based on different segmentation results. Park JE et al. constructively proposed obtaining reproducible features through various strategies, such as retesting, model studies, robust segmentation, and standardization [86]. The Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI), launched in 2020 by scholars including Alex Zwanenburg, aims to address the reproducibility issues in radiomics feature calculation. The multicenter research team reached computational consensus on 169 features, with prospective clinical validation on 51 cases of soft tissue sarcoma multimodal imaging showing reproducibility greater than 0.9 [87]. Although IBSI has initially established a standardized computational framework, providing a methodological foundation for the translation of radiomics from research to clinical applications, its applicability across a broader range of tumor types and imaging modalities still requires further validation. Currently, standardization and reproducibility remain major challenges in the field of radiomics. Building upon existing research, more effective solutions to this issue will be key to enhancing the quality of future studies.

Model interpretability

Interpretability, as a core attribute of Artificial Intelligence (AI) models, refers to the transparency of the internal logic by which a model generates predictions based on input features to human understanding. Currently, mainstream high-performance radiomics models often face barriers to clinical adoption due to their “black-box” nature—physicians find it difficult to understand why highly abstract mathematical features can drive high-precision predictions, leading to doubts about the model’s reliability.

To address the challenges posed by algorithmic opacity, the Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) technical system has emerged. Among various methods, Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) provides intuitive explanations by constructing local surrogate models: it trains a simple, interpretable model (such as linear regression) within the prediction neighborhood of a target sample to approximate the behavior of complex models. Its “model-agnostic” feature enables this technique to be widely applied across various algorithms (from traditional machine learning to deep neural networks) [88]. For example, in predicting isocitrate dehydrogenase mutations in gliomas, LIME successfully quantified the contribution of each radiomics feature, generating a top-down importance ranking to assist physicians in focusing on key imaging biomarkers [89]. The application of LIME technology in the medical field is on the rise, enhancing the interpretability of machine learning models used for diagnosis and prognosis [90]. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) has become the mainstream tool for interpretable radiomics research due to its axiomatic foundation based on Shapley values from game theory. SHAP not only quantifies the positive/negative contribution of features to individual sample predictions (visualized through swarm plots) but also reveals global feature importance patterns (ranked by weights in bar charts). However, its limitations must be cautioned: SHAP heatmaps cannot be directly mapped to image spatial positions, and computational costs increases significantly with feature dimensions [91]. These constraints may affect their practical application in clinical settings. To address the need for spatial interpretation, Class Activation Mapping (CAM) and its variants (such as gradient-weighted CAM) provide supplementary solutions. These methods generate pixel-level heatmaps to visually highlight the decision-focused regions of deep learning models for specific categories. Research has shown that this method has certain effectiveness in identifying biologically significant areas relied upon by models, such as the tumor-lung interface microstructure in spread through air spaces (STAS) prediction [92]. However, such methods depend on specific network architectures, and the resolution of heatmaps is constrained by the convolutional layers of the model, potentially making it difficult to precisely locate minute features. It is worth noting that model selection itself is a strategy for interpretability. When differences in predictive performance are limited, inherently interpretable models (such as Cox regression and logistic regression) are more readily accepted in clinical settings because they provide clear feature weights and hazard ratios. Tree-based models like random forests strike a balance between performance and interpretability through feature importance rankings.

In summary, while existing interpretability methods each have their own strengths, they also exhibit significant limitations, necessitating selection based on clinical needs. Future research on interpretability should avoid excessive pursuit of technical complexity and instead focus on addressing practical demands in clinical practice, thereby advancing the translation of radiomics from research to clinical application.

Radiomics software

Radiomics software serves as the core tool for feature extraction and analysis, and its selection can impact the reliability and comparability of research findings. Current literature reports various radiomics software, including in-house developed tools [93], open-source solutions [94, 95], freeware [96], commercial products [97], and cloud-based platforms [98]. Open-source platforms typically provide complete source code and documentation, facilitating methodological transparency and result verification. In contrast, commercial platforms prioritize user experience and technical support but often do not disclose details of core algorithms. Such differences may affect the integration and comparison of results across multi-center studies.

In peritumoral radiomics analysis, the common implementation process involves: first performing image segmentation and region expansion around the tumor using software such as 3D-Slicer and ITK-SNAP, then importing the segmentation results into tools like PyRadiomics or MaZda for feature extraction and data analysis [99]. Some studies [100, 101] have achieved one-stop segmentation, expansion, extraction, and analysis of peritumoral radiomics features by leveraging the built-in Pyradiomics packages within software like 3D-Slicer and ITK-SNAP, which to some extent streamlines the workflow.

Differences in algorithm implementation, feature calculation methods, and user interfaces among various software packages are significant factors affecting the reproducibility of research. These differences manifest specifically in: (1) variations in image preprocessing algorithms (e.g., resampling, intensity discretization) [102]; (2) settings for feature calculation [103]; and (3) the extent of support for filters and feature categories [104]. Such technical discrepancies may lead to non-negligible variations in extracted feature values even when processing the same set of image data across different software. To address these challenges, the IBSI has clarified the mathematical definitions and computational steps for various features [87] and established a standardized filter reference system [105], providing crucial guidance for both software developers and users. Platforms adhering to IBSI standards can improve the consistency and reproducibility of feature calculations, though some individual features may still exhibit poor consistency across platforms [106]. A study [102] systematically evaluated the performance of seven radiomics software packages on both the IBSI standard phantom and a custom ImSURE phantom. The results revealed that even when software claims compliance with IBSI standards, implementation differences in image preprocessing (e.g., interpolation, intensity discretization) and feature aggregation methods still lead to significant feature variations. Particularly in multicenter radiomics studies, such inter-software variability is most likely to obscure true biological signals, resulting in difficulties in reproducing research and generating unreliable findings.

It is particularly noteworthy that, to our knowledge, no study has systematically explored the impact of different software on the heterogeneity of peritumoral radiomics features, nor is there sufficient evidence evaluating the consistency of peritumoral features across IBSI-compliant platforms. Therefore, IBSI should establish a more comprehensive quality control system in the future to ensure consistent calculation of features (including deep learning features) across different platforms and centers. Future software platforms should enhance specialized support for peritumoral region analysis and develop intelligent recognition functions based on anatomical and biological characteristics. Additionally, more studies are needed to directly compare the performance of different software in extracting peritumoral features, providing empirical evidence for tool selection. These efforts will help facilitate the translation of peritumoral radiomics research into clinical practice.

Peritumoral region definitions

The definition of the peritumoral region is the primary task in peritumoral radiomics research, directly determining whether the peritumoral area can provide incremental value to predictive models. It should be noted that, however, that there are no universally accepted, biologically or imaging-based criteria for the precise definition of the peritumoral region. Definitions vary significantly across studies, such as fixed-distance expansion (1 mm, 3 mm, 5 mm, 10 mm, 15 mm, 20 mm), approaches based on edema zones [107] or vascular distribution [108], or heterogeneous habitat (Habitat Imaging) analysis [45, 109, 110]. This discrepancy makes it difficult to directly compare and integrate research findings, posing a significant methodological challenge in the current field. It is noteworthy that the peritumoral region is not a static entity—its extent, composition, and imaging features may dynamically evolve during tumor progression and treatment, implying that fixed definitions may fail to capture this spatiotemporal heterogeneity [2, 111]. From a technical perspective, manually delineating peritumoral ROIs suffers from high workload, strong subjectivity, and poor reproducibility. Although automatic segmentation algorithms can improve efficiency, their accuracy and robustness still need enhancement, given the blurred boundaries and complex structures characteristic of tumor peripheries.

To address the aforementioned challenges, it is necessary to explore the association strength between different peritumoral distance ranges and key clinical outcomes or specific biological markers based on large-scale multicenter data and multi-omics/pathological correlation analyses. This will help recommend “biologically relevant” peritumoral region ranges for different tumor types. For example, in a study by Shang et al. on predicting EGFR mutation status in lung adenocarcinoma patients using peritumoral radiomics, the optimal peritumoral range was validated to be 4 mm [69]. Meanwhile, a stratified definition strategy (e.g., near-peritumoral 0–5 mm, far-peritumoral 5–10 mm) can further reveal the gradient characteristics of the peritumoral microenvironment [28]. More importantly, the limitations of fixed-distance extension should be transcended to radiomics habitat analysis. The core advantage of this method lies in not predefining fixed boundaries but instead performing unsupervised clustering based on the heterogeneity of imaging features within and around the tumor, identifying subregions with distinct biological characteristics. It then analyzes the spatial distribution of these habitats—particularly at the tumor-peritumoral interface—and their relationship with clinical/biological indicators [112]. This approach is inherently more aligned with the biological heterogeneity of the peritumoral microenvironment. Ultimately, it should be emphasized that international collaboration is called for to develop minimum reporting standards and recommended definition methods for peritumoral radiomics studies for specific tumor types, which is an effective way to promote universalization and application translation of study results. Future research should focus on addressing the standardization of definitions, while recognizing that different tumor types may require distinct definition strategies. Progress in this area will directly impact the clinical translation potential of peritumoral radiomics.

Multi-omics integration

Multi-omics integration aims to combine multidimensional data such as radiomics, genomics, transcriptomics, pathomics, and proteomics to provide a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the core mechanisms driving tumor development and progression, thereby advancing personalized medicine.

Current progress indicates that the integration of radiomics with multi-omics has demonstrated the potential to overcome the limitations of single-omics approaches [113–115]. Radiogenomics research primarily focuses on predicting and correlating known biological features. It can predict gene mutation status through radiomics [116–118] and also be used to decode the underlying biological mechanisms of newly discovered imaging biomarkers [119].Radiomics-transcriptomics studies investigate the correlation between imaging features extracted from medical images and gene expression profiles. Recent research has explored the combined application of radiomics and transcriptomics. For instance, Tixier et al. [120] demonstrated that Fluorodeoxyglucose Positron Emission Tomography (FDG-PET) radiomics features can be used to infer gene expression and cellular pathway activity in tumor tissues by analyzing FDG-PET image features and transcriptomic data from patients with locally advanced head and neck cancer. Pathomics is an innovative interdisciplinary field that focuses on transforming traditional histopathological slides into high-resolution digital images, from which quantitative morphological, textural, spatial, and cellular or tissue-level features beyond human visual recognition are extracted. These features are then integrated with other omics data and clinical information for various disease prediction tasks [121]. Proteomics aims to identify proteins and their post-translational modifications, functions, and protein-protein interactions using two-dimensional chromatography (2D-LC) and two-dimensional liquid chromatography/tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) techniques [122]. In addition, further integration of multi-dimensional omics data such as metabolomics, spatial omics, and immunomics is expected to build more accurate decision-making models. A typical case is the study by Su et al., who integrated multicenter breast cancer DCE-MRI radiomics, whole-exome sequencing, transcriptomics, metabolomics, and digital pathology data to propose a precision treatment strategy targeting ferroptosis in high intratumoral heterogeneity. For the first time, they established a closed-loop correlation between imaging-based intratumoral heterogeneity phenotypes and multi-omics mechanisms [123].

However, multi-omics integration research also faces challenges [124]. Different omics data (with hundreds to thousands of imaging features and tens of thousands of gene expressions) exhibit significant differences in scale, type, and dimensionality. Effectively integrating high-dimensional heterogeneous data and extracting biologically meaningful information presents substantial technical challenges, often leading to overfitting or information redundancy. Developing fusion algorithms that effectively capture the complex nonlinear relationships among multi-omics data while maintaining interpretability and robustness is highly challenging, and the resulting models are often difficult to interpret. Obtaining paired, high-quality imaging and multi-omics data (especially spatial omics) is costly, which limits large-scale cohort studies. Obtaining paired, high-quality imaging and multi-omics data (especially spatialomics) is costly and limits large-scale cohort studies. Future related studies should focus on solving these existing outstanding problems, achieving the deep integration of multi-omics and multi-dimensional data, and exploring the path of personalized tumor diagnosis and treatment.

Limitations

This review also has certain limitations. First, it failed to comprehensively assess the risk of bias in the included studies, which may lead to overly optimistic or misleading conclusions. Future research should employ standardized bias risk assessment tools to systematically evaluate the quality of included studies and test the sensitivity of conclusions to bias, thereby enhancing the reliability of the evidence. Second, relevant literature was only retrieved from PubMed, resulting in incomplete database coverage and a lack of prospective clinical trial data to support real-world clinical impact. This may limit the comprehensiveness and translational value of the evidence. Future studies should expand the scope of literature searches to cover multiple databases and include prospective clinical trials and registry studies to strengthen the generalizability and clinical relevance of the findings. Third, inconsistencies in the definitions of peritumoral regions, imaging protocols, and feature extraction tools among the included studies may limit the comparability of conclusions. Future research should focus on screening and comparing consistent aspects across studies to draw more definitive conclusions. Fourth, most studies did not validate mechanisms through pathological controls, resulting in weak associations between imaging features and the tumor microenvironment. Future research could include studies that directly compare imaging features with histopathological results to strengthen the empirical foundation of imaging-biology correlations.

Inclusion

This review systematically summarizes the research progress in peritumoral radiomics and analyzes its potential value in clinical oncology practice and biological mechanism studies. Current evidence suggests that peritumoral radiomics may provide incremental information beyond traditional intratumoral analysis in specific applications, even demonstrating relative advantages. However, it is important to recognize that this field still faces multiple obstacles on its path toward clinical translation, and current models require further optimization and refinement. We offer several practical recommendations to facilitate the translation of peritumoral radiomics research findings into clinical applications.
