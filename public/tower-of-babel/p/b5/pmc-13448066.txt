Unintended tripping of healthy parallel lines during single-line-to-ground faults: field evidence and mitigation
Authors: Farhad Aghajanian Sabagh, Farzad Hajimohammadi, Bahador Fani, Iman Sadeghkhani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDistance relays may maloperate under various conditions such as improper settings, measurement distortion, or abnormal network operating states. This article investigates an overlooked mechanism leading to distance relay maloperation in parallel sub-transmission circuits containing underground cable sections. During a single-phase-to-ground fault on one circuit, a portion of the fault current may return through the neutrals of receiving-end transformers and flow back toward the source through the parallel healthy circuit. This reverse fault current component can become aligned with the load current of a healthy phase, resulting in a significant reduction of the apparent impedance measured by the distance relay. Consequently, the impedance trajectory may enter the relay operating characteristic from the load region, causing undesired zone operation and tripping of the healthy line. Such a misoperation may lead to simultaneous disconnection of both parallel circuits and severe downstream outages, despite the fault being confined to only one line. The mechanism is analytically explained and validated using disturbance records from a real 63 kV network. In addition, DIgSILENT PowerFactory simulations are conducted to reproduce the observed behavior and confirm the current amplification. Key factors influencing the severity of the phenomenon, including cable shield grounding conditions and fault proximity to the receiving-end substation, are also discussed. Finally, practical countermeasures are proposed to improve protection security and prevent unnecessary tripping.Keywords: Cable-overhead lines, Distance relay maloperation, Parallel sub-transmission lines, Protection coordination, Reverse fault current effectSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Distance relays are widely employed in transmission and sub-transmission networks due to their fast response and reliable fault-clearing capability. These relays operate based on impedance measurement, where the apparent impedance is determined from the ratio of the measured voltage to current at the relay location. When the calculated impedance falls within a predefined protection zone, the relay issues a trip command to isolate the faulty section of the network. Owing to their inherent selectivity and high operating speed, distance relays remain one of the most effective protection schemes for high-voltage and sub-transmission lines1–4.

Despite their widespread application, distance relays may suffer from maloperation under various system disturbances and non-ideal operating conditions. Such maloperations may result in either unnecessary tripping of healthy lines or failure to trip for internal faults, both of which can significantly jeopardize power system security. Several well-known phenomena can distort the apparent impedance measured by distance relays, including power swings, load encroachment, voltage instability, and the presence of fault resistance. Power swings, caused by major disturbances such as faults or sudden changes in network configuration, may lead to oscillations in voltage and current phasors. These oscillations can shift the apparent impedance trajectory toward the relay characteristic and potentially cause undesired operation, including tripping of adjacent healthy lines and cascading outages5–7. Load encroachment is another critical issue, where heavy loading conditions reduce the apparent impedance seen by the relay, forcing the load impedance into the relay operating zone8–11. Furthermore, voltage instability conditions, typically associated with inadequate reactive power support or excessive loading, can influence voltage profiles and consequently alter relay impedance calculations12–14. Fault resistance also plays an important role, as it may lead to relay underreach for internal faults or overreach for external faults if not properly accounted for in relay setting calculations15–17. On the other hand, several advanced protection and fault location algorithms have been proposed for double-circuit transmission lines, including methods based on modal currents18, transverse differential protection19, time-domain distance protection for mixed overhead-cable lines20, source-agnostic protection for lines connected to power-electronics-based sources21, and optimization-based fault location for untransposed lines22. These works primarily focus on algorithmic innovations and are validated through simulations or hardware-in-the-loop tests.

In addition to these commonly addressed phenomena, a specific and less-discussed mechanism may arise in parallel sub-transmission lines, particularly when cable sections are involved. During a single-phase-to-ground fault in one of the parallel circuits, a phenomenon referred to as the reverse fault current effect may occur in the healthy line23. In this condition, a portion of the fault current returns through the neutrals of transformers located at downstream substations and flows back toward the source through the parallel circuits. The magnitude of this reverse current contribution becomes more pronounced as the fault location approaches the destination substation, due to the increased share of the fault current passing through the transformer HV-side neutrals. Since single-phase-to-ground fault currents are generally lagging and return from the ground to the source with an approximately 180-degree phase shift relative to the original fault current, the angle of this reverse fault current closely aligns with the load current angle and the voltage angle of one of the healthy phases, while it leads the faulted-phase voltage. For example, if the fault occurs on phase a of the faulted circuit, the reverse fault current may align with phase c of the healthy circuit. Consequently, this current adds to the pre-fault load current of the affected healthy phase, significantly increasing its magnitude and reducing the apparent impedance calculated by the distance relay. Since the resultant current angle becomes aligned with the load current angle, the measured impedance trajectory may enter the relay operating characteristic from the load region along the resistive axis, leading to undesired zone operation and maloperation of the distance relay on the healthy line24. It should be emphasized that both the magnitude and phase angle of the load current can either intensify or mitigate the severity of this phenomenon. Moreover, it should be noted that the reverse fault current is fundamentally different from zero-sequence mutual coupling and induced currents in parallel circuits where the undesired current in the healthy circuit is induced while the reverse fault current effect does not arise from induction.

This article investigates the reverse fault current effect and its contribution to distance relay maloperation in parallel sub-transmission cable lines. The mechanism of the phenomenon is explained in detail and validated through a real case study in an actual power network, supported by disturbance records and relay event data. Moreover, the conditions that amplify or reduce the intensity of this effect are analyzed, and simulation studies are carried out using DIgSILENT PowerFactory to confirm the observed behavior. Finally, practical mitigation strategies are proposed to reduce the risk of distance relay maloperation and improve the reliability of protection systems in parallel overhead-cable sub-transmission networks.

Field case study: maloperation mechanism in parallel sub-transmission lines

In this section, a field case study is presented to clarify the operating mechanism of the reverse fault current effect and its impact on distance relay performance in parallel sub-transmission lines. Fig. 1 shows the single-line diagram (SLD) of the studied 63 kV network, where two parallel circuits, denoted as L1 and L2, connect substation A (source substation) to substation B (receiving substation). Due to right-of-way limitations, both circuits include underground cable sections. In the SLD, dashed lines represent the cable segments, while solid lines represent overhead line sections. At substation B, two transformers (T1 and T2) with a 63/20 kV rating and Ynd1 vector group are connected to the 63 kV bus.

Single-line diagram of the studied network under normal operating conditions.

During the disturbance shown in Fig. 2, a single-phase-to-ground fault occurred on the cable section of line L1, close to substation B (approximately 3.8 km from substation B). Following the fault inception, the distance relays of the faulted line (L1) operated correctly at both substations A and B, and line L1 was tripped by the operation of the phase-to-ground zone-1 element (Dis-Z1-AN). However, simultaneously, the healthy parallel line (L2) was incorrectly tripped at substation A due to maloperation of the phase-to-ground distance element (Dis-Z1-CN), which resulted in a complete outage at substation B. Since the fault was confined to line L1, only the faulted circuit should have been isolated, while the downstream substation should have remained energized through line L2. Therefore, the undesired tripping of L2 represents a critical protection misoperation with severe operational consequences.

The maloperation of the distance relay on the healthy circuit is attributed to the reverse fault current effect, which causes an abnormal increase in the current of one healthy phase and consequently a significant reduction in the apparent impedance measured by the relay. To explain this mechanism, the pre-fault load currents of both lines at substation A are shown in Fig. 3. For simplicity, the load is assumed to be predominantly resistive. After the occurrence of a phase-a-to-ground fault on line L1, the phase a current of the faulted circuit increases significantly. Due to the inductive-resistive nature of the network and the positive-sequence impedance of the line, the fault current exhibits a lagging phase angle, as shown in Fig. 4. Fig. 5 shows that the fault current reaches the fault location through two main paths. Path 1 corresponds to the direct contribution from substation A through the faulted line L1. Path 2 corresponds to an indirect contribution from substation A through the healthy line L2 and the transformers (T1 and T2) at substation B, which inject current into the fault point. Since the impedance of Path 2 is considerably higher than that of Path 1, the majority of the fault current initially flows through Path 1, prior to the isolation of the faulted line by protective relays. It should be noted that this phenomenon is not effectively detected by the power swing blocking (PSB) function of distance relays. PSB schemes typically operate based on the rate of impedance movement and are designed to distinguish slow impedance trajectories caused by power swings from fast trajectories associated with faults. In the studied event, the reverse fault current effect causes a sudden decrease in the apparent impedance of the healthy phase during the fault, resulting in a rapid impedance movement that is similar to fault behavior. Therefore, the impedance trajectory may enter the relay operating zone without being blocked by PSB logic. Moreover, it should be noted that while the mutual coupling can lead to distance relay underreach or overreach, this effect has not been explicitly factored into the analysis of the presented phenomenon in this article. The primary mechanism discussed herein is the reverse fault current returning through the receiving-end substation transformer neutral. This specific path is independent of, and not directly influenced by, the parallel line current or the mutual coupling between the two lines themselves.

Sensitivity of the reverse fault current effect to network parameters

The severity of the reverse fault current effect depends strongly on the available zero-sequence return paths and the electrical conditions of the parallel circuits during a single-phase-to-ground fault. In practice, the magnitude and phase angle of the reverse fault current component are influenced by several parameters, including fault location, grounding conditions and cable sheath bonding integrity. This section discusses the most critical factors that can amplify the reverse fault current effect and consequently increase the probability of distance relay maloperation.

Effect of cable shield grounding integrity

In cable-based transmission and sub-transmission lines, the cable metallic sheath (shield) plays an essential role in providing a low-impedance return path for ground fault currents. Depending on the cable length, bonding method, and number of joints, the shield may be grounded at one or multiple points25. In normal operating conditions, the cable shields are electrically continuous along the route and properly bonded at joint locations.

Under healthy conditions with intact and continuous shielding, a phase-to-ground fault establishes a conductive path between the cable conductor and the metallic sheath. As a result, a significant portion of the ground fault current returns to the source through the cable shield, thereby limiting the amount of fault current that is forced to flow through the neutrals of downstream transformers and through the phase conductors of adjacent parallel circuits. As shown in Fig. 12a, when the shield is continuous, the fault current divides between two main return paths: (i) a direct return path through the cable sheath toward the source substation, and (ii) an indirect return path through the receiving-end grounding system and transformer neutrals. In this case, the reverse fault current contribution through the parallel healthy line remains limited. However, if the continuity of the cable shield is disrupted due to improper bonding, damaged joints, or grounding failures, the sheath return path becomes ineffective. Consequently, a larger share of the fault current is forced to return through the receiving-end transformer neutrals and subsequently through the phase conductors of the parallel circuits, as shown in Fig. 12b. This condition significantly increases the magnitude of the reverse fault current component flowing in the healthy line, thereby intensifying the reduction of the apparent impedance seen by the distance relay and increasing the risk of maloperation.

Ground fault current return paths in a cable section. (a) continuous and properly bonded cable shield, and (b) discontinuous shield causing increased neutral return current and amplified reverse fault current effect.

Effect of fault location along the parallel line

Fault location is another dominant factor influencing the intensity of the reverse fault current effect. It is evident that as the fault point approaches the destination (receiving) substation, a larger portion of the single-phase-to-ground fault current is likely to return through the grounded neutrals of transformers located at that substation. This increases the reverse fault current component flowing back toward the source through the parallel circuits. Therefore, faults occurring close to the receiving-end substation produce a more severe reverse fault current effect compared to faults occurring near the sending-end substation. In such cases, the increased reverse current contribution in the healthy circuit leads to a more pronounced reduction in the apparent phase-to-ground impedance measured by the distance relay. As a result, the impedance trajectory may enter the relay operating characteristic, increasing the likelihood of undesired zone operation.

Relay records and field evidence of the reverse fault current effect

This section presents field evidence extracted from protection relay disturbance records, confirming the reverse fault current effect and its impact on distance relay performance. The distance relay used in the reported event is a numerical Micom-P139 device operating with a quadrilateral characteristic and fully compliant with IEC 60255 requirements. The relevant protection settings are shown in Fig. 13. In the reported incident, a single-phase-to-ground fault occurred on phase a of a 63 kV two-circuit sub-transmission overhead-cable line. Although the faulted circuit was correctly isolated by the distance protection, the adjacent healthy circuit was simultaneously tripped due to an undesired distance relay operation, resulting in a complete outage at the downstream substation. It should be emphasized that the system configuration, fault location, line composition (cable-overhead), transformer characteristics, and network single-line diagram are identical to the case study introduced in Section Field case study: maloperation mechanism in parallel sub-transmission lines. Simulation-based validation of the reverse fault current effect

To further validate the reverse fault current effect and support the field observations reported in Section Relay records and field evidence of the reverse fault current effect, a representative parallel sub-transmission network was modelled in DIgSILENT PowerFactory. It is important to clarify that the DIgSILENT simulations presented in this section are intended primarily to confirm the existence and qualitative behavior of the reverse-fault-current mechanism, not to reproduce a specific recorded event or to perform a full transient study. Nevertheless, to ensure technical relevance, all key parameters influencing the steady-state zero-sequence paths–such as network topology, transformer vector groups, and grounding configurations–were modeled consistent with the real system. The simulation results confirm that, under a single-phase-to-ground fault condition, the fault current contribution returning through the receiving-end transformer neutrals can significantly distort the apparent impedance measured by the distance relay of the adjacent healthy circuit.

In the simulated scenario, a single-phase-to-ground fault was applied on phase a of one of the parallel circuits. Practical mitigation measures for reverse fault current-induced maloperation

Since the reverse fault current effect may lead to undesired distance relay operation on healthy parallel circuits and consequently expand the outage area, it is essential to implement practical mitigation measures. Based on field observations and engineering considerations, the following approaches can be adopted to reduce the risk of relay maloperation:

The main advantages and practical limitations of each solution are discussed in the following subsections.

Restoring cable shield continuity and grounding integrity

Loss of cable shield continuity, particularly at joint locations, can significantly alter the return path of single-phase-to-ground fault currents. Under such conditions, a larger portion of the fault current may return through the destination substation transformer neutrals and subsequently flow back toward the source via the parallel circuits. This increases the magnitude of the reverse fault current component and therefore amplifies the probability of distance relay maloperation on the healthy line.

Accordingly, the continuity and grounding integrity of the cable shield should be periodically inspected, with special attention to cable joints, terminations, and bonding points. Any discontinuity, corrosion, or defective bonding connection should be repaired to ensure that the majority of the fault current returns through the intended shield path, thereby reducing the neutral return contribution and minimizing the reverse fault current magnitude. It should be noted that repairing shield discontinuities is often associated with practical challenges, since it may require de-energizing the cable section, opening joints, replacing damaged segments, and installing new joint kits. Depending on the number and location of defective points, this solution can be time-consuming and costly. However, it remains one of the most effective methods for mitigating the root cause of the reverse fault current amplification in cable-overhead lines.

Limiting the resistive reach of the distance relay characteristic

In overhead-cable line configurations, the calculated resistive reach (R-reach) of distance relay zones is often relatively large due to the higher resistance contribution of cable sections. However, the reverse fault current phenomenon causes the apparent impedance trajectory of the healthy circuit to approach the relay operating region from the load side, mainly along the resistive axis. Therefore, reducing the resistive coverage of zone 1 (and potentially zone 2) can significantly decrease the likelihood of load-side impedance encroachment entering the operating characteristic during the fault. In practice, this can be achieved by restricting the relay characteristic on the R-axis while maintaining adequate reach along the reactance axis for internal fault coverage.

The main advantage of this approach is its simplicity and practicality: it only requires relay setting adjustment and can be implemented rapidly without requiring an outage or additional hardware investment. However, the limitation of this method is that excessive reduction of the resistive reach may reduce sensitivity to high-resistance internal faults, particularly in cable sections where fault resistance can be considerable.

Application of line differential protection

A robust solution to prevent undesired tripping caused by impedance mismeasurement is the application of line differential protection, particularly for parallel overhead-cable lines and critical sub-transmission circuits. Since differential protection is a unit-based scheme relying on current comparison between both ends of the protected line, it is inherently immune to the apparent impedance reduction phenomenon associated with reverse fault current contributions. In other words, although the reverse fault current effect may distort the impedance seen by distance relays on the healthy circuit, it does not create a false internal differential current condition for the healthy line. Consequently, line differential protection can provide secure operation and eliminate the risk of maloperation observed in this phenomenon.

Nevertheless, the implementation of line differential relays requires reliable communication infrastructure (typically fiber-optic links), additional equipment investment, and often scheduled outages for installation and commissioning. Therefore, this solution is usually more suitable for high-priority lines where the economic impact of unnecessary outages justifies the associated costs.

Conclusion

Parallel sub-transmission lines, particularly those including underground cable sections, are widely used to ensure supply continuity for downstream substations. Under normal protection philosophy, a single-phase-to-ground fault on one circuit should be cleared by isolating only the faulted line, while the adjacent healthy circuit remains in service to maintain load supply. However, this article demonstrates that in parallel overhead-cable configurations, a less-recognized phenomenon referred to as the reverse fault current effect may lead to undesired distance relay operation on the healthy circuit. During a single-phase-to-ground fault, a portion of the fault current may return to the source through the neutrals of transformers at the receiving-end substation and subsequently flow back through the parallel circuit. This reverse current component can become aligned with the load current and voltage phasors of one of the healthy phases. As a result, the apparent impedance measured by the distance relay of the healthy line may suddenly decrease and enter the relay operating characteristic from the load region along the resistive axis, leading to zone 1 pickup and unintended tripping.

The practical consequences of this phenomenon are significant, as it may cause the simultaneous disconnection of both parallel circuits and lead to complete outages at downstream substations. The mechanism was validated using real disturbance records obtained from protective relays in an actual 63 kV network, where a healthy line tripped incorrectly during a single-line-to-ground fault on the adjacent circuit. In addition, DIgSILENT PowerFactory simulations reproduced the same behavior and confirmed the strong amplification of the healthy phase current due to the reverse fault current contribution. Finally, practical countermeasures were discussed, including restoration of cable shield grounding continuity, limiting the resistive reach of distance relay zones to prevent load-side encroachment, and applying line differential protection for critical overhead-cable lines. These measures can substantially improve protection security and reduce the risk of cascading outages caused by reverse fault current-induced maloperation. Extending the proposed analysis to ‘stitched lines’-a configuration where two circuits are connected at the beginning and end of the route and enter the high-voltage substations by a single line-can be considered as future work. Moreover, a comprehensive analysis of using line differential protection represents a valuable direction for future research.
