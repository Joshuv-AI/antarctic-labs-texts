Interleukin-6 is associated with high-risk plaque on coronary computed tomography angiography in asymptomatic women
Authors: Nishant Vatsa, Raymundo A. Quintana, Phillipp Knebel Doeberitz, Carlotta Onnis, Sonika Vatsa, Noah Newman, Tanveer Kauser, Alireza Rahbar, Daniel A. Gold, Vardhmaan Jain, Matthew E. ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary high-risk plaque (HRP) is associated with inflammation and adverse outcomes in symptomatic individuals with coronary artery disease (CAD), yet determinants of HRP in asymptomatic individuals without known CAD remain ambiguous.ObjectivesWe examined the association between interleukin 6 (IL-6) levels and HRP in asymptomatic women without known CAD and hypothesized higher IL-6 levels will be associated with HRP.MethodsWe measured serum IL-6 levels and performed Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography (CCTA) in 77 women without known CAD enrolled in the Atlanta Women’s Interagency HIV Study (median age 54, 92.2% Black, 67.5% living with HIV). Those with spotty calcification, napkin ring sign, low attenuation, or positive remodeling coronary plaque features on visual or semi-automated analyses were categorized as having HRP. We used logistic regression models adjusted for clinical and sociodemographic risk factors or the pooled cohort equation (PCE).ResultsThe median PCE score was 5.4%, the median IL-6 level was 2.18 [1.33, 3.59] pg/mL, 51.9% had coronary plaque and 38.9% had HRP on CCTA. Women with IL-6 concentrations in the top quartile (IL-6 > 3.59 pg/mL) were more likely to have HRP than those in lower quartiles (63.2%¦vs. 31%, p=0.01). IL-6 levels were associated with HRP prevalence, independent of cardiovascular risk factors (OR [95% CI]: 1.43 [1.12, 1.92], p=0.008) or PCE score (OR: 1.36 [1.09, 1.77], p=0.01) or HIV status.ConclusionIn asymptomatic, predominately Black women, IL-6 mediated inflammation is associated with a greater likelihood of the presence of HRP.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Despite advances in therapies for CAD, there is substantial residual risk even after guideline-based management [2,3]. With the advent of new imaging techniques, there is growing evidence that this residual risk is partially attributed to the presence of high-risk coronary plaque (HRP) – defined qualitatively as coronary atherosclerotic plaque with a napkin ring sign, positive remolding, low attenuation, or spotty calcification [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Post-hoc analysis of the Prospective Multicenter Imaging Study for Evaluation of Chest Pain (PROMISE) trial found the risk of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) in symptomatic patients with HRP to be 1.7 times higher than in those without HRP, with HRP-associated risk for MACE being most pronounced in women and those with non-obstructive CAD [4,8].

Despite an increasing understanding of HRP-related cardiovascular risk in women, determinants of HRP have yet to be fully illuminated in women. Progression of atherogenesis is believed to be due to exposure to conventional CAD risk factors and is further potentiated by inflammation. Elevated circulating levels of inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin 6 (IL-6), are independent predictors of MACE [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Most studies that have established an association between IL-6 levels and HRP features were performed in symptomatic cohorts of men and women [14,15]. A sub-study of the Randomized Trial to Prevent Vascular Events (REPRIEVE) trial, recently established an association between IL-6 levels and HRP in an asymptomatic cohort living with HIV that was predominately male and white [16]. However, the relationship between inflammation and HRP in women asymptomatic for CAD, especially those who identify as Black, has not been completely elucidated. Establishing a connection between inflammatory biomarkers and HRP could help further risk stratify this group, whose risk for CAD is poorly estimated by existing risk calculators and has elevated HRP-associated risk [4,8,17,18].

In this study, we investigated the association between inflammation, estimated by serum IL-6 concentrations, and the presence of HRP in a predominately Black cohort of asymptomatic women with and without HIV enrolled from the Atlanta Women’s Interagency HIV Study (Atlanta WIHS) [19]. Because this cohort has a high prevalence of HIV, which is a risk-enhancing factor and has been associated with non-calcified plaque, we also examined how HIV affected the relationship between inflammation and HRP [[20], [21], [22]]. We hypothesized that higher IL-6 levels will be independently associated with greater HRP prevalence.

Methods

Study design

We leveraged data from the Bone, Brain, and Heart (B/B/H) cohort, derived from the Atlanta WIHS cohort. This cohort included women ≥ 30 years living with well-controlled HIV, defined as self-reported antiretroviral therapy for ≥ 2 years with HIV RNA of <50 copies/mL, and a matched group of women without HIV, who were enrolled from the Atlanta-metropolitan area clinics between 2019 and 2023. Those who were pregnant or breastfeeding, non-ambulatory, had a history of organ transplantation, bone disorders, autoimmune disorders, cancer, chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease, CAD, acute coronary syndrome history, heart failure, or stroke were excluded from this cohort [23].

We collected participants' vitals, anthropometric measurements, medication history, comprehensive cardiovascular medical history, and blood samples. Demographics, clinical risk factors history, and social determinants of health variables, including age, race, history of hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, statin use, current smoking status, income, marital status, and education level, were self-reported by participants in interviews and confirmed with medical record review. Lipid profiles and creatinine concentrations were obtained from blood samples. Within six months of B/B/H entry visits, participants were required to complete coronary computed tomography angiograms (CCTA) and non-contrast gated chest CT.

IL-6 and hs-CRP measurements

Blood samples were collected by venipuncture into serum separation tubes (SST) (BD Vacutainer®; Becton, Dickinson and Company, Franklin Lakes, NJ). SST tubes were left at room temperature to clot for 30 min and centrifuged at 1300 X g for 10 min in a swinging bucket centrifuge. Following centrifugation, samples in SST tubes were aliquoted and stored at −80 °C. IL-6 measurements were performed in plasma samples using the Quantikine IL-6 high-sensitivity immunoassay kit (R&D systems, Minneapolis, MN). Samples were run in duplicates with appropriate controls following the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured using a colorimetric plate reader from Molecular Devices (San Jose, CA). Inter-assay variability in IL-6 measurements was between 2 and 4 %.

Hs-CRP measurements were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions on serum samples in duplicates using a commercial high-sensitive CRP ELISA assay (Eagle Biosciences, Amherst, NH). The kit employs a monoclonal antibody to human CRP immobilized on a microtiter plate to bind the human CRP in the sample or standard. Absorbance was measured using a colorimetric plate reader from Molecular Devices (San Jose, CA). The average inter-assay variability of hs-CRP measurements was 6 %. Results were reported as mg/L. Samples with CRP concentration less than 0.1 mg/L were reported as <0.1mg/L; samples with values greater than ten were reported as >10mg/L.

CCTA protocol and high-risk plaque measurement

Patients underwent CCTA using the Siemens Somatom Force, Somatom Definition AS, or a Somatom Definition Flash at two Emory-affiliated sites. Based on the participants' body habitus, scanner tube voltage ranged from a peak of 80 to 120 kVp with currents between 320 and 580mAs. During studies, participants’ heart rates were maintained below 70 beats per minute by administering oral or intravenous metoprolol at the discretion of a supervising physician. Reconstructed CCTA images were analyzed by two independent cardiovascular imaging specialists blinded to patient characteristics. Coronary HRP features – spotty calcification, napkin ring sign, and positive remodeling – were visually identified. After manually defining the centerline coronary tree, we utilized Elucid (VascuCAP, Wenham, MA), an AI-enhanced software, to detect low attenuation plaque (LAP) plaque (<45 Hounsfield Units) [24]. The inter-reader variability in LAP measurements had a Pearson’s coefficient of 0.96 [25]. We categorized those with any plaque (high-risk, non-calcified, mixed, or calcified plaque) on visual or Elucid CCTA assessments as having coronary plaque. We grouped those with spotty calcification, napkin ring sign, positive remodeling, or low attenuation plaque features as prevalent HRP. We categorized those without high-risk coronary plaque features as having no HRP.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as proportions, and continuous variables as means (SD) or medians [IQR]. Kruskal Wallis tests, one-way ANOVA tests, Wilcoxon rank sum tests, and two-sample t-tests were used to compare continuous variables, as appropriate, and chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables. We compared IL-6 concentrations in those with and without HRP and HRP prevalence across IL-6 concentration quartiles to measure the crude association between IL-6 and HRP prevalence. HRP prevalence was regressed on IL-6 or hs-CRP serum concentrations in univariate and multivariate logistic models with relevant covariates added in a stepwise manner to assess adjusted associations between HRP and these biomarkers. Model 1 was unadjusted with IL-6 or hs-CRP serum concentration as independent variables. Model 2 adjusted for age and race. Model 3 adjusted for Model 2 variables and clinical factors, including HIV status, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, current smoking status, body mass index, statin use, and serum creatinine as a surrogate of kidney function. Model 4 included Model 3 variables and social determinants of health variables, including income, marital status, and educational attainment. Because of the high prevalence of HIV in our cohort, we also examined how HIV modified the association between IL-6 and HRP by assessing the effect of the interaction between HIV and IL-6 on HRP prevalence through a multivariate model adjusted for the above demographics, clinical risk factors, and social determinants of health. All statistical analyses were performed using R.2.3 (Vienna, Austria).

Since the atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) 10-year risk score calculated by the pooled cohort equation (PCE) is a commonly used clinical tool for cardiac risk stratification, we calculated PCE scores for participants in our sample using lipid profile, hypertension history, smoking history, sex, race, and hypertension medication history data. Those with a risk score below 5 % were considered low risk, those with risk scores 5 % to 7.5 % were deemed borderline, those with a risk score between 7.5 and 20 % were considered intermediate risk, and those with a risk score above 20 % were regarded as high risk [26]. We measured the association between PCE scores and HRP prevalence in a univariate logistic regression model. We also assessed whether inflammatory cytokine’s association with HRP was independent of this clinical tool through model 5, which was adjusted for the PCE score. We compared model 5 to the PCE univariate model utilizing the likelihood ratio test and assessed both these models’ discrimination of prevalent HRP by calculating these two models’ c-statistics.

Results

Ninety-three women completed CCTA of whom 77 had serum IL-6 concentrations measured (Fig. 1) [23]. The median age of the 77 enrolled women was 54 [49, 58.8] years, more than two-thirds (67.5 %) were living with HIV, and 92.2 % were Black. Mean BMI (SD) was 34.1 (7.4) kg/m2, 48.1 % smoked, and 54.6 % had hypertension with a median PCE score of 5.4 % [2.43, 10.9]. Median levels of IL-6 and hs-CRP were 2.2 [1.3, 3.6] pg/mL and 4.9 [2.2, 10.1] mg/L, respectively. A vast majority of women had an annual income of less than $24,000 (81.3 %). Of the total, 40 women (51.9 %) had coronary plaque and 30 (38.9 %) had HRP features. Low attenuation plaque was the most frequent HRP feature, prevalent in 32.5 %. No individuals had napkin ring sign on CCTA evaluation (Table 1). Relationship between IL-6 levels and HRP

The demographic characteristics, clinical risk factors, medication use, and social determinants of health were similar across the IL-6 quartiles; however, those with higher IL-6 levels had lower income and higher hs-CRP levels (Table 1). There was a higher prevalence of HRP with increasing IL-6 levels, such that those with IL-6 concentrations in the top quartile (IL6 > 3.6 pg/mL) were more likely to have HRP than those within the lower three quartiles (63 % vs. 31 %, p = 0.01, Fig. 2). Correspondingly, individuals with HRP had higher circulating levels of IL-6 than those without HRP (2.6 pg/mL [1.6, 6.3] vs. 1.9 pg/mL [1.1, 2.8], p = 0.03, Fig. 3). These associations were primarily driven by the higher prevalence of low attenuation plaque in those with IL-6 concentrations in the fourth quartile versus those with IL-6 concentrations in the bottom three quartiles (63.2 % vs 22.4 %, p < 0.001, Table 1). Logistic models showed a similar positive relationship between IL-6 serum concentrations and HRP prevalence. In unadjusted models and models adjusted for demographics, clinical risk factors, and social determinants of health, higher IL-6 serum concentrations were associated with a higher prevalence of HRP (Model 4 OR [95 % CI] per one pg/mL increase: 1.43 [1.12, 1.92], p = 0.008, Fig. 4).Fig. 2Prevalence of HRP by IL-6 quartile.Fig 2Fig. 3IL-6 concentrations by HRP status.Fig 3Fig. 4Unadjusted and adjusted associations between IL-6 concentration and HRP prevalence.Fig 4

Relationship between IL-6, PCE score, and HRP

In unadjusted models, the PCE score had a near-significant association with HRP prevalence (OR [95 % CI] per percent increase: 1.06 [1, 1.13], p = 0.06). The association between serum IL-6 concentration and HRP prevalence was independent of the PCE score (Model 5 OR [95 % CI] per one pg/mL increase: 1.36 [1.1, 1.8], p = 0.01, Fig. 4). In risk discrimination analyses, a model that only included the PCE score had an area under the curve (AUC) [95 % CI] of 0.69 [0.55, 0.83] for determining the presence of HRP. The addition of serum IL-6 concentration to this model improved the AUC [95 % CI] to 0.79 [0.67, 0.92] (likelihood ratio test χ2, 1 degree of freedom: 7.44, p < 0.001).

Relationship between hs-CRP and HRP

Most demographics, clinical risk factors, medication use, and social determinants of health characteristics were similar across hs-CRP quartiles. However, smoking prevalence trended to be higher in those with lower hs-CRP levels (first quartile [0 to 2.27 mg/L]: 63.2 %; second quartile [2.28 to 5.12 mg/L]: 63.2 %; third quartile [5.13 to 10.1 mg/L]: 31.6 %; fourth quartile [10.11 to 328.1 mg/L]: 33.3 %, p = 0.07). In univariate (OR [95 % CI] per one mg/L: 1 [0.98, 1.01], p = 0.8) and multivariate logistic regression models (Model 4 OR [95 % CI] per one mg/L: 0.99 [0.95, 1.01], p = 0.3), there was no association between serum hs-CRP concentrations and HRP prevalence.

Relationship between HIV, inflammation, and HRP

The prevalence of HRP did not differ between women living with HIV (38.5 %) and women living without HIV (40 %). HIV was not associated with HRP in unadjusted (OR [95 % CI]: 0.94 [0.35, 2.53], p = 0.9) and multivariate models adjusted for aforementioned risk factors and IL-6 levels (Model 4 OR [95 % CI]: 1 [0.22, 4.86], p= [0.35, 2.53], p = 0.99). Additionally, HIV did not modify the association between IL-6 levels and HRP in adjusted models (p-interaction: 0.87).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate an association between systemic IL-6 mediated inflammation and HRP prevalence, independent of demographics, clinical risk factors, social determinants of health, HIV status, and CAD risk calculators, in a cohort of predominantly Black women without CAD symptoms. Those with higher IL-6 concentrations were more likely to have HRP, predominantly manifested as low attenuation plaque. The addition of IL-6 levels to the PCE risk score, a ubiquitously used clinical tool, significantly improved discrimination for the presence of HRP in this cohort. Additionally, HIV did not modify the association between IL-6 and HRP.

Our results are consistent with investigations correlating IL-6 levels with HRP features in symptomatic cohorts without HIV and asymptomatic cohorts with HIV that have a high prevalence of men and individuals who do not identify as Black [[14], [15], [16],27,28]. In Japanese participants with clinically indicated angiography, those with thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA) had higher levels of IL-6, and those with IL-6 levels >3.3 pg/mL were more likely to have TFCA [14,28]. A study from Farenick and colleagues that leveraged data from the PROMISE trial showed IL-6 levels were positively associated with low attenuation plaque – an HRP feature – in a cohort of symptomatic participants [14,15,28]. IL-6 concentrations are also associated with HRP in asymptomatic, predominately men living with HIV [16]. We expand on these findings by showing a positive association between IL-6 levels and HRP prevalence in a unique cohort of predominately Black women without CAD symptoms.

These findings underscore the mediation of atherogenesis through inflammation [29]. By binding to membrane-bound receptors on hepatocytes, endothelial cells, and macrophages, IL-6 induces coagulation, disrupts the vascular endothelium, promotes the expression of adhesion molecules on inflammatory cells and endothelium, and stimulates the production of inflammatory cytokines – all of which contribute to the pathogenesis and instability of atherosclerotic plaque [9,10,12,30]. Experimental studies have shown increased atherosclerotic plaque size in mice treated with IL-6, and reductions in IL-6 preceded histologic stabilization of carotid plaque in humans [31,32]. IL-6′s role in atherogenesis may explain findings from genomic studies showing reduced odds of coronary heart events in individuals with lower IL-6 signaling [33].

Previous literature has indicated that commonly used cardiac risk assessment tools, such as the PCE, have limited prognostic utility in women and those with risk-enhancing factors, such as HIV [34]. The suboptimal performance of PCE to discriminate vulnerable plaque in our cohort of asymptomatic women with risk-enhancing factors may underscore the above limitations. Furthermore, our results suggest utility in incorporating inflammatory biomarkers to better estimate HRP prevalence in asymptomatic women with risk-enhancing factors, thereby leading to more precise risk stratification of this group.

Inflammatory biomarker testing in asymptomatic women could lead to the timely recognition of HRP and the commencement of preventive therapies targeting inflammation, such as statins, diet modulation, and increased physical activity, which could lead to plaque stabilization [[35], [36], [37]]. In the context of our findings, recent evidence from the REPRIEVE trial showing the benefit of statins in asymptomatic individuals with HIV could be due to the stabilization of HRP [38]. Other anti-inflammatory medications, such as canakinumab and colchicine, which have shown to reduce the residual risk of cardiac events among individuals with stable coronary disease, should be investigated in asymptomatic individuals with elevated IL-6 and/or HRP to determine if these agents have cardiovascular benefit in groups similar to the cohort in this study [[39], [40], [41]].

Limitations/future direction

Although this study benefited from having a cohort of asymptomatic individuals who underwent inflammasome profiling and advanced cardiac imaging techniques, the associations found in this study need further validation due to certain study design limitations. This study was a cross-sectional study, so these results do not demonstrate a causal relationship between inflammation and HRP in asymptomatic individuals. Consequently, prospective studies that examine inflammation and development of HRP as well as the impact of anti-inflammatory medications on HRP progression and cardiac outcomes in asymptomatic cohorts are needed. Our study included a small cohort of predominantly Black women with a high prevalence of HIV and of lower socioeconomic status, which may impact the generalizability of these results. More extensive studies are warranted to examine the relationship between IL-6 and HRP prevalence in asymptomatic cohorts of different demographics and clinical characteristics. Finally, we demonstrated the potential utility of IL-6 in risk stratifying “low cardiac risk” individuals according to current risk calculators, such as the pooled cohort equation; whether IL-6 offers similar risk discrimination amoung individuals categorized as “low cardiac risk” by future risk calculators, such as PREVENT, should be studied.

Conclusion

Systemic IL-6-mediated inflammation is associated with the presence of HRP in a cohort of predominately Black women without CAD symptoms. IL-6 levels improved the discrimination for HRP prevalence. Whether anti-inflammatory therapy will stabilize HRP and mitigate adverse outcomes needs further investigation.
