CCL5 improves radiation resistance through activation of the AMPK/mTOR pathway in adenoid cystic carcinoma
Authors: Mingyu Tan, Yanliang Chen, Jinxin Yang, Qian Wang, Lu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHead and neck adenoid cystic carcinoma (HNACC) is an uncommon but aggressive salivary gland malignancy with high recurrence and metastasis rates. Its “immune-cold” phenotype limits the efficacy of systemic therapy, and thus, radiotherapy is the principal treatment for unresectable cases. However, frequent radioresistance suggests the involvement of distinct molecular drivers. This study investigated the role of CCL5 in HNACC radioresistance, its mechanistic link to autophagy, and its potential as a radiosensitization target.MethodsTranscriptome sequencing of radiation-sensitive (SACC-83) and radiation-tolerant (SACC-LM) cell lines, integrated with GEO datasets, identified candidate genes. For functional validation, genetic manipulation and pharmacological inhibition with the CCL5/CCR5 antagonist maraviroc were performed. Cellular proliferation, invasion, apoptosis, cell cycle, and radiosensitivity were assessed. Autophagy was examined by transmission electron microscopy and western blotting, with a focus on AMPK/mTOR signaling. A xenograft mouse model was used to evaluate the therapeutic effect of maraviroc combined with radiotherapy.ResultsCCL5 expression was elevated in radioresistant cells and further induced by irradiation. High CCL5 levels promoted proliferation, migration, and invasion while reducing radiation-induced apoptosis and G₂/S arrest. Mechanistically, CCL5 activated AMPK, suppressed mTOR, and enhanced autophagy, supporting cell survival under radiation stress. CCL5 inhibition reduced autophagy, restored radiosensitivity, and synergized with irradiation, whereas autophagy activation reversed these effects. In vivo, maraviroc combined with radiotherapy decreased tumor burden, suppressed CCL5 expression, and inhibited autophagy.ConclusionCCL5 promotes radioresistance in HNACC through maintenance of AMPK/mTOR-dependent autophagy. Targeting the CCL5/CCR5 axis enhances radiosensitivity and represents a promising therapeutic strategy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08236-5.Keywords: Adenoid cystic carcinoma, CCL5, Radioresistance, Autophagy, AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway

Introduction

Head and neck adenoid cystic carcinoma (HNACC) is an uncommon but aggressive malignancy of the salivary glands and accounts for approximately 1% of head and neck cancers and 10% of salivary gland tumors [1, 2]. Despite the gradual decline in incidence reported in SEER analyses [3], HNACC remains clinically challenging because of its invasive growth, frequent perineural spread, and high rates of local recurrence (40–60% within five years) and distant metastasis, particularly to the lungs, resulting in a 5-year overall survival (OS) of only 40–65% [4, 5].

Although multimodal therapeutic strategies have been explored, their impact on survival remains limited. Cisplatin-based chemoradiotherapy, which improves outcomes in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [6], has been tested in HNACC, where small studies reported moderate survival outcomes but lacked confirmatory evidence [7]. Postoperative chemotherapy has also been suggested to improve local control in retrospective analyses [8]. Targeted therapy has been integrated with radiotherapy because more than 70% of HNACC patients exhibit EGFR overexpression; the effects of cetuximab in combination with intensity-modulated radiotherapy and carbon-ion boost have been evaluated in clinical studies, but the survival benefit remains uncertain [9, 10]. Immunotherapy has shown even more limited activity. PD-1 blockade has low response rates in HNACC because of its immune-cold phenotype [11, 12], and combination immune checkpoint therapy with nivolumab plus ipilimumab failed to improve efficacy in a phase II trial [13].

Radiotherapy therefore remains central to local control, but its effectiveness is constrained by intrinsic and acquired radioresistance, with a 5-year OS after definitive radiotherapy still less than 50% [8]. Although mechanisms such as dysregulated autophagy and impaired DNA damage repair have been proposed [11], the molecular networks driving HNACC radioresistance remain poorly defined. The lack of validated radiosensitization targets limits the development of effective combination strategies.

To address this critical knowledge gap, the present study integrated clinical data with experimental validation data to identify key molecules associated with HNACC invasiveness and radioresistance, focusing on their role in irradiation-induced autophagy. By elucidating these mechanisms, we aimed to provide a biological basis for molecularly-guided radiosensitization strategies to improve therapeutic outcomes.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and culture

The adenoid cystic carcinoma cell line SACC-83 and its radiation-tolerant derivative SACC-LM (Fenghui Biotechnology) were used. The cells were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath, the tube surface was disinfected with 75% ethanol, and the cell suspension was transferred to a centrifuge tube containing 3 mL of complete RPMI-1640 medium. After centrifugation at 1000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 4 mL of complete medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced the next day, and the cells were routinely cultured. When the confluence reached ~ 80%, the cells were washed twice with PBS and digested with 0.25% trypsin for 3 min. The digestion was terminated by the addition of complete medium, and the cells were collected by centrifugation, resuspended in complete medium, and seeded into new culture flasks. For cryopreservation, the cells were suspended in freezing medium and stored at − 80 °C before being transferred to liquid nitrogen.

X-ray irradiation

SACC-83 and SACC-LM cells in the logarithmic growth phase (~ 80% confluence) were irradiated at room temperature using a vertical X-ray device (225 kV/13.3 mA, 2 Gy/min). Cells were exposed to doses of 1, 2, or 4 Gy as indicated. Morphological changes were observed microscopically before and after irradiation.

Colony formation assay

Following trypsin digestion and counting, 1000–3000 SACC-83 or 500–1500 SACC-LM cells were seeded into 6-well plates. After 24 h, the cells were irradiated with 1, 2, or 4 Gy and cultured for 14 days or until > 50 cells formed colonies. The medium was changed every 2 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS, stained with crystal violet for 30 min, air-dried, and photographed. The plating efficiency and surviving fraction were calculated.

Cell proliferation assay (CCK-8)

A total of 5 × 10³ cells per well were seeded into 96-well plates (six replicates per group). After 24 h of incubation, the cells were irradiated with 0, 1, 2, or 4 Gy. At 12, 24, and 48 h postirradiation, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured after 1 h. The cell viability was calculated on the basis of the ratio of the absorbance of the treated wells to that of the control wells.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

SACC-LM cells were harvested after irradiation. RNA extraction, library construction, and high-throughput sequencing were performed by SeqHealth (Wuhan, China). After quality control and removal of low-quality reads and PCR duplicates, sequences were aligned to the reference genome using STAR. Differentially expressed genes were identified with edgeR (|fold change| ≥ 2 and adjusted P < 0.05). GO and KEGG pathway analyses were conducted, and weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed. Hub genes were identified using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database and the CytoHubba plugin and overlapped with differentially expressed genes to determine candidate targets.

Quantitative RT‒PCR

RNA was extracted with TRIzol, precipitated with isopropanol, washed with 70% ethanol, and dissolved in DEPC-treated water. cDNA was synthesized using a PrimeScript kit. Primers for GAPDH, CCL5, and CCR5 were synthesized by Qingke. Relative gene expression was determined using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II with the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH used as the internal control.

Gene transfection

CCL5 shRNA was transfected into SACC-LM cells using the GP-Transfect Mate to establish a stable knockdown model (control: Lv-Ctrl). SACC-83 cells were transfected with a CCL5 overexpression plasmid using Lipo8000™ (control: Oe-Ctrl). The medium was replaced after 24 h, and the cells were harvested after 48 h for subsequent experiments.

Cell migration and invasion assays

For the scratch assay, confluent cells were irradiated with 4 Gy, and a linear wound was generated with a pipette tip. Images were taken at 0, 24, and 48 h, and wound closure was quantified using Image-Pro Plus. For the Transwell invasion assay, Matrigel-coated chambers were seeded with 4 × 10⁴ cells in the upper chamber. After 24–48 h of incubation, the cells that invaded through the membrane were fixed, stained with crystal violet, and counted.

Apoptosis and cell cycle analysis

Apoptosis was assessed by Annexin V-PE/7-AAD double staining followed by flow cytometry. For cell cycle analysis, cells were fixed in 70% ethanol, stained with PI, and analyzed by flow cytometry using ModFitLT software.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and the protein concentration was determined by the BCA method. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 12.5% SDS‒PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk and incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Signals were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL), and band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase or after irradiation were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde for 2 h, fixed in 4% osmium tetroxide for 1 h, dehydrated, and embedded in Epon-812 resin. Ultrathin Sect. (70 nm) were mounted on copper grids, stained with 1% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and examined under a transmission electron microscope. Representative images were collected for analysis.

Animal experiments and immunohistochemistry

BALB/c-nu mice were subcutaneously injected with 2 × 10⁶ SACC-LM cells and randomly assigned to groups (n = 6). The mice received a single dose of 10 Gy of irradiation and an intraperitoneal injection of maraviroc (30 mg/kg every 2 days). The dose of maraviroc was selected on the basis of previous preclinical pharmacodynamic studies demonstrating effective CCR5 blockade without observable systemic toxicity. When body surface area normalization is used, this dose corresponds to clinically achievable exposure ranges in humans, supporting its translational relevance. Body weight and tumor volume were measured regularly. At the end of the study, the mice were euthanized in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and approved protocols. Specifically, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital at 50 mg/kg and monitored until loss of consciousness. Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of a pedal withdrawal reflex upon toe pinch using fingers or forceps. Cervical dislocation was then performed as the physical method of euthanasia, after which cessation of heartbeat and respiration was observed to confirm death. The tumors were subsequently collected for western blotting and immunohistochemistry with DAB staining to evaluate protein expression and histological changes.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v9.5) and SPSS (v27.0). Data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs) from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Experiments involving two independent variables (e.g., radiation dose and gene manipulation) were analyzed using two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc correction. A value of P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ns (P ≥ 0.05), P < 0.05 (*), P < 0.01 (**), P < 0.001 (***), and P < 0.0001 (****).

Results

ACC cells exhibit distinct intrinsic radiosensitivity phenotypes

To establish a biological model for radiation resistance, we first compared the intrinsic radiosensitivity of two ACC cell lines. Under baseline conditions, SACC-LM cells formed dense colonies, whereas SACC-83 cells produced fewer and more dispersed colonies. Following 4 Gy irradiation, survival decreased significantly in both lines, but the reduction was markedly greater in SACC-83 cells, indicating greater radiosensitivity (Fig. 1A–B). Short-term proliferation assays further supported this distinction. CCK-8 analysis revealed sustained proliferation suppression in SACC-83 cells after irradiation, whereas SACC-LM cells exhibited a transient proliferative rebound at 12 h before declining at 48 h (Fig. 1C). These findings reveal that SACC-LM cells are relatively radioresistant and that SACC-83 cells are radiosensitive, providing a functional platform for mechanistic studies.

Clonogenic assays (A, B) demonstrated that SACC-LM cells formed compact colonies while SACC-83 cells generated fewer, dispersed colonies; both showed reduced survival after irradiation, with SACC-83 being more radiosensitive (ns, *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). CCK-8 assays (C) further confirmed decreased viability post-irradiation, with SACC-LM showing a transient rebound at 12 h and subsequent suppression, whereas SACC-83 exhibited continuous inhibition. Transcriptomic analysis of irradiated SACC-LM cells revealed distinct clustering of differentially expressed genes (F), with GO and KEGG enrichment of upregulated genes (D, E) highlighting autophagy-related processes and AMPK/mTOR signaling, while downregulated genes (G, H) were enriched in pathways associated with transcriptional reprogramming. Integration with GEO datasets further validated radiation-induced autophagy as a conserved response. Finally, qRT-PCR confirmed that CCL5 expression (I) was significantly higher in SACC-LM than in SACC-83 and markedly upregulated after irradiation, consistent with its role as a hub gene in radiation resistance

Radiation induces an autophagy-associated transcriptional program and identifies CCL5 as a candidate hub gene

To explore the molecular programs associated with this phenotype, RNA sequencing was performed in irradiated SACC-LM cells. Differential expression analysis revealed 571 genes whose expression clearly differed between the irradiated and control samples (Fig. 1F). The functional enrichment results demonstrated the significant involvement of autophagy-related processes and AMPK/mTOR signaling pathways (Fig. 1D–E), suggesting that autophagy represents a central adaptive response to irradiation. Integration of two independent GEO datasets yielded 1602 differentially expressed genes, with consistent enrichment of AMPK/mTOR and autophagy pathways. Network analysis using WGCNA, STRING, and CytoHubba prioritized CCL5 as a central hub gene linked to the radiation response and tumor biology (Fig. 1I).

CCL5 expression is correlated with radioresistance

Consistent with this prediction, CCL5 expression was significantly higher in SACC-LM cells than in SACC-83 cells at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2A–F). Radiation further upregulated CCL5 expression, particularly in the radioresistant SACC-LM cells, mirroring their survival phenotype. These expression–function correlations suggested that CCL5 may contribute to radioresistance.

qRT-PCR (A, D) and Western blot (B, C, E, F) confirmed successful overexpression and knockdown of CCL5 in SACC-83 and SACC-LM cells, respectively. Functional assays demonstrated that CCL5 overexpression enhanced survival under 4 Gy irradiation without affecting baseline proliferation, whereas CCL5 knockdown reduced viability and invasion. Transwell invasion assays (G, H) showed increased invasiveness in CCL5-overexpressing cells, while flow cytometry (I, J) revealed decreased apoptosis, consistent with a radioresistant phenotype. CCK-8 assays (K) further confirmed enhanced proliferation after irradiation in the CCL5-overexpressing group, which was significantly attenuated by the CCR5 antagonist Maraviroc, highlighting the critical role of the CCL5/CCR5 axis in ACC radioresistance. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

Functional manipulation of CCL5 modulates radiosensitivity and malignant behaviors

To test causality, CCL5 expression was modulated in both cell lines. In SACC-LM cells, CCL5 knockdown significantly reduced colony formation, proliferation, migration, and invasion, with maximal suppression observed when combined with irradiation (Fig. 3A–G). Apoptosis and G₂/M arrest were correspondingly increased (Fig. 3H–I). Conversely, CCL5 overexpression in SACC-83 cells increased survival under irradiation conditions without altering baseline growth, and these effects were attenuated by the CCR5 antagonist maraviroc (Fig. 2G–K), confirming the functional involvement of the CCL5/CCR5 axis.

Clonogenic assays (A, B) showed that CCL5 knockdown markedly reduced colony formation, which decreased to < 40% of baseline when combined with 4 Gy irradiation. Scratch assays (C, D) further revealed impaired migration, while CCK-8 assays (E) confirmed significantly reduced proliferation, with the strongest inhibition observed in the knockdown + irradiation group. Transwell assays (F, G) demonstrated suppression of invasion by either CCL5 knockdown or irradiation, with maximal inhibition when combined. Flow cytometry (H, I) indicated that apoptosis approximately doubled and G₂/M arrest was significantly increased in the knockdown + irradiation group. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

CCL5 promotes radiation tolerance through AMPK/mTOR-dependent autophagy

Ultrastructural analysis revealed marked autophagosome accumulation after irradiation, especially in CCL5-overexpressing cells, while knockdown or maraviroc treatment reduced the number of autophagic structures (Fig. 4). Western blotting revealed increased Beclin1 levels, LC3-II/LC3-I ratios, and p-AMPK/AMPK ratios, along with decreased p62 levels and p-mTOR/mTOR ratios, following irradiation; these changes were enhanced by CCL5 overexpression and reversed by CCL5 inhibition (Fig. 5). These findings indicate that CCL5 promotes autophagy activation through AMPK activation and mTOR suppression, thereby enhancing radiation tolerance.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) showed few autophagosomes in control cells, while irradiation induced abundant autophagosomes (green arrows) and autolysosomes (red arrows). This effect was enhanced in CCL5-overexpressing SACC-LM cells (B) but attenuated in CCL5-knockdown cells (A) or upon Maraviroc treatment. Western blot analysis (C) demonstrated increased Beclin1 expression (D), reduced p62 levels (E), and elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (F) following irradiation, all of which were partially reversed by Maraviroc. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

Western blot analysis showed that irradiation increased Beclin1 expression, LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, and p-AMPK/AMPK ratio, while reducing p62 and p-mTOR/mTOR levels. These effects were more pronounced in CCL5-overexpressing SACC-LM cells (A–F) but were reversed by either CCL5 knockdown (G–L) or CCR5 inhibition with Maraviroc, indicating that CCL5 enhances radiation tolerance by activating AMPK and suppressing mTOR. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

Pharmacologic Modulation Confirms the Central Role of the CCL5–AMPK–mTOR Axis

Pharmacological experiments further established pathway dependency. The AMPK inhibitor dorsomorphin suppressed autophagy activation and malignant phenotypes in OE-CCL5 cells, whereas the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin partially restored autophagy and cellular functions in sh-CCL5 cells (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). These data confirm that the CCL5-driven radioresistance phenotype is mediated through the AMPK–mTOR–autophagy axis.

Western blot analysis demonstrated that the AMPK inhibitor Dorsomorphin suppressed Beclin1 expression and the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, restored p62 and p-mTOR levels, and attenuated the effects of CCL5 overexpression on proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis resistance in SACC-LM cells (A–F). Conversely, treatment with the mTOR inhibitor Rapamycin partially restored autophagy markers and cellular functions in CCL5-knockdown cells (G–L), supporting the central role of the CCL5–AMPK/mTOR–autophagy axis in mediating radiation tolerance. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

Scratch assays (A, C) showed that the AMPK inhibitor Dorsomorphin reduced migration in CCL5-overexpressing SACC-LM cells, while the mTOR inhibitor Rapamycin partially restored migration in CCL5-knockdown cells (B, D). CCK-8 assays further confirmed that Dorsomorphin suppressed proliferation in OE-CCL5 cells (E), whereas Rapamycin rescued proliferation defects in sh-CCL5 cells (F), highlighting the functional importance of the CCL5–autophagy axis in radiation resistance. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

Transwell assays (A, E) showed that the AMPK inhibitor Dorsomorphin significantly reduced invasion in CCL5-overexpressing SACC-LM cells, while the mTOR inhibitor Rapamycin partially restored invasion capacity in CCL5-knockdown cells (B, F). Flow cytometry assays (C, G) demonstrated that Dorsomorphin increased apoptosis in OE-CCL5 cells, whereas Rapamycin attenuated apoptosis in sh-CCL5 cells (D, H). These findings further confirm that the CCL5–autophagy axis is a key determinant of invasion and apoptosis in response to irradiation. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

In Vivo Inhibition of CCL5 Synergizes with Radiation to Suppress Tumor Growth

In xenograft models, irradiation reduced tumor growth, whereas maraviroc alone had a moderate inhibitory effect. The combination therapy resulted in the greatest suppression of tumor volume and weight (Fig. 9A–C). After irradiation, the expression of CCL5/CCR5 and autophagy markers increased in tumor tissues, but these changes were reversed by maraviroc (Fig. 9D–L), confirming the in vitro mechanism and supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting the CCL5/CCR5 axis to increase radiosensitivity.

In BALB/c nude mice xenografted with SACC-LM cells, 10 Gy irradiation suppressed tumor growth by approximately 70%, while Maraviroc alone reduced growth by about 40%. Combination therapy produced > 80% reduction in both tumor volume and weight (A–C). Immunohistochemistry revealed increased CCL5 and CCR5 expression after irradiation, which was suppressed by Maraviroc (D–F). Western blot analysis of tumor tissues (G–L) confirmed changes in autophagy markers consistent with in vitro findings, including increased Beclin1 expression, decreased p62, elevated LC3-II/LC3-I and p-AMPK/AMPK ratios, and reduced p-mTOR/mTOR levels, supporting the critical role of the CCL5/CCR5–autophagy axis in mediating radioresistance and radiosensitization. ns, not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001

Discussion

This study identified a previously unrecognized, nonimmune role of CCL5 in an immune-cold tumor context and demonstrated that CCL5 promotes radiation resistance in HNACC through AMPK/mTOR-regulated autophagy. Although CCL5 is traditionally viewed as an immune chemokine that recruits macrophages, CD8⁺ T cells, and dendritic cells via CCR5 signaling [14] and has been extensively studied in tumor immunity in breast, gastric, and colorectal cancers [15–19], HNACC presents a distinct biological environment with sparse immune infiltration. In this context, our findings shift the functional paradigm of CCL5 from immune modulation to tumor cell–intrinsic stress adaptation, revealing that autophagy regulation is a key mechanism through which CCL5 promotes survival under irradiation.

Rather than acting through immune recruitment, CCL5 enhances malignant phenotypes and radioresistance via the activation of AMPK and the suppression of mTOR signaling, thereby facilitating autophagy-dependent tolerance to radiation-induced stress. Pharmacologic inhibition of CCR5 with maraviroc reversed these effects, suppressed tumor growth, and reduced the expression of autophagy markers both in vitro and in vivo. These data suggest that CCL5 is a central mediator of radiation resistance in HNACC and highlight the CCL5–AMPK–mTOR–autophagy axis as a potential radiosensitization target.

Functionally, our data further clarify the signaling hierarchy connecting CCL5/CCR5 activation to autophagy induction. Although direct protein–protein interactions between CCR5 and the autophagy machinery were not assessed, pharmacologic pathway interrogation defined the directionality of signal transmission. The blockade of CCR5 with maraviroc consistently abolished CCL5-induced AMPK phosphorylation, restored mTOR activity, and normalized the expression of autophagy markers, including Beclin1, LC3-II/I, and p62, indicating that CCL5 exerts its ability to adapt to intracellular stress through its canonical receptor CCR5 [14] rather than through indirect immune-mediated mechanisms. Importantly, inhibition of AMPK with dorsomorphin (Compound C) prevented Beclin1 upregulation and LC3 conversion while rescuing p-mTOR levels, demonstrating that AMPK activation is required for the induction of autophagy by CCL5 and is upstream of the autophagy execution machinery. Conversely, pharmacologic inhibition of mTOR activity with rapamycin partially restored autophagic activity and malignant phenotypes in CCL5-knockdown cells, confirming that mTOR suppression is the essential downstream effector that links CCL5 signaling to autophagy. Collectively, these findings reveal a functional signaling cascade in which CCR5 activation leads to AMPK phosphorylation, followed by mTOR inhibition and subsequent Beclin1/LC3-dependent autophagy, thereby providing tumor cells with an adaptive survival advantage under radiation stress.

In addition to ACC, CCL5/CCR5 signaling promotes proliferation, migration, and invasion via the canonical PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways [20, 21] and facilitates immune escape by attracting and functionally reprogramming immune cells [22]. In PDAC, CCR2/5 activation contributes to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors, whereas CCR2/5 blockade combined with radiotherapy enhances T-cell infiltration and antitumor effects [23]. CCL5 expression also supports immune escape via FOXP3-mediated Treg recruitment and PD-L1 synergy, where combined PD-L1 blockade and CCL5 inhibition improve therapeutic outcomes [24]. Furthermore, the STING pathway links the DNA damage response and immune activation through CCL5 induction, enhancing the efficacy of checkpoint blockade [25, 26]. These findings underscore CCL5 as a molecular hub connecting DNA damage, immune regulation, and treatment resistance.

CCL5-driven resistance appears to be a universal phenomenon. In NSCLC, fibroblast-derived CCL5 enhances cisplatin resistance by upregulating HOTAIR expression and inhibiting apoptosis [27]. In ovarian cancer, CCL5 activates STAT3 and PI3K/AKT signaling to promote chemoresistance [28]. The upregulation of CCL5 expression has also been observed in cisplatin-resistant gastric cancer cells, linking it to inflammatory pathways [29]. Similarly, in response to radiotherapy, CCL5 has been implicated in resistance across tumor types: in nasopharyngeal carcinoma, it is upregulated in resistant cells and associated with type I interferon, EGFR, and NF-κB signaling [30], and in melanoma, repeated irradiation induces CCL5 expression, accompanied by reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, suggesting the involvement of an immune escape mechanism [31].

Given that HNACC is immune-cold, our findings highlight that the interplay between CCL5 and autophagy is central to its radioresistance. Prior studies also support this connection. In melanoma, autophagy deficiency induces CCL5 overexpression, while CCL5 secretion in turn activates autophagy via AMPK, enhancing migration [32, 33]. In prostate cancer, endothelial-derived CCL5 induces autophagy, downregulates androgen receptor expression, and promotes metastasis [34]. CCL5-mediated autophagy also alters macrophage polarization in immunosuppressive contexts [35]. Our data extend these insights by revealing that in HNACC, CCL5-driven autophagy provides a survival mechanism under irradiation.

This study is the first to define the regulatory role of the CCL5–AMPK/mTOR–autophagy axis in HNACC radioresistance. As a CCR5 antagonist, maraviroc effectively suppressed AMPK activation, restored mTOR phosphorylation, inhibited autophagy, and enhanced radiosensitivity. Prior studies corroborate this concept: CCR5 antagonists inhibit DNA damage repair, increase the efficacy of chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and exhibit low toxicity in normal tissues [36]. Maraviroc has also been shown to prevent radiation-induced macrophage recruitment and reduce tumor invasion [37]. Furthermore, combining maraviroc with autophagy inhibitors such as chloroquine enhances antitumor effects [38]. These findings strengthen the understanding of combined therapeutic strategies targeting CCL5 and autophagy in ACC. In addition to its mechanistic implications, our findings have translational relevance. Maraviroc is an FDA-approved CCR5 antagonist with established clinical safety, and the dose used in our animal model falls within clinically achievable exposure ranges, supporting therapeutic feasibility. The marked upregulation of CCL5 after irradiation suggests its potential as a dynamic biomarker of radiation tolerance. As HNACC is an immune-cold tumor with a limited response to immunotherapy, targeting tumor cell–intrinsic autophagic adaptation represents a nonimmune-dependent radiosensitization strategy. These results provide a rationale for the future evaluation of radiotherapy combined with CCR5 inhibition, particularly in unresectable or locally advanced HNACC.

The sustained activation of autophagy in ACC has been linked to tumor progression and treatment tolerance. Proteins such as LC3A, LC3B, and Beclin1 are upregulated in ACC tissues, whereas p62 and p-mTOR are downregulated, which is consistent with high basal autophagy [39–42]. Hypoxia and cisplatin stress further activate autophagy, promoting immune evasion and apoptosis resistance [43]. In response to radiotherapy, persistent mTOR inhibition can promote growth arrest and improve treatment efficacy [44]. Our findings highlight the central role of AMPK/mTOR signaling in mediating these effects.

The mTOR pathway itself is a critical hub for autophagy and tumor metabolism. CCL5 activates mTOR via CCR5 to promote glycolysis and protein synthesis in breast cancer, increasing cell viability [45–48]. In radiation resistance, mTOR activation has been implicated in both squamous cell carcinoma and lung cancer models, with inhibitors such as rapamycin effectively restoring radiosensitivity [49, 50]. Dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitors (e.g., NVP-BEZ235) and selective PI3K/mTOR inhibitors (e.g., PF-05212384) have demonstrated radiosensitizing effects by targeting HIF-1α, VEGF, and PI3K/mTOR activation [51, 52]. These findings align with our observation that targeting AMPK/mTOR signaling via the CCL5/CCR5 axis reverses radioresistance in HNACC.

This study has several limitations. First, it primarily used two ACC cell lines; additional subtypes and primary tumor samples are needed to generalize the findings. Second, autophagy dynamics require more precise temporal and spatial monitoring, such as live-cell imaging. Third, given the complexity of signaling networks, additional downstream or compensatory pathways beyond AMPK/mTOR may contribute to resistance and warrant further exploration. Future work should be expanded to clinical cohorts, analysis of the correlations between CCL5 expression and prognosis, and evaluation of multitarget combinatorial interventions to improve radiosensitivity.

Conclusion

This study systematically revealed, for the first time, the pivotal role of CCL5 in mediating radiation resistance in adenoid cystic carcinoma. We demonstrated that irradiation induces autophagy in ACC cells and that CCL5 enhances autophagic flux via AMPK activation and mTOR inhibition, providing a survival advantage. The inhibition of the CCL5/CCR5 pathway or the blockade of autophagy significantly enhanced radiosensitivity both in vitro and in vivo. These findings identify the CCL5–autophagy axis as a promising therapeutic target and lay the groundwork for the clinical translation of molecular radiosensitization strategies in HNACC.
