Genetic palmoplantar keratoderma associated with SERPINA12 mutations
Authors: Lulu Pan, Zhangyu Bu
Published: 2026

A 3-year-old girl presented to our dermatology clinic with a 2-year history of pruritic, symmetric skin thickening and erythema involving the palms and soles, extending to the dorsal surfaces (Figure 1). She had had mild flexural eczema since infancy; however, her persistent palmoplantar lesions showed no improvement after 1 year of dupilumab therapy, administered for presumed atopic dermatitis. She did not have a family history of skin conditions; her father had a history of mild allergic rhinitis. As the presentation suggested a primary keratinization disorder, we suspected hereditary palmoplantar keratoderma (PPK) and performed a diagnostic skin biopsy. Histopathologic examination confirmed the diagnosis of PPK. Subsequent whole-exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous pathogenic variants in SERPINA12 (c.635–7A > G and c.970_971del), establishing the diagnosis of autosomal recessive hereditary PPK. Treatment with a topical retinoid combined with 0.03% tacrolimus ointment applied twice daily resulted in marked clinical improvement within 4 weeks.

SERPINA12-related palmoplantar keratoderma in a 3-year-old girl. (A) Palmar view showing symmetric, diffuse skin thickening and erythema with hyperkeratosis. (B) Dorsal views of the hands and feet, showing extension of the erythematous and hyperkeratotic plaques from the palms and soles.

Hereditary PPK is one of a genetically heterogeneous group of skin disorders frequently misdiagnosed as treatment-refractory eczema.1 Nagashima-type PPK, the most commonly encountered subtype, is characterized by erythema and mild hyperkeratosis of the palms and soles, with extension to the dorsal aspects of the fingers and toes, the flexor surfaces of the wrists, the Achilles tendon region, and occasionally the knees and elbows. In contrast, SERPINA12-related PPK, seen in this patient, is rare in clinical practice and typically presents with more pronounced palmoplantar inflammation.2 As a result, the condition is particularly prone to misdiagnosis as atopic dermatitis, contact dermatitis, chronic eczema, or palmoplantar psoriasis. Given the high risk of misdiagnosis and delayed treatment, the presence of symmetric palmoplantar erythema with a poor or suboptimal therapeutic response should prompt consideration of palmoplantar keratoderma. In such cases, early referral to a dermatologist is essential for accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.3
