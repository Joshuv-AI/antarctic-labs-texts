Impact of Doxycycline Prophylaxis on Reducing Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma‐Related Complications in Lung Transplant Recipients
Authors: Lara Jones, Byeong Choi, Maria Velez, Stephanie Levine, Jason Gauthier, Edward Sako, Holly Keyt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Ureaplasma and Mycoplasma species (spp.) infections have been associated with increased morbidity and mortality in lung transplant (LTx) recipients due to their association with hyperammonemia syndrome (HS) and wound dehiscence. This study aims to assess the clinical outcomes in LTx recipients treated with preventative doxycycline.MethodsWe conducted a single‐center retrospective review of LTx recipients from January 2020 to February 2024. In November 2023, our center began administering doxycycline prophylaxis (ppx) postoperatively until bronchoalveolar lavage PCR results for Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp. were available. Doxycycline was discontinued if the PCR was negative and continued for 14 days if positive.ResultsA total of 98 LTx recipients were reviewed, with 3 excluded due to death before surveillance. Of these, 70 tested negative for Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp., 8 were not tested, and among the 17 positives, 11 received doxycycline ppx. The incidence of HS was lower in the ppx group (0% vs. 50%). The ppx group had shorter median duration of mechanical ventilation (MV) (3 vs. 20 days), ICU stay (9 vs. 22 days), and hospital stay (24 vs. 30 days). No significant difference was found in the rates of anastomotic dehiscence (18% vs. 17%).ConclusionsDoxycycline ppx was associated with a lower incidence of HS, decreased duration of MV, and shorter ICU and hospital length of stay in this cohort of LTx recipients. These findings suggest that doxycycline, an inexpensive and relatively well‐tolerated intervention, may prevent HS after LTx.

Introduction

Lung transplant (LTx) remains the definitive therapeutic intervention for patients suffering from end‐stage pulmonary diseases, significantly improving both survival and quality of life. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and immunosuppression, infectious complications remain prominent causes of morbidity and mortality posttransplant [1, 2]. Among these, infections with atypical organisms such as Mycoplasma hominis and Ureaplasma species (spp.) (Ureaplasma urealyticum and Ureaplasma parvum) have emerged as clinically significant pathogens due to their fastidious nature, resistance to standard antimicrobial prophylaxis (ppx), and severe clinical manifestations [3, 4].

Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma infections can present variably, from asymptomatic colonization to severe pneumonia, empyema, disseminated infection, and the life‐threatening complication of hyperammonemia syndrome (HS), characterized by encephalopathy, elevated serum ammonia levels, and high mortality rates [3, 4, 5]. Mycoplasma spp. has also been implicated in posttransplant complications such as wound dehiscence and other surgical site infections [6, 7, 8]. Recent studies indicate donor‐derived transmission as a primary route, with colonization rates in donor lungs reported between 10% and 28% [4, 5, 9]. Notably, untreated or delayed treatment of these infections may result in significant patient morbidity, increased length of hospitalization, graft dysfunction, and elevated mortality [3, 4, 5, 10, 11].

Currently, there is significant variability among transplant centers regarding prophylactic antimicrobial strategies to mitigate these infections. Empiric and targeted treatment protocols typically utilize macrolides, fluoroquinolones, and tetracyclines due to their in vitro activity against these organisms [10, 12]. However, standardized prophylactic regimens to prevent donor‐derived Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma infections remain controversial, with limited prospective or retrospective evidence demonstrating efficacy. Recent literature suggests that potential benefits from prophylactic antibiotic strategies initiated perioperatively or immediately posttransplant, highlighting reduced incidence and severity of infection‐related complications [10, 13]. Rising concerns regarding macrolide‐resistant Ureaplasma strains and adverse effects of fluoroquinolones necessitate the exploration of alternative therapies [14, 15].

Doxycycline, a broad‐spectrum tetracycline antibiotic with activity against both Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp., represents an attractive prophylactic option given its favorable safety profile, oral bioavailability, and cost‐effectiveness [12, 16]. Unlike other antibiotics, doxycycline's efficacy in this context is not yet overshadowed by widespread resistance, although patterns of regional resistance have been observed [17]. However, published evidence evaluating doxycycline ppx specifically in the LTx population remains sparse, leaving clinical teams without clear guidance.

The objective of this single‐center retrospective study was, therefore, to evaluate the impact of doxycycline ppx on the incidence of Mycoplasma‐ and Ureaplasma‐related complications in adult LTx recipients. Our hypothesis is that perioperative doxycycline ppx significantly reduces the incidence and severity of Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma infection–associated complications in LTx recipients.

Methods

Study Population and Design

We conducted a retrospective review of adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent single or bilateral LTx at UT Health San Antonio between January 2020 and February 2024. Clinical data were collected via chart review of electronic medical records. Patients were excluded if they died prior to postoperative surveillance for Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp. This study protocol was approved by the UT Health San Antonio Institutional Review Board (STUDY00000339).

Data Collection

Demographic and clinical data included recipient age, sex, underlying lung disease and association with CTD (connective tissue disease), induction immunosuppression type, ECMO (extracorporeal membrane oxygenation), CPB (cardiopulmonary bypass), and peak serum ammonia levels. HS was defined as the onset of new neurological abnormalities (e.g., altered mental status, seizures, and cerebral edema) accompanied by serum ammonia levels > 50 µmol/L, normal hepatic transaminases, and no alternative diagnosis.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incidence of HS. Secondary outcomes included mechanical ventilation (MV) duration, intensive care unit (ICU) and hospital lengths of stay, discharge disposition, renal replacement therapy (RRT) requirement, and surgical wound complications.

Surveillance and Screening Protocol

Postoperative surveillance involved PCR testing for Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp. on a single BAL fluid sample obtained within the first 24 h after the transplant. Detection of Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp. was performed using a commercial, laboratory‐developed, real‐time PCR assay at ARUP Laboratories. The assay qualitatively detects organism‐specific nucleic acid sequences and provides species‐level identification. Specific gene targets and primer/probe sequences are proprietary to the performing laboratory and are not publicly disclosed. Recipients were not tested pre‐ or peri‐transplant. Serum ammonia level was obtained on postoperative day (POD) 1 and checked daily until a negative Mycoplasma/Ureaplasma PCR or a downtrend in ammonia levels; the peak value was used for analysis.

In November 2023, we implemented a standardized doxycycline ppx protocol. Under this protocol, all LTx recipients, unless contraindicated, received doxycycline 100 mg twice a day postoperatively pending BAL PCR results. Doxycycline was discontinued immediately upon negative PCR results; however, if PCR was positive, doxycycline was continued for a total of 14 days. Prior to this protocol, patients with positive PCR were treated only after receipt of confirmatory results.

Immunosuppression and Antimicrobial Prophylaxis

Postoperative immunosuppression consisted of induction therapy with basiliximab, followed by maintenance immunosuppression with tacrolimus, mycophenolate mofetil, and tapering glucocorticoids. Routine perioperative antimicrobial ppx included broad‐spectrum antibiotics with cefepime and vancomycin, adjusted subsequently according to intraoperative donor and recipient cultures and susceptibilities. Additional ppx included trimethoprim‐sulfamethoxazole, posaconazole, and valganciclovir.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact tests as appropriate. Continuous variables were expressed as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) and compared using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

A total of 98 adult LTx recipients were evaluated during the study period. Three patients were excluded due to death prior to postoperative surveillance. The remaining 95 patients were analyzed and stratified based on PCR results and doxycycline ppx status.

Clinical Outcomes in PCR‐Positive Patients

Among PCR‐positive patients, HS occurred exclusively in the non‐prophylaxis group (three of six patients; 50%) compared to no cases (0%) in the ppx group (p = 0.029). HS occurred at a median of 5.5 days post‐LTx. Clinical outcomes favored the ppx group, notably shorter MV duration (median 3 days [IQR 1–6] vs. 20 days [IQR 4–35], p = 0.084), ICU stay (median 9 days [IQR 5–19] vs. 22 days [IQR 21–40], p = 0.057), and total hospital stays (median 24 days [IQR 20–28] vs. 30 days [IQR 26–33], p = 0.066), although these differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). Of the three patients without ppx who developed HS, one was positive for U. parvum, and the other two were positive for both M. hominis and U. urealyticum.

Serum Ammonia Levels

Peak serum ammonia levels revealed higher median levels among PCR‐positive patients who did not receive ppx (65.5 µmol/L) compared to those receiving ppx (47 µmol/L); however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.216). Among PCR‐negative patients, peak ammonia levels did not differ between ppx and non‐prophylaxis groups (54 vs. 53 µmol/L, p = 0.947).

Secondary Outcomes

Secondary outcomes revealed significantly higher rates of RRT requirement in PCR‐positive patients without ppx (83%) compared to ppx recipients (9.1%, p = 0.005). No significant differences were observed in surgical site or airway complications. Airway dehiscence in both groups was managed identically with surveillance bronchoscopy and a shortened prednisone taper. A greater proportion of patients in the ppx group were discharged home after transplant compared with the non‐prophylaxis group (82% vs. 33%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.10). There was one death reported in each group during the study period. Median duration of ppx in PCR‐negative group was 8 days.

Discussion

There is growing emphasis on initiating prophylactic treatment during the interval between BAL sampling and obtaining Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma PCR results, given the heightened risk of complications associated with delays in initiating effective therapy [4, 10, 13]. Despite this consensus, considerable debate persists regarding optimal prophylactic agents, treatment duration, and whether universal or targeted ppx should be employed [10, 11, 13]. This single‐center retrospective study evaluated the impact of early doxycycline ppx in LTx recipients, specifically targeting complications associated with Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma infections.

In our cohort, none of the PCR‐positive patients who received doxycycline ppx developed HS, whereas 50% of PCR‐positive patients without ppx did. This significant reduction aligns with previously published data by Bharat et al. [13] and Roberts et al. [10], both of whom demonstrated a clear association between early antimicrobial intervention and improved outcomes, notably reduced incidence of HS in LTx recipients. Additionally, peak ammonia levels were similar in the ppx and non‐prophylaxis groups; however, the clinical significance of this finding remains unclear and warrants further exploration.

Beyond HS prevention, an important finding from our study was doxycycline's broader beneficial impact on patient outcomes. Patients who received ppx had shorter durations of MV, ICU stay, and overall hospitalization compared to those who did not. Specifically, the median ICU stay was shorter (9 vs. 22 days), as was the median duration of MV (3 vs. 20 days) in the ppx group. These findings corroborate prior studies suggesting improved clinical outcomes when infections with Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma are proactively managed [10, 11, 13].

Interestingly, our analysis showed no significant difference in surgical site complications between the ppx and non‐prophylaxis groups. This suggests doxycycline ppx may have minimal impact on surgical site infection rates, possibly because surgical site complications in our cohort were not directly related to Mycoplasma [6]. Alternatively, the absence of an effect might reflect inherent resistance of certain isolates to doxycycline.

The broad‐spectrum activity of doxycycline against Mycoplasma and Ureaplasma spp., coupled with its favorable safety profile, supports its use for ppx in the transplant setting [7]. In our cohort, the number needed to treat was 2, indicating benefit; however, the number needed to harm could not be determined due to multiple postoperative factors that also contribute to common doxycycline‐associated adverse effects (e.g., gastrointestinal symptoms). Universal use must be balanced against potential drawbacks, including tetracycline resistance, as it has been documented in specific geographic areas or populations with heavy antibiotic exposure [18, 19, 20, 21]. The increasing availability of rapid molecular diagnostics may enable targeted ppx, reserving doxycycline for recipients at the highest risk based on donor‐derived factors or positive PCR results. Optimal prophylactic duration remains uncertain, as prolonged antibiotic exposure theoretically risks promoting resistance development [15].

The broader question of universal versus targeted ppx strategies remains unresolved. While targeted ppx—initiating treatment only for PCR‐positive cases—limits unnecessary antibiotic use, universal ppx may mitigate the risks associated with delayed treatment, especially given the severity of complications like HS [10, 13]. Until faster and more reliable diagnostic assays become widely available, it is reasonable to consider ppx, at least in high‐risk transplant populations.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective, single‐center analysis with a small sample size, which limits precision and generalizability and highlights the need for prospective multicenter trials. Second, our comparisons were not adjusted for key confounders (illness severity, donor/recipient factors, immunosuppression intensity, and perioperative events), so residual confounding may explain some or all observed associations. Prospective, multivariable‐adjusted, multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings.

In conclusion, this study supports the prophylactic use of doxycycline to reduce the incidence of HS and improve overall morbidity following LTx. Future research should focus on clarifying optimal prophylactic strategies, including choice of antimicrobial agents, treatment duration, and patient selection criteria. A comprehensive approach combining early detection, rapid diagnostics, and tailored ppx appears to provide the greatest opportunity for enhancing clinical outcomes in LTx recipients.
