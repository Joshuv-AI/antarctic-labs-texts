Understanding Human Cortical Expansion Using Pluripotent Stem Cell‐Derived Brain Organoids: Insights Into Progenitor Evolution and Diversity
Authors: Wendy W. Y. Choi, Julien Muffat, Yun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe evolution of the human brain underlies our higher‐order cognitive functions. In particular, the cerebral cortex, the outermost layer of the brain, has rapidly evolved to contain a disproportionately large number of neurons relative to the rest of the brain. Much of this expansion is attributed to the enlargement and diversification of the pool of neural precursors, which proliferate and differentiate into the neurons and glia of the brain. How the human cerebral cortex has evolved remains an active area of investigation. With the advent of pluripotent stem cell and brain organoid technologies, comparative genomic studies between humans, mice, and nonhuman primates have identified human‐specific genes or pathways during neurodevelopment. The utility of these models relies on the ability of brain organoids to preserve the cytoarchitecture and species‐specific developmental trajectories of diverse neural and glial cell types that are observed in vivo. This review will discuss how brain organoids recapitulate aspects of interspecies differences during cortical development, specifically neural precursor expansion, neurogenesis, and gliogenesis, and how these models can be improved to enable a deeper understanding of human brain evolution. Unraveling the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying cross‐species differences in brain expansion could also provide key insights into neurodevelopmental diseases, particularly those where brain size is affected.

Introduction

The evolutionary expansion of the human brain, particularly the cerebral cortex, is considered one important aspect of our unique intellectual abilities. The human cortex contains 1200‐fold more neurons than the mouse cortex and 10‐fold more than the rhesus macaque cortex. Importantly, humans show a disproportionately higher cortical‐to‐subcortical neuron ratio relative to other mammals, a substantial increase that cannot be explained solely by differences in body size (Herculano‐Houzel 2017). The increase in cortical neuronal abundance underpins many complex cognitive functions such as language, abstract thought, and working memory (Molnár et al. 2019). Cellularly, this numerical increase is driven primarily by the unique proliferative and differentiative behaviors of human cortical neural stem and progenitor cells, collectively termed neural precursors (NPs). This contributes to the massive amplification of the embryonic NP pool, which generates most of the neurons and glia that make up the postnatal brain. While there has been much focus on understanding the amplification of the human cortical NP population, the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

Our exceptional cognitive capacities likely come with a biological cost. Humans carry a disproportionate susceptibility to complex neurodevelopmental and psychiatric disorders, such as autism spectrum disorder and schizophrenia. Growing genomic evidence suggests this vulnerability is intertwined with our evolutionary history: many of the genetic innovations that enabled cortical expansion reside within the same loci where pathogenic mutations could disrupt normal brain development and give rise to disease. Yet the mechanisms linking human‐specific evolutionary changes to neurological disease susceptibility remain poorly understood, in part because the experimental systems needed to study them in a species‐comparative context were largely unavailable until recently.

Investigating the cellular and molecular mechanisms governing human brain evolution has long been bottlenecked by the ethical and physical inaccessibility of fetal tissue from humans and nonhuman primates and the scarcity of models capable of preserving native, species‐specific developmental programs. The advent of pluripotent stem cell (PSC) technology and three‐dimensional organoid platforms has transformed this field by enabling the generation of living neural tissue from any species for which PSCs can be derived and by permitting direct genetic manipulation and real‐time observation of human‐specific developmental events in vitro. In this review, we first describe the key differences in cortical development across species that organoid studies seek to model. Next, we evaluate how well current organoid systems recapitulate these differences, including their cytoarchitecture, cellular diversity, and developmental tempo. We then summarize how organoid‐based approaches have revealed human‐specific mechanisms in metabolism, cell signaling, epigenetic and transcriptional regulation, and cellular architecture. Finally, we highlight emerging experimental strategies and discuss non‐cortical brain regions, which will deepen our mechanistic understanding of human brain expansion and its relationship to neurological disease.

Species‐Specific Features in Human Cortical Development

Early stages of neural development are highly conserved across mammalian species, including rodents and primates, despite substantial differences in developmental timing and ultimate brain size. During early human embryogenesis, the neural tube, derived from ectodermal tissue, closes at around gestational week (GW) 4 (Stiles and Jernigan 2010). The neural tube initially consists of a pseudostratified neuroepithelium surrounding a hollow lumen that later forms the ventricular system of the brain (Sauer 1935). Similar neuroepithelial organization is observed across mammalian species, including mice, reflecting conserved developmental programs underlying early cortical patterning. Neuroepithelial cells initially undergo symmetric proliferative divisions to expand and thicken the neuroepithelium before transitioning toward asymmetric divisions to generate radial glial cells and initiate neurogenesis (Chenn and McConnell 1995). In humans, this transition occurs at a comparatively prolonged developmental timescale and is accompanied by the progressive emergence of expanded germinal zones that support increased progenitor amplification and cortical growth.

The ventricular zone (VZ) represents the primary germinal layer of the developing cortex and is broadly conserved across mammalian species. Within the VZ, ventricular radial glia (vRG) and intermediate progenitor cells (IPCs) derived from neuroepithelial cells function as apical progenitors due to their apical processes contacting the ventricular surface (Gal et al. 2006). vRG, also termed apical radial glia, are among the earliest radial glia populations generated from neuroepithelial cells and exhibit a characteristic bipolar morphology with processes extending toward both the ventricular and the pial surfaces (Malatesta et al. 2000; Miyata et al. 2001; Noctor et al. 2001). These cells provide both a structural scaffold for neuronal migration and a proliferative progenitor population that generates neurons either directly or indirectly (Noctor et al. 2004; Rakic 1972). Although the overall architecture and function of the VZ are highly conserved between rodents and primates, species differences emerge through alterations in proliferative dynamics, developmental timing, and the generation of downstream progenitor populations that ultimately contribute to cortical expansion.

In contrast to the relatively conserved VZ, the subventricular zone (SVZ) exhibits substantial evolutionary expansion and increased organizational complexity in primates, particularly in humans. The SVZ can be subdivided into the inner SVZ (iSVZ) and the outer SVZ (oSVZ), each containing overlapping and distinct NP populations including IPCs and outer radial glia (oRG). IPCs within the SVZ are primarily multipolar basal progenitors that typically lack apical attachments and undergo several rounds of proliferative divisions prior to neuronal differentiation (Hansen et al. 2010; Haubensak et al. 2004; Miyata et al. 2004; Noctor et al. 2004). The presence of expanded IPC subtypes has been identified in the developing human cortex, suggesting increased IPC heterogeneity as an important feature of primate cortical evolution (Pebworth et al. 2021).

While a rudimentary SVZ is present in rodents, primates possess a markedly enlarged SVZ and a prominent oSVZ enriched for oRG, a progenitor population strongly associated with cortical expansion and folding (Lui et al. 2011). oRG emerge in the developing human cortex around GW 13 and display several distinctive cellular features that distinguish them from other NP populations, most notably mitotic somal translocation, in which the cell soma rapidly translocates along the basal process immediately prior to cell division (Hansen et al. 2010). Human oRG also exhibit extensive proliferative and neurogenic capacity, contributing to neuronal production during mid‐gestation, particularly of late‐born upper‐layer neurons (Hansen et al. 2010; Lukaszewicz et al. 2005). Single‐cell transcriptomic studies have further identified molecular specialization within oRG populations, including enrichment of growth factor and extracellular matrix signaling pathways that may support maintenance of the oSVZ NP niche (Pollen et al. 2015). In addition to neurogenesis, oRG contribute to gliogenesis, generating astrocytes and oligodendrocytes during later stages of corticogenesis (Fu et al. 2021; Huang et al. 2020; Rash et al. 2019).

Although homologous NP populations are present across mammals, substantial species‐specific differences exist in their abundance, molecular identity, and neurogenic behavior. Comparative studies have shown that gyrencephalic species such as humans and macaques generally possess expanded oRG populations relative to lissencephalic species such as rodents, supporting a strong association between oRG abundance and cortical expansion and gyrification (Figure 1) (Romero and Borrell 2015). However, this relationship is not absolute. For example, the common marmoset possesses a relatively smooth cortex despite containing a substantial oRG population, suggesting that oRG abundance alone is insufficient to cause gyrification (Garcia‐Moreno et al. 2012; Kelava et al. 2012). Consistent with this idea, direct cross‐species comparisons have revealed important qualitative differences in oRG biology. While rare oRG‐like cells have been identified in the lissencephalic mouse cortex, they undergo more direct neurogenesis compared to human oRG (Wang et al. 2011). Similarly, although ferrets possess an abundant oRG population and are widely used as a model for cortical expansion, human oRG display longer mitotic somal translocation distances and a greater propensity to generate IPCs (Gertz et al. 2014). Additional heterogeneity has also been observed within gyrencephalic species, including the identification of a HOPX‐ oRG population in the ferret developing cortex with reduced self‐renewal capacity relative to HOPX+ oRG (Matsumoto et al. 2020). Putative HOPX‐ oRG have also been reported in human cortical organoids, although their presence and functional significance in the developing human cortex remains to be explored (Walsh et al. 2024). Together, these findings suggest that evolutionary expansion of the cortex likely depends not only on oRG abundance, but also on species‐specific adaptations in progenitor behavior, proliferative capacity, and lineage potential.

Species differences in cortical neuronal output are shaped not only by progenitor abundance and behavior, but also by evolutionary changes in developmental timing. Although the overall sequence of corticogenesis is broadly conserved across mammals, humans exhibit markedly prolonged, or bradychronic, neurodevelopment relative to rodents and nonhuman primates (Lindhout et al. 2024). During fetal corticogenesis, both the NP expansion and neurogenic phases are substantially extended in humans compared with macaques and mice, thereby increasing the time available for NP proliferation and neuronal production (Lewitus et al. 2014; Rakic 1974). Consequently, heterochronic expansion of developmental windows is thought to be a major contributor to increased cortical size and complexity in humans. This slower developmental pace persists beyond fetal stages. Relative to macaques, the human brain is considerably less mature at birth, reaching only 25%–30% of its maximum adult volume (Alldritt et al. 2024). Postnatal maturation is likewise extended in humans, with myelination continuing into the third decade of life and synaptic pruning occurring over a substantially longer timescale than in macaques (Lindhout et al. 2024). Together, these findings suggest that prolonged developmental timing across both prenatal and postnatal stages represents a key evolutionary feature of human brain development.

Organoids as Models to Study Cortical Development in Vitro

The development of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology has transformed the study of human neurodevelopment and evolution by enabling experimental access to otherwise inaccessible developmental stages and species‐specific cellular programs (Takahashi and Yamanaka 2006). Because fetal brain tissue is difficult to obtain across species, particularly during early corticogenesis, PSC systems provide a scalable and genetically tractable platform for modeling cortical development in vitro. In addition, PSCs can be readily manipulated using CRISPR‐based genome engineering approaches, allowing direct interrogation of candidate evolutionary mechanisms and human‐specific genetic programs (Cerneckis et al. 2024). Over the past decade, researchers have developed two‐dimensional (2D) and three‐dimensional (3D) neural differentiation systems capable of generating region‐specific neural cell types, including those of the developing cortex. Importantly, these in vitro models recapitulate several hallmark features of human corticogenesis, including expanded progenitor zones, increased progenitor heterogeneity, abundant oRG, and prolonged developmental timing. As such, PSC‐derived neural cultures have emerged as powerful comparative systems for investigating the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying human cortical expansion.

Prior to the development of 3D cortical organoid systems, early in vitro modeling of corticogenesis primarily relied on 2D monolayer differentiation approaches, which established many of the foundational patterning principles used in current organoid protocols. Neural induction through dual SMAD inhibition efficiently direct PSCs toward a neuroectodermal fate, generating cortical NPs that self‐organize into polarized rosette‐like structures resembling the fetal ventricular zone (Chambers et al. 2009; Shi et al. 2012). These NPs can subsequently differentiate into major cortical cell types, including neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes, through staged modulation of developmental signaling pathways and mitogen withdrawal (Chambers et al. 2009; Douvaras and Fossati 2015; Hu et al. 2009; Shi et al. 2012; Tcw et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2013). Distinct differentiation paradigms have also been developed for mesoderm‐derived microglia (Abud et al. 2017; Douvaras et al. 2017; Haenseler et al. 2017; Muffat et al. 2016; Pandya et al. 2017; Takata et al. 2017). Importantly, despite their relative simplicity, 2D cortical cultures preserve key temporal features of corticogenesis, including the sequential generation of deep‐layer neurons first, upper‐layer neurons, and glial populations (Shi et al. 2012). However, because these systems lack higher‐order tissue organization and expanded germinal zone architecture, they incompletely model many of the cellular and spatial features associated with human cortical expansion.

3D brain organoid technology has emerged as a powerful platform for modeling the spatial and temporal complexity of human corticogenesis in vitro. Unlike 2D monolayer cultures, organoids more efficiently recapitulate the progenitor organization and cellular diversity of the developing cortex, making them particularly valuable for studying mechanisms underlying cortical expansion across species. Early self‐organized cortical organoids generated from PSCs demonstrated the spontaneous formation of neuroepithelial structures resembling the developing cerebral cortex, while subsequent incorporation of extracellular matrix support and developmental patterning strategies enabled the generation of increasingly complex and reproducible cortical tissues (Eiraku et al. 2008; Kadoshima et al. 2013; Lancaster et al. 2013). These organoids develop ventricular‐like rosette structures containing VZ‐ and SVZ‐like germinal zones, including a distinct oSVZ‐like compartment enriched for oRG (Lancaster et al. 2013). Importantly, organoids preserve the sequential inside‐out generation of cortical layers, prolonged neurogenesis, and later gliogenesis characteristic of human corticogenesis, features difficult to achieve in simpler 2D systems (Pasca et al. 2015; Sloan et al. 2017).

The cellular and molecular fidelity of cortical organoids has been extensively validated through transcriptomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and spatial profiling approaches, demonstrating strong correspondence with fetal cortical development (Ditzer et al. 2025; Elmkvist et al. 2024; He et al. 2024; Kanton et al. 2019; Uzquiano et al. 2022). Critically, cortical organoid systems can now be generated from multiple mammalian species, including mouse, macaque, chimpanzee, gorilla, orangutan, and human PSCs, enabling direct comparative analyses of species‐specific developmental programs in experimentally accessible systems (Benito‐Kwiecinski et al. 2021; Lloyd‐Davies Sánchez et al. 2024; Nolbrant et al. 2025; Pollen et al. 2019). Many of these comparative organoid studies have been transcriptionally or epigenetically characterized (Table 1), revealing preservation of species‐specific developmental tempo in vitro. For example, human cortical NPs transition from deep‐layer to upper‐layer neurogenesis later than macaque NPs, while single‐cell transcriptomic analyses demonstrated delayed neurogenic‐to‐gliogenic transitions in human relative to chimpanzee and macaque organoids (Kanton et al. 2019; Otani et al. 2016). In addition, human NPs exhibit prolonged mitotic progression compared with chimpanzee NPs, suggesting that species‐specific differences in cell‐cycle dynamics may contribute to human cortical expansion (Mora‐Bermúdez et al. 2016). Together, these findings establish cortical organoids as a uniquely powerful experimental platform for dissecting the cellular, temporal, and molecular mechanisms underlying human brain evolution.

Insights Into Human Cortical Expansion From Organoid Studies

Comparative organoid studies are beginning to reveal the cellular and molecular programs that contribute to human cortical expansion. Rather than arising from a single evolutionary change, increased cortical size and complexity appear to involve coordinated alterations in multiple processes, including metabolism, gene regulation, and signaling activity. Functional interrogation of human‐specific genetic variants in organoid systems has provided important mechanistic insight into how these pathways collectively influence NP behavior and neuronal production. In the following sections, we highlight representative organoid studies that illuminate how species‐specific developmental programs contribute to expanded neurogenesis and prolonged corticogenesis.

Metabolism

Increasing evidence suggests that metabolic regulation, particularly mitochondrial function, plays a central role in coordinating developmental timing and NP expansion during human corticogenesis. Mitochondrial dynamics have emerged as an important regulator of progenitor behavior, influencing whether NPs undergo self‐renewal or commit to neuronal differentiation. Iwata et al. demonstrated that post‐mitotic newborn neurons acquire a short period of fate plasticity immediately after mitosis, during which increased mitochondrial fusion promotes self‐renewal, whereas enhanced mitochondrial fission biases cells toward neuronal differentiation (Iwata et al. 2020). Interestingly, the window of fate plasticity is lengthened in human relative to mouse progenitors, potentially providing human NPs with increased opportunity for self‐renewal and progenitor pool expansion (Iwata et al. 2020). Beyond NP fate decision, mitochondrial metabolism also controls the pace of neuronal maturation. Mouse cortical neurons, which mature more rapidly than human neurons, exhibit greater mitochondrial metabolic activity, suggesting that slower metabolic progression may contribute to the prolonged developmental timing characteristic of human corticogenesis (Iwata et al. 2023).

Several human‐specific genetic changes implicated in cortical expansion appear to converge on mitochondrial and metabolic pathways. ARHGAP11B, a human‐specific partial duplication of ARHGAP11A that emerged after divergence from the chimpanzee lineage, promotes NP proliferation through a splice site mutation unique to the human gene (Florio et al. 2015, 2016). ARHGAP11B is specifically expressed in SVZ progenitors and enhances NP expansion in part through induction of mitochondrial metabolic programs (Namba et al. 2020; Xing et al. 2024). Consistent with its proposed role in human cortical expansion, ectopic expression of ARHGAP11B in chimpanzee cortical organoids increases NP abundance (Fischer et al. 2022). Human‐specific structural genomic variation has also been linked to metabolic regulation of corticogenesis. Johansson et al. identified a species‐specific variation in a tandem repeat region downstream of the KRAB zinc‐finger protein ZNF558, where shortening of the repeat sequence in humans permits NP‐specific ZNF558 expression not observed in chimpanzees (Johansson et al. 2022). ZNF558 represses SPATA18, a regulator of mitophagy, and CRISPR interference (CRISPRi)‐mediated repression of ZNF558 reduces human cortical organoid size, implicating mitochondrial quality‐control pathways in human cortical growth.

Lipid metabolism has similarly emerged as an important regulator of progenitor behavior and developmental pacing. Human TKTL1 increases oRG abundance and upper‐layer neurogenesis relative to the archaic TKTL1 variant conserved in Neanderthals, by promoting fatty acid synthesis, potentially supporting the extensive cellular process characteristic of oRG (Pinson et al. 2022). Most recently, comparative organoid studies demonstrated increased mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation in mouse NPs compared to human, contributing to species differences in developmental tempo (Iwata et al. 2025). Experimental induction of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation accelerated developmental progression in human cortical organoids, further supporting a mechanistic link between metabolic state, heterochronic development, and cortical expansion. Together, these findings suggest that metabolic rewiring may represent a fundamental mechanism through which human cortical development achieves extended progenitor maintenance, delayed maturation, and increased neuronal output.

Epigenetic and Transcriptional Regulation

Many evolutionary differences underlying human corticogenesis are thought to arise from alterations in gene regulation rather than large‐scale changes in protein‐coding sequences. Human accelerated regions (HARs) are genomic sequences that remained highly conserved throughout vertebrate evolution but accumulated an unusual number of nucleotide substitutions in the human lineage (Hubisz and Pollard 2014). Because many HARs are located within noncoding regions of the genome, they have been proposed to function as developmental regulatory elements that modulate gene expression during cortical development. Comparative epigenomic analyses in human and chimpanzee PSC‐derived NPs identified many HARs associated with active enhancer signatures and open chromatin states, suggesting widespread evolutionary divergence in regulatory activity during corticogenesis (Whalen et al. 2023). Consistent with this idea, massively parallel reporter assays demonstrated that orthologous human and chimpanzee HAR sequences frequently exhibit differential enhancer activity in NPs (Girskis et al. 2021; Whalen et al. 2023). Functional evidence further supporting a role for HARs in cortical expansion came from CRISPR‐based perturbation screens, where disruptions of several putative cortical enhancers reduced human NP abundance (Geller et al. 2024). Together, these findings suggest that evolutionary rewiring of enhancer activity may contribute to species‐specific regulation of progenitor proliferation and developmental timing.

In addition to broad comparative analyses of regulatory evolution, several studies have functionally characterized individual human‐specific regulatory mechanisms implicated in cortical expansion. TBC1D3, a hominoid‐specific gene generated through a segmental duplication event on chromosome 17, promotes NP proliferation and expansion of an oRG‐like population in the mouse cortex (Ju et al. 2016). Mechanistically, TBC1D3 inhibits the histone methyltransferase G9a, linking chromatin regulation to progenitor maintenance (Hou et al. 2021). Overexpression of TBC1D3 in human cortical organoids enhances NP proliferation, whereas knockdown promotes premature neuronal differentiation (Hou et al. 2021). Human HAR1984 provides another example of species‐specific regulatory evolution affecting corticogenesis. Compared with the chimpanzee ortholog, human HAR1984 exhibits greater enhancer activity and increases radial glial and neuronal abundance in chimpanzee cortical organoids (Mosti et al. 2025). Mechanistically, human HAR1984 promotes chromosomal interactions with the target genes ETV5 and TRA2B, thereby elevating their expression, while recruitment of the transcription factor ETV5 further amplifies ETV5 expression through a positive feedback mechanism. Similarly, IRF1, a transcription factor recently identified to be uniquely expressed in human, but not mouse, radial glia, upregulates JUNB and cell cycle progression genes, providing another example of human‐specific transcriptional regulation supporting progenitor expansion (Javed et al. 2026). Together, these studies illustrate how evolutionary modification of chromatin architecture and gene regulatory activity can fine‐tune developmental gene expression programs to promote prolonged progenitor expansion and increased cortical complexity in humans.

Cell Signaling Pathways

Comparative organoid studies have demonstrated that human cortical expansion is associated with species‐specific modulation of conserved developmental signaling pathways. Several human‐specific genetic changes appear to enhance progenitor maintenance and delay neuronal differentiation through altered NOTCH and WNT pathway activity during corticogenesis. NOTCH2NL is a human‐specific gene family consisting of four paralogs that arose from partial duplication of NOTCH2 (Fiddes et al. 2018). NOTCH2NL genes exhibit NP‐biased expression in the developing human cortex, and NOTCH2NL overexpression increases self‐renewal capacity of human NPs in vitro (Fiddes et al. 2018; Suzuki et al. 2018). Functional studies further demonstrated that ectopic expression of NOTCH2NL delays neuronal differentiation and increases mouse cortical organoid size, whereas NOTCH2NL deletion causes premature neuronal differentiation and reduces human cortical organoid size, solidifying the role of NOTCH2NL in human cortical expansion by prolonging NP maintenance and extending developmental timing (Fiddes et al. 2018).

Human‐specific regulatory changes affecting WNT signaling have similarly been implicated in cortical expansion. The human accelerated regulatory enhancer HARE5 is located near FZD8, which encodes a receptor involved in canonical WNT signaling (Boyd et al. 2015). Comparative studies demonstrated that the human HARE5 sequence increases NP proliferation more strongly than the chimpanzee ortholog in both mouse and in PSC‐derived neural cultures (Boyd et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2025). Furthermore, mice carrying the human HARE5 have larger cortices and increased excitatory neuronal production, supporting the idea that evolutionary modification of developmental signaling pathways can directly influence progenitor expansion and cortical neuronal output (Liu et al. 2025). Together, these studies illustrate how human‐specific genetic and regulatory changes may fine‐tune conserved signaling networks to promote sustained neurogenesis and increased cortical complexity.

Cellular Architecture and Morphology

Comparative organoid studies have further shown that species‐specific differences in progenitor cellular architecture and morphology contribute to prolonged expansion during human corticogenesis. During early cortical development, neuroepithelial cells undergo a morphological transition toward a less proliferative state, and this transition occurs later in humans than in other great apes, thereby extending the proliferative phase of early NPs (Benito‐Kwiecinski et al. 2021). Human‐specific genetic changes affecting subcellular structure have also been implicated in cortical expansion. CROCCP2, a human‐specific partial duplication of the ciliary gene CROCC, promotes proliferation of basal progenitors within the SVZ by shortening primary cilia and enhancing mTOR signaling (Van Heurck et al. 2023). Together with metabolic, transcriptional, and signaling adaptations, evolutionary modification of progenitor morphology and cellular architecture represents an additional mechanism through which human corticogenesis achieves extended progenitor maintenance and increased neuronal output.

Limitations and Future Perspectives

Limitations of Current Comparative Organoid Models

Despite their substantial utility for modeling human corticogenesis, current cortical organoid systems incompletely recapitulate several important features of in vivo brain development. While cortical organoids effectively model early fetal corticogenesis, they incompletely capture later developmental processes such as circuit refinement, synaptic pruning, and advanced glial maturation, which are substantially prolonged in humans compared with other primates. One major limitation is the absence of vascularization, which restricts oxygen and nutrient diffusion during long‐term culture and contributes to cellular stress, necrosis, and impaired maturation in large organoids. Because metabolic regulation and developmental timing appear closely linked to cortical expansion, these physiological limitations may influence the very evolutionary mechanisms under investigation. These limitations are particularly relevant when interpreting species‐specific differences in developmental tempo, as long‐term in vitro cultures may not fully reproduce the environmental and physiological context required for later stages of human cortical maturation. In addition, cortical organoids lack many extrinsic developmental inputs present in vivo, including immune, sensory, and long‐range circuit interactions, all of which may shape progenitor behavior and neuronal maturation during corticogenesis.

Another important challenge is the variability and incomplete reproducibility of organoid systems across differentiation protocols, PSC lines, and species. Furthermore, cellular subtypes are not as well represented due to increased glycolytic stress in organoids compared to the fetal brain (Bhaduri et al. 2020). Although improvements in directed patterning strategies have increased consistency, comparative studies remain complicated by differences in developmental timing, cellular composition, and regional identity between organoids generated from different species. Moreover, there are additional aspects contributing to human cortical evolution, including relative expansion of the association cortex and subplate regions, as well as increased connectivity between cortical areas (Ardesch et al. 2019; Duque et al. 2016; Hill et al. 2010; Kostovic and Rakic 1990). However, cortical organoid models are not entirely suitable for studying these differences due to their lack of areal specification and cellular maturation.

Mosaic and Composite Organoids

Emerging organoid technologies are increasingly enabling more precise interrogation of species‐specific developmental mechanisms during corticogenesis. One major challenge in comparative studies is distinguishing cell‐intrinsic evolutionary differences from effects introduced by distinct extracellular environments or culture conditions across species. To address this, mosaic and composite organoid systems have recently been developed in which PSCs from multiple individuals or species are cultured within the same environment. Mosaic organoids generated from pooled human PSC lines have been used to reduce line‐to‐line variability while preserving developmental trajectories and cellular diversity (Antón‐Bolaños et al. 2024; Caporale et al. 2025). Composite human–chimpanzee PSC systems, which involve the cellular fusion of human and chimpanzee PSCs, have enabled direct interrogation of cis‐ and trans‐regulatory contributions to species‐specific gene expression during neurodevelopment, identifying human‐specific regulation of genes such as SSTR2 that are involved in neuronal calcium signaling (Agoglia et al. 2021). Intriguingly, composite PSC systems also revealed preferential retention of human mitochondria over chimpanzee mitochondria, suggesting that evolutionary divergence in mitochondrial genomes may influence cellular metabolism even between closely related primate species (Schaefer et al. 2025; Schmitz et al. 2025). Together, these approaches provide increasingly sophisticated platforms for disentangling how intrinsic genetic programs, metabolism, and extracellular signaling collectively shape species‐specific developmental timing and progenitor behavior.

Scalable Functional Interrogation of Evolutionary Mechanisms

Advances in scalable functional genomics are also expanding the ability to systematically interrogate human‐specific developmental mechanisms in organoid systems. CRISPR‐based perturbation screens combined with single‐cell transcriptomics, lineage tracing, and CROP‐seq approaches have already identified regulators of NP fate specification and neurodevelopmental disease phenotypes in human cortical organoids (Esk et al. 2020; Fleck et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023; Meng et al. 2023). However, most studies to date have focused primarily on protein‐coding genes within human systems alone. Extending these approaches to comparative organoid models may enable large‐scale functional interrogation of HARs, species‐specific enhancers, and other evolutionary genetic innovations implicated in cortical expansion. Recent CRISPR perturbation studies targeting putative neurodevelopmental enhancers in human NP cultures have already identified cis‐regulatory elements important for progenitor proliferation, highlighting the feasibility of systematically interrogating noncoding evolutionary mechanisms in developing neural tissue (Geller et al. 2024).

Comparative Studies of oRG and Progenitor Diversity

Given the proposed importance of oRG in cortical expansion, comparative functional studies of progenitor diversity remain a major future direction in the field. While previous studies have identified species‐specific differences in oRG abundance, proliferative behavior, and transcriptional profiles between human and other mammalian species, the molecular mechanisms governing these differences remain incompletely understood (Gertz et al. 2014; Vaid et al. 2018). Recent work combining CRISPRi perturbation, single‐cell transcriptomics, and lineage tracing in primary human fetal radial glia cultures identified transcription factors capable of modulating developmental tempo and cell fate decisions in both human and macaque progenitors (Ding et al. 2026). Applying similar approaches to long‐term comparative organoid cultures may help clarify how species‐specific progenitor populations contribute to prolonged neurogenesis and increased cortical complexity in humans. Moreover, most of the existing studies focused on comparisons between humans and nonhuman primates to study the most recent evolutionary events. However, other species with large, gyrencephalic cortices, such as dolphins and elephants, have been postulated to contain oRG. Skin fibroblast and iPSC cultures have successfully been derived from dolphins and elephants, respectively, raising the possibility of generating organoids and studying oRG from these species (Appleton et al. 2024; Ferretti et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2024). Inclusion of non‐primate species for comparative studies would reveal both highly conserved and primate‐specific mechanisms of oRG biology.

Beyond the Cortex

Future comparative studies will likely expand beyond the cerebral cortex to additional brain regions that also underwent evolutionary diversifications in humans. Although the neocortex has experienced substantial expansion during human evolution, other regions including the cerebellum and ganglionic eminence also exhibit increased complexity and cellular diversity relative to other species (Barton and Venditti 2014; Horton and Paredes 2025). Organoid protocols modeling these regions have been developed (Aprile et al. 2025; Atamian et al. 2024; Birey et al. 2021; Miura et al. 2020; Scuderi et al. 2025). There have been observations that progenitor populations with oRG‐like features exist outside the cortex, including within the human cerebellar rhombic lip and the ganglionic eminence (Haldipur et al. 2019; Keefe and Nowakowski 2020; Micali et al. 2023; Shi et al. 2021; Zhao et al. 2022). These findings raise the possibility that developmental mechanisms underlying cortical expansion, including prolonged progenitor maintenance and increased progenitor diversity, may contribute more broadly to the evolution of other brain regions. Comparative organoid studies across multiple brain regions may therefore help determine whether shared or region‐specific developmental programs underlie the expansion and increased complexity of the human brain.

Beyond Transcription

Increasing evidence further suggests that species‐specific developmental programs are regulated across multiple molecular layers beyond gene expression alone. Comparative studies have implicated alternative splicing, chromatin state, histone modification, gene silencing, and protein turnover kinetics in regulating developmental tempo and neural differentiation across species. For example, alternative splicing (AS) has been proposed to contribute substantially to species‐specific neuronal functions and developmental programs (Barbosa‐Morais et al. 2012). The brain is also enriched for microexons, a specialized class of short exons (3‐27 nucleotides) that become increasingly included during neuronal maturation and are frequently dysregulated in neurodevelopmental disorders (Irimia et al. 2014). NOVA1 encodes a splicing regulator during cortical development, which interestingly contains a modern human‐specific genetic variant that modifies neuronal connectivity relative to archaic NOVA1 (Trujillo et al. 2021). Given that AS is crucial for proper cortical development and has evolved across species, it would be of interest to identify differential splicing events between species during organoid development. Single‐cell AS analysis utilizing long‐read sequencing has previously been described in human cortical organoids, which could be applied to organoids from other species (Yang et al. 2023). In parallel, epigenetic repression mediated by the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), which catalyzes trimethylation of lysine 27 of histone H3 (H3K27me3), has been shown to regulate the timing of progenitor differentiation and neuronal maturation both in mouse and human systems (Amberg et al. 2022; Pereira et al. 2010). Studies of the segmentation clock and motor neuron differentiation further demonstrated that species‐specific biochemical reaction kinetics and protein turnover rates can influence developmental pace across mammals (Lázaro et al. 2023; Matsuda et al. 2020; Rayon et al. 2020). Emerging multi‐omic approaches incorporating long‐read sequencing, epigenomic profiling, proteomics, and metabolic analyses in comparative organoid systems may therefore provide a more integrated understanding of how developmental timing and progenitor behavior are coordinated during human corticogenesis.

Insights Into Neurological Disorders

Finally, comparative organoid systems may help clarify why the evolutionary processes that enabled human cortical expansion may have also increased vulnerability to neurodevelopmental diseases. Many genes and regulatory elements implicated in NP expansion, developmental timing, and neuronal diversification are also associated with disorders including microcephaly, autism spectrum disorder, schizophrenia, and intellectual disability. Human‐specific genes involved in NP proliferation, such as NOTCH2NL and ARHGAP11B, are associated with congenital brain malformation (Fiddes et al. 2018; Florio et al. 2015; Heide and Huttner 2021). HARs have been found to be enriched for variants associated with autism spectrum disorder and neuropsychiatric diseases such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder (Shin et al. 2024; Whalen et al. 2023). Organoid models derived from patients with neurodevelopmental disorders have additionally revealed abnormalities in NP proliferation, neuronal differentiation, and developmental timing (Ding et al. 2025; Rao et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025). Integrating comparative evolutionary approaches with disease modeling may therefore provide important insight into developmental pathways that are uniquely sensitive in the human brain and may ultimately inform therapeutic strategies targeting early neurodevelopmental processes.

Concluding Remarks

PSC‐derived organoid cultures have transformed the study of human brain evolution by enabling direct experimental investigation of developmental processes previously inaccessible in the human fetal brain. Comparative organoid studies have already revealed distinct contributions of metabolism, gene regulation, signaling, and cellular architecture to species‐specific differences in progenitor expansion and developmental timing. While current systems remain limited in modeling later stages of corticogenesis and broader features of cortical evolution, emerging strategies such as mosaic and composite organoids, large‐scale functional perturbation, and inclusion of non‐primate species and non‐cortical brain regions offer promising avenues to address these challenges. We anticipate that continued advances in comparative organoid studies will deepen our understanding of both human brain evolution and its relationship to neurological diseases.
