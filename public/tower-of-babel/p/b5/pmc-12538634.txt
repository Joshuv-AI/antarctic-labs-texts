Granulomatous Omentitis in Multiple Sheep Associated With Trueperella pyogenes and Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis: A Case Series
Authors: Mohammad Ebrahim Mahaki, Sirous Sadeghian Chaleshtori, Mostafa Abdollahi, Iradj Ashrafi Tamai, Hasti Azarabad, Nima Mahaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGranulomatous omentitis is a relatively rare but significant inflammatory condition in sheep, often associated with persistent bacterial infections. Dual infection with Trueperella pyogenes and Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis is rare, and detailed studies elucidating their combined role in disease pathogenesis remain limited.ObjectivesThis study investigated the occurrence, pathological features, and microbial aetiology of granulomatous omentitis in sheep from a slaughterhouse in Rey, Iran, with a focus on molecular detection of T. pyogenes and C. pseudotuberculosis.MethodsA total of 6000 sheep were inspected post‐mortem between 2022 and 2024. Gross examination identified omental nodules, from which tissue samples were collected. Histopathological analysis, PCR amplification of species‐specific genes, and correlation of molecular findings with histology were performed.ResultsNodular omental lesions were present in 0.5% of examined sheep. Histopathology demonstrated granulomatous inflammation characterized by epithelioid macrophages, multinucleated giant cells, and central caseous necrosis. PCR confirmed co‐infection in all cases (25/25). The pathological and molecular evidence suggests a synergistic role of both pathogens in chronic omental inflammation.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the importance of considering co‐infections in granulomatous omentitis and suggest that future studies should focus on the pathogenesis of dual infections and the development of effective control measures, such as targeted vaccination strategies.

Introduction

Granulomatous inflammation represents a specialized immune response aimed at sequestering and controlling persistent pathogens or irritants resistant to eradication. This histopathological hallmark is characterized by aggregation of activated macrophages (epithelioid cells), multinucleated giant cells formed by macrophage fusion, lymphocytic infiltration, and often central necrosis. In veterinary medicine, granulomatous lesions have been extensively studied in organs such as lungs, lymph nodes, and liver (Anderson 2007; Maxie 2016; Rodríguez‐Largo et al., 2025).

The omentum, a large peritoneal fold, is susceptible to inflammatory insults due to its vascularity and role in immune surveillance. Granulomatous omentitis in sheep is often linked to chronic infections or foreign body reactions; however, specific microbial aetiologies may vary geographically.

Trueperella pyogenes is a Gram‐positive facultative anaerobe, widely recognized as an opportunistic pathogen responsible for diverse suppurative conditions in domestic ruminants (Rzewuska et al. 2019; Wei et al., 2024; Magossi et al., 2025). Virulence factors such as pyolysin, neuraminidase, and extracellular enzymes contribute to its pathogenicity, facilitating tissue invasion and immune modulation (Ribeiro et al., 2015).

Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis, the cause of caseous lymphadenitis (CLA), induces chronic granulomatous abscesses primarily in internal and superficial lymph nodes, but also visceral organs. Its phospholipase D exotoxin enhances vascular permeability, promoting dissemination and lesion formation (Baird and Fontaine 2007; Dorella et al. 2006; Khanamir et al., 2023).

Recent reviews have highlighted the emerging significance of bacterial co‐infections in ruminants, which can alter disease presentation and complicate diagnosis (Aftabuzzaman et al., 2021; Rodríguez‐Largo et al., 2025). Furthermore, the prevalence and dominant strains of pathogens like C. pseudotuberculosis exhibit considerable geographical variation, influencing the clinical and pathological manifestations of diseases such as caseous lymphadenitis (Baird and Fontaine 2007; Khanamir et al., 2023). Despite their recognized pathogenic roles individually, reports of their co‐infection causing granulomatous omentitis in sheep are scarce, particularly from the Middle East region. The synergistic interaction of these bacteria might exacerbate disease severity, complicate diagnosis, and affect flock productivity (Didkowska et al., 2020; Aftabuzzaman et al., 2021). This study provides a detailed investigation of granulomatous omentitis in sheep, analyzing clinical, pathological, and molecular aspects to better understand disease dynamics and inform control strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional observational study was chosen as it is the most efficient design for determining the prevalence and initial characterization of a condition within a specific population at a single point in time (Thrusfield 2018). The slaughterhouse setting provided a unique opportunity for systematic macroscopic examination of a large number of omenta, which would be impractical in a clinical setting. Sampling was targeted to all grossly visible lesions to maximize the chance of identifying the etiological agents.

A cross‐sectional observational study was done in the Shahr‐e Rey slaughterhouse, Iran, between 2022 and 2024. The study aimed to assess the prevalence and characterize granulomatous omental lesions in sheep.

Sample Collection and Processing

Systematic post‐mortem inspection of 6000 sheep was performed. Omental surfaces were inspected visually and palpated to detect nodular lesions. Lesions were measured, described, and sampled aseptically.

Tissues were divided for histopathological examination and molecular testing. Specimens for histology were fixed in 10% formalin, processed according to routine procedures, and finally stained with haematoxylin and eosin (Bancroft and Gamble 2013). Histopathological examinations were performed by the experienced veterinary pathologists using a Nikon Eclipse E600 light microscope at magnifications of 40×, 100×, 400×, and 1000× (oil immersion).

Histopathological Evaluation

Sections were examined microscopically for granuloma morphology, cell types, necrosis patterns, fibrosis, and vascular changes. Lesions were categorized based on established criteria for granulomatous inflammation (Maxie 2016).

DNA Isolation and Polymerase Chain Reaction

DNA was isolated from tissue specimens via a commercial tissue DNA isolation kit (CinnaPure DNA, Cat. No. PR891613, SinaClone, Iran), according to the manufacturer's protocol. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and analysis were conducted by a molecular biologist (Hasti Azarabad). Each PCR reaction had a total volume of 25 µL, comprising 12.5 µL of 2X Amplicon Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark, Cat. No. A180301), 1 µM of each primer, approximately 100 ng of template DNA, and sterile distilled water up to 25 µL.

The identification of T. pyogenes and C. pseudotuberculosis was performed by PCR using genus‐ and species‐specific primers. For T. pyogenes, primers TRU‐F (5′‐GTTTTGCTTGTGATCGTGGTGGTTATGA‐3′) and TRU‐R (5′‐AAGCAGGCCCACGCGCAGG‐3′) were applied to amplify a 122‐bp part of the 16S–23S rDNA intergenic section. For C. pseudotuberculosis, primers Cor‐F (5′‐CCGCACTTTAGTGTGTGTG‐3′) and Cor‐R (5′‐TCTCTACGCCGATCTTGTAT‐3′) targeted an 816‐bp part of the 16S rRNA gene (Dorella et al. 2006; Rzewuska et al. 2019).

PCR amplification was performed using a BioRad T100 Thermal Cycler. PCR products were electrophoresed on 1.0% agarose gels (SinaClone, Iran) in 1X TBE buffer and visualized under ultraviolet (UV) illumination using a BioRad Gel Doc XR+ System. Each PCR sample was prepared in the volume of 25 µL, comprising 12.5 µL of Master Mix (Amplicon, Denmark), 1 pmol of each primer, 100 ng of template DNA, and sterile distilled water to reach the volume of 25 µL.

The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 62°C (for T. pyogenes) or 56°C (for C. pseudotuberculosis) for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 7 min.

The amplification was carried out in a BioRad T100 Thermal Cycler. The PCR products were then separated by electrophoresis on 1.0% agarose gels in 1X TBE buffer, stained with GelRed, and visualized under UV light using a BioRad Gel Doc XR+ Imaging System.

Reference strains of T. pyogenes (PTCC 1925) and C. pseudotuberculosis (ATCC 31833) were used as positive controls in each run.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequency and prevalence (expressed as a percentage with a 95% confidence interval calculated using the Wilson score interval method), were used to summarize lesion prevalence and PCR positivity rates. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics summarized lesion prevalence and PCR positivity rates. Histopathological findings were correlated with molecular results.

Results

Gross Pathology

Nodular lesions (Figure 1) were detected in 25 sheep (0.5%). Lesions were firm, white, and measured 1–4 cm in diameter, predominantly on the greater omentum. Distribution was multifocal, with no gross abnormalities in other visceral organs. Based on dental wear estimation, all affected animals were estimated to be adults (>2 years old).

The observed nodular lesions on the greater omentum of sheep.

Histopathology

As shown in Figure 2, microscopy revealed well‐demarcated granulomas with central caseous necrosis, surrounded by epithelioid macrophages and multinucleated giant cells of Langhans‐type. Peripheral lymphocytes and plasma cells were present. Areas of fibrosis and vascular congestion were common.

(D) Aggregation of epithelioid macrophages (★) with surrounding lymphocytes and plasma cells. H&E staining. Scale bars = A and B: 100 µm and C and D: 50 µm.

Molecular Findings

PCR analysis confirmed the presence of T. pyogenes and C. pseudotuberculosis DNA in all 25 samples (100%; 95% CI: 86.3%–100%), confirming consistent co‐infection (Figure 3).

Discussion

The findings from this case series may have implications beyond ovine medicine. The omentum is a conserved organ in mammals, and its response to chronic infection shares similarities across species. Understanding the dynamics of polymicrobial infections in this immune‐reactive tissue could provide insights into similar pathological processes in other animals and even in human medicine, particularly concerning intra‐abdominal infections and adhesions.

This study has several limitations. The lack of bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing prevents a more comprehensive analysis of the bacterial strains involved and their antibiotic profiles. Furthermore, the cross‐sectional design and the absence of detailed epidemiological data (e.g., age, farm of origin, husbandry practices) limit our ability to infer risk factors for this co‐infection. The sample size, while sufficient for this case series, was ultimately based on prevalence, and future longitudinal studies with larger cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings and explore infection dynamics.

Granulomatous omentitis is an uncommon but important chronic inflammatory condition in sheep, characterized by granuloma formation in the omental tissue. The pathogenesis typically involves persistent infectious agents that evade or resist host immune responses. This study reports granulomatous omentitis associated with co‐infection of T. pyogenes and C. pseudotuberculosis in sheep.

The detection of this co‐infection in Iran is significant. Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis is endemic in Iranian sheep flocks, with seroprevalence rates varying from 10% to 45% across different regions (Esmaeili et al., 2025). Trueperella pyogenes is also a common isolate from suppurative conditions in Iranian ruminants, though specific prevalence data for omental infections are lacking.

Granulomatous inflammation is a hallmark of chronic infection with intracellular or facultative intracellular pathogens. The central caseous necrosis observed histologically in this study is characteristic of infections such as caseous lymphadenitis caused by C. pseudotuberculosis (Baird and Fontaine 2007; Mahaki et al. 2024; Rodríguez‐Largo et al., 2025).

Corynebacterium pseudotuberculosis produces phospholipase D toxin, which facilitates vascular permeability and dissemination, potentially reaching the omentum. Once established, the bacteria provoke a granulomatous immune response as macrophages attempt to isolate the infection.

Trueperella pyogenes is traditionally known as an opportunistic pathogen associated with pyogenic infections. Its presence in granulomatous lesions suggests its capability to persist in tissue, contributing to chronic inflammation. The co‐infection scenario may potentiate the pathogenicity of both bacteria, leading to more extensive omental involvement (Aftabuzzaman et al., 2021). Reports of co‐infection causing granulomatous omentitis are scarce.

Didkowska et al. (2020) described similar findings, indicating that co‐infections may be underdiagnosed due to limitations in routine diagnostic techniques. Our study confirms the coexistence of these pathogens, underlining the importance of molecular diagnostics in chronic inflammatory conditions. Granulomatous omentitis can impair gastrointestinal motility and cause carcass condemnation. The detection of co‐infection suggests that management practices should consider multi‐pathogen control strategies. Vaccination against C. pseudotuberculosis has shown variable efficacy; understanding the role of T. pyogenes in mixed infections might improve immunoprophylaxis (Ribeiro et al., 2015; Magossi et al., 2025).

Integrating histopathology with molecular diagnostics is crucial. Routine culture can be challenging due to the fastidious nature of these bacteria. Thus, PCR assays targeting virulence genes provide a rapid and sensitive approach, as demonstrated in this study.

The study's limitations include the lack of bacterial culture and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Epidemiological data on farm management and animal demographics were also unavailable.

Future research should focus on infection dynamics, immune responses, and vaccine development. Studies exploring pathogen interaction at the omental surface could reveal targets for intervention (Rodríguez‐Largo et al., 2025).

Conclusion

This case series provides compelling evidence of granulomatous omentitis in sheep caused by co‐infection with T. pyogenes and C. pseudotuberculosis. The synergistic pathogenicity likely exacerbates lesion severity and chronicity. These findings, therefore, underscore the critical need for implementing comprehensive diagnostic protocols and integrated management strategies to control such co‐infections in sheep flocks.
