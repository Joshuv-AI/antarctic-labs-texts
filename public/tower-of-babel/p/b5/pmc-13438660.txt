Swarm intelligence-guided ROI selection for deep learning assessment of HER2 in colorectal cancer
Authors: Jiaming Qiu, Yongjun Liu, Zihao Zhang, Xiaoxing Lin, Chao Ling, Haitong Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate assessment of Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2) status in colorectal cancer (CRC) is pivotal for precision therapy, yet the gigapixel resolution of Whole Slide Images (WSIs) presents a significant computational bottleneck for traditional deep learning workflows that rely on exhaustive sliding-window tiling. Addressing this challenge, we propose a novel coarse-to-fine framework that mimics the pathologist’s cognitive screening process by integrating swarm intelligence with deep learning. Specifically, we treat the low-magnification WSI as a two-dimensional search space and employ a Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm to autonomously navigate and identify diagnostically relevant Regions of Interest (ROIs). The PSO search is guided by a fitness function based on color deconvolution metrics—prioritizing the proportion and intensity of 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining—thereby effectively filtering out non-informative background and negative tissue without the need for full-slide scanning. In the subsequent stage, these high-value candidate ROIs are extracted at high resolution and analyzed using state-of-the-art deep learning models, such as ResNet or Swin Transformer, to classify HER2 status. Experimental results demonstrate that this swarm intelligence-driven approach reduces computational overhead while achieving favorable patch-level discrimination by focusing analysis on key pathological areas. To clarify how the selected ROI patches behave at the WSI level, we further report per-WSI prediction-count distributions and include an Attention-Based Multiple Instance Learning (ABMIL) baseline. By combining intelligent sampling with deep learning classification, our method provides an interpretable and computationally efficient ROI preselection framework for digital pathology workflows, while broader multi-center validation will be required before clinical deployment.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality worldwide1,2. While traditional therapies have improved survival rates, the emergence of targeted therapies has shifted the focus toward precision medicine. Specifically, Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2 (HER2) has been identified as a promising therapeutic target in a subset of CRC patients, particularly those resistant to EGFR inhibitors3,4. Consequently, the accurate assessment of HER2 overexpression is critical for identifying patients who may benefit from anti-HER2 therapies, such as trastuzumab and lapatinib5.

In routine clinical practice, HER2 status is evaluated by pathologists using immunohistochemistry (IHC) on whole slide images (WSIs). This process involves a visual scan of the slide to identify invasive tumor regions, followed by a semi-quantitative scoring of membrane staining intensity (1+, 2+, 3+). However, manual assessment is inherently subjective, labor-intensive, and prone to inter-observer variability, especially in cases with intratumoral heterogeneity6. The advent of digital pathology and deep learning (DL) has offered automated solutions to standardize this process, with numerous studies demonstrating the efficacy of Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) in cancer diagnosis and prognosis7,8.

Despite these advancements, the gigapixel resolution of WSIs poses a significant computational challenge. The prevailing approach in computational pathology is the “sliding window” or exhaustive tiling strategy, where the entire tissue region is tessellated into tens of thousands of small patches for analysis9–11. While effective, this brute-force method is computationally expensive and inefficient. In many CRC slides, a substantial portion of the tissue consists of stroma, necrosis, or normal mucosa12, which are irrelevant for HER2 scoring but consume vast processing resources13,14. Furthermore, training models on such massive datasets often requires extensive pixel-level or patch-level annotations, which are difficult to obtain.

To mitigate the computational burden, recent studies have explored two complementary directions: attention-based multiple instance learning (MIL) for weakly supervised slide-level prediction and coarse-filtering techniques (e.g., Otsu’s thresholding) for removing obvious background13,14. Attention-based MIL methods are primarily designed to learn slide-level decisions from patch-level information under slide-level supervision; their attention maps may also highlight discriminative regions, but region localization is a byproduct rather than the primary objective. At the same time, these frameworks still typically require feature extraction from a large set of tissue patches before importance weighting and slide-level decision learning. In contrast, simple thresholding techniques lack the semantic awareness to distinguish tumor-relevant staining from non-specific artifacts. Therefore, there remains a need for an “intelligent” preselection strategy that can mimic the pathologist’s cognitive workflow: rapidly scanning the slide at low magnification to locate suspicious “hotspots” before zooming in for detailed assessment.

In this study, we propose a novel coarse-to-fine framework that integrates Swarm Intelligence with deep learning to accelerate HER2 assessment in CRC. Inspired by the foraging behavior of biological swarms, we model the low-magnification WSI as a 2D search space and employ Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to autonomously navigate and identify diagnostic Regions of Interest (ROIs). Unlike exhaustive tiling, our PSO algorithm is guided by a biologically interpretable fitness function based on color deconvolution, specifically targeting the intensity and proportion of 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining. This allows the algorithm to effectively ignore irrelevant regions and focus computational resources on high-value tumor areas. These selected candidates are then classified by a deep neural network (e.g., ResNet/Swin Transformer) to predict HER2 status. To relate the proposed patch-level module to slide-level WSI analysis, we additionally examine the per-WSI distribution of ResNet50 predictions from PSO-selected ROI patches and compare the results with an ABMIL baseline. Related work

Deep learning in whole slide image analysis

The emergence of whole slide imaging (WSI) has transformed pathology from a qualitative to a quantitative discipline. Deep learning (DL), especially Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), has become the cornerstone of modern computational pathology15,16. Early studies focused on patch-based supervised learning, where pathologists manually annotated regions of interest (ROIs) for model training17,18. While effective for specific tasks such as mitosis detection or nuclei segmentation, these approaches scale poorly for slide-level classification due to the enormous size of WSIs and the scarcity of pixel-level annotations.

To alleviate the annotation bottleneck, Weakly Supervised Learning (WSL) has emerged as a dominant paradigm. Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) treats a WSI as a “bag” of instances (patches) and requires only a slide-level label. Representative methods, such as CLAM (Clustering-constrained Attention Multiple Instance Learning)19 and DSMIL (Dual-Stream MIL)20, employ attention mechanisms to automatically identify diagnostically relevant sub-regions. These methods learn slide-level labels from thousands of patch features (e.g., cancer subtype or genetic mutation). However, they typically rely on exhaustive tiling: all tissue regions must first be tessellated and features extracted before attention can assign importance weights. This “scan-all-then-select” paradigm incurs significant computational and storage overhead, limiting deployment in resource-constrained clinical settings21.

More recently, Transformer-based MIL frameworks and Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have been introduced to capture long-range dependencies across patches22,23. While these approaches achieve higher predictive performance, they still require feature extraction for all patches, resulting in substantial computational burden.

Automated HER2 assessment in colorectal cancer

HER2 testing is pivotal for selecting patients eligible for targeted therapies and has been extensively studied in breast and gastric cancers24. In colorectal cancer (CRC), HER2-positive tumors often exhibit significant intratumoral heterogeneity and unique basolateral membrane staining patterns, posing additional challenges for automated analysis25. The prevalence of HER2 positivity in CRC is approximately 5–15%, and it is associated with resistance to EGFR inhibitors, highlighting the clinical importance of accurate HER2 assessment.

Existing automated HER2 scoring methods can be categorized into classical image analysis and deep learning-based approaches. Classical methods rely on color deconvolution to separate hematoxylin and DAB channels, followed by thresholding to quantify staining intensity26. These methods are computationally inexpensive but lack semantic understanding and are susceptible to background noise or stromal staining. Deep learning methods have achieved high accuracy in breast cancer HER2 scoring27,28, but direct application to CRC often performs suboptimally due to differences in tissue morphology, staining protocols, and intratumoral heterogeneity. Moreover, most DL-based approaches still depend on pathologist-guided ROI selection or random sampling, introducing selection bias and failing to fully capture tumor heterogeneity.

Intelligent sampling and ROI selection strategies

ROI selection is a critical preprocessing step that significantly impacts downstream model performance. Common strategies include simple tissue segmentation (e.g., Otsu thresholding) followed by random or grid-based sampling. Although such methods remove white background, they often retain large amounts of non-diagnostic tissue, such as fat, necrosis, and normal mucosa29,30. Hard-mining approaches leverage a coarse classifier to filter patches, but this requires training an additional model and performing inference on the entire slide, which partially negates the computational savings.

Reinforcement Learning (RL) has also been explored for WSI ROI selection. RL agents can learn policies to select high-value patches at low magnification, emulating a pathologist’s coarse-to-fine screening process31. However, RL requires extensive training data and policy networks, and the interpretability of the learned strategy is often limited.

Swarm Intelligence (SI) algorithms, such as Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), and Genetic Algorithms (GA), have been applied successfully in medical image registration, segmentation, and feature selection due to their fast convergence and efficient search capabilities32. Unlike RL, PSO is a gradient-free optimization method that can directly search for optimal regions based on a predefined fitness function without requiring exhaustive feature extraction. For the present task, PSO is particularly attractive because ROI localization on a low-magnification WSI can be naturally formulated as a continuous two-dimensional optimization problem, and PSO offers a favorable balance of implementation simplicity, search efficiency, and limited hyperparameter burden. Compared with GA or ACO, PSO does not require crossover/mutation design or path-construction rules, making it easier to integrate as a lightweight search module for large WSIs. Despite this suitability, PSO has rarely been explored as an active search agent for ROI selection in digital pathology.

Multi-scale or coarse-to-fine strategies are also widely used, where candidate regions are first screened at low resolution and then refined at high resolution to reduce computational costs33. In this work, PSO should be viewed as a representative and classical SI baseline rather than the only possible optimizer. Our goal is to first establish whether an active swarm-based search paradigm is effective for HER2-oriented ROI localization. Nevertheless, most existing methods rely on thresholding or coarse classifiers, lacking domain-specific, interpretable fitness functions to guide the search towards diagnostically relevant regions. A broader comparison against other SI optimizers is an important direction for future work.

Methods

Our proposed framework consists of a coarse-to-fine pipeline. This structured multi-stage design—combining heuristic search, pathology-informed scoring, and deep learning classification—follows principled practices for systematic experimental design and careful interpretation in hybrid medical image analysis frameworks34. In the first stage, we formulate the ROI selection as a global optimization problem over a low-magnification WSI, solved via Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) guided by a domain-specific fitness function. In the second stage and third stage, the selected ROIs are mapped back to high resolution for downstream deep learning classification.

Color deconvolution and DAB quantification

In IHC-stained slides for HER2, the hematoxylin (H) stain highlights cell nuclei in blue, while the 3,3’-Diaminobenzidine (DAB) stain highlights HER2 protein expression in brown. To construct an effective fitness function, we first decouple these color channels. Importantly, the low-magnification DAB signal is not intended to replace high-magnification morphological assessment; rather, it serves as a coarse screening proxy for prioritizing candidate regions. Because HER2 IHC grading is closely related to the extent and strength of membranous brown staining, the spatial distribution and concentration of DAB at low resolution can provide a practical cue for where diagnostically informative tissue is more likely to reside. The final grade prediction is still produced in the subsequent high-resolution deep learning stage.

Particle swarm optimization for ROI search

Instead of exhaustively sliding a window across the gigapixel WSI, we deploy a swarm of N particles to actively search the 2D space \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {S}$$\end{document} at the low magnification level.

The particles are initialized with uniformly distributed random positions and velocities. Positions are clamped to ensure particles do not fly outside the WSI boundaries.

Diverse top-K ROI selection

After T iterations, the PSO search generates a pool of candidate patches. To ensure the final selected ROIs capture the spatial heterogeneity of the tumor and avoid redundant overlapping samples, we apply a diversity filter. Candidates are sorted in descending order of their fitness scores. A candidate is added to the final set of K ROIs only if its spatial distance to all previously selected ROIs exceeds a minimum threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d_{min}$$\end{document}, and its Intersection over Union (IoU) with any selected ROI is below a threshold \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\theta _{iou}$$\end{document}. These diversity constraints reduce repeated sampling from the same local hotspot and help limit the over-representation of highly overlapping patches in the downstream dataset. Finally, the coordinates of the selected low-magnification ROIs are scaled by the level downsample factor to extract the corresponding high-resolution (Level 0) patches for downstream neural network analysis.

Experimental

Dataset

The dataset utilized in this study was collected from the Department of Pathology, Affiliated Changshu Hospital of Nantong University. A total of 62 whole-slide image (WSI) cases of colorectal cancer were included. Based on clinical HER2 grading, the dataset comprises 31 cases scored as 1+, 22 cases scored as 2+, and 9 cases scored as 3+. This distribution reflects a real-world clinical scenario with a natural class imbalance, characterized by a relative scarcity of strong positive (3+) cases. Given the limited cohort size, especially the small number of 3+ cases, the reported classification results should be interpreted with appropriate caution and viewed as preliminary evidence pending validation on larger and more diverse datasets.

Introduction to platform and parameter settings

All experiments were conducted on a high-performance workstation equipped with an NVIDIA A800 GPU (80 GB memory).

The proposed pipeline involves several critical hyperparameter settings designed to balance computational efficiency and feature sensitivity. In the first stage (low-magnification ROI search), the search was conducted at WSI level 3 to accelerate processing. The swarm intelligence algorithm was initialized with 40 particles and iterated 25 times to identify candidate regions. To improve sampling stability, an additional 200 random samples were drawn. The final selection retained the top 20 ROIs, enforced by diversity constraints including a maximum Intersection over Union (IoU) of 0.3 and a minimum center distance threshold of 0.8, so that highly redundant candidates were automatically excluded. During fitness evaluation, the weights for DAB proportion, DAB intensity, and tissue penalty were uniformly set to 1.0. Regions with excessive white background (mean RGB > 245) were directly penalized.

In the second stage (patch extraction), the selected ROIs were cropped at level 1 using a non-overlapping sliding window strategy with a patch size of 224× 224 pixels. Patches containing predominantly empty background (mean RGB > 240) were discarded.

For the third stage (deep model training), the extracted patches were divided into training and validation sets using a stratified patch-level split ratio of 8:2 with a fixed random seed (42) to ensure reproducibility. Therefore, the reported patch-level quantitative results mainly reflect patch-level discrimination performance under the current experimental design. The models were trained for 200 epochs using a batch size of 512. Standard data augmentation techniques, including random resized cropping to 224× 224 and random horizontal flipping, were applied alongside ImageNet normalization. For convolutional architectures (e.g., ResNet50), we employed the SGD optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001 and a momentum of 0.9. Conversely, for Vision Transformer variants (e.g., ViT-B/16), the AdamW optimizer was utilized with a learning rate of 0.0001 and a weight decay of 0.01 to prevent overfitting.

To further examine how patch-level predictions are distributed at the WSI level, we added a supplementary slide-level evaluation. These slide-level experiments used a stratified slide/patient-level split with a fixed random seed of 42, and all patches from the same WSI were kept within the same split to avoid slide-level leakage. This resulted in 48 training WSIs and 14 held-out WSIs. For the proposed PSO-based pipeline, we report the per-WSI counts of ROI-derived patches classified by ResNet50 as HER2 1+, 2+, or 3+, together with the reference HER2 category for each WSI. In addition, we implemented Attention-Based Multiple Instance Learning (ABMIL) as a representative weakly supervised slide-level baseline, where each WSI is treated as a bag of patch features and supervised only by the slide-level HER2 label. The ABMIL baseline used a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet50 as the feature extractor rather than the patch-level classifier trained in this study. For each WSI, up to 256 tissue patches were sampled at OpenSlide level 1 using a patch size of 224× 224 pixels; tissue filtering used a white threshold of 235 and a minimum tissue fraction of 0.25. Input patches were resized to 256 pixels, center-cropped to 224× 224, and normalized using ImageNet statistics. The ABMIL module used a hidden dimension of 256, an attention dimension of 128, and dropout of 0.25, and was trained for 30 epochs using AdamW with a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document} and a weight decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1\times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}. ABMIL slide-level performance was evaluated using accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, weighted F1-score, class-wise precision, recall, F1-score, and support.

Multi-component medical imaging systems benefit from explicit baselines and component-oriented analysis35. The objective of this experiment is to evaluate the search efficiency in identifying high-value Regions of Interest (ROIs) across Whole Slide Images (WSIs) of varying HER2 grades (e.g., 1+, 2+, and 3+).

For both methods, we recorded the best fitness score found over the number of evaluations. The fitness function comprehensively accounts for DAB staining intensity, DAB positive ratio, and valid tissue area fraction. As illustrated in the convergence curves across multiple representative slides (such as Slides 2201428A, 2201804B, B2210805A, and B2301817B), PSO consistently outperforms Random Search.

While both algorithms require nearly identical computational times for the same number of evaluations, PSO achieves significantly higher best fitness scores and converges in fewer iterations. By sharing information among particles and leveraging historical and global best positions, PSO can more rapidly guide the search towards regions with intense and concentrated DAB staining. In contrast, Random Search relies on uniformly distributed independent samples, which may fail to locate high-value regions within a limited evaluation budget. Discussion

This study presents a coarse-to-fine framework for HER2 status classification in colorectal cancer WSIs by integrating swarm intelligence with deep learning, aiming to address the computational challenges posed by gigapixel-scale images. By formulating low-magnification WSIs as a search space and leveraging PSO to identify diagnostically relevant ROIs, the proposed method effectively reduces redundant computation associated with exhaustive sliding-window approaches. The experimental results demonstrate that this strategy substantially decreases training time and improves patch-level classification performance across both CNN- and Transformer-based models. In response to the need for slide-level interpretation, we further added supplementary per-WSI prediction-count analysis for PSO-selected ROI patches and an ABMIL baseline. These experiments clarify whether the selected ROI patches show class distributions consistent with the reference WSI labels, while also highlighting that slide-level HER2 assessment remains more challenging than patch-level discrimination.

The proposed method should therefore be understood as an interpretable ROI preselection and patch analysis module rather than a replacement for weakly supervised slide-level MIL frameworks. MIL methods such as ABMIL are designed to learn slide-level decisions from bags of patch features under slide-level supervision. In contrast, our PSO component actively searches for DAB-enriched candidate ROIs before downstream classification. The two paradigms are complementary: PSO can reduce the number of candidate patches and provide pathology-informed regions, whereas MIL can learn a slide-level prediction from instance-level information. The per-WSI prediction-count analysis provides a transparent description of the class distribution among PSO-selected ROI patches, and the ABMIL experiment provides a representative weakly supervised comparison.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the PSO-based search strategy, while effective in reducing the search space, may suffer from convergence to local optima, potentially leading to suboptimal ROI selection in certain cases. This limitation could affect the completeness of diagnostically relevant region coverage, particularly in highly heterogeneous tissue samples. Second, the current fitness function relies primarily on color deconvolution metrics, which, although interpretable, may not fully capture complex morphological patterns associated with HER2 expression. In particular, using low-magnification DAB density as a screening cue is a pragmatic task-specific assumption rather than a definitive surrogate for high-resolution diagnostic evidence. Incorporating more advanced or learnable fitness criteria, such as high-resolution pathology foundation model embeddings, could further test and strengthen this assumption. Third, the present study uses a relatively limited single-center cohort, with an imbalanced class distribution and only 9 cases in the HER2 3+ category. As a result, the reported results should be interpreted with caution, especially when considering broader clinical deployment. Although supplementary slide-level analyses were added, these experiments remain preliminary because of the limited number of WSIs and the absence of external validation. HER2 staining patterns in CRC may vary across institutions due to differences in specimen preparation, staining protocols, and scanning devices. Additionally, while the proposed method reduces computational overhead during training, the iterative nature of PSO still introduces additional complexity compared to purely feedforward pipelines.

Future work will therefore focus on evaluating the framework on larger public or multi-center datasets when available, comparing PSO with other swarm-intelligence optimizers, testing higher-resolution feature-guided ROI scoring, and integrating PSO-selected ROIs more deeply into weakly supervised slide-level MIL pipelines for a more clinically realistic assessment.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study proposes a novel swarm intelligence-driven coarse-to-fine framework for efficient HER2 assessment in colorectal cancer WSIs. By integrating PSO-based ROI selection with state-of-the-art deep learning models, the method alleviates the computational burden of gigapixel image analysis while maintaining favorable patch-level performance. Supplementary per-WSI prediction-count analysis and an ABMIL baseline further examine how selected ROI patches relate to WSI-level HER2 categories. Although challenges such as potential convergence to local optima, limited cohort size, lack of external validation, and preliminary slide-level evaluation remain, the framework provides an interpretable and computationally efficient ROI preselection component for future digital pathology pipelines. Future work will focus on enhancing the robustness of the search strategy, validating the method on larger public and multi-center cohorts, and integrating the proposed ROI selection strategy into more comprehensive weakly supervised slide-level assessment protocols.
