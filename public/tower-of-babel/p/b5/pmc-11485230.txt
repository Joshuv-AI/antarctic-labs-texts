Spectral location for the universal scaling regime in Martian atmospheric turbulence
Authors: Miya C Y Coimbra, Manuel de la Torre Juárez, Beverley J McKeon, Mercedes Marín, Naomi Murdoch, Sara Navarro, José Antonio Rodríguez-Manfredi, Alexander Stott
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtmospheric turbulence, irregular fluctuations of the fluid state, is studied on Mars. Universality of the turbulence spectrum underpins atmospheric models where computational requirements preclude full fidelity simulations of the smallest scales. However, there are discrepancies among reports on the existence and spectral location of universal scaling in Martian atmospheric data. Here, results indicate the smallest resolvable structures from Martian wind speed data are still associated with the energetic regime, which may ultimately explain why multiple reports have not found a consistent Kolmogorov-like spectral regime on Mars. Universal spectral scaling of wind data from Perseverance’s Mars Environmental Dynamics Analyzer is used to estimate the thresholds that separate three turbulence regimes: energetic, inertial, and molecular dissipation. Wind measurements at 2-Hz, the fastest sampling rate for direct wind sensor measurements on Mars, resolves turbulence in the energetic regime and approaches the inertial regime, which is consistent with reported Martian dust devil sizes.Subject terms: Atmospheric dynamics, Atmospheric dynamics, Inner planets

Introduction

Turbulence is a multiscale phenomenon with temporal and spatial scales that extend over orders of magnitudes—time scales span from seconds to hours, and length scales range from centimeters to kilometers in the Earth’s and Mars’s atmospheric boundary layers. Turbulence effects extend from small near-surface gusts to large planetary-scale circulation patterns, influence flight dynamics, structures, aeolian interactions between the atmosphere and surface, and the modeling of atmospheric processes. Spatially large-scale forcing, modulated by the local topography, or effects with long temporal periods such as the diurnal cycle, which heats the planet’s surface and thereby injects energy into the atmosphere, contribute to the large scales present in atmospheric turbulence. The energy associated with these large scales is transferred to smaller scales, where it is ultimately dissipated at the smallest scales through the action of molecular viscosity. The range of scales where molecular dissipation dominates is referred to as the dissipation regime. Universal scaling1 occurs on a range of scales where the turbulence statistics are uniform across flows with similar geometry, regardless of the physical processes forcing the flow. This range of scales is classified into the inertial and dissipation regimes. By contrast, the energetic regime, forced by the local surface heating and large-scale pressure gradients and modulated by the topography, is not universal and contains the information that makes it discernible from other flows. As a result, resolving the inertial subrange provides a form of verification that all unique flow information has been captured.

Studies of atmospheric fluctuations on Earth have provided an understanding of the universal properties of near-surface turbulence on our planet. Now, instruments on Perseverance allow us to verify the “universality” of turbulence scaling on another planet. Since the first landing on Mars, instruments aboard the different landers and rovers have analyzed wind and temperature2–5, pressure6, and sound waves7 at various sampling frequencies. Turbulence scaling theories for these variables implicitly assume the existence of the inertial regime in velocity fluctuations. However, a consistent inertial regime that would follow universal scaling predictions, specifically, the −5/3 slope that is characteristic of the inertial regime in the wind speed power spectrum8–11, has remained elusive for Martian data in all these variables. Previous works reported varying expressions for spectral scaling in the inertial regime, indicating non-universal scaling laws. Prior analyses of Viking and Phoenix temperature fluctuations2,3 reported occasional −5/3 slope occurrences, while at other times, measurements showed a steeper scaling, implying a faster energy dissipation rate and a non-Kolmogorov-like scaling. Perseverance’s Mars environmental dynamics analyzer (MEDA) temperature measurements5 show that the high-frequency regimes can be separated into two dynamical regimes with different slopes whose scaling changes independently with the time of the sol (a Martian day). During the daytime, spectral slopes approached the universal −5/3 scaling primarily in a lower frequency range than predicted by previous estimates2, indicating an inconsistency in the frequency range of the inertial regime. These discrepancies between previous studies, from the expected frequency range where a −5/3 slope was reported to the variability of the steepness of the slope itself, motivated this study.

Past studies have subsequently interpreted the turbulence regimes on Mars by using models and results developed for Earth’s atmosphere. Estimates for the Kolmogorov length, the characteristic length scale for the regime in which molecular dissipation dominates, for the Martian atmosphere3,12,13 have been calculated theoretically using a rescaling of empirically determined fits to terrestrial measurements14–16. However, because the terrestrial and Martian atmospheres may exhibit different behaviors, this study introduces an alternative approach using observational data to avoid extrapolations that rely on constants tuned to Earth.

This work uses spectral scaling analysis to determine whether turbulence regimes can be resolved with the current equipment designed for large-scale weather measurements on Mars. The small-scale limit for the turbulence energetic regime and constraints on the expected spectral location of the inertial regime are determined. Since scaling theories for turbulence are derived from the properties of flow velocities, we use wind speed data from MEDA (shown in Fig. 1a) collected at a 2-Hz sampling rate, the fastest sampling rate for direct wind speed measurements on Mars thus far. Note that this is twice the 1- Hz sampling frequency used with cup anemometers to find the turbulent regimes, including a −5/3 inertial scaling, over the Earth’s ocean surface17. Using the same spectral normalizations as for canonical flows on Earth18,19, we explore whether universal scaling still occurs and whether it enables us to analyze the possible existence of some universal properties of turbulence in the Martian atmosphere.

Results and discussion

The spectral gap

Background weather and variations over the timescale of a sol, as well as turbulent fluctuations, contribute to the total wind speed and can be separated via the spectral gap20, which typically manifests as a low-energy region between the diurnal and high-frequency regimes. To locate the spectral gap, the background velocity (U) and fluctuations (u′) are separated via the decomposition u=U+u′, where u is the full wind speed signal. The power spectral densities (PSD) for each component in the decomposition are shown in Fig. 2. For figure clarity, Fig. 2 shows results from a single data collection interval at approximately 0700 local true solar time (LTST) for sol 116. The results are consistent across other time periods, shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The background velocity is found by taking a running mean with five varying window sizes, ranging from 2.5 to 17.5 min.

Figure 2a shows the premultiplied power spectral density for a single data collection interval. The minimum near f ~6 × 10−3 [Hz] indicates the location of the spectral gap. Regardless of the running mean window size, the PSDs for u′ collapse on the high-frequency end of the spectrum (Fig. 2e), and the PSDs for U collapse on the low-frequency end (Fig. 2c). Both the high- and low-frequency ends of the spectra collapse on either side of the spectral gap, shown in Fig. 2a, d. The consistent location of this gap provides a justification for choosing any window size within this range (2.5 - 17.5 minutes) to separate the background velocity from the turbulent fluctuations. This range of running mean window sizes are consistent with those used by previous authors2–5. This work studies the turbulence scales (everything to the right of the spectral gap), and scales attributed to weather phenomena (everything to the left of the spectra gap) are ignored.

Near-neutral region

The spectral analysis section of this study focuses on the near-neutral periods of a sol to mitigate the effects of vertical thermal gradients that can favor energy dissipation through buoyancy over wind speed fluctuations; these periods offer the conditions closest to three-dimensional isotropic turbulence that is required to observe Kolmogorov-like scaling in the inertial range. As a result, in the near-neutral periods, this problem becomes comparable to past works in the near-neutral terrestrial atmospheric surface layer21,22 and, by extension, becomes comparable to canonical wall-bounded flows on which these normalizations were initially applied18,19. The near-neutral period is traditionally identified through the stability parameter, stemming from Monin–Obukhov (MO) similarity theory 21,23. In MO similarity theory, the stability parameter is computed using the friction velocity, which is determined through an empirical relationship fitted to terrestrial data24. Alternatively, the friction velocity can be related to the standard deviation of the wind speed fluctuations via another empirically determined constant25. We note that surface shear effects imply the flow is not isotropic at large scales, but at small enough scales (high wavenumbers) the flow can be considered locally isotropic. Near-neutral periods with Ri < 0.005 typically occur during the morning transition (sunrise, LTST ~ 0700) and evening transition (sunset, LTST ~ 1700). Additional information can be found in Supplementary Fig. 2. An additional period associated with a low Richardson number occasionally occurred and is associated to “downslope” flows26—an effect specific to Perseverance’s position within a crater. While downslope flows may not necessarily be considered non-neutral, since some periods around LTST ~ 2100 satisfied the Ri < 0.005 criterion, we have included these periods in the analysis and found the results to be consistent with those from other periods satisfying the same Ri criterion.

Spectral scaling analysis

There is particular interest in capturing and understanding energy dissipation throughout all turbulent flow regimes on Mars, as has been done on Earth17,22. Since the energetic regime does not follow universal scaling, it encloses all large-scale forcing information that makes the behavior of the Martian atmosphere different from that of Earth. As a result, resolving the entire energetic regime would allow for the verification of Mars atmospheric models. Additionally, capturing inertial regime scales in the data would allow for the universality of the small scales to be reasonably invoked for modeling purposes. We determine whether or not this is the case for the data collected by MEDA by looking at the behavior of the scaled spectra.

Normalizing the power spectra by the typical lengths of the energetic regime (large scales) results in a collapse of the spectra at the wavenumbers (k), obtained by applying Taylor’s hypothesis27 to the temporal MEDA records, that fall within the energetic regime. However, above some cut-off wavenumbers, the spectra normalized by energetic regime scales fail to collapse, implying that this scaling fails at the higher wavenumbers. The equivalent is true for normalizing with the Kolmogorov length (small scales); the spectra collapse for wavenumbers corresponding to Kolmogorov-type scales and diverge once this scaling is no longer representative. The inertial subrange, if present, exists between the wave number cut-offs, i.e., where both energetic and dissipative scaling holds, and where the wind speed spectrum is predicted to vary like k−5/3.

Figure 3a shows a collapse of the spectra, indicating that scaling by the energetic length scale is reasonable for kS* ≲ 1. This also suggests that the 2-Hz wind speed measurements by MEDA capture turbulent fluctuations approaching the inertial regime. Fig. 3b, however, shows that current measurements do not reach large enough normalized wavenumbers, kη, to show the collapse of the spectra in the dissipative regime when normalized by Kolmogorov scales. The larger spread in the spectra for kη ≤ 0.1 is consistent with prior experimental studies on Earth18,19, which brings confidence to the normalization results. One can approximate an upper bound for the smallest length scales present in the energetic regime as being on the order of ~3.6 m, using the limiting condition that the large-scale normalization is valid until kS* ≈1. At typical windspeeds of 1–10 m/s, this would translate to a required sampling frequency of 0.3–3 Hz, which surpasses the current wind speed sampling rate of MEDA.

This analysis suggests that the disagreement regarding the inertial regime among past reports is because the expected spectral region for a Kolmogorov-like spectrum had not been previously measured for the Martian atmosphere. Spectral scaling analysis showed that sampling frequencies higher than 3-Hz will be needed to consistently observe the inertial regime and its associated universal scaling properties. The presented framework demonstrates a necessary first step in determining the appropriate atmospheric boundary layer models that can be utilized to learn about the Martian atmosphere. These results inform engineering hardware design requirements for future turbulence studies on Mars. Unlike on Earth, where, at 30 m above the ocean surface, 1-Hz sampling rates are enough to reach the inertial regime, the MEDA sampling rate of 2-Hz is not sufficient for measuring turbulence scales that span the entirety of the inertial regime at Jezero. Wind speeds suggest that the regime resolved with MEDA’s sampling, however, does approach the large-scale end of the inertial subrange, and captures a majority of the large-scale forcing that is driving the turbulence in the atmosphere on Mars. Additionally, it has been found that the characteristic size of the smallest dust devils measured in Jezero is on the order of 3–5 m30,31, suggesting that structures smaller than this scale are unstable and their energy is dissipated, which would be consistent with the scales found from this analysis. On the higher-frequency end of the spectrum, the microphone data7,32–34 onboard Perseverance has been limited to characterizing, thus far, dissipation properties in the regime of molecular dissipation, but efforts to measure the small-scale end of the inertial regime are ongoing. This is consistent with the scaling transitions reported here. Ultimately, this study not only provides a framework to determine where the different turbulence regimes are found on Mars without using extrapolation of terrestrial values, but it also provides a justification as to why, if it exists, the universal −5/3 inertial regime scaling remained, and continues to remain, unobserved in data collected by previous and current Mars missions.

Method

Perseverance carries the Mars environmental dynamics analyzer (MEDA), shown in Fig. 1a. It provides “surface weather measurements to validate global atmospheric models”35. MEDA houses wind speed, temperature, pressure, humidity, radiation, dust, and thermal infrared sensors35. Wind data are obtained by the wind sensor (WS), and additional stability parameters are obtained from the atmospheric temperature sensor (ATS), thermal infrared sensor (TIRS), and pressure sensor (PS). The wind sensor utilizes hot film anemometry and measures up to a 2-Hz sampling rate at a height of 1.45 m above the surface35. The MEDA wind sensor response time has been validated, showing that its different components, or channels, respond in 0.7 s or faster inside a wind tunnel at equivalent Martian wind speeds of 20 m/s35 (see Supplementary Fig. 3). For steeper wind changes, modeling of the wind sensor dynamics supports a time response of ~0.25 s to capture 90% of the amplitude for wind speed changes of 30 m/s36.

For this study, data sets from sols 100–149 are used. During this time, Perseverance was at 18°N latitude. In power spectra computations, only wind speed records with signal lengths longer than 1024 data points are taken (i.e. data sets at least 512 s long) in order to resolve the low-frequency/large-scale end of the spectra. The wind speed power spectra were computed using Welch’s method. The resulting spectra were smoothed using a Gaussian filter with a radius of 25 points and a standard deviation of 10 points; a comparison between smoothed and non-smoothed data is shown in the Supplementary Fig. 4. Details can be found in the accompanying data analysis code, which can be found at the following link: 10.5281/zenodo.10655485.

To illustrate this point, an upper integral limit two orders of magnitude above the wavenumber cut-off imposed by MEDA results in an ϵ such that the Kolmogorov length changes by a factor of only a 3.16—a correction of less than an order of magnitude. For this data set (sols 100–149), the average Kolmogorov length scale is 0.03 m, which is in the same order as previous reports of the Kolmogorov length scale on Mars2,3. It is also important to note that the Martian atmosphere is thinner than that of Earth’s and, as a result, has a lower heat capacity. This results in significant temperature changes throughout a single sol, which affect the density and viscosity of the atmosphere. Sutherland’s law,

is used to determine the dynamic viscosity (μ), and it follows that the kinematic viscosity can be calculated by ν(T) = μ(T)/ρ(T), where ρ is the density. The values used in Sutherland’s law are consistent with those defined for the Martian atmosphere38. Supplementary Fig. 5 shows the variation of density and kinematic viscosity across a sol for sols 100–149. Throughout one sol, the kinematic viscosity can vary by more than 30%. Since Kolmogorov parameters are defined by the dissipation and kinematic viscosity of the system, it is relevant to account for the diurnal changes in kinematic viscosity.
