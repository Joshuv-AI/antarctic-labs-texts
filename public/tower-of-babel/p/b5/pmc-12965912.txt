Efficacy of micronized purified flavonoid fraction in reducing pain after anal fistula surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Y-W Tsai, K-T Kiu, T-C Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis prospective randomized controlled trial investigated the efficacy of micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF) in managing postoperative pain and complications following anal fistula surgery. MPFF, a phlebotropic agent with anti-inflammatory and microcirculatory benefits, has demonstrated efficacy in hemorrhoidectomy recovery but remains unexplored in fistula surgery.MethodsA total of 106 patients undergoing fistulotomy or fistulectomy were allocated to receive either MPFF at a dose of 1 g twice daily or serve as controls. The primary endpoints were daily pain scores measured by visual analog scale (VAS) and the frequency of dressing changes. Secondary outcomes included analgesic consumption, postoperative complications, and wound healing at 3 months.ResultsAmong the enrolled patients, 104 completed the study, comprising 52 patients in the MPFF group and 52 in the control group. VAS scores were significantly lower in the MPFF group compared to the control group at multiple time points from 8 h post-surgery to day 7, with statistically significant differences observed on days 2 (3.02 ± 2.37 vs. 3.92 ± 2.16; P = 0.045), 4 (2.54 ± 2.29 vs. 3.40 ± 1.89; P = 0.038), and 5 (2.34 ± 2.09 vs. 3.25 ± 1.88; P = 0.022). Additionally, the frequency of dressing changes was reduced in the MPFF group relative to the control group from day 1 to day 7 post-surgery, with statistically significant differences observed from day 4 onward (day 4, P = 0.036; day 5, P = 0.012; day 6, P = 0.012; day 7, P = 0.012). Secondary outcomes, including analgesic use, wound healing, and postoperative complications such as infection, bleeding, urinary retention, delayed healing, and recurrence, showed no significant differences between groups. No patient required re-intervention for hemorrhage.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that MPFF not only decreases postoperative pain but also lowers the frequency of dressing changes, supporting its role as a valuable therapeutic option for improving recovery after anal fistula surgery.Clinical trial registrationThis study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov, and its registration number is NCT06184438.Keyword: Postoperative analgesia, Phlebotropic agent, Wound management

Introduction

Micronized purified flavonoid fraction (MPFF), a phlebotropic agent composed of 90% diosmin (3′,5,7-trihydroxy-4′-methoxyflavone-7-rhamnoglucoside) and 10% hesperidin ((2S)-7-[[6-O-(6-deoxy-α-l-mannopyranosyl)-β-d-glucopyranosyl]oxy]-2,3-dihydro-5-hydroxy-2-(3-hydroxy-4-methoxyphenyl)-4H-1-benzopyran-4-one), exerts its clinically validated effects through multiple mechanisms, such as enhancing venous tone, reducing capillary hyperpermeability, suppressing vascular endothelial activation, and modulating inflammatory responses [1–3]. These properties make MPFF suitable for managing chronic venous disease and hemorrhoidal disorders [1–3], where it alleviates both acute symptoms such as pain and bleeding and chronic manifestations such as edema and discharge [3–7]. After hemorrhoidectomy, particularly when performed using open-wound techniques like the Milligan-Morgan technique, MPFF treatment can significantly reduce postoperative pain, dependence on analgesics, and wound complications such as bleeding and exudate [3, 8–11].

Anal fistula, a common anorectal disease, typically requires fistulotomy, fistulectomy, or other surgical procedures [12–16]. These procedures result in perianal open wounds, which present unique healing challenges, such as persistent exposure to fecal contaminants, mechanical trauma during defecation, and sustained inflammatory stimuli. Consequently, patients undergoing fistulotomy or fistulectomy experience prolonged postoperative pain, significant wound discharge, and increased risks of dehiscence and secondary bleeding. Notably, these complications are similar to those associated with hemorrhoidectomy wounds [9–11, 17].

Although these procedures share postoperative challenges, several key distinctions exist. Fistulotomy or fistulectomy often involves partial division of the anal sphincter, which may compromise continence, whereas hemorrhoidectomy generally spares the sphincter. Furthermore, the underlying etiologies differ: anal fistulas originate from infected anal glands with subsequent tract formation, while hemorrhoids result from vascular dilatation and connective tissue changes. These differences necessitate procedure-specific recovery strategies, despite the overlap in fundamental wound-healing principles.

Although current evidence highlights the efficacy of MPFF in reducing postoperative pain and complications after hemorrhoidectomy, its utility after anal fistula surgery has not been investigated [3]. Therefore, we conducted this randomized controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of MPFF in relieving postoperative pain, decreasing wound exudate, and lowering the risk of complications following anal fistula surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with the CONSORT guidelines [18]. The study protocol was approved by the Joint Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee [19]. All eligible patients received comprehensive information about the study objectives and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria [20]. This study was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov. All participants underwent surgery between December 2023 and September 2024 at a single tertiary referral hospital.

Patient selection

This study included adult patients over 20 years of age who underwent anal fistula surgery, either fistulotomy or fistulectomy. Because various surgical options exist for the management of anal fistulas, we restricted our analysis to fistulectomy and fistulotomy, excluding other procedures such as ligation of the fistula tract, mucosal advancement flap, and fistuloscopy to minimize potential bias. The definition of fistulotomy is laying open (incising) the roof of the fistula tract, while fistulectomy refers to the complete excision (coring out) of the entire fistula tract and surrounding tissue. Additional exclusion criteria were patients with concurrent colorectal cancer, liver cirrhosis, coagulopathy, or human immunocompromised virus infection.

Group allocation

Patients were randomly allocated to either the MPFF group or the control group in a 1:1 ratio using a computer-generated sequence. Group assignments were concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Each participant was assigned to a group by opening the next sequential envelope.

A blinded design was implemented to ensure objectivity. The operating surgeons remained unaware of group assignments throughout the study. Additionally, all personnel involved in data collection and analysis were blinded to group assignments, ensuring methodological rigor and minimizing potential bias.

Intervention and comparison

The MPFF group received 1 g of MPFF twice daily from postoperative day 0 through day 7. The control group received a regimen of 500 mg vitamin B twice daily for the same duration. The first dose of the study medication was administered according to a standard postoperative practice: it was given after the patient returned to the ward from the post-anesthesia care unit. As all surgeries were completed before noon, this process typically resulted in the first dose being administered within 1 to 2 h after the completion of surgery. The second dose was administered in the evening, about 6 PM to 7 PM on the postoperative day.

All patients underwent surgery in the jackknife position. Spinal anesthesia was preferred, with general anesthesia administered only in special circumstances, such as for patients with a bleeding risk or those unable to cooperate with regional anesthesia. Two surgeons performed the surgeries following the same standard procedures. Once complete hemostasis was achieved, an absorbable gelatin sponge was inserted into the patient’s anal canal to complete the procedure.

The following data were collected from each patient: age, sex, preoperative symptoms and their durations, fistula type, surgical method, anesthesia type, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, postoperative hospital stay, postoperative analgesic type and dose, and postoperative complications.

Outcome measures

The primary outcomes were the intensity of postoperative pain for 7 days after surgery and the frequency of dressing changes during this period. The secondary outcomes included total oral analgesic use during the first postoperative week, incidence of surgical site infections within 30 days after surgery, urinary retention during the first postoperative week, and complete wound healing at the 3-month follow-up.

Postoperative pain was measured using a visual analog scale (VAS), with a total score ranging from 0 to 10, where 0 indicated no pain and 10 indicated the worst pain ever experienced. Surgeons provided a standardized form to each patient to document their daily VAS scores, dressing change frequency, and oral analgesic use over the 7 days following surgery. Outpatient follow-up evaluations were conducted at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months after hospital discharge. During these visits, the attending surgeon systematically reviewed all patient-completed forms to assess recovery progress and treatment outcomes.

Postoperative bleeding was defined as any hemorrhagic event necessitating either surgical reintervention or hospital readmission within the 30-day postoperative period. Urinary retention was defined as the clinical requirement for catheterization during the first postoperative week. Surgical site infection was defined as either hospital admission for intravenous antibiotic therapy or the need for wound-related surgical management within 30 days after the initial procedure. All recorded complications were systematically classified according to the Clavien-Dindo grading system, ensuring consistency in severity assessment.

Postoperative analgesia

All patients strictly followed a standardized postoperative analgesic regimen. The primary regimen consisted of oral diclofenac, 25 mg four times daily. Patients with documented hypersensitivity to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or impaired renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) received an alternative regimen of oral acetaminophen, 500 mg four times daily. Medication use was discontinued if the patient became pain-free upon hospital discharge. During hospitalization, rescue analgesia was available for breakthrough pain management. Patients reporting severe pain (VAS score of 4 or higher) could receive intravenous parecoxib, 40 mg, as supplemental analgesia upon request.

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The required sample size was determined on the basis of previously reported changes in VAS scores following hemorrhoidal surgery. Evidence suggests that the mean VAS scores on postoperative day 3 range from 3.48 to 3.5 for patients receiving MPFF and from 5.0 to 6.16 for those receiving a placebo [8, 9]. A power analysis using a two-tailed independent-samples t test indicated that a minimum of 84 patients were needed to achieve a statistical power of at least 0.9, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a medium effect size (d) of 0.4. To account for an estimated 20% loss due to unusable or missing data, the target recruitment was set at 106 patients.

Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard deviation values, while categorical data are presented as count and percentage values. A two-sample t test was used to compare continuous data, and a chi-square or Fisher exact test was used for categorical data. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a P value of < 0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Study cohort

The study initially included 106 patients undergoing anal fistula surgery. After randomization, 53 patients were allocated to each of the study groups. One patient in the MPFF group was excluded as a result of undergoing mucosal flap repair instead of the planned procedure, and one patient in the control group withdrew consent preoperatively. Discussion

This study demonstrated that the use of MPFF after anal fistula surgery significantly reduced postoperative pain and the frequency of wound dressing changes. However, no significant differences were observed between the MPFF and control groups regarding analgesic consumption or postoperative complications. These findings suggest that the clinical indications for MPFF may be expanded to include patients undergoing anal fistula surgery, offering benefits in pain reduction and decreased wound care burden.

The perianal soft tissues are highly sensitive to pain, making postoperative discomfort a significant concern for patients undergoing anal surgery [21]. In this study, patients in the MPFF group consistently reported lower VAS scores than those in the control group during the first postoperative week, with statistically significant differences observed on postoperative days 2, 4, and 5. These findings suggest that MPFF exerts a comparable analgesic effect after fistula surgery and hemorrhoidectomy. However, although our study confirmed the pain-reducing benefits of MPFF, the magnitude of this effect appeared less pronounced than that reported in previous studies involving hemorrhoidectomy [8, 9]. This may be attributable to variations in surgical wound characteristics across procedures. Traditional hemorrhoidectomy, such as the Milligan-Morgan technique, typically involves resection of hemorrhoids in three quadrants, resulting in three postoperative open wounds. In contrast, over 95% of patients in our cohort presented with a single anal fistula tract, leading to only one surgical wound. Fewer surgical wounds may explain the milder pain in fistula surgery compared to hemorrhoidectomy. Although MPFF can alleviate postoperative pain, the inherently low pain severity in single-wound fistula surgery likely explains the lack of significant pain reduction. This interpretation aligns with findings from another randomized controlled trial investigating the benefits of MPFF after stapled hemorrhoidopexy—a procedure without open wounds—which similarly reported no significant analgesic effects [22].

Anal fistula surgery may involve partial compromise of the internal anal sphincter; however, both procedures generally include excision of perianal cushions or hemorrhoidal tissue, resembling the removal of superficial soft tissue above the anal musculature, as in hemorrhoidectomy. Consequently, the wound characteristics of anal fistula surgery are largely comparable to those of hemorrhoidectomy. MPFF exerts its analgesic effect in hemorrhoidectomy by suppressing inflammatory mediators and free radical production, alleviating venous congestion in traumatized tissues, and enhancing lymphatic drainage to reduce edema and pain [9, 11, 23]. Despite the involvement of muscle injury in anal fistula surgery, the overall wound characteristics remain comparable to those in hemorrhoidectomy. Therefore, it is plausible that the analgesic effect of MPFF in fistula surgery is mediated through comparable mechanisms.

The mechanisms of tissue injury in hemorrhoidectomy and anal fistula surgery share notable similarities. Anal fistula surgery may involve partial compromise of the internal anal sphincter, both procedures typically entail excision of perianal cushion or hemorrhoidal tissue, resembling the removal of superficial soft tissues above the anal musculature in hemorrhoidectomy. MPFF exerts its analgesic effect in hemorrhoid surgery by suppressing inflammatory mediators and free radical production, alleviating venous congestion in traumatized tissues, and enhancing lymphatic drainage to reduce edema and pain [9, 11, 23]. Despite the involvement of muscle injury in anal fistula surgery, the overall wound characteristics remain comparable to those in hemorrhoidectomy. Thus, the analgesic effect of MPFF in fistula surgery is likely mediated through similar mechanisms.

MPFF confers multifaceted therapeutic benefits, including decreased venous tone, enhanced venous and lymphatic drainage, and attenuation of postoperative inflammation [11]. Also, MPFF has been shown to reduce wound exudation and local discomfort in hemorrhoidal surgery, likely through its combined anti-inflammatory and microcirculatory effects [9, 11]. Similarly, both fistulectomy and fistulotomy result in open perianal wounds with persistent exudation, and the need for frequent wound care can substantially affect patients’ quality of life. While exudate from anal wounds is difficult to measure or quantify directly, this study employed the frequency of dressing changes as a surrogate marker for exudate volume. Notably, a trend toward fewer dressing changes during the first postoperative week was observed in the MPFF group compared with the control group, with statistically significant differences emerging from postoperative days 4 to 7. These findings suggest that, through mechanisms similar to those observed in hemorrhoidectomy, MPFF reduces wound exudation and thereby decreases the frequency of dressing changes.

In this study, although postoperative analgesic use was lower in the MPFF group than in the control group from postoperative day 1 to day 7, the difference did not reach statistical significance. This may be explained by the fact that, while MPFF alleviated postoperative pain to some extent, the mean difference in VAS scores between the two groups was about one point, which does not exceed the minimal clinically important difference [24, 25]. Consequently, the reduction in pain may not have been sufficient to alter patients’ need for analgesics. By contrast, previous studies on hemorrhoidectomy have reported that MPFF reduced pain scores by approximately two points, a difference large enough to translate into a measurable reduction in analgesic consumption [8, 9].

MPFF has been shown to effectively reduce hemorrhoidal bleeding in both acute and chronic phases and to prevent secondary bleeding after hemorrhoidectomy [4, 6, 26]. In the present study, no patients in either group required rehospitalization or surgical intervention for postoperative hemorrhage. The generally low bleeding risk associated with fistulotomy may have limited the ability to detect a potential hemostatic effect of MPFF. Moreover, in anal surgery, meticulous intraoperative hemostasis remains the most effective strategy for preventing postoperative bleeding. Other postoperative complications such as urinary retention, local abscess, and wound delayed healing were not different between the two groups, indicating that the use of MPFF after fistula surgery is safe and does not cause other complications.

Since most anal fistulas are of the simple type—such as intersphincteric fistulas and lower type transsphincteric, which account for approximately 70% of all anal fistulas—these patients can achieve favorable treatment outcomes through traditional procedures that involve cutting the affected internal sphincter, such as fistulectomy or fistulotomy [27–29]. Therefore, in our pioneering study, we exclusively included patients with simple or low type anal fistula. Conversely, other surgical approaches—such as fistula tract ligation, mucosal flap repair, laser fistula ablation, or fistuloscopy—are typically indicated for high type or more complex anal fistulas [30]. These cases were excluded from our trial to minimize potential confounding factors and to ensure a homogeneous study population, thereby strengthening the interpretability and validity of our findings.

This study has some limitations. First, patient blinding was not feasible because of the distinct appearance of the MPFF and control tablets, which may have introduced detection bias for subjective outcomes. Second, it included only patients from a single high-volume referral institution, which may reduce the applicability of the findings to cohorts undergoing surgery with different techniques and postoperative care regimens. Third, the fixed 7-day MPFF regimen and the 3-month follow-up period may not represent the optimal treatment duration or capture long-term outcomes such as delayed wound remodeling and recurrence. Fourth, the exclusion of complex fistula surgeries, such as fistula tract ligation and mucosal flap repair, precluded the investigation of MPFF’s potential utility in more challenging surgical scenarios. Finally, although patients were instructed in standardized wound care protocols at enrollment, the frequency of dressing changes varied substantially across patients. This variability was due to differences in pain tolerance, perceived wound moisture levels, and prior self-care experiences, which may have introduced measurement bias.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial suggests that postoperative MPFF administration is associated with reduced postoperative pain (postoperative days 2,4, and 5) and decreased frequency of dressing changes (postoperative days 4–7) in patients undergoing anal fistula surgery, without increasing complications. It should be noted that the analgesic effect exhibited a variable temporal pattern (significant reduction on days 2, 4, and 5, but not on day 3). These findings indicate a potential benefit of MPFF in this surgical context and support the need for further investigation to confirm its efficacy, elucidate the underlying mechanisms, and explore its applicability in other anorectal procedures.
