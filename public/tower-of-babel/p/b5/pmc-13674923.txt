Use of situational judgment test for evaluating nonacademic attributes in ophthalmology fellowship selection
Authors: Ruby Kala Prakasam, Anjali Tripathi, Aditya Kapoor, Kavya M Bejjanki, Avinash Pathengay, Snigdha Snigdha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:In medical fellowship selection, evaluating both academic and nonacademic attributes is essential for clinical success. This study integrates cognitive assessments with construct-driven situational judgment tests (SJTs) to evaluate nonacademic traits, aiming to identify well-rounded candidates during the ophthalmology fellowship selection process.Methods:A retrospective analysis was conducted on 68 candidates who appeared for the interview in 2024. Academic attributes were assessed through structured interviews in which candidates prepared and delivered PowerPoint presentations on ophthalmology topics. These were evaluated using a rubric aligned with Bloom’s six cognitive levels: recall, understand, apply, analyze, evaluate, and create. Nonacademic attributes were assessed using verbal SJTs based on the 5Cs framework: communication, collaboration, critical thinking, curiosity, and creativity.Results:The median difference between presentation scores (61.1%, interquartile range (IQR) = 35) and SJT scores (85.4%, IQR = 25) was statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P < 0.001), with candidates performing better in the SJT. Further, the rank-based correlation between presentation and SJT scores was not statistically significant (Spearman correlation, P = 0.545), indicating that candidates who scored high on the Bloom-based presentation did not consistently score high or low on the SJT, suggesting that the two assessments measure distinct attributes.Conclusion:Academic and nonacademic attributes represent distinct skill sets. Therefore, a combined evaluation that incorporates both domains may more effectively identify well-rounded candidates for ophthalmology fellowships. This dual-assessment approach can be implemented through a virtual platform, enabling a more holistic and equitable evaluation of applicants for entry-level fellowship selection.

Admission examinations in the medical field are designed to evaluate the qualifications and readiness of applicants for entry into training programs. The medical school admission process follows best practices proposed in the literature,[12] with criteria traditionally focusing on assessing both academic and nonacademic attributes. In addition to the general criteria,[1] structured and unstructured interviews are commonly used as selection methods in medical school, residency programs, and fellowship programs.[34] Research comparing these interview formats indicates that structured interviews are more effective than unstructured ones. Unstructured interviews tend to have lower validity and are susceptible to biases related to factors such as age, gender, and academic performance.[45]

The situational judgment test (SJT) has become increasingly prevalent in structured interviews and personnel selection processes.[6] It is designed to assess how individuals would approach a range of hypothetical scenarios they are likely to encounter in the target role. The literature discusses two primary approaches,[7] to SJT development: traditional and construct driven. The traditional approach primarily focuses on evaluating candidates’ responses to job-specific situations, while the construct-driven approach aims to measure underlying personality traits such as resilience and dependability. In medical admissions, SJTs have demonstrated strong psychometric properties in terms of reliability, validity, and fairness.[678] Studies have also identified factors that may influence the quality of SJTs, such as the test development process, the number of test items, and scoring methods. These factors can be managed by carefully developing SJTs in alignment with the job’s objectives.

A dual-assessment selection model was implemented in the ophthalmology fellowship candidate selection program at the Institute. This model employed a structured interview framework to evaluate both academic and nonacademic attributes considered essential for clinical competence. Academic attributes were assessed through a structured presentation designed according to Bloom’s cognitive domain, encompassing recall, application, analysis, evaluation, and creation.[8] This method facilitates deep learning and higher-order cognitive processing, both of which are integral to clinical reasoning, innovation, and problem solving.[91011] Nonacademic attributes were evaluated using SJTs. These assessments targeted collaboration, communication, curiosity, critical thinking, and creativity (5Cs) through realistic clinical scenarios requiring sound judgment and decision-making. Such attributes are indispensable in clinical practice, where teamwork, ethical reasoning, and effective communication have a direct impact on patient outcomes.[12]

Although our interview process includes assessing both academic and nonacademic attributes, there was a felt need to structure the assessment methods for nonacademic attributes through an evidence-based assessment tool, such as the SJT, to make them more objective. The adoption of this dual-assessment model is particularly advantageous in ophthalmology, a discipline in which technical proficiency must be complemented by professional conduct, empathy, and patient-centered care.[1213]

This study aims to evaluate the significance of integrating nonacademic attributes by:

Comparing nonacademic performance (SJT on 5Cs) with academic assessments to examine their relationship and complementarity.

Methods

This is a retrospective correlational study that received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Ethics Committee (Ethics Ref No: LEC-BHR-R-138). All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Selection process

The selection process for ophthalmology fellows is conducted biannually. This study includes data collected from recent fellowship admissions at the institute in 2024. A retrospective analysis was conducted on 68 candidates who appeared for the interview. The fellowship selection coordinator organized the interviews, and the interviewers were blinded regarding the candidates’ prior academic performances. The selection involved structured interviews conducted by an executive panel comprising 12 members, who were then divided into 6 small panels, each consisting of 2 members, and the candidates were randomly assigned to each pair. Each interview panel comprised two senior faculty members: an ophthalmologist and a medical educator, both with prior experience as evaluators in fellowship selection processes. The interview format was communicated clearly to all panel members in advance, and an electronic rubric was shared to guide the evaluation. Each interview lasted approximately 15–20 min per candidate, with time equally divided between the two interviewers. One interviewer assessed academic competencies, while the other evaluated nonacademic attributes. As they focused on different aspects, the assessments were conducted independently without consensus scoring.

Academic attributes were assessed using Bloom’s Taxonomy of the cognitive domain.[14] Candidates delivered PowerPoint presentations, which were evaluated using a predesigned rubric [Supplementary Table 1] structured around the six hierarchical levels of Bloom’s Taxonomy: Recall, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, and Create. Scores were assigned based on how effectively each candidate demonstrated these cognitive levels within their presentation. The academic assessment was followed by the assessment of nonacademic attributes using SJT based on the 5Cs framework, which included the aspects of communication, collaboration, critical thinking, curiosity, and creativity. In addition to the 5Cs, the SJT also assessed candidates’ overall grooming and interview preparedness. The SJT was conducted verbally, with interviewers presenting clinical or professional scenarios and grading responses in real time according to a predefined rubric [Supplementary Table 2]. The SJT scenarios and the assessment rubrics for both evaluations (SJT and PPT presentation) were developed by the study investigators. These materials were revised iteratively based on feedback from interview panel members before implementation.

Score description: Responses were rated using domain-specific rubrics and converted to percentage scores. Percentage scores were calculated as: Percentage score (P) = (raw rubric score (R) ÷ maximum possible score for that rubric) ×100. Scores across the subdomains (5Cs) were then averaged with equal weighting to generate the final SJT score per candidate: Final SJT Score = (sum of subdomain percentage scores) ÷ number of subdomains. For example, if a candidate scored 2 on a rubric with a maximum score of 4, the percentage score would be (2 ÷ 4) ×100 = 50%. If the same candidate’s percentage scores across subdomains were 60%, 50%, 66%, and 75%, the final nonacademic score would be (60 + 50 + 66 + 75) ÷4 = 63%. A similar percentage-based scoring scheme was also applied to Bloom’s cognitive assessment.

Statistical analysis

The survey data were analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Frequencies and summary statistics were calculated for all variables, and the results were presented as valid percentages. Descriptive statistics were used to present demographic information. The Wilcoxon signed rank test was used to compare the mean scores between academic and nonacademic test scores of the candidates interviewed for the ophthalmology fellowship. The Spearman’s correlation coefficient test was conducted to determine rank-based correlation between two assessments. The statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Applicants interviewed in the August 2024 ophthalmology fellowship selection (n = 68) were evaluated based on both academic and nonacademic attributes. The mean age of participants was 32 ± 4.8 (mean, standard deviation) years. Of the total, 66% (n = 45) were female applicants and 34% (n = 23) were male applicants.

The box plot [Fig. 1] compares candidates’ performance scores between Bloom-based presentation on a subspecialty topic and the 5Cs-based SJT. The median score for SJT (85.4%, IQR = 25) was greater than the median score of the presentations (61.1%, IQR = 35). This difference was statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed rank test, z = −5.72, P < 0.001).

The rank-based correlation graph [Fig. 2] shows a weak positive association between candidates’ scores on the PPT presentation and the SJT, which was not statistically significant (Spearman’s rank correlation, P = 0.545). This suggests that high performers in the presentation were not necessarily the same individuals who scored highly on the SJT, indicating that the two assessments capture distinct constructs.

Discussion

Previous studies evaluating medical selection processes[12151617181920] have highlighted the importance of integrating nonacademic attributes alongside traditional academic measures to enable fairer and more holistic candidate evaluation. Although academic and nonacademic assessments capture distinct skill domains, applying statistical analyses allows researchers to determine whether these domains overlap, complement each other, or provide unique information about candidates. Responding to this need, the present study evaluated a dual-assessment framework for ophthalmology fellowship selection at the Institute. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported attempt in this context to integrate Bloom’s taxonomy with construct-driven SJTs into a unified evaluation model.

The first stage of the Institution Selection Model incorporated construct-driven SJTs to evaluate nonacademic attributes alongside academic competencies. Prior research has similarly applied the SJT framework in admissions to assess a range of nonacademic qualities. For example, SJTs delivered in multiple-choice formats have been used to present realistic, profession-specific scenarios targeting interpersonal domains such as relationship building and communication.[15] Clinical[1821] and surgical[2223] performance with SJTs has also been employed to evaluate behavioral competencies among medical trainees. Evidence suggests that SJTs can effectively assess both broad personality traits, such as conscientiousness, and more specific attributes, including organization, planning, empathy, communication, adaptability, resilience, mindset, emotional regulation, professional ethics, and motivation.[19] Wolcott et al.[17] validated the use of SJTs by analyzing participants’ cognitive response processes and applying think-aloud interviews to measure constructs such as empathy. In such studies, verbalized reasoning was transcribed and coded for analysis.

In alignment with previous studies,[151718] the current study utilized a structured interview format in which multiple-choice SJT scenarios were administered using a think-aloud protocol. However, diverging from traditional methodologies that involve post-hoc transcription and coding, this study employed real-time scoring. Participants verbalized their reasoning during the interview, and responses were evaluated immediately using a standardized rubric based on the 5C framework [Supplementary Table 2]. Each response option represented a different approach and was assigned a weighted percentage score, reflecting its relative appropriateness. There was no single correct answer. By integrating SJTs with academic evaluations, this dual approach offers a holistic and predictive assessment of a candidate’s readiness for real-world clinical responsibilities.

Nonacademic attributes assessed through SJTs are often referred to in the literature,[24] as soft skills. Among these, the “4Cs”-creativity, critical thinking, communication, and collaboration-have been identified as essential competencies in 21st-century education.[25] These foundational skills support the development of advanced professional behaviors, including sound decision-making, effective interpersonal judgment, and emotional intelligence.[26] The construct-driven SJTs in this study assessed candidates’ responses to hypothetical clinical situations, providing insights into how they might perform in dynamic, high-pressure environments. The inclusion of SJTs enhances the predictive validity of selection processes across a broader spectrum of candidate competencies, demonstrating complementary value to academic assessments.[20] While the 4Cs are widely acknowledged as core 21st-century skills, this study proposes an expansion of the framework to include curiosity. Curiosity serves as a foundational driver of inquiry, innovation, and lifelong learning. In the context of medical education, it supports evidence-based reasoning, reflective practice, and the continual acquisition of new knowledge, key attributes in navigating complex clinical environments.[27]

Similar to our study objectives, prior work has directly compared academic performance with nonacademic measures, demonstrating that SJTs and structured interviews assess competencies largely independent of academic scores and can add complementary value to selection decisions.[420] Analysis of test results, in the present study, revealed significantly higher median scores in the SJT-based nonacademic component compared to the academic component. Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis indicated no significant relationship between academic and nonacademic scores, suggesting that the two assessments measure distinct dimensions of candidate potential. While these differences could be mainly attributed to the fundamental distinctions in the domains being assessed, they may also partly stem from the unique formats and real-time implementation methods adopted in the interview model presented. Overall, these findings underscore the limitations of relying solely on academic metrics, which may overlook critical behavioral competencies essential for clinical practice. Effective patient care relies heavily on communication, empathy, and professionalism, attributes not captured by traditional academic indicators such as grade point average (GPA) or standardized test scores.[1516] The dual-assessment approach addresses this gap by providing a more holistic evaluation of candidates’ academic and nonacademic competencies, thereby supporting the selection of fellows who demonstrate both intellectual ability and the interpersonal, ethical, and professional qualities required for patient-centered ophthalmic care. By integrating SJTs with Bloom’s taxonomy–based academic assessments, this study proposes a comprehensive and evidence-informed model for fellowship selection.[16]

Limitations

This retrospective study has a number of limitations. The online interview format may have influenced candidate performance due to technical challenges, inconsistent connectivity, and reduced interpersonal interaction, factors that can affect communication dynamics and candidate comfort. Time constraints during the interview may have limited candidates’ ability to fully articulate their responses. While a validated rubric was used to enhance scoring objectivity, inter-rater reliability was not assessed, as each assessment, academic or nonacademic, was conducted by a single evaluator, and multiple ratings per candidate were not available. Additionally, due to its retrospective design, the study is limited in its ability to assess the long-term predictive validity of the selection process. Future research should consider hybrid interview models and extended response time to enhance assessment quality. Prospective, longitudinal studies are recommended to evaluate the reliability and predictive validity of nonacademic assessments over time and to further strengthen selection frameworks.

Conclusion

While academic excellence remains a fundamental criterion in medical selection, the integration of construct-driven SJTs focused on the 5Cs provides critical insight into nonacademic attributes essential for clinical effectiveness. This layered selection approach enables the identification of candidates who are not only intellectually capable but also demonstrate key behavioral competencies such as teamwork, ethical decision-making, and patient-centered care, hallmarks of a well-rounded ophthalmology fellow. Although the retrospective design limits the ability to assess long-term predictive validity, the findings support the complementary role of SJTs in enhancing the fairness and depth of candidate evaluation. Future prospective, longitudinal studies are warranted to validate these outcomes and further strengthen selection frameworks.
