Surgical management of refractory ischemic-side aneurysms in chronic internal carotid artery occlusion: insights from cases unfit for endovascular treatment
Authors: Xiangping Zhong, Zhongying Ran, Weidong Xia, Meng Zhang, Maohua Ding, Lijin He, Xiaoguang Tong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManaging ipsilateral intracranial aneurysms (IAs) in chronic internal carotid artery occlusion (ICAO) is surgically challenging, particularly when endovascular treatment is not viable. This study delineates patient characteristics, individualized microsurgical strategies, and outcomes.MethodsA retrospective review was conducted of patients with chronic ICAO and ipsilateral IAs treated between 2015 and 2023. Based on surgical stroke risk, patients were stratified into low- and high-risk groups and received individualized treatments, including aneurysm clipping, external carotid artery–radial artery–middle cerebral artery (ECA-RA-MCA) or superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass, or combined procedures. Outcomes were assessed via angiography and the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).ResultsThe cohort included 14 patients (mean age 50.56 ± 15.69 years) with 17 aneurysms. Symptoms included subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH, n = 5) and cerebral ischemia (n = 5), among others (n = 4). ICAO was right-sided (n = 6), left-sided (n = 7), bilateral (n = 1); atherosclerosis was the main cause (n = 11). Most aneurysms were saccular (n = 12) and irregular (n = 4) morphologies (mean diameter 5.38 ± 2.75 mm), located within the ipsilateral circle of Willis. Cerebrovascular abnormalities such as stenosis, agenesis, and tortuosity were frequently observed. Atherosclerosis was the predominant etiology (n = 11). Circle of Willis compensation patterns were type I (n = 3), II (n = 5), III (n = 4), and IV (n = 2). Collateral flow grades were: grade 4 (n = 2), grade 3 (n = 4), grade 2 (n = 5), and grade 1 (n = 3). In the low-risk group (n = 10), 7 received STA-MCA bypass with clipping, 2 had bypass alone, and 1 underwent clipping only. In the high-risk group (n = 4), 3 underwent ECA-RA-MCA bypass with clipping, and 1 had bypass alone. One patient succumbed to death within 30 days postoperatively, one developed focal cerebral edema accompanied by ptosis, and one Manifested ipsilateral thalamic infarction. The 30-day stroke incidence was 7.14%, with an overall complication rate of 21.42%, CT-P/MR-P imaging demonstrates a perfusion improvement rate of 57.14%. Over a mean follow-up of 41.46 ± 26.82 months, bypass patency remained 100% (n = 10), ipsilateral stroke recurrence was 7.14%, and mRS scores improved. Clipped aneurysms (n = 11, 64.70%) resolved on imaging; untreated ones (n = 4, 23.52%) remained stable.ConclusionsIn cases of chronic ICAO concomitant with ipsilateral IAs, a stratified approach based on surgical stroke risk factors, coupled with tailored bypass procedures and aneurysm clipping, represents a safe and effective strategy with promising clinical applicability for complex cases unfit for endovascular intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12893-025-03155-6.Keywords: Chronic internal carotid artery occlusion, Intracranial aneurysms, Collateral circulation, Bypass surgery

Introduction

With the continual refinement of angiographic techniques, the detection rate of concomitant intra- and extracranial vascular pathologies has significantly increased. In 2014, the Chinese CICAS study group reported that 46.6% of stroke patients exhibited intracranial occlusive disease [1]. Furthermore, the prevalence of IAs in this cohort is notably elevated compared to the general population [2–4]. Djordje Radak and colleagues observed that the incidence of IAs among patients with severe carotid artery stenosis ranges between 2.3% and 7.0%. At present, the principal treatment modalities for aneurysms associated with chronic ICAO include isolated microsurgical clipping or endovascular intervention [5, 6]. Emanuela Crobeddu [7] has reported satisfactory outcomes utilizing single-modality strategies, including endovascular intervention, microsurgical clipping, or conservative management, for the treatment of aneurysms located ipsilateral or contralateral to the ICAO. However, it is noteworthy that treating IAs on the ischemic side theoretically entails a higher risk of cerebral infarction, and addressing the IAs alone does not rectify the underlying cerebral hypoperfusion.

Given the paucity of literature regarding the management of ipsilateral ICAO with concurrent aneurysm, the majority of studies that simultaneously address cerebral ischemia and aneurysms are predominantly case reports.Therefore, this study aims to systematically review and analyze our center’s therapeutic strategies and outcomes for chronic ICAO complicated by intervention-resistant ischemic-side IAs. Moreover, we seek to explore individualized treatment strategies and management protocols, with the goal of providing valuable insights for future research and clinical practice.

Methods

This study conducted a retrospective review of clinical cases Managed at our center from January 2015 to December 2023, involving patients with chronic ICAO accompanied by IAs. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Age range of 18 to 80 years; (2) Preoperative confirmation of ICAO with ipsilateral IAs using digital subtraction angiography (DSA); (3) Surgical interventions comprising craniotomy with aneurysm clipping, extracranial-intracranial bypass, or a combination of these techniques; (4) Duration of ICAO exceeding 4 weeks, irrespective of cerebral perfusion status. The exclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Moyamoya disease with associated aneurysms; (2) Patients presenting with severe systemic comorbidities precluding surgical tolerance; (3) Aneurysms amenable to endovascular intervention; (4) Acute ICAO. The data encompasses demographic characteristics, radiological findings, medical history, clinical presentation of the aneurysm, occlusion-related symptoms and signs, surgical techniques employed, follow-up outcomes, and therapeutic results. All patients underwent CT or MRI on the first postoperative week to evaluate for intracranial hemorrhage or ischemic complications, followed by DSA or computed tomography angiography (CTA). During the post-discharge follow-up phase, comprehensive evaluations were conducted via inpatient reassessments, outpatient consultations, advanced neuroimaging, or telephonic follow-ups. Neurological functional outcomes were systematically evaluated by two neurosurgical residents using the mRS, whereas vascular imaging during the follow-up period was independently reviewed and interpreted by a senior neurointerventionalist. The scores of 0–2 were defined as having a “favorable prognosis”, while scores of 3–6 were indicative of an “unfavorable prognosis”.

This work has been carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of the World Medical Association, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tianjin Huanhu Hospital, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

Perioperative management

All patients underwent comprehensive preoperative evaluations, including MRI, CT, and DSA, accompanied by cardiopulmonary functional assessments prior to induction of general anesthesia. For those presenting with unruptured aneurysms, a regimen of 100 mg aspirin daily was administered for a minimum of 7 consecutive days preoperatively. Antithrombotic therapy was omitted during the cerebrovascular bypass procedure. Preoperative assessment of both the RA and STA was conducted using DSA and duplex ultrasonography, with the RA additionally evaluated by Allen’s test to ensure adequate collateral circulation. Upon harvest, the vascular grafts were immersed in a heparinized saline solution supplemented with papaverine, and vasospasm was alleviated through controlled intraluminal pressurization to facilitate vascular relaxation and luminal expansion. Intraoperatively, meticulous neuroprotection and preservation of adjacent neurovascular structures were prioritized. Postoperative Management included routine antiepileptic prophylaxis and strict blood pressure control. Antiplatelet therapy was initiated once postoperative CT confirmed the absence of intracranial hemorrhage. Upon discharge, aspirin was continued for a minimum of 6 months, with cessation determined by subsequent imaging and clinical evaluations.

Surgery method

The therapeutic protocol was meticulously devised by our neurosurgical team following an interdisciplinary consensus, which included two senior endovascular neurosurgeons, one senior skull base neurosurgeon, two cerebrovascular neurosurgeons, and one neuroanesthesiologist. The core of the surgical strategy lies in assessing collateral cerebral perfusion in the occluded hemisphere, the duration of intraoperative cerebral ischemia, and the technical intricacy of aneurysmal clipping, as well as other relevant perioperative stroke risk factors.

Following the exclusion of endovascular treatment feasibility, surgical indication for ICAO is established based on an integrated evaluation encompassing CT perfusion (CT-P) or MR perfusion (MR-P), cerebral angiography, and clinical manifestations—such as recurrent watershed infarctions or ischemic episodes involving eloquent cortical territories, tandem high-grade stenosis or occlusion of two or more major intra- or extracranial arteries, and perfusion abnormalities in at least two parameters among cerebral blood flow (CBF), cerebral blood volume (CBV), time-to-peak, and mean transit time. Under such conditions, combined extracranial-intracranial bypass and microsurgical aneurysm clipping may be indicated. In the absence of these criteria, standalone aneurysm clipping is advised. Intraoperatively, unruptured aneurysms may be left untreated if they exhibit the following benign characteristics: diameter less than 5 mm, localization beyond the surgical corridor, and morphologically regular configuration [8].

Subsequently, patients were further stratified into low-risk and high-risk cohorts. The low-risk cohort fulfilled the following criteria: (1) The aneurysm was situated beyond the confines of the circle of Willis, or aneurysm clipping performed without impairing the integrity of the circle of Willis (Willis classification: (I) anterior communicating artery [AcomA] compensation only, (II) posterior communicating artery [PcomA] compensation only, (III) combined AcomA and PcomA compensation, (IV) absence of collateral compensation); (2) According to the American Society of Interventional and Therapeutic Neuroradiology/Society of Interventional Radiology (ASTIN/SIR) collateral circulation grading system, the patient’s collateral perfusion were classified as Grade 3–4, indicating a well-developed collateral network and robust hemodynamic compensation within the ischemic territory; (3) Aneurysmal morphology was straightforward, with a well-defined neck suitable for direct microsurgical clipping; (4) Temporary occlusion of the parent artery or the circle of Willis was anticipated to be brief, thus minimizing the risk of prolonged ischemic insult to the compromised hemisphere. In such cases, a low-flow STA-MCA3 bypass via the transsylvian approach was implemented to augment perfusion of the affected hemisphere.

Conversely, the high-risk cohort demonstrated the following characteristics: (1) Hemodynamic compensation was dependent on a single communicating artery within the circle of Willis, with the aneurysm closely associated with this critical vessel; (2) ASTIN/SIR grading of Grade 1–2, reflecting inadequate collateral perfusion to the compromised cerebral hemisphere; (3) The aneurysm exhibited a complex architecture with a broad or irregular neck not amenable to direct clipping; (4) Prolonged intraoperative interruption of hemispheric perfusion was required, or permanent sacrifice of a compensatory artery within the circle of Willis was necessitated, resulting in sustained or irreversible loss of blood flow to the affected territory. For this group, a high-flow ECA-RA-MCA2 bypass was employed to reconstitute the perfusion dynamics of the circle of Willis, safeguard perfusion of the ischemic hemisphere during the procedure, preserve flow through eloquent perforators, and ensure durable postoperative augmentation of cerebral blood flow to ameliorate inadequate collateral circulation.(Refer to Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas non-normally distributed variables were reported as median (interquartile range). Categorical data were represented using proportions or percentages.

Results

Patient and aneurysm characteristics

Between January 2015 and December 2023, a total of 14 patients who met the inclusion criteria, harboring 17 aneurysms in aggregate, underwent craniotomy in our center. The cohort comprised 7 males (50.00%) and 7 females (50.00%), aged between 18 and 72 years, with a mean age of 50.56 ± 15.69 years. In the present study, 5 patients were admitted following SAH, while an additional 5 were hospitalized due to cerebral infarction. The remaining 4 patients presented with prodromal neurological symptoms, including episodic headache, dizziness, and transient ischemic attack. Within the cohort, six patients demonstrated occlusion of the right ICA, while one patient exhibited bilateral ICAO. Five patients presented with left-sided ICAO, and an additional two cases were characterized by segmental occlusion localized to the C7 portion of the left ICA. With the exception of Case 10, in which ICAO was iatrogenic in origin and demonstrated neither perfusion impairment nor intracranial–extracranial vascular abnormalities, the remaining 13 patients exhibited ICAO primarily secondary to underlying vasculopathies, including advanced atherosclerotic disease (11 cases), Takayasu arteritis (1 case), and Moyamoya syndrome (1 case). Most patients had a documented history of chronic hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and cerebral infarction. Preoperative CT-P or MR-P consistently demonstrated regional hypoperfusion and infarct foci within corresponding vascular territories. High-resolution vascular imaging further delineated concomitant cerebrovascular anomalies, including absence, stenosis, or pathological tortuosity of major intracranial and extracranial vessels: anterior cerebral artery (ACA, n = 7), basilar artery (BA, n = 1), MCA (n = 3), posterior cerebral artery (PCA, n = 2), vertebral artery (VA, n = 5), and ICA (n = 1).

The distribution of aneurysms (n = 17) was as follows: 6 in the AcomA, 3 in the PcomA, 1 at the PcomA-PCA junction, 1 in the A2 segment of ACA, 3 in the P1 segment of PCA, 1 in the P2 segment of the PCA, 1 in the V4 segment of VA, and 1 in the BA. These aneurysms were predominantly located on the same side as the ICA occlusion. The aneurysm sizes ranged from 3.0 to 18.0 mm, with a mean dimension of 5.38 ± 2.75 mm. 4 aneurysms displayed irregular morphologies, 1 was a multilobulated saccular aneurysm, and the remaining 12 exhibited classic saccular configurations. Preoperative mRS scores were as follows: 0 in 2 patient, 1 in 2 patient, 2 in 3 patients, 3 in 5 patients, 4 in 1 patient, 5 in 1 patient. Treatment outcomes and follow‑up

Cases 1 through 4 were categorized into the high-risk cohort. ASTIN/SIR collateral circulation grading predominantly revealed insufficient hemodynamic compensation (Grade 4 in one case; Grades 1–2 in three cases). Cases 1, 3, and 4 were admitted due to SAH and demonstrated aneurysms with complex morphologies, including large sac size, broad necks, or the presence of multiple aneurysms. All were accompanied by Type 2 Circle of Willis configurations and necessitated prolonged temporary occlusion of the cerebral hemisphere to enable aneurysm clipping. These patients underwent high-flow ECA–RA–MCA2 bypass followed by microsurgical clipping of the ruptured aneurysms and other aneurysms within the operative field. Case 2 presented with bilateral ICAO, resulting in diffuse cerebral hypoperfusion. The Circle of Willis exhibited Type 3 compensation. While the right PcomA aneurysm was unruptured, the parent artery demonstrated aneurysmal remodeling with fusiform ectasia. A left-sided high-flow ECA–RA–MCA2 bypass was performed to restore adequate perfusion.

Cases 5 through 14 were assigned to the low-risk cohort. The ASTIN/SIR grading evaluation demonstrates a balanced distribution between individuals exhibiting adequate and inadequate collateral compensation (Grades 3–4 in five cases; Grades 1–2 in five cases). Circle of Willis configurations were distributed as follows: two cases as Type 4; three cases as Type 3; two as Type 2; and three as Type 1. The aneurysms in this cohort displayed simple morphologies with narrow necks, and none required prolonged interruption of the Circle of Willis or ipsilateral hemispheric perfusion from the occluded ICA. In cases 8 and 13, owing to terminal occlusion of the ICA, aneurysm clipping was conducted without necessitating the interruption of perfusion to the ischemic cerebral tissue on the occluded side. Intraoperative exploration in Cases 5 and 9 revealed localized arterial ectasia without obvious wall fragility, and thus bypass surgery was performed exclusively. Case 10 underwent simple aneurysm clipping. The remaining patients underwent STA–MCA3 bypass followed by aneurysm clipping.

Postoperative complications within 30 days were confined to the high-risk group. Case 1 developed a right temporo-insular–basal ganglia Low-density lesion with ptosis at discharge. Case 3 experienced a left thalamic infarction on postoperative day two, resulting in right-sided motor weakness, which improved following rehabilitation. Case 4 suffered recurrent aneurysmal hemorrhage preoperatively and intraoperatively, culminating in progressive clinical deterioration, postoperative circulatory collapse, and death. No new neurological deficits or ischemic events were observed in the remaining patients. The 30-day postoperative stroke rate in the affected hemisphere was 7.14%, and the overall complication rate was 21.42%. CT-P/MR-P imaging demonstrates a perfusion improvement rate of 57.14%.

The mean follow-up duration was 41.46 ± 26.82 months. During follow-up, all surgically clipped aneurysms (n = 11, 64.70%), excluding the deceased patient, remained radiographically obliterated. Unclipped aneurysms in Case 2 (PcomA), Case 1 (VA4 segment), Case 5 (AcomA), and Case 9 (PCA1) remained radiographically stable (n = 4, 23.52%), with no evidence of growth, recurrence, hemorrhage, or de novo aneurysm formation. The patency rate of bypass grafts (n = 10) was 100%. In Case 1, the intracranial Low-density lesion and ptosis resolved completely. Case 3 experienced a recurrent left thalamic infarction in 2021 but regained independence following rehabilitation. The overall new ipsilateral stroke rate during the follow-up period was 7.14%. At last follow-up, mRS scores were 0 in 11 patients, 1 in one patient, and 2 in one patient. Detailed surgical techniques and outcomes are presented in Table 2.

Case 1: A 50-year-old female patient presented with a sudden-onset headache accompanied by vomiting for 1 day and was subsequently admitted to the hospital. DSA revealed a right ICAO with concurrent aneurysms located at AcomA, right PCA1, and right VA4 segment. The right cerebral hemisphere was supplied exclusively by collateral flow through the PcomA. Preoperative CT-P imaging indicated significant hypoperfusion in the right frontal, temporal, and parietal regions. The initial surgical intervention entailed definitive microsurgical clipping of the AComA aneurysm. In contrast, the aneurysm located at the P1 segment of the PCA exhibited intricate angioarchitecture, necessitating prolonged temporary occlusion of the parent vessel to facilitate precise aneurysmal remodeling and clip reconstruction. In scenarios where complete exclusion of the aneurysm proved unachievable, deliberate sacrifice of the P1 segment became a contingency, which would critically compromise perfusion to the right cerebral hemisphere and jeopardize eloquent territories supplied by the anterior thalamoperforating arteries. To avert these ischemic consequences, a high-flow ECA-RA-MCA2 bypass was employed, conferring both intraoperative neuroprotection and sustained postoperative hemodynamic support. This revascularization strategy not only preserved distal MCA perfusion but also enabled retrograde augmentation of proximal MCA flow, thereby ensuring continued patency of the perforating branches. Postoperative imaging confirmed complete aneurysmal obliteration, sustained graft patency, and a marked reduction in compensatory PcomA flow. Although ipsilateral upper eyelid ptosis was evident at discharge, the condition had fully resolved by the one-year follow-up, indicating complete functional restitution. (Refer to Figs. 2 and 4)

Discussion

This study retrospectively reviewed the Management outcomes of 14 cases of chronic ICAO associated with ischemic-side IAs, with a particular focus on the strategic application of high-flow bypass techniques in several cases. Throughout the postoperative and follow-up periods, the bypass grafts Maintained a patency rate of 100%. Longitudinal follow-up revealed notable improvements in both mRS scores and neurological symptomatology. The study findings demonstrate that individualized therapeutic strategies, tailored according to perioperative stroke risk, are both safe and efficacious in managing such patients. The synergistic approach of vascular bypass combined with aneurysm clipping effectively facilitates both the restoration of intracranial circulation and the control of aneurysm pathology.

Etiology and pathogenesis

Current evidence suggests that hemodynamic disturbances resulting from ICAO are closely implicated in the pathogenesis of IAs [9, 10]. Under conditions of elevated collateral flow, vessels within the circle of Willis, which are normally quiescent, are predisposed to aneurysmal formation or dilation. Shakur SF et al. [11] reported that in ICAO cases, patients with aneurysms exhibited significantly higher blood flow volume, flow velocity, and wall shear stress in the ACA1 segment compared to non-aneurysmal counterparts, with aneurysms predominantly arising in the AcomA and the PCA1 segment, both of which serve as critical collateral conduits. Werner C [12] observed that 72.6% of solitary aneurysms arose on the same side as carotid artery stenosis or occlusion; nonetheless, the presence of contralateral aneurysms was likewise non-random, with both phenomena intimately associated with hemodynamic perturbations. In our findings, all aneurysms, except for one in the VA4 segment, exhibited a close association with the circle of Willis. Of particular note, Case 10 demonstrated an aneurysm whose formation was concordant with flow dynamics altered by iatrogenic ICAO [13].

Surgical indications

In general, surgical indications for chronic ICAO complicated by IAs can be classified into two categories. The first pertains primarily to Manifestations of cerebral ischemia. Despite multiple studies, including the 2023 Chinese COSS clinical tria [14], refuting the efficacy of extracranial-intracranial bypass in the Management of ICAO, approximately 15% of ischemic lesions within the ICA territory remain attributable to occlusion. Furthermore, even under optimized pharmacologic therapy, the annual incidence of recurrent cerebrovascular events persists at a concerning rate of 7–18% [3, 15, 16]. Consequently, it is widely recognized in the academic community that only subtypes manifesting profound hemodynamic impairment are considered suitable candidates [17, 18]. In a long-term follow-up study, Teo KAC [19] utilized STA-MCA bypass to treat patients with severe internal carotid artery stenosis or occlusion accompanied by impaired cerebral perfusion confirmed by TCD and SPECT. Ischemic events occurred in 13% of cases, demonstrating superior outcomes compared to historical data on medical therapy alone. Given that the most widely utilized imaging modalities for evaluating cerebral tissue perfusion in clinical practice are CT-P and MR-P, we contend that cases exhibiting the following characteristics are optimal candidates for extracranial-intracranial bypass surgery, which include: recurrent watershed infarctions or ischemic episodes involving eloquent regions; occlusion or severe stenosis of two or more Major intracranial or extracranial arteries; and abnormalities in at least two perfusion metrics such as CBF, CBV, time-to-peak, or mean transit time on MR-P or CT-P imaging. Moreover, we underscore the meticulous correction of CT-P and MR-P discrepancies through DSA and CTA which provide superior precision in evaluating distal blood flow re-circulation, compensatory mechanisms, and cerebral perfusion velocities. These imaging modalities also enable a comprehensive preoperative assessment of both intracranial and extracranial vascular variations and pathologies. Among the final study cohort, the incidence of ipsilateral stroke within 30 days postoperatively and throughout the follow-up period was 7.14%. CT-P/MR-P imaging revealed a 57.14% improvement in cerebral perfusion, demonstrating efficacy comparable to that of pharmacologic intervention.

The second category is primarily defined by SAH or progressive aneurysmal enlargement. In this study, five cases presenting with SAH and one aneurysm (Case 10) demonstrating growth during long-term surveillance were identified. Masahiro Indo [20] reported a case of bilateral ICAO with a ruptured basilar artery aneurysm, where bilateral STA-MCA bypasses were performed due to the complexity and infeasibility of aneurysm clipping, with follow-up demonstrating gradual aneurysm regression. We firmly adhere to the practice of clipping all ruptured aneurysms, as it is consistent with the prevailing consensus within the academic community. However, for unruptured aneurysms, the decision to proceed with clipping should be meticulously determined intraoperatively, based on factors such as aneurysm morphology, sac consistency, and anatomical location [8]. In this series, four aneurysms, including those suspected of arterial wall bulging, were left unclipped, and no evidence of enlargement or rupture was observed during follow-up, underscoring the viability of a conservative approach.

Cerebrovascular event risk assessment

Due to the intricate nature of ICAO accompanied by ipsilateral IAs, a meticulous preoperative assessment is imperative. First, in cases where the aneurysm exhibits irregular morphology and significant size, controlling both the proximal and distal segments of the parent artery demands substantial traction, which may exacerbate existing cerebral ischemia. Lukas Goertz [21] emphasized that managing complex aneurysmal necks necessitates additional procedural interventions (such as temporary occlusion and clip adjustments), thereby elevating the risk of infarction. Furthermore, the onset of new ischemic stroke on the occluded side is intrinsically linked to the status of the Willis circle. Cassot F [22] noted that patients with an absent AcomA have a higher risk of stroke compared to those with a patent segment, likely attributable to variations within the Circle of Willis. Mohammed Oumer et al. [23] also emphasized that morphological variations in the Circle of Willis, such as hypoplasia or absence of PcomA and AcomA, are positively correlated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke. Within our cohort, only a small fraction of cases demonstrated a complete Willis circle; occlusion of the remaining functional portion could lead to profound ischemia in the occluded hemisphere. Moreover, an elevated Hunt-Hess grade and diminished collateral circulation suggest compromised cerebral blood flow regulation and reduced capacity to withstand fluctuations in blood flow. In particular, when collateral circulation is inadequate, ischemic risk significantly escalates during intraoperative arterial occlusion or hypotension [24]. Lastly, the capacity of the Willis circle to distribute and buffer cerebral blood flow remains a pivotal consideration for the surgeon [25, 26]. For instance, Case 2 involved bilateral ICAO, a tortuous and attenuated basilar artery, and a partially aneurysmal PcomA, rendering aneurysm clipping unfeasible. Consequently, we directed blood flow from the left ECA through the Willis circle to the right hemisphere, thereby mitigating the risk of rapid blood flow that could potentially induce rupture or progression of the right PcomA aneurysm. In light o f these factors, we stratified the cases into low-risk and high-risk groups.

Surgical methods

Previous reports have documented isolated cases utilizing STA-MCA bypass to Manage ICAO concomitant with IAs. In 1997, Takahashi [27] first pioneered the application of STA-MCA bypass in a patient with MCA occlusion complicated by an aneurysm. Subsequently, Inoue T [28] reported two cases of ICAO associated with unruptured AcomA aneurysms, in which STA-MCA2 bypass was performed prior to aneurysm clipping. Postoperative evaluations demonstrated significant enhancement of ipsilateral cerebral hemodynamic reserve, sustained graft patency, and absence of aneurysm recurrence. The outcomes observed in the low-risk subgroup of our study cohort further substantiate the efficacy and viability of this surgical strategy.But Inoue T advocated against the use of RA grafts for high-flow bypass, citing the elevated risk of hyperperfusion syndrome and the considerable technical intricacies inherent to the procedure.

However, one of the principal limitations contributing to the suboptimal efficacy of traditional surgical approaches for ICAO lies in the inadequate perfusion capacity afforded by a solitary STA graft [16, 29]. Chuan Chen [30] and Akihiro Inoue [31] eported that STA trunks with greater flow capacity, as well as double-barrel STA bypasses, can achieve favorable outcomes in the surgical management of ICAO. Grubb RL posited that high-flow RA bypasses confer superior efficacy in re-establishing cerebral perfusion in cases of chronic ICAO [32]. Consequently, in patients classified within the high-risk cohort, we opted for the ECA exhibiting robust flow rates between 100 and 200 ml/min as the inflow conduit [33], ensuring an ample and sustained blood supply to the ischemic cerebral tissue on the occluded side of the ICA. Furthermore, the RA anastomosis is positioned segment to the proximal MCA, with retrograde perfusion approaching the Willis circle, facilitating the maintenance of patency in critical perforating vessels, such as the MCA1 and ACA1 segments, while mitigating the compensatory burden placed on the Willis circle postoperatively. Although complications were encountered in the ECA-RA-MCA2 bypass procedures, the ultimate prognosis was deemed acceptable. For example, in Case 1, the patient depended exclusively on the PcomA to sustain anterior circulation. The aneurysm located at the PCA1 segment exhibited a highly irregular configuration, rendering simple clipping unfeasible and necessitating prolonged proximal PCA occlusion with meticulous aneurysmal remodeling. Therefore, an ECA-RA-MCA2 bypass was undertaken. Postoperative DSA confirmed complete aneurysmal exclusion, accompanied by a pronounced reduction in compensatory hemodynamic flow across the circle of Willis. In Case 3, the entire left PcomA was aneurysmal and ruptured, devoid of compensatory flow through the AcomA. Complete sacrifice of the PcomA was requisite. Postoperative thalamic infarction was attributed to the compromised PcomA, yet the RA continued to provide sufficient perfusion to the left cerebral hemisphere, with the patient subsequently demonstrating partial functional recovery after rehabilitative therapy.

Limitations of the study

The principal limitation of this study lies in its retrospective design and relatively modest sample size, with particular emphasis on the significant influence of high-risk cases on prognosis, which complicates the accurate evaluation of the method’s efficacy. Moreover, the ECA-RA-MCA2 surgical technique is intricate and demands expert execution by highly skilled surgeons, thus restricting its broader implementation. To enhance the robustness and generalizability of the findings, future investigations should focus on expanding the sample size and conducting multicenter trials.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive review of the individualized surgical treatment approach for patients with chronic ICAO and ipsilateral IAs, particularly in the context of limited interventional therapeutic options. Through the stratified management of surgical stroke risk, coupled with the strategic selection of low-flow or high-flow bypass grafting and aneurysm clipping, we successfully achieved cerebral perfusion restoration and meticulous control of aneurysm-related risks. The findings suggest that this strategy is not only safe and feasible but also holds significant clinical potential in the surgical management of such complex cases.
