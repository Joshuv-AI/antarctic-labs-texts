Life orientation and career adaptability among young adults in Poland
Authors: Anna Paszkowska-Rogacz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the correlation between career adaptability and developmental tasks among young adults concerning life orientation. Additionally, it sought to ascertain whether self-regulation functions as a mediator in the relationship between life orientation and career adaptability. The study included a total of 435 young adults aged between 18 and 34 years. The research employed the Polish versions of three questionnaires: the Social Participation Questionnaire, the Self-Regulation Scale, and the Career Adapt-Abilities Scale-5. The conducted research demonstrated that the assimilation and integrative social participation types predominated, and young adults were more likely to display a transitive life orientation than a moratorium orientation. The results also showed, as expected, a positive relationship between the promotional strategy and all components of the career adaptability. Mediation analysis revealed that the promotion regulatory focus acted as a mediator in the relationship between transitive life orientation and all five categories of career adaptability within the transitive orientation dependence model and, similarly to preventive regulatory focus, between moratorium orientation and the dimensions of concern and control. Preventive regulatory focus turned out to be a mediator between transitive orientation and career adaptability variables only in the case of three out of five variables—concern, control and curiosity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74344-1.Keywords: Young adults, Career adaptability, Regulatory focus, Life orientation, Mediation analysisSubject terms: Psychology, Psychology and behaviour

Introduction

In recent decades, most Western societies have undergone significant economic, demographic, and social changes closely tied to the transition into adult life. A discernible trend has emerged, indicating a delay in achieving traditional adulthood milestones, such as completing education, leaving the parental home, establishing independent households, marrying, or starting a family1–4. This delay may be attributed to the necessity of adapting to the continuously evolving conditions of the labor market characterized by globalization, instability, and uncertainties. Individuals, by experimenting and actively seeking novel experiences, shape their identity in various domains of life like romance, career, or worldview5,6. Nationwide studies conducted on young Polish adults aged 18–297,8 have verified the presence of personalized plans for entering adulthood. In these plans, the constituent tasks are not externally prescribed but rather internally gauged based on a sense of preparedness to undertake them.

Significantly, a notable portion of respondents rejects the responsibilities associated with adulthood, preferring to remain in the carefree realm of childhood and relish the freedoms of an unburdened life9. Responding to this, Rękosiewicz10 proposes the consideration of subjective indicators alongside objective markers, such as the birth of a child or securing stable employment, to denote the transition to adulthood. Subjective indicators include a sense of maturity and the shift from a present-focused moratorium orientation, centered on immediate opportunities, to a transitive orientation geared toward accomplishing subsequent adult developmental tasks.

A similar perspective is presented by Zagórska11, associating subjective feelings of adulthood with a willingness to engage in tasks typical of this life stage and possessing a self-concept aligned with functioning as an adult. These processes fall under the construct of emerging adulthood1,12, encompassing autonomy from parents, identity formation, establishing intimate relationships, and assuming accountability for decisions and actions, facilitating independence in goal-setting.

However, in this study, the concept of “young adults” was preferred over the notion of emerging adulthood. Both groups share the characteristic delay in assuming adult responsibilities, as evidenced by recent demographic analyses presented in Poland by the Central Statistical Office13, indicating a significant increase in the age of individuals entering close relationships and starting families, extending up to the ages of 30–34.

One of the primary developmental tasks during early adulthood, aside from assuming family and parental responsibilities, involves making pivotal decisions concerning career development. It is during this life stage that individuals typically make choices that significantly influence their vocational trajectory in the future. Consequently, researchers have been intrigued by a specific facet of adulthood: an individual’s capacity to adapt to a career and its correlation with developmental milestones such as social participation. Furthermore, an individual’s expectations regarding career tasks and the motivating factors behind their actions play a fundamental role in their performance. From this perspective, the concept of regulatory focus becomes pertinent. According to Higgins’ research14 this concept delineates how individuals regulate themselves to achieve ideal goals or avert negative outcomes through various strategic approaches. Depending on their regulatory focus, individuals either pursue desired aspirations via a promotion focus or attain required objectives while evading undesirable consequences through a prevention focus.

In this context, the present research aims to explore the mediating role of young adults’ regulatory focus in elucidating the relationship between their life orientation and career adaptability. The study also seeks to identify predominant types of life orientation and their combination creating types of social participation among the studied group of young adults, ascertain the levels of components associated with ability to adapt to a career and self-regulatory focus, and examine the interrelationships among the variables encompassed within the mediation model.

Conceptual framework and hypotheses

Career adaptability, life orientation, and social participation

The construct of career adaptability is one of the most significant recent additions to the field of vocational psychology. According to Super15 and commentators of his works (among others, Goodman,16), the term Career Adaptability refers to an individual’s maintaining a balance between the professional and private environment. The components of career adaptability are values connected with work, such as autonomy and ability to take one’s own decisions, long-term planning, looking for and taking new positions, taking decisions and willingness to reflect upon one’s own experience. Super’s disciple and collaborator Savickas17,18 developed the notion of career adaptability, which is characterised by a new perspective on the job market. It no longer consists in finding a permanent suitable place for the individual, like in the conventional approach; instead, it consists in creating and preparing a job tailored to one’s needs, preferences and abilities. The distinctive features of a new career are focus on feelings, passion and what truly brings one satisfaction with life. In this way, career becomes a form of one’s life creation. In Savickas’s view, career adaptability is a multi-faceted construct, which consists of such elements as: Concern – focus on one’s own professional future, Control – taking control of one’s future career, Curiosity – looking for new possibilities to grow and ideas for future, as well as Confidence, or reinforcing one’s own competencies in such a way as to safeguard achievement of professional goals. Thus, career adaptability becomes a psycho-social meta-competence, which facilitates appropriate reactions to changes and expectations of new economic reality18–22. It is connected with curiosity of the world and oneself as well as flexibility and openness to change, or even resilience23. This kind of adaptability is not a permanent trait. It can be better seen as a resource which is developed under the influence of experience and which is crucial to good functioning on the job market. It helps one effectively manage developmental tasks, such as transition from school to work or change of workplace or job24.

According to Chen et al.25 there are five main topics in career adaptability research, namely: the boundaryless mindset, career construction, the proactive personality, career adaptability scale, and life design. Rudolph et al.26 conducted a meta-analysis encompassing 90 studies that investigated the associations between career adaptability and three distinct sets of variables: traits indicative of adaptability, such as proactivity; adaptive responses, including career planning; and outcomes of adaptation, such as job satisfaction. The analysis revealed a positive correlation between adaptability and engaging in career planning, exploring professional opportunities, and possessing a sense of self-efficacy (both generally and concerning professional decision-making). Moreover, adaptability emerged as a predictor of work identification, experiencing a vocational calling, overall life satisfaction, engagement in work and education, employability, self-assessed performance, income, and positive emotional states. Additionally, significant correlations were observed with age and educational attainment. Importantly, career adaptability demonstrated negative associations with occupational stress, intentions to quit employment, and negative emotional states.

The ongoing research aims to explore the link between career adaptability and subjective characteristics, alongside its ramifications, notably commitment to work27, career agility and career embeddedness28, as well as job performance and life satisfaction29. However, there remains a need for further investigation into the relationship between general maturity and career adaptability among young adults. In the limited studies conducted thus far on the developmental paradigm, age hasn’t consistently emerged as a significant predictor in the development of career adaptability30–32.

The concept’s proponents, Savickas and Porfeli33, propose that various developmental variables, including identity statuses, may serve as predictors of the components constituting career adaptability. In the standardization of the American version of the Career Adapt-Ability Scale (CAAS), these scholars utilized vocational identity research methodology34, identifying six fundamental identity statuses. Among these statuses, four, as noted by Brzezińska2, based on Marcia’s model35, are delineated through a combination of two phases: exploration and commitment. These statuses encompass identity achievement, foreclosed identity, diffused identity, and moratorium identity. Additionally, two more statuses emerged by subdividing the exploration category into in-depth versus extended and the commitment category into making versus identification. Consequently, a 'searching moratorium,' also referred to as tentative commitment, was positioned between the achieved and moratorium identity statuses. Moreover, acknowledging that a fraction of young individuals doesn’t distinctly fit into any of these five statuses, a sixth status, ‘undifferentiated,’ was delineated. The study groups exhibited a systematic increase in CAAS scores across all dimensions34, displaying escalating developmental potential from diffused identity to achieved identity. Research that assumes a developmental approach to the determinants of career adaptability was conducted by Gregor et al.36. The purpose of this study was to investigate the relations among agentic characteristics (personal growth initiative and grit), barriers (perceptions of academic and educational barriers and coping with barriers), and career adaptability in a sample of diverse community college students. This research has shown support for personal growth as a predictor of career adaptability. Although not directly referencing the concept of career adaptability, Polish research in the developmental realm by Turska and Stasiła-Sieradzka37,38 unveiled significant correlations between attitudes toward transitioning into the labor market and variables pertinent to young people’s development: some manifested a transitive orientation (i.e., a positive outlook), while others exhibited a moratorium attitude (i.e., a negative stance). The authors also draw from Reinders and Butz39, who outlined four developmental trajectories among young individuals based on high versus low intensity of transitive life orientation and moratorium life orientation. These trajectories correspond to four types of social participation: Integration (characterized by a high level of both orientations), Assimilation (exhibiting a strong transitive orientation and weak moratorium stance), Segregation (marked by a low level of transitive orientation but high moratorium disposition), and Marginalization (where both levels are low). It emerged that individuals following the assimilation path displayed a more favorable attitude toward the transition from education to the labor market compared to those following the segregation path.

Based on the previously established close relationship between developmental factors and career adaptability, it is hypothesized that the type of life orientation and social participation significantly relate to career adaptability. Specifically, the transitive orientation, along with the assimilation and integration types of social participation, are anticipated to exhibit a positive association with dimensions of career adaptability. Conversely, the moratorium orientation, in addition to the marginalization and segregation types of social participation, are expected to display a negative association with career adaptability (Hypothesis 1).

Regulatory focus as a mediating process

Regulatory focus serves as one of the widely embraced approaches for examining an individual’s motivational mechanisms, as delineated in Higgins’ regulatory focus theory14,40–42. This theory posits that sources of motivation stem from the configuration of an individual’s self-structure. Higgins highlights individuals’ motivational standards as self-regulatory focus and delves into the mechanisms governing their engagement in taking action, termed self-regulatory adjustment. These motivational standards shape expectations concerning an individual’s actions. Those with a high promotion focus set goals aligned with personal aspirations, directing their attention toward future positive outcomes. Conversely, individuals with a high preventive focus establish goals based on obligations and safety needs, focusing on evading failure. According to regulatory focus theory40,41, aligning self-regulatory focus with environmental demands significantly impacts an individual’s engagement in an activity. This theory suggests that individuals function more effectively when their self-regulatory orientation corresponds with the characteristics of the task or situation in which they are involved. Moreover, regulatory focus theory posits that the self-regulation processes vary based on the needs they fulfill. The promotion regulatory focus aims to meet nurturance needs linked to ideas and aspirations, while the preventive regulatory direction caters to security needs. These two regulatory orientations are not opposing ends of a single continuum but rather independent dimensions, constituting separate systems of self-regulation. It is possible for both orientations to be distinctly active simultaneously, or for one or neither of them to be engaged.

Further research by Lockwood et al.43 suggests probable alterations in the significance and intensity of regulatory focus throughout an individual’s life span. Generally, younger individuals exhibit a more promotional inclination, emphasizing the positives they can achieve while paying lesser attention to potential threats or failures. As individuals age, the importance of preventive focus tends to increase, although it doesn’t entirely replace the promotion focus. Consequently, individuals with a preventive focus are more characteristic of those exhibiting a transitive orientation, which also develops with age10,44. This theory has sparked numerous studies investigating how promotional and preventive strategies influence human functioning. Several noteworthy relationships have been found with traits such as openness to change45, decision-making46, temperament47, personality48, and job satisfaction49. However, relatively few studies have analyzed the impact of individual motivation and engagement on the process of career adaptation in relation to self-regulation. An illustrative example of such a relationship can be found in a study on the Spanish adaptation of the CAAS questionnaire50, which highlighted a positive relationship between self-regulation and all components of career adaptability. Dutch researchers51 also delved into the role of self-regulation in the context of career adaptability. Their findings aligned with expectations, showcasing that a high level of promotion regulatory focus and engagement in pro-development activities correlated positively with three scales of adaptability: concern, curiosity, and confidence. Conversely, the results of the preventive regulatory focus scale, associated with resistance to change and the selection of obligatory actions while minimizing losses, exhibited a negative correlation with confidence and a positive correlation with control.

Based on these arguments, it is anticipated that a significant relationship exists between life orientations and self-regulation. In the case of a transitive orientation, a positive relationship is expected with preventive focus and a negative one with promotion focus, while concerning a moratorium orientation, there will likely be a positive relationship with promotion focus and a negative one with preventive focus (Hypothesis 2). In line with empirical underpinnings a significant relationship between self-regulation and all components of career adaptability was anticipated. It was expected that in the case of a promotion focus, this relationship would be positive, while in the case of a preventive focus, it would be negative (Hypothesis 3).

Considering arguments supporting the independence of both regulatory foci as distinct systems, it was hypothesized that both promotion and prevention focus would mediate the relationship between life orientation types and career adaptability (Hypothesis 4).

Method

Participants and procedure

Data were collected from a cohort of 435 participants recruited in Poland, comprising 274 women and 160 men, aged between 18 and 34 (M = 23.76; SD = 3.37), through an online survey. The participant pool primarily consisted of full-time students (75,9%) and recent graduates from various fields such as technical, artistic, medical, and humanities disciplines. Within this group, 63.9% were employed individuals with work experience, 25.3% were previously employed but presently unemployed, and 6.1% were individuals without prior professional experience, initiating their careers with their first job. The group was differentiated by place of permanent residence—22. 8% lived in the countryside, 15.6% in cities with up to 50,000 inhabitants, 15.6% in cities with 50,000–200,000 inhabitants and 40% in cities with more than 200,000 inhabitants.

The research was discussed by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Psychology, which found no violations of ethical principles. Therefore, I did not submit an application to the university ethics committee. because these studies are nor subject to evaluation by the Ethics Committee of University of Lodz. Research that does not constitute medical experiments is not subject to evaluation by the Commission: surveys and questionnaires, especially if the subjects are adults who express informed consent to the research.

Participants received an information letter outlining the broad research goals, the relevance of the overall project, the policy regarding confidentiality, and detailed information on the sampling procedure, and a statement confirming that informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. Participation was voluntary, and respondents could stop completing the research protocol at any time without further explanation. Because participation was voluntary, we took participants’ responses to the various questionnaires as an indication of consent. An invitation to participate in the study was distributed via university social media channels. Interested individuals who volunteered to take part accessed the online survey using a web link provided in the invitation. Participants were asked to use individual desktop computers to complete all questionnaires in a counterbalanced sequence.

Measures

Career Adaptability Scale – 5

The Career Adaptability Scale – 552,53 is a Polish adaptation of an instrument developed by Paszkowska-Rogacz in 2020. The original Career Adapt-Ability Scale (CAAS) was developed by Savickas and Porfeli24. It is used for measuring both general career adaptability and development of competencies in its particular areas. The original scale consists of 24 statements, which make up four equinumerous sub-scales: Concern, Control, Curiosity and Confidence. The statements describe skills necessary for the development of a professional career. The study subject expresses his or her judgement of each of them using a 5-point scale from 1 (not strong) to 5 (strongest). The international version of the questionnaire, available in English, was developed on the basis of the analysis of data from 13 countries. The Cronbach’s α reliability coefficient for the original instrument was 0.92 for the general score and, respectively, (0.83), (0.74), (0.79) and (0.85) for the scales of concern, control, curiosity and confidence. The confirmatory analysis for all the studied groups showed good fit indices (CFI = 0.94; RMSEA = 0.05). In the subsequent years, the standardisation covered another ten countries: Australia, Spain, Hong Kong54, Croatia55, Greece56, Iran57, Lithuania58, Germany59, Romania60, and Turkey61. In all the aforementioned studies, the reliability coefficients for the scales exceeded 0.80 and fit indices were satisfactory. According to the existing reports on the standardisation of CAAS, originally career adaptability had a 5-factor structure, including the dimension of Cooperation, which referred to the interpersonal competencies of an individual52. The authors of the scale were of the opinion, however, that even though the cooperation subscale shows brilliant psychometric properties, it is not consistent with the other dimensions of CAAS in terms of its content. Ultimately, they did not include the factor of cooperation in the international version of the questionnaire, because the 5-factor model did not work very well in relation to all the 13 countries which took part in the standardisation process. On the other hand, scholars such as Einarsdóttir et al.62 or McMahon, et al.63 noticed that the cooperation scale can turn out to be a reliable and accurate technique in collective cultures in which the quality of social relations is an important predictor of career adaptability. They suggested that CAAS could measure the internal resources of an individual, while the auxiliary cooperation scale could reflect environmental resources. Furthermore, Pulakos et al.64 identified ‘interpersonal adaptability’ as one of eight sub-dimensions of general adaptability, consisting of flexibility and openness in contact with others, listening and taking into account their views and opinions as well as developing effective relations with individuals representing different personality types. A similar operation was carried out by Ployhart and Bliese65 in their theory of adaptation by the inclusion of the dimension of interpersonal competencies within general adaptability. On the basis of the aforementioned arguments, Nye et al.52 made an attempt at enriching the original CAAS instrument with the dimension of cooperation using the 11 items proposed by Savickas and Porfeli. The study subjects were from individualistic (USA) and collective (Taiwan and China) cultures. The final version of CAAS-5 includes six items with the greatest factor loadings. The analysis demonstrated the accuracy of the 5-factor model of career adaptability both in the case of American students with professional experience and students from China and Taiwan; importantly, the subjects from China and Taiwan scored significantly higher than Americans on the cooperation scale (Cohen’s d effect of, respectively, 0.92 and 0.57). The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients for this scale, even though slightly below the other scales, were similar in all the studied groups and amounted to 0.77 (USA and China) and 0.84 (Taiwan).

Polish adaptation, comprising 30 items, extends the CAAS questionnaire introduced by Savickas and Porfeli. In this scale, respondents evaluate the degree to which they have developed specific skills across equinumerous five subscales: Concern (e.g., preparing for the future), Control (e.g., making independent decisions), Curiosity (e.g., observing various task approaches), Confidence (e.g., overcoming challenges), and Cooperation (e.g., understanding others’ perspectives). All statements within the scale are positively phrased and structured similarly to the original tool. Scores are then tallied for each subscale and for all test items to derive a global score. A major rationale for a five-factor model of career adaptability that includes the cooperation dimension used in this study comes from cross-cultural research, suggesting a cultural bias toward individualistic conceptualizations of career constructs such that relational or interpersonal dimensions are often ignored66,67. Poland’s deep grounding in family values leads the country to be more collectivistic than many other Western countries68. As a result, it regularly gets misleadingly defined as ‘Eastern’ when compared to Central Europe.

The Polish version of the questionnaire53 demonstrates satisfactory fit ratios: χ2/df = 3.3; goodness-of-fit index (GFI) = 0.89; comparative fit index (CFI) = 0.93; Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) = 0.89; root-mean-square error of approximation (RMSEA) = 0.06; standardized root-mean-square residual (SRMR) = 0.08; normed fit index (NFI) = 0.87. The possibility of comparing reliability indices from related studies was analysed and Cronbach’s alphas were calculated for each scale but due to the limitations associated with the use of Cronbach’s alpha—offers a more accurate and flexible assessment of reliability by considering the factor structure, allowing for different item contributions, and providing robustness against violations of assumptions inherent to Cronbach’s α69–71, for the purposes of this research, it was also decided to calculate McDonald’s Omega for each scale. All scales in the 5-factor version are also characterized by high reliability, as indicated by Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega (in brackets) : Concern—0.86 (0.84), Control—0.81(0.82), Curiosity—0.83 (0.82), Confidence—0.86 (0.86), Cooperation—0.77 (0.78), and overall Adaptability—0.92(0.92).

Social Participation Questionnaire—SPQ

The SPQ is an original Polish tool72,73 developed by Brzezińska et al.2, grounded in Reinders et al. theory39, which posits that social participation types evolve during the transition from adolescence to adulthood. This scale aims to diagnose Moratorium Life Orientation (e.g., preferring to avoid responsibilities and enjoy leisure) and Transitive Life Orientation (e.g., actively pursuing a job ensuring future success), thereby assessing four dimensions of social participation: integration (high in both moratorium and transitive orientation), segregation (high moratorium, low transitive), marginalization (low in both orientations), and assimilation (high transitive, low moratorium). The present study utilized the abbreviated version of the tool (KPSS-2) recommended for individuals aged 20–35. This version comprises two subscales, each containing 10 items, totaling 20. Respondents rate each statement on a 5-point scale (ranging from definitely not to definitely yes). A higher score on the Moratorium Orientation scale signifies a focus on current goals and opportunities, while a higher score on the Transitive Orientation scale indicates a focus on future goal setting.

For the purposes of this research the factorial structure of SPQ was validated with use of confirmatory factor analysis. Diagonally weighted Least Squares (DWLS) was also used as the estimation method. The fit of the assumed factorial structure was satisfactory. The values of fit indices were equal to CFI = 0.95 (above recommended value of 0.90), NFI = 0.92 (above recommended value of 0.90), SRMR = 0.07 (below the recommended value of 0.08), RMSEA = 0.06 (below the recommended value of 0.08). However, in order to achieve this satisfactory additional intercorrelations between items needed to be taken into account. All additional correlations linked items belonging to the same scales. The correlations were added on the basis of modification indices. Without these correlations the fit of the factor structure was not satisfactory. The values of fit indices were equal to CFI = 0.87 (below recommended value of 0.90), NFI = 0.84 (below recommended value of 0.90), SRMR = 0.10 (above the recommended value of 0.08), RMSEA = 0.09 (above the recommended value of 0.08).

Previously, the tool displayed satisfactory psychometric properties with Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficients at 0.86 for the Moratorium Orientation Subscale and 0.84 for the Transitive Orientation Subscale10. In this study, the obtained Cronbach’s alpha reliability indicators and McDonald’s omega (in brackets) were 0.81 (0.80) and 0.83 (0.82), respectively. In this case, McDonald’s omega values turned out to very similar to Cronbach’s alpha and amounted to 0.8.

Scale of promotional and preventive regulatory focus – SSPP

This scale74 diagnoses promotion and prevention biases, conceived as relatively stable characteristics, based on Higgins’ regulatory bias theory14. Comprising 27 items, respondents rate each statement on a five-point scale, from 1 indicating definitely no to 5 indicating definitely yes. The statements form five subscales: Promotional Standards (SPro) (e.g., In life, I usually do what I want); Promotional Self-control (KPro) (e.g., I like to act spontaneously); Preventive Standards (SPre) (e.g., I usually do what I have to); Preventive Self-control (KPre) (e.g., I know I can be wrong, so I act carefully); and Strength of Motivation (M) (e.g., It’s hard to discourage me when I make up my mind). Additionally, the subscales for standards and self-control amalgamate into two overarching scales: Promotion (Pro) with nine items, Prevention (Pre) with 11 items, and Strength of Motivation (M) with seven items, constituting a three-factor structure.

The factorial structure of SSPP was validated with use of confirmatory factor analysis. Diagonally weighted Least Squares (DWLS) was used as the estimation method. The fit of the assumed factorial structure was not satisfactory. The values of fit indices were equal to CFI = 0.82 (below recommended value of 0.90), NFI = 0.78 (below recommended value of 0.90), SRMR = 0.09 (above the recommended value of 0.08), RMSEA = 0.09 (above the recommended value of 0.08). Also, the modification indices indicated the need to assign items to the factor they did not originally belong. Therefore, to examine the factorial structure further principal component analysis was applied. On the basis of the scree-plot shape two components were extracted with the use of Oblimin rotation. The first component explained 22.1% of variance, while the second one explained 15.5% of variance. The extracted components corresponded well to the original scales, component no. 1 to Promotional self-control and component no. 2 to Preventive self-control.

As per the questionnaire authors, the general scales exhibit satisfactory reliability, with values of 0.77 for both Pro and Pre scales, 0.73 for M, and ranging between 0.57 (KPro) and 0.74 (KPre) for the detailed scales. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega coefficients (in brackets) were 0.75 (0.74) for the Pre scale, 0.81 (0.81) for the Pro scale, and 0.83 (0.83) for the M scale. Consequently, for ease of comparison with prior research, this study includes results solely for the general scales. In this research, only the results of two scales are taken into account – Promotion (Pro) and Preventive (Pre).

Data analysis

The research was designed using a non-experimental quantitative approach, specifically a descriptive correlational method. The determination of the number of subjects was based on the calculation technique outlined by Gay and Diehl as cited in Cosby75 for the descriptive correlational research method.. The collected sample size met the minimum requirement for path analysis using Structural Equation Modeling (SEM), which necessitates a minimum sample size of 200 individuals76.

Before the main analyses, the distribution of all scale scores was examined, and assumptions for multivariate analysis, including measurement scale reliability, as well as checks for missing and outlier values, were conducted. After examining the measures of skewness and kurtosis, it was found that they ranged from − 1 to 1. This suggests that none of the distributions exhibit significant asymmetry or deviate substantially from a normal distribution in terms of kurtosis77.

To assess the potential for common method variance (CMV) in our dataset, we conducted Harman’s single-factor test78. This test involves an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to determine the number of factors that are necessary to account for the variance in the variables. The EFA was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) with no rotation. According to the test, if a substantial amount of common method variance exists, a single factor will emerge from the factor analysis or one general factor will account for the majority of the covariance among the measures. The results of the analysis indicated that 17 factors had eigenvalues greater than 1.0, and these factors together accounted for 63.45% of the total variance. The first factor accounted for 18.22% of the variance, which is less than the threshold of 50%, suggesting that common method variance is not a major concern in our dataset.

The collected data underwent processing through univariate repeated measures ANOVA analyses to identify the predominant types of life orientation, self-regulation strategies, and career adaptability. To select four types of social participation based on the results of transitive and moratorium orientation, k-means cluster analysis was employed, using moratorium and transitive orientation as criteria.

To examine the hypothesis regarding the relationship between life orientation, social participation, self-regulation focus, and career adaptability, correlations among all continuous variables (two life orientations, two self-regulation focuses, and five career adaptability dimensions) were computed. Additionally, ANOVA analyses were conducted to compare levels of career adaptability across the four types of social participation clusters.

For testing the mediating effect of promotion and preventive regulatory focus on the relationship between life orientation and career adaptability, multiple mediation analyses were performed using the Hayes PROCESS macro in IBM SPSS 27 software79. Employing a multiple mediator model allows the evaluation of more than one mediating pathway between a predictor and an outcome80. The process involved bootstrapping resampling procedures, repeatedly sampling from the dataset with replacement to estimate the total indirect effect and specific indirect effects. Bootstrapping generated bias-corrected lower and upper confidence intervals for each effect based on specified confidence levels.

To ascertain significance, examination of the confidence intervals was crucial. A zero between the lower and upper confidence interval values suggests a nonsignificant effect, while the absence of zero indicates a significant effect. The total indirect effect determines whether both mediators together influence the relationship between life orientation and career adaptability indicators. Meanwhile, the specific indirect effects assess each mediator’s unique contribution in explaining the effect of life orientation on career adaptability indicators in comparison to the other mediator in the model81.

Comparison of the specific indirect effects of each mediator helps determine if there’s a significant difference in their abilities to mediate the relationship between life orientation and career adaptability indicators. The software utilized for these analyses was SPSS 27.

Results

Preliminary analyses

The first step of the analysis was to check the role of demographic variables, so Pearson correlations of the results of all scales with age were checked and the only significant, negative relationship appeared between age and moratorium orientation (r = − 0.20; p < 0.001). Comparisons between women and men made using One-Way ANOVA (sums of equinumerous scales were compared) showed higher scores for men in terms of control, than women: (man: M = 21.95, SD = 4.70; woman: M = 20.12, SD = 5.31), F(1, 433) = 13.04; p < 0.001; η2 = 0.07), moratorium orientation: (man: M = 32.28, SD = 6.27; woman: M = 30.17, SD = 6.33), F(1, 433) = 11.38; p < 0.001; η2 = 0.06 (both effects are medium), women in terms of transitive orientation: (man: M = 35.81, SD = 5.89; woman: M = 37.21, SD = 5.73), F(1, 433) = 6.15; p < 0.001; η2 = 0.04 (indicates small effect), and preventive focus (man: M = 38.61, SD = 5.89; woman: M = 40.00, SD = 6.20), F(1, 433) = 5.38; p = 0.02; η2 = 0.04 (indicates small effect). Place of residence and vocational and educational status did not differentiate the results.

The second step involved identifying predominant life orientation types, self-regulation focuses, and career adaptability factors. When comparing the mean scores the findings revealed that the transitive orientation prevailed over the moratorium, F(1, 434) = 152.92; p < 0.001; ω2 = 0.55 while preventive focus outweighed promotional focus, F(1, 434) = 84.49; p < 0.001; ω2 = 0.10. Omega Squared effect sizes were notably large and medium, respectively. The lowest scores were observed on the Concern scale, whereas the highest scores were on the Control scale. Post hoc comparisons revealed that differences between the Control and Confidence, Control and Cooperation, and Confidence and Cooperation scales were not statistically significant. Consequently, the following independent groups were distinguished: (1) Integrative type: high moratorium orientation and high transitive orientation (27.7% of respondents); (2) Assimilation type: low moratorium orientation and high transitive orientation (34.9% of respondents); (3) Segregation type: high moratorium and low transitive orientation (13.7% of respondents); (4) Marginalization type: low transitive and low moratorium orientation (23.7% of respondents).

Career adaptability subscale profiles across four types of social participation.

Life orientation, type of social participation, and career adaptability

Hypothesis 1, assuming specific relationships between social participation and the dimensions of career adaptability, was tested in two ways. First, Pearson’s r correlation was performed analysis utilizing which indicated significant positive correlations between transitive orientation and all CAAS-5 subscales, particularly strong with the concern scale. Moratorium orientation exhibited a negative correlation with the concern scale and a positive one with the control scale; however, the correlation was relatively weak. No correlation was observed with the other three scales (Table 1).

Secondly ANOVA was applied to the Career Adaptability (CAAS-5) subscale scores across four types of social participation clusters: Integration, Segregation, Marginalization, and Assimilation (Table 2). Following the ANOVA, post hoc tests were conducted to identify which specific groups differed from each other. The GT2 Hochberg method was used for these comparisons. Significant differences were observed across the social participation types. Post hoc comparisons indicate that the Concern scores for the Integration and Assimilation groups are significantly higher than those for the Segregation and Marginalization groups. These results suggest that individuals in Integration and Assimilation contexts exhibit higher levels of concern regarding their career adaptability. The Segregation group scored significantly higher than the Marginalization group in control, with Integration and Assimilation showing intermediate values. This indicates variability in the sense of control over career adaptability, with segregation contexts potentially fostering a stronger sense of control compared to marginalization.

Life orientation and self-regulation

The verification of the second hypothesis was based on correlation analysis and showed that the promotion strategy has a significant and positive relationship with both transitive and moratorium orientations. Conversely, the preventive strategy correlates positively with transitive orientation but negatively with moratorium orientation (see Table 1). This implies that Hypothesis 2 was confirmed only in relation to the preventive strategy’s negative correlation with moratorium orientation.

Self-regulation and career adaptability

Correlation analysis using Pearson’s r revealed significant positive correlations between promotional regulatory focus and all CAAS-5 subscales, consistent with the expectations posited in the third hypothesis (see Table 1). Notably, promotional regulatory focus exhibited the strongest correlation with the control scale. However, the correlations regarding prevention focus did not entirely align with expectations. While a negative correlation was observed with the control scale, positive correlations were found with the concern and curiosity scales. The remaining two correlations were deemed insignificant. This confirms Hypothesis 3 concerning the positive relationships between promotional regulatory focus and career adaptability dimensions.

Testing for mediation effects

Multiple mediation analysis allows the assessment of competing hypotheses within a unified model9. In this study, bias-corrected (BC) 95% CI bootstrapping based on 5000 samples was employed to concurrently assess two competing explanations of the mediating role between life orientations and indicators of career adaptability: promotion and prevention regulatory focus. For the transition orientation, promotion and prevention regulatory focus were analyzed as parallel mediators with the exception of the models in which confidence or cooperation were analyzed as explained variables. There was no statistically significant correlations between prevention and confidence and between prevention and cooperation (see Table 1). Therefore in the mediation models for the confidence and cooperation as the explained variable promotion was the only mediator. For the moratorium orientation only concern and control were analyzed as explained variables due to the lack of statistically significant correlations between moratorium orientation curiosity, confidence and cooperation (see Table 1).

Mediation models for each of the five indicators of career adaptability, using standardized (β) regression coefficients, are depicted in Fig. 2, portraying transitive orientation as an independent variable, and in Fig. 3, presenting moratorium orientation as the independent variable for the two indicators of career adaptability. Analysis of the direct effects in Fig. 2 indicates a significant positive association between transitive orientation and all dimensions of career adaptability. In all five models, transitive orientation is significantly and positively linked with promotion focus.

Discussion

The distribution of participation types identified in the study aligns with findings reported by other authors in studies involving young adults10,24,37,38. Among the observed types, assimilation and integrative personalities predominantly prevailed within the group. Furthermore, young adults were more inclined to exhibit a transitive orientation rather than a moratorium orientation. It could be speculated that this inclination may amplify with age, although the positive effects on individuals undertaking professional tasks remain unknown.

Marcia35 emphasized, in accordance with the societal norms of the 1960s, the importance of concluding the moratorium phase and progressing towards decision-making and final choices. A contemporary viewpoint, articulated by Mianowska83, suggests that moratorium behavior can be interpreted as young individuals seeking their unique lifestyle by exploring new forms of activities not accessible in earlier life stages. This period allows individuals to gain better insight into their strengths, weaknesses, and opportunities for educational and professional growth. Additionally, given the unpredictable nature of the current labor market, there is a pressing need to develop strategies for life in the moratorium phase and temporary solutions that may eventually prove effective.

Consequently, it’s unsurprising that a preventive strategy might be favored over a promotional one. Those who self-regulate based on a preventive strategy tend to make more deliberate and analytical choices, albeit more slowly yet accurately48. Similar inclinations have been observed among young adults in prior studies47.

Our current analysis of the CAAS-5 scales indicates that the studied group exhibits less adaptability towards a career compared to those described in earlier international studies84. However, it’s noteworthy that these prior studies utilized the four-factor version of the test, whereas the present study employs the five-factor version. The mean CAAS-5 scores for each scale are comparable to those obtained in studies employing other five-factor questionnaires among young adults from Korea and China but notably lower than those from America52.

Our findings partially support our hypotheses regarding the potential correlation between life orientation, social participation type, and career adaptability. It appears that participants characterized by an assimilation-type (low moratorium, high transitivity) demonstrate a weaker sense of control concerning their future careers. This observation might signify a realistic evaluation of the current labor market, as previously indicated in a study conducted in Poland by Kasprzak7. Conversely, individuals exhibiting an integration-type orientation (high in both moratorium and transitivity) display the highest levels of career adaptability across all dimensions. Conversely, those identified with marginalization and segregation tendencies exhibit the lowest adaptability. Intriguingly, individuals in the latter group, although demonstrating higher control compared to those with a greater moratorium orientation, seem to have a weaker sense of control. Similar findings were also noted by Turska and Stasiła-Sieradzka37,38, who observed these individuals showing reluctance towards transitioning into the labor market.

As anticipated, our results indicate a positive association between the promotional strategy and all facets of career adaptability. These findings align with results reported by Vianen et al.51. However, contrary to our study, the authors did not identify a strong positive relationship between the promotional strategy and control. Interestingly, while they noted that a preventive attitude exhibited negative associations with all five adaptive abilities, our study only found a significant negative correlation between the preventive strategy and control. This distinct relationship aligns with expectations outlined by Higgins40, suggesting that pursuing a preventive strategy tends to be linked with diminished resources of control and self-assurance. Individuals following a preventive approach often harbor concerns regarding the potential negative consequences of their actions, thereby undermining their confidence in overcoming obstacles. Hence, it is surprising to discover strong positive correlations between the promotional focus and both the transitive orientation (as anticipated) and the moratorium orientation (unexpectedly). Higgins indicates that the realization of a self-regulation process depends on the needs it aims to fulfill. A promotional attitude satisfies needs for achievement, growth, and development, encouraging individuals to embrace challenges and risks. It’s plausible that these needs are specifically linked to both orientations. In contrast, a preventive attitude42, grounded in duty-based standards, fulfills the need for security, favoring stability and a cautious approach. In our study, a negative correlation emerged between the preventive strategy and the moratorium orientation, contradicting our predictions. It’s essential to note that the two regulatory strategies described by Higgins are distinct systems of self-regulation, rather than opposing poles of a single dimension. It’s more accurate to consider each as having varying levels of activity or strength. Both strategies can be active simultaneously, with neither distinctly dominating.

The conducted research illustrates that motivational states—promotional and preventive attitudes—affect the relationship between life orientation and career adaptability differently. Individuals with a preventive attitude14 prioritize safety, monitoring their environment for potential threats that might hinder their goal attainment. However, prior studies in Poland found no association between either the transitive or moratorium orientation and the need for security38. Consequently, it’s unsurprising that this relationship wasn’t influenced by the preventive regulatory focus in our study group of risk-averse young individuals. On the other hand, a promotional attitude aims at fulfilling the development need, emphasizing control and seeking objects aligned with desired goals. It’s profit-oriented, focusing on positive event occurrences. This aligns with our finding that mediation effects were observed solely with promotional regulation. Research exploring the correlation between career adaptability and self-regulation, employing the Higgins concept, should investigate differences among individuals with varied profiles of self-regulation strategies: those with both high intensities, both low intensities, and combinations of high and low intensities. Moreover, it should encompass diverse educational and professional statuses among young people.

The results of the current study offer a number of implications for practice with the young adults population. Career counselors and development professionals should incorporate assessments of life orientation (transitive vs. moratorium) and regulatory focus (promotion vs. prevention) into their practice. Understanding these dimensions can help tailor interventions that enhance career adaptability among young adults. Programs should be designed to foster both promotion and prevention regulatory focuses. For instance, activities that promote goal-setting and future planning (promotion focus) should be balanced with those that emphasize risk management and careful decision-making (prevention focus). Young adults should be encouraged to self-assess their life orientation and regulatory focus. Understanding whether they have a transitive or moratorium orientation, and whether they are more promotion- or prevention-focused, can help them make more informed career decisions. Developing a balanced approach to career planning that includes setting aspirational goals while also preparing for potential setbacks can enhance overall career adaptability. Educational institutions can also integrate career adaptability skills into their curricula, focusing on enhancing both self-regulatory strategies and adaptability skills. This can include workshops, seminars, and courses that address goal-setting, time management, resilience, and coping strategies. Educators should create opportunities for students to engage in experiential learning that challenges both their promotional and preventive focuses. This can include internships, project-based learning, and problem-solving tasks that simulate real-world career challenges.

The presented study exhibits several limitations. Primarily, the sample selection lacks representativeness, being skewed towards women and individuals with longer work experience. However, although the sample isn’t probabilistic, it does encompass individuals from various disciplines. Furthermore, the study relies on self-report measures, susceptible to factors like limited self-awareness and the desire for social approval. Lastly, employing self-reported scales might have inflated correlations between variables due to shared variance. To counteract this tendency, the order of the questionnaires presented to respondents was randomly altered.

In conclusion, our findings contribute to comprehending the developmental aspects of career adaptation and the mediating role of self-regulation strategies. By identifying the typical dimensions of career adaptability among young adults, it becomes feasible to better elucidate and predict the behaviors of individuals with diverse life orientations. By implementing practical implications, young adults can contribute to the development of a more adaptable, resilient, and well-prepared workforce, capable of navigating the complexities of modern career landscapes.
