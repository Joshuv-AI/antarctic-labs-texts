Progress in Computer‐Assisted Navigation for Total Knee Arthroplasty in Treating Knee Osteoarthritis with Extra‐Articular Deformity
Authors: Chen Meng, Chuan Li, Yongqing Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTotal knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a well‐established treatment for end‐stage knee osteoarthritis. However, in patients with concomitant extra‐articular deformities, conventional TKA techniques may lead to unsatisfactory outcomes and higher complication rates. This review summarizes the application of navigated TKA for treating knee osteoarthritis with extra‐articular deformities. The principles and potential benefits of computer navigation systems, including improved component alignment, soft tissue balancing, and restoration of mechanical axis, are discussed. Research studies demonstrate that navigated TKA can effectively correct deformities, relieve pain, and improve postoperative joint function and quality of life compared with conventional methods. The advantages of navigated TKA in terms of surgical precision, lower complication rates, and superior functional recovery are highlighted. Despite challenges like the learning curve and costs, navigated TKA is an increasingly indispensable tool for achieving satisfactory outcomes in TKA for knee osteoarthritis patients with extra‐articular deformities.Keywords: Computer‐Assisted Navigation, Extra‐Articular Deformities, Functional Improvement, Knee Osteoarthritis, Surgical Accuracy, Total Knee Arthroplasty (TKA)

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis is a debilitating condition characterized by the progressive degeneration of articular cartilage and changes to the surrounding bone structure, often leading to chronic pain, stiffness, and decreased mobility. 1 When accompanied by extra‐articular deformities, which are abnormalities existing outside the joint space itself, the complexity of successfully performing a total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is significantly heightened. These deformities may arise from various etiologies, including malunited fractures, congenital disorders, or post‐traumatic sequelae, adding an intricate layer of complexity to an already challenging surgical intervention. 2

Conventional TKA techniques have been the mainstay treatment for knee osteoarthritis; however, in cases involving extra‐articular deformities, achieving satisfactory outcomes becomes increasingly complicated due to the need for precise prosthetic positioning and limb alignment. 3 Suboptimal surgical techniques can lead to malalignment of the knee joint, resulting in pain, reduced function, and eventual failure of the prosthetic joint. Traditional intramedullary or extramedullary alignment guides may prove inadequate in the presence of deformities or retained hardware, as these factors can interfere with the accurate placement of the prosthesis. 4

The advent of computer‐assisted navigation systems has ushered in a paradigm shift in the approach to TKA for patients with knee osteoarthritis complicated by extra‐articular deformities. These advanced navigation systems, such as the VectorVision system from BrainLAB and the Ci system from DePuy, allow for improved accuracy in implant placement, ensuring better alignment and reducing the chance of postoperative complications. 5 , 6 By facilitating intraoperative mapping of anatomical landmarks and real‐time tracking of surgical instruments, these systems significantly enhance the surgeon's ability to execute precise bone cuts and optimize alignment, overcoming the limitations associated with traditional jigs and external guides.

The integration of computer‐assisted navigation in TKA represents a significant advancement in the field of orthopedic surgery, offering renewed hope for patients with knee osteoarthritis associated with complex deformities. By empowering surgeons to deliver more predictable and effective treatments that were previously difficult to achieve, these navigation systems have the potential to revolutionize the standard of care for this challenging subset of patients. This paper seeks to examine the strides made in this arena, evaluating the impact of computer‐assisted navigation systems on clinical outcomes, surgical techniques, and the future of TKA for patients with knee osteoarthritis and extra‐articular deformities.

Literature Screening

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases. The search terms included combinations of “total knee arthroplasty,” “computer‐assisted navigation,” “extra‐articular deformity,” and “knee osteoarthritis.” The search was limited to English‐language articles published between January 2000 and April 2024.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Studies involving patients with knee osteoarthritis and extra‐articular deformities; (2) studies discussing computer‐assisted navigation TKA; (3) original research articles, systematic reviews, and meta‐analyses; (4) studies reporting clinical outcomes, radiological outcomes, or technological advancements.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Case reports and case series with fewer than five patients; (2) studies not specifically addressing extra‐articular deformities or computer‐assisted navigation in TKA; (3) studies focusing solely on intra‐articular deformities; (4) animal studies or in vitro experiments; (5) articles without full text available or not available in English.

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of identified articles. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then evaluated. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The review process focused on extracting key information related to the application of computer‐assisted navigation in TKA for patients with extra‐articular deformities, including surgical techniques, outcomes, and technological advancements. The initial search yielded 1253 articles. After removing duplicates, 987 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 156 full‐text articles were assessed for eligibility. Finally, 97 articles met the inclusion criteria and were included in this review, which were synthesized and organized into thematic sections, including the role of computer‐assisted navigation in TKA, outcomes and benefits, comparative studies, patient‐specific considerations, and advances in surgical techniques and technology. The Role of Computer‐Assisted Navigation in TKA

Traditional TKA surgeries rely on intramedullary or extramedullary alignment guides for positioning, which may not provide the necessary precision in cases with extra‐articular deformities or residual implants. The presence of deformities or implants can interfere with the accurate placement of these guides, leading to imprecise implant positioning and poor limb alignment. 7 In contrast, computer‐assisted navigation systems offer a significant advantage by utilizing advanced imaging and tracking technologies to provide real‐time guidance and feedback to the surgeon during the procedure, ensuring accurate implantation and mechanical alignment of the knee prosthesis, which is crucial for the long‐term success of the surgery. 8

The advantages of computer navigation are particularly prominent in cases where traditional intramedullary guide devices are not applicable or contraindicated. 9 For instance, extra‐articular deformities caused by malunited fractures or congenital diseases can hinder the use of intramedullary guide devices. 10 Similarly, the presence of residual implants can also obstruct the use of traditional alignment guides, leading to poor implant positioning and limb misalignment. 11 In such situations, computer navigation systems offer a valuable solution, allowing the surgical team to continuously transfer anatomical data to the computer during the procedure, ensuring accurate bone cuts regardless of any angular deformities, canal stenosis, or residual implants. Besides improving the precision of implant positioning, computer navigation systems also enable more accurate soft tissue balancing during TKA, which is essential for ensuring postoperative knee stability, range of motion, and normal patellar tracking. 12 Through the navigation system, surgeons can precisely assess and adjust the tension of surrounding soft tissues such as collateral ligaments and the retinacular structures to achieve optimal balance and alignment.

In summary, the integration of computer navigation systems enables TKA surgeries to address anatomical variations and pathologies, enhancing the precision, reproducibility, and soft tissue management capabilities of the surgery, thus ensuring favorable outcomes and setting a new standard of surgical care for patients with complex knee conditions. As technology continues to advance, these navigation systems will become increasingly sophisticated, further improving the accuracy and efficiency of knee replacement surgeries. In addition to the conventional large‐console navigation systems, handheld accelerometer‐based navigation systems have emerged as a portable surgical navigation solution for TKA. This technology does not require computed tomography (CT) guidance, whether image‐based or imageless navigation techniques, as commonly used in most CAS systems. 13 Instead, it relies on a sensor system connected to a cutting guide or directly to the bone, providing intraoperative cut matching and overall alignment without violating the intramedullary canal during each surgical step. 8 , 14 The first validation study of this system was conducted in 2011 using cadaveric specimens. 15 Compared with large‐console systems, handheld accelerometer navigation offers several advantages, including no initial setup costs, no “line‐of‐sight” issues associated with optical tracking, avoidance of pin‐site complications as it does not require transcortical bone fixators for femoral and tibial trackers, and reduced surgical time. 16 , 17

Researchers have reported successful applications of handheld accelerometer navigation systems in TKA cases involving extra‐articular deformities. 8 , 18 Pietsch et al. used an accelerometer‐based handheld navigation system for component‐semiconstrained TKA in 10 patients with extra‐articular deformities, achieving significant improvements in Knee Society Scores and range of motion without any intraoperative or postoperative complications. 19 Similarly, studies by Matassi et al. and Cozzi Lepri et al. demonstrated accurate restoration of neutral mechanical alignment and optimal implant positioning in TKA for extra‐articular deformity cases using accelerometer‐based handheld navigation systems. 18 , 20 These accelerometer‐based portable navigation devices have been proven to be as accurate as computer‐assisted surgery systems in producing anatomical limb alignment, offering a valuable alternative to conventional navigation systems while avoiding drawbacks such as prolonged surgical time, learning curve requirements, high costs, and the need for preoperative imaging. 21 , 22 , 23

Outcomes and Benefits of Navigation‐Assisted TKA

The integration of computer‐assisted navigation in TKA has yielded significant benefits that extend beyond the precision achieved during the surgical procedure itself. Notably, the employment of navigation systems has led to substantial improvements in postoperative clinical outcomes, underscoring their pivotal role in enhancing the overall surgical treatment of knee osteoarthritis. 24 , 25 Studies have found that compared with conventional TKA, navigation‐assisted TKA can significantly improve Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) knee scores, range of motion, and mechanical axis correction, thereby enhancing postoperative clinical outcomes. 26 Moreover, navigation‐assisted TKA can extend the longevity of the implant. 16 Overall, the application of this technology remarkably improves patients' postoperative quality of life.

These tangible benefits of navigation‐assisted TKA underscore the pivotal role of this technology in enhancing the surgical treatment of knee osteoarthritis. By delivering precise prosthetic positioning and optimal limb alignment, particularly in patients with extra‐articular deformities, navigation‐assisted TKA has proven to be an effective intervention. 27 The remarkable improvements in clinical scores, such as the HSS knee score and range of motion, coupled with the extension of prosthesis longevity, offer patients a greater likelihood of returning to a pain‐free, active lifestyle. 28 These advancements ultimately contribute to the better management and treatment of knee osteoarthritis, a condition that affects countless individuals worldwide. The integration of computer‐assisted navigation in TKA marks a significant progress in the field of orthopedic surgery, setting a new standard for surgical care and providing hope for patients with complex knee conditions.

Comparative Studies and Meta‐Analyses

In the continuous pursuit of refining surgical techniques and improving patient outcomes, comparative studies and meta‐analyses play a crucial role in assessing the efficacy of navigation‐assisted TKA relative to conventional TKA for the treatment of knee osteoarthritis. 29 These comprehensive analyses synthesize data from numerous studies, allowing researchers to draw more powerful conclusions and provide robust evidence to guide clinical practice. Meta‐analyses investigating navigation‐assisted TKA have focused on evaluating both clinical outcomes and radiological precision of prosthesis positioning. A key inquiry in this area is to determine whether the implementation of a navigation system can demonstrate substantial benefits in these domains. By collating data from multiple high‐quality studies, meta‐analyses offer a comprehensive view that individual studies might not be able to provide on their own. 30

One of the primary objectives of these meta‐analyses is to evaluate postoperative knee function, pain levels, and the precision of prosthesis alignment in navigation‐assisted TKA compared with conventional TKA. Multiple meta‐analyses that synthesized data from several high‐quality studies consistently demonstrated the advantages of navigation‐assisted TKA over conventional TKA in terms of implant positioning accuracy, mechanical axis correction, and patient clinical outcomes. These meta‐analyses also explored the long‐term economic benefits of navigation‐assisted surgery. 29 , 31 , 32 In addition to clinical and radiographic outcomes, meta‐analyses have also explored the potential economic implications of navigation‐assisted TKA. While the upfront costs of implementing navigation systems may be higher, some studies have suggested that the improved long‐term outcomes and reduced revision rates could potentially offset these initial expenses over time, leading to cost‐effectiveness in the long run.

Overall, comparative studies and meta‐analyses have played a pivotal role in establishing navigation‐assisted TKA as a superior surgical approach for the treatment of knee osteoarthritis. By synthesizing data from multiple high‐quality studies, these comprehensive analyses reinforce the capability of navigation‐assisted TKA to enhance the standard of care for patients afflicted with the debilitating effects of knee osteoarthritis. The evidence provided by these meta‐analyses not only supports the clinical benefits of navigation‐assisted TKA but also highlights the potential for improved patient outcomes, reduced complications, and potential cost‐effectiveness in the long term.

Patient‐Specific Considerations

In the realm of TKA, a one‐size‐fits‐all approach is often inadequate, particularly in cases involving severe extra‐articular deformities or complex anatomical variations. These scenarios necessitate a patient‐specific consideration and individualized treatment approach to achieve optimal outcomes. Computer‐assisted navigation systems have emerged as valuable tools in facilitating tailored surgical interventions for such complex cases. Addressing severe extra‐articular deformities in TKA may require a combination of techniques, including one‐stage TKA with corrective osteotomy. 33 This approach allows for the simultaneous correction of both the intra‐articular pathology of osteoarthritis and the extra‐articular deformities that can affect knee biomechanics and alignment. Severe extra‐articular deformities may arise from various causes, such as malunited fractures, congenital conditions, or post‐traumatic sequelae. 34 For patients with such complex deformities, standard TKA procedures alone might not suffice, as these deformities could compromise the mechanical axis of the leg, leading to suboptimal outcomes. By integrating corrective osteotomy with TKA, surgeons can address the extra‐articular component by realigning the bone while simultaneously performing joint replacement for the arthritic knee. 35 This comprehensive approach can significantly enhance the mechanical alignment and overall functionality of the knee, potentially eliminating the need for subsequent surgeries. The precision offered by computer‐assisted navigation systems is instrumental in ensuring accurate implant positioning and limb alignment, even in the presence of significant deformities.

Another patient‐specific consideration in navigated TKA is the management of patellar subluxation and alignment. Patellar subluxation, a condition where the kneecap shifts out of its normal anatomical groove during leg movement, can lead to pain, instability, and deterioration of knee function if left unaddressed, potentially necessitating revision surgery. 36 Computer‐assisted navigation systems provide significant advantages in addressing this complication by enabling precise alignment of the knee prosthesis components, including the patellar tracking mechanism. 37 The accurate measurement and correction of rotational deformities of the femur and tibia, often associated with patellar maltracking, are facilitated by navigation systems. 38 , 39 By ensuring that the femoral component is correctly placed relative to the patient's anatomy, and by guiding the performance of any necessary lateral releases or medial tightening procedures, navigation systems can significantly reduce the risk of postoperative patellar complications. 40 Furthermore, navigation‐assisted TKA allows for the identification and preservation of critical anatomical structures, such as the medial collateral ligament insertion site, which may be compromised in cases of severe deformities. 41 This attention to detail ensures that the integrity of surrounding soft tissues is maintained, contributing to optimal biomechanics and functional outcomes.

In conclusion, patient‐specific considerations, such as addressing severe extra‐articular deformities with one‐stage TKA and corrective osteotomy, as well as the management of patellar subluxation and alignment, are key aspects where navigation‐assisted TKA provides surgeons with the enhanced capability to achieve tailored, precise, and optimal outcomes for patients with complex knee pathologies. 3 The integration of computer‐assisted navigation systems enables a personalized approach, accounting for individual anatomical variations and delivering customized surgical solutions for each patient's unique needs.

Advances in Surgical Techniques and Technology

Innovations in Simultaneous Bilateral TKA for the Treatment of Osteoarthritis

Among the recent advancements in surgical techniques and technology for TKA, the emergence of simultaneous bilateral TKA represents a significant milestone in the treatment of bilateral knee osteoarthritis. This innovative approach addresses both affected knees in a single surgical event, offering several potential benefits while also presenting unique challenges that require careful consideration and meticulous planning.

Simultaneous bilateral TKA represents a substantial advancement for patients suffering from debilitating bilateral knee osteoarthritis. By consolidating the treatment of both knees into a single surgical procedure, this approach reduces the overall time spent in the hospital and lowers the cumulative risk associated with undergoing two separate anesthetic events. 42 Patients can potentially experience a faster recovery trajectory, as they undergo a single rehabilitation period, rather than enduring the arduous process twice in succession. However, simultaneous bilateral TKA is not without its complexities, and its implementation requires a well‐coordinated multidisciplinary team approach. 43 Intraoperative considerations, such as fluid balance management, anesthesia administration, and postoperative care, must be carefully orchestrated to ensure patient safety and optimize outcomes. Extensive preoperative planning and risk stratification are essential to identify suitable candidates for this procedure. 44 While the simultaneous approach offers convenience and the advantage of a single rehabilitation period, it is generally reserved for patients who are medically fit and capable of withstanding the stress associated with a longer operative time. Preoperative optimization, including conditioning exercises and risk factor modification, plays a pivotal role in preparing patients for the demands of this extensive surgical intervention. 45

Intraoperatively, meticulous surgical planning and execution are paramount to minimize potential complications, such as excessive blood loss and cardiovascular strain. Strategies to mitigate blood loss, such as the use of tourniquets, tranexamic acid, and advanced hemostatic techniques, may be employed to ensure a safe and controlled surgical environment. 46 The adoption of computer‐assisted navigation systems in simultaneous bilateral TKA has further enhanced the precision and efficiency of this procedure. These advanced navigation technologies facilitate accurate implant positioning, limb alignment, and soft tissue balancing, reducing the risk of postoperative complications and ensuring optimal functional outcomes for both knees. 27 Postoperatively, a comprehensive rehabilitation program, tailored to the unique demands of simultaneous bilateral TKA, is crucial for maximizing functional recovery and minimizing the risk of adverse events. Close monitoring and multidisciplinary care, involving orthopedic surgeons, physical therapists, and other healthcare professionals, are essential for successful patient outcomes.

In summary, the innovation of simultaneous bilateral TKA represents a significant advancement in the surgical treatment of bilateral knee osteoarthritis. While offering convenience and potential benefits, its implementation requires careful patient selection, meticulous preoperative planning, and a well‐coordinated multidisciplinary team approach. The integration of advanced technologies, such as computer‐assisted navigation systems, further enhances the precision and safety of this procedure, paving the way for improved patient outcomes and quality of life. 47

Refinements in Navigation for Cruciate‐Retaining TKA in Patients with Advanced Valgus Arthritic Knees

The field of computer‐assisted navigation in TKA has witnessed significant refinements, particularly in the realm of cruciate‐retaining procedures for patients with advanced valgus arthritic knees. This subset of patients presents unique challenges due to the presence of deformities and lateral compartment disease, making the preservation of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) a delicate endeavor. 42 Cruciate‐retaining TKA aims to preserve the PCL, a crucial stabilizing structure that can provide enhanced proprioception and knee kinematics following surgery. 48 However, in cases of advanced valgus arthritic knees, maintaining the integrity and functionality of the PCL can be particularly challenging due to the complex deformities and soft tissue imbalances involved. 49 Navigation systems have played a pivotal role in refining the surgical approach for these intricate cases by providing real‐time data on limb alignment, ensuring accurate bone cuts for implant preparation, and facilitating precise adjustment of soft tissue balancing. 50 With the aid of these advanced technologies, surgeons can continuously monitor and adjust their approach, not only to preserve the PCL but also to ensure its effective functioning after the prosthesis is installed.

One of the critical aspects of cruciate‐retaining TKA in valgus knees is the management of soft tissue balance. In these deformities, the lateral structures may be contracted, while the medial structures are often elongated, creating a complex soft tissue environment. Computer‐assisted navigation systems guide surgeons in performing the appropriate releases and adjustments, such as lateral releases or medial tightening procedures, to achieve a balanced knee that can accommodate the preserved PCL. 51 Furthermore, navigation systems play a crucial role in determining the appropriate rotation of the femoral component, a factor that is paramount for optimal patellar tracking and the long‐term success of the arthroplasty. By providing real‐time feedback on the surgical field, these systems enable surgeons to make intraoperative adjustments, ensuring accurate component positioning and minimizing the risk of patellar maltracking and associated complications.

The integration of navigation technology in cruciate‐retaining TKA for advanced valgus arthritic knees has led to significant refinements in surgical techniques. Surgeons can now approach these complex cases with greater confidence, leveraging the precision and real‐time guidance offered by navigation systems. This, in turn, translates into improved outcomes for patients, including better functional recovery, enhanced stability, and a reduced risk of complications associated with soft tissue imbalances or patellar maltracking. 52 As the field of computer‐assisted navigation continues to evolve, further advancements in surgical techniques and technologies are expected, particularly in addressing the intricate challenges posed by complex deformities and anatomical variations. The ongoing refinements in navigation for cruciate‐retaining TKA in patients with advanced valgus arthritic knees exemplify the commitment to personalized, precision‐focused care in the field of orthopedic surgery.

Surgical Robotic Assistance for TKA

Robotic‐assisted surgery evolved from computer‐navigated surgery, with the key difference being that robotic systems can perform precise bone resections using a mechanical arm and can also assess ligament balancing based on the amount of bone resected and prosthetic positioning during surgery. 53 Unlike navigation systems, which are passive, robotic systems can be passive (fully surgeon‐controlled), semi‐active (collaborative between the surgeon and robotic arm), or active (fully automated bone cuts without surgeon involvement). 53 , 54 Both computer navigation and robotic technologies fall under the broader category of “computer‐assisted” surgical techniques, but robotic‐assisted TKA is significantly more expensive compared with computer navigation systems. 16 , 55

Several case reports have demonstrated the successful application of robotic‐assisted TKA in patients with extra‐articular deformities. A. Alturki et al. reported a case of robotic‐assisted TKA for a patient with extra‐articular deformity, where the knee range of motion at 3 weeks post‐op was 0°–110° flexion, with stable varus and valgus stress tests and good quadriceps strength. 56 At 6‐month follow‐up, the patient was pain‐free, satisfied, and had a final range of motion of 0°–130° flexion. 56 Cook et al. described a case of robotic‐assisted TKA for a patient with both femoral and tibial extra‐articular deformities, with 6‐month follow‐up x‐rays showing good mechanical axis restoration and alignment, a final range of motion of 0°–130° flexion, and stable varus and valgus stress tests. 57

Robotic assistance allows for preoperative visualization, surgical planning, and simulation, avoiding the impact of anatomical landmark variability on bone resections. 58 Intraoperative execution of the preoperative plan is facilitated by the robotic arm's precise bone cuts. 59 This approach provides an effective solution for TKA in patients with extra‐articular deformities, although long‐term outcomes require further observation (Figure 2).

3D Printed Patient‐Specific Instrumentation for Bone Cutting

Patient‐specific instrumentation (PSI) involves designing customized cutting guides based on preoperative CT or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images of the patient's knee joint anatomy. 60 A surgical plan is created for bone resections and implant positioning, and the patient‐specific cutting guides are then 3D printed. 61 During surgery, pin guides are used to fix the cutting guides to the distal femur and proximal tibia using pins, allowing the surgeon to perform precise bone cuts through the slotted guides. 62 Compared with computer navigation and robotic assistance, PSI has a shorter learning curve and can reduce surgical time and blood loss in extra‐articular deformity cases, as the anatomical landmarks are predetermined during preoperative planning. 53 A meta‐analysis by Song Gong et al., including 23 randomized controlled trials and 2058 knees, further indicated that PSI offers advantages over standard instrumentation in terms of femoral component axial alignment, operative time, and perioperative blood loss, with no significant differences in alignment of other components, outlier rates, or length of hospital stay. 63

Several clinical studies have demonstrated the successful application of PSI in complex TKA cases involving extra‐articular deformities or retained hardware. Xu Rao et al. conducted a study on 60 patients with knee osteoarthritis and extra‐articular deformities undergoing TKA, randomly assigned to a 3D‐printed PSI group or a conventional TKA group. 64 The PSI group had shorter operative times, less blood loss, better mechanical axis alignment, and higher HSS scores compared with the conventional group. Zhongguo Xiu et al. reported on 31 cases of one‐stage TKA using PSI for knee osteoarthritis with extra‐articular deformities, with a mean follow‐up of 37.35 months. 65 Significant improvements were observed in range of motion, knee scores, and pain relief, while the mean mechanical axis deviation was corrected from 16.17 ± 5.73° preoperatively to 2.93 ± 0.58° postoperatively. 65 These studies demonstrate that PSI, by enabling intra‐articular compensatory bone resections combined with soft tissue balancing techniques in a single‐stage TKA, can effectively restore mechanical axis alignment and achieve satisfactory outcomes in patients with extra‐articular deformities.

Patient‐Centric Outcomes

Evaluating the functional outcomes of TKA in patients with advanced osteoarthritis is crucial in assessing the efficacy of this surgical intervention and its impact on patients' quality of life. A comprehensive study has utilized the widely recognized Knee Society Scoring system to assess the postoperative results in a cohort of patients with advanced osteoarthritis knee treated with TKA. The Knee Society Scoring system is a standardized and widely accepted method for evaluating the outcomes of knee arthroplasty procedures. 66 This scoring system is subdivided into clinical and functional components, providing a robust measure of TKA success in terms of pain relief, stability, range of motion, and the ability to perform daily activities. 67 By comparing the Knee Society clinical scores preoperatively and postoperatively, the study aimed to offer insights into the significant improvements achieved through this surgical intervention.

Several studies have evaluated the postoperative efficacy of navigation‐assisted TKA using standardized tools such as the Knee Society Scoring system. The results indicate that compared with traditional surgery, this technology can significantly improve patients' pain levels, joint mobility, and ability to perform daily activities, thereby substantially enhancing their quality of life. Long‐term follow‐ups have also confirmed that the functional improvements and high satisfaction rates brought about by navigation‐assisted surgery can be sustained over an extended period. 68 , 69 , 70

In summary, the comprehensive study of functional outcomes in patients with advanced osteoarthritis knee treated with TKA, using the Knee Society Scoring system, provides valuable insights into the efficacy of this surgical intervention. 71 By quantifying improvements in pain, stability, range of motion, and functional capacity, the study contributes to the ongoing evaluation and refinement of TKA procedures, ultimately benefiting patients suffering from this debilitating condition.

Disparities and Utilization Rates

TKA is a widely accepted and effective treatment for advanced knee osteoarthritis, offering significant improvements in pain relief, functional capacity, and overall quality of life. In cases where both knees are affected by osteoarthritis, bilateral TKA, which involves simultaneous or staged replacement of both knees, may be recommended. 72 However, studies have revealed significant variations in the utilization rates of bilateral TKA across different populations, highlighting potential disparities in access to this treatment option. 73

Age and gender are two factors that have been shown to influence the likelihood of receiving bilateral TKA. Older patients, particularly those above the age of 75, are less likely to undergo bilateral TKA compared with their younger counterparts. 74 , 75 This may be due to concerns about increased surgical risk, longer recovery times, and the ability to tolerate the rehabilitation process associated with bilateral procedures. Additionally, some studies have suggested that women are more likely to receive bilateral TKA than men, possibly due to differences in pain perception, functional impairment, and willingness to undergo surgical interventions. 76 Research has found disparities in the utilization rates of bilateral TKA due to differences in age, gender, race, socioeconomic status, and geographic location. 77 Addressing these disparities and ensuring equitable access to high‐quality healthcare resources, including bilateral TKA, for all patients requires a multifaceted effort involving education, policy adjustments, and strengthening of healthcare delivery systems.

Socioeconomic status plays a significant role in determining access to healthcare services, including joint replacement surgeries. Individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds may face financial barriers, lack of health insurance coverage, and limited access to specialized orthopedic care, ultimately reducing their chances of receiving bilateral TKA. 78 Additionally, lower educational attainment and health literacy levels can contribute to disparities in the utilization of this treatment option. Geographic location and regional variations have also been observed in the utilization rates of bilateral TKA. 79 Patients residing in rural or underserved areas may have limited access to orthopedic surgeons and specialized medical facilities capable of performing these procedures. Furthermore, regional differences in healthcare delivery systems, reimbursement policies, and patient preferences can influence the likelihood of receiving bilateral TKA.

These disparities in the utilization of bilateral TKA highlight the need for concerted efforts to address the underlying factors contributing to these inequalities. Awareness campaigns aimed at educating healthcare providers, patients, and communities about the benefits and availability of bilateral TKA can help reduce knowledge gaps and biases. Additionally, policy changes and interventions aimed at improving access to healthcare services, reducing financial barriers, and enhancing healthcare delivery systems in underserved areas are crucial steps toward ensuring that all patients with osteoarthritis have equal access to high‐quality care, including the option of bilateral TKA when clinically indicated. 80

Furthermore, research efforts should continue to investigate the root causes of these disparities and develop targeted strategies to address them. 81 Collaborations between healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organizations can facilitate the implementation of culturally sensitive and tailored interventions to improve the utilization rates of bilateral TKA among underserved populations. By addressing these disparities and ensuring equitable access to bilateral TKA, healthcare systems can contribute to improving the overall quality of life and functional outcomes for patients with osteoarthritis, regardless of their demographic or socioeconomic background. 82

Future Directions

Potential for Further Advancements in Computer‐Assisted Navigation for TKA

The integration of computer‐assisted navigation systems in TKA has revolutionized the field of orthopedic surgery, providing surgeons with unprecedented precision and accuracy in implant positioning and limb alignment. However, this technology continues to evolve, and there is significant potential for further advancements that could further enhance surgical outcomes and patient experiences. One area of potential advancement lies in the continued refinement of navigation system software and hardware. As computing power and imaging technologies advance, navigation systems may become even more precise, enabling surgeons to achieve near‐perfect implant positioning and alignment. Additionally, the incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning algorithms could revolutionize surgical planning and execution. 83 These advanced systems could analyze patient‐specific anatomical data, predict potential complications, and provide real‐time feedback to surgeons during the procedure, allowing for more personalized and optimized surgical plans.

The integration of computer‐assisted navigation with robotic‐assisted surgery is another exciting frontier. Robotic systems, guided by advanced navigation software, could potentially perform certain aspects of the surgery with unparalleled accuracy and consistency, reducing human error and improving surgical outcomes. 47 This technology could be particularly valuable in cases of severe extra‐articular deformities or complex revisions, where precision is paramount. Furthermore, the development of augmented reality (AR) technologies holds promise for enhancing the surgeon's visualization during TKA procedures. 84 AR systems could overlay patient‐specific anatomical data, surgical plans, and real‐time navigation information onto the surgeon's field of view, providing a seamless and intuitive surgical experience. 85 This could streamline the surgical workflow, reduce operative times, and potentially improve patient safety by minimizing the risk of complications.

In addition to technological advancements, there is potential for further refinements in surgical techniques and protocols when using computer‐assisted navigation. As more surgeons adopt this technology and share their experiences, best practices and standardized protocols may emerge, leading to more consistent and reproducible outcomes across different healthcare settings. Another area of potential development is the integration of patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) and wearable technology into computer‐assisted navigation systems. 86 By incorporating PROs and data from wearable devices, navigation systems could provide more comprehensive and personalized assessments of a patient's functional status, enabling surgeons to tailor surgical plans and rehabilitation protocols accordingly. Moreover, the continued development of less invasive surgical techniques, such as minimally invasive TKA or outpatient procedures, could be facilitated by advancements in computer‐assisted navigation. 87 These techniques, combined with precise implant positioning and alignment, could lead to shorter recovery times, reduced post‐operative pain, and improved patient satisfaction.

As computer‐assisted navigation technology continues to evolve, it is essential to conduct rigorous clinical studies and long‐term follow‐ups to evaluate the efficacy and safety of these advancements. Collaborative efforts among surgeons, engineers, and researchers will be crucial in driving innovation and translating these advancements into improved patient outcomes and enhanced quality of life for individuals undergoing TKA.

Novel Approaches and Systems

As the field of TKA continues to evolve, researchers and clinicians are actively exploring novel approaches and systems to improve surgical outcomes, particularly in cases of osteoarthritis with extra‐articular deformity. These complex cases pose unique challenges and often require innovative solutions to achieve optimal results. Several promising avenues are currently under investigation, each offering the potential to enhance patient care and long‐term outcomes. One area of active research is the development of patient‐specific implants and instrumentation. 88 Traditional off‐the‐shelf implants may not always accommodate the unique anatomical variations present in cases with extra‐articular deformity. To address this, researchers are exploring the use of 3D printing technology to create customized prostheses that are tailored to each patient's specific anatomy. 89 These custom implants could provide a better fit, improved biomechanics, and more natural knee function post‐surgery, leading to improved patient satisfaction and reduced risk of complications. Another promising approach involves the use of advanced imaging and computational modeling techniques to more accurately plan and simulate surgical procedures. 90 By integrating patient‐specific data from CT scans, MRI, and other imaging modalities, surgeons can create detailed 3D models of the patient's anatomy, including any deformities or retained hardware. 91 These models can then be used to virtually plan the surgery, test different implant positions and alignments, and anticipate potential challenges or complications before entering the operating room. This preoperative planning can help optimize surgical outcomes and reduce intraoperative decision‐making, potentially leading to shorter operative times and improved accuracy.

In addition to implant design and surgical planning, researchers are also investigating novel surgical techniques and approaches specifically tailored for cases with extra‐articular deformity. 92 These may include minimally invasive techniques that minimize soft tissue disruption, staged or combined procedures involving corrective osteotomies, or the use of specialized instrumentation designed to navigate complex anatomical variations. 93 By developing techniques that address the unique challenges posed by extra‐articular deformities, surgeons may be better equipped to achieve optimal implant positioning, restore proper limb alignment, and improve long‐term functional outcomes. Furthermore, the field of regenerative medicine holds promise for the treatment of osteoarthritis, potentially offering alternatives or adjuncts to TKA in certain cases. 94 Researchers are exploring the use of stem cell therapies, growth factors, and biomaterials to support cartilage regeneration and joint preservation. 95 While these approaches are still in the early stages of development, they may eventually provide nonsurgical or less invasive treatment options for patients with early or moderate stages of osteoarthritis, delaying or potentially avoiding the need for TKA in some cases.

As these novel approaches and systems continue to be developed and refined, it is essential to conduct rigorous clinical trials and long‐term follow‐up studies to evaluate their safety, efficacy, and potential benefits for patients with osteoarthritis and extra‐articular deformity. Collaborative efforts among orthopedic surgeons, biomedical engineers, material scientists, and researchers from various disciplines will be crucial in driving these innovations forward and translating them into clinical practice. By embracing these novel approaches and systems, the field of TKA has the potential to offer more personalized and effective treatment options, improve surgical outcomes, and enhance the overall quality of life for patients with osteoarthritis and complex deformities. 96

Conclusion

Computer navigation systems have revolutionized TKA for knee osteoarthritis with extra‐articular deformities by enabling precise assessments and overcoming anatomical complexities. Their integration has facilitated optimal implant positioning, reduced complications, minimized soft tissue trauma, and accelerated recovery. However, with the advent of AI in orthopedics, novel navigation aids like robotic systems, sensor‐based technologies, and 3D‐printed PSI are being introduced, aiming to provide safer, more precise, and personalized single‐stage TKA procedures for these complex cases. As emerging technologies, the reported clinical cases involving these navigation aids in TKA with extra‐articular deformities remain limited, and further long‐term, high‐quality follow‐up studies are needed to elucidate their impact on functional results and implant survivorship.

As the field of AI continues to advance in orthopedic applications, various novel navigation aids have been introduced for TKA. The clinical implementation of robotic systems, sensor‐based technologies, and 3D‐printed PSI has enabled safer, more precise, and personalized single‐stage TKA procedures for patients with knee osteoarthritis and concomitant extra‐articular deformities, leading to satisfactory clinical outcomes. However, as emerging technologies, the reported clinical cases involving these navigation aids in TKA with extra‐articular deformities remain limited, and further long‐term, high‐quality follow‐up studies are needed to elucidate their impact on functional results and implant survivorship.

Handheld accelerometer‐based navigation systems offer a portable and cost‐effective alternative to conventional large‐console navigation systems, eliminating the need for preoperative imaging and potentially reducing surgical time and pin‐site complications. Robotic‐assisted TKA leverages robotic arms for precise bone resections and ligament balancing assessments, enabling accurate execution of preoperative plans tailored to each patient's unique anatomy. 3D‐printed PSI facilitates intra‐articular compensatory bone resections and soft tissue balancing techniques in a single‐stage procedure, effectively restoring mechanical axis alignment while avoiding the need for intramedullary instrumentation.

As these novel navigation technologies continue to evolve and gain wider adoption, they hold significant promise in advancing the field of TKA, particularly for cases involving complex extra‐articular deformities. With the potential to enhance surgical precision, reduce complications, and improve functional outcomes, these innovations are poised to revolutionize the standard of care for patients with debilitating knee osteoarthritis and intricate anatomical variations. Ongoing research and collaborative efforts among surgeons, engineers, and researchers will be crucial in refining these technologies, optimizing surgical protocols, and ultimately delivering personalized, effective, and transformative solutions to improve the quality of life for patients undergoing TKA.
