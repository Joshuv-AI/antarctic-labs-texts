Cost comparison analysis of continuous versus intermittent antimicrobial therapy infusions in inpatient and outpatient care: real-world data from Finland
Authors: Tuukka Hakkarainen, Mari Lahelma, Terhi Rahkonen, Ville Lehtinen, Julian Shepelev, Trine Gram, Eija Heikkila
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study compared the costs and nursing time associated with the delivery of continuous infusion of antibiotics via elastomeric infusion pumps (EIP) versus conventional intermittent infusion (CII) across different care pathways.DesignRetrospective real-world data informed a cost comparison analysis that compared costs and nursing hours between infusion of antibiotics via EIP versus CII across eight care pathways in inpatient or outpatient care during infection episodes. Real-world data were obtained from patients treated within a year with parenteral antimicrobial therapy in Päijät-Häme Region, Finland.SettingInpatient care with hospital admission and outpatient care at hospital at home in Päijät-Häme Region in Finland.Participants3778 patients with a total of 4214 infection episodes treated with intravenous antimicrobial therapy.InterventionsEight treatment strategies with various combinations of EIP and CII administered in inpatient or outpatient care.Primary and secondary outcome measuresDirect costs and nursing time.ResultsSkin and soft tissue infections accounted for the highest number of episodes treated with EIP overall (30.8%; 74 out of 240 episodes) and in outpatient care specifically (53.3%; 128 out of 240 episodes). Compared with inpatient care costs with CII (€4590 per episode), treating skin and soft tissue infections in outpatient care with EIP or CII incurred only 24% (€1104) and 35% (€1620) of the costs, respectively. Across all treatment strategies and infections studied, the use of EIP consistently required less nursing time. The highest nursing time in the outpatient care was observed in sepsis episodes treated with CII (37 hours with CII vs 7 hours with EIP per episode).ConclusionDelivery of antimicrobial therapy using continuous infusions with EIP instead of CII can significantly decrease the nursing time and cost in both inpatient and outpatient care. For skin and soft tissue infections and sepsis, the utilisation of EIP is a cost-saving option in outpatient care compared with the use of CII.Keywords: INFECTIOUS DISEASES, Anti-Bacterial Agents, HEALTH ECONOMICS, Hospital to Home Transition, Nursing Care

Introduction

Outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) allows clinically stable patients to receive intravenous (IV) antibiotics outside the hospital environment. OPAT prevents unnecessary (re-)admissions and in-hospital stay while ensuring the same therapeutic effect of antibiotics. OPAT is suitable to treat various infections, such as cellulitis, bone and joint infections and infective endocarditis.1 It offers substantial benefits to both the healthcare systems and patients. Patients can enhance their quality of life by maintaining their daily routines at home,2 3 while reducing the risk of hospital-acquired infections. Moreover, OPAT contributes to cost savings by decreasing the number of in hospital bed days.4

Elastomeric infusion pumps (EIP) have advanced OPAT care by enabling continuous infusion of antibiotics, requiring only once-a-day pump changes, in contrast to the conventional intermittent infusion (CII) methods that require several injections throughout the day. Consequently, the use of EIPs leads to a decreased frequency of daily nurse visits compared with CII. Furthermore, the EIPs play an important role in enhancing antibiotic stewardship by optimising the administration of time-dependent antibiotics and facilitating the use of narrow-spectrum antibiotics.5 6 Given that EIPs require fewer nursing resources compared with CIIs in OPAT care, it is reasonable to assume that they would also reduce nursing time in inpatient care, where the shortage of nurse resources is also a significant challenge. However, this has not yet been studied.

Many studies have shown that OPAT can provide substantial cost savings compared with inpatient treatment. For instance, Dimitrova et al7 demonstrated cost reductions of 22%–56% across various OPAT models in the UK, while Durojaiye et al4 found that their OPAT costs were only 39% of equivalent inpatient costs. Additionally, Loesch et al8 reported 50% cost savings and reduced inpatient stay duration at a trauma hospital in Brasilia. There are only a few studies comparing the costs of using EIPs and CII. Dimitrova et al7 found that in the UK, EIPs were slightly more expensive than CII in OPAT care due to higher medication costs. Conversely, Ferreiro et al showed that EIPs had lower costs compared with CII in inpatient care in Spain.9 However, there is a lack of studies comparing EIPs and CII in both inpatient and outpatient care.

It is well established that OPAT care has significant cost benefits compared with antimicrobial therapy administered in inpatient care. However, there is limited understanding of how the use of EIPs compared with CII in OPAT care affects costs in different healthcare systems. The shortage of nursing resources is a growing challenge worldwide.10 According to the largest Finnish pension provider, there was a shortage of 16 000 nurses in 2023.11 OPAT care, particularly with the incorporation of EIPs, has shown evidence of reducing nursing time. Given that patients often begin antimicrobial therapy in inpatient care before transitioning to outpatient care, it is crucial to understand how the choice between EIPs and CII affects nursing time, operational efficiency and costs across the entire patient pathway. However, there have been no studies on this research question.

The aim of this this study was to compare the nursing time and all costs associated with the utilisation of EIPs versus CII for administering parenteral antimicrobial therapy across various care pathways within both inpatient and outpatient care in the Finnish healthcare system.

Methods

Study design

A cost comparison analysis was conducted to compare the cost in eight distinct parenteral antimicrobial treatment strategies and used the real-world data (RWD) from Päijät-Häme region in Finland. Our study adhered to the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS)12 used as the best practice for reporting a health economic evaluation, ensuring transparency and completeness of data analysis and results.

Päijät-Häme region data

The cost comparison analysis used retrospective electronic health record-based data between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2022 from the Päijät-Häme Well-being Services County in Southern Finland. Wellbeing Services County of Päijät-Häme is a public authority that provides social and healthcare for a population of 210 000 people (2017) accounting for approximately 4% of the total population of Finland. Päijät-Häme region is mostly sparsely populated, and Päijät-Häme Central Hospital is Finland’s second largest central hospital and the seventh largest provider of specialist care. The use of continuous infusion with EIPs started in Päijät-Häme in 2019.

In Päijät-Häme, the OPAT care (outpatient care) is delivered through the hospital at home unit, which is a mobile hospital and part of the Emergency Care Department at Päijät-Häme Central Hospital. The services are provided around the clock at the patient’s home as an alternative to inpatient care (at a hospital). The unit has 34 specially trained nurses who make daily visits to patients’ homes to provide all care, and the specialist doctor consultation services are available at all times. The units are equipped with the bed-side laboratory tests, EKG and defibrillator, oximetry, wound care sets, multiple oral and IV medications (including EIPs), and there is a possibility to start medications immediately after doctor consultations by phone or video calls. Occasionally, patients are accepted to the hospital at home (outpatient care) directly from a home or sheltered housing, when, following consultation with the doctor, the nurse makes a comprehensive evaluation of the patient’s situation. However, most patients are admitted to inpatient care (hospital) or outpatient care (hospital at home) through the emergency care department and doctor’s referral. Patient-level reported data were collected, collated, linked, quality checked and pseudonymised before being provided to the research group.

Study population, variables and statistical analysis

The study population comprised of all patients suitable for IV antibiotic treatment either via CII or EIP during the study period (between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2022). The inclusion criteria consisted of (1) admission to inpatient or outpatient care (either at hospital or hospital at home), and (2) intravenous administration of the following antibiotics at least once during the whole treatment period: cloxacillin, benzylpenicillin, piperacillin/tazobactam, cefuroxime or vancomycin.

Variables collected included patient demographics, dates of admission and discharge, place of admission, inpatient or outpatient (hospital or hospital at home), primary diagnoses of admission, antimicrobial regimens and route of antibiotic administration. The EIP or CII use was defined based on drug prescriptions by a physician and nurse notes. Treatment days on EIPs or CII were calculated as the number of days between the first prescription and the end of the admission. If the route of administration changed (EIP or CII) during the admission, the first method of administration was considered to end and the identified new route of administration to begin until the next change or the end of the episode, whichever occurred first.

Categorical variables were described by frequencies (%), and continuous variables by median (IQR). All data analyses for patient and infection episodes, as well as cost comparison analysis, were performed using R V.4.3.0.

Decision-tree model

A decision-tree model was developed to analyse nursing time and all associated healthcare costs across eight antimicrobial treatment strategies (S1–S8) utilising a cost comparison analysis framework.13 The distribution of treatment days across different care delivery models within a treatment strategy was based on RWD from the Päijät-Häme region. The included patient population consisted of patients with either (1) pneumonia, (2) erysipelas, cellulitis, or abscess, (3) unspecified bacterial infection or (4) sepsis. The infection groups were defined based on the most incident infection episodes with EIPs in Päijät-Häme region and clinician consultation. All patients entered the model at the onset of their infection episode and were followed until its conclusion. The duration of an infection episode was determined based on Päijät-Häme data. Neither separate health economic analysis plan nor study protocol was published.

Four care delivery models were identified based on the antimicrobial drug administration either with EIP or CII in inpatient and outpatient care: (a) CII administered in inpatient care, (b) EIP administered in inpatient care, (c) CII administered in outpatient care and (d) EIP administered in outpatient care. In all care delivery models, the antimicrobial medication was pharmacy prepared and administered by a nurse. In the outpatient care, the nurse would be required to travel to the patient’s home.

We identified eight treatment strategies that represented common antimicrobial treatment practices in Päijät-Häme region based on the interviews with local healthcare professionals (HCP). The strategies shown in figure 1 encompass a combination of the aforementioned care delivery models that can be used to treat an infection episode. S1 and S2 are exclusive inpatient care approaches, treating infections entirely within inpatient care using either CII or EIP. Conversely, S7 and S8 are confined to outpatient care, using either CII or EIP. S3 through S6 involve a hybrid approach, initiating treatment in inpatient care with either CII or EIP, followed by a transition to outpatient care at home hospital.

The strategies were compared based on their overall costs, inpatient care costs, nursing time costs, travel compensation and antimicrobial medication costs. Base case results of the cost comparison analysis are presented as estimated mean costs and nursing time per infection episode across all eight treatment strategies. A comparative analysis between EIP and CII was conducted within both inpatient and outpatient care. The cost comparison analysis was carried out in Microsoft Excel.

Healthcare resource use and costs

The data used and healthcare resources utilisation analysis were performed from a healthcare service provider’s perspective and within 1 year time frame, therefore discounting was not applicable. Costs considered in the analysis were reported in Euros (2021) and included fixed cost for inpatient care, nursing time, antimicrobial medications and related disposables, elastomeric infusion devices and the travel costs to patient’s home (table 1). Costs were estimated using a bottom-up approach, by first modelling the estimated healthcare resource use (HCRU) for each treatment strategy and then assigning each HCRU component a unit cost.

The nursing time needed for the preparation and administration of antimicrobial medication for each of the care delivery models and the frequency of daily CII administrations for each antimicrobial medication was based on Päijät-Häme HCP consultation. As the EIPs are supplied by the wholesaler, a portion of the pumps may expire on the hospital pharmacy shelf before use. The share of EIPs expiring before use was based on Päijät-Häme hospital pharmacy data. Cost for expired EIP products was incorporated in the mean EIP cost (table 1).

Sensitivity analysis

To analyse the robustness of the results, a one-way deterministic sensitivity analysis (DSA) was performed for all parameter groups by varying the parameter value ±30% from the baseline results values. The results of the DSA are depicted using tornado diagrams and tables.

Results

Patient and infection period characteristics

The characteristics of patients and infection episodes included into the study are described in table 2. Overall, 4214 infection episodes with a total of 3778 patients were identified between 1 January 2022 and 31 December 2022 (table 2, online supplemental figure 1, online supplemental table 1). The median age of the patients was 73.3 (IQR 59.2–82.3) years, and half were men (52.5%) (table 2). Antimicrobials most commonly administered via EIP were piperacillin/tazobactam (anatomical therapeutic chemical (ATC) code, J01CA12) for pneumonia (International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) J12–J18), benzylpenicillin (ATC code, J01CE01) for skin and soft tissue infections (ICD-10, A46, L02–L03) and unspecified bacterial infection (ICD-10, A49) and cloxacillin(ATC code, J01CF02) for sepsis (ICD-10, A40–A41). Among the infection groups, patients with skin and soft tissue infections had the highest proportion of patients who received antibiotics via EIP in outpatient care (table 2).

Cost comparison analysis results

For all infection groups, treatment in outpatient care was the organisational model with lowest total costs (figure 2, online supplemental table 2). Specifically, S8, which entails complete treatment in outpatient care with EIP, was the least expensive for erysipelas, cellulitis or abscess (€1104) and sepsis (€2644) constituting 24% and 27% of the cost of S1, respectively. For pneumonia and unspecified bacterial infection, S7—full treatment in outpatient care with CII—was most economical, costing €2059 and €1787, respectively, constituting 35% of the cost of the S1. In contrast, S2 —full treatment in inpatient care with EIP— was the most expensive across all infection’s groups (€4867−€10 622). The highest proportion of costs is attributed to inpatient care in strategies 1–6, nursing time costs in S7 and antimicrobial expenses in S8.

Regarding nursing time efficiency, strategies with the use of EIP outperformed strategies with the use of CII across all infection groups. S2, involving exclusive EIP use in inpatient care, required the least nursing time across all infections, ranging from 1.3 hours for erysipelas, cellulitis or abscess to 2.7 hours for sepsis. The second most time-efficient strategy, S6, which starts with EIP in inpatient care and transitions to EIP in outpatient care, required between 2.8 and 5.5 hours depending on the infection type. S7 required the most nursing time across all infection types, with the duration spanning from 17.6 hours for erysipelas, cellulitis or abscess to 36.9 hours for sepsis per infection episode.

A cost comparison between EIP and CII treatment periods within inpatient care showed that S1 (CII) incurred lower overall costs than S2 (EIP), particularly in pneumonia cases, with a notable difference of €1144 (−20%) per episode. For unspecified bacterial infection, sepsis and erysipelas/cellulitis or abscess, the cost differences were €992 (−20%), €906 (−9%) and €277 (−6%), respectively. In contrast, S2 was associated with significantly less nursing time per infection episode compared with S1, with differences ranging from 8.3 hours for erysipelas, cellulitis or abscess to 17.7 hours for sepsis.

In outpatient care, the cost differences between CII and EIP were comparable. S8 (EIP) was marginally more economical than S7 (CII) for erysipelas, cellulitis or abscess and sepsis with a difference in total costs of €516 (−47%) and €755 (−22%), respectively. Conversely, S7 was little less costly for pneumonia and unspecified bacterial infection by €134 (−7%) and €117 (−7%), respectively. However, S8 consistently required significantly less nursing time across all infection groups, with time savings between 14.0 hours for erysipelas, cellulitis or abscess and 29.8 hours for sepsis.

Discussion

In this cost comparison analysis, we aimed to investigate how the use of EIP versus CII affected costs and nursing time across different infection treatment strategies in both inpatient and outpatient care, including the transition between these care types. Our results showed that outpatient care at home had significantly lower total costs compared with outpatient care posthospital discharge or complete inpatient care. Particularly, using EIP in outpatient care emerged as the most cost-saving strategy for conditions such as erysipelas, cellulitis and abscesses or sepsis. On discharge from the inpatient to outpatient care, the most economical approach involved using CII during inpatient care and transitioning to EIP during outpatient care. Additionally, across all strategies and infection groups, the use of EIP consistently resulted in reduced nursing time compared with the CII. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to address the question of whether choosing EIP over CII across inpatient and outpatient care leads to cost and nursing time savings.

Similar to our study, many other studies47 17,20 have shown that OPAT care models are consistently less expensive than their respective inpatient care for patients treated with infections. A systematic review of six recent comparative cost analysis studies revealed a substantial cost reduction in OPAT care, ranging from 11% to 47% compared to inpatient care.21 This variance in cost savings might be attributed to differences in OPAT care delivery models, antibiotic administration methods (IV bolus, elastomeric device and oral antimicrobial treatment), treated infections and healthcare systems organisation.

OPAT care models have been reported to include self (or carer)-administered antibiotics, nurse home visits and outpatient clinics. Studies from the UK7 and the USA19 22 23 have described all three care delivery models. In Canada, OPAT care occurs in outpatient clinics or via nurse home visits.24 In France,5 similar to our study in the Päijät-Häme region of Finland, OPAT care involves nurses visiting patients at home to manage antibiotic boluses or elastomeric devices. Previous studies indicate that self (or carer)-administration yields the highest cost savings, while outpatient clinics demonstrate the least cost savings among these models.

Our study found that costs for OPAT with nurse home visits accounted for 24%–38% of inpatient care, varying based on infection and treatment method. The results showed that the soft and skin tissue infections including erysipelas, cellulitis and abscesses were the most treated infections in outpatient care and with the use of EIP at Päijät-Häme region. Compared with inpatient care costs with CII, treating these infections with EIP in OPAT consisted of 24% and with CII 35% of the costs. Despite EIP’s higher medication costs, its once-daily administration resulted in lower nursing time and overall expenses compared with CII, which requires multiple daily nurse visits. Similarly in sepsis, higher nursing time in CII makes EIP the cost-saving option. However, in pneumonia and unspecified bacterial infection, EIP becomes marginally more expensive due to higher medication costs, despite significantly lower nursing time. Our findings emphasise that the use of EIP in outpatient care with nurse visiting patient’s home only once per day can yield cost savings, particularly in treating skin and soft tissue infections. However, it is important to note that not all patients are eligible for OPAT care or the use of EIP. For example, the EIP is not used in Päijät-Häme region for patients with cognitive decline, memory loss, or those who require a walker.

Few studies have compared the cost of CII and EIP in inpatient or outpatient care. Dimitrova et al7 compared the cost of different OPAT care delivery models including outpatient clinic, self-administration at home and nurse home visits in the UK. The cost of using EIP and CII in the self-administration model showed that EIP was slightly more expensive due to higher medication costs. However, the costs of using EIP and CII with nurse home visits or in inpatient care were not compared. Ferreiro et al compared the use of EIP at outpatient care to CII use at inpatient care in Spain,9 but the use of EIP to CII was not compared in inpatient or outpatient setting.

Our results showed higher total costs associated with EIP use in inpatient care compared with CII due to increased medication expenses, despite lower nursing time across all studied infections. This suggests that the use of EIP in inpatient care could improve hospital ward and nursing operational efficiencies and allow more hands-on care for the patients, although with a slight rise in total costs. When comparing EIP and CII on hospital discharge, the least expensive approach involved using CII during inpatient care and transitioning to EIP in outpatient care. The strategy with the lowest nursing time involved starting EIP during inpatient care and continuing its use on discharge, which was also the second least expensive option. In this strategy, introducing and training patients with EIP use during their inpatient stay phase facilitates its adoption for home use on discharge, an important consideration when selecting such treatment strategy.

We did not investigate the clinical outcomes in our study, as the focus was on nursing time and HCRU costs. However, several studies have shown that OPAT care yields comparable recovery rates to inpatient care.1 4 25 26 Additionally, EIPs have demonstrated clinical efficacy.6 27 Specifically, the nurse home visit model of OPAT care, which we utilised, has been associated with better recovery rates.28 Based on these studies, one could assume that clinical outcomes would be comparable in our study, whether the patient is treated in inpatient or outpatient care or across them using either EIP or CII. However, further research is needed to investigate the clinical outcomes in detail, particularly concerning different infection types.

A strength of this study is the comparison of cost and nursing time between EIP and CII use in inpatient and outpatient care using RWD. The analysis included eight clinically relevant treatment strategies across these care types, providing detailed insights into not only economic aspects but also operational efficiencies, particularly related to nurse workload. The study has some limitations. First, the study was conducted in a single region in Finland, the Päijät-Häme region, which may limit generalisability of results. However, the OPAT care model, including nurse home visits, is also employed in other parts of Finland and other European countries. Second, the study did not assess the clinical outcomes or hospital readmissions following EIP or CII use in outpatient care. Finally, there was no probabilistic sensitivity analysis performed.

When generalising our results and applying them to other healthcare systems, the following must be considered. The cost and nursing time comparison was made with four distinct diagnosis groups, including pneumonia, soft and skin tissue infections (erysipelas, cellulitis and abscess), unspecified bacterial infection and sepsis. The skin and soft tissue infections constituted the largest infection group treated in outpatient care, consistent with findings from other healthcare systems.21 Other patient characteristics that should be considered include the median age (73 years) and the nearly equal female-to-male ratio. The patient population in the study had a poor overall condition, requiring parenteral antibiotic administration and nurse visits, thereby making them unsuitable for oral antibiotics. It is also important to consider that the collected data were reported in 2022, during a period that experienced COVID-19 restrictions for part of the year. These restrictions might have influenced the incidence of certain infections, including pneumonia.

In conclusion, our results suggest that the use of EIP in outpatient care can significantly reduce the cost and nursing time compared with the CII, especially when treating skin and soft tissue infections or sepsis. Within inpatient care, while the utilisation of EIP may result in a slightly higher expense, it demonstrates potential in alleviating nurse workload, facilitating earlier hospital discharge and enabling a transition to EIP within outpatient care. Further research is needed to evaluate the cost-effectiveness and applicability of EIPs for different infections and IV medication administration in outpatient and inpatient settings. This research should consider both healthcare system and user perspectives and involve larger patient populations.
