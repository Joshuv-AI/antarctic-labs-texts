Brain intraparenchymal hemorrhage following urgent cesarean section: a case report

Background

Acute intracranial hemorrhage (ICH) is a clinical emergency that results from the rupture of blood vessels and the extravascular accumulation of blood in different spaces within the skull [1, 2]. ICH is a type of stroke that accounts for 10–15% of all cases worldwide and is associated with high mortality and long-term disability rates [3]. ICH is caused by a plethora of pathologies, including trauma, hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, hemorrhagic conversion of ischemic infarction, cerebral aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, vasculitis, and venous thrombosis [4].

ICH encompasses four different types of hemorrhage: epidural hemorrhage, subdural hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and intraparenchymal hemorrhage [2]. A computed tomography (CT) head scan is the imaging of first choice for urgent investigation, revealing the location and extent of the hemorrhage, midline shift, and intraventricular blood spread [5]. Spontaneous ICH accounts for 85% of hemorrhagic strokes, the majority of which are hypertension related [6]. However, a significant fraction of ICH cases have an undetermined etiology [7]. Hemorrhagic stroke accounts for 58.6% of all strokes during pregnancy [8, 9].

ICH during pregnancy is a rare but potentially catastrophic occurrence with a significant risk of poor maternal and fetal outcomes, with an estimated incidence of 12.2 per 100,000 deliveries [10] and a maternal mortality rate of 1.4 deaths per 100,000 deliveries [11, 12]. Pregnancy prompts profound modifications that alter the cardiovascular system, which may predispose expectant women to ICH [9]. Risk factors include maternal age above 35 years, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (such as preeclampsia and eclampsia), and a pregnancy-induced hypercoagulability state [7]. The risk of pregnancy-related ICH seems to be greater during the third trimester, when it reaches the same level during labor and the postpartum period [13]. Symptoms of ICH include severe headache, blurry vision, nausea and vomiting, seizures, altered mental status, and focal neurological impairments [14]. In most cases, surgical intervention is required to remove blood clots and reduce intracranial pressure to avoid secondary brain injury [15, 16]. The prognosis for ICH survivors depends on several factors, and the main complications include recurrent hemorrhage, ischemic stroke, permanent neurological deficits, seizures, and death [17].

Among the different subtypes of ICH, spontaneous intraparenchymal hemorrhage, which may involve different areas of the cerebral hemisphere, represents a relatively common finding [11]. In particular, the basal ganglia, a group of nuclei located deep within the brain, represent the typical bleeding area accounting for 50% of all cases [18]. Basal ganglia hemorrhage (BGH) usually results from hypertensive crisis and is associated with high morbidity and mortality [19]. However, two cases of intraparenchymal hemorrhage after spinal puncture in nonpregnant patients [20, 21] have been reported in literature, whereas in recent decades, a rare case of frontoparietal intracerebral hematoma following spinal anesthesia for an elective cesarean section (CS) has been described [22]. More recently, Çubuk et al. reported a new case of an intracerebral hematoma that occurred 3 days after spinal anesthesia in a 22-year-old parturient [23]. However, the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage after spinal anesthesia is exceedingly low, and a definitive causal link has not been established. Most available evidence comes from single case reports or small case series, and confounding factors, such as unrecognized vascular malformations, transient hypertensive spikes, or individual variations in cerebrospinal fluid dynamics, often cannot be completely excluded [24–26].

Despite extensive research, significant uncertainties remain regarding the mechanisms underlying postpartum ICH, particularly when it occurs in the absence of hypertension, coagulopathy, or structural vascular abnormalities. The potential association between neuraxial anesthesia and intracranial bleeding remains controversial. While spinal anesthesia is considered safe and widely used in obstetrics, a causal link between dural puncture and intraparenchymal hemorrhage has not been definitively established. Proposed mechanisms, including cerebrospinal fluid leakage leading to intracranial hypotension, venous engorgement, and traction on cerebral vessels, are largely theoretical and supported only by isolated case reports. Moreover, it remains uncertain whether postpartum physiological changes, rapid hemodynamic fluctuations, or unrecognized microvascular vulnerability may synergistically increase the risk of hemorrhage after delivery. These unresolved issues highlight the need for additional case-based evidence to better characterize this rare complication and clarify its pathophysiological basis [27].

The present report describes a massive basal ganglia hemorrhage occurring in the early postpartum period in an otherwise healthy young woman after an uneventful cesarean section performed under spinal anesthesia. This case is clinically relevant because it occurred in a patient without traditional risk factors for ICH. Moreover, it adds to the limited number of reported intraparenchymal hemorrhages after obstetric spinal anesthesia and it underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by neurological symptoms in the immediate postpartum setting. Ultimately, it highlights the need for increased clinical vigilance even after normal pregnancy, delivery, and neuraxial anesthesia.

By presenting this case, we aim to expand the understanding of rare postpartum neurological emergencies, promote awareness of severe complications potentially associated with spinal anesthesia, and contribute to ongoing discussions about risk stratification and early recognition of ICH in the obstetric population.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old nulliparous white woman with a body mass index of 26 kg/m2 at 36 + 5 weeks of a bicorial and biamniotic twin pregnancy underwent an urgent cesarean section owing to premature rupture of membranes followed by the onset of uterine contractions. At preadmission anesthesia evaluation, the patient’s anamnesis and clinical examination were unremarkable. She had no allergies, did not smoke, and denied the use of illicit drugs. Family history was positive for hypertension in both parents, although no hereditary neurological or vascular diseases were reported. Routine blood test results, including coagulation status, were within the normal range. A baseline preoperative neurological examination was normal, with no focal deficits, normal level of consciousness, and no signs or symptoms suggestive of increased intracranial pressure. The patient was scheduled for an elective CS the following week for breech presentation. Following confirmation of the feasibility of spinal anesthesia, large-bore intravenous access was secured. Antibiotic prophylaxis (2 g of cefazolin) and antireflux therapy (10 mg of metoclopramide and 40 mg of pantoprazole) were promptly started, and a 500 ml bolus of crystalloid was administered. The patient was carefully monitored and placed in the right lateral decubitus position. Subarachnoid block was performed under sterile conditions on the first attempt, using a 9-cm 25-gauge Sprotte needle in the L2–L3 vertebral interspace. The cerebrospinal fluid was clear, and 2.2 ml of 0.5% (11 mg) hyperbaric bupivacaine mixed with 75 µg of morphine was slowly injected. A conventional CS was performed via a Pfannenstiel skin incision and a transverse lower uterine segment incision; the amniotic sacs were opened sequentially, and the neonates were delivered without complications. The patient’s vital signs remained stable throughout the entire procedure. After placenta extraction, 8 mg of dexamethasone, 4 mg of ondansetron, 1 international unit of oxytocin bolus, and 20 international units of oxytocin at a 250 ml/hour infusion rate were administered intravenously. The surgery ended uneventfully, with an estimated blood loss of 300 ml, and the patient left the operating theatre fully conscious, with a blood pressure of 110/70 mmHg, a heart rate of 75 beats per minute (bpm), and 97% peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2). Anticoagulant prophylaxis was not prescribed given the absence of risk factors for deep vein thrombosis.

However, 6 hours after surgery, the patient suddenly lost consciousness while speaking with her husband. The ward nurse promptly alerted the anesthesiologist, who assessed a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 10 (E4 V1 M5) a blood pressure of 135/80 mmHg, a heart rate of 70 bpm, and SpO2 of 98%. The emergency noncontrast CT of the brain revealed an extensive left-sided BGH measuring 6.4 cm × 3.7 cm × 4.7 cm, involving the homolateral frontotemporal white matter with surrounding hypodensity, a 5-mm midline shift, and compression of the left lateral ventricle. A neurosurgical consultation was obtained, and a contrast-enhanced brain CT scan was subsequently performed, confirming the indication for urgent surgical intervention (Fig. 1). The patient was endotracheally intubated and transferred to the operating room slightly more than 2 hours after the onset of loss of consciousness for surgical evacuation of the hematoma (Fig. 2). At the time of transfer, the patient’s blood pressure had risen to 150/90 mmHg and was unresponsive to antihypertensive treatment.

This rapid diagnostic and therapeutic pathway was facilitated by the occurrence of the event during daytime hours, with full availability of medical staff and hospital resources.

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the neurointensive care unit, where antihypertensive polytherapy was given. On the first postoperative day, sedation was gradually withdrawn. A detailed postoperative neurological examination revealed pronounced right-sided hemiplegia, facial nerve damage, and mixed transcortical aphasia characterized by impaired speech initiation with preserved repetition. No signs of brainstem dysfunction were observed. Pupillary reflexes remained normal. On day 5, following clinical stabilization, the patient was transferred to the neurosurgery ward. Cerebral angiography was inconclusive, and 13 days after the acute event, the patient was referred to the rehabilitation department because of significant and persistent neurological sequelae. At 1-month follow-up the patient manifested severe neurological impairment. Functional outcome was characterized by persistent right-sided hemiplegia requiring assistance and supportive devices, global aphasia with marked expressive impairment, dysphagia for both solids and liquids, and urinary and fecal incontinence. The patient was fully dependent in activities of daily living.

At 2-month follow-up, moderate neurological recovery was observed. Lower limb hemiplegia showed improvement, whereas upper limb hemiplegia persisted. Gradual functional gains of speech, swallowing function, and sphincter control were noted. At this stage, the patient achieved partial autonomy in activities of daily living.

Lastly, at 6-month follow-up, significant neurological improvement was observed with persistent mild deficits in the right limbs and residual dynamic aphasia.

Discussion

This case illustrates the devastating consequences of postpartum intraparenchymal hemorrhage in an otherwise healthy woman following an uncomplicated cesarean section under spinal anesthesia. While hemorrhagic stroke accounts for 10–15% of all strokes during pregnancy and the puerperium, it remains a rare and unpredictable complication [28]. Hypertension is considered a major etiologic factor in ICH and is driven by multiple mechanisms, including pain, stress, and postpartum preeclampsia. Elevated cerebrovascular resistance and the loss of cerebral autoregulation may influence risk [14, 29]. Cesarean delivery has been identified as an independent risk factor for postpartum stroke, regardless of the anesthesia strategy used, even following a normotensive pregnancy [30–32]. However, nearly a quarter of strokes have an undeterminable cause underscoring the complex and multifactorial pathophysiology of postpartum cerebrovascular events [11].

Genetic predisposition also contributes to ICH risk. Several genetic loci have been associated with the development of ICH, reflecting complex interactions between genetic and environmental factors. For example, genes encoding angiotensin-converting enzyme, apolipoprotein E, and collagen type IV alpha 1 have been implicated in the pathogenesis of ICH [33–35]. However, research on the contribution of genetic and epigenetic mechanisms to ICH risk is still ongoing, and the implementation of targeted therapies remains a distant goal. In the present case, genetic predisposition was not investigated, as no further testing beyond standard clinical management was performed.

The basal ganglia are one of the brain regions typically involved in hypertension-related ICH. Hence, a hypertensive peak surging on an unfavorable vascular bed was considered the underlying etiology, as no other causes could be identified. However, potential confounding factors should be taken into account when interpreting this case.

The potential link between neuraxial anesthesia and intracranial hemorrhage remains controversial. While spinal anesthesia is widely considered safe and is the standard of care for cesarean delivery, isolated cases of intracerebral hematoma following dural puncture have been reported [24, 36, 37]. Several pathophysiological mechanisms have been proposed, such as cerebrospinal fluid leakage leading to intracranial hypotension, compensatory venous dilation, downward brain displacement, or increased transmural pressure across fragile vessels, but these remain speculative. Importantly, causality cannot be inferred from this case, and the available evidence is insufficient to establish spinal anesthesia as an independent risk factor for parenchymal bleeding [26, 36, 38].

Despite the severity of the initial presentation, a progressive clinical improvement was observed over time, offering cautious optimism regarding the potential for further neurological recovery.

Conclusion

While uncommon, ICH remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in pregnant and puerperal populations. Prompt recognition and timely appropriate management are pivotal for limiting neuronal damage and promoting recovery [39]. Identifying risk factors, such as ethnicity, maternal age, smoking, obesity, cesarean delivery, and alterations of the coagulation system, is paramount for identifying patients at high risk for postpartum hypertension, ultimately reducing the threat of ICH [8, 40].

Importantly, the risk associated with hypertensive complications does not end with delivery. Close clinical monitoring during the postpartum period is vital for detecting early signs of deterioration and initiating appropriate interventions [41].

This case report highlights the potential symptoms, clinical presentation, and severe consequences of postpartum intraparenchymal hemorrhage, an uncommon but life-threatening condition.
