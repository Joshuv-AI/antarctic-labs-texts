A cross-varietal comparative analysis of mesocarp microstructure influencing physical and sensory attributes in date palm fruits
Authors: Mushtaque Ahmed Jatoi, Muhammad Tayyab Rashid, Najamuddin Solangi, Gholamreza Abdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The physical and sensory attributes of date palm fruits are crucial to consumer acceptance and their market value; however, relationship to microstructural morphometrics, which govern the texture stability and overall sensory perception of date fruits during postharvest handling and storage, is poorly understood. A combined methodology of morphometrics and statistical analysis was employed using two high-quality Pakistani date palm cultivars (cv), Aseel and Dhakki, to address this research gap. The mesocarp microstructure was quantified using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) at 20, 50, and 100 μm scales and examined with ImageJ to determine porosity, cell equivalent diameter, wall thickness, edge density and collapse proxies. Cv. Dhakki exhibited significantly higher fruit length (5.03 cm) and flesh weight (14.65 g) compared to cv. Aseel (3.58 cm and 8.47 g, respectively). SEM analyses highlighted cultivar-specific variation in microstructural attributes, with cv. Dhakki fruits present larger cells and thinner walls associated with higher porosity (~ 25–30%) as a basis for the softer and juicier texture. While cv. Aseel had lower porosity (~ 10–15%), yet was superior in aromatic intensity, where more compact cells with thicker walls and denser fiber bundles explained its firmness and stronger aroma retention. The microstructural morphometries were positively correlated with fruit size and texture/mouthfeel but negatively correlated with odor/aroma and taste. This investigation integrates SEM-derived microstructural morphometrics with physical and sensory profiling of date palm fruits and contributes to understanding the relationship between these traits, with practical implications for cultivar selection, processing, and consumer-oriented quality improvement in the date palm sector.

Introduction

The date palm (Phoenix dactylifera L.) is a cultivated speciesthat has continuously played a significant role in nutrition, economy, and culture in the arid and semi-arid countries of the Middle East, North Africa, and South Asia for over 5,000 years. Recent FAOSTAT statistics 1 indicate that the countries of the Middle East and North Africa are leading the date production, and Saudi Arabia, Egypt, and Algeria are the largest producers in the world. Pakistan is one of the ten largest producers, with an annual production of approximately 604,000 tons (t) on approximately 93,000 ha at an average yield of approximately 6.5 t ha− 1. Despite such a large production base, only about 124,600 tons of Pakistani dates reach the export market, generating about 50 million US dollars in export revenue. This threefold production volume, moderate yield and relatively low export value differences show a definite gap between production potential and market value, and a better cultivar choice, orchard management, and postharvest value addition would help improve the status of Pakistan in the international date trade. Pakistan has a rich date palm varietal structure, and among popular cultivars, Aseel (Sindh province) and Dhakki (Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province) stand prominent. Khairpur district (Sindh) has often been considered a national biodiversity hub, with a collection of more than 300 date palm cultivars2,3. Regional processes vary in different production areas; generally, the fruits of the cvs. Aseel and Dhakki are collected at the khalal or rutab stages to avoid damage due to the monsoon. They are further processed by boiling and drying into the “chuhhara (boiled dried dates or khalal matbookh” product or cured in the sun for further processing or storage as the tamar stage or fully dried stage4,5.

Dates are nutrient-rich fruits characterized by high levels of carbohydrates, dietary fiber, essential nutrients, and bioactive substances6,7, which make them a very attractive natural sweetener and functional food ingredient for the new generation of clean-label and minimally processed meals8. However, consumer acceptance depends not only on nutritional value but also on physical and sensory characteristics, which greatly influence their market value. Variations in size, weight, flesh-to-seed ratio, texture, juiciness, flavor, and aroma among cultivars directly reflect their suitability for various applications, such as fresh consumption, syrup production, and confectionery manufacturing9.

The perception of fruit quality is directly connected to the structural organization of mesocarp tissues, particularly their cellular structure. In several fleshy fruits, cell size, wall thickness, porosity, and intercellular adhesion have been shown to control texture, juiciness, and flavor release10. Large, thin-walled cells with extensive intercellular spaces enhance juiciness but reduce firmness, whereas smaller, densely packed cells with thicker walls provide greater mechanical resistance11. Cell adhesion is determined by the integrity of pectin in the middle lamella that regulates stiffness and tenderness. Additionally, orientation and the presence of vascular bundles or fibrous strands affect textural heterogeneity, which is associated with hardness and fibrousness. The process of ripening leads to textural modifications caused by the degradation of cell wall materials, turgor loss, and tissue collapse of parenchyma, which greatly influence physical and sensory attributes12,13.

Another aspect that needs careful consideration in fruit quality is their microstructural characteristics, which play an essential role in the diffusion of volatiles and soluble solids and influence flavor perception. Thicker, less porous tissues are more likely to retain volatiles, releasing a more constant stream of fragrance; conversely, porous tissues release a quick burst of flavor due to rapid diffusion 14,15. Based on well-reported associations in other climacteric fruits, cellular architecture plays a vital role in defining perceived quality, which underlines the need to know the microstructural basis of flavor perception in date palm fruits16,17.

Compared to other fruits, little is known about the microstructural factors that influence the texture and flavor characteristics of date palm fruits. Hence, the goal of this study was to investigate the correlations between physical, sensory, and microstructural properties in date palm fruits, using Aseel and Dhakki as representative cultivars. Specifically, the objectives were to determine morphometric parameters such as cell size, wall thickness and porosity. By integrating data from these different levels, this work attempts to establish, for the first time a mechanistic relationship between microstructural organization and physical-sensory perception, thereby improving the current understanding of structure-property relationships in date palm fruits. Overall, this study is of particular relevance to date palm breeders, postharvest technologists, and processing industries because it offers a mechanistic foundation for the processes of cultivar selection using a textural performance criterion, sensory quality and end-use appropriateness.

Materials and methods

Fruit samples and cultivar selection

Dried fruits of two date palm cultivars, Aseel and Dhakki, were collected at the tamar stage (full ripened and dried form) of maturity from the dates market at Sukkur and Khairpur (Sindh province, Pakistan) (Fig. 1). The samples were not collected at a controlled orchard, but through market channels, so all the data regarding the cultivation practices and environmental conditions could not be available in detail. Hence, fruits were screened for uniform maturity and appearance and placed under controlled laboratory conditions before analysis to minimize the postharvest variability. Although the fruit’s physical characteristics in the tamar stage are much reduced compared to their Khalal stage (fresh stage), the processing and marketing of dates are usually done at their tamar stage in Pakistan and on a global level. Damaged or diseased fruits were discarded. The samples were brought to Date Palm Research Institute (DPRI), Shah Abdul Latif University, Khairpur, Sindh, Pakistan, for physical and sensory data analysis18. Meanwhile, the Scanning Electron Microscope (SEM) analysis and ImageJ analysis were conducted at the College of Food Science and Engineering, Henan University of Technology, Zhengzhou, China.

Physical measurements

Physical attributes of individual fruits were measured using a digital Vernier caliper (± 0.01 cm) (Mitutoyo, model CD-15CP, Japan) and an analytical balance (± 0.001 g) (Shimadzu, model AY220, Japan). Fruit dimensions were recorded by measuring length (cm) and width (cm) at their maximum points. Whole fruit weight (g) was determined before seed removal. Following separation, the seed was weighed individually, and flesh weight (g) was calculated as the difference between total fruit weight and seed weight. Seed length (cm) and seed weight (g) were also measured separately to provide a complete assessment of fruit physical characteristics. Measurements on all the physical parameters were made across 30 independent fruits per cultivar (nBio_phys = 30), with the individual fruit considered as the experimental unit. Each fruit was measured once on each trait, and the obtained value was used to analyze the data statistically. Repeated readings were performed where variation had to be addressed, but statistical analysis was conducted using a single value per fruit. Biological replicates were used to find the means plus standard errors at the cultivar level (n = 30).

Sensory evaluation

A trained panel of 10 assessors (who are affiliated with date processing factories/research institutes) evaluated the sensory attributes of the two cultivars. An orientation was done to guide the assessors on the evaluation criteria and scoring process. For each cultivar, a total of 30 independent fruits (nBio_sens = 30) were used to prepare standardized edible mesocarp portions. The selection of fruits was random, and they were mixed in cultivars to guarantee representative samples. Freshly prepared pooled batch samples were evaluated during each panel session. Samples were identified by random three-digit numbers and displayed at random to reduce bias. Experiments were done under controlled conditions (room temperature, controlled lighting conditions appropriate to appearance testing and reduction of extraneous odors by separating the preparation testing space and ventilation). The preferences of the panelists were recorded on a 5-point hedonic scale where 1 = very poor, 2 = poor, 3 = fair, 4 = good, and 5 = excellent to measure color/appearance, odor/aroma, taste, texture/mouthfeel, aftertaste, and uniformity of size. The evaluation of each cultivar was conducted across three separate sessions, and the mean scores were analyzed statistically. The experimental procedure involving human participants was reviewed and approved by the Departmental Research Committee of Date Palm Research Institute, Shah Abdul Latif University, Khairpur, Pakistan. All participants were adults, and informed consent was obtained from each participant prior to the study.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

Sample preparation

Fruit flesh (mesocarp) samples were excised (~ 5 mm3 cubes) from the mid-region of each fruit to avoid skin and seed proximity. Samples were immediately fixed in glutaraldehyde (2.5% v/v, phosphate buffer, pH 7.2) for 24 h (h), rinsed in buffer, and dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30–100%). Critical-point drying was carried out in CO2, and dried samples were mounted on aluminum stubs with conductive adhesive. The samples were coated with gold in a Sputter Coater (BALZERS, model SCD050).

Imaging analysis and morphometric quantification

Samples were examined under a Scanning Electron Microscope (JEOL model JSM 5800LV) at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Images were acquired at magnifications corresponding to 20-, 50-, and 100-μm scale bars for both cultivars. For each cultivar, three independent fruits (nBio_SEM = 3) were used as biological replicates. For each fruit, five non-overlapping SEM fields were analyzed at each magnification (20, 50, and 100 μm), resulting in 15 images per fruit and a total of 45 images per cultivar. Images were sampled from the central mesocarp region to capture within-fruit variability. An open-source platform for analyzing biological images, ImageJ software (Fiji, NIH, USA), is used to extract morphometric data, such as porosity (%area), cell equivalent diameter (µm), cell wall thickness (µm), edge density and gradient variance, Laplacian variance, and surface roughness19. The image scale bar was used to calibrate each measurement to obtain values in µm. The embedded scale bar was used to calibrate each SEM image (scales to micrometers/pixel) before measurement. The Regions of interest (ROIs) were determined in the central mesocarp parenchyma and excluded the epidermis/skin, vascular bundles, cracks, and preparation artefacts. The ROI area was held constant across cultivars and magnifications (ROI area = [X] µm2). To measure pore size andporosity, the images were converted to 8-bit, and pore locations were identified by thresholding (Otsu / Yen / manual threshold rule) to produce a binary pore mask. Porosity (%) was determined as pore area fraction = pore mask area /ROI area) × 100. Binary mask was used to get pore area (µm²) (Analyze Particles with size filter = [X-Y] µm2; circularity = [a-b]) and pore equivalent diameter (µm) using area (ECD = √(4 A/π)]. Calibrated ROIs measured cell-equivalent diameter and wall thickness, yielding [n] cells per image.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses were performed using XLStat statistical software (ver. 2014). Differences between cultivars were tested using one-way ANOVA followed by Least Significant Difference (LSD) comparisons (p < 0.05). Since the panel mean scores were normally distributed and the assumption of homogeneity of variance was met, one-way ANOVA was conducted with the LSD test (p < 0.05) to analyze sensory data, which is widely used in sensory evaluation research.

Results and discussion

Physical attributes

Physical characteristics of two date palm cultivars, Dhakki and Aseel, exhibited considerable cultivar differences (Fig. 2A). Fruits of cv. Dhakki were significantly higher than cv. Aseel in terms of length, width, and flesh weight (p < 0.01), is often considered a large fruit cultivar of date palm in Pakistan. Though seeds of cv. Dhakki were considerably longer but not significantly different from cv. Aseel. The flesh-to-seed ratio was significantly higher in cv. Dhakki, signifying the visual quality of the fruit and more acceptable in the fresh fruit market and its suitability for fresh consumption due to a high edible fraction and potential associations for perceived mouthfeel due to the high thickness of the mesocarp. In contrast, the fruits of cv. Aseel were characterized by a smaller size but with higher density and a more compact structure, which enhances its mechanical damage resistance during handling, transport, and storage, thus making it preferable for processing into different byproducts, for instance, powders and confectionery, and long-distance trade where such features as texture and flavor profile are fundamental. Such attributes of appearance not only affect consumer choice but also postharvest management and marketing of the two cultivars.

These cultivar-dependent findings are widely reported among date palm cultivars across the Middle East and North Africa, as well as in Pakistan20–22 highlighting the significance of morphological characterization in adapting cultivar selection to local requirements, such as those of Sindh province processing and marketing strategies.

Sensory attributes

Organoleptic or sensory attributes have prime importance in fruit grading in processing industries, not only for dates but for other fruit crops as well. Significant sensory differences were present between the two date palm cultivars based on trained panel evaluation (Fig. 2B). Fruits of cv. Dhakki scored significantly higher for texture and mouthfeel (p < 0.05), as they were softer and more succulent. Fruits of cv. Aseel performed well in odor and aroma strength, which again justifies consumers’ perception of its rich, prolonged aromatic intensity. All other sensory aspects, such as appearance, taste, aftertaste, and size uniformity, did not differ significantly between the cultivars. However, Dhakki scored higher for appearance due to its larger fruit size. These diverse sensory profiles have clearly reflected the market niche differentiation of the cultivars, and fruits of Dhakki are selected for fresh consumption markets for their succulence, while fruits of Aseel find value in various processed and export markets for their firmness and rich aroma.

The obtained findings support the prior studies that determine the influence of the environmental and genetic factors on the organoleptic quality of date palm cultivars23, and shed light on the values of sensory assessment on cultivar grading and processing optimization in the local date industry24–26.

SEM micrographs were used to observe apparent cultivar-specific differences in mesocarp tissue organization of two date palm cultivars at varying magnifications (20, 50 and 100 μm) (Fig. 3A-F). Dhakki always presented higher values of entropy, edge density, gradient magnitude, and Laplacian variance than cv. Aseel. These results indicate that the tissue structure of cv. Dhakki is freer and less regular. At greater resolutions, the surface of cv. Dhakki showed smoother pore edges and stronger surface differences, whereas the microstructure of cv. Aseel looked smoother and denser. These discrepancies between scales indicate the dissimilarity of the textures: cv. Dhakki has a fibrous structure which is compatible with its lighter mouthfeel, and cv. Aseel has a denser mesocarp, which makes it harder in its touch and structure.

Notably, cv. Dhakki fruit mesocarp had larger but loosely arranged parenchyma cells, with obvious intercellular spaces, yielding higher porosity (%) across all scales (Fig. 3D-F). In line with this observation, quantitative image segmentation revealed more pore area fraction (porosity) and larger pore descriptors in cv. Dhakki has a denser architecture, with sa maller void fraction in cv. Aseel, matching its firmer texture (Tables 2 and 3) (Fig. 3A-C). In addition, edge density and gradient measures tended to be greater on cv. Dhakki between SEM scales (Table 1), which is in line with stronger cell boundaries/void interfaces and increased heterogeneity among mesocarp architecture. Conversely, cv. Aseel had reduced edge density and gradient magnitude, which are in line with a denser, more fused parenchymal matrix (Table 1). High magnification revealed partial cell collapse in cv. Dhakki, indicative of softer tissue that is susceptible to rupture during mastication, although cv. Aseel retained more intact cellular architecture and greater cell wall integrity (Table 2).

The image parameters derived from SEM were interpreted using established image analysis principles27. An increase in edge density and the degree of gradient is typically linked to a rise in structural complexity and tissue heterogeneity, as well as higher porosity and clearer cell boundary definition . On the same note, a more complex grayscale distribution is represented by higher entropy values and more intense transitions between intensities by the Laplacian variance. The metrics have been widely applied to characterize microstructural organization in plant tissues and provide a quantitative basis for the relationship between mesocarp architecture and functional quality characteristics28. The microstructural morphometrics align consistently with the cultivars’ measured physical and sensory attributes, confirming a strong structure–quality relationship (Table 3). The denser mesocarp structure of cv. Aseel restricts cellular expansion during fruit development, resulting in smaller fruit dimensions (length = 3.58 cm and weight = 9.38 g). Conversely, the porous and fibrous tissue structure of cv. Dhakki permits relatively increased cell swelling and water absorption, resulting in much longer (5.03 cm) and heavier (15.68 g) fruits. SEM micrographs of the cv. Aseel has shown tightly interwoven cell walls and a lack of intercellular spaces, a sign of a dense matrixthat restricts water retention and tissue elasticity. This microstructural compactness coincides with the firmness and reduced juiciness characteristics of cv. Aseel, but with the open mesocarp network helping to collect moisture and contributing to the reduced textural characteristics of the cv. Dhakki. Mesocarp structure of cv. Dhakki is partially collapsed, and this aspect, along with its porousness and thin-wall structure, is associated with its high scores in texture and juiciness, whereas the denseness and thick-wall structure of cv. Aseel is associated with high scores for firmness and aromatic intensity. The presence of increased intercellular spaces in the mesocarp of cv. Dhakki would presumably promote cellular deformation and release moisture during mastication, a factor that would lead to the perceived softness. The small microstructure in cv. Aseel, on the other hand, is expected to inhibit chemical volatility, resulting in slower aroma release and, therefore, prolonged odor perception. The connection between structure and function was also highlighted by quantitative morphometric analysis of calibrated images of SEM by quantifying porosity, cell diameter and cell wall thickness.

The structural-sensory associations are highly implicated in the cultivar differentiation and use. The mesocarp of cv. Dhakki is porous, compliant and makes it appropriate for fresh consumption and syrup-based products whenever soft, juicy textures are needed. In contrast, the compact, fibrous structure of cv. Aseel makes it suited to confectionery, powder formulations, and long-distance export, where firmness and scent retention are advantageous. Morphometrics derived through SEM, when coupled with sensory and physical data, provide a mechanistic rationale for understanding the relationship between fruit microstructure and perceived quality, and therefore inform best practices for optimal cultivar utilization and product development.

The correlation heatmap graph also offers quantitative support to the suggested structure-quality relationships, by demonstrating that SEM-based microstructural parameters are positively correlated with fruit size and texture/mouthfeel, but negatively correlated with odor/aroma and taste (Fig. 4). A combination of these trends implies the existence of a functional balance in the quality of fruits, with open mesocarp structure supporting softness and development of flesh, and compact tissue structure maintaining the intensity of aroma and flavor. Aftertaste did not exhibit an apparent relationship with mesocarp microstructure, likely because this characteristic did not differ significantly among the samples under investigation. Despite being exploratory, this analysis enhances the mechanistic explanation of the nature of how the mesocarp structure controls various aspects of fruit quality and offers the basis for subsequent multi-cultivar validation research.

The correlation between mesocarp microstructure and quality characteristics observed in this study is in line with previous SEM-based studies on date fruits and other fleshy crops. According to Haider et al.29, the variations in the organization of fibers and cell wall integrity between Aseel and Dhakki cultivars were also linked to the dynamics of textural firmness and moisture during ripening. On the same note, microstructural analysis of snake fruit tissues indicated that the difference in porosity and surface morphology plays an important role in determining the mechanical softness and sensory perception30. In the current investigation, the bigger and more loosely structured parenchyma cells and porosity in cv. Dhakki justifies its better juiciness and lower mouthfeel, whereas the tight cellular structure and thicker cell walls in cv. Aseel justifies the increased firmness and higher aroma retention. The similarity in structure-to-function relationships in the various fruit systems confirms that mesocarp architecture is a major determinant of the physical and sensory quality attributes.

On the whole, the obtained findings help to prove the essential role of the microstructural arrangement in defining the macroscopic texture and sensory attributes of dates31–34. Such quantifiable associations will allow us to further control the selection of cultivars, optimzepostharvest management processes, and establish the structure of all-purpose breeding and genetic improvement to enhance structure-associated quality traits in dates.

Conclusion

This study identifies a unique correlation between the physical, sensory, and microstructural properties of the Aseel and Dhakki date palm cultivars. The larger size, greater flesh weight, and higher ratio of flesh to seed were associated with higher texture and juiciness ratings, which could be explained by a mesocarp microstructure with larger parenchyma cells, increased porosity, and thinner cell walls. Conversely, the smaller size and hard texture of cv. Aseel was associated with a denser microstructure, including smaller cells, thicker walls, and a higher density of fiber bundles, which enhanced firmness and aroma preservation. SEM-derived mesocarp parameters (edge density, gradient magnitude, entropy, Laplacian variance) were positively associated with fruit length, fruit weight, flesh weight, and texture/mouthfeel (r = 0.59–0.72), and negative with odor/aroma and taste (r = − 0.59 to − 0.72), and aftertaste was not significantly related. The findings have a quantitative foundation of the role of mesocarp microstructure in regulating the major physical performance and sensory perception in date fruits.
