Comparative outcomes of motion-preserving techniques for lumbar spondylolysis in young athletes
Authors: Hui Wang, Peng Niu, Xiu Yang, Xin Lin, Guanyin Wu, Xiaotang Sun, Feiyue Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveSymptomatic lumbar spondylolysis often disables young athletes. We compared three motion-preserving techniques—modified Scott (MST), pedicle screw system (PSS), and dual-stability construct (DSC)—for pars healing and functional recovery.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 103 active adults (18–40 years) with L3/L4/L5 spondylolysis treated at a single centre between 2017 and 2023. Patients were allocated to modified Scott (MST, n = 33), pedicle-screw (PSS, n = 27) or dual-stability construct (DSC, n = 43). All underwent isthmic debridement, autologous grafting and identical rehabilitation. CT (computed tomography) evaluated pars healing; VAS (Visual Analogue Scale) and JOA-L (Japanese Orthopaedic Association) scores, complications and Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) Pfirrmann grading were recorded.ResultsAt 17.5 ± 6.5 months, DSC achieved the highest healing rate (95.3%) versus PSS (88.9%) and MST (60.6%) (P < 0.001). DSC and PSS were independent predictors of healing (Risk ratios (RR) 12.9 and 4.6 vs. MST). JOA improvement was greatest with DSC (80.53%) and PSS (79.89%) versus MST (67.74%). MST exhibited 12% implant failure (spinous cut-through). No adjacent-disc degeneration was observed in PSS or DSC, supporting short-term motion preservation. CT type II/III defects and overweight status predicted non-union.ConclusionPSS and DSC offer significant advantages over MST for treating symptomatic lumbar spondylolysis in young active adults, with DSC yielding the most favorable outcomes. The dual-stability construct, integrating intra- and intersegmental fixation, enhances pars healing and maintains disc health, supporting its use as a motion-preserving and biomechanically robust technique. These findings may guide surgical decision-making and contribute to improved recovery and functional restoration in athletic youth.

Background

Lumbar spondylolysis, characterized by a defect in the pars interarticularis, is a prevalent cause of lower back pain among physically active young adults, especially athletes [1].The L4 and L5 vertebral levels are most frequently involved due to their anatomical susceptibility to high mechanical stress during spinal extension and rotation [2, 3]. The repetitive microtrauma in these regions, combined with limited vascular supply and the potential presence of spondylolisthesis, often leads to nonunion of the pars defect and chronic instability [4, 5]. This condition not only impairs spinal biomechanics but also significantly affects physical performance and quality of life in affected individuals, representing a substantial clinical and socioeconomic burden.Therefore, optimising internal fixation to achieve local stability and effective fracture-end contact remains a key research focus. Before surgical repair is considered, conservative management—activity restriction, physiotherapy, and bracing—remains the first-line treatment. Masaki Tatsumura et al. [6]conducted a combined MRI and CT evaluation to assess the healing rate of conservative treatment for adolescent pars interarticularis defects. The overall success rate of conservative treatment was 76%, and the healing rate of unilateral isthmic fissure was 96%, much higher than that of bilateral fissure. However, healing rates were notably lower for advanced-stage lesions or those classified as stage II on the sagittal plane. In established pseudarthrosis the goal shifts to relieving low-back pain via core-strengthening and physiotherapy, but bony union cannot be restored. CT findings of progressive-stage isthmic defects, L5 involvement, bilateral lesions, or terminal-stage changes are independent risk factors for non-union [7, 8].

Recent advances in the management of symptomatic spondylolysis have focused on surgical repair strategies aimed at preserving segmental motion and promoting osseous healing of the pars defect. Traditional techniques such as Buck’s and Scott’s methods, and their subsequent modifications, have been widely employed in cases without disc degeneration or vertebral slippage [9, 10]. Intersegmental fixation methods, including pedicle screw constructs, have also emerged as alternatives, particularly for cases involving spondylolisthesis or failed previous repairs [11]. Compared with Buck method and Scott method, MST and PSS can provide higher fixed strength and have been widely used in clinical practice in recent years. More recently, the dual-stability construct (DSC), which combines Buck’s intrasegmental repair with temporary intersegmental fixation, has been proposed to further improve healing rates and reduce micro-motion at the defect zone [12, 13].

However, several controversies and gaps remain unresolved in this field. First, the ideal surgical technique for spondylolysis repair—particularly in patients with high physical demands but minimal disc degeneration—remains debated. While intrasegmental techniques are less invasive, their biomechanical limitations may hinder effective healing, especially in cases of spinous process dysplasia or associated slippage [14]. Conversely, intersegmental fixation offers superior initial stability but raises concerns about long-term impact on adjacent segment degeneration, as well as the justification for its invasiveness in young patients [15]. Moreover, few studies have directly compared multiple fixation strategies within a single cohort, and there is a paucity of standardized imaging-based evaluations of postoperative disc health and fusion outcomes [16].

To address these gaps, the present study aimed to retrospectively compare the clinical efficacy, radiological outcomes, and complication profiles of three distinct surgical techniques—MST, PSS, and DSC—in a cohort of active young adults with symptomatic lumbar spondylolysis. By assessing pain relief, functional recovery, pars healing, and adjacent disc integrity using standardized outcome measures, this study seeks to provide evidence-based insights for optimizing surgical decision-making in this complex and clinically relevant population.

Our hypothesis is that the dual-stability construct (DSC), which incorporates additional laminar screw fixation, will further enhance the pars healing rate. Moreover, timely removal of the intersegmental fixation (i.e., pedicle screws, titanium rods, and laminar screws) will restore the mobility of the intervertebral space without causing significant adverse effects on the fixed segment or adjacent intervertebral discs. The primary objectives of this study are to compare the clinical and radiographic outcomes of these three surgical techniques and to identify independent predictors of successful outcomes.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at a single tertiary spine center and adhered to the STROBE (STrengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cohort studies. Institutional Review Board (IRB:2025-058) approval was obtained prior to data collection, and all participants provided informed consent for surgical procedures and follow-up assessments. The study period spanned from January 2017 to December 2023.

Participants and eligibility criteria

We reviewed the medical records of physically active young adults who underwent surgical treatment for symptomatic lumbar spondylolysis. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) imaging-confirmed bilateral or unilateral lumbar spondylolysis with or without Grade I spondylolisthesis, (3) chronic low back pain (LBP) persisting for ≥ 6 months despite conservative management, with imaging-confirmed lumbar spondylolysis with or without Grade I spondylolisthesis, and mild disc degeneration (Pfirrmann grade I–III) to minimize the potential interference of discogenic low back pain due to disc degeneration, (4) absence of clinical neural compression symptoms, and (5) evidence of mild disc degeneration (Pfirrmann grade I–III) on MRI at the affected segment. All patients participated in regular sports or high-intensity physical activities.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) age >40 years, (2) isolated unilateral defects, (3) spondylolisthesis Grade ≥ II, (4) spinal canal stenosis or nerve root compression, (5) laminar or spinous process dysplasia precluding standard fixation, (6) severe disc degeneration (Pfirrmann grade IV/V), (7) incomplete radiological or clinical follow-up, and (8) comorbidities interfering with bone healing or adherence to postoperative protocols.

Surgical group allocation and bias considerations

A total of 103 eligible patients were included and categorized into three groups based on the surgical technique received: MST (n = 33), PSS (n = 27), and DSC (n = 43). Surgical assignment arose from the concurrent influence of institutional technical evolution and case-by-case evaluation. Initially, MST was the only motion-preserving option available, so every patient received this technique. Subsequent follow-up revealed that athletes with high functional demands, wide pars gaps, or hypoplastic laminae treated with MST experienced low union rates and high implant failure. Consequently, the team switched to PSS for all patients with mild spondylolysis or any of the aforementioned risk factors. Nevertheless, even after intraoperative placement of pedicle screws and titanium rods for intersegmental fixation, subsequent clamping of the lamina still revealed persistent micromotion at the pars defect. This residual motion may significantly contribute to reduced healing rates in the following period; DSC was therefore introduced and quickly became the default choice, with PSS reserved for only a minority of low-risk cases.

To minimize selection bias arising from non-random assignment driven by surgeon preference, patient factors, or defect characteristics, the final decision was made by the same surgical team after integrating the then-current institutional preference with defect morphology, presence of spondylolisthesis, and patient activity level, followed by thorough informed consent. Baseline demographic and clinical variables were rigorously compared across groups, confirming comparability with no significant differences (Table 1, p > 0.05).

Perioperative management

Preoperative Preparation

The preoperative assessments included standard lumbar anteroposterior and lateral, bilateral oblique, and dynamic flexion-extension radiographs, as well as three-dimensional computed tomography (CT) and MRI scans. Special focus was placed on detecting vertebral instability, developmental deficiencies of the pedicles and lamina, and the degree of intervertebral disc degeneration in the affected segment. To minimise the risk of infection, a 1-g dose of cefazolin sodium was administered intravenously 30 min before surgery. Each patient received general anaesthesia and was placed in the prone position. The abdomen was then elevated, and the thoracolumbar spine and buttocks were fully exposed. Iodophor was applied for disinfection, and a sterile drape was placed.

Surgical techniques

Modified Scott technique (MST)

Fluoroscopic guidance was used to identify the target pars region, and a posterior midline approach was implemented. Dissection was performed along the spinous processes and lamina to expose the facet joints and pars interarticularis of the adjacent vertebrae. Fibrous scar tissue in the pars region was then excised, and sclerotic bone surfaces were drilled and decorticated until a rough, bleeding surface was obtained. Through the same incision, subfascial dissection was extended laterally to expose the posterior superior iliac spine. A cortical window was created in the posterior superior iliac spine and autologous cancellous bone was harvested using curettes of various sizes. The harvested bone was compressed and grafted bilaterally into the prepared pars. Local infiltration of 0.1% ropivacaine (5 mL) was performed at the donor site, followed by haemostasis with gelatine sponge packing. The wounds were closed in layers without drainage. Two monoaxial pedicle screws were inserted in the affected vertebra. The screws were placed slightly lateral to reduce stimulation and disturbance of the facet joint. A hole was drilled through the spinous process, through which a 1.8-mm cable was threaded. The cable was then looped around the base of the pedicle screw heads bilaterally and tightened on one side to pull the spinous process closer to the pars region, thereby compressing the isthmus (Fig. 1). In the three cases involving both L4 and L5 pars defects, we used individual intrasegmental fixation. Dual-stability constructs (DSC)

The procedures for surgical exposure, isthmus cleaning, and bone extraction from the posterior superior iliac spine were the same as those used for MST (described above). The key difference in the DSC procedure lies in the method of fixation. Pedicle nailing screws were inserted into the affected vertebra and the inferior adjacent vertebra, at the same placement points as in MST. In cases of spondylolisthesis, monoaxial pedicle screws with extended reduction heads were inserted into the affected vertebra, with deeper penetration than the inferior screws to facilitate reduction. Pre-bent titanium rods were then secured to the screws, and spondylolisthesis was reduced by tightening the inner nuts, as confirmed using intraoperative fluoroscopy.

Laminar screws were inserted by creating an entry point at the inferior edge of the lamina of the affected vertebra using rongeurs and a 2.8-mm cortical drill while maintaining convergence and caudal angles. Screws (diameter: 3.5 mm; length: 30–34 mm) were then inserted across the pars into the pedicle region (Fig. 2). Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed the correct placement of the laminar screw, as there was a potential risk of it breaching the lamina and entering the spinal canal, analogous to the position of the metal probe shown in Fig. 3.The mobile lamina was stabilized with bilateral laminar screws (Fig. 4). After irrigation with normal saline and confirmation of haemostasis, bilateral drainage tubes were inserted and the wound was closed in layers.

Briefly, all patients underwent isthmic debridement and autologous iliac crest bone grafting. In the DSC group, bilateral laminar screws were used in addition to standard pedicle screw constructs to enhance local stability [13].All patients underwent exposure and debridement of the pars defect using curettes and laminar clamps to remove sclerotic areas and fibrous structures. The defect was then filled with autologous cancellous bone harvested from the posterior superior iliac spine. This procedure was performed in all three surgical techniques (MST, PSS, and DSC).

Postoperative management and outcome measures

The patients were encouraged to perform axial rolling in bed postoperatively. Drainage tubes were removed after 48 h, while the lumbar brace was worn for 2 months. All patients underwent a standardized follow-up protocol: initial visits at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months post-surgery, followed by biannual checkups until final evaluation. The timing of temporary instrumentation removal was determined based on radiographic evidence of pars interarticularis healing, with removal occurring between 6 and 12 months postoperatively.

Clinical outcomes were assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) for LBP and the Japanese Orthopaedic Association Lumbar Scoring Scale (JOA-L, 29-point) at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, and the final follow-up [17]. Although JOA-L was originally developed for lumbar spinal stenosis, previous studies have applied it to assess pre- and postoperative symptom changes in patients with lumbar spondylolysis [18, 19]; therefore, we retained this scale in accordance with existing literature.The JOA improvement rate was categorized as excellent (>75%), good (50–75%), fair (25–50%), or ineffective (< 25%) [20].

Radiographic evaluation included standard anteroposterior and lateral lumbar X-rays at each follow-up interval, supplemented by SCT scans at 6 and 12 months and the final visit to assess pars healing and spondylolisthesis reduction. Pars fusion was defined as the presence of continuous trabecular bridging across the defect on CT scans, visible on three or more consecutive sagittal and axial slices. Implant failure was recorded based on breakage, loosening, or migration.

MRI assessments were performed postoperatively after implant removal using the Pfirrmann classification to evaluate disc signal changes. The intervertebral discs evaluated on MRI were the adjacent levels to the operated segment (e.g., for L5 spondylolysis, the L4/5 disc was observed; for L4 spondylolysis, the L3/4 and L5/S1 discs were observed). All imaging studies were independently reviewed by two board-certified radiologists with > 5 years of spinal imaging experience; both reviewers were blinded to surgical technique, group allocation, and all clinical outcomes. Any disagreements were adjudicated by a third senior radiologist. Inter-observer agreement for fusion assessment was excellent (Cohen’s κ = 0.902 at 6 months and κ = 0.901 at final follow-up; P < 0.001 for both).

In this study, overall treatment success was defined as the simultaneous fulfilment of two criteria: (1) clinical success—postoperative improvement rated as “excellent” or “good” according to the Japanese Orthopaedic Association lumbar (JOA-L) scale; and (2) radiological success—thin-slice CT demonstrating bilateral solid bony union of the pars defect, evidenced by continuous trabecular bridging across the isthmic gap.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-hoc correction. Non-normally distributed variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages) and compared using Pearson’s chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

For longitudinal outcomes such as VAS and JOA scores at different time points, repeated-measures ANOVA was used to evaluate within- and between-group differences. A post-hoc power analysis was planned to assess the statistical power of the present sample size for detecting intergroup differences in healing rates, with an α level set at 0.05 and using Cohen’s f as the effect size measure.

To identify independent predictors of clinical efficacy, multivariate linear regression analyses were performed with VAS and JOA improvement rates as dependent variables, and surgical technique, age, and BMI (body mass index) as independent variables. Multivariate Poisson regression with robust error variance was used to assess associations between surgical technique and binary outcomes such as pars interarticularis healing at 6 months and final follow-up. Risk ratios (RRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteriscs

A total of 103 physically active young adults with symptomatic lumbar spondylolysis were included in the final analysis: 33 (32.0%) patients in the MST group, 27 (26.2%) in the PSS group, and 43 (41.7%) in the DSC group. Three (9.1%), five (18.5%), and 10 (23.3%) patients in the MST, PSS, and DSC groups, respectively, were diagnosed with grade I spondylolisthesis. The demographic and clinical baseline characteristics were comparable across the three groups (P > 0.05 for all), including age, sex distribution, BMI, affected vertebral level, CT-based defect classification(Fig. 6) [8], and presence of Grade I spondylolisthesis (Table 1). The mean follow-up duration was 17.5 ± 6.5 months. The shortest follow-up time was 8 months and the longest follow-up time was 35 months.

Although this is a retrospective study, a post-hoc power analysis was performed using the observed healing rates (MST = 60.6%, PSS = 88.9%, DSC = 95.3%) and the actual sample sizes (n = 33/27/43). The calculated Cohen’s f = 0.54 indicates a large effect size. Under α = 0.05, the achieved power is 0.97 (> 0.80), confirming that the current cohort of 103 patients is sufficient to detect the observed inter-group difference without a type II error risk.

Surgical parameters and complications

Compared to the other groups, the MST group exhibited significantly shorter operative time, lower intraoperative blood loss, and reduced postoperative drainage volume (P < 0.05, Table 1). No significant differences were observed between the PSS and DSC groups for these surgical parameters.

Regarding complications, wound discharge occurred in two patients in the MST group and one in the DSC group. Reoperation was required in these cases to evacuate fluid collections at the iliac crest or lumbar deep fascia. No patients experienced neurological injury. one patient experienced screw loosening in the pedicle during surgery due to an operative error, and reduction was not attempted in the DSC group. No reduction of spondylolisthesis was achieved in the MST group (0%), whereas full reduction was achieved in the PSS group (100%, 5/5) and in 90% (9/10) of the DSC group (Table 2).

Pars healing and implant integrity

At final follow-up, the bilateral pars healing rate was significantly higher in the PSS (88.9%) and DSC (95.3%) groups compared to the MST group (60.6%) (P < 0.001). No significant difference in healing rate was observed between PSS and DSC. Four patients (12.1%) in the MST group experienced implant failure due to cable cut-through at the spinous process(Fig. 7), while only one implant failure was observed in the DSC group (2.3%) due to a fractured pedicle screw (Table 2; Fig. 8). The overall implant failure rate showed a trend toward difference but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.057).

These patients remained in the observation cohort (the primary reason being that the patients’ pain symptoms had improved, and they were unwilling to undergo further surgery).

Intervertebral disc signal evaluation

Multivariate analysis

In the multivariate linear regression analysis, the VAS improvement rate and JOA improvement rate were used as dependent variables, and surgical modality (MST, PSS, DSC), age (continuous variable), and BMI (continuous variable) were uniformly included as independent variables to evaluate their independent effects on functional recovery. Surgical technique was independently associated with the improvement rate of JOA scores. Compared with the MST group (reference), the use of surgical technique was significantly associated with better JOA improvement (β = 0.074, 95% CI: 0.032–0.116, P = 0.001). However, no significant association was found between surgical technique and VAS improvement rate (β = − 0.004, 95% CI: − 0.051 to 0.043, P = 0.874). Age and BMI were not significantly correlated with the improvement rates of either VAS or JOA scores (Table 5).

Bony healing of the pars was assessed at 6 months and at final follow-up using multivariable Poisson regression models that included surgical technique, BMI category, and CT-based isthmic defect type (grades I/II/III) as covariates to adjust for potential confounders. At 6-month follow-up, both DSC and PSS were significantly associated with higher likelihoods of pars interarticularis healing compared to MST (RR = 23.74, 95% CI: 4.52–124.76, P < 0.001 for DSC; RR = 9.24, 95% CI: 1.89–45.12, P = 0.006 for PSS). BMI classification showed a trend toward lower healing probability in the overweight group (RR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.08–1.16, P = 0.082), but the difference was not statistically significant. At the final follow-up, DSC and PSS remained significantly associated with higher healing probability (RR = 12.91, 95% CI: 2.57–64.96, P = 0.002 for DSC; RR = 4.55, 95% CI: 1.09–18.97, P = 0.038 for PSS). Overweight status was associated with reduced healing probability (RR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.09–0.90, P = 0.032), while the obese group lacked sufficient data for analysis (Table 6).

Discussion

This retrospective comparative study aimed to evaluate and contrast the clinical efficacy, radiographic healing, and safety profiles of three surgical techniques—MST, PSS, and DSC—in treating symptomatic lumbar spondylolysis among physically active adolescents and young adults. Our primary objective was to determine which approach offered optimal outcomes in terms of pars interarticularis healing, functional recovery, pain relief, and preservation of adjacent segment integrity, especially in the context of growing demand for motion-preserving strategies.

All three techniques significantly improved both pain (VAS) and function (JOA) from baseline. However, both univariate and multivariate analyses confirmed that the DSC and PSS groups outperformed MST in terms of radiographic healing rates at 6 months and final follow-up, with DSC showing the highest fusion rate. Multivariate regression further demonstrated that DSC was independently associated with a significantly greater improvement in JOA scores (β = 0.074, P = 0.001), while no technique showed superiority in VAS improvement, indicating comparable pain relief across groups. Additionally, CT-based classification revealed that type II and III defects were associated with significantly lower healing rates, reinforcing the relevance of anatomical subtype in surgical planning.

A unique contribution of this study is the integration of DSC—a dual-stability construct combining intersegmental and intrasegmental fixation principles—which exhibited superior biomechanical stability and healing efficacy without increasing the surgical burden. Notably, MRI assessments after implant removal revealed no significant early degeneration of adjacent intervertebral discs in either the DSC or PSS groups, suggesting that temporary intersegmental fixation is a safe and effective motion-preserving strategy.

The high prevalence of L5 spondylolysis (72.8%) observed in this study is consistent with prior reports indicating that the L5 pars interarticularis is biomechanically more vulnerable due to cumulative shear stress during lumbar extension and rotation [23]. By enrolling a physically active, young adult population with minimal age-related confounders, our study provides a focused evaluation of treatment efficacy in a clinically relevant subgroup.

Studies indicate that intrasegmental direct fixation allows 2–3× greater rotational micromotion at the pars than intersegmental fixation, whereas DSC’s laminar screws further reduce shear force by ~ 15% [12, 24]. This biomechanical gradient directly parallels the observed stepwise increase in union rates. Our findings affirm the clinical utility of intersegmental fixation methods (PSS and DSC) in achieving superior pars healing compared to MST. Prior studies have noted the limited biomechanical stability of intrasegmental techniques such as Buck’s and the traditional Scott approach, particularly in the presence of laminar dysplasia or vertebral slippage [12]. While MST aimed to address some of these limitations by incorporating pedicle screw compression, our data show that it still resulted in the lowest healing rate (60.6%) and highest rate of implant failure (12.1%), particularly among patients who resumed athletic activities prematurely—supporting prior observations that motion control is critical for bony union [25].

In contrast, PSS and DSC techniques provided enhanced local stability and higher healing success (88.9% and 95.3%, respectively), with fewer complications. These results are aligned with recent clinical and biomechanical analyses suggesting that intersegmental fixation facilitates isthmic union by limiting rotational and flexion-extension micromotion [12, 13]. Moreover, although DSC required additional laminar screw insertion, it did not significantly prolong operative time or increase perioperative morbidity compared to PSS, highlighting its feasibility in routine clinical practice.

Notably, the DSC approach represents an evolution from traditional fixation strategies by combining dorsal and ventral control elements. This dual-stability design may account for its particularly high healing rate and lowest final VAS scores. Finite element studies have supported the theoretical biomechanical advantage of this approach [12], though our study offers one of the first clinical datasets to validate its efficacy and safety.

Another unique feature of this study is the inclusion of MRI-based disc assessments after implant removal. Concerns have been raised that intersegmental fixation, even when temporary, may accelerate adjacent disc degeneration [15]. However, our results demonstrated no significant change in Pfirrmann grading during short-term follow-up in either the PSS or DSC group, suggesting that short-term rigid fixation may be safe in young adults when appropriately timed and removed. These findings are in line with prior observational studies of motion-preserving spinal procedures [26].

Additionally, our study sheds light on the multifactorial contributors to postoperative symptom relief. Despite a lower fusion rate in the MST group, some patients still achieved favorable clinical outcomes. This supports prior hypotheses that surgical debridement, denervation of the fibrous defect, and the formation of stabilizing scar tissue may independently contribute to pain resolution [27]. Nonetheless, our data suggest that osseous healing remains the most robust predictor of long-term success, particularly in high-demand patients.

Finally, by evaluating outcomes across three techniques within a standardized follow-up protocol, this study provides comparative insights that are relatively scarce in the literature. While previous studies have focused on single techniques or limited comparisons, our analysis offers a more comprehensive framework for surgical decision-making in active young adults with spondylolysis.

Our findings confirm that intervertebral fixation techniques yield favorable clinical outcomes in young patients with lumbar spondylolysis [13]. Consistent with reports by Yamashita D et al. [28],and Zhang et al. [29], all three techniques significantly improved pain and function without serious complications.

This study directly compares three fixation strategies—MST, PSS, and DSC—within a single cohort. The DSC technique showed the highest JOA improvement and fusion success, corroborating prior biomechanical evidence that combined intersegmental and intrasegmental fixation enhances stability [12]. Both DSC and PSS were independently associated with higher healing probabilities compared to MST, aligning with the view that intersegmental fixation improves stability and promotes healing [11]. Unlike previous studies, we incorporated multivariate regression to adjust for confounders including CT defect type, BMI, and age, strengthening the validity of our conclusions.

Our study assessed fusion success based on the most recent available CT scan, prioritising methodological consistency across all surgical groups. Notably, clinical observations revealed that 73.8% of fused cases (n = 76/103) demonstrated definitive bony bridging on the 6-month postoperative CT—a temporal pattern analogous to fracture callus maturation timelines reported in spinal fusion literature [30]. Another important and less frequently explored aspect is the impact of anatomical defect type on surgical outcomes. Our study confirmed that CT type II and III lesions were associated with significantly reduced healing rates, a finding consistent with prior classifications proposed by Fujii and colleagues [31]. While such anatomical considerations are often qualitatively described in prior reports, our quantitative regression analysis reinforces their prognostic value.

Furthermore, the lack of significant difference in VAS improvement among the three groups suggests that pain relief may be achieved through any of the three surgical techniques, but long-term functional recovery is more dependent on fusion success. This reinforces the idea that structural healing of the pars interarticularis is a prerequisite for complete functional restoration in young active patients [32].

Accurately determining the precise origin of low back pain in patients presenting with spondylolysis, disc degeneration, and spondylolisthesis remains a considerable diagnostic challenge. Discography has been recognized in the literature as a sensitive tool for diagnosing discogenic pain and may help guide surgical planning in such cases [33]. Although the study applied strict inclusion criteria to minimize confounding effects from disc degeneration and slippage, the lack of confirmatory diagnostic blocks prevents definitive identification of the primary pain source. Nevertheless, the strong correlation observed between bony healing and meaningful clinical improvement in our research suggests that instability originating from the pars defect is likely a contributor to pain in this selected patient population.

Based on the findings of this study, the following individualized treatment protocols are recommended: The use of Modified Scott technique(MST) requires careful consideration due to its relatively low healing rate. For patients with CT II/III classification and BMI ≥ 25, particularly those in young populations requiring rapid recovery from extensive physical activity following postoperative, the DSC procedure demonstrates 88.3% postoperative healing rate at six months and an overall pars healing rate of 95.3%. This approach serves as the preferred treatment option while meeting clinical demands for early removal of internal fixation devices.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study employed a retrospective, single-center design in which the three surgical techniques were implemented in sequential phases, inevitably introducing selection and temporal biases. We mitigated these effects by (1) comparing baseline characteristics across groups and confirming no significant differences (Table 1, p >0.05); (2) restricting all procedures to a single, highly experienced surgical team (>10 years); and (3) using multivariable regression to adjust for potential confounders (CT classification, BMI, age). Nevertheless, residual confounding may persist. Prospective randomized controlled trials that incorporate additional variables are warranted to validate these findings. Secondly, while post-hoc power analysis demonstrated that the study’s detection efficacy for the primary endpoint (0.97) significantly exceeded conventional thresholds, the relatively small sample size may have limited statistical power to detect rare complications, secondary outcomes, or subtle clinical variations. The findings should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the choice of surgical plan is clinically significant for lumbar spondylolysis in young athletes. Future research should prioritize large-scale, multicenter trials to validate these conclusions. Third, although MRI-based Pfirrmann grading demonstrated no early adjacent-segment deterioration, the mean follow-up of 17.5 months is too short to determine long-term return-to-sport success or functional durability beyond 5–10 years—representing a critical gap in the current evidence—and larger, longer-term cohorts are required to verify sustained motion preservation and disc health. Fourth, We did not perform diagnostic procedures such as pedicle infiltration or provocative discography to differentiate pain origins from pedicle defects, intervertebral discs, or other structures. For patients with both disc degeneration and spondylolisthesis, this approach may obscure the precise source of low back pain. Future studies should adopt more accurate methods for differential diagnosis. Finally, the generalizability of the DSC technique may be constrained by its limited use in specific geographic or institutional contexts; multicenter trials across diverse surgical settings are needed to establish its broader applicability.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that all three surgical techniques—MST, PSS, and DSC—are effective in improving pain and functional outcomes in active adolescents with symptomatic lumbar spondylolysis. Among them, DSC achieved the highest rates of pars fusion, superior radiographic reduction, and sustained functional improvement, with no observed short-term disc degeneration following implant removal. By integrating intrasegmental and intersegmental stabilization principles, DSC offers a biomechanically robust yet motion-preserving approach that addresses both healing and segmental stability. These findings provide clinically relevant guidance for surgical decision-making in young, physically active patients and support the selective adoption of DSC as a promising option in this challenging population. Further prospective multicenter studies with long-term follow-up are needed to confirm its durability and broader applicability.
