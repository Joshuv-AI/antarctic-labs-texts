Human subject study of a transmission-aided attenuation correction for precision PET neuroimaging
Authors: Sima Lotfimarangloo, Syed T. Rahman, Mi-Ae Park, Baowei Fei, Ramsey D. Badawi, Spencer L. Bowen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPET data-driven attenuation correction (AC) methods, including deep learning, are attractive options for quantitative brain imaging on CT-less brain PET systems and low-dose PET/CT. However, current schemes have performance and practical limitations. We previously developed a CT-less transmission-aided AC that combines coincidences from a weak positron source, and the patient, to estimate attenuation with physics alone. In this work, we aim to optimize and assess this new AC method during human [18F]FDG neuroimaging on whole-body PET/CT.MethodsOur approach, TRansmission-aided μ-map reconstruction (TRU) AC, includes 1) a low-profile and physically fixed transmission source filled with ~ 14 MBq of 18F, 2) a modified maximum likelihood reconstruction of attenuation and activity algorithm, and 3) scatter corrections using the exam data alone. We imaged N = 5 patients with the transmission source, immediately after their clinical [18F]FDG PET/CT. The clinically-consistent protocol included a CT and 10-minute brain-focused PET exam. Using this 10 minutes of patient PET data alone, radiotracer images were reconstructed with the vendor’s algorithm and TRU-AC or CT-AC (reference standard), with all else matched. For quantitative analysis, we placed brain-structure volumes of interest with an atlas, and computed error in mean standardized uptake values of TRU-AC relative to CT-AC.ResultsTRU-AC PET showed qualitatively strong agreement with CT-AC. For the VOI analysis, absolute relative error in standardized uptake values for TRU-AC was within 3.6%, across all brain structures and patients. Normalized root mean square error of activity bias for TRU-AC was 1.8%, and voxel-wise noise in the cerebellum showed a very minor increase of 0.2%. Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated that TRU-AC and CT-AC have statistically significant agreement, assuming a maximum allowed difference of ± 5%.ConclusionsTRU-AC enables quantitative PET for human neuroimaging. This approach may particularly benefit exams where deep learning-based AC schemes show reduced performance, including those focused on radiotracer development, new patient cohorts, and/or pathologies that often lack sufficient training data.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40658-025-00803-x.

Background

PET imaging is a powerful tool in neuroimaging research and patient workup, including for neurological and psychiatric disorders [1]. Radiotracers for amyloid-β plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles, combined with semi-quantitative uptake measurements, act as the reference standard for assessing pathological protein burden in Alzheimer’s disease [2]. [18F]FDG PET is clinically indicated to evaluate neurodegeneration, for parkinsonian syndromes, oncological workup, and epilepsy [3]. Cross-sectional and longitudinal radiotracer uptake differences on PET are often subtle (e.g. ~ 1% annual increase in amyloid with normal aging [4]). Further, there is frequent use and development of radiotracers targeting a diverse portfolio of proteins for neuroimaging [1], where quantitative analysis is integral. Thus, it is important that PET images are generated with accurate radiotracer quantification.

Correction for the attenuation of 511 keV gamma rays, termed attenuation correction (AC), often has the largest impact on radiotracer quantification of any data correction used in human imaging. For PET/CT, CT-based AC (CT-AC) is the reference standard. However, for neuroimaging the CT exam is frequently only used for AC. Low-dose CT-AC delivers ~ 0.7 mSv of effective radiation dose, which is > 11% of the total dose for [18F]FDG and [18F]FDOPA brain PET/CT exams [5]. Alternatively, investigators have developed AC methods that use the PET data alone [6–14], termed CT-less AC. With the recent release of dedicated brain PET systems [15], which lack a CT in all but one case [16], there is a large focus on these CT-less AC methods.

Deep learning-based AC schemes (DL-AC) represent a particularly active research area [7–11]. These methods train on paired non-AC PET images and CT-AC μ-maps or AC PET images. However, DL-AC schemes can suffer from large outliers. Bias in the ratio of putamen-to-cerebellar uptake, relative to CT-AC, exceeded 30% for amyloid PET scans [8]. In a study of three neuroimaging radiotracers, including amyloid and tau, more than 3% of PET images generated with DL-AC had uptake error > 9% [10]. These methods have limited performance for radiotracers with relatively restricted biodistribution [10] or low uptake (e.g. > 12% putamen bias for [11C] raclopride [11]). Further, DL-AC schemes have increased error when applied to radiotracers, pathologies, cohorts, scanners, or acquisition strategies where paired CT and PET training data are limited or unavailable [17].

Separately, or combined with DL-AC, simultaneous activity and attenuation estimation strategies have shown promise. These methods estimate attenuation based on physics, using counts originating from the patient injected radiotracer. A common scheme is maximum likelihood reconstruction of activity and attenuation (MLAA). However, MLAA methods suffer from cross-talk between radiotracer and μ-map estimates, only reconstruct μ-map values up to a constant, and often rely on CT or MR data for scatter or normalization corrections, resulting in activity bias > 10% [18]. For optimal performance, these schemes typically require accurately segmenting the patient body contour and utilize an intensity prior that assumes the linear attenuation coefficient for a specific tissue [14], which is challenging when using the PET data alone. Some MLAA limitations are reduced by using photons from the PET detectors, arising from 176Lu radioactivity. But, even on a total-body PET/CT (axial field-of-view = 1 m), brain activity bias was > 10% [19]. DL and MLAA have been combined [20, 21], but cerebellar activity error was > 10% in one report.

We recently developed a CT-less transmission-aided AC that combines coincidences from a weak positron source, and the patient, to estimate attenuation with physics alone [22]. The approach does not extend exam time, uses no prior image data or μ-map assumptions (e.g. no DL), and does not require segmenting the patient body contour. The main components are: 1) a low-profile and physically fixed transmission (TX) source that is easy to fill and place, 2) a modified MLAA algorithm that combines counts from the patient and TX source, and 3) scatter corrections using PET data alone. We refer to this algorithm, optimized here, as TRansmission-aided μ-map reconstruction AC (TRU-AC). The approach leverages the higher count rates from coincidences originating from the patient, increasing μ-map SNR, while using the inherently higher μ-map accuracy afforded by inputting coincidences from the TX source. Activity bias for the previous version of TRU-AC was within 5% of CT-AC for cardiac phantoms [22]. Based on these promising results and those from a brain phantom [23], we aimed to optimize and assess the performance of TRU-AC during human [18F]FDG brain exams performed on a whole-body PET/CT. This represents the first study of TRU-AC on human subjects. The AC scheme has a new algorithm pipeline and TX source scatter correction compared with our prior report [22].

Methods

Transmission-aided attenuation map reconstruction

Fundamental aspects of TRU-AC were previously detailed [22]. We summarize the shared core components of TRU-AC here for clarity, focus on elements of the algorithm relevant to the results, and detail the new processing pipeline and TX source scatter correction. For all studies we used a time-of-flight (TOF) capable whole-body PET/CT, the Siemens Biograph mCT Flow (Siemens Medical Solutions USA, Inc; 4 detector ring configuration).

Transmission source hardware: A sparse transmission source was implemented by helically wrapping a single fillable plastic tube (inner diameter = 1.6 mm, length = 14.4 m, fill volume = 30 ml) around a minimally-attenuating cylindrical support (outer diameter = 76.2 cm). The tube is filled with a weak activity of 18F (~ 14 MBq) to generate 511 keV gamma rays. The source geometry spans the 22.1 cm PET axial field-of-view (FOV). Data acquisition and pre-processing: Acquisitions with the TX source in place, for TRU-AC, and to generate data for patient radiotracer reconstructions were simultaneous and had matched duration. Specifically, we did not perform a separate patient acquisition for TRU-AC alone. A separate μ-map calibration acquisition, with nothing in the FOV (“blank scan”), was performed after every patient scan. All acquisition parameters were matched except duration. PET list-mode data were binned into TOF projections offline. We used the radial offset from the center of the FOV, based on raw TOF and projection bin coordinates, to segment counts originating from the TX source and radiotracer in the patient [13].

Radiotracer image reconstruction

For CT-AC, CT images were acquired and then converted into a μ-map by scaling with the bilinear transform [24]. Radiotracer PET images, for all attenuation correction methods, were reconstructed with a TOF-implementation of OSEM (5 iterations, 21 subsets, point spread function modelling, 2.0 zoom factor) followed by post-reconstruction smoothing with a 3 mm FWHM Gaussian. Images had a matrix size of 400 × 400 × 109 and a voxel size of 1.0 × 1.0 × 2.0 mm. We used the vendor’s e7tools to generate CT-AC μ-maps and for radiotracer reconstructions. For an honest comparison of TRU-AC against CT-AC, only the μ-map was substituted for radiotracer reconstructions, while all other inputs and parameters were matched. The reconstruction protocol is consistent with societal guidelines [3] and our institution’s clinical protocol. In addition to AC, we applied all data corrections, including randoms, dead-time, normalization, and scatter. PET images were output in units of standardized uptake values (SUVs).

Patient imaging

We conducted a prospective human subjects study to evaluate TRU-AC. The study was approved by our institution’s review board and all subjects signed an informed consent form. We recruited N = 5 patients already scheduled for a clinically indicated PET/CT. Eligible patients were at least 18 yo and lacked brain structural pathology based on their clinical history (e.g. due to cancer). For clinical workup, patients were injected with 4.8 MBq/kg of [18F]FDG (range of total injection activities = 296–444 MBq), underwent a 60 min uptake period, followed by a PET/CT. Immediately after, the TX source was filled with [18F]FDG (activity range at start of imaging = 13.8–14.7 MBq) and placed in the PET/CT, and the research exam started. The patient was positioned in a rigid head holder, then imaged with a CT protocol with automatic kV selection and mA modulation for radiation dose reduction, and a 10 min brain-focused PET acquisition at a single table position (duration from [18F]FDG injection to start of research PET = 101–117 min). The 10 min PET acquisition is the minimum recommended by societal guidelines [3]. Blank scans were acquired for 45 min right after each patient scan, to account for inter-exam changes due to manual preparation of the TX source prototype (see Discussion).

Quantitative image analysis

To automatically define volumes of interest (VOIs) we implemented a PET neuroimaging analysis workflow consistent with studies lacking MRI, using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12) and the MarsBaR extension in MATLAB [25]. We utilized the Dementia-Specific [18F]FDG-PET template [26], to compute PET image spatial normalization to standard space, and the Automated Anatomical Labelling (AAL) atlas [27] parcellation to place structural VOIs. Nine large VOIs, consistent with those employed in studies on AC methods for neuroimaging [7–9, 11, 28], were used for assessing PET quantification. These included the frontal, occipital, parietal, and temporal lobes, and the thalamus, hippocampus, cerebral white matter (WM), a composite of other structures (e.g. the amygdala and parts of the basal ganglia), and the cerebellar gray matter excluding the vermis. Statistical analysis

Linear regression was used to assess the association between TRU-AC and CT-AC mean uptake estimates, and correlation assessed with the coefficient of determination (R2). We used Bland–Altman analysis to evaluate the agreement in mean uptake between TRU-AC and CT-AC. The mean and 95% confidence intervals for REs were calculated. We considered TRU-AC and CT-AC to have statistically significant agreement when the upper and lower 95% confidence intervals were within ± 5% [29]. The significance level was set at 0.05.

Discussion

TRU-AC generates quantitatively accurate PET images during human [18F]FDG neuroimaging. Error in SUV values from TRU-AC, relative to CT-AC, were all within ± 3.6%, and noise amplification was < 2.9%. These results were generated from a conventional whole-body PET/CT, with an axial FOV consistent with scanners most often used for neuroimaging (i.e. ≤ 30 cm). This is an important advantage of TRU-AC versus recent promising CT-less schemes that leverage the relatively high photon sensitivity and/or intrinsic 176Lu radioactivity of total-body PET/CTs (axial FOV ≥ 1 m) [19, 20]. Combined with our report on cardiac phantom imaging [22], this study shows the versatility of TRU-AC to enable quantitative imaging for [18F]FDG exams that encompass large variations in radiotracer and attenuation distributions.

VOI performance from TRU-AC exceeds physics-only MLAA schemes, and approaches recent DL-AC and anatomy-constrained MLAA methods, for [18F]FDG neuroimaging. An MLAA method that serially estimates the μ-map with intrinsic 176Lu counts and MLAA had brain REs ranging from -25 to 25% on a total-body PET/CT [19]. DL-AC methods produced REs within ± 5% [7–11]. On PET/MR, an MLAA-scheme incorporating patient-specific CT images for normalization, and MR images for a soft-tissue prior, produced REs ranging from -9 to 11% [14]. We did not directly compare TRU-AC against DL-AC or MLAA methods. Thus, differences in the scanner, injection protocol, data processing and analysis, limits our comparison.

There remain areas for improvement with TRU-AC. We measured slightly positive REs for cortical brain structures. One possible cause is the roughness penalty function in (1), which is necessary to minimize noise propagation from the TRU-AC μ-map to the patient radiotracer reconstruction. We chose optimal function parameters based on review of patient PET images, but this may have oversmoothed the skull versus CT-AC. We note SUV bias in cortical tissue near the skull was also observed in MLAA [14] and DL-AC schemes [8, 10]. Separately, we measured slightly negative bias in deep brain structures. This could have resulted from underestimation of patient or TX scatter. Absolute scatter scaling methods are negatively impacted when there is a large fraction of multiple-order and/or out of FOV scatter [31]. Advanced noise reduction and scatter estimation schemes are needed.

Differences in TRU-AC algorithms and exam types restrict our comparisons of these two studies. However, the larger optimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α here was likely driven by the fivefold higher activity concentration in human brain scans relative to phantoms. TRU-AC uses the TX counts alone instead of the patient body contour, or a linear attenuation coefficient intensity prior, typically employed in conventional MLAA (i.e. using counts from tracer in the patient alone). This, in turn, required a higher \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α to optimize the bias-to-noise tradeoff in the μ-map update. Scatter correction limitations, detailed above, may have also contributed to differences in optimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α. Alternatives to hand-tuning \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}α are needed to improve the generalizability and performance of TRU-AC.

To facilitate proof of principle studies we used a TX source that was manually filled with 18F and placed in the scanner. For routine human imaging, it is necessary to implement hardware that maintains patient throughput and does not increase the technologist’s workload. This includes eliminating 45 min blank scans after each patient scan, which were needed here to account for inter-exam variations in 18F-filling and placement of our manual setup. To achieve these goals we plan on using a long half-life radionuclide for the TX source, such as 68Ge (half-life = 271 days), combined with a scheme to precisely and automatically reposition the source. This would enable the blank to be acquired ~ once daily at convenient clinic hours, and maintain patient throughput.

Despite promising results, our study has several limitations. We used a whole-body PET/CT (detector ring diameter = 84.2 cm, TOF resolution = 580 ps [33]), which has substantially different geometry and performance compared to recent dedicated brain PET systems. These brain PET scanners have ring diameters of 24.2–52.4 cm, TOF resolutions of 236–359 ps, and typically higher photon sensitivity than the study scanner [15, 16]. This could impact segmentation of TX counts, and benefit the SNR of TRU-AC μ-maps, leading to different performance of TRU-AC on dedicated brain PET systems than in this report. We evaluated TRU-AC on [18F]FDG brain exams alone. Preliminary phantom results [23] for a dopamine transporter radiotracer also show quantitatively accurate PET images. A key advantage of TRU-AC versus DL-AC is its potential for quantitative imaging independent of the radiotracer, and without training data. We will evaluate this in future studies.

The TRU-AC scheme is expected to benefit neuroimaging research and patient workup with CT-less PET exams, particularly where DL-AC methods are challenged. Quantitatively accurate and precise imaging with TRU-AC on dedicated brain PET systems could improve individualized selection and monitoring of Alzheimer’s disease treatments [34], where outliers from DL could change patient care. For research with these scanners, TRU-AC will enable the study of new radiotracers, patient cohorts, pathologies, animal models, and acquisition strategies with no or limited training data. In pilot studies, including for testing of new radiotracers, the number of recruited patients is often N ≤ 10 [35], which is below the N = 40–170 used for DL-AC training and testing [7–11]. For PET/CT, using TRU-AC would eliminate the radiation dose from CT-AC, benefiting vulnerable populations. We estimate effective dose from the TX source was < 1 μSv in this study (15 MBq fill activity, 10 min scan) [36], versus 0.7 mSv for low-dose CT-AC exams [5]. Beyond neuroimaging, TRU-AC could benefit standalone application-specific PET scanners, intended for breast and extremity imaging [37].

Conclusions

This report presents and evaluates an optimized CT-less AC scheme, TRU-AC, during human [18F]FDG PET neuroimaging. TRU-AC enables quantitatively accurate PET during [18F]FDG brain exams acquired on a conventional whole-body PET/CT. The new AC scheme may especially benefit low-dose PET/CT and dedicated brain PET exams focused on radiotracer development, semi-quantitative method validation, new patient cohorts or animal models, and/or pathologies that often lack sufficient paired CT and PET training data needed for DL-AC schemes.
