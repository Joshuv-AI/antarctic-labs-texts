Microstructural characterization and creep response of Gleeble-simulated heat-affected zone of electron beam welded Grade 91 steel
Authors: J Vanaja, G V Prasad Reddy, Ramanathaswamy Pandian, H C Dey, A Nagesha, M Vasudevan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe manuscript presents microstructure and creep behavior of Gleeble-simulated heat-affected zones (HAZs) representative of electron beam (EB)-welded Grade 91 steel joints. Using phase transformation temperatures from dilatometry studies, the Gleeble simulator was employed to replicate the microstructures of coarse-grain (CGHAZ), fine-grain (FGHAZ), and intercritical (ICHAZ) regions constituting the narrow HAZ in EB weld joints. Vickers hardness of simulated HAZ specimens ranged from 235 ± 5 HV (CGHAZ) to 195 ± 5 HV (ICHAZ), closely matching values in the corresponding weld joint regions. Microstructural comparisons further substantiated the successful HAZ replication, with CGHAZ showing marginal grain coarsening, finer M23C6 carbides and a higher fraction of low-angle grain boundaries. In contrast, FGHAZ and ICHAZ exhibited coarser carbides and finer grains compared to the base metal. A custom-designed creep specimen, developed in this study, enabled precise evaluation of creep performance of Gleeble simulated microstructures pertaining to localised regimes of very narrow HAZ, overcoming the limitations of standard specimen geometries. The creep behavior of simulated HAZ specimens was evaluated at 873 K and 160 MPa and compared with the base metal and EB weld joint. Among the simulated regions, CGHAZ showed a ~ 17.5-times increase in creep rupture life compared to base metal, attributed to refined microstructural features. In contrast, ICHAZ retained only ~ 5% of the base metal’s life, highlighting the adverse effects of limited grain boundary pinning and coarsened carbides.Keywords: Grade 91 steel, Heat affected zone, Gleeble simulation, Microstructure, CreepSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Dissimilar weld joints (DWJs) between austenitic alloys and ferritic-martensitic steels form a vital part of various high-temperature components in power generation systems (e.g. coal-fired power plants, nuclear plants), and chemical industries. In coal-fired thermal power plants, ferritic-austenitic DWJs appear mainly in high temperature steam piping (e.g., superheater outlet and main steam line) and feedwater piping (e.g. joint between stainless steel feedwater pipe and steel economizer) systems1,2. In sodium cooled fast reactor, DWJs appear at steam generator inlet and outlet nozzles3. These joints are often necessitated by the need to combine the superior high-temperature properties of austenitic alloys with the competent creep resistance of ferritic-martensitic steels at lower temperatures. However, the inherent differences in physical and metallurgical properties between the two materials pose considerable fabrication challenges and often constitute the weakest link in structural components. Among ferritic-martensitic steels, Grade 91 (Gr.91) is widely used for power plant components with its creep strength primarily derived from the tempered martensitic microstructure and the fine dispersion of MX and M23​C6 precipitates. However, creep degradation associated with precipitate coarsening and subgrain formation, particularly in the heat-affected zones (HAZ) leading to premature failures, remains a critical concern. Hence, understanding the effect of welding thermal cycles on its creep performance is essential for reliable joint design. With the advent of advanced welding techniques employing lower heat inputs, the performance of dissimilar weld joints has garnered increased attention. In particular, low heat input processes like electron beam (EB) welding technique has emerged as a promising method, combining precision with minimal thermal distortion. Its successful implementation in the fabrication of thick-section components, such as small modular reactor vessels, further highlights its industrial relevance4. The technique has recently been explored for its effectiveness in producing high-quality dissimilar joints, particularly between ferritic-martensitic steel and austenitic steels, which have long served as workhorse materials in both conventional and nuclear power plants5,6. Santosh et al.7, using SYSWELD simulations, reported that for low heat input process like laser welding, thermal cycles in HAZ of Gr. 91 steel remain above the phase transformation temperature, Ac1, for only a fraction of a second, which is insufficient for prior austenite grain coarsening and carbide dissolution/coarsening. The computational results obtained from the above study indicated that the high temperatures experienced by the HAZ region do not persist long enough to support the kinetics necessary for carbide coarsening/dissolution and grain growth. However, creep studies on weld joints fabricated using such low heat input processes have revealed Type IV failure in HAZ of Gr. 91 steel, a characteristic mode of failure occurring at the fine grain or intercritical regions of HAZ under creep conditions8.

Our previous work on dissimilar joint, between 316LN SS and Gr.91 steel, has demonstrated significant improvement in creep strength and weld strength reduction factor through the use of EB welding technique6. Notably, the weld joint has shown no interface failure, but rather a Type IV cracking, which is commonly observed in the HAZ of the ferritic-martensitic steel6. This indicates that while the damage to the material caused by the EB weld thermal cycle may be less pronounced compared to high heat input or multi-pass welding processes, it still causes microstructural changes that could contribute to creep damage and failure initiation in the HAZ. The development of the HAZ microstructure is therefore critical for Gr.91 steel, as the thermal cycle imposed by welding modifies the original microstructural features including the optimised distribution of precipitates and their interparticle spacing which are essential for maintaining the high creep strength. Although the mechanisms of Type IV damage in conventional welds are well established, the influence of short-duration thermal exposures typical of low heat input technique on the creep response of Gr.91 steel remains insufficiently addressed. Hence, reproducing these transient thermal conditions through controlled simulations is essential to understand and predict HAZ behaviour under such techniques.

Over the years, researchers and engineers have employed different methods to simulate the HAZ, and one such technique that has gained prominence is the Gleeble simulation. The Gleeble system is powerful thermo-mechanical testing equipment capable of reproducing the complex thermal cycles experienced by the HAZ during welding. For simulating high heat input welding processes such as gas tungsten arc welding (GTAW) and shielded metal arc welding (SMAW), heating rates in the range of 60–80 K/s, along with carefully controlled cooling rates of 30–40 K/s, are typically implemented9,10. One of the key advantages of Gleeble simulation is that it effectively overcomes the limitations of conventional furnace heating which allows sufficient time for diffusion phenomenon causing dissolution and coarsening of precipitates, excessive grain growth, deviating from the actual welding conditions specifically for low heat-input welding cases as described by Santosh et al.7. Mayr et al.11 demonstrated that even at elevated peak temperatures of 1373 K and 1473 K, the Gleeble simulator achieved remarkable control over the microstructure, preventing prior austenite grain boundary coarsening which is observed during furnace simulation. While the Gleeble system has been extensively used for microstructural studies, its application in mechanical property evaluation remains relatively limited. In addition, studies by Midawi et al.12, Kovács et al.13, and Amraei et al.14 have validated the Gleeble’s capability to replicate the thermal histories of various welding processes and to evaluate the corresponding mechanical properties across distinct HAZ regions. In low heat input processes such as EB or laser welding, extremely high heating and cooling rates are the required conditions which can be effectively reproduced only using the Gleeble system.

The present manuscript aims to simulate the microstructures of various HAZ regions developed by EB welding, using a Gleeble simulator. Due to the limited size of the simulated zone (5–15 mm), obtaining specimens with a uniform microstructure in the test section for evaluating mechanical properties particularly creep remains a significant challenge. Owing to this limitation, creep studies are very limited on the Gleeble simulated HAZ microstructures. To overcome this limitation, custom-designed creep specimens was used in the study that enabled precise evaluation of the creep properties of different HAZ sub-zones. Creep tests were conducted at 873 K to systematically assess and compare the deformation behavior and rupture life across these regions.

Experimental details

The chemical composition of the Gr.91 steel used in the study is (in wt%): 0.10 C, 8.72Cr, 0.90Mo, 0.08Nb, 0.22 V, 0.05 N, 0.46Mn, 0.32Si and bal-Fe. The material was used in normalized (1323 K for 15 min, air-cooled) and tempered (1053 K for 1 h, air-cooled) condition. To simulate the various regions of the HAZ developed by EB welding, phase transformation temperatures (Ac1 and Ac3) were first identified using dilatometry tests performed on the Gleeble™ 3500 thermomechanical simulator. These tests provided essential data on the ferrite-to-austenite transformation temperature, which were used to design the specific thermal cycles for each HAZ subregions. The EB welding of the reference joint was carried out using the typical heat input parameters (beam current 128 mA, accelerating voltage 60 kV, welding speed 800 mm/min) as reported in our previous study6. Due to the narrow HAZ width resulting from the low heat input of the EB welding process, accurately replicating the individual subzones becomes technically challenging. Therefore, Gleeble-based thermal simulations were employed to effectively isolate and investigate the creep behaviour of each subzone. Since the transformation temperatures are crossed in just fraction of a second due to high heating rate, several trial runs were conducted to achieve the target microstructure and hardness values, closely matching those in the HAZ regions of the Gr.91 EB welded joint.

Figure 1a shows the schematic of the designed thermal cycles for the HAZ simulations, finalized after several trial runs. The peak temperatures for the three subzones were selected based on phase transformation characteristics and thermodynamic constraints. For the CGHAZ simulation, a peak temperature of 1473 K was chosen. JMatPro calculations for the Grade 91 composition indicate a solidus temperature of approximately 1705 K, confirming that the chosen peak temperature lies well below the solidus and prevents any risk of incipient melting. The calculations also show that δ-ferrite is stable above 1559 K, thus, selecting 1473 K ensures complete austenitization above Ac3 while remaining below the δ-ferrite stability limit. Based on these considerations, peak temperatures of 1473 K, 1273 K and 1148 K were selected to simulate the CGHAZ, FGHAZ and ICHAZ, respectively. Figure 1b presents the corresponding thermal profiles for each zone.

Schematic representation of (a) the thermal cycle used in Gleeble simulations to replicate HAZ microstructures in Gr. 91 steel, (b) the thermal profiles corresponding to the CGHAZ, FGHAZ and ICHAZ simulations, (c) specimen employed for Gleeble simulation of HAZ (shaded region) indicating location of thermocouples and (d) fabricated creep specimen design.

For generating simulated samples, Gr.91 steel bars (120 mm long × 10 mm diameter, Fig. 1c) were mounted between two half-contact water-cooled copper grips in the Gleeble chamber. During Gleeble HAZ simulation, three K-type thermocouples were spot-welded to the specimen, one at the centre and two positioned 5 mm away on either side (shown in shaded area of Fig. 1c). The central thermocouple was used for temperature control through real-time feedback, while the side thermocouples served to verify thermal field uniformity across the simulated zone.

The heating rate was initially set at 200 K/s upto (Tpeak – 100 K), then reduced to 100 K/s to prevent overshooting and to ensure precise control. The specimens were held at the peak temperature for 1 s, followed by exponential cooling such that it reaches 773 K within 20 s. The average cooling rate calculated within the transformation range (upto Ac1) was found to be 110 K/s. Subsequently, the simulated specimens were subjected to heat treatment at 1033 K for three hours to replicate post-weld heat treatment (PWHT) conditions of the weld joints (recommended as per ASME Section IX for ASME SA-387 Grade 91 steel).

After heat-treatment, the simulated bars were machined to fabricate the creep specimens as per the geometry sketched in Fig. 1d. The specimen design ensures that the entire gauge length of the creep specimen covers the simulated region exclusively. Creep rupture tests under constant load were conducted in air at 873 K and 160 MPa. Temperature stability was ensured within ± 2 K throughout the duration of the experiment. The selected temperature of 873 K corresponds to typical service temperatures (823–873 K) of Grade 91 steel components in power plant applications15. The stress level of 160 MPa was chosen for a minimum exposure of 1000 h with reference to base metal Gr.91 steel, and the same was chosen for simulated specimens for comparison.

For microstructural studies, Villella’s reagent (1 g picric acid + 5 ml hydrochloric acid + 100 ml ethanol) was used as etchant after polishing the specimens to reveal the grain boundaries. Microhardness measurements were obtained using a Vickers hardness tester employing a load of 200 gf with a hold time of 15 s. Microstructural examination and fractography were performed using a field emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; Carl Zeiss Gemini) M23C6 precipitate morphology was quantified using ImageJ software. High-resolution images, captured at a magnification of ×25,000, were used for quantification to ensure clear contrast between precipitates and the matrix. Image processing utilised grayscale threshold function followed by particle analysis to determine precipitate size, area fraction, and shape descriptors such as circularity and aspect ratio (AR). Circularity, as defined in ImageJ, is given by {4π × Area / (Perimeter)²}, where a value of 1.0 corresponds to a perfect circle and lower values represent increasingly elongated shapes. Very fine MX precipitates (< 50 nm), which were below the resolution limit of the imaging conditions, were not quantified. Electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD) analysis was conducted on polished samples with a step size of 0.2 μm using an EBSD detector, and the data were processed with HKL Channel 5 software. Grain boundary maps and Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) maps were generated; for KAM mapping, a misorientation threshold of up to 3° was applied.

Results and discussion

During welding, Gr.91 steel undergoes phase transformation due to the thermal cycle, resulting in continuous microstructural changes within the HAZ. The peak temperatures are higher near the fusion line and decrease rapidly as the distance from the fusion line increases and the microstructure developed in a particular region is a direct result of the thermal history of that location. Based on the magnitude of peak temperature and associated microstructural changes due to phase transformation, the HAZ is further classified into 3 main subzones in sequence from the fusion line toward the base metal (BM) i.e. the coarse-grain (CGHAZ), fine-grain (FGHAZ), and intercritical (ICHAZ) heat-affected zones. The width of the HAZ and the subzones depends on the heat input of the welding technique.

The electron beam welding process referred in this study is a high-energy density process with the advantage of higher speed and lower heat input. In our previous study on dissimilar joint, fabricated using the EB welding, the weld zone was observed to be extremely narrow, measuring roughly 2 mm in width. The macrostructure of the EB weldment exhibited a characteristic ‘wine-glass’ shaped weld sandwiched between the two base metals, with distinct dark and bright regions corresponding to spatial variations in the resultant microstructure6. In addition, the width of HAZ on the Gr.91 was significantly narrow, measuring approximately 0.7–0.8 mm on Gr. 91 steel, compared to the 3–5 mm wide HAZ typically observed in conventional welding processes6,16.

Gleeble simulation of HAZ microstructure

The phase transformation temperatures (Ac1 and Ac3) of Gr.91 steel were determined using dilatometry, equipped with the Gleeble™ simulator to identify the transformation characteristics under rapid thermal cycles. The austenite formation is a nucleation and growth phenomenon, making the critical transformation temperature significantly influenced by the heating rate, with higher rates raising the critical temperature17. The previous studies conducted at our laboratory, indicated Ac1 (austenite start temperature) and Ac3 (austenite finish temperature) temperatures as 1110 K and 1176 K respectively for the Gr.91 steel at a heating rate of 10 K/min18. In the current study, the simulation sample was heated to 1373 K with a uniform heating rate of 100 K/s, and the corresponding dilatometry curve is presented in Fig. 2a. The ferrite-to-austenite transformation temperature range, is observed to be between 1144 K and 1193 K. The reduced exposure time at high heating rate constrains the complete diffusion of alloying elements19,20. Therefore, the start and finish temperatures of austenite transformation increased. The observed transformation range is consistent with values reported in the open literature for Gr.91 steel17,18.

(a) Dilatometric curve for determination of transformation temperatures; (b) Simulated welding thermal cycle curves of FGHAZ simulated specimens.

These transformation characteristics provided the basis for defining the peak temperatures used in the subsequent HAZ simulations (Fig. 1b). Accordingly, the peak temperatures chosen for CGHAZ, FGHAZ, and ICHAZ simulation were validated by monitoring the thermal field during Gleeble trials. As shown in Fig. 2b, the three thermocouple readings confirm a uniform temperature distribution over a span of approximately 10 mm, demonstrating that the imposed thermal cycles represent the intended HAZ conditions uniformly.

Microhardness after PWHT

After the HAZ simulation followed by heat-treatment (replicating PWHT), the uniformity of simulated thermal zone across 10 mm length midsection of the specimen was validated using microhardness measurements. Figure 3a shows the hardness variation graph for the samples corresponding to CGHAZ, FGHAZ and ICHAZ. The graph shows consistent microhardness values across the mid-region of the specimens, indicating uniform hardness upto 10–10.5 mm. The black horizontal line at the centre in the above figure represents the base metal hardness for comparison.

(a) Hardness variation across the midsection of simulated specimens after PWHT, (b) Comparison of hardness between HAZ of actual EB weld joint and simulated HAZ specimens.

Figure 3b displays the hardness values of the simulated specimens along with the hardness values of corresponding HAZ regimes of Gr.91 steel in the EB weld joint. The CGHAZ exhibits the highest hardness value of around 230 HV. In contrast, the ICHAZ displays a marked reduction in hardness to approximately 190–200 HV. The trend is consistent with previous observations on Gr.91 weld joints8,21,22, and the similarity in hardness between the simulated specimens and the HAZ regions of the actual EB-welded joint (Fig. 3b) indicates that the applied thermal cycles effectively replicated the corresponding HAZ regimes.

Microstructure

The initial microstructure of Gr. 91 steel consists of tempered martensitic structure (Fig. 4a,b), characterized by a hierarchical arrangement of prior austenite grain boundaries, packet and block boundaries, and lath boundaries. The average prior austenite grain size is found to be 32 μm with a standard deviation of ± 10 μm. The boundaries are decorated with coarse M23C6 carbides, where M represents elements such as chromium, iron, and molybdenum. In addition to it, fine MX precipitates, consisting of vanadium or niobium rich carbides, nitrides or carbonitrides, are observed on the lath boundaries and dispersed in the matrix as reported in an earlier study23. The SEM micrographs presented in this section highlight the distribution of M₂₃C₆ precipitates (Fig. 4b, with a high-magnification inset). EDS analysis confirms these precipitates to be Cr-rich carbides, with a composition of 2.65 wt% C, 21.47 wt% Cr, 73.02 wt% Fe, and 2.86 wt% Mo, At the chosen magnification, only the relatively coarser M23C6 carbides are discernible. Additionally, Thomas Paul et al.24 provided detailed TEM and SAED evidence confirming that the boundary precipitates formed in the HAZ regions of Gr.91 steel during welding are M23C6 carbides. The stability and distribution of M23C6 precipitates play a crucial role in the performance of the steel particularly in high-temperature applications, as MX precipitates are relatively stable and exhibit resistance to rapid coarsening under thermal exposure. The preservation of the hierarchical microstructure is closely linked to the stability of M23C6 carbides, making their morphology and distribution crucial in sustaining long-term performance during service.

(a) Optical and (b) scanning electron micrograph of Gr.91 steel; The inset in (b) shows a higher-magnification view of the M23C6 carbides with its corresponding EDS spectrum.

Figure 5 presents SEM micrographs of the actual HAZ regions in the EB weld joint, alongside the corresponding simulated HAZ regions, highlighting the evolution of grain structure and precipitates as a result of varying peak temperatures experienced by each HAZ region. Figure 5 illustrates the CGHAZ formed adjacent to the fusion zone. In the actual weld joint, the CGHAZ showed a relatively narrow region of less than 0.1 mm wide (spanning only few grains), indicating momentary exposure to elevated temperatures as compared to conventional welding process. The rapid cooling effectively minimizes grain coarsening by limiting high-temperature exposure, resulting in only a marginal increase in the average prior austenite grain size to 40 ± 10 μm. Since the zone experiences temperatures well above the Ac3, the M23C6 particles dissolve during the thermal cycle. PWHT facilitates the re-precipitation of finer M23C6 particles, contributing to the final microstructure. Quantitative analysis indicates an average carbide size of 0.09 ± 0.01 μm and a mean circularity of 0.685 (AR:1.94).

Comparison of SEM microstructures between EB weldment and Gleeble-simulated specimens: (a,b) CGHAZ, (c,d) FGHAZ, and (e,f) ICHAZ.

Figure 5c,d depicts the adjacent FGHAZ, which is the widest among the three HAZ regions (in actual weld joint) and is exposed to a temperature range slightly above Ac3. In this region, the combination of time and temperature is insufficient for the complete dissolution of precipitates. As a result, austenite grain growth is constrained by the partial dissolution of carbides and grain size is observed to be ranging from 7 to 10 μm. Following PWHT, quantitative analysis of these particles revealed an average diameter of 0.13 μm and a mean circularity of 0.756 (AR:1.7), indicating coarsened precipitates compared to the CGHAZ.

The ICHAZ (Fig. 5e,f), located adjacent to the FGHAZ, is a very narrow region subjected to peak temperatures between Ac1 and Ac3. In this zone, partial transformation of tempered martensite to austenite occurs without significant dissolution of M23C6 carbides. Upon cooling, the newly formed austenite transforms back into fresh martensite, while the untransformed tempered martensite experiences considerable recovery during the subsequent PWHT. Notably, the original alignment of carbides, which were located along prior austenite grain boundaries and block boundaries, remains largely intact due to incomplete carbide dissolution (marked by arrows in Fig. 5e,f). However, the carbides undergo coarsening during PWHT while retaining the original positions. The quantitative image analysis of these particles revealed an average diameter of 0.15 μm and a mean circularity of 0.81 (AR:1.66). Additionally, the newly formed grain boundaries exhibit limited carbide precipitation, resulting in reduced grain boundary strengthening.

The present study, focused on EB-welded joints, reveals significantly finer carbide distributions across the HAZ regions compared to earlier reports on multipass GTAW and SMAW welds. In those conventional processes, much coarser precipitates were observed, with sizes of 176 ± 45 nm in CGHAZ, 180 ± 42 nm in FGHAZ, and 205 ± 52 nm in ICHAZ25. Vaishali et al.26 further reported even larger carbide sizes in SMAW joints. The relatively refined precipitates observed in the current work are attributed to the inherently lower heat input of EB welding and the precise thermal control enabled by Gleeble simulation. These observations emphasises the distinct microstructural evolution in EB-welded joints, distinguishing them from conventional welding techniques.

To further understand the grain boundary character, EBSD analysis was performed to examine the distribution of low-angle grain boundaries (LAGBs) and high-angle grain boundaries (HAGBs), which serve as indicators of the substructural evolution during the imposed thermal cycle. The grain boundaries with misorientations in the range of 2–15° and > 15° are classified as LAGBs and HAGBs, respectively27. Figure 6a–d displays the EBSD grain boundary maps of the base metal and the simulated HAZ regions. The fraction of LAGBs (shown as red lines) and HAGBs (black lines) were evaluated for each region. The initial BM microstructure exhibited an approximately equal fraction of LAGBs and HAGBs. In the CGHAZ specimen, the LAGB fraction has increased to approximately 64.8%. This increase is consistent with the finer lath sizes characteristic of the CGHAZ. The FGHAZ exhibited a moderately higher LAGB fraction of ~ 53.1%, compared to the BM. In contrast, the ICHAZ showed a decrease in the LAGB fraction to 45.5%, with a corresponding increase in HAGB fraction to ~ 54.5%. This shift is attributed to the partial austenite transformation with the formation of new grains during thermal cycling. Such variations in boundary characteristics reflect the interplay between thermal peak temperatures and transformation behaviour, influencing the refinement or coarsening of laths and subgrains. The predominance of LAGBs observed in this study, particularly after welding simulation followed by PWHT/tempering, is consistent with the earlier observations by Karuppiah et al.23.

Grain boundary maps obtained from EBSD scans, with low-angle grain boundaries (2º–15º) represented in red and high-angle boundaries (> 15º) in black for (a) Base metal and Gleeble simulated microstructures of (b) CGHAZ (c) FGHAZ and (d) ICHAZ.

The Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) maps, obtained from EBSD analysis, captures subtle variations in lattice orientation at the microscale. KAM effectively quantifies local plastic strain gradients within individual grains and serves as a reliable indicator of geometrically necessary dislocation density. Regions with higher KAM values correspond to elevated lattice distortion and localised stored strain energy, which are indicative of increased dislocation density and higher creep resistance. Figure 7 presents the KAM maps of the simulated heat-affected zones. The CGHAZ map exhibit the higher KAM values over most of the microstructure (evidenced by the increased presence of yellow-green colored regions). In contrast, in ICHAZ, regions of lower KAM values predominates across the microstructure, indicating the presence of untransformed martensite that has undergone considerable recovery. The later microstructural feature, as also reported by Wang et al.28, contributes to a softer matrix characterized with reduced dislocation density.

EBSD Kernel Average Misorientation (KAM) maps of Gleeble simulated sub-regions of HAZ after PWHT, showing variations in local plastic strain.

These KAM maps also facilitate microstructural distinction among the sub-regions. The CGHAZ and FGHAZ primarily consist of re-austenitized martensite that has undergone post-weld tempering, with the key difference being the grain size. In contrast, the ICHAZ contains a mixture of partially recovered martensite and regions of re-austenitized martensite, as ICHAZ did not experience complete austenitization during the thermal cycle. This mixed microstructure is consistent with the lower KAM values observed due to the recovered-martensitic regions in ICHAZ. The normalized average KAM values are 0.65°, 0.59°, and 0.51° for CGHAZ, FGHAZ, and ICHAZ, respectively, illustrating a gradual decrease in dislocation density and stored strain energy across these regions. This gradient in lattice distortion reflects the varying extent of recovery and microstructural stability among the HAZ sub-zones. These collective microstructural attributes, including precipitate distribution, boundary characteristics and local misorientations play a pivotal role in governing the creep response and damage evolution in regions subjected to thermal cycling, as discussed in the subsequent section.

Creep behaviour

Creep tests were conducted on Gr.91 BM, Gleeble-simulated HAZ specimens (CGHAZ, FGHAZ, and ICHAZ), and compared with the creep life of the EB welded dissimilar joint tested at 873 K and a stress level of 160 MPa. As shown in Fig. 8a, the creep strain versus time plot reveals distinct behaviour across the specimens. The creep failure behavior of dissimilar joints between 316LN SS and Gr. 91 steel has been comprehensively reported in our previous work6, where Type IV cracking was observed in the HAZ along with a relatively high weld strength reduction factor. Since the Gr. 91 HAZ is the weakest link under service conditions, the creep rupture life of the base metal is used as the reference for comparison.The comparison of creep life across the tested specimens highlights the influence of microstructural variations. The base metal (Gr. 91 steel) failed after about 1009 h, while the dissimilar weld joint exhibited a shorter creep life of 544 h. In contrast, the CGHAZ specimen showed exceptional performance with a creep rupture life of 17691.8 h, which is ~ 17.5 times longer than the creep life of base metal. On the other hand, the creep life of FGHAZ and ICHAZ significantly reduced to 93 h and 53 h, respectively, corresponding to just 9% and 5% of the base material’s creep life.

(a) Creep curves and (b) Creep rate curves of Gr. 91 base metal, simulated specimens and specimen extracted from EB welded dissimilar weld joint tested at 873 K and 160 MPa.

Figure 8b illustrates the evolution of creep strain rate with time. All specimens displayed a characteristic primary or transient creep stage in which the creep rate decreases over time, followed by a tertiary or acceleration creep regime, where the creep rate increases after reaching the minimum creep rate. Among the simulated specimens, the ICHAZ specimen exhibits the highest creep rate evolution, followed by the FGHAZ, base metal, and finally, CGHAZ indicates highest resistance to creep deformation. The gradient in creep rate aligns with the observed creep life, indicating microstructural control over creep resistance. In terms of ductility, the CGHAZ specimen showed limited necking with a reduction in area of approximately 60%, whereas the FGHAZ and ICHAZ specimens exhibited comparatively higher ductility, with reductions in area around 90–91%. All specimens failed by ductile fracture, exhibiting the characteristic cup-and-cone feature. Figure 9 presents the fractographs of the tested samples. The FGHAZ and ICHAZ specimens exhibit very fine, uniformly distributed dimples and micro-voids. In contrast, the CGHAZ displays coarser and shallow dimples, consistent with the microstructural degradation expected after long-term thermal exposure.

Fractographs showing ductile fracture features for (a) Gr.91 base metal and Gleeble-simulated samples of (b) CGHAZ (c) FGHAZ, and (d) ICHAZ.

Figure 10 shows the fractured creep specimens, all of which failed within the gauge section, validating the effectiveness of the modified specimen design. The creep specimen design ensured that deformation occurred exclusively in the simulated region alone, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the results. Additionally, post-creep test diameter measurements of the specimen shoulders showed no deviation from the initial dimension of 8.3 mm (Fig. 1d). The same was observed even in the CGHAZ specimen, which endured for over 17,000 h, despite the presence of a soft ICHAZ region outside the gauge length.

Photographs of various Gleeble simulated creep ruptured specimens showing failure within gauge length.

Previous studies have attempted to assess creep behaviour using Gleeble-simulated specimens subjected to varying peak temperatures, either through small-scale testing methods29 or by employing standard uniaxial specimen designs9. However, small‑scale tests were inadequate in capturing complete deformation behaviour, while standard specimens, with a 30 mm gauge length inevitably encompassed both the intended peak temperature region and adjacent HAZ regions with progressively lower peak temperatures, leading to failure near the ICHAZ. In contrast, the modified specimen design adopted in the present study confined the gauge length entirely to the simulated region, thereby eliminating the influence of adjacent zones and enabling a reliable evaluation of localized creep properties.

The aforementioned observations on the creep behavior of individual HAZ regions emphasize the critical role of microstructural heterogeneity in governing creep resistance. Figure 11 shows a high-magnification image of HAZ regions, illustrating key microstructural features. As seen in Fig. 11b, the CGHAZ similar to base metal (Fig. 11a), displays carbide-decorated boundaries but with finer precipitates. Notably, the superior creep performance of the CGHAZ can be directly linked to its distinct microstructural features. This zone displayed a higher fraction of finer precipitates and a greater proportion of LAGBs (as shown in Fig. 6b), which collectively contributed to matrix strengthening. These observations align with reports in the literature, where researchers have observed the absence of carbides (in as-welded condition) and the presence of finer martensitic laths in the CGHAZ of weldments30–32. Remarkably, despite the sample being exposed to the peak temperature of 1473 K for only ~ 1 s, (a condition emulated in actual weld scenarios where exposure is limited to mere fractions of a second as discussed by Santosh et al.7) the resulting microstructure exhibited strengthened matrix that enhanced the creep life to approximately 17.5 times that of the base metal.

In (d),black arrows indicate carbide arrays at former grain boundaries, whileyellow-marked areas represent new grain boundaries lackingprecipitates. (e) Schematic microstructure resembling SEM view of BM(left) and ICHAZ (right).

In contrast, the FGHAZ and ICHAZ showed lower creep life. Under low heat input single-pass welding conditions, minimal microstructural degradation is generally expected, which is consistent when comparing the width of the region and precipitate sizes reported in multipass joints25,26. However, in the present study, Fig. 5c–f reveals that during the welding thermal cycle, new grains evolve and the boundaries shift due to phase transformation while the original locations of carbides remain unchanged. As seen in Fig. 11d, the ICHAZ region notably lacks precipitates on many grain boundaries (few marked in yellow). The precipitates in both FGHAZ and ICHAZ, are reported to be comparatively coarser and more spheroidal33, consistent with the present observations. These coarse carbides in FGHAZ/ICHAZ are believed to have originated from undissolved particles that survived the rapid thermal cycle during welding, and subsequently coarsened during PWHT, retaining their original positions. Black arrows in Fig. 11d highlight the retained carbide arrays marking former boundary locations.

This phenomenon is further illustrated schematically in Fig. 11e, which compares microstructural evolution from BM (left), where carbides decorate various boundaries (prior austenite grain boundaries, block and packet), to the ICHAZ (right), where new grains evolve following thermal cycling and lack precipitates even after PWHT. The absence of precipitates on the newly formed boundaries, which were initially present (and played a significant role in stabilising the sub-grain boundary structures during deformation), can no longer provide boundary hardening benefits. Importantly, the higher carbide circularity observed in the ICHAZ reflects reduced grain boundary pinning, as coarsened or sparsely distributed precipitates offer limited resistance to dislocation movement34. The combined effect of matrix softening, carbide coarsening and unpinned boundaries weakens the microstructure, thereby accelerating creep deformation in both FGHAZ and ICHAZ.

Figure 12 presents the SEM micrographs of the creep-ruptured base metal and Gleeble-simulated specimens (CGHAZ and ICHAZ). The microstructural assessment revealed that the base metal, FGHAZ, and ICHAZ samples did not show significant changes in precipitate size or morphology due to their comparatively short creep rupture lives. In contrast, the CGHAZ sample, which exhibited the longest rupture life (~ 17,700 h), showed pronounced coarsening and coalescence of M23C6 carbides (indicated by arrows). It must be noted that the tempered martensitic microstructure of Grade 91 steel is known to undergo internal recovery during long-term creep exposure, characterized by reduction in dislocation density, lath boundary annihilation, substructure formation, and progressive carbide coarsening as reported in prior studies34,35.

Post-creep microstructures showing significant M23​C6_carbide coarsening and coalescence (arrows) in the CGHAZ, while the base metal and ICHAZ exhibit only limited microstructural changes due to their shorter creep exposure.

Creep tests on Gleeble-simulated samples revealed a clear gradation in creep strength across the heat-affected zone. However, in an actual weld joint, these distinct microstructural regions are interconnected, forming a continuous transition zone within the HAZ. This spatial arrangement leads to complex interactions during creep deformation, making the overall creep behaviour of the weld joint more intricate than that of the individual regions. As evident from the creep results, the EB-DWJ exhibited a shorter creep life than both the simulated CGHAZ and the base metal. However, its creep life remained higher than that of the simulated FGHAZ and ICHAZ specimens under identical test conditions. Interestingly, the result is initially perplexing, as the weakest region of the EB-DWJ lies within the FGHAZ/ICHAZ, implying that the joint’s creep life should be comparable to that of the ICHAZ/FGHAZ simulated specimens. However, the relatively higher creep life of the weld joint can be attributed to the very narrow region of ICHAZ surrounded with stronger regions. Consequently, the constraint from surrounding stronger regions such as the CGHAZ/FGHAZ and BM, limits the deformation in soft ICHAZ, highlighting the complex stress redistribution and deformation behavior of the weld joint under high-temperature service conditions. Similar consequences of the constraint in retarding local deformation and improving apparent creep life have been reported in notched 9Cr-1Mo steel specimens, where stress triaxiality imposed by geometric constraint delayed creep damage accumulation36. Additionally, finite-element analysis on ferritic/austenitic DWJs have shown pronounced stress redistribution and triaxiality arising from the steep creep strength gradients between CGHAZ, ICHAZ and the weld metal37. Thus, ICHAZ in the weld joint accommodates strain gradually. Under prolonged exposure the ICHAZ in the weldment undergoes gradual microstructural degradation involving precipitate coarsening, void formation and their eventual linkage, culminating in failure. In contrast, the simulated specimens lack such mechanical constraint, allowing them to deform freely. Consequently, the failure life of the FGHAZ/ICHAZ simulated specimen is lower than that of the weld joint, further emphasizing the role of constraint effects in influencing the creep resistance of welded joints.

The aforementioned creep results emphasize the critical role of HAZ microstructures in determining the creep performance of welded joints. A thorough understanding of the mechanisms is essential for designing welds with enhanced creep resistance and ensuring long-term structural integrity in service. The study demonstrated Gr. 91 steel’s sensitivity to thermal cycles, even with low heat-input EB welding process. Microstructural changes, such as carbide coarsening and inadequate grain boundary strengthening, were identified as factors compromising creep strength. The employed creep specimen design enabled reliable evaluation of creep behaviour of various simulated regions, revealing distinct strength variations associated with underlying microstructural features. Such information also lays the groundwork for developing constitutive models that capture the creep behaviour of individual regions. These models can be integrated into finite element simulations to aid in predicting and optimizing the performance of welded joints under high-temperature service conditions.

Summary

The extremely narrow heat-affected zone (HAZ) of electron beam (EB) welded Grade 91 steel makes it challenging to evaluate the creep characteristics of individual HAZ sub-zones through mechanical testing. The present study employed Gleeble-based thermal simulations to replicate the characteristic microstructures of HAZ in an EB-welded Grade 91 joint, and the creep properties of these simulated HAZ microstructures were systematically examined at 873 K and 160 MPa. The key outcomes are summarized below:

i.Representative EB-weld thermal cycles were developed using Gleeble dilatometry by selecting appropriate peak temperatures to replicate the CGHAZ, FGHAZ, and ICHAZ microstructures in specimens suitable for reliable mechanical testing. The close correspondence between the simulated and actual HAZ microstructures was substantiated through hardness measurements and detailed microstructural observations.

ii.SEM and EBSD analyses revealed clear quantitative differences among the simulated HAZ regions.
