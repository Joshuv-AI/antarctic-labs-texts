Demystifying machine learning approaches in digital bone imaging using microCT and HRpQCT☆
Authors: Michael A. David, Kyle G. Williams, Evangelia P. Constantine, Julia Matthias, Virginia L. Ferguson, Douglas J. Adams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Micro-computed tomography (microCT) and high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HRpQCT) generate three-dimensional digital images capturing bone structure and quality. Radiomic analytical approaches applied to these images extract quantitative measures of bone microarchitecture (e.g., bone volume and density). Automating and interpreting radiomics data using conventional image analysis techniques (e.g., bone segmentation) and statistical approaches is often inadequate due to their limited capacity to accommodate complex, nonlinear relationships. These limitations are especially apparent in bone research when integrating imaging outcomes with results from complementary analytical methods (e.g., biomechanics and histology) and experimental factors (e.g., clinical data). Machine learning (ML) offers opportunities in bone research by leveraging powerful computational tools; for example, to enhance bone spatial resolution, accelerate digital image segmentation, and reveal hidden patterns and relationships within high-dimensional bone data. Insights into key ML model inputs, which may be interpreted as primary biological phenotypes or therapeutic targets, can be revealed using standard (i.e., parametric and non-parametric analyses) or advanced statistical methods (i.e., dimensionality reduction and data integration). Overall, this narrative review has three main objectives: (1) to introduce current applications of ML in preclinical and clinical bone research using microCT and HRpQCT; (2) synthesize the interconnectedness of the field of bone and machine learning through user-friendly scientometric and bibliometric analyses and visualization using our novel software called SciNetX; and (3) to provide an accessible, high-level understanding of how ML models are developed and interpreted. These elements aim to provide a foundational guide to incorporating ML into bone research using digital imaging techniques.

Introduction

Digital imaging of bone provides a valuable computational tool in studies of skeletal health and disease. Quantifying bone architecture using X-ray computed tomography (CT) enables imaging of larger volumetric regions in vivo or before destructive analyses, such as histological sectioning or mechanical testing. The adoption of micro-computed tomography (microCT) (Genant et al., 1999; Bouxsein et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2021; Kague et al., 2024) and high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HRpQCT) (Boutroy et al., 2005; Manske et al., 2015; Whittier et al., 2020a; Khosla et al., 2006; Burghardt et al., 2010; Samelson et al., 2019) has been critical in preclinical and clinical research for quantifying bone microarchitecture (e.g., cortical thickness, trabecular spacing, and bone density). The common application of radiomics, the extraction of quantitative features from biomedical images, can be utilized with standard and advanced image analysis for digital bone images. High-resolution digital images of bone obtained via microCT or HRpQCT, coupled with radiomics, provide computational tools capable of detecting subtle differences in bone microarchitecture, which improves interpretation, diagnosis, and treatment.

MicroCT and HRpQCT analyses present several analytical challenges: (1) constraints in spatial resolution; (2) variability in how bone regions of interest are selected for quantitation; (3) difficulty translating a large number of microarchitecture parameters into actionable disease insights; and (4) complexity in statistically evaluating interrelated parameters, particularly when integrating them with clinical or biological variables such as age or biomarkers. Quantitative digital bone imaging data are often evaluated using traditional statistical hypothesis testing (e.g., parametric and nonparametric testing), which increases the risk of both type 1 and type 2 errors when making multiple comparisons with increased numbers of knowingly, or potentially, interrelated parameters (Coulombe et al., 2021a; Neeteson et al., 2024; Albers, 2019; Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016). Thus, alternative analytical methods are needed to overcome the challenges of current strategies for analyzing digital images of bone with microCT and HRpQCT.

Machine learning (ML) is a broad computational field and a set of tools within artificial intelligence (Fig. 1A) that focuses on “teaching” computers to automate tasks and uncover complex patterns without explicit human programming. Deep learning is a subset of ML (Fig. 1A) that is inspired by the interconnected nature of human neurons and often referred to as convolutional neural networks. While ML and deep learning are related, they differ in their data requirements, computational costs, use cases, and limitations. Compared to ML, deep learning: (1) requires minimal human feature engineering (e.g., models learn edges and shapes of bone from full images instead of a user-defined region); (2) excels at two- and three-dimensional image-based tasks (e.g., bone segmentation) rather than tabular data; (3) often requires more samples; and (4) is prone to overfitting small datasets. Although these are critical distinctions, direct comparison between ML and deep learning is outside the scope of this review. ML can help analyze multi-dimensional and non-linear datasets, such as radiomic measures from digital bone imaging combined with related biological and clinical factors, in an unbiased manner. Furthermore, ML methods require minimal assumptions about the underlying data structure and do not rely on explicitly programmed rules. While ML models may be linear or non-linear in their mathematical structure, they do not require strict assumptions about the underlying data distribution, such as normality and heteroscedasticity, unlike traditional parametric and non-parametric statistical comparisons, or user-defined values in radiomic pipelines. For example, traditional digital bone image radiomics often rely on the assumptions that bone microarchitectural parameters are statistically independent, are normally distributed, and vary equally across groups (Coulombe et al., 2021a; Neeteson et al., 2024). Likewise, traditional bone radiomics requires programming steps via human-defined image processing, such as selecting numerical thresholds (e.g., a grayscale value) and utilizing numerical techniques to assist in extracting desired information (e.g., bone “dilation and peel” or applying a Gaussian filter to suppress noise) (Bouxsein et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2021; Kague et al., 2024; Whittier et al., 2020a; Burghardt et al., 2010). Taken together, ML might enable agnostic, automated analysis that accelerates discovery and improves clinical bone diagnosis and treatment monitoring.

Despite the potential of the rapidly evolving field of ML, most bone researchers have limited knowledge of mathematical theories and computational programming for developing and deploying models, difficulty interpreting model outcomes, and a lack of guidelines and awareness of accessible tools. Although significant information is available in the public domain, particularly with the rise of large language ML models (e.g., Deepseek, ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini), the sheer breadth of tools, models, metrics, and interpretability measures creates a steep learning curve. The time-consuming, arduous learning process can be a significant barrier to entry for bone researchers seeking to explore and adopt ML approaches. A holistic narrative review that guides researchers through the diversity and complexity of ML would improve the adoption of ML strategies and their integration into the routine workflows of bone research. Additionally, there is no quantitative snapshot in time on the body of scientific literature of ML as it relates to digital bone imaging using microCT and HRpQCT, warranting a complementary bibliometric and scientometric analysis.

Overall, this narrative review, coupled with our novel bibliometric and scientometric pipeline and visualization software, termed SciNetX (RRID:SCR_028186), highlights and provides practical initial guidance on ML approaches for digital bone imaging using microCT and HRpQCT. This narrative review includes a mixture of unpublished and synthetic data generated for educational and demonstrational purposes; the distinction between the two is noted in the manuscript, figure captions, and table legends. To facilitate a focused discussion, this review is structured in the following way to mimic a typical bone research question and an analytical pipeline assisted with ML (Fig. 1B):(1)synthetic enhancement of image spatial resolution via ML super-resolution models;(2)automatic region of interest and bone segmentation in digital images via ML segmentation models;(3)generate a large number of radiomic parameters of bone microarchitecture and then reduce the number to identify the most important ones via advanced statistical dimensionality reduction and data integration;(4)develop and understand the outcome of ML prediction, classification, and clustering models to uncover biologically and clinically relevant insights;(5)consider bone-specific technical and computational resources when developing ML models.

Overview of ML approaches

Typical applications of ML, irrespective of bone imaging data, involve an iterative training process leading to an ML “model” (Fig. 1C), whereby mathematical algorithms “learn” from data by adjusting the contribution (e.g., weight) of the tested connections based on performance feedback (i.e., model predictions and error magnitudes). ML models are commonly developed using approaches of supervised learning, unsupervised learning, dimensionality reduction, and data integration (Fig. 1D). In supervised learning, models are trained on input data with a known output. Supervised learning is ideal for tasks related to super resolution (i.e., improving spatial resolution), classification (e.g., distinguishing between osteoporotic bone and healthy bone), and prediction (e.g., bone strength from digital images). For context, one of the most fundamental supervised learning approaches for prediction is linear regression, which many readers are accustomed to. Linear regression fits a line to data points (on two-dimensional x-y coordinates) to obtain a regression coefficient that describes a biological phenomenon. The fitted linear regression line is now a “model” that can be used as a prediction tool. However, linear regression is often used only for descriptive modeling (i.e., focused solely on inference from the current dataset) and lacks inherent “learning” or metrics typical of ML training and prediction. For classification, a standard ML model that many readers might know of is logistic regression. In contrast to supervised learning, unsupervised learning groups or clusters data by similar patterns using labeled input (e.g., human distal radius bone volume and density) and unlabeled output (e.g., learned ML-derived patient clusters). Dimensionality reduction, such as principal component analysis (PCA), and data integration, such as canonical correlation analysis (CCA), also help to reduce the number of inputs and identify radiomic or clinical data of high importance for analyses or ML models. While these dimensionality reduction and data integration techniques can also be considered ML approaches, in this review, they are referred to as advanced statistical methods. They reduce the number of inputs by transforming the data into a new set of variables that preserve the key sources of variability (e.g., converting a 50-column Excel document into only three key transformed columns), making patterns easier to visualize and interpret. An example is reducing the number of interrelated trabecular parameters (e.g., thickness, volume, spacing) across multiple treatment groups (e.g., bone anabolic agents and/or loading stimuli). This narrative review covers topics across these ML approaches (Fig. 1E), each with its own unique model structure and outcomes, and uses a systematic approach to selecting publications.

Systematic approach for selecting publications to review

A focused and systematic PubMed search up to July 31st, 2025, was conducted using the terms bone, machine learning, deep learning, U-Net, principal component analysis, and canonical correlation analysis, combined with various spellings of microCT and HRpQCT. PubMed search results were exported as CSV files, merged and deduplicated using Python, and then manually reviewed (See Supplementary Material and GitHub for the exact search files and code). Publications were manually excluded for 4 reasons: 1) were conference proceedings or preprints; 2) used ML for data not involving digital bone imaging; 3) did not use models in the context of training and testing models (e.g., descriptive statistics only); and 4) did not involve a microCT or HRpQCT scanner. Ultimately, this search yielded an initial count of 382 unique publications from PubMed, after which 140 peer-reviewed publications were included in the narrative review. The review included preclinical and clinical studies, in vivo and ex vivo bone imaging, and a wide range of species and ML models. Additional non-search-term-related bone imaging publications were included to highlight guidelines for bone imaging and radiomic methods, clinical predictive analytics, and open-source code, packages, datasets, and community resources.

Scientometric and bibliometric analytical pipeline and visualization software: SciNetX

Scientometric and bibliometric analyses are emerging quantitative approaches to highlight a body of scientific literature and its network of articles, including co-citations, co-authors, co-occurrence of topic words, funding agencies, and origin countries, and how these are connected and temporally projected (Donthu et al., 2021). Such a holistic, quantitative, and visual approach enables the identification of emerging trends, topic areas, and collaborations and has been used to explore orthopedic conditions (Bao et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023) and bone imaging (Xiang et al., 2024). Scientiometric and bibliometric analyses involve programming and software for querying publicly available databases of scientific literature and metadata (e.g., OpenAlex, PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Knowledge). Although prior open-source software and packages exist, such as BERTopic (Grootendorst, 2022), Bibliometrix (RRID:SCR_023744) (Aria and Cuccurullo, 2017), Citespace (RRID:SCR_025121) (Chen, 2006), VOSviewer (RRID:SCR_023516) (van Eck and Waltman, 2010), ScientoPy (Ruiz-Rosero et al., 2019), and PyBibX (Pereira et al., 2025), they collectively have several limitations in user-focused adaptability: they either require local software installation needing programming experience, are limited in their ability to scale with cloud computing for large datasets, or provide minimal support for expanded analyses using custom key terms and categories (e.g., the umbrella terms, species, and ML models in this review). Further, they are limited in their ability to offer a comprehensive bibliometric and scientometric analysis, supplemented by the open-source OpenAlex database (https://openalex.org) (Priem et al., 2022) and the Research Organization Registry (https://ror.org), with extensive user-modifiable analyses and visualizations across multiple platforms (Macintosh, Windows, and cloud computing). Additionally, the umbrella terms used to structure this review (i.e., super-resolution, segmentation, dimensionality reduction, data integration, prediction, classification, clustering, and interpretability) do not consistently appear across PubMed (or other databases). As such, it was challenging to use the available open-source software for bibliometrics and scientometrics for this structured narrative review. Consequently, we developed a novel Python-based scientometric and bibliometric analytical and visualization software, termed SciNetX (Fig. 2), to assist this review and add to the emerging collection of available tools. SciNetX, which requires minimal setup and clicks from a researcher, is source-available under a distributed license that ensures transparency and rigor and supports both non-profit and for-profit pursuits. SciNetX (v1.0.0) uncovered a breadth of interconnected, dispersed manuscripts on digital bone imaging using ML and advanced statistical approaches, thereby forming vast research networks (Fig. 3; Table 1). Since the early 2000s, there has been a steady increase in publications applying ML and advanced statistical approaches to digital images from microCT and HRpQCT, with a noteworthy increase around 2010 (Fig. 3A). Key bibliometric statistics identified a constant growth of papers with a high level of collaborations spanning multiple countries, and institutions supported by various funding sources (Fig. 3B; Table 1). Two thematic concepts emerged as expected based on the structure of this narrative review: 1) ex vivo, microCT, animals/mouse, and PCA; and 2) Humans, HRpQCT, females, and bone density. The PCA thematic term was in concordance with the early, steady use of PCA in publications. Citation coupling identified relationships between current work and prior citations, as well as a historical perspective. Emerging research trends included animals, deep learning, bone density, and females, whereas waning or absent trends included ML and osteoporosis. Among the analytic measures, there was notable variation in the terminology and spelling of microCT, HRpQCT, ML, and deep learning, highlighting the lack of consensus on terminology for digital bone imaging and ML. Ultimately, the variability in terminology and spelling of similar concepts/terms underscored the need to add custom terms (e.g., review-specific ML terms, species, modality, models) to this review's SciNetX analytical pipeline. Enhancing digital bone image analysis with ML: applications in microCT and HRpQCT

Synthetic enhancement of image spatial resolution via ML super-resolution models

Despite the clinical benefits of volumetric visualization and quantification via high-resolution CT imaging, its use is limited due to the inherent risks associated with cumulative X-ray exposure. As a result, the spatial resolution achievable with current CT technology is rarely fully leveraged due to prohibitive radiation exposure, motion artifacts, and scan duration. An application of a supervised ML approach, known as super-resolution, has shown promise in generating computationally “synthetic” CT images to improve spatial resolution (Fig. 4A). Super-resolution models are trained on spatially aligned image sets, where low-resolution images result from shorter scans and high-resolution images from longer, higher-quality acquisitions. Super-resolution models establish a mathematical connection between low- and high-resolution images. This mathematical linkage can be applied to new images acquired at low resolution to create (synthetically) similar images at high resolution. Super-resolution models have been applied to microCT images (Chan and Rajapakse, 2023; Leuliet et al., 2022; Devine et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2022; Puel et al., 2025; Guo et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Tolgyesi et al., 2025; Gillet et al., 2025) and used to generate images with microCT-like resolution from clinical CT scanners (excluding HRpQCT) (Ge et al., 2024; Frazer et al., 2024; Ahmadian et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2024). One published study has used a super-resolution approach on HRpQCT images alone through upsampling (Angermann et al., 2024). Most published studies use super-resolution models based on convolutional neural network frameworks that aim to capture the spatial relationships between bone tissues and pixels in images. Specifically, there were various custom forms of convolutional neural networks (e.g., different layer structures and numbers). The most common model framework used was U-Net, whether used alone or as part of a larger framework. Notably, the U-Net framework was originally designed for nucleus segmentation in microscopy images (Ronneberger et al., 2015), demonstrating its utility and making it a potential initial architecture of choice for bone images. In general, most super-resolution model accuracies were ~ 80–90% across specific ML metrics (e.g., structural similarity index measure). In our unpublished work, we have demonstrated the utility of super-resolution models using the U-Net framework applied to microCT images of excised mouse femurs and HRpQCT images of the human cadaveric forearm (Fig. 4B and C; Table 3, Table 4; ongoing/unpublished data). In any application of super-resolution models, differences between the actual high-resolution images and the synthesized images need to be evaluated. In these examples (Fig. 4; ongoing/unpublished data), the synthesized resolution successfully qualitatively revealed small spatial features, such as porosities, that were not captured at lower-resolution imaging. The synthetic image can reveal subtle details that may otherwise be missed or filtered out in the lower-resolution images during bone segmentation and analysis. However, some desirable attributes within the as-acquired images may be compromised, such as the pixel-level grayscale values calibrated to bone density. Likewise, if spatial features are not captured adequately in the lower-resolution images, the probability of being reproduced or recovered is diminished (Fig. 4D; Table 3, Table 4; ongoing/unpublished data). Automatic region of interest and bone segmentation in digital images via ML segmentation models

Region of interest (ROI) selection typically involves delineating bone types and compartments within a digital image, such as separating trabecular and cortical bone (Fig. 5A) or isolating the trabecular compartment of the vertebral body (Fig. 5B). While for demonstration purposes the trabecular compartment is highlighted, segmenting the cortical bone is important and can be problematic in areas of thin or porous bone often experienced with HRpQCT images. The approach to selecting an ROI and separating bone from background and other tissues is referred to as image segmentation. Image segmentation is a key step in quantifying radiomic parameters of bone microarchitecture, and inaccurate segmentation can skew the final quantitative outcomes. Unfortunately, segmentation is often labor-intensive and is susceptible to variability stemming from the need for subjective manual segmentation. Traditional rule-based image analysis (e.g., Otsu thresholding with a specific Hounsfield Unit) and filtering steps (e.g., bone “dilation and peel” or applying a Gaussian filter to suppress noise) can bypass labor-intensive manual segmentation by using an automated script (Bouxsein et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2021; Kague et al., 2024; Whittier et al., 2020a; Burghardt et al., 2010). While rule-based algorithms for segmentation are often available, they can fail in complex bone morphologies and areas of bone loss. In contrast, ML models using a supervised learning approach aim to achieve automated segmentation for both challenging and straightforward bone segmentations (Fig. 5C). To train ML bone segmentation models, the input is the raw CT image, and the output is the bone-segmented image. Additionally, ML segmentation models can improve accuracy through pre- and post-processing steps (e.g., removing spurious pixels) of the images. Lee et al., 2025). Many of these models often use convolutional neural networks (Sapkota et al., 2025; Motch Perrine et al., 2025; Lagzouli et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Neeteson et al., 2025), or specifically the U-Net framework and derivatives (Rytky et al., 2021; Mahdi et al., 2023; Grass et al., 2024; Baltruschat et al., 2021; Madireddy and Wu, 2022; Leger et al., 2022; Caron et al., 2023; Hussain et al., 2021; Battista et al., 2023; Buss et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2024; Sapkota et al., 2025; A.H. Lee et al., 2025; Yu et al., 2025; Mangano et al., 2025; Folle et al., 2021; Neeteson et al., 2023; Y. Lee et al., 2025; Neeteson et al., 2025). Others used more complex segmentation using unsupervised ML clustering approaches (i.e., fuzzy c-means (Dunmore et al., 2018; Shiraishi et al., 2021; Westhauser et al., 2015; Pinto et al., 2020) and k-means (Benz et al., 2024)). Regardless of the network, most achieved high accuracy according to ML metrics (~80–90% via Dice-Sørensen coefficient). U-Net continues to demonstrate utility across ML approaches. Our unpublished work has demonstrated the success of ML with U-Net in segmenting the trabecular bone of the mouse vertebral body within microCT images (Fig. 5D; Table 3, Table 4; ongoing/unpublished data). Generally, the segmentations are accurate (~90%); however, anomalous anatomical areas, such as missing or thin cortex and spurious segmentations in the wrong regions, tend to decrease the model's accuracy. Improvements to these issues can be achieved by applying common pre- or post-processing steps, such as image filtering or analytical cropping of the image. Overall, ML segmentation models can enhance speed and precision in acquiring radiomic parameters of bone microarchitecture from microCT and HRpQCT.

Generating radiomic parameters of bone microarchitecture via standard and advanced methods

Once images are acquired and the bone ROI has been segmented, generating quantitative data indicative of bone health is the next critical step using microCT and HRpQCT. To generate quantitative data, researchers often employ radiomics methods, which are techniques to extract detailed quantitative information from digital bone images to assess bone health (Bouxsein et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2021; Kague et al., 2024; Whittier et al., 2020a; Burghardt et al., 2010; Y. Lee et al., 2025; S. Lu et al., 2023; Shirvaikar et al., 2016; Rytky et al., 2020; Valentinitsch et al., 2013; Abidin et al., 2017; Basran et al., 2022). Radiomics is a broad term, encompassing methods, such as standard and advanced image analysis, including ML, that focus on extracting quantitative features from medical images. Radiomic parameters of bone microarchitecture are assessed through standardized parameters, such as classic image shape features (e.g., bone volume or trabecular thickness) and intensity-based features (e.g., calibrated bone density) (Bouxsein et al., 2010; Kim et al., 2021; Kague et al., 2024; Whittier et al., 2020a; Burghardt et al., 2010). More advanced parameters, including image texture and contrast (Y. Lee et al., 2025; S. Lu et al., 2023; Shirvaikar et al., 2016; Rytky et al., 2020; Valentinitsch et al., 2013; Abidin et al., 2017; Basran et al., 2022), provide image-based descriptions of the spatial and mineralized heterogeneity of bone. For context, although not obtained using CT or ML, the clinical trabecular bone score (TBS) obtained from dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) utilizes texture algorithms to quantify the structural connectivity of trabecular bone from planar projection radiography (Silva et al., 2014; Rajan et al., 2020).

Reducing the number of radiomic parameters quantifying bone microarchitecture via advanced statistics of dimensionality reduction and data integration

MicroCT and HRpQCT produce extensive radiomic data on bone microarchitecture, which, combined with clinical data, can overwhelm researchers seeking clear biological or clinical insights. Computationally, multiple parameters derived from each type of data can be considered an independent “dataset” matched to each subject/animal/sample, resulting in parameters exhibiting potentially inherent redundancy and colinearity. Advanced statistical approaches of dimensionality reduction and data integration can streamline (Table 2) analyses by simplifying large datasets and facilitating clearer interpretations, either alone or as preprocessing steps for ML models (e.g., prediction, classification, and clustering).

PCA is the most common advanced statistical technique used to reduce the number of independent variables, or dimensionality, that similarly describe a dependent variable. Such “dimensionality reduction” is typically applied to a single dataset to preserve critical information and variance while simplifying the visualization and interpretation of relationships (Fig. 6A). The dataset can include data from multiple modalities and sources (e.g., bone radiomics and biomechanics). Although researchers often refer to combining multiple sources of data as integration, here we specifically refer to advanced statistical methods that simultaneously merge datasets at the quantitative and correlative levels. Data integration techniques, such as CCA, statistically merge multiple independent datasets (e.g., imaging data with clinical data) to uncover cross-dataset relationships and complementary information that might be obscured if the datasets were analyzed separately (Fig. 6B). Examples of data integration used in clinical care and research of osteoporosis, which are not based on CCA, are the FRAX (Kanis et al., 2009, Kanis et al., 2011, Kanis et al., 2024; Jaiswal et al., 2025) and μFRAC (Whittier et al., 2023) models of fracture risk assessment. These fracture risk assessments use DXA scores (FRAX) and HRpQCT bone microarchitecture (μFRAC), along with patient demographic data (e.g., parental history of fragility fracture), to estimate a 10-year probability of fracture risk (FRAX) and the use of simulated bone strength to predict a categorical probability of fracture risk (μFRAC).Fig. 6Advanced statistical approaches of dimensionality reduction and data integration help to reduce the number of quantitative bone microarchitectural radiomic parameters in digital images from microCT and HRpQCT. Dimensionality reduction techniques have been used extensively with microCT images of bones from humans (Baba et al., 2003; Giesen et al., 2003; Hakulinen et al., 2006; Kupczik and Hublin, 2010; Roque et al., 2013; Faccia et al., 2014; Gong et al., 2016; Scherf et al., 2016; Hutchinson et al., 2017; Watson et al., 2018; Hudson et al., 2020; Bosman et al., 2020; Al-Barghouthi et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2021; Ajami et al., 2022; Day et al., 2022; Thornton et al., 2024; Lukova et al., 2025) and a range of species (Coulombe et al., 2021a; Devine et al., 2022; Motch Perrine et al., 2025; Lukova et al., 2025; Morgan et al., 2009; McDougall et al., 2009; Yue et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2012; Percival et al., 2016; Lefebvre et al., 2016; Palci et al., 2016; Robinson and Terhune, 2017; Vidal-Garcia and Scott Keogh, 2017; Martig et al., 2018; Komo and Grassberger, 2018; Pogoda and Kupfer, 2018; Ivanovic and Arntzen, 2018; Farina et al., 2019; Coutel et al., 2019; Westeen et al., 2020; Crawford et al., 2020; Scarano et al., 2020; Chang et al., 2021; Schwarze et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2022; Migotsky et al., 2022; Berio et al., 2022; Farman et al., 2023; Van Ankum et al., 2024; Murata et al., 2024; Ince et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2025), and less commonly with HRpQCT images of human bone (Neeteson et al., 2024; Vico et al., 2008; Watson et al., 2011; Bui et al., 2013; Kamer et al., 2016; Durosier-Izart et al., 2017; Shaw et al., 2018; Chandran et al., 2019; Ahrend et al., 2021; Fujii et al., 2021), to visualize and compare bone data. Generally, these use cases include reduced representation of bone morphometry as geometric inputs to non-ML models (e.g., finite element models) and the visualization and comparison of bone morphometry and radiomic parameters between groups. Other publications have used reduced data as a prior (preprocessing) step in standard statistical and ML models (Rytky et al., 2020; Fujii et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2017; Kochetkova et al., 2023; Scholtes et al., 2022; Coulombe et al., 2021b; Vilayphiou et al., 2010, Vilayphiou et al., 2011; Bevers et al., 2021). Publications on data integration techniques are limited, with a few studies using canonical variant analysis (CVA) (Farman et al., 2023; Van Ankum et al., 2024; Murata et al., 2024). CVA is a technique similar to CCA in that it integrates data to identify data separation by incorporating the group label. Often, interpreting and visualizing the results of dimensionality reduction and data integration involves visualization using contributions and biplots. Reduced data can be further evaluated by inspecting the specific input contributions of the original data using standard statistical regression models without any ML training (Hakulinen et al., 2006; Gong et al., 2016; Morgan et al., 2009; Martig et al., 2018; Bui et al., 2013; Fujii et al., 2021). Typically, the number of reduced components chosen for PCA, CCA, and CVA is determined when the components account for 80% of the data's variance. Overall, advanced statistical dimensionality reduction and data integration approaches enable simultaneous assessment of multiple datasets and types, identifying patterns that would be difficult to uncover when evaluating traditional single-dataset analyses. Despite their availability, dimensionality reduction and data integration are among the most underutilized techniques in bone research, and we encourage bone researchers to consider leveraging these methods alongside traditional statistical analyses.

ML prediction and classification models to study and diagnose bone diseases

Some of the most actively explored ML applications seek to predict and classify bone pathologies using both radiomic features and clinical factors (e.g., age, sex, weight). Prediction (Fig. 7A) and classification (Fig. 7B) are considered ML approaches with supervised learning, using inputs of images or tabular data to generate the output of a prediction (e.g., bone strength) or classification (e.g., healthy or osteoporotic bone or mild versus high risk of fracture). Although the terms prediction and classification are often used interchangeably, prediction models estimate continuous numerical variables, whereas classification models assign data to categorical variables. Further, the underlying mathematics and metrics of prediction and classification models differ. Predictive models are usually evaluated by comparing actual versus estimated numerical outputs using regression-specific metrics (Fig. 7C; synthetic data for demonstration). Classification models are typically evaluated based on their ability to correctly assign cases to predefined categories, with the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) being the most frequently reported measure (Fig. 7D; synthetic data for demonstration). Model accuracies are evaluated using standard regression-based metrics, including R-squared (R2) and error magnitude metrics (e.g., mean absolute error [MAE] and root mean squared error [RMSE]). The interpretability of prediction models encompasses feature importance, particularly through statistical approaches, which help uncover key biological insights. (B) The general approach to ML classification models is a supervised ML approach, where the input is tabular data of numbers or images, and numerical predictions are the output. Model accuracies are evaluated using standard regression-based metrics, including the area under the receiver operating characteristic curves (AUC) and F1 scores for precision and recall. Publications use ML prediction and classification models across a wide range of applications with digital bone images from microCT and HRpQCT. ML prediction models have been developed to predict the effects of mechanical loading superimposed on bone loss associated with aging (Asgharzadeh et al., 2020). Other prediction models have estimated bone strength using PCA-reduced bone radiomics from microCT images via stepwise regression (Shirvaikar et al., 2016; Morgan et al., 2009) and using HRpQCT images as inputs to convolutional neural networks (Y. Lu et al., 2023). Additional models have utilized synthetically generated DXA images derived from two-dimensional projected microCT images to predict microarchitectural parameters (Xiao et al., 2020). Generally, these image-based ML prediction model frameworks used custom convolutional neural networks with similar accuracies (80–90% via R2 values). ML classification models have employed raw microCT and HRpQCT images themselves (i.e., without explicit determination of radiomic microarchitectural parameters) and derived bone radiomic measures to classify fracture type and risk (S. Lu et al., 2023; Valentinitsch et al., 2013; Basran et al., 2022; Jaiswal et al., 2025; Whittier et al., 2023; Kochetkova et al., 2023; Bevers et al., 2021; Atkinson et al., 2012; Korfiatis et al., 2018; Edwards et al., 2016), bone quality (e.g., osteoporotic vs. healthy) (Coulombe et al., 2021a, Coulombe et al., 2021b; Yu et al., 2025; Y. Lee et al., 2025; Abidin et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2013; Folle et al., 2022; Rakowski et al., 2020; Ritter et al., 2024), and embryonic (Heidrich et al., 2013), deer antler (Lefebvre et al., 2016), and root canal (Wu et al., 2024) bone identification. Additional classification applications have focused on conditions such as classifying osteoarthritis severity from microCT contrast-enhanced texture of cartilage (Rytky et al., 2020) or structure of subchondral trabecular bone (Dorraki et al., 2022). Other classification models assign scores or grades to the degree of motion artifacts captured during HRpQCT imaging (Walle et al., 2023; Benedikt et al., 2024). Generally, the types of ML model frameworks used for classification included logistic regression, support vector machine, forest-based, and convolutional neural networks, with similar accuracies (~70–90%) often evaluated via AUC. Although the breadth of prediction and classification models is growing, a significant gap remains: ML applications for predicting or classifying bone response to treatment. These studies are likely limited by the number of patients required to capture variability in clinical outcomes. As ML prediction and classification models mature, they offer new potential for translating digital bone imaging into clinically meaningful tools for predicting fracture risk and personalizing patient care.

Unlike supervised methods, ML clustering uses input data consisting of variables without predefined output labels, such as indices quantifying bone microarchitecture (Fig. 8A). Clustering is often preceded by dimensionality reduction and data integration. The choice between linear and non-linear methods and between two- and higher-dimensional analyses fundamentally shapes how patterns and clusters are revealed and interpreted (Fig. 8B; synthetic data for demonstration). While most studies visualize clusters in two dimensions and are often color-coded by experimental groups (e.g., clinical data), analysis in three- or N-dimensional spaces may reveal subtle or nuanced patterns that are overlooked in simplified representations.

A challenge in cluster analysis is determining the optimal number of clusters. Increasing the number of clusters enhances sensitivity to subtle biological differences but complicates interpretability. Conversely, too few clusters risk oversimplifying the data and obscuring important biological signals. Tools such as the elbow plot help to objectively guide an appropriate number of clusters by visually assessing the cluster compactness, with the “elbow” or bend indicating the ideal number of computational clusters (Fig. 8C; synthetic data for demonstration). Other metrics (e.g., Silhouette score and Rand Index) help to quantify how clusters separate from one another and, if desired, how well the assigned cluster is related to the original experimental grouping or conditions (Table 2).

Once clusters are defined using standard metrics, interpreting their biological relevance typically involves visualization and statistically comparing the features of the original dataset across clusters (see Section 5.7, ML model interpretability or explainability; Table 2). This step bridges computational clustering results with meaningful biological or clinical insights, driving hypothesis generation and data interpretation. Overall, ML clustering coupled with dimensionality reduction and data integration is a powerful approach in bone research for uncovering novel groupings, hidden sources of variation, and new hypotheses from microCT and HRpQCT images.

ML model interpretability or explainability

Understanding how ML models arrive at an estimation, often referred to as interpretability or explainability, is essential for evaluating and trusting ML models' outcomes. In particular, understanding the biological underpinnings of these models' outcomes will help translate their inner workings into actionable disease insights. For instance, interpretability enables a researcher or clinician to identify HRpQCT radiomic parameters or image hotspots, in combination with clinical factors, that are critical for ML models predicting bone health and disease. Beyond the model input, understanding the directionality and magnitude is a key aspect of interpretability; for example, knowing that not only trabecular thickness and body weight, but also low trabecular thickness and high body weight, are associated with adverse disease outcomes. ML interpretability will be critical as the number of bone radiomic model inputs and non-traditional measures increases, alongside other data (e.g., clinical factors). While this “black box” may seem daunting or full of unknowns, techniques can shed light on them, aiming to unlock the translational value of interpretable ML in bone research.

Approaches to ML interpretability used primarily with prediction and classification models are grounded in the models' inherent structure, combined with statistical and visual techniques (Fig. 9; Table 2). Specific ML prediction and classification models have interpretability built directly into their structure. Example models include linear and logistic regression with their coefficients and tree-based models with their tree nodes/splits. Models without inherent transparency can use statistical approaches by quantifying the impact and magnitude of each input on the model's predictions. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) (Lundberg and Lee, 2017), for instance, is a statistical method that reveals the weighted “impact” of each feature, indicating its contribution to the ML model's accuracy (Fig. 9A; synthetic data for demonstration). In bone research, SHAP can reveal which radiomic parameters of bone microarchitecture (e.g., calibrated density) and their magnitudes (e.g., high vs. low density) influence model outcomes and accuracy. Other statistical approaches to determining feature impact and importance, without considering feature magnitude (e.g., high density) like SHAP, include Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (Ribeiro et al., 2016). For image-based models, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Folle et al., 2022; Selvaraju et al., 2017) provides visual explanations by showing which regions of an image the model focuses on for its predictions, such as anatomical hotspots for classifying osteoporotic bone (Fig. 9B; synthetic data for demonstration). ML clustering models are among the most challenging to interpret, as they require translating computational groupings into biologically and clinically relevant insights. ML computational considerations and open-source resources and communities for bone microCT and HRpQCT

Developing and deploying ML models for bone imaging involves practical considerations, including programming language selection and computational resources, especially when working with large three-dimensional imaging datasets from microCT and HRpQCT. Bone researchers commonly utilize one of four programming languages and computing environments for ML: Dragonfly (RRID:SCR_025150), MATLAB (RRID:SCR_001622), Python (RRID:SCR_008394), and R (RRID:SCR_001905). Each has strengths depending on the type of ML task. For instance, Dragonfly, Python, or MATLAB are preferred for handling three-dimensional imaging data due to their strong support for image processing, whereas R is favored for tabular data and statistical modeling. Unlike Python, R, and Dragonfly, MATLAB requires a paid software license, which can limit accessibility and collaboration. While personal computers can handle smaller computational tasks, larger microCT or HRpQCT imaging datasets often require dedicated hardware (e.g., local powerful computers, servers, or cloud-based services). Notably, a useful computational platform for researchers with limited computational resources is Google CoLab (RRID:SCR_018009). Although graphical processing unit (GPU)-accelerated computing can dramatically speed up image-related tasks, the computer's random-access memory (RAM) capacity can limit data loading and processing, especially in memory-intensive tasks, such as manipulating three-dimensional bone imaging data.

Published work has generally used the aforementioned programming languages to interact with open-source tools for preprocessing, ML development, bone radiomics, and visualization. For instance, standard Python open-source packages include MONAI (Cardoso et al., 2022), NumPy (RRID:SCR_008633) (Harris et al., 2020), NiBabel (RRID:SCR_002498) (Brett et al., 2020), nnUNET (Isensee et al., 2021), OpenCV (RRID:SCR_015526) (Bradski, 2000), Pandas (RRID:SCR_018214) (Mckinney, 2010), SciPy (RRID:SCR_008058) (Virtanen et al., 2020), scikit-learn (RRID:SCR_002577) (Abraham et al., 2014), SimpleITK (RRID:SCR_024693) (Lowekamp et al., 2013), h5py (RRID:SCR_024812) (Collette et al., 2025), Segmentation Models PyTorch (GitHub, 2019), PyTorch (RRID:SCR_018536) (Paszke et al., 2019), and TensorFlow (RRID:SCR_016345) (Abadi et al., 2015). Bone-related Python open-source packages include ORMIR_XCT (Kuczynski et al., 2024), Pyradiomics (RRID:SCR_026019) (van Griethuysen et al., 2017), and ITK (RRID:SCR_001149) with ITKIOScanco (Yoo et al., 2002). Additional open-source software, such as BoneJ (RRID:SCR_018166) (Doube et al., 2010), ITK-SNAP (RRID:SCR_002010) (Yushkevich and Gerig, 2017), 3D Slicer (RRID:SCR_005619) (Fedorov et al., 2012), and Dragonfly (Battista et al., 2023; Buss et al., 2023; Xi et al., 2024; Mangano et al., 2025), provides frameworks for consistently quantifying radiomic measures of bone microarchitecture across studies and research groups. In the future, developing standardized, transparent workflows for converting and moving data while preserving metadata and image pixel information (e.g., 16-bit integers vs uint8) between proprietary scanner software and open-source platforms will be essential. Open-source tools, while critical, highlight the challenges of reproducibility due to constantly evolving package versions and computing environments (e.g., local Macintosh vs. Windows vs. cloud computing). Python-based tools, such as the UV package manager (GitHub, 2024) and Docker (RRID:SCR_016445) (Docker, Inc., 2024), help with version control. It is highly recommended that researchers be transparent about the package and version used when disseminating work to enable reproducible science. Ultimately, the choice of programming language, libraries and packages, and computational setup depends on the specific ML model, dataset, and task.

Open-source microCT and HRpQCT datasets, normative curves/references, and community-led initiatives will enable standardized benchmarking and reproducibility in ML-based bone research. Proper attribution to the original code (Coulombe et al., 2021a; Neeteson et al., 2025; Rytky et al., 2020; Rakowski et al., 2020; Dorraki et al., 2022) and data generators/repositories is essential to acknowledge contributions and maintain transparency (Coulombe et al., 2021a; Devine et al., 2022; Hussain et al., 2021; Xi et al., 2024; Robinson and Terhune, 2017; Yan et al., 2017; Rakowski et al., 2020; Burt et al., 2016; Whittier et al., 2020b; Gabel et al., 2017; Quintiens et al., 2024; Rowe et al., 2018). While these studies are a strong start to open-access datasets, there remains a paucity of such datasets or dedicated repositories. Further, open access to bone imaging data faces hurdles beyond patient and institutional protections, including data type and size (e.g., scanner-specific formats or TIFF images), inconsistent image quality and resolution (e.g., poor scans with artifacts), and, importantly, the funding and infrastructure for hosting data. The National Institutes of Health aims to support these endeavors with other biomedical datasets (e.g., Gene Expression Omnibus (https://ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) and Osteoarthritis Initiative (https://nda.nih.gov/oai)) and scientific data-sharing policies and initiatives, which can serve as frameworks for these efforts. Open-source communities will play a crucial role in guiding these initiatives and advancing computational methods for bone imaging, offering collaborative spaces to share tools, datasets, workflows, and technical insights, and to host workshops (Bonaretti et al., 2023; Scheller et al., 2024). A timely example of such data standards was put forth by the Open and Reproducible Musculoskeletal Imaging Research (ORMIR) community with the MIDS data format (Santini et al., 2026). Bone researchers should consider how open dissemination of data and code may influence intellectual property rights and future patentability. Ultimately, community-led efforts to actualize these dataset repositories and standards will only enable the creation of more reproducible and rigorous datasets to test novel ideas and ML models.

Additional concepts and technical details on ML model development for microCT or HRpQCT for the curious reader

ML model generalizability

The successful development of ML models relies on their ability to “generalize” or work as intended on unseen datasets after the model training/development process. An unseen dataset is commonly referred to in a few ways, often used interchangeably: validation, test, and external test (described further below). Model generalizability fosters researchers' trust in these models' estimations and outcomes, particularly in providing insights into the biological underpinnings and therapeutic targets. Caution should be exercised when evaluating both the ML model metrics and related bone microarchitecture to assess model generalizability. An ML model may appear to perform well based on training metrics, but underperform when generating synthetically derived or post-segmentation bone microarchitecture, or vice versa. Furthermore, in cases involving ML classification models, understanding misclassifications and determining whether the resulting accuracy level is “sufficient” will be a key trade-off. If the model trains accurately but does not generalize, researchers can only cautiously use it as an exploratory descriptive modeling tool rather than a successfully developed predictive model (Shmueli, 2010). While there is no universally accepted sample size for determining whether a model is robust and generalizable, the appropriate sample size depends on data quality, feature dimensionality, model complexity, and task-specific goals (e.g., segmentation vs. prediction). Additionally, the sample size requirement is influenced by the ratio of samples to input features, with more samples required for more input features. As pre-processing steps, data normalization (e.g., scaling values between 0 and 1) can be performed to standardize the data and reduce noise in ML models. Overall, model generalizability is a function of many interrelated components that researchers should be aware of when developing ML models, in general, and for digital bone imaging.

Often, part of the standard model development pipeline to improve model generalizability involves data augmentation to synthetically increase sample size, impute missing data, and address class imbalance. Depending on the model type and task (e.g., prediction vs segmentation), different data augmentation techniques can be used. For instance, tabular-based models, such as prediction and classification, can use data augmentation techniques by sampling from the median of a parameter, K-nearest neighbors, or the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique. Image-based ML models, such as super-resolution and segmentation models, can use data augmentation techniques involving image rotation, blurring, and stretching. Importantly, in many bone research studies, there will be a class imbalance, with far fewer patients with fractures and osteoporosis than healthy individuals. Class imbalance is critical, especially clinically, because a model may appear accurate by classic ML metrics but be poor in practice at identifying the cases that matter most (e.g., fractures). To address class imbalance, ML models can be weighted toward a particular class during training or use class-weighted loss functions, enabling models to learn the underrepresented class more effectively or de-emphasize the majority class. Further, a researcher could use an ML model framework that internally handles class imbalance (e.g., gradient boosting and tree-based models), and the aforementioned data augmentation techniques could be used to over- or under-sample a class. In summary, data augmentation ultimately provides more variance for models to learn from, thereby improving generalizability.

A few final key points of emphasis on model generalizability center on how to split datasets for training and evaluation. First, there are usually three ways to use the data to train and evaluate ML models: train/validation/test splitting, k-fold cross-validation, and external testing. For instance, if there are 100 patients, the dataset is typically split into train/validation/test sets with a 70/10/20 ratio (i.e., 70, 10, and 20 patients, respectively). In this split ratio, a model is trained entirely on the training set to determine the “best” model. The validation is evaluated during training as an in-progress snapshot of a model's generalizability, but it is not always used or reported. The final metrics reported in manuscripts are those of the “best” model evaluated on the reserved test set. Conversely, cross-validation involves splitting the available data into k-folds or subsets (e.g., 5 datasets of 20 patients) and training/test models on those folds (i.e., 15/5 for a train/test split) to obtain model replicates for determining the “best” model to statistically report. A limitation is that you are not evaluating on a larger set of reserved test sets, as it's sampled from the current dataset population (e.g., 100 patients). Cross-validation is often used in clinical datasets because sample sizes are usually small, but it yields generalizability estimates for the population median/mean and variance. Lastly, there is an external test set that may evaluate the “best” models on a new dataset collected or on datasets from entirely different institutions and/or digital bone-imaging scanners. Underlying all of these methods for splitting a dataset is a hyperparameter tuning step (i.e., searching the latent space for ML models to learn), in which cross-validation is performed on the training set to identify the “best” model. This “best” model during training is then evaluated on the validation and test sets. Depending on the data-splitting method and the sample size, models may overfit or exhibit biases that become apparent during training (or epochs), as reflected by a drop in accuracy from the training to test sets (e.g., from 90% to 50%) or diverging training and validation curves. Researchers can attempt to reduce model overfitting by using data augmentation, reducing the number of model inputs, applying manual or automatic feature engineering (e.g., Lasso and Elastic Net regression), choosing a different ML model framework, or reducing the number of predictors (e.g., fewer categorical labels or tissues for segmentation).

For ML models involving the digital bone images themselves (i.e., not the numerical outputs of bone radiomic measures), there are two ways to refer to the number of samples or total images: either the number of actual bones/patients or the number of images generated from a given bone/patient. The latter comparisons can introduce model overfitting, in which ML models applied to sequential two-dimensional slices of a three-dimensional volume become too similar. As a result, the model memorizes these relationships and does not generalize to the test set. While not directly assessed in this narrative review, the included studies used a mix of two-dimensional and three-dimensional images, further complicating direct comparisons of sample sizes and ML model metrics across studies. Ultimately, there is no consensus on a standard ML approach and pipeline for bone imaging, and readers should be aware of these considerations, particularly given the vast heterogeneity of bone diseases, patient populations, and treatment responses.

Interplay between ML and statistics

The interplay between standard statistics (e.g., parametric and nonparametric analyses), advanced statistics (e.g., dimensionality reduction and data integration), and ML involves a trade-off between interpretability, exploration of complex and interconnected data, and computational resources (Fig. 9E). Traditional statistics is more hypothesis-driven and constrained by data assumptions that make interpretation more straightforward. Conversely, advanced statistics (e.g., dimensionality reduction and data integration) and ML allow the simultaneous analysis of all datasets with non-linear, complex relationships, but require more programming and computational resources. Further, traditional statistics are crucial for comparing original bone microarchitecture or clinical data between bone groups or phenotypes derived from ML models. Advanced statistics can help with dimensionality reduction and data integration, as well as interpreting how an ML model arrives at an estimation (e.g., SHAP). When ML models fail to produce meaningful results, resorting to traditional statistical methods may be necessary. Overall, ML models combined with statistics enable researchers to tackle research-specific tasks, thereby accelerating and focusing their attention on the most influential variables.

Technical and computational considerations for ML super-resolution models

Two key concepts should be considered in training super-resolution models for digital bone imaging: 1) how the different image resolutions are acquired, and 2) how to process the imaging data post-acquisition. For acquiring bone images, ideally, imaging data pairs are bone samples acquired in the same physical space (e.g., in the same spatial location in a scanner via a holder) and as-is from the scanners for both resolutions. A key technical challenge is sample repositioning, or misalignment, in x-y-z imaging space during or between acquisitions. Subtle positional shifts can be introduced and might occur because of imaging preparation (e.g., poor fixation to holders or casts) and scanner-related mechanical movement. Minor shifts can be accounted for post-acquisition by performing computational image registration; however, this registration (i.e., pixel interpolation) may blur or smooth non-real bone structures. Alternatively, images of bone can be derived synthetically using computationally upsampled or down-sampled resolution post-acquisition. For instance, one can acquire a high-resolution image and computationally downsample it to generate the low-resolution image, and vice versa. While computational synthetic data is a valid approach, the images do not represent the sample in its actual acquisition state.

In either image acquisition mode, researchers must decide between selecting two-dimensional images or three-dimensional volumes for developing super-resolution models. Traditional CT scanners reconstruct images using specific computational algorithms, after which bone radiomic analysis is often performed in two- or three-dimensions. For two-dimensional slices, an inherent decision must be made regarding how to handle discrepancies in the number of slices spatially between the low-resolution and high-resolution paired images. For instance, if the high-resolution acquisition is double the number of voxels per linear dimension, this creates two high-resolution image slices for every low-resolution slice. Options include averaging the two, selecting one, or duplicating the low-resolution slice. Using three-dimensional volumes, on the other hand, provides contextual information from all directions, which may enhance model accuracy. However, computational costs, namely data size and training time, are critical considerations.

Computational considerations of ML bone segmentation models

Crucial considerations for model accuracy in ML bone segmentation include image resolution, whether the imaging data are two- or three-dimensional, and whether to use single-tissue or multi-tissue segmentation models. For instance, developing a bone segmentation model that works on any microCT resolution of mouse bone may not be feasible; however, training models for each resolution would be an alternative. Similar to the ML approach of super-resolution models to enhance resolution, ML segmentation models can also utilize two-dimensional images or three-dimensional volumes, with the same computational considerations and model accuracies. Another consideration is whether to train separate models for each bone type (e.g., cortical versus trabecular compartment or fracture callus versus native bone) or a single multi-class model that segments all bone structures. These choices involve potential trade-offs in accuracy that should be weighed carefully and experimentally determined.

Choosing between linear and non-linear dimensionality reduction and data integrative techniques

The choice between linear and non-linear methods for dimensionality reduction and data integration hinges on the interpretability the researcher seeks. Linear approaches allow for directly identifying the original features after transformation, whereas non-linear methods lose this traceable relationship. Additionally, linear methods offer quicker computational speed and efficiency than non-linear approaches. The standard linear data reduction method is PCA, which linearly transforms the original variables into a smaller number of principal components that explain the maximum variance in the original dataset. The PCA loadings are often used to interpret the contribution of the original data inputs to the new data transformation representation. Other linear dimensionality reduction techniques include Linear Discriminant Analysis. Non-linear reduction methods include Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE). For data integration, CCA and generalized CCA are linear approaches, whereas deep generalized CCA is a non-linear approach.

Future considerations

ML can be applied to various aspects of bone research using microCT and HRpQCT, and we are currently in the early stages of ML use in this domain. Our review does not delve deeply into every ML approach (e.g., large language models, reinforcement learning, or transfer learning), mathematical theory, or comparisons across different ML models. Transfer learning, however, will likely serve as a critical alternative to training ML models from scratch, and it will likely be the first approach for bone imaging datasets. Many bone labs and researchers will face small datasets that might not be sufficient to develop generalized ML models for their research. Transfer learning, on the other hand, is the process of taking a trained ML model from one research area or task and retraining it on a smaller, different dataset. For instance, a bone researcher might use an ML segmentation model developed to delineate cortical bone, then retrain/tune or transfer the learned features to adapt it for segmenting the trabecular network. Transfer learning is likely to have a greater impact when developing an ML model for one species and then retraining it for another. However, transfer learning also requires sharing both datasets and trained models, which has its limitations. Ultimately, transfer learning dramatically reduces data and time requirements for ML model development, but has not been explored by current bone researchers. Another area of future exploration involves combining multiple models in end-to-end pipelines. These end-to-end pipelines could provide complete automation and objectivity in bone research analysis, both preclinically and clinically. In addition to the topics discussed in this review, these ML approaches extend well beyond microCT and HRpQCT imaging techniques (e.g., nanoCT, other CT, DXA, and magnetic resonance imaging). They can be integrated with additional imaging data and analytical methods (e.g., genetics, biomechanics, histology/immunohistochemistry, microbiome, spatial transcriptomics, etc.). Integrating multimodal and length-scale data will help drive the era of personalized medicine, which aims to improve patient-centered care. While ML can significantly assist clinicians by automating tasks such as segmentation and prediction, we see these models as complementary to, rather than replacements for, human expertise.

In the coming years, translating generalizable ML models will incur considerable challenges. For instance, there is a lack of standardization, consistent reporting of data manipulation and software/programming packages, and open-access bone imaging datasets. These realities hinder our ability to fully validate models developed worldwide across various scanners, patient populations, demographics, bone conditions, and treatment types. An obvious consideration is protecting human data and complying with regulations, which also challenges data repositories. Data-sharing agreements or guidelines will need to be brought to the forefront when establishing future recommendations and steps. Many of these factors will require partnership among various stakeholders: the IT department for storage and infrastructure; clinicians and scientists to work with humans; and ML researchers/engineers or similar roles to train, tune, and deploy models in the continuous ML life cycle. Many of these challenges can be overcome, but it will take strategic planning, community support, and commitments from funding agencies.

Translating these ML models for bone imaging into clinical practice as commercial medical devices will face varying levels of scrutiny and regulations (Muehlematter et al., 2021; Sivakumar et al., 2025). In particular, the regulations will likely depend on the type of ML model and whether it's a research, clinical support, or clinical decision tool. The regulatory pathways for these tools will also depend on the country where they are used, as regulations may vary by country. Regulators are still navigating the landscape of ML in general, and consensus on the product type and pathway to commercialization remains unclear. There has been a rapid rise in the number of approved ML-assisted medical devices/tools over the last decade. Caution is needed when trusting or deploying them in practice, as many of the tools are not yet mature, and the impact of different scanners and bone diseases will challenge these ML tools. While other CT-based scanners have driven these approvals over the last decade, HRpQCT remains largely a research tool, likely leading to a lag in commercialization and regulatory-approved clinical tools. Another topic of discussion is whether insurance or government agencies will cover a patient based on such an ML model, as this is already a challenging topic outside of ML models. Ultimately, demonstrations of utility, accuracy, and transparency are necessary for patients to trust physicians who use ML for digital bone imaging as a clinical tool.

The bone research community is a vast worldwide network of dedicated researchers and clinicians, and by putting our minds together, we can solve problems with the tools of ML and digital bone imaging. We encourage readers to use this review as a springboard for their journey of using ML for bone research.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our narrative review, coupled with scientometric and bibliometric analyses, provided a high-level overview of how bone researchers are using ML with microCT or HRpQCT. We also introduce SciNetX, a Python-based scientometric and bibliometric analytical and visualization software, that helped illuminate the scientific knowledge and networks of collaborations, funding, institutions, co-authors, countries, and more associated with ML approaches for digital bone imaging. Our narrative review, coupled with SciNetX, uncovered that the landscape of ML approaches for digital bone imaging is rapidly evolving, with many publications from the last decade spanning in vivo and ex vivo use cases across numerous species. ML for digital bone imaging has been used primarily to enhance image resolution, automate bone segmentation, reduce the number of experimental variables, cluster data, and combine these with the prediction and classification of bone health and fracture risk. Notably, the UNet framework was used across many ML tasks (i.e., super resolution, segmentation, and prediction), demonstrating its robust utility and consistent model choice. ML can profoundly impact bone research, but should be approached with a statistical and intuitive understanding of the ML toolbox. Trusting and integrating ML models with caution ultimately will allow for the adoption of automated, objective analyses of bone health and disease using microCT and HRpQCT. In the coming decades, integrating ML models of digital bone imaging with other datasets, such as multi-omics and longitudinal datasets, will be critical for the anticipated wave of novel diagnostic and therapeutic tools and a focus on personalized medicine. We hope this review helps lower the barrier to entry for bone researchers to incorporate ML into future studies of bone health and disease using digital bone imaging.
