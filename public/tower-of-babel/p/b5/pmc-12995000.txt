Characterization of premium soft ice creams produced from Jersey and Holstein milk
Authors: Ji-Woo Mun, Seung-Yeon Oh, Eung-Ryool Kim, Da-Mi Choi, Ji-Yeon Chun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study compared the physicochemical, structural, and antioxidant properties of premium soft ice creams (14–16% fat) made from Jersey milk (JIC) and Holstein milk (HIC). Both JIC and HIC were successfully formulated with over 14% fat content. Although most nutritional components showed no significant differences, JIC contained a higher level of saturated fat. Despite the lower viscosity of the Jersey milk mix (JM), and similar overrun values in both samples, JIC exhibited a slower melting rate, greater melting resistance, and larger, more irregular air cells than HIC. Color analysis revealed an increase in lightness after freezing, with JIC maintaining a relatively darker tone. Antioxidant activity, as assessed by the ABTS assay, was higher in casein isolates from Jersey milk; however, this trend was reversed in the final ice cream products, likely due to protein modifications and the formation of Maillard reaction products during processing. Overall, the results suggest that Jersey milk contributes to improved melting resistance and structural quality, making it a suitable raw material for producing high-fat premium ice cream.

Introduction

Jersey cattle, originally from the British island of Jersey, are a dairy breed characterized by a smaller body size and a light-brown coat, in contrast to Holsteins. They produce milk with higher levels of protein, fat, and calcium, making it nutritionally superior to Holstein milk (Stergiadis et al., 2013). Because of its higher fat content, Jersey milk imparts a richer and creamier flavor. Studies have reported that, under identical processing conditions, Jersey milk offers better suitability for dairy product manufacturing (Lim et al., 2020), contributing to its increasing demand in the premium market. The Jersey breed is now recognized as the second most important dairy breed globally due to its favorable milk composition and processing efficiency (Bland et al., 2015).

Ice cream is a globally consumed frozen dessert made from a mixture of milk, cream, stabilizers, emulsifiers, and sweeteners. Structurally, it is a complex colloidal system comprising dispersed air bubbles, ice crystals, and partially coalesced fat droplets within a continuous serum phase (Liu et al., 2024). Premium ice cream is distinguished by high-quality ingredients, elevated fat content, and low overrun (Clarke & Cox, 2024). Among the various milk components that influence ice cream quality, while milk protein composition, such as casein content, affects dairy products, fat content and fat globule characteristics more strongly influence the physical and sensory properties of ice cream. Fat plays a critical role in texture, creaminess, melting resistance, and mouthfeel (Goff, 1997; Muse & Hartel, 2004). Moreover, ice cream is classified based on milk fat content: super premium (16–18%), premium (14%), regular (12%), and economy (10%) (Guinard et al., 1996). Adequate milk fat is essential for maintaining ice cream structure during freezing and melting, while reduced fat levels can significantly compromise quality (Akbari et al., 2019). Aime et al. (2001) reported that increased milk fat enhances viscosity, smoothness, and mouth coating, but reduces coldness and firmness. However, excessive processing of high-fat mixtures may lead to fat agglomeration and churning.

Jersey milk has inherently higher fat content and larger, more variable fat globules than Holstein milk (Carroll et al., 2006; Czerniewicz et al., 2006), which are also influenced by feeding, genetics, and lactation stage. These differences in fat globule properties may still affect soft ice cream quality.

Several ice cream quality attributes including mix viscosity, melting resistance, overrun, air cell structure, and sensory perception are influenced by milk type, ingredient composition, and processing steps like mixing, homogenization, and freezing (Goff, 1997; Muse & Hartel, 2004). Among these, mix viscosity plays a pivotal role in stabilizing air bubbles and fat globules, contributing to enhanced melting resistance (Freire et al., 2020). Overrun, the amount of air incorporated during freezing, is another critical factor. While high overrun improves thermal insulation and slows melting (Warren & Hartel, 2018), it may reduce creaminess. Milk composition also affects overrun; for instance, higher fat content typically lowers overrun but improves mouthfeel. Moreover, the morphology of air cells particularly their size and distribution is crucial for texture and stability. Smaller, uniformly distributed air cells lead to smoother texture and better melting resistance. Wu et al. (2019) reported that high overrun combined with controlled fat destabilization yields finer air cells and reduces drip-through.

This study aimed to optimize the formulation of premium soft ice cream (14–16% milk fat) using Jersey and Holstein milk and to evaluate their impact on physicochemical, structural, and sensory properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

The Jersey and Holstein milk used in this study were provided by Jeju Milk Co., Ltd. (Jeju, Korea). The following ingredients were used for the preparation of soft ice cream: skim milk powder (Sung One F&B Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea), high-maltose syrup (Sajo CPK, Seoul, Korea), sweetened egg yolk liquid (Ganong, Gyeonggi-do, Korea), monoglyceride (Namyung, Gyeonggi-do, Korea), mixed stabilizer (Hansol E&S, Seoul, Korea), EM-7 emulsifier (Brenntag Korea Co., Ltd., Gyeonggi-do, Korea), heavy cream (Seoulmilk, Seoul, Korea), and granulated sugar (Beksul, Seoul, Korea).

For antioxidant analysis, the following chemicals were used: 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt, 98% (Alfa Aesar, Haverhill, USA), potassium persulfate (Sigma, St. Louis, USA), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Welgene, Gyeongsang-do, Korea), and ascorbic acid (Sigma).

Preparation of the ice cream mix and soft ice cream

The ice cream mix was prepared using raw milk (28.6%), cream (34.1%), refined sugar, mixed skim milk powder, corn syrup, sweetened egg yolks, emulsifiers (monoglycerides, EM-7, compound stabilizer), and purified water, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Cream was used in the formulation of both Jersey and Holstein milk ice cream samples, resulting in a final milk fat content of approximately 14–16%. For proprietary reasons, the precise ingredient ratios used in the formulation are not disclosed; however, all formulations were prepared under standardized and controlled conditions to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

Fig. 1Flowchart of soft ice cream manufacturing

Flowchart of soft ice cream manufacturing

Equal amounts of purified water and emulsifiers were mixed and then homogenized using a homogenizer (Homo Disper, MTOPS®, Seoul, Korea) at 70℃ for 15 min. Refined sugar, mixed skim milk powder, and corn syrup were dissolved in an equal amount of purified water, mixed with dissolved emulsifiers, and stirred for 20 min. Cream and sweetened egg yolk were added and further stirred for 15 min, followed by sterilization at 95℃ for 15 min. After sterilization, the mixture was stirred to prevent the formation of lumps and cooled before being aged in a refrigerator at 4℃ for 24 h. The aged ice cream mix was processed into soft ice cream using an ice cream maker (ISI-161TH; ICETRO, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) for quality analysis. All the manufacturing and analysis processes were performed in triplicate.

Nutritional composition

The nutritional composition of the soft ice cream samples was analyzed according to the Korean Food Code (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety, 2024). Prior to the analysis, the samples were thawed at room temperature until they reached a fully liquid state. The analyzed parameters included caloric, moisture, ash, protein, fat, total sugar, saturated fat, trans-fat, sodium, potassium, and dietary fiber content. Fatty acid composition was determined based on 36 fatty acid components. Components that were not detected in either sample were excluded from the analysis.

Viscosity of the ice cream mix

The viscosity of the ice cream mixture was measured using a Brookfield Viscometer DV1MLVTJ0 (Brookfield, Middleboro, USA). After aging at 4℃ for 24 h, the ice cream mix was brought to room temperature and stirred uniformly with a spatula. The viscosity of the soft ice cream mix was measured at 10℃ using an LV.1 viscometer spindle at 10 rpm. The viscometer was allowed to rotate for 30 s to stabilize the viscosity readings, which were recorded at 30 s intervals and expressed in centipoise (cP).

Brix value, color, and appearance

The Brix value and color of both Jersey and Holstein ice cream mixtures and soft ice cream were measured. Brix values were measured using a refractometer (Abbemat 350; Anton Paar, Seelze-Letter, Germany). The thawed soft ice cream and ice cream mix were brought to equilibrium at room temperature and uniformly stirred with a spatula before Brix values were measured. The color was assessed using a colorimeter (3NH Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China), with color coordinates expressed on the CIE-LAB scale as lightness (CIE L*), redness (CIE a*), and yellowness (CIE b*). The standard white plate used for calibration had the values L* = 92.73, a* = 0.76, and b* = −7.49. Melting resistance property (MRP) and melting rate

The MRP and melting rate of the soft ice cream was assessed using a modification of the method described by Shin and Yoon (1996). A digital scale was placed inside a incubator set to 25℃, and a 5 mm wire mesh was installed atop a scale stand. The soft ice cream (40 g) was scooped and placed on a wire mesh. The melted weight of the soft ice cream was recorded every 10 s, and the experiment was terminated when no further changes in weight were observed. Optical and field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM) analyses

The following three samples were prepared to observe the air-cell properties: ice cream mix, frozen ice cream, and freeze-dried ice cream. For optical microscopy analysis (CX23LEDRFS1; OLYMPUS, Tokyo, Japan), a small portion of the ice cream mix and frozen ice cream was placed in a well (100 μm depth × 25 mm width × 24 mm length) on a glass slide, and observations were conducted at ×400 magnification. For FE-SEM analysis, soft ice cream samples were frozen at −80℃ before freeze-drying. The freeze-dried samples were finely crushed and preprocessed using a sputter coater (Pt) (Quorum Technologies, Lewes, UK). The structures of both soft ice creams were observed using an FE-SEM (MIRA3; TESCAN, Brno, Czech Republic) at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV and magnification ranging from ×200 to ×600.

2,2-Azino-bis-3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulphonic acid (ABTS) assay

Ice cream extracts were prepared for ABTS radical scavenging activity assessment following the method described by Sayar et al. (2022). For each Jersey and Holstein soft ice cream sample, 25 g was mixed with 75 mL of 90% ethanol. The mixture was then extracted using an shaking incubator (SI-600R; JEIO TECH, Daejeon, Korea) at 20℃ and 210 rpm for 6 h. The extract was concentrated at 50℃ and 80 rpm until the volume was reduced to 25 mL, after which 25 mL of deionized water was added. The final solution was stored at −20℃ for further analysis. Casein was extracted from Jersey and Holstein milk according to the method described by Woo et al. (2009). Milk samples were centrifuged at 4,000 rpm for 20 min at 4℃ using a centrifuge (LaboGene 1248R; LABOGENE, Daejeon, Korea) to remove the cream layer, yielding skim milk. The skim milk was adjusted to pH 4.6 with 1 N HCl at 20℃ and then centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 10 min at 4℃ to precipitate acid casein. The acid casein was washed with distilled water, reprecipitated using 1 N HCl, and then dialyzed against distilled water at 4℃ for 72 h. The dialyzed casein was freeze-dried and used in subsequent experiments.

ABTS radical scavenging activity was measured as described by Re et al. (1999). Solutions of 7.4 mM ABTS and 2.6 mM potassium persulfate were prepared and mixed in a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was then incubated in the dark at room temperature for 24 h to generate the ABTS radical cation (ABTS•⁺). The resulting solution was diluted with PBS (pH 7.4) to an absorbance of 0.700 ± 0.020 at 732 nm. For the assay, 50 µL of the diluted sample was mixed with 950 µL of the diluted ABTS solution and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. After incubation, the mixture was transferred to a 96-well plate and the absorbance was measured at 732 nm using a microplate reader. Statistical analysis

For all data in this study, at least three experiments were repeated to obtain the results, expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 24 for Windows; IBM, Armonk, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test was used to compare differences between more than two groups, and an independent-samples Student’s t-test (two-tailed) was used to compare differences between two groups. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Nutritional composition

When comparing the nutritional composition of soft ice cream made from Jersey milk and Holstein milk, there were no significant differences observed in caloric, carbohydrate, water, ash, protein, fat, sugar, cholesterol, sodium, potassium, and dietary fiber content (Table 1). However, the saturated fat content was significantly higher in Jersy ice cream (JIC) than in Holstein ice cream (HIC; p < 0.05). This trend is consistent with that of previous studies of Jersey milk and dairy products made from Jersey milk (Yoo et al., 2022). Nevertheless, in the fatty acid composition analysis, most fatty acids showed no significant differences between JIC and HIC (Table 2). This is likely because of the addition of cream to achieve a high milk fat content of 16%, as required for premium ice cream, which may have masked the differences between milk types.

Viscosity of ice cream mixtures

The viscosity profiles of the Jersey ice cream mix (JM) and Holstein ice cream mix (HM) are shown in Fig. 2. Viscosity measurements at a constant shear rate (30 RPM) over 30 min demonstrated a gradual decrease for both JM and HM, indicating thixotropic behavior. Notably, HM consistently exhibited higher viscosity than JM throughout the measurement period, with initial values of 417.6 cP and 313.2 cP, respectively. Parid et al. (2021) reported a viscosity of 360.0 cP for an ice cream mix prepared with carboxymethyl cellulose, which was similar to that of HM in this study. Generally, the viscosity of ice cream mixtures ranges from 100 to 800 cP (Martínez et al., 2020), and is influenced by multiple factors, including fat content, stabilizers, and other additives. In particular, fat content has been shown to significantly affect ice cream properties, such as viscosity, creaminess, texture, and meltdown behavior (Cruz et al., 2022).

In the color analysis, JM and HM had CIE L* values of 86.60 ± 1.02 and 87.25 ± 0.53, respectively. After processing into ice cream, the CIE L* values significantly increased to 90.28 ± 1.75 for JIC and 91.65 ± 0.25 for HIC (p < 0.05), indicating a perceptible lightening of color during ice cream manufacturing. JIC showed lower CIE L* values ​​than HIC (p < 0.05). The CIE a* values, indicating redness, were highest in HIC (0.20 ± 0.03), although the difference from JIC (0.17 ± 0.04) was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). However, significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between the mixtures and the final products, with JM and HM showing negative CIE a* values (−0.63 ± 0.04 and − 0.36 ± 0.21, respectively), indicating a shift from greenish tones in the mixtures to more reddish hues in the ice creams. Regarding CIE b* values (yellowness), HM exhibited the highest yellowness (10.60 ± 0.27), which was significantly different from that of JM (10.27 ± 0.17; p < 0.05). In contrast to the trend in CIE a* values, the CIE b* values decreased to 8.52 ± 0.43 and 8.21 ± 0.17 in JIC and HIC, respectively, after processing, suggesting a reduction in yellow tones and a shift toward cooler hues in the final ice cream products. The ΔE values, representing the overall color difference from a white standard plate, indicated that all samples exhibited a noticeable color difference from the reference white. Regardless of the milk type, the ice cream mixtures showed significantly higher ΔE values than their ice cream counterparts (p < 0.05), implying that the transformation process reduced the color contrast with the white standard. Melting resistance property (MRP) and melting rate

As shown in Table 4, JIC exhibited a significantly longer time to first drop (8.77 ± 2.09 min) compared to HIC (3.17 ± 0.01 min, p < 0.05). The total melting time was also substantially different: JIC required 351.35 ± 3.67 min, while HIC melted completely within 47.04 ± 2.01 min. The final MRP for HIC reached 96.34 ± 1.29%, while JIC retained more than two-thirds of its mass (33.58 ± 0.54%) as a thick, cream-like residue.

It was suggested that this reduced flow ability is attributed to the higher fat content in JIC and prior studies support this observation. According to research by Smet et al. (2010), ice creams with higher saturated fat content and greater fat destabilization tend to retain a more stable structure, resulting in slower melting. Additionally, increased protein content correlates with faster melting (Roy et al., 2022), while higher fat content enhances network structure and induces partial coalescence during freezing, leading to reduced melt rate (Muse & Hartel, 2004; Wu et al., 2019). When an ice cream mix contains a greater number of fat globules, the probability of collisions between them increases, which intensifies emulsion destabilization (Liu et al., 2022). This phenomenon directly affects the melting resistance property (MRP), as destabilized fat networks contribute to the structural integrity of the ice cream during melting (Liu et al., 2022).

Figure 4 shows the melting rate (g/min) of ice creams produced from Jersey milk and Holstein milk over a 45 min period. A clear difference in melting rate between the two samples was observed. The HIC exhibited a rapid increase in melting rate from approximately 5 min, reaching 0.88 g/min at around 35 min, after which it remained stable. The JIC demonstrated a consistently low melting rate, with a slight increase to approximately 0.025 g/min at 26 min, and did not exceed 0.1 g/min throughout the test. These results suggest that the lower melting rate observed in JIC may be attributed to a fat destabilization (Liu et al., 2022). This difference may result from the compositional variations between the two milk types. As shown in Table 1, Jersey milk contains a higher level of solids, including fat and protein, compared to Holstein milk. This higher solid content positively influences the ice cream’s hardness and shape retention (Lomolino et al., 2020). A higher MRP and slower melting rate are key characteristics of premium ice cream products; therefore, Jersey milk is suggested as a suitable raw material for high-fat premium ice cream.

Overrun

Overrun is a critical factor in optimizing the texture and mouthfeel of the final ice cream product. According to Afriana and Kurnia (2024), the overrun increases with the amount of air incorporated during the freezing process, resulting in a lower density and smoother texture. As shown in Table 4, the overrun values of JIC and HIC were 33.55 ± 3.54% and 34.73 ± 3.63%, respectively, with no significant difference between them (p > 0.05). The overrun value was closely related to the viscosity of the ice cream mix and its melting rate. Romulo et al. (2021) reported that a higher overrun value leads to a lower melting rate and improved melt resistance. In this study, although there was no significant difference in the overrun values between the two ice cream samples, JIC exhibited a notably slower melting rate than HIC. These results suggest that overrun is influenced not only by the mix viscosity and melting rate, but also by other factors, such as the Brix value and air cell size (Muse & Hartel, 2004; Sofjan & Hartel, 2004; Wu et al., 2019). Therefore, how these properties interact to influence the quality and characteristics of ice cream cannot be explained by a single factor. In this study, our findings suggest that viscosity and fat content may play a more significant role in determining air cell morphology than overrun, this conclusion is based on a limited dataset involving only two milk types with relatively similar overrun values. In further study, involving a wider variety of milk compositions, fat levels, and overrun conditions are warranted to better elucidate the interactions among these variables.

Air cell properties

Figure 5 shows the distribution of air cell diameters in JIC and HIC, as observed by optical microscopy. The mean air cell diameters of JIC and HIC were approximately 38.14 μm and 30.94 μm, respectively. According to the results of the independent t-test (p < 0.001), the difference in air cell size between the two samples was statistically significant. The air cell analysis revealed that JIC contained irregularly sized and arranged air cells. While such structures might initially be considered unfavorable, several factors suggest they can still positively contribute to product quality. Markowska et al. (2023) reported that air cell sizes between 31 and 150 μm are optimal for high-quality ice cream. In this study, most air cells in JIC ranged between 30 and 40 μm and did not exceed 100 μm (Fig. 5B), which aligns well with this standard.

In terms of physical characteristics, JIC exhibited a significantly slower melting rate compared to HIC, with a portion of the sample remaining on the mesh during testing. Moreover, the overrun values of JIC and HIC also were not significantly different. Although there is a correlation between overrun and air cell structure, it is not a straightforward one-to-one relationship. In other words, air cells are influenced by various factors such as viscosity of mix, freezing rate, emulsifier, aeration and so on (Ko et al., 2014). Therefore, overrun and air cell structure are related but not directly proportional. Various studies have shown that multiple factors including viscosity, emulsifier and stabilizer content, freezing temperature, fat destabilization, ice crystal size, air cell structure, and overrun collectively influence key ice cream quality attributes such as melting resistance, hardness, and texture (Chang & Hartel, 2002; Goff & Jordan, 1989; Liu et al., 2023; Russell et al., 1999). In this study, it was suggested that the broader air cell size distribution in milk type. To further understand air cell structural behavior, future studies should explore how specific manufacturing variables affect air incorporation and stabilization during freezing.

Figure 6 presents the field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) images of freeze-dried JIC (upper images) and HIC (lower images). All samples were observed under similar conditions (5.0 kV, approximately ×800 magnification, 100 μm scale bar). The air cells observed by optical microscopy in this study were consistent with this description, showing a spherical geometry and broad size distribution, as previously demonstrated in the air cell size histogram. Most of the observed areas exhibit a porous structure, which was considered to be the remnant left behind after the evaporation or sublimation of frozen air bubbles, ice crystals and fat globules. Unlike ice crystals or fat crystals, air cells are typically perfectly spherical. By contrast, ice crystals often exhibit dendritic growth, extending laterally or vertically, resulting in an elliptical or irregular morphology.

ABTS radical scavenging activity

The ABTS radical scavenging activity is determined based on the formation of the ABTS•⁺ radical cation, which results in a blue-green color upon reaction between ABTS and potassium persulfate. In the presence of antioxidants, ABTS•⁺ is reduced to its colorless form (ABTS), and the extent of decolorization varies depending on the antioxidant concentration and reaction time (Garg et al., 2024). As ABTS is soluble in both aqueous and organic solvents, this method can be applied to both hydrophilic and lipophilic antioxidants (Garg et al., 2024). Therefore, it is suitable for evaluating the antioxidant activity of amphiphilic substances, such as casein and ice cream, which contain both hydrophilic and lipophilic components. As shown in Fig. 7B, casein samples exhibited a concentration-dependent increase in radical-scavenging activity. JMC at a concentration of 100 mg/mL showed significantly higher antioxidant activity (61.80%) than HMC (38.68%; p < 0.05). At 50 mg/mL, the antioxidant activity of JMC and HMC reached 31.83% and 23.70%, respectively, with a significant difference (p < 0.05). At 3.13 mg/mL, there was no statistically significant difference between the antioxidant activity of HMC and JMC (p > 0.05).

As shown in Fig. 7C, in the ice cream samples, HMC demonstrated significantly higher antioxidant activity than JMC at 125 mg/mL (96.69% vs. 94.85%) and 62.5 mg/mL (80.51% vs. 78.13%; p < 0.05). While casein alone showed a stronger antioxidant capacity in JMC than in HMC, this trend was reversed in the ice cream samples. Oh et al. (2013) demonstrated that Maillard-reacted milk proteins exhibited higher antioxidant activities than native proteins in 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl, ABTS, and Ferric reducing antioxidant power assays. Therefore, thermal, freezing, and mixing treatments during ice cream production may alter protein structure and antioxidant properties. During manufacture, casein–sugar interactions can also induce the Maillard reaction (Viora et al., 2024), potentially modifying antioxidant-related structures and contributing to the observed activity differences. In this study, we hypothesize that processing-induced protein unfolding and Maillard reaction product formation underlie the reversal in antioxidant activity between casein isolates and finished ice cream. However, our current data cannot definitively establish this causal pathway. Future work will include quantification of residual protein content post-processing (e.g., by BCA assay) to more robustly validate this mechanism.

Conclusions

This study underscores the potential of Jersey milk as a high-value ingredient for the development of premium soft ice cream. Although its lower yield compared to Holstein milk has limited its commercial application, the higher fat and protein contents of Jersey milk positively influenced product quality, particularly in terms of viscosity, air cell structure, and antioxidant potential. Compared to HIC, JIC demonstrated superior fatty acid composition, structural stability, and functional properties, supporting its suitability for premium formulations. Notably, favorable characteristics such as increased Brix and reduced color change were maintained throughout processing. While antioxidant activity varied depending on composition and processing conditions, the unique properties of Jersey milk suggest its capacity to deliver differentiated sensory quality and functional benefits. Overall, these findings highlight the potential of Jersey milk to support premium regional branding and contribute to sustainable diversification within the dairy industry.
