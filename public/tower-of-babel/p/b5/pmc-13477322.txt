Association Between Optimal Revascularization for Chronic Total Occlusion and Long‐Term Outcomes in Patients With Heart Failure With or Without Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Yanjun Song, Zhangyu Lin, Jining He, Rui Fu, Kefei Dou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between optimal revascularization for chronic total occlusion (CTO) lesions and long‐term clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure with or without type 2 diabetes (T2D) remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between optimal or nonoptimal CTO percutaneous coronary intervention (CTO‐PCI) and long‐term adverse clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure with or without T2D.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 1591 patients with heart failure who underwent CTO‐PCI at Fuwai Hospital between January 2017 and December 2018. Patients were categorized based on CTO‐PCI outcomes: failure, suboptimal revascularization, and optimal revascularization. The primary end point was a composite of cardiovascular death and target‐vessel myocardial infarction (TVMI), whereas the secondary end point was a composite of all‐cause death and myocardial infarction.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 3.3 years, 89 (5.6%) cases of cardiovascular death/TVMI occurred. Patients who received optimal revascularization had significantly lower risks of both cardiovascular death/TVMI (hazard ratio [HR], 0.45 [95% CI, 0.27–0.76]) and all‐cause death/myocardial infarction (HR, 0.53 [95% CI, 0.33–0.86]) compared with those with CTO‐PCI failure. Subgroup analysis showed that these associations were significant in patients with T2D (cardiovascular death/TVMI: HR, 0.25 [95% CI, 0.13–0.49]; all‐cause death/myocardial infarction: HR, 0.28 [95% CI, 0.15–0.53]), but not in those without T2D (cardiovascular death/TVMI: HR, 0.87 [95% CI, 0.32–2.40]; all‐cause death/myocardial infarction: HR, 1.06 [95% CI, 0.40–2.85]).ConclusionsOur findings suggest that optimal revascularization during CTO‐PCI is associated with a lower risk of adverse cardiovascular events in patients with heart failure, particularly in those with T2D.Keywords: chronic total occlusion, heart failure, percutaneous coronary intervention, type 2 diabetesSubject Categories: Heart Failure

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This study demonstrates that optimal chronic total occlusion revascularization in patients with heart failure is associated with lower long‐term risks of cardiovascular death and target‐vessel myocardial infarction, an association that was particularly pronounced in those with type 2 diabetes, where it also extended to lower risks of all‐cause death and myocardial infarction.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Clinicians may consider pursuing optimal revascularization in patients with heart failure, particularly in those with concomitant type 2 diabetes, as a strategy that may be associated with a lower risk of adverse cardiovascular events.

Coronary artery disease, accompanied by ischemic myocardial damage and advancement to heart failure (HF), is a predominant cause of mortality globally. 1 Patients with HF often develop chronic total occlusion (CTO) in the coronary arteries. 2 Approximately 40% of patients with HF possess ≥1 considerable coronary CTO lesions. 3 Moreover, the presence of CTOs is associated with poorer outcomes in patients with ischemic systolic HF. 3 An optimal percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for CTO has demonstrated improved anginal symptoms, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and long‐term survival rates. 4 , 5 , 6 Notably, attaining optimal revascularization can enhance HF outcomes, making it a key goal in coronary revascularization for this population. 7 However, the long‐term effect of optimal CTO‐PCI on patients with HF remains unclear; consequently, many cardiologists are unwilling to perform CTO‐PCI for symptomatic patients owing to concerns over procedure‐related complications.

The prevalence of type 2 diabetes (T2D) in patients undergoing CTO‐PCI ranges from 27% to 45%. 8 , 9 A previous study demonstrated that individuals with T2D having CTO‐PCI possess less favorable baseline cardiovascular risk profiles than patients without T2D. 10 Consequently, T2D is recognized as a considerable risk factor in the risk–benefit evaluation of complex revascularization treatments. 10 Currently, no comparative evidence exists to ascertain the advantages of optimal versus nonoptimal revascularization for CTO‐PCI in patients with HF with or without T2D.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between optimal or nonoptimal CTO‐PCI and long‐term adverse clinical outcomes in patients with HF with or without T2D.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

This prospective cohort study consecutively enrolled patients with HF who underwent PCI for at least 1 CTO lesion at Fuwai Hospital, National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases, between January 2017 and December 2018.

HF was characterized by a prior HF diagnosis, LVEF of <50% or LVEF ≥50% with HF symptoms and signs, and elevated NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels (>300 or 900 pg/mL in sinus rhythm or atrial fibrillation, respectively). 11 CTO diagnosis was based on coronary angiographic findings, assessed by at least 2 cardiologists. CTO lesion was defined by TIMI (Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction) flow grade of 0 in a coronary artery for ≥3 months, or lesions with bridging collateral vessels but unknown occlusion duration. 12 According to the PCI procedural outcomes, participants were categorized into 3 groups: optimal recanalization, suboptimal recanalization, and failure. Optimal recanalization was defined as successful reperfusion of the target occluded vessel with restoration of TIMI flow grade 3, along with perfused side branch and residual stenosis <30%. Suboptimal recanalization was defined as reperfusion of the main occluded vessel with at least 1 of the following criteria: (1) ongoing significant side branch occlusion, (2) a final TIMI flow grade of 1 or 2, or (3) residual stenosis >30%. Procedural failure was defined as an inability to cross the lesion using a balloon angioplasty catheter with a TIMI flow grade of 0. Patients with multiple CTO lesions who had any lesion with unsuccessful recanalization were placed in the failure group. Similarly, patients with multiple CTO lesions who achieved reperfusion of all treated lesions but had suboptimal recanalization in at least 1 lesion were categorized into the suboptimal recanalization group.

The retry lesion was defined as a CTO lesion that had been previously attempted (but failed) at another tertiary hospital and was then treated for the first time at our institution. This definition was based on the patient's medical history obtained at enrollment. Therefore, patients with a retry lesion could subsequently achieve either successful (optimal or suboptimal) or failed revascularization at our center.

This study initially screened 1627 patients in the first stage. During the study, 23 patients were lost to follow‐up, including 19 who could not be contacted and 4 who refused participation in the follow‐up. Thereafter, we excluded individuals aged <18 years, with missing crucial baseline data, and those presenting with acute myocardial infarction (MI) or HF at the time of admission. A total of 1591 participants were finally included. Based on the PCI outcomes, 253, 131, and 1207 patients were categorized into the failure, suboptimal, and optimal groups, respectively (Figure 1).

Study flow.

CTO indicates chronic total occlusion; HF, heart failure; and PCI, percutaneous coronary intervention.

This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the institutional review board of Fuwai Hospital, National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases. All patients submitted written informed consent before enrolment. The reporting of results complied with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Baseline Information and Biochemical Analysis

During hospitalization, all interventions and medical therapies were conducted according to established clinical guidelines and the cardiologist's clinical judgment. Patients were prescribed daily aspirin (100 mg/d) and clopidogrel (75 mg/d) for at least 6 days, or ticagrelor (90 mg twice daily), or received loading doses of aspirin (300 mg) and clopidogrel (600 mg) or ticagrelor (180 mg) before PCI. The comprehensive PCI strategy was developed by experienced interventional cardiologists. The selection of coronary wires, devices, and additional diagnostic modalities, such as intravascular ultrasound and optical coherence tomography was made at the discretion of the intervention team. Upon discharge, patients continued aspirin therapy indefinitely (100 mg/d), whereas clopidogrel (75 mg/d) or ticagrelor (180 mg/d) was prescribed for a minimum of 6 months.

Before coronary angiography, blood samples were collected from the cubital vein of each participant following an overnight fast. All laboratory analyses were performed by the clinical chemistry department using standardized methods. Triglycerides, total cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and creatinine levels were measured via enzymatic testing with an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7150; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) was assessed using a Tosoh Automated Glycohemoglobin Analyzer (HLC‐723G8; Tosoh, Tokyo, Japan). Angiographic and procedural data were obtained from catheterization laboratory records and reviewed by 3 experienced interventional cardiologists. Demographic information, cardiovascular risk factors, clinical characteristics, laboratory and imaging results, coronary angiographic findings, procedural details, and discharge medication regimens were prospectively collected using standardized questionnaires by independent research staff.

Patients with a history of diabetes, undergoing glucose‐lowering therapy, or exhibiting an FBG ≥7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥6.5%, or 2‐hour plasma glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L in an oral glucose tolerance test, along with the exclusion of type 1 diabetes, were classified as having T2D. 13 Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, or antihypertensive medication administration. 14 Dyslipidemia was characterized by triglycerides >150 mg/dL, total cholesterol ≥200 mg/dL, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥130 mg/dL, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol <40 mg/dL, or cholesterol‐lowering medication administration. 15 Chronic kidney disease was defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 sustained for a minimum duration of 3 months. 16 LVEF values were measured at the time of enrollment and calculated using the biplane‐modified Simpson rule. 17

Intervention and Angiographic Analysis

Coronary angiography was performed and reviewed by at least 2 cardiologists. Based on the angiographic findings, target vessels and lesions for intervention were identified. The PCI procedure for CTO lesions was conducted by a team of at least 3 certified specialists. The antegrade wire escalation technique was initially attempted. If antegrade wire escalation failed, alternative strategies, including the parallel‐wire technique, antegrade dissection and re‐entry, and retrograde wiring (such as retrograde wire escalation and reverse controlled antegrade and retrograde tracking), were considered. The technique that successfully crossed the lesion with a balloon angioplasty catheter was documented. The selection of the guide catheter, stent type, and the use of intravascular imaging were left to the discretion of the interventional cardiologists. Lesion‐specific angiographic data, including the SYNTAX (Synergy Between Percutaneous Coronary Intervention With Taxus and Cardiac Surgery) score, the J‐CTO (Multicenter CTO Registry in Japan) score, the PROGRESS‐CTO (Prospective Global Registry for the Study of Chronic Total Occlusion Intervention) score, collateral vessel scores (including Rentrop grade and Werner classification of collateral circulation vessels grade), and procedural complications, were assessed by an independent core laboratory (Interventional Cardiovascular Imaging Core Laboratory, National Center for Cardiovascular Diseases, Beijing, China). Subsequently, angiographic data were re‐reviewed by at least 2 cardiologists.

Follow‐Up and End Points

Following discharge, participants were monitored at 6‐month intervals until December 31, 2021. End point data were acquired from medical records, clinical visits, or telephone interviews conducted by trained investigators blinded to the clinical data. The main outcome was the combination of cardiovascular death or target‐vessel myocardial infarction (TVMI). The secondary objectives included the composite of all‐cause death and MI, and each component, which were used in the survival analysis. Death was deemed cardiac until a definitive noncardiovascular cause could be identified. MI was characterized by elevated cardiac troponins accompanied by typical chest pain, distinctive serial electrocardiogram alterations, intracoronary thrombus detection via angiography or autopsy, or imaging that reveals fresh loss of viable myocardium or a newly identified regional wall‐motion abnormality. 18 TVMI was defined as MI attributable to the vessel treated for CTO. All occurrences were meticulously validated and assessed by 2 independent cardiologists.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data are presented as the mean±SD when normally distributed; otherwise, they are reported as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables are given as frequencies (percentages). Group differences were analyzed using 1‐way analysis of variance, Kruskal‐Wallis H test, Pearson χ2 test, or Fisher exact test, as applicable. The cumulative incidence of clinical events was evaluated using Kaplan‐Meier curves, and differences were analyzed with the log‐rank test. A multivariable Cox regression model was performed to determine the association between PCI outcomes and long‐term risk of clinical outcomes, which was presented by hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual tests, and no significant violations were found for the covariates (all P>0.05; Table S1). The functional form of continuous variables was assessed with Martingale residuals, confirming that the linearity assumption was satisfied for all continuous predictors (all P>0.05; Table S2). We also assessed the correlation between optimal revascularization of CTO‐PCI and the risk of clinical outcomes stratified by T2D diagnosis. To test whether the association between procedural outcomes and clinical end points differed between patients with and without T2D, we included a product term in the multivariable Cox regression models and reported the P values for interaction. For patients diagnosed with T2D, we further analyzed 2 additional subgroups based on baseline blood sugar control: the uncontrolled (defined as baseline FBG ≥7 mmol/L and HbA1c ≥6.5%) and the well‐controlled (defined as baseline FBG <7 mmol/L and HbA1c <6.5%) groups. This stratification aimed to assess the impact of glycemic control status on revascularization effectiveness in this population.

Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the following criteria: age (≥65 years), sex, LVEF (<40% and ≥40%), prior MI, obesity (body mass index ≥30 kg/m2), current smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, previous recanalization, right coronary artery CTO, left anterior descending artery CTO, Rentrop grade (less than grade 2 or grade 2 and above), Werner classification of collateral circulation grade (less than grade 2 or grade 2 and above), J‐CTO score (<2 points or ≥2 points), PROGRESS‐CTO score (<2 points or ≥ 2 points), and intravascular ultrasound guidance use. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding patients who experienced clinical events within 30 days of the initial procedure, those without dual antiplatelet therapy, those with 2 to 3 CTO lesions, and those not on anti‐HF medications, including β‐blockers, angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors/angiotensin receptor blockers, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists, and sodium‐dependent glucose transporters 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors.

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A 2‐tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics

The baseline features and comparisons across patients with varying procedure results are presented in Tables 1 and 2. The study population had a mean age of 60.3±10.8 years, with 78.1% of the participants being men. There were 253, 131, and 1207 instances of CTO‐PCI failure, poor revascularization, and optimal revascularization, respectively. Compared with patients experiencing CTO‐PCI failure, those classified as optimal revascularization were predominantly women, did not have T2D, and had lower FBG and HbA1c levels. In regard to lesion characteristics and treatment, patients who underwent optimal revascularization exhibited a reduced likelihood of multivessel coronary disease, recurring lesions, ambiguity in the proximal cap of CTO, moderate‐to‐severe vessel tortuosity, lesion bending exceeding 45°, lesion lengths ≥20 mm, moderate‐to‐severe calcification, ambiguity in the distal cap of CTOs, lower J‐CTO scores, and diminished CTO score progression. In addition, patients with optimal revascularization received more intravascular ultrasound guidance and experienced lower incidences of procedural complications.

Optimal Revascularization of CTO‐PCI and the Long‐Term Prognosis in Patients With HF

During the follow‐up with a median time of 3.3 years, 89 (5.6%) cases of cardiovascular death/TVMI, including 65 (4.1%) cases of cardiovascular death and 25 (1.6%) TVMI, were recorded. The association of the procedural outcomes with clinical outcomes is presented in Table 3. Compared with patients with CTO‐PCI failure, those with optimal revascularization showed a significantly lower risk of cardiovascular death/TVMI (HR, 0.45 [95% CI, 0.27–0.76]). The survival analysis also revealed that optimal revascularization was significantly associated with a lower risk of all‐cause death/MI (HR, 0.53 [95% CI, 0.33–0.86]), cardiovascular death (HR, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.28–0.88]), MI (HR, 0.37 [95% CI, 0.14–0.96]), and TVMI (HR, 0.32 [95% CI, 0.10–0.99]). Kaplan‐Meier plots presented consistent results (log‐rank P<0.001) (Figure 2). In patients with optimal or suboptimal recanalization, final TIMI flow 1 to 2 grade was associated with a significantly higher risk of cardiovascular death/TVMI (HR, 4.82 [95% CI, 1.78–13.02]) and all‐cause death/MI (HR, 4.47 [95% CI, 1.76–11.35]) (Table S3).

Interaction tests confirmed that the association between optimal revascularization and improved outcomes was significantly modified by T2D status for the composite of cardiovascular death/TVMI (P for interaction=0.014), the composite of all‐cause death/MI (P for interaction=0.006), all‐cause death (P for interaction = 0.011), cardiovascular death (P for interaction=0.011), and MI (P for interaction=0.047) (Table 4).

In regard to the non‐T2D subgroup, patients with optimal revascularization had comparable cardiovascular death/TVMI incidences compared with those with CTO‐PCI failure. No significant differences could be observed between suboptimal revascularization and CTO‐PCI failure in the incidence of all clinical outcomes (P>0.05). Similar trends could be seen in the occurrence of all secondary outcomes (Table 4).

In regard the T2D subgroup, patients with optimal revascularization had a significantly lower risk of cardiovascular death/TVMI (HR, 0.25 [95% CI, 0.13–0.49]), all‐cause death/MI (HR, 0.28 [95% CI, 0.15–0.53]), all‐cause death (HR, 0.29 [95% CI, 0.16–0.53]), cardiovascular death (HR, 0.26 [95% CI, 0.13–0.53]), MI (HR, 0.09 [95% CI, 0.02–0.42]), and TVMI (HR, 0.07 [95% CI, 0.02–0.57]) than those with CTO‐PCI failure. No significant differences were observed between suboptimal revascularization and CTO‐PCI failure in the incidence of all clinical outcomes (P > 0.05) (Table 4). Furthermore, within the T2D cohort, compared with CTO‐PCI failure, optimal revascularization was associated with reduced risks for the composite end point of cardiovascular death and TVMI (HR, 0.17 [95% CI, 0.05–0.63]) and the composite of all‐cause death and MI (HR, 0.19 [95% CI, 0.06–0.61]) only in those with adequate glycemic control. In contrast, the prognostic benefits of optimal revascularization were not statistically significant in patients with poor glycemic control (P>0.05) (Table S6).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Subgroup analyses revealed that age, LVEF, and prior MI were significantly associated with the relationship between optimal recanalization and reduced risk of cardiovascular death/TVMI (P for interaction=<0.001, <0.002, and <0.005, respectively), as well as all‐cause death/MI (P for interaction=0.001, 0.003, and 0.003, respectively) (Tables S7 and S8). The correlation between optimal recanalization and reduced risk of clinical outcomes remained stable across several subgroups stratified by atrial fibrillation, sex, obesity, smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, previous recanalization, CTO lesion in the right coronary artery, CTO lesion in the left anterior descending artery, Rentrop grade, Werner classification of collateral circulation grade, J‐CTO score, PROGRESS‐CTO score, and intravascular ultrasound guidance (P for interaction>0.05; Tables S7 and S8). In sensitivity analyses, the association between optimal recanalization and reduced risk of clinical outcomes remained significant after excluding participants who experienced adverse events within 30 days, those without dual antiplatelet therapy, those not receiving anti‐HF medications, and those with 2 to 3 CTO lesions (Table S9).

DISCUSSION

This study assessed the correlation between optimal CTO‐PCI and clinical outcomes over 3 years in patients with HF with or without T2D. Our major finding was that optimal CTO‐PCI in patients with HF was significantly associated with decreased risk of clinical outcomes, and the association was especially significant in patients with T2D.

Recently, the number of successful CTO‐PCI procedures has notably increased despite ongoing debate about its clinical benefits. Emerging evidence points to several advantages. 19 These include improved anginal symptoms, reduced need for antianginal medications, better exercise capacity, enhanced left ventricular function, reduced arrhythmia risk, fewer coronary artery bypass grafts, and improved outcomes during future coronary occlusion events, such as acute coronary syndrome, compared with failed CTO‐PCI cases. Notably, CTOs are a common barrier to achieving optimal revascularization. Studies indicate that suboptimal revascularization results in poorer outcomes than optimal revascularization for patients experiencing CTOs. 6 , 20 , 21 Clinically, many patients with HF and CTO undergo incomplete PCI to minimize high radiation exposure, procedural failure rates, and inadequate revascularization of other lesions. 22 The long‐term efficacy of optimal revascularization for patients with both CTO and HF remains insufficiently explored, lacking comprehensive outcome data. In our study, we first found that optimal revascularization of CTO lesions in patients with HF significantly improves long‐term prognoses.

The clinical benefits of optimal revascularization for CTO lesions in patients with HF primarily stem from restoring blood flow to the underperfused myocardium, which can reverse left ventricular dysfunction. 7 Hoebers et al 23 conducted a meta‐analysis of 34 studies involving 2243 patients, revealing a significant 4.44% LVEF increase following successful CTO‐PCI after 1 to 36 months of follow‐up. Although a 5% LVEF improvement is typically considered clinically significant, this meta‐analysis may have underestimated the impact due to study heterogeneity, including differences in cohort sizes, CTO definitions, locations, success criteria, imaging methods, and follow‐up durations. 24 However, in regard to occlusion of the target CTO vessel, LVEF was similar and even relatively worse than that at baseline (0.15%). This result might be explained by the loss of the protective effect of collaterals after the initial restoration of antegrade flow. 25 Moreover, 8 studies reported significant decreases in left ventricular end‐diastolic volume. 25 Galassi et al 26 demonstrated significant LVEF improvement 6 months following successful CTO‐PCI in patients with severely impaired LVEF. Cardona et al 27 also found significant LVEF improvements and reduced end‐systolic volume following successful CTO‐PCI. Therefore, percutaneous revascularization of CTOs affecting viable myocardial regions of hemodynamic importance can enhance LVEF and reduce adverse remodeling, contributing positively to clinical outcomes.

Our findings highlight the prognostic benefits of optimal CTO revascularization in patients with HF, particularly in those with T2D. T2D exacerbates coronary atherosclerosis and microvascular dysfunction, which might contribute to both CTO complexity and HF progression. 28 In patients with T2D, chronic hyperglycemia and insulin resistance promote endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and myocardial fibrosis, which may accelerate adverse remodeling in HF and reduce revascularization efficacy. 29 Prior studies have reported conflicting outcomes of CTO‐PCI in patients with diabetes, with some suggesting reduced procedural success rates due to diffuse calcification or multivessel disease, whereas others propose sustained benefits in symptom relief and clinical outcomes when technical success is achieved. 10 , 30 , 31 Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating that successful CTO recanalization in patients with diabetes and HF is associated with a marked reduction in composite cardiovascular events, potentially reflecting improved myocardial perfusion and reverse remodeling in this population. The interaction between diabetes and HF in CTO management may also relate to metabolic derangements. Specifically, insulin resistance and elevated free fatty acids in T2D impair myocardial energetics, exacerbating ischemia in territories supplied by CTO vessels, 32 , 33 and optimal revascularization might mitigate this by restoring oxygen supply–demand balance, particularly in hibernating myocardium, which is prevalent in patients with HF and CTO. 34

Our study also highlights the prognostic benefits of optimal CTO revascularization in patients with HF and T2D who maintain adequate glycemic control. Although prior cardiovascular outcome trials, such as Veterans Affairs Diabetes Trial (VADT), 35 Action to Control Cardiovascular Risk in Diabetes (ACCORD) trial, 36 and Action in Diabetes and Vascular Disease: Preterax and Diamicron Modified Release Controlled Evaluation (ADVANCE) study, 37 reported no significant reduction in cardiovascular events with intensive glycemic control compared with standard care. Other studies, including Diabetes Control and Complications Trial/Epidemiology of Diabetes Interventions and Complications (DCCT/EDIC) study 38 and United Kingdom Prospective Diabetes Study (UKPDS), 39 demonstrated that sustained glycemic management may attenuate long‐term cardiovascular risks. These conflicting findings suggest that the impact of glycemic control may depend on patient‐specific factors or therapeutic contexts. Our analysis adds a novel perspective to this debate by demonstrating that glycemic control status significantly modulates the effectiveness of revascularization in patients with T2D. Specifically, patients with well‐controlled blood glucose (FBG ≤7 mmol/L and HbA1C ≤6.5%) derived greater prognostic benefits from CTO revascularization compared with those with suboptimal control. This divergence may reflect the role of hyperglycemia in exacerbating microvascular dysfunction, endothelial inflammation, and myocardial remodeling, factors that could diminish revascularization outcome durability. 28 , 32 Conversely, adequate glycemic control may stabilize the coronary microenvironment, enhancing the functional and structural benefits of revascularization. 28 , 32 These findings align with emerging evidence that glycemic control synergizes with PCI to optimize outcomes. For instance, improved glycemic status may enhance endothelial repair mechanisms or reduce plaque vulnerability, thereby amplifying the therapeutic gains of revascularization, 40 underscoring the need for integrating rigorous diabetes management into the periprocedural care of patients with T2D, CTO, and HF.

In addition, our study found that among patients undergoing suboptimal revascularization, postoperative TIMI 1 to 2 slow blood flow emerged as the strongest predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, whereas statistical significance was not observed for major branch occlusion or residual stenosis >30%, consistent with previous literature highlighting the critical role of TIMI flow in determining prognosis. Suboptimal recanalization is strongly associated with periprocedural and long‐term MI, particularly when using the fourth universal MI definition, which may better capture myocardial injury than the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions criteria. 41 , 42 Mechanistically, TIMI 1 to 2 flow likely exacerbates myocardial vulnerability through transient ischemia and collateral network disruption, whereas persistent outflow impairment may drive late MI via restenosis or reocclusion. 43 , 44 Notably, procedural complexities, such as prolonged CTO attempts leading to suboptimal stent deployment, and structural complications (including uncovered hematomas) may synergistically impair recovery in these patients. 22 Although side branch occlusion contributed to MI events, its prognostic impact appeared attenuated compared with TIMI flow abnormalities, potentially reflecting compensatory mechanisms in stable collaterals. These findings indicate that impaired microvascular perfusion, rather than anatomic residual stenosis alone, may be the primary cause of poor outcomes, emphasizing the need for intraprocedural strategies to optimize coronary flow in this high‐risk cohort.

Current guidelines do not specify management strategies for patients with CTO and HF. Achieving optimal revascularization in patients with HF and CTO is complex owing to anatomical and patient factors. 45 Patients with HF often present with more comorbidities and may struggle to tolerate the hemodynamic effects and contrast load from complex procedures. CTOs in HF frequently involve multivessel and diffuse disease. 46 , 47 These challenges suggest inherent procedural risks associated with CTO‐PCI, potentially exacerbated in patients with HF. However, recent advancements in CTO‐PCI techniques and technology have substantially improved outcomes. Procedural protocols, including the hybrid algorithm, provide structured approaches for these cases. 48 Improved devices, such as guidewires and microcatheters, have enabled expert operators to achieve 85% to 90% success rates, 49 which must be weighed against potential procedural risks, especially with retrograde approaches. 50 Our study suggests that optimal revascularization could be a viable target for improving clinical outcomes in patients with HF. Future studies should investigate the impact of contemporary CTO‐PCI through well‐designed randomized controlled trials.

To the best of our knowledge, this study may be the first and largest to highlight the significance of CTO‐PCI in predicting outcomes for patients with HF. However, some limitations should be considered. First, the study used a prospective observational design rather than a randomized controlled trial, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. Second, there were insufficient data on glycemic control, LVEF, medication use, risk factor management, and lifestyle changes over the follow‐up period. Future studies should aim to incorporate related measurements to provide a more comprehensive understanding of those dynamic changes’ effect on clinical outcomes. Third, despite adjusting for multiple covariates, the potential for unmeasured confounding remains. Importantly, our observational design lacked a non‐PCI control group, which precludes a direct comparison with no revascularization and limits risk–benefit assessment. Residual confounding by indication is likely, given that clinical factors influenced the decision to perform PCI. Therefore, our findings are hypothesis‐generating and require confirmation in randomized controlled trials. Fourth, the procedural outcome was not a random or elective decision by the operators, but was largely determined by intrinsic lesion complexity. Although we adjusted for a comprehensive set of 36 covariates, including detailed angiographic and complexity scores, residual confounding by baseline lesion characteristics cannot be fully eliminated. Unlike a randomized trial, our observational design cannot balance unmeasured or imprecisely measured anatomical factors between the outcome groups. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating, and confirmation in future randomized controlled trials is required.

In conclusion, optimal revascularization via CTO‐PCI in patients with HF was associated with a lower risk of adverse cardiovascular events, compared with failed CTO‐PCI, particularly in those with T2D.
