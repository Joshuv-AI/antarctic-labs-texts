Aortic atresia, interrupted hypoplastic right aortic arch, single ventricle and bilateral arterial duct – a unique anatomy with surgical palliation
Authors: Edward Malec, Pawel Dryzek, Joanna Blasiak, Katarzyna Januszewska

Background

Aortic atresia (AA) with interrupted aortic arch (IAA) is an extremely rare anatomical and physiological association. The newborn can survive if there is sufficient blood flow in the ascending aorta, supplying the brain and the coronary circulation from additional lesions, like as bilateral arterial duct, aorto-pulmonary window, double aortic arch or a pulmonary-to-coronary fistula [1–4].

We present a newborn with AA, right hypoplastic IAA, {S, L, L} transposition of the great arteries, double inlet left ventricle (DILV) and bilateral arterial duct, who was successfully surgically palliated.

Case presentation

Male newborn after 36-week pregnancy born with a weight of 2.7 kg was transferred to our hospital shortly after birth with prenatal diagnosis of double inlet left ventricle, interrupted aortic arch and common arterial trunk. Prostaglandin infusion was started shortly after birth. The child remained stable with normal acid/base balance and normal renal and neurological function. Additionally, our patient was affected by the partial duplication of chromosome 21.

Echocardiography revealed a complex heart disease {S, L, L} transposition of the great arteries, an AA, hypoplastic right IAA, the atrioventricular valves connected with only one well developed, morphologically left ventricle (DILV), bilateral arterial duct and very small left sided ascending aorta (AscAo). The blood flow in the 1.5–2 mm diameter AscAo, was retrograde.

Additionally computed tomography was performed which confirmed the diagnosis and clarified the detailed anatomy: aortic atresia, hypoplastic right aortic arch with interruption between right and left subclavian arteries (the first vessel from the proximal arch was the left common carotid artery, the second right common carotid artery and the last one right subclavian artery), very small left and anterior sided AscAo and bilateral arterial ducts (Fig. 1A-B).

A-B Computed tomography of the preoperative anatomy

On the 7th day of life, the child underwent bilateral pulmonary artery banding. 15 days later cardiac catheterization was performed and confirmed a complex form of interrupted right aortic arch and bilateral arterial duct. The right aortic arch was interrupted between the right and left subclavian artery. Blood supply to the very hypoplastic AscAo and coronary arteries was provided by right arterial duct (R-PDA), connecting the right pulmonary artery (RPA) and the right subclavian artery. Through the left arterial duct (L-PDA), the descending aorta (DescAo) was perfused as well as the left subclavian artery. The R-PDA was tortuous and distinctly smaller in caliber than the L-PDA which was contiguous with and the same size as the DescAo. Two stents were placed in both ducti. The R-PDA was not completely covered by the stent due to tortuous course of the vessel (the stent was too short and the placement of the second one was not possible).

Prostaglandin E1 infusion was discontinued. Few hours after stopping prostaglandin infusion the child started to deteriorate. Echocardiography revealed depressed ventricular function and significantly reduced diameter of the distal part of the R-PDA, which was not covered by stent. Increased troponin T level was noticed. Prostaglandin infusion was restarted with clinical improvement of the child, normalization of the ventricular function and troponin level. Decision was made not to place another stent but to perform modified Norwood procedure with reconstruction of the aortic arch. The operation was performed at the age of almost 8 weeks with arterial cannulation of the main pulmonary artery and the right atrial appendage for cardiopulmonary bypass. The procedure was undertaken at deep hypothermia with 48 min of circulatory arrest. During the cooling the bilateral pulmonary bandings were removed and the right and left pulmonary arteries were snared. During the circulatory arrest the arterial ducti were transected and both stents as well as ductal tissues were removed. The main pulmonary artery and very small ascending aorta were transected at the same level. Both orifices of the pulmonary arteries were divided (as a one button) from the proximal main pulmonary artery. The proximal AscAo and proximal pulmonary artery was anastomosed in side-to-side fashion with resorbable suture. The distal AscAo and proximal arch were incised along the minor curvature. After mobilization of the arch, the distal AscAo was anastomosed with the distal arch and the proximal arch (after the origin of the right subclavian artery) with the proximal main pulmonary artery on its right side. The entire aortic arch and distal part of AscAo were augmented with pulmonary homograft patch. The reconstructed aortic arch was anastomosed with the double aorto-pulmonary root (proximal end of transected pulmonary artery -neo aorta and proximal end of AscAo) (Fig. 2A-B). During a rewarming a systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunt (PTFE tube, 6 mm in diameter, Venaflo II ePTFE Vascular Graft, Bard Peripheral Vascular, Tempe, AZ) from distal pulmonary artery (common button of the pulmonary arteries) to the left ventricle was constructed (the shunt was right-sided in relation to the neo-aorta). After rewarming and weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass the diameter of the shunt was reduced using vascular clips, to receive balance between systemic to pulmonary circulation. The patient was weaned from cardio-pulmonary bypass and the chest was closed.

A-B Schema of preoperative anatomy and surgical technique of aortic arch reconstruction

Postoperative echocardiography showed good ventricular function, no regurgitation of the neo-aortic and atrioventricular valves, and systolic gradient across the aortic arch of 10mmHg.

The child was extubated on the postoperative day 3. He was discharged home 3 weeks after surgery, gaining weight and clinically well.

Due to retrospective nature of the study, as well as lack of the presented in the manuscript personal identifiable details the approval from the institutional review board, as well as informed consent of the patients’ guardians was not required.

Discussion and conclusions

The survival of a newborn with aortic atresia and interrupted aortic arch is only possible due to the coexistence of other heart defects and vascular malformations [1–4]. In our case, the presence of bilateral arterial ducts, supplied sufficient flow to the whole systemic circulation (through the R-PDA to the head, right arm and coronary circulation, through L-PDA to the left arm and lower body). Additionally in the presented patient it was a heart with single ventricle physiology and interrupted right aortic arch.

When planning the therapeutic procedure, we took into account two palliative options: hybrid or surgical. The decision regarding the hybrid procedure was considered as less risky allowing the child time to gain weight. After the bilateral banding of the pulmonary arteries with consecutive placement of two stents in both arterial ducts and discontinuation of prostaglandin, the child’s condition deteriorated in a short time. Echocardiography showed that there was reduction in the lumen of the distal segment of the right arterial duct, which was responsible for supplying the AscAo and coronary circulation. The location of the R-PDA in this infant was from the right pulmonary artery to the confluence of the right carotid and right subclavian artery, characteristic of persistent right six primitive aortic arch [5]. The R-PDA was much smaller in caliber and longer with tortuosity than L-PDA. Probably that was the problem with complete stenting of R-PDA and reason to contract after stopping the prostaglandin infusion. The perfusion through the R-PDA was crucial for the survival of the child, as this was the only source of the coronary circulation supply (deterioration of the ventricular function due to cardiac ischemia, which was a life-threatening condition, was observed after the discontinuation of the PGE1 infusion and constriction of the not covered by the stent part of the R-PDA). In similar cases reported in the literature [2, 3] there was no situation of coronary perfusion dependency on ductal patency. I our child an implantation of an additional stent in the R-PDA was assessed as too risky due to the total dependence of coronary circulation on the patency of this duct. Hence the decision not to place additional stent into the R-PDA but to perform a modified Norwood operation with reconstruction of the aortic arch which was assessed to be safer. At the age of only two months the child was in our opinion too young to perform combine first (i.e. Norwood procedure) and second (i.e. bidirectional Glenn anastomosis) stage of single ventricle palliation.

To our knowledge, this is a unique case of a newborn with aortic atresia, an interrupted hypoplastic right aortic arch with a heart with single ventricle physiology described in the literature. Only one case with AA and IAA described in the literature was successfully palliated (by the hybrid procedure) [4]. Our child was finally palliated surgically, using modified Norwood procedure and at the age of 6 months bidirectional Glenn procedure with division of the systemic-to-pulmonary artery shunt. We anticipate Fontan repair pathway. In spite of the partial duplication of chromosome 21 the child at the age of one year already, develops completely normal without delay and does not present the phenotype of trisomy 21.
