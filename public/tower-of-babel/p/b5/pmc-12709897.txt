Exploring the depth of the maize canopy LAI detected by spectroscopy based on simulations and in situ measurements
Authors: Jinpeng Cheng, Jiao Wang, Dan Zhao, Fenghui Duan, Qiang Wu, Yongliang Lai, Jianbo Qi, Shuping Xiong, Hongbo Qiao, Xinming Ma, Hao Yang, Guijun Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe vertical distribution of leaves plays a crucial role in the growth process of maize. Understanding the vertical spectral characteristics of maize leaves is crucial for monitoring their growth. However, accurate estimation of the vertical distribution of leaf area remains a significant challenge in practical investigations. To address this, we used a 3D RTM to simulate the layered canopy spectra of maize, revealing the impact of canopy structure on remote sensing penetration depth across different growth stages and planting densities. The results of this study revealed differences in detection depth across growth stages. During the early growth stage, the depth was concentrated in the bottom 1 to 3 leaves of the canopy, reaching 1 to 4 leaves at the ear stage and 1 to 7 leaves during the grain-filling stage. The planting density had a notable effect on the detection depth at the bottom of the canopy. Moreover, compared with the other spectral bands, the near-infrared spectral range exhibited greater sensitivity to density variations. In terms of LAI inversion, a FuseBell-Hybrid model was constructed. We analyzed VIs across different planting density and canopy structural scenarios and found that compared with lower layers, increased density reduced the relative change rate in the upper leaf layers. The sensitivity patterns differed between plant architectures: VIred exhibited density-dependent sensitivity, with distinct responses between plant types, and MTVI2 demonstrated optimal performance for mid-canopy monitoring. This study highlights the influence of the heterogeneous structural characteristics of maize canopies on remote sensing detection depth during different phenological stages, providing theoretical support for enhancing multilayer crop monitoring in precision agriculture.Keywords: 3D radiative transfer model, Vertical LAI distribution, UAV remote sensing, Maize, Detection depth

Introduction

The leaf area index (LAI) is a critical indicator reflecting crop growth, productivity, and photosynthetic capacity [1]. The leaves of maize are the primary sites for physiological activities, such as photosynthesis, respiration, and transpiration [2]. However, under field conditions, the canopy LAI of maize is influenced by several factors, such as nutrient status, leaf senescence, nitrogen absorption and transport, and the light environment. This results in a vertically distributed and highly heterogeneous structure [3]. Changes in the vertical LAI are related to leaf profile height and serve as key determinants of energy, water, and nutrient fluxes [4]. In terms of photosynthesis, the contributions of leaves at different vertical intervals vary significantly [5]. Therefore, studying the vertical and heterogeneous distributions of the LAI is highly important.

Traditional LAI surveys often rely on manual measurements, which require extensive labor and are time-consuming [3]. In contrast, remote sensing, a flexible and efficient technology for acquiring farmland environmental and crop growth information, has been widely applied in agricultural production and research. Employing remote sensing as a nondestructive, rapid, large-scale, and cost-effective method for the investigation of the maize LAI enables a more comprehensive and timely understanding of plant growth [6].

Although studies have indicated a certain relationship between light transmission and reflection in the canopy and leaf area [7], many of these investigations are based on simplified 1D canopy models [8]. Information regarding the vertical distribution of crop parameters remains limited. For crops, such as maize, which exhibit strong vertical heterogeneity, the relatively consistent appearance of leaves generally follows a bell-shaped distribution pattern [9,10]. The light environment varies across different leaf layers, with several factors, such as shading, leaf irradiance, and extinction coefficients, which is due to scattering and absorption losses influencing leaf layer development [11]. From this perspective, previous studies have quantitatively analyzed vertical heterogeneity in relation to variations in nitrogen content and total chlorophyll [12,13]. However, these studies have yet to address in detail how deeply remote sensing can penetrate.

From agronomic and remote sensing perspectives, the canopy coverage of maize significantly changes during different growth stages, greatly reducing sensor performance when the middle and lower leaf layers are observed. Theoretically, vertical leaf layers contribute differently to spectral reflectance, which underscores the importance of using quantitative remote sensing techniques to investigate these contributions and their impact on the LAI [14].

Currently, many empirical statistical and physical methods have been developed using single or multiple sensors to extract key crop information from remote sensing data for precise monitoring [15]. Among empirical methods, vegetation indices (VIs) are widely used because of their simple construction and strong correlation with target parameters [16]. For example, Haboudane et al. [17] proposed an algorithm for predicting the green LAI of crop canopies. Yu et al. [18] estimated the leaf chlorophyll content (LCC) by reducing structural effects using spectral indices. These VIs are typically used to evaluate crop physiological parameters. However, such methods may not fully meet the requirements for quantitative crop remote sensing. For instance, the NDVI often presents saturation effects in regions with high vegetation cover, affecting retrieval accuracy [19]. Overall, empirical statistical methods are influenced by canopy structure, environmental conditions, and crop variety, necessitating more robust remote sensing approaches to provide support [20].

Sensors on UAV platforms have unique advantages in capturing detailed feature information. Among them, hyperspectral cameras offer more spectral bands and higher spectral resolution [21,22]. However, the signals from passive optical remote sensing technologies originate primarily from the top of the canopy. As a result, canopy reflectance provides limited information about the distribution of vertical vegetation [23]. Canopy reflectance is also affected by the soil background, lighting conditions, observation geometry, and the shielding effect of different leaf layers under different planting densities [24]. These factors make accurate inversion of the LAI and exploration of the detection depth of passive sensors difficult.

As alternatives to empirical models, vegetation radiative transfer models can be used to address varying conditions [25]. Simulation-based methods can accurately simulate the radiation characteristics of real landscapes under various conditions with the support of a radiation transfer model. However, under the influence of crop heterogeneity, one-dimensional radiative transfer models often overlook gaps and distributions between plants, making it challenging to describe discontinuous vegetation canopies [26]. With respect to maize, which is tall and has large, broad leaves and deep canopies, significant vertical heterogeneity is observed in the LAI and LCC in complex farmland environments. This limits the application of 1D radiative transfer models in high-resolution remote sensing image simulations of maize. Although the relationship between canopy spectra and the actual LAI is well documented [27], information on the vertical distribution of maize canopy structures is relatively sparse. Despite the relatively constant leaves, the effects of uneven vertical distribution on leaf spectral reflectance cannot be ignored.

In recent years, three-dimensional radiative transfer models, such as DART [28], RAPID [29], and HELIOS [30], have become more comprehensive, detailed, and accurate. These models can simulate complex terrain, 3D scenes, and multiple scattering in landscapes and are capable of accounting for diffuse illumination from the atmosphere. This provides new perspectives for evaluating the expression of the LAI using spectral sensors through 3D radiative transfer model simulations.

This study aims to simulate the vertical distribution of different maize leaf layers by combining a 3D radiative transfer model (LESS) [31] with field-measured maize growth parameters. The objective is to derive the vertical LAI of large-scale maize canopies from spectral reflectance, thereby quantifying and characterizing the impact of maize vertical structural differences on remote sensing. Furthermore, this study explores the variation characteristics of maize under different planting densities and growth stages.

Materials

Study sites and experimental designs

The study was conducted at the China National Precision Agriculture Research Center, which is located in Changping District, Beijing, China (40.17°N, 116.43°E). This region is characterized by a warm temperate, semihumid to semiarid monsoon climate, with hot and rainy summers and cold, dry winters. The annual sunshine duration ranges from 2000 to 2800 ​h, with an average temperature of approximately 13.9 ​°C and an annual precipitation of approximately 450 ​mm. Field experiments on maize were carried out between 2018 and 2022 and were designed to investigate the effects of varying planting densities and maize varieties. UAV spectral imagery data

From 2018 to 2019, remote sensing data were collected using a Parrot Sequoia sensor (MicaSense Inc., USA) mounted on a DJI PHANTOM 4 PRO drone (SZ DJI Technology Co., China). From 2021 to 2022, data acquisition was conducted using a DJI PHANTOM 4 Multispectral drone (SZ DJI Technology Co., China). To ensure data quality, all the UAV operations were carried out between 10:30 and 14:30, covering the areas of Exp 1 to Exp 4. The flight plan was designed with an 80 ​% overlap both across- and along-track at a flight altitude of 30–50 ​m.

The Parrot Sequoia sensor is equipped with four lenses that capture reflectance in the following spectral bands: 550 ​nm (green, bandwidth ​= ​40 ​nm), 660 ​nm (red, bandwidth ​= ​40 ​nm), 735 ​nm (red edge, bandwidth ​= ​10 ​nm), and 790 ​nm (near-infrared, bandwidth ​= ​40 ​nm). After data acquisition, image processing was performed using Agisoft Metashape to generate orthophoto maps, and radiometric calibration was conducted following the method proposed by Yang [34].

The P4M sensor includes five spectral bands: blue (450 ​nm ​± ​16 ​nm), green (560 ​nm ​± ​16 ​nm), red (650 ​nm ​± ​16 ​nm), red edge (730 ​nm ​± ​16 ​nm), and near-infrared (840 ​nm ​± ​26 ​nm). Image stitching, geographic alignment, and radiometric calibration were carried out using Terra v2.3.3 software (SZ DJI Technology Co., China) to produce orthophoto maps.

Hyperspectral data acquisition and processing

During each growth stage of maize, hyperspectral data were collected from fully expanded leaves at different layers of the main stem using an ASD FieldSpec3 spectroradiometer (Analytical Spectral Devices Inc., Boulder, CO, USA) equipped with a leaf clip. The device covers a spectral range of 350–2500 ​nm, with sampling intervals of 1.4 ​nm (350–1000 ​nm) and 2 ​nm (1000–2500 ​nm) and a resampling interval of 1 ​nm. To minimize interference with the leaf spectra, spectral data were obtained on both sides of the spectrum, avoiding the center of the vein.

To ensure data quality, whiteboard radiation calibration is performed every 3 ​min. The hyperspectral data were preprocessed using ViewSpectPro software, which included reflectance transformation and binary export of the data for subsequent analysis.

Modeling of the vertical leaf area distribution

In this study, a FuseBell–Hybrid model was developed to predict the vertical leaf area distribution (LAD) at the canopy scale. First, a hybrid approach that combines the coupled LESS model and the CNN architecture for the inversion of the LAI was used. The LESS model was utilized to generate a pretraining dataset, which was used to pretrain the model to acquire prior knowledge [45]. The knowledge was subsequently transferred to the retraining model, and partially measured data were used to fine-tune the model, which was ultimately applied to predict the true LAI across multiple growth stages over two years. The deep learning network model adopted was a 1D CNN, consisting of an input layer, convolutional layers, a fully connected layer, and an output layer, with the network structure illustrated in Fig. 4.

1D CNN architecture for maize true LAI retrieval.

Afterward, we obtained the vertical LAD from the modeled LAI using a configured bell-shaped function. The bell-shaped function framework originally proposed by Dwyer and Stewart [46] primarily characterizes the variation patterns of individual leaves in maize plants. This mathematical approach demonstrates high accuracy in modeling the distribution of leaf area across different vertical canopy layers. Here, we linked the LAI modeled from the spectral data with the LAD of the largest leaf layer and then used the bell function to decipher the LAD of each leaf layer. Results

Spatiotemporal distribution of the LAI across different leaf layers under various treatments

The vertical distribution of the LAD differed between flat and compact maize genotypes (Fig. 5). Under high-density planting conditions, flat maize experienced a mutual shading effect on the bottom leaves at the 6-leaf stage (Fig. 5-a), reducing the bottom leaf area by 15 ​% compared with that under low-density planting conditions. By the R1 stage (Fig. 5-c), the leaf area became highly concentrated in the upper canopy (the upper leaf area reached 0.5 ​m2/m2), while the area of the bottom leaves almost completely senesced to less than 0.05 ​m2/m2. In contrast, the compact maize displayed density tolerance: at the 12-leaf stage (Fig. 5-f), under high density, 65 ​% of its leaf area was concentrated in the middle canopy layers with high photosynthetic efficiency (the leaf area of layers 6–8 was 0.4 ​m2/m2). By the R3 stage (Fig. 5-h), the leaf area above 0.3 ​m2/m2 was maintained throughout 80 ​% of the canopy depth, with the total leaf area being 40 ​% greater than that of the flat maize at the same density. Critically, under moderate density, the compact genotypes optimized vertical resource allocation, consistently maintaining the leaf area in layers 12–16 above 0.25 ​m2/m2 during the reproductive growth stages. Conversely, the plate-shaped maize suffered irreversible light competition damage because of structural collapse of the lower canopy (bottom leaf area below 0.05 ​m2/m2 at R3 under high density).

Spectral response to canopies at different leaf depths

In this study, 15-leaf-stage maize was employed, and sequential leaf removal was implemented from the bottom to the top (Fig. 6). The LAI demonstrated an overall linear decline with decreasing numbers of leaves, decreasing from 6.9 for all 15 leaves to 0.91 under single-leaf conditions, while accelerated attenuation occurred following removal of the middle leaf layer; notably, a 27.6 ​% decrease in the LAI occurred between the 12-leaf stage and the 10-leaf stage. Conversely, the leaf-to-background ratio (LBR) displayed a nonmonotonic response characterized by an initial 13.3 ​% increase (from 72.13 ​% at 15 leaves to 81.7 ​% at 9 leaves), peaking at the 8-leaf stage. It decreased to 21.05 ​% for a single leaf. The canopy structure exhibited dual critical thresholds: retention of 10–11 leaves maintained the LBR within peak values (78.12–81.7 ​%) with an LAI >55 ​% of the baseline, indicating high photosynthetic efficiency of the canopy, whereas reduction to ≤8 leaves triggered synchronous declines (18.8 ​% LBR decrease and 31.5 ​% LAI reduction from 8 to 6 leaves), indicating a structural shift in the canopy toward soil background dominance. The correlation between the LBR and LAI reversed during the leaf removal process. When 9–15 leaves were retained, the LBR increased because of the enhanced reflectance from the reduced shading of the middle leaves, indicating a negative correlation with the LAI (R2 ​= ​0.90). When the leaf number was ≤8, the contribution of the background soil increased dramatically, leading to a positive correlation (R2 ​= ​0.95). This confirmed that the middle leaf layer was the primary contributor to canopy sensing depth, with the spectral characteristics of the apical leaves progressively converging toward the soil background following lower-layer removal, which is consistent with the observed sensing depth patterns.

To investigate the sensitivity of the overall structure of maize plants to remote sensing techniques, in this study, the LESS model was used to simulate the maize spectra. The leaf layer removal sequence adopted in this study followed a bottom-up approach, starting from the lowest leaf layer of the canopy and progressing upward incrementally, with the spectral changes shown in Fig. 7.

This study revealed that during the seedling stage, the stepwise removal of bottom leaves caused only minor changes in reflectance, with the sensing depth primarily concentrated on the first to third leaves from the bottom. As maize grew and developed, the sensing depth range gradually narrowed. During the tasseling stage, the depth response extended from the first to the fourth leaves, whereas during the grain-filling stage, the sensing range further contracted to the first seven leaves. Notably, significant spectral changes were observed after the removal of the bottom four leaves, indicating that the middle leaves (from the 5th to the 12th) are particularly critical for contributing to canopy sensing depth. Using a stepwise removal approach from the bottom upward, it was found that when only the top two to three leaves remained, their spectral characteristics gradually resembled those of the soil spectra.

Sensitivity of the LAI to canopy spectral information under different scenarios

To further verify the conclusions drawn in Section 4.2, ten commonly used VIs were constructed to evaluate their sensitivity to the stepwise removal of maize leaves from the bottom to the top (Fig. 8). On the basis of the relative changes in canopy spectral information and the LAI under different density scenarios in the LESS-simulated dataset, the relative changes ranged from 0.1 to 0.8. Three scenarios—low, medium, and high densities—were plotted to highlight the effects of density on the sensitivity of the leaf layer.

Relative changes in different maize densities and plant types during different growth stages (a full view is provided in Fig. A2).

The results revealed that under different scenarios, the shapes of the spectral reflectance curves probing changes at various leaf layer depths were similar. However, as the planting density increased, the changes in the upper leaf layers gradually decreased compared with those in the bottom layers. Under low-density conditions, compact maize-type canopies exhibited heightened sensitivity in the upper leaves: the MCARI values increased by 0.058 from layers 15 to 13 (compared with only a 0.004 increase in the flat-type canopy). In high-density environments, flat maize types showed more pronounced responses in the middle layers (MCARI change of 0.163 across layers 15-8). Among them, VIred was density sensitive: the compactness type decreased by 0.061 in the middle layer under low density, whereas the flat type increased by 0.124; both increased under high density, but the compactness type remained 30 ​% greater. MTVI2 demonstrated optimal performance for middle canopy monitoring (layers 6–9). Saturation predominantly occurred in the bottom leaves (layers 1–4) for the NDVI, with fluctuations remaining within ±0.01 across all canopy types and densities. Although the performance of the other indices is average for characterizing canopy spectral information, it still has a certain reference value.

Inversion of the vertical LAD

The FuseBell-Hybrid model demonstrated robust performance in LAI inversion, with R2 values of 0.77 for the training set, 0.74 for validation, and 0.71 for testing (Fig. 9). These results confirmed the generalizability of the model and the effective mitigation of overfitting in LAI estimation for heterogeneous vegetation canopies. The test performance surpassed that of conventional vegetation index-based empirical methods, validating the advantages of integrating radiative transfer modeling with transfer learning architectures.

Discussion

In this study, the LESS model was employed to simulate maize spectra under varying plant morphologies, densities, and multigrowth stages. It specifically addresses the spectral characteristics of maize vertical structures by conducting layer-by-layer spectral simulations for different leaves and analyzing the spectral response patterns of the LAI. Additionally, the impact of maize leaf phenotypic traits on their spectra was investigated, thereby assessing the contribution of maize leaves to canopy spectra under different conditions.

Effects of different plant morphologies and densities on maize spectra

The field-measured traits of all the maize leaves revealed that both the bottom and upper leaves exhibited low sensitivity to spectral changes. Compared with the middle and upper leaf layers, the bottom leaves were more affected by soil and shading from taller maize leaves (Lei et al., 2019b; [47]), which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [48]. The insensitivity of bottom leaves to spectral changes may be due to their smaller size and limited leaf area as new leaves grow, resulting in less absorption and reflection of spectral energy, making subtle spectral changes more difficult to capture. The significant differences between the bottom leaves and other leaf layers can be explained by shading effects. At lower positions on the plant, these leaves have lower chlorophyll contents during nutrient absorption periods and tend to senesce, weakening their spectral response[24].

According to earlier research, the strong vertical heterogeneity of biochemical properties within the maize canopy and differences in light interception significantly affect canopy spectral reflectance [49]. This explains why bottom leaves have a weaker response to spectral changes. When the upper leaves are at relatively high positions, as the budding leaves expand and unfold, they are usually upright [9,50], limiting the sensor's ability to effectively monitor these leaves. These findings were also verified in Section 4.3. Additionally, variations in maize plant architecture were analyzed, revealing that differences in canopy structure due to plant type influence spectral changes. The spectral curves during the seedling, ear, and grain-filling stages displayed similar shapes, with consistent positions of peaks and troughs. These findings indicate that basic spectral change patterns were not significantly altered across growth stages or plant types. Both the field measurements and the simulation results aligned with expectations.

As the planting density increased, competition among the maize plants intensified, leading to a decrease in photosynthetically active radiation use efficiency. Consequently, the simulated results for leaf depth were influenced by density variations. At low densities, bottom leaves significantly affected the remote sensing detection depth, whereas at medium and high densities, the influence of bottom leaves became negligible. This study focused primarily on medium-density results, with low- and high-density evaluations varying slightly around the medium-density findings. As shown in Fig. 7, the detection depth varied across maize plant types and growth stages.

Thus, plant architecture and planting density are key factors influencing canopy spectral characteristics. Different plant types and densities result in significant differences in spectral reflectance across various bands. However, despite these differences, the spectral characteristics in the visible and near-infrared bands are relatively consistent across the leaf layers (Fig. 7).

Specifically, the differences in spectral reflectance were minimal in the visible bands but significantly greater in the near-infrared bands as the planting density increased. This phenomenon indicates that the near-infrared band is more sensitive to spectral differences caused by density changes, a finding corroborated by related studies [51].

The key finding of this study is that both the planting density and the maize variety significantly influence critical canopy structural parameters, particularly the vertical distribution of the LAD and the leaf inclination angle, which subsequently govern the ability of different spectral bands to penetrate the canopy depth [52,53]. Increased planting density exacerbates intraplant competition, leading to reduced photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) use efficiency and altering the geometric structure of canopy light interception [54]. Our simulations clearly demonstrate that this structural shift significantly modulates spectral detection depth: estimates of detection depth were substantially influenced by bottom leaves under low-density conditions, whereas their impact became negligible at medium and high densities. Variations in maize varieties inherently result in differences in canopy structure, such as leaf erectness and compactness. Although spectral curve shapes (peak and trough positions) remain relatively consistent across different growth stages and varieties, the primary insight lies in how structural differences affect the penetration ability of various spectral bands. The differences in reflectance between the leaf layers are minimal and relatively insensitive to the structural changes induced by density, suggesting that the effective detection depth in the visible spectrum is shallow and primarily captures information from well-illuminated leaves. This penetration capacity is quickly attenuated by the dense upper canopy, making it difficult to effectively assess the status of the lower canopy, especially under high-density conditions[55]. In contrast, the near-infrared spectrum is strongly influenced by multiple scattering within the canopy, enabling it to sample information from deeper internal leaf layers more effectively than the visible bands.

Effects of the phenotypic traits of upright maize leaves on their spectra

The results of this study revealed that the overly upright nature of the upper maize leaves caused differences in the results compared with those of the other leaf layers, resulting in relatively poor R2 and RMSE values. This disparity is attributed to factors such as variations in sunlight distribution across growth stages and leaf layers, differences in intercepted and absorbed light energy, and aging effects.

To further identify the causes of these differences, the measured dataset was analyzed, revealing that the top three leaves, which exhibited significant differences, accounted for approximately 0–30 ​% of the total LAI. Additionally, two models (illustrated in Fig. A5) were used to evaluate the impact of erect and non-erect leaves on spectral reflectance.

The results revealed that under varying planting densities, the spectral reflectance in the near-infrared band differed by 0.05 between the upper upright and non-upright maize leaves.

Novelty and uncertainty of our method

Li et al. [56] focused primarily on improving canopy reflectance spectra for the estimation of crop physiological and biochemical parameters by optimizing environmental factors. However, this approach remains heavily constrained by environmental conditions. Jay et al. [15] suggested that for highly heterogeneous crops, PROSAIL inversion might outperform the VI. Additionally, the VI has certain limitations related to oversaturation and information extraction [57]. Therefore, this study combines UAV spectral data with the 3D radiative transfer model LESS to predict the sensing depth of passive optical sensors for maize canopies. On the basis of our research framework, the newly developed method, which integrates maize population scenarios with LESS applications, is efficient and nondestructive. It also offers new perspectives for optimizing UAV spectral data acquisition strategies. In addition, the application of a FuseBell hybrid to model the vertical LAI of maize proved effective. By utilizing multiyear datasets (Fig. 9), the fitting results between the measured and predicted LAIs demonstrated that the model achieved generally high prediction accuracy (as indicated by the R2 and RMSE values), particularly for the 2018, 2019, and 2021 datasets. The reduced effectiveness in modeling the top canopy layers within the validation dataset resulted mainly from two factors: (1) inconsistent growth stages caused by variations in maize varieties and planting densities and (2) significant fluctuations in leaf count per plant during field sampling, which affected data quality. Critically, the successful calibration of the hybrid method using 650 samples, coupled with the strong correlation observed between the predicted and measured LAI (Fig. 10), not only validates the ability of the LAI prediction but also indirectly confirms the ability of the model to capture key canopy structural features relevant to spectral responses. The robustness of this structural model across multiple annual datasets strongly supports its ability to interpret spectral detection depth results related to the LAI distribution and density effects.

While this study demonstrates the efficacy of combining UAV spectral data with the LESS model to predict sensing depth in maize canopies, we acknowledge that its broader applicability requires further validation. Although our framework accounts for variations in maize plant architecture and planting density, the use of crops with fundamentally different structural traits (e.g., layered canopies in sparse vegetation in arid crops) or unique biochemical properties (e.g., the distribution of chlorophyll in broadleaf vs. monocot species) may introduce new challenges. In addition, canopy reflectance is influenced by multiple environmental factors. The inability to comprehensively evaluate the combined effects of these factors on spectral variations may limit the generalizability of the results, making them challenging to apply to a wider range of crop types and ecosystems. Future research will focus on validating the method with other crops to explore its potential in broader application scenarios, aiming to provide innovative ideas and practical experiences for advancing remote sensing monitoring technology.

Conclusions

In this study, a 3D radiative transfer model (LESS) and an inversion model combined with prior knowledge were used to estimate the vertical distribution of maize LAI in heterogeneous scenarios. First, UAV datasets combined with the LESS model are used to simulate real field scenarios, and the effect of the spectral sensor sensing depth on the LAI is evaluated. Finally, the impact of maize plant heterogeneity on remote sensing depth detection was explored by progressively removing the leaf layers.
