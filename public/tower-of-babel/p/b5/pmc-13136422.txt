Beyond iron deficiency: A comprehensive national survey of anaemia etiology in Sri Lankan young adults
Authors: Dinusha Amarasingha, Ruwindi Silva, Lakshman Perera, Nirmani Wickramasinghe, Dammika Piyathunga, Lallindra Gooneratne, Renuka Jayatissa, Sugandi Dharmabandu, Ping Chen, Suthat Fucharoen, Shiromi Perera, Anuja Premawardhena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevious studies in Sri Lanka have explored the prevalence and causes of anaemia, mainly emphasizing iron deficiency while overlooking other important factors such as enzymopathies, membranopathies, and haemoglobinopathies. Moreover, many studies reported unexplained cases of anaemia even after detailed evaluation. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and underlying causes of anaemia among community-based young adults in Sri Lanka. A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from January 2023 to December 2024 among young adults aged 18–30 years. Data and blood samples were collected using a pretested questionnaire. Anaemic individuals underwent serum iron studies, CRP, vitamin B12 and folate assays, and thalassaemia screening. Those with uncharacterized anaemia were further assessed using red cell enzyme assays, EMA dye binding assays, and whole-exome sequencing. The mean (± SD) age of the study participants was 23.90 ± 1.98 years. Anaemia prevalence was 15.0%. The main causes were iron deficiency (49.3%), folate deficiency (27.8%), vitamin B12 deficiency (14.4%), and haemoglobinopathy traits were identified in 25.6% of anaemic individuals, frequently in combination with nutritional deficiencies. A substantial proportion of anaemic individuals exhibited coexisting aetiologies rather than a single cause. Initially, 17.4% remained uncharacterized, but advanced testing identified variants suggestive of hereditary spherocytosis and congenital dyserythropoietic anaemia or dyskeratosis congenita. This first community-based Sri Lankan study integrating advanced diagnostics revealed that, although iron deficiency predominates, genetic and enzymatic disorders contribute notably, highlighting the need for broader diagnostic strategies in anaemia screening in the community.Keywords: Anaemia, Enzymopathies, Haemoglobinopathies, Nutritional anaemia, Membranopathies, Uncharacterized anaemiaSubject terms: Diseases, Genetics, Health care, Medical research, Risk factors

Introduction

Anaemia is defined as the reduction in haemoglobin concentration in red blood cells (RBCs) below established cut-off values. Haemoglobin concentrations of less than 120 g/L in adult females and less than 130 g/L in adult males are the cutoff values for anaemia at sea level. The estimated global prevalence of anaemia was 24·3% in 20211–3.

The causative factors for anaemia in any population would include nutritional deficiencies of iron, folate, and vitamin B12, anaemia of acute and chronic disease (ACD), parasitological infestations, haemoglobinopathies, red cell enzyme disorders, and red cell membrane disorders in varying frequencies. Iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) is the most significant contributor to anaemia. Although the primary cause of anaemia is iron deficiency, it is hardly present in isolation. More frequently, it coexists with several other causes, such as other nutritional deficiencies, parasitic infestations, enzymopathies, membranopathies, and haemoglobinopathies. Importantly, thalassaemia carrier states are usually asymptomatic and, in isolation, are often insufficient to produce clinically significant anaemia. In many population settings, anaemia observed in individuals with thalassaemia traits is frequently attributable to coexisting nutritional deficiencies or other causes. Therefore, the presence of a carrier state should not automatically be interpreted as the sole aetiology of anaemia. Among individuals identified with β-thalassaemia trait, α-thalassaemia trait, or structural haemoglobin variants, a considerable proportion had concurrent iron, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiencies4–6.

Anaemia remains a significant public health problem in Sri Lanka, with varying prevalence reported across different geographical areas and age groups. Numerous research groups have tried to figure out the prevalence of anaemia in Sri Lanka. However, most of these studies were limited to specific geographical areas or specific populations. Only a few of them had attempted to assess the island-wide prevalence of anaemia. The existing data about the prevalence of anaemia is heterogeneous. At the same time, there appears to have been an overestimation of anaemia prevalence in Sri Lanka7–9.

Several study groups tried to identify the causative factors for anaemia and the contribution of these factors to the total anaemia burden in Sri Lanka. Most of these studies had mainly focused on IDA, and in some studies, IDA and anaemia were used synonymously. Several articles discussed the prevalence of folate deficiency and vitamin B12 deficiency in Sri Lanka. The contribution of enzymopathies, membranopathies and haemoglobinopathies to the anaemia burden in Sri Lanka had been largely ignored. Interestingly, in several population studies of anaemia, the cause for anaemia could not be explained in up to 30% of the study subjects after the preliminary investigations. We hypothesised that this unexplained/uncharacterised anaemia could be due to uncharacterized alpha thalassemia, membranopathies or enzymopathies, which had not been analysed in any of those population studies7,10,11. Thus, this study was designed to assess the prevalence and causative factors of anaemia among young adults in Sri Lanka with the view of addressing a detailed study of unexplained anaemia, if any such was discovered.

Methodology

Ethical Clearance was obtained from the Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, University of Kelaniya (P/191/09/2019). A descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from 31.01.2023 up to 01.12.2024 in all 9 provinces in Sri Lanka. A quota-based provincial sampling strategy was employed, targeting vocational training centers to capture a representative cohort of the Sri Lankan young adult population of 18 to 30 years of age. Information sheets and consent forms were distributed and informed written consent was obtained. Blood samples and data were collected from consenting participants. A pretested, structured questionnaire was used for data collection. The height and weight of the study participants were measured, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated. In addition, Full blood count (FBC), haemoglobin HPLC, blood picture and reticulocyte count were performed in each study participant.

The haemoglobin concentrations less than 120 g/L in adult females and less than 130 g/L in adult men were considered as the cutoff values, and the population with anaemia was identified. Mild anaemia was defined as Hb values between 12.9 and 11.0 g/dl in males and between 11.9 and 11.0 g/dl in females. Severe anaemia was defined as Hb < 8 g/dl and moderate Hb values in between. Serum ferritin (EIA-4408), serum iron and total iron binding capacity (STANBIO No. 0370), C-reactive protein (Mindray BS240), vitamin B12 level (EIA-58488), folate level (CDC microbiological assay), and alpha/beta thalassaemia molecular studies (Fluorescence Melting Curve Analysis) were conducted in all anaemic individuals. Iron deficiency was defined as a serum ferritin below 15 µg/L, and iron deficiency anaemia as serum ferritin less than 15 µg/L together with the previously specified cutoff for low Hb. If the serum ferritin was between 15 µg/L to 30 µg/L, serum iron and total iron binding capacity (TIBC) were performed and transferrin saturation (TC) was calculated. TIBC higher than 450 µg/dL, TC less than 20.00%, serum iron less than 60 µg/dL in males and 50 µg/dL in females was used as the cutoff value to define iron deficiency in both male and female study participants. Iron deficiency anaemia was defined by the concurrent presence of abnormal serum iron, total iron-binding capacity, and transferrin saturation. CRP was utilized to identify individuals with active inflammation (CRP > 5 mg/L), as this can mask iron deficiency by falsely elevating ferritin levels.

A serum folate level less than 7nmol/L was defined as the folate deficiency, and a serum B12 level less than 200pg/mL was defined as B12 deficiency. Beta thalassemia trait was defined based on HbA2 level > 3.5%. Then, the anaemic individuals without nutritional deficiencies, thalassemia, and structural variants of haemoglobin were identified and classified as the “uncharacterized anaemia” population (UA). A repeat EDTA blood sample was collected from those with UA who consented for a repeat venesection. The individuals with UA who were reluctant to give a repeat blood sample were considered drop-outs. Further testing done in this group included blood picture, enzyme assays, and EMA dye binding assay. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed in 11 selected patients. Figure 1 summarises the step-wise diagnostic process. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 20. Anemia prevalence was expressed as proportions with 95% confidence intervals. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, and compared using the chi-square test and independent t-test.

Overall, 1800 study participants (100 females and 100 males from each province) representing all nine provinces in Sri Lanka were enrolled for the study.

Prevalence of anaemia

The prevalence of anaemia among the study participants was 15.00% (n = 270/1800). Among the anaemic individuals, 78.5% (n = 212/270) were diagnosed with mild anaemia, 21.1% (n = 57/270) with moderate anaemia, and 0.4% (n = 1/270) with severe anaemia. Prevalence estimates were calculated without weighting and reflect the study sample. Sex-based differences in anaemia prevalence were assessed using a chi-square test. Anaemia prevalence was significantly higher among females (25.4%, n = 229/900) compared with males (4.6%, n = 41/900) (p < 0.001).The prevalence of anaemia was 13.84% (n = 210/1517) in ethnic Sinhalese, 22.83% (n = 50/219) in ethnic Tamils, and 16.39% (n = 10/61) in Muslims. There was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of anaemia between the different ethnic groups (p = 0.240).

Causes for anaemia

Iron deficiency was the leading cause for anaemia in the population, with 7.38% (n = 133/1800) having IDA. Folate deficiency accounted for 4.16% (n = 75/1800) and B12 deficiency accounted for 2.12% (n = 39/1800) of all those who were anaemic. Overall, haemoglobinopathies accounted for anaemia in 3.83% (69/1800). In many individuals, more than one cause contributed to anaemia. Notably, several individuals with thalassaemia traits had concurrent nutritional deficiencies, indicating that the carrier state alone was unlikely to be the sole cause of anaemia in these cases. Prevalence of iron deficiency anaemia

The prevalence of iron deficiency among anaemic individuals was 49.26% (n = 133/270). The prevalence of IDA among males was 0.44% (n = 4/900) and 14.33% (n = 129/900) in females. The prevalence of IDA was significantly higher in females compared to males (χ² test, p < 0.000). The prevalence of IDA was 61.31% (n = 93/1517) among Sinhalese, 15.52% (n = 34/219) among Tamils and 9.83% (n = 6/61) among Muslims. The prevalence of IDA is statistically significantly higher in Tamils compared to Sinhalese (χ² test, p = 0.006).

Prevalence of folate deficiency

The prevalence of folate deficiency anaemia among study participants was 4.16% (n = 75/1800). The prevalence of folate deficiency with anaemia among males was 0.88% (n = 8/900) and 7.44% (n = 67/900) in females. There is no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of folate deficiency with anaemia in females compared to males (χ² test, p = 0.261).

Prevalence of vitamin B12 deficiency

The prevalence of vitamin B12 deficiency anaemia among study participants was 2.17% (n = 39/1800). The prevalence of vitamin B12 deficiency with anaemia among males was 0.77% (n = 7/900) and 3.44% (n = 31/900) in females. There is no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of vitamin B12 deficiency in females compared to males (χ² test, p = 0.444).

Prevalence of β-thalassemia trait and haemoglobin variants

The prevalence of β thalassemia trait in the anaemia population was 10.00% (n = 27/270), and the prevalence of the haemoglobin E trait in the anaemia population was 1.11% (n = 3/270). And the prevalence of haemoglobin D traits in the anaemia population was 0.37% (n = 1/270).

Among the β thalassemia trait, 62.96% (n = 17/27) were diagnosed with IVS 1–5 G > C mutation, 22.22% (n = 6/27) were diagnosed with IVS I-1 G > A (HBB: c.92 + 1G > A) mutation, 3.70% (n = 1/27) was diagnosed with CD17 (A > C) mutation and 3.70% (n = 1/27) was diagnosed with IVS I-130 G > C (HBB: c.93-1G > C) mutation. Underline genetic mutation could not be identified in 2 beta-thalassemia traits with the available facilities. All thalassemia E traits diagnosed with capillary electrophoresis were confirmed to have the (codon 26 GAG > AAG) mutation.

Prevalence of α thalassemia

Non-deletional α thalassemia genetic mutations were not detected in the anaemia population. In the anaemia population, αα/-α3.7 mutation was detected in 12.22% (n = 33/270), αα/-α4.2 mutation was detected in 2.22% (n = 6/270), and -α3.7/-α3.7 was detected in 0.74% (n = 2/270). Deletional α thalassemia genetic mutations were detected in 15.18% (n = 41/270) anaemic individuals.

Uncharacterised anaemia population

The underlying cause for anaemia could not be identified in 17.41% (n = 47/270) of study participants using the primary investigations performed, and they were categorised as the uncharacterized anaemia population. In the uncharacterised anaemia population, 31.91% (n = 15/47) were males and 68.08% (n = 32/47) were females. A repeat EDTA blood sample was collected from 57.45% (n = 27/47) of them after a 6 weeks interval, and 42.55% (n = 20/47) of them were not willing to give a repeat blood sample. The individuals who were reluctant to give a repeat blood sample were considered drop-outs.

Based on the repeat FBC, uncharacterized anaemic individuals were categorised into two groups: uncharacterized anaemia individuals who continued to have low haemoglobin levels on repeat FBC (Anaemia group) and those individuals who showed normalised haemoglobin levels on subsequent (repeat) FBC (No-Anaemia). There were 51.85% (n = 14/27) in the anaemia group and 48.15% (n = 13/27) in the no-anaemia group. The EMA dye binding assay was performed in all 14 uncharacterized anaemia individuals. Among them, one individual had a low Mean Channel Fluorescence (MCF) value, while all others had normal MCF values. G6PD enzyme assay, PK enzyme assay and GPI enzyme assay were performed in all 14 uncharacterized anaemia individuals. However, none of them showed any abnormality.

Whole exon sequencing (WES) was performed in 11 samples out of the 14. Financial restrictions prevented us from doing WES for all 14 subjects. On Whole Exome Sequencing the person who was identified to have a low MCF value on EMA binding assay was shown to have the NC_000014.9: g.64,752,206 C > T mutation in the SPTB gene. This, is a novel mutation not previously described in ClinVar with a low predicted impact.

Variants suggestive of Dyskeratosis Congenita were identified in 3 other individuals. However, none of these 3 individuals had clinical features such as skin pigmentation, nail dystrophy and leukoplakia. Nor did they have features of pancytopenia at the time of the study. Thus, the clinical significance of these mutations in relevance to their mild anaemia needs further assessment.

In four more individuals with UA, variants suggestive of the carrier status of Congenital Dyserythropoietic anaemia, Type Ia, were identified. However, all of these mutations were heterozygous, and a single mutation itself is not sufficient to cause disease. Two of these mutations were of uncertain significance, and their clinical relevance remains unclear. The other two mutations were not reported in ClinVar. These two mutations may be very rare or novel.

Thus, at the end of the study, we could further characterise 57.14% (n = 8/14) of those with UA individuals, but 6 more remain uncharacterized.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first community-based study in Sri Lanka to employ a comprehensive, multi-tiered diagnostic algorithm to characterise the aetiology of anaemia, particularly addressing the previously unexplained “uncharacterized anaemia” group. Prior national and regional surveys have either been limited by their focus on iron deficiency or have faced technical constraints that prevented a thorough investigation into genetic causes. By integrating advanced diagnostic methodologies, including red cell membrane and enzyme assays and next-generation sequencing, we were able to provide a more granular understanding of the anaemia burden in the Sri Lankan adolescent population.

In our study, the national prevalence of anaemia among young adults was found to be 15.00%. This finding directly addresses our initial hypothesis that the true prevalence of anaemia in Sri Lanka may have been historically overestimated. While some older or highly localized studies have reported much higher rates, those figures likely reflected specific vulnerable subpopulations or geographical pockets rather than a true national average. Our community-based prevalence of 15.00% aligns much more closely with recent, rigorously designed cross-sectional surveys of general, apparently healthy populations in the country, such as the community-based survey of secondary school children conducted by Allen et al.. By capturing a diverse, provincial-quota-based sample of young adults, our data supports the expectation that the overall national burden is more moderate than previously assumed, though still a significant public health concern7. Our results also reaffirm that iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) remains the predominant aetiology, accounting for nearly half of the cases. However, our data underscore a significant prevalence of nutritional deficiencies beyond iron, with folate and vitamin B12 deficiencies collectively contributing to over 40% of the cases. This highlights the importance of a broader nutritional screening protocol in public health initiatives. Haemoglobinopathy traits were identified in approximately 25% of anaemic individuals. However, in a substantial proportion of these cases, nutritional deficiencies coexisted, indicating that the trait itself was not necessarily the sole cause of anaemia. Thus, this figure reflects the prevalence of carrier states within the anaemic cohort rather than definitive causal attribution.

It is important to interpret the contribution of thalassaemia traits with caution. Carrier states are typically associated with mild microcytosis and are often insufficient on their own to cause clinically significant anaemia. In our cohort, a considerable proportion of individuals with haemoglobinopathy traits also had coexisting iron, folate, or vitamin B12 deficiencies, suggesting a multifactorial basis for their anaemia. These findings reinforce the need to avoid attributing anaemia solely to carrier states without a comprehensive evaluation for nutritional or other causes.

The most notable contribution of our study lies in the successful characterisation of previously uninvestigated anaemia cases. A substantial portion of the cohort initially classified as having “uncharacterized anaemia” was found to have conditions that were previously overlooked in community surveys. A significant number of these individuals experienced a spontaneous normalisation of haemoglobin levels upon re-evaluation, suggesting that their initial anaemia may have been transient, possibly related to a subclinical infection or an inflammatory process, or was a result of a suboptimal laboratory definition. However, in those with persistent anaemia, our advanced diagnostic workup yielded novel findings. The discovery of a case of hereditary spherocytosis via EMA dye binding assay and the identification of variants suggestive of congenital dyserythropoietic anaemias (CDA) and dyskeratosis congenita through whole-exome sequencing are of profound significance. These findings confirm that red cell membranopathies and congenital bone marrow failure syndromes, while rare, contribute to the community burden of anaemia, albeit at a smaller frequency. The absence of G6PD, PK, and GPI deficiencies in this subgroup of persistent uncharacterized anaemia warrants further investigation, as their contribution to the overall anaemia burden may be more pronounced in other demographic groups.

The identification of variants suggestive of hereditary spherocytosis, congenital dyserythropoietic anaemia, and dyskeratosis congenita highlights the potential contribution of rare inherited red cell and marrow disorders to unexplained anaemia. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The variants identified were largely heterozygous, of uncertain significance, or previously unreported, and functional validation was not performed. As such, these results are exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory, and further studies with larger cohorts and functional analyses are required to establish clinical relevance.

Despite our comprehensive approach, a small number of cases remained uncharacterized, even after whole-exome sequencing was performed. It is conceivable that these cases may be linked to other unassessed nutritional deficiencies, such as those of zinc or copper, or to uninvestigated non-helminthic parasitic infestations. While these possibilities remain conjectural, they present a crucial avenue for future research.

In conclusion, our study provides the first detailed aetiological breakdown of anaemia in a representative Sri Lankan adolescent cohort, moving beyond the traditional focus on iron deficiency. The successful characterisation of a significant proportion of previously uncharacterized cases demonstrates that advanced diagnostic technologies add an extra dimension for revealing the true aetiological landscape of anaemia. These findings have direct clinical implications for improving diagnostic algorithms and are pivotal for informing public health policies aimed at implementing targeted screening and management programs for inherited red cell disorders in the region.

Conclusions

This descriptive, cross-sectional study provides a detailed overview of the aetiology of anaemia among young adults in Sri Lanka, moving beyond the traditional focus on iron deficiency alone. Nutritional deficiencies remain the predominant contributors to anaemia in this population. Inherited red cell disorders, including haemoglobinopathy traits and rare membranopathies, were also identified and appear to contribute modestly but meaningfully to the overall anaemia burden, often in combination with nutritional deficiencies rather than as isolated causes.

The findings highlight the multifactorial nature of anaemia in this age group and highlight the importance of comprehensive evaluation when feasible. However, given the cross-sectional design and the exploratory nature of some genetic findings, these results should be interpreted as descriptive of the observed population rather than as evidence of causal relationships or as a direct basis for policy decisions. Further longitudinal and population-representative studies are needed to better define the relative contributions of nutritional and inherited factors to anaemia across different age groups in Sri Lanka.

Limitations

This study is limited by its cross-sectional design and the relatively small sample size available for Whole Exome Sequencing. Additionally, while quota-based sampling across provinces was used, the results may not be fully representative of the entire national adolescent-to-young-adult transition population.
