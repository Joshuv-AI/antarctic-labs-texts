Causal modeling of school aversion in psychiatrically referred adolescents: a DoWhy-based analysis
Authors: Mengmeng Zhang, Xin Ma, Hui Li, Botao Huang, Yuhan Luo, Jie Qian, Meijuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSchool aversion is increasingly recognized in adolescents and has been linked to emotional distress, suicide risk, and academic failure. However, studies rely on correlational analysis, making it unclear which risk factors truly cause school aversion. To address this gap, we combined directed acyclic graph (DAG)-based modeling, DoWhy-based causal effect estimation, and counterfactual simulation to explore psychosocial pathways related to school aversion in clinically referred adolescents.MethodsParticipants were clinically referred adolescents from mental health services who completed standardized psychosocial assessments. Variables were obtained through validated instruments and clinical intake records, covering demographics, adversity, personality, and symptoms. Causal inference was conducted through a combined framework of DAG learning, DoWhy estimation with backdoor propensity-score weighting and logistic-model-based counterfactual simulation. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11.8, with pgmpy, DoWhy, and scikit-learn as core libraries.ResultsA total of 423 adolescents were assessed, with 57.7% showing school aversion or refusal and 39.7% reporting suicidal thoughts or attempts. DAG modeling identified several school aversion-adjacent relationships, with family structure, bullying, and digital addiction retained as primary school-aversion-centered pathways in downstream analyses, whereas trauma remained a nearby contextual factor. DoWhy analysis showed that the retained associations were SchoolAversion → Bullying [average treatment effect (ATE) = 0.046, p = 0.001] and SchoolAversion → DigitalAdd (ATE = 0.036, p = 0.008), while family structure showed a significant negative effect on school aversion (ATE = -0.162, p = 0.031). Counterfactual simulation showed that removing bullying or digital addiction had relatively small effects on school aversion. In exploratory counterfactual simulations, SchoolAversion → Suicide showed a risk difference of 0.266 (bootstrap 95% CI 0.182 to 0.346).ConclusionsAmong clinically referred adolescents, school aversion appeared not only as an outcome linked to upstream psychosocial context, but also as a central factor associated with downstream bullying, digital addiction, and suicide risk in the present framework. This integrated causal framework may help refine risk screening and intervention prioritization in clinical populations, while further validation in non-clinical and longitudinal samples is needed.

Background

School aversion and school refusal are growing concerns among adolescents globally [1, 2]. They are commonly defined as chronic reluctance or outright refusal to go to school, primarily due to psychological discomfort [3]. This issue carries implications across psychiatry, public health, and educational planning. Studies have reported that school aversion is present in up to 28% of adolescents with psychiatric symptoms [4]. Clinical presentations vary widely, including sporadic school avoidance and chronic disengagement from formal education [1].

School aversion typically arises from a combination of personal, social, and environmental risk factors [2, 5]. Studies have linked school avoidance to adverse life events, peer rejection, and personality traits such as neuroticism [6]. Factors such as digital addiction, low social connectedness, harsh parenting, and poor emotional regulation have all been implicated in school aversion [7].

The effects of school aversion extend beyond academics and may disrupt psychological well-being, family relationships, and future adjustment [8]. Adolescents exhibiting school aversion often show higher levels of psychological distress, including emotional instability and suicidal tendencies [9]. In addition to emotional risks, school aversion can strain family relationships and lead to increased conflict between adolescents and caregivers [10]. The condition may also harm social functioning, limit peer interaction, and increase isolation, leading to broader developmental setbacks [11].

Despite growing interest, most studies on school aversion rely on correlational designs, lacking formal causal frameworks [6]. However, correlation does not imply causation and cannot support intervention decisions. Such approaches are limited in addressing key intervention-oriented questions, such as what might happen if a given risk factor were removed. Without causal evidence, it remains unclear which factors truly drive school aversion and which are merely correlated. Designing effective prevention strategies requires analytic tools capable of identifying causal effects and simulating hypothetical interventions.

Longitudinal cohort studies are ideal for causal inference, but in many psychological studies, including school aversion research, such designs are limited by feasibility, making cross-sectional causal inference essential. DoWhy is a causal inference framework that enables researchers to estimate treatment effects using observational data [12]. Unlike traditional regression models, DoWhy integrates causal graph theory and counterfactual logic for robust effect estimation. In this study, we applied DoWhy to assess key causal paths and extended the analysis with counterfactual simulations. This approach helps bridge the gap between theoretical causal models and applied intervention scenarios, even in the absence of longitudinal data.

To address the lack of causal modeling in school aversion research, this study applied an integrated analytic approach to identify key drivers and estimate intervention effects. Specifically, we aimed to identify potential psychosocial and environmental factors that are causally related to school aversion among adolescents, and to simulate the potential effects of modifying these factors using counterfactual models. We hypothesized that certain psychosocial risks would show direct causal associations with school aversion. In addition, we expected that school aversion may serve as a mediating factor increasing downstream risks such as suicidal ideation.

Methods

Study participants and data collection

The study included adolescents referred to mental health services for emotional or behavioral difficulties. Participants were assessed at Shanghai 10th People's Hospital (Shanghai, China) between August 2024 and March 2025. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their caregivers prior to participation. All participants completed the full assessment protocol, with questionnaires administered under the guidance of trained psychologists or research assistants. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shanghai 10th People's Hospital, China (SHSY-IEC-6.0/24K211/P01).

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 10-18 years; (2) presenting with emotional or affective symptoms (e.g., anxiety, depression) and seeking help at psychiatric outpatient clinics; (3) ability to complete the full battery of psychosocial questionnaires; (4) provision of informed consent from both the adolescent and their legal guardian. Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) current psychotic symptoms or manic episodes; (2) primary diagnosis of non-affective psychiatric disorders, including neurodevelopmental disorders [e.g., attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), autism spectrum disorder (ASD)], tic disorders, or conduct disorders, based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) diagnostic criteria; (3) severe comorbid physical illnesses (e.g., epilepsy, chronic pain conditions, or other debilitating somatic diseases) that could interfere with psychological functioning and assessment validity.

Measures and variable coding

Variables were selected to capture several psychosocial domains that have been implicated in school aversion in prior research, including family and contextual influences, trauma and adversity exposure, proximal life stress, personality-related vulnerability, and emotional or behavioral symptom burden [2]. Family- and caregiving-related factors were included because school aversion has been linked to family functioning, parental factors, and caregiving context [13]. Trauma and adversity variables were included because adverse experiences are associated with emotional dysregulation, school disengagement, and related mental health difficulties in adolescence [14]. Recent life events from the Adolescent Life Events Scale (ALES) were included to capture proximal stress exposure, particularly in academic, interpersonal, and adjustment-related domains. Personality traits from the Adolescent Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (AEPQ) were included because individual differences in emotional reactivity, introversion, and behavioral tendencies may shape avoidance, coping, and school engagement [15]. Symptom dimensions from the Symptom Checklist (SCL)-90 were included to represent the broader clinical burden in this psychiatrically referred sample.

Demographic and social-emotional adversity items

Adolescents provided demographic information and details of exposure to adverse experiences via intake questionnaires. Basic demographic items included age, gender, and current school status. Variables on family background included caregiver type, family intactness, and only-child status. Information on school aversion, suicidal risk, trauma, and bullying was obtained from standardized clinical records, based on participants’ self-reports during intake. For graph learning and downstream causal analyses, variables were coded according to the analysis workflow used in the study; binary indicators were created where required for treatment-effect estimation and counterfactual simulation, whereas continuous questionnaire-derived variables were standardized using z-scores.

Adolescent life events scale

The ALES was used to assess recent stressors and negative experiences in daily life. The scale contains 27 items across seven dimensions: punishment, loss, interpersonal interference, academic pressure, health/property loss, romantic interference and others’ influence. Participants indicated whether each event occurred and how stressful it was, on a scale from 0 to 5. The scale shows strong psychometric properties, with Cronbach’s α values above 0.85 in most subscales.

Adolescent eysenck personality questionnaire

The AEPQ was used to assess core personality traits in adolescents. The instrument includes four subscales: Extraversion (E), Neuroticism (N), Psychoticism (P), and Lie Scale (L). Each item was scored as 1 (yes) or 0 (no), and raw scores for each dimension were computed by addition. The scale has demonstrated good construct validity (Cronbach’s α = 0.70–0.85) and has been widely used in Chinese adolescent research.

SCL-90 symptom checklist

The SCL-90 was used to evaluate a wide range of psychological symptoms in adolescents. The scale consists of 90 items covering nine symptom dimensions: somatization, obsessive–compulsive, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (not at all) to 4 (extremely). Prior research reports high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α values above 0.85 for most subscales.

Integrated causal analysis: graph modeling, estimation, and simulation

This study adopted an integrated causal analysis framework combining three stages. First, we derived a data-driven Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) to map potential causal paths. The graph was constructed using the pgmpy library with score-based structure learning [HillClimbSearch with a Bayesian Dirichlet equivalent uniform (BDeu) score], followed by bootstrap-based evaluation of edge presence and directional support. Then, we estimated the average treatment effect (ATE) with DoWhy, followed by counterfactual outcome simulations under hypothetical interventions.

All analyses were conducted in Python 3.11.8 using Google Colab. Key packages included pgmpy for DAG modeling, DoWhy for causal inference, and scikit-learn for counterfactual simulation. Bootstrap resampling and all downstream estimation procedures used fixed random seeds to improve reproducibility. Causal graph construction via DAG modeling

To identify potential causal relationships among psychosocial variables, we used a DAG as the basis for structural modeling. The graph structure was learned from data using the pgmpy with HillClimbSearch and a BDeu score, and edge stability was subsequently examined by bootstrap resampling. All nodes around school aversion were assigned to one of four domains: life events, psychological symptoms, personality traits, and family/contextual factors. School aversion-adjacent edge pairs were summarized using their bootstrap presence probabilities and directional probabilities. For each adjacent pair, the preferred direction (“winner”) was defined as the orientation with the larger directional bootstrap probability. Directional support was interpreted more confidently when the directional probability departed sufficiently from 0.5, following the bootstrap orientation criterion used in the analysis. These school aversion-adjacent pairs were used to guide subsequent effect estimation and simulation analyses.

ATE estimation with DoWhy

To estimate the causal effect of specific risk factors on school aversion, we used the DoWhy framework for treatment effect estimation. The causal analysis was conducted using DoWhy’s standard pipeline, which supports interpretable and robust ATE estimation. Treatment variables were selected based on their direct connections to the school aversion node in the DAG. The final set of ATE rows also depended on whether valid and interpretable estimates could be obtained under the specified DoWhy model. Control variables were chosen based on adjacency to treatment variables in the DAG. Backdoor adjustment sets were determined from the learned graph and implemented using propensity-score weighting in DoWhy. In this approach, the graph-derived backdoor adjustment set was used to estimate each participant’s probability of receiving the observed treatment level. Individuals were then weighted by the inverse of that probability, so that the treated and untreated groups became more comparable with respect to measured confounders before estimating the ATE. We used the backdoor propensity-score weighting method in DoWhy to estimate the ATE. The ATE results provide a population-level estimation of causal impact, beyond simple associations.

Counterfactual simulation via logistic models

To estimate the practical impact of risk factors, we simulated counterfactual scenarios where key exposures were removed. Simulations were based on logistic models trained on the same covariate-adjusted dataset used in ATE analysis. We generated counterfactual predictions under do(T=1) and do(T=0), while keeping other features unchanged. Simulated effects are reported in terms of mean differences and proportional change under hypothetical conditions. Counterfactual simulations were performed for the primary school-aversion-centered relationships as well as selected additional relationships that yielded interpretable scenario-based estimates. For school-aversion-adjacent edge pairs identified in the bootstrap structure-learning analysis, the preferred direction was defined as the orientation with the larger directional bootstrap probability. Relationships were retained as primary when they were adjacent to school aversion, showed a clear preferred direction in the structure-learning analysis, and yielded interpretable results in downstream causal-effect analyses. Additional adjacent pairs or reverse-direction estimates that were estimable but not retained as primary school-aversion-centered direction were treated as exploratory. Accordingly, primary findings referred to school-aversion-adjacent relationships that were structurally proximal, directionally supported, and retained in downstream estimation, whereas exploratory findings referred to additional adjacent, reverse-direction, or downstream relationships that were estimable but not emphasized as primary school-aversion-centered pathways.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 423 participants completed the assessment. The mean age of the participants was 14.63 (1.82) years old, including 292 females (69.0%) and 131 males (31.0%). Participants came from various educational levels: 30 primary (7.1%), 217 middle (51.3%), and 176 high school (41.6%). A total of 244 adolescents (57.7%) exhibited school aversion or refusal, including 216 (51.1%) with aversion and 28 (6.6%) with full refusal. In addition, 39.7% of participants were identified as being at risk for suicide, including 108 (25.5%) with suicidal ideation and 60 (14.2%) with a history of suicide attempt (Table 1). Causal inference results via DoWhy

Table 3 presents the results of the causal analysis conducted using DoWhy with propensity score weighting for estimating the ATE of the school-aversion-adjacent relationships for which valid and interpretable effect estimates were obtained in the DoWhy analysis. Among the school-aversion-adjacent pairs identified in Table 2, only Family → SchoolAversion, SchoolAversion → Bullying, and SchoolAversion → DigitalAdd yielded valid and interpretable ATE estimates; the suicide-related pair did not yield a valid estimable ATE row and was therefore not retained in Table 3. The edge Family → SchoolAversion showed a significant negative effect on school aversion with an ATE of − 0.162 (p = 0.031). SchoolAversion → Bullying (ATE = 0.046, p = 0.001) and SchoolAversion → DigitalAdd (ATE = 0.036, p = 0.008) likewise showed significant positive effects and were retained as the primary school-aversion-centered ATE findings. Additional analyses were conducted for the reverse directions Bullying → SchoolAversion and DigitalAdd → SchoolAversion. Both reverse-direction estimates were significant (Bullying → SchoolAversion: ATE = 0.378, p = 0.002; DigitalAdd → SchoolAversion: ATE = 0.433, p = 0.003) and were retained as exploratory downstream associations alongside the primary school-aversion-centered effects. By contrast, family structure was modeled primarily as a contextual antecedent of school aversion in the present study; therefore, only the Family → SchoolAversion direction was retained in the main ATE table. Overall, these findings indicate that family structure was negatively associated with school aversion, while bullying and digital addiction showed robust primary associations with additional reverse-direction estimates in supplementary analyses. Counterfactual simulation of risk factors

To further characterize the intervention-relevant implications of the learned structure, we conducted counterfactual simulations for the primary school-aversion-centered relationships and selected additional exploratory relationships that yielded interpretable scenario-based estimates in the logistic-modeling framework. The counterfactual analysis estimates the potential outcomes if specific risk factors were removed. Table 4 provides the results of this simulation, showing P1_do [probability of the outcome under do(T=1)], P0_do [probability of the outcome under do(T=0)], and the ATE_risk_diff (risk difference). For the edge Family → SchoolAversion, the ATE_risk_diff was −0.165. The primary school-aversion-centered counterfactual results also included SchoolAversion → Bullying (ATE_risk_diff = 0.033) and SchoolAversion → DigitalAdd (ATE_risk_diff = 0.022). Additional exploratory counterfactual results were observed for SchoolAversion → Suicide (ATE_risk_diff = 0.266), Bullying → SchoolAversion (ATE_risk_diff = 0.254), and DigitalAdd → SchoolAversion (ATE_risk_diff = 0.266). These findings indicate that the primary school-aversion-centered relationships remained supported in the counterfactual framework, while suicide, bullying, and digital addiction also showed additional downstream or reverse-direction associations in exploratory analyses. Discussion

This study aimed to identify causal risk factors for school aversion in adolescents using an integrated framework combining DAG, DoWhy, and counterfactual simulation. The findings emphasize the complex and multifactorial nature of school aversion, where no single factor operates in isolation. One of the key findings of this study is that family structure emerged as a significant contextual factor associated with lower school aversion. Adolescents from intact families were less likely to exhibit school aversion, consistent with the view that family stability may provide emotional support, monitoring, and continuity that help sustain school engagement [16]. This finding is consistent with prior studies showing that family stability provides crucial emotional support, which helps adolescents avoid school aversion behaviors [17]. Clinically, this suggests that assessment and intervention should pay attention not only to the adolescent’s symptoms, but also to caregiving stability, family support, and the continuity of adult involvement surrounding school engagement.

Bullying was also significantly associated with school aversion in the exploratory reverse-direction analysis. Bullying, especially in school settings, appears to create an emotionally unsafe environment for adolescents, which leads them to seek avoidance [18]. This relationship can be explained by the fear-avoidance model, which suggests that individuals who perceive an environment as threatening will actively withdraw from it [19, 20]. In line with this, Rudolph and Hammen have shown that peer victimization is strongly associated with school withdrawal, particularly when adolescents lack effective coping strategies [21]. The fear of being victimized and the emotional distress caused by bullying are likely to reinforce negative perceptions of school, leading to school aversion. Our finding is consistent with previous studies that have shown that peer victimization leads to withdrawal from school contexts, particularly in adolescents with limited coping strategies [22]. From an intervention perspective, this finding supports early identification of peer victimization, timely school-based responses to bullying, and targeted support for adolescents whose school avoidance appears to be reinforced by fear and emotional distress in the peer environment.

Digital addiction, as we found in this study, showed a primary school-aversion-centered association, together with additional exploratory reverse-direction associations in downstream estimation. This pattern suggests that digital engagement is closely linked to school aversion within the broader psychosocial network of adolescents in this clinically referred sample. On the one hand, excessive digital use can replace face-to-face interactions, including those with peers and teachers at school, thereby exacerbating feelings of isolation and disengagement from school activities [23]. Excessive digital use may reduce adolescents’ motivation and executive control, disrupting their capacity to engage with structured school routines [24]. On the other hand, as adolescents experience school aversion, they may turn to digital devices as a coping mechanism, further deepening their withdrawal from school [25]. Taken together, these findings suggest that digital addiction may be both closely linked to school aversion and relevant to intervention planning, although the primary direction retained in the present study was from school aversion to digital addiction. Accordingly, interventions may need to address both digital behavior and school engagement rather than assuming a single causal route. More specifically, this may include helping adolescents replace avoidant digital use with alternative coping routines, establishing more structured patterns of screen use, and gradually rebuilding engagement with school-based activities and peer contact. For instance, interventions may focus on promoting healthier coping strategies and fostering positive school engagement [26].

In addition to these findings, trauma appeared as a nearby factor in the local network surrounding school aversion, but its downstream effect estimates were weak and inconsistent. This suggests that while trauma may contribute to the development of school aversion, its role appeared less direct compared to bullying or digital addiction in the present analysis. Trauma, particularly in childhood, can lead to a range of emotional and behavioral difficulties that make it challenging for adolescents to engage with their school environment [27]. Research has shown that trauma can cause long-lasting effects on mental health, and this may manifest as difficulties in adjusting to school routines and relationships [28]. Accordingly, trauma may be better understood as part of the broader psychosocial context surrounding school aversion rather than as a primary causal pathway in the present analysis.

Our counterfactual simulations further underscore the significance of school aversion as a key modeled risk factor for suicidal ideation. While removing individual risk factors such as bullying and digital addiction had relatively modest effects, eliminating school aversion itself led to a marked reduction in suicide risk. This finding highlights the importance of addressing school aversion not only as a standalone issue but also as a core factor contributing to broader mental health outcomes, including suicidality [29]. School aversion may reduce protective social contact and increase perceived failure, both of which are key risk factors for adolescent suicidality [8]. In addition, this association may be particularly salient in educational contexts where academic performance is strongly emphasized and future opportunities are perceived to depend heavily on school achievement, as prior work in Chinese and East Asian settings has shown that stressful school environments, effort-reward imbalance, and school-related academic pressure are closely linked to adolescent distress and suicidality [30–32]. Such an interpretation may be particularly relevant in the present context and could be further examined in future studies across different school systems. This finding is consistent with the hopelessness theory of depression, which suggests that prolonged disengagement from social structures, including school, can exacerbate suicidal ideation [33]. This also suggests that suicide risk assessment in clinically referred adolescents should pay explicit attention to persistent school aversion, and that interventions aimed at restoring school engagement may have value not only for attendance, but also for broader suicide prevention.

However, the modest counterfactual effects observed for bullying and digital addiction are consistent with the multifactorial nature of school aversion, in which meaningful change may depend on multiple interacting psychosocial influences rather than on the removal of any single factor alone. School aversion may require a threshold accumulation of risks, meaning that removing one input alone is insufficient for meaningful change [34]. At the same time, the stronger counterfactual effect observed for school aversion on suicidality supports its interpretation as an important downstream intervention target, suggesting that efforts to restore school engagement may have value not only for attendance-related outcomes but also for broader suicide prevention.

This study presents a novel methodological framework combining DAG, DoWhy, and counterfactual simulation in the context of adolescent mental health. Unlike traditional correlation-based approaches, our framework allows for directional inference and estimates of treatment effects. Compared with prior descriptive or correlational approaches, our study adds formal structure to test causality in school aversion pathways. More specifically, this framework helps distinguish between proximal network relationships, estimable causal-effect rows, and counterfactual downstream findings, thereby offering a more structured way to interpret the multiple pathways surrounding school aversion. This analytic pipeline may be particularly relevant for early risk screening, intervention modeling, and predictive systems in clinical adolescent psychiatry, where adolescents are already referred because of emotional or behavioral difficulties. Although some of the identified relationships may also be relevant to broader adolescent populations, the present study does not provide a sufficient basis for direct extrapolation beyond clinically referred samples. In this sense, any broader implications should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than population-wide conclusions.

Limitations and future directions

Despite the novel methodology and relevant findings, the study has limitations that warrant careful interpretation. First, the study relied on cross-sectional data from a single clinical site and included adolescents referred to mental health services, which likely reflects a substantial selection process. Accordingly, the present findings are most directly applicable to clinically referred populations and should not be generalized directly to non-clinical adolescents with school aversion. Second, several key variables such as bullying and trauma were extracted from intake records, which may be subject to underreporting or interview bias. Sample size constraints may have limited statistical power in detecting weaker causal effects in the DAG structure. Finally, the counterfactual simulation was constrained by the observed feature space and did not consider unmeasured latent dynamics. To enhance generalizability and temporal inference, longitudinal and geographically diverse data are needed in future research. Further validation in community-based, school-based, and longitudinal samples will be necessary to assess the extent to which these patterns generalize beyond clinical settings. Future work should incorporate cognitive variables such as executive functioning, emotional regulation, and school context measures. Future efforts should bridge simulation findings with real-world intervention priorities and resource allocation.

Conclusions

This study identified key causal pathways related to school aversion and its downstream consequences among clinically-referred adolescents. Our analysis found that family structure, bullying, and digital addiction were retained as the primary school-aversion-centered pathways, whereas trauma was identified as a nearby contextual factor; school aversion in turn was associated with suicidal tendencies. This work shows that causal-effect estimation can be meaningfully explored even in the absence of longitudinal data, through the use of principled analytic tools. The framework presented here may help inform risk assessment and intervention prioritization in clinical adolescent populations, while broader application to non-clinical settings requires further validation.
