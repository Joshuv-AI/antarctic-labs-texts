Global, Regional, and National Burden of Blindness due to Diabetic Retinopathy, 1990–2021
Authors: Yang Meng, Yuan Liu, Yuan Ma, Ziye Chen, Runping Duan, Lan Jiang, Tao Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to analyze the patterns and trends in the burden of blindness due to diabetic retinopathy (DR) from 1990 to 2021 at the global, regional, and national levels.MethodsWe conducted a population-based analysis using the latest data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. We examined the prevalence and number of cases of DR-related blindness stratified by type of diabetes, sex, age, and sociodemographic index (SDI) level. The trends in the blindness burden were quantified via the average annual percentage changes. A decomposition analysis was performed to identify key drivers of the changing burden. A Bayesian age‒period‒cohort model was used to predict the future burden of DR-related blindness from 2022 to 2035.ResultsGlobally, the number of people blind due to DR increased by 326.0% from 1990 to 2021, with the age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) increasing from 7.59 to 15.28 per 100,000 population. Regionally, Tropical Latin America had the highest ASPR in 2021, whereas South Asia and East Asia had the greatest number of prevalent cases. Nationally, India and China had the highest number of prevalent cases in 2021, whereas Marshall Islands reported the highest ASPR. In 2021, the middle SDI group had the greatest number of patients who became blind due to DR. Decomposition analysis revealed that the increased global burden between 1990 and 2021 was driven by population growth, population aging, and epidemiological changes. Predictions suggest the burden will continue to rise through 2035.ConclusionsThe global burden of DR-related blindness has increased significantly over the past three decades, with notable disparities across regions, countries, and SDI groups. Introduction

Diabetes comprises a group of chronic, heterogeneous disorders characterized by sustained hyperglycemia due to insufficient insulin secretion and/or insulin resistance [1]. Its global prevalence has risen sharply over recent decades, posing a major public health challenge in the 21st century [2]. Patients with diabetes are prone to vascular damage due to chronic hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and disorders of other metabolic pathways, leading to a series of macrovascular and microvascular complications [3].

Among these complications, diabetic retinopathy (DR) is the most common microvascular complication of diabetes. DR is a silent killer of vision that often remains latent and asymptomatic in its early stages [4]. This insidious nature allows DR to progress unnoticed in early stages. Without timely intervention, DR can rapidly progress to vision-threatening stages, leading to irreversible loss of sight and, in severe cases, blindness [4]. Currently, DR is the most prevalent cause of vision loss among the diabetic population, particularly among those of working age [5].

The impact of blindness is profound, affecting both individuals and society. For patients, blindness can impair their quality of life, limit their ability to do daily activities, and damage their mental health [6]. For society, blindness puts a heavy strain on healthcare systems and resources [7]. Moreover, indirect costs, such as lost productivity due to disability and the need for caregivers, also have an impact on the overall economic burden [8, 9].

Given the increasing global prevalence of diabetes, a comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology of blindness related to DR is needed to help inform public health policies and intervention strategies. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study provides a valuable platform for this purpose, offering high-quality health estimates for over 300 diseases (including DR) across 204 countries and territories [10]. Using previous versions of GBD data, some studies have investigated the burden of DR globally or in some countries, and have provided some valuable information [5, 11–13]. However, to our knowledge, no study has yet comprehensively reported the burden and trends of DR-related blindness at the global, regional, and national levels simultaneously. Besides, currently, no study has reported the burden of DR via the most recent GBD data, i.e., the GBD 2021.

This study aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the global, regional, and national burden of blindness due to DR from 1990 to 2021 using the latest data from the GBD 2021. We also made predictions for the burden of DR-related blindness up to 2035.

Methods

Overview

This study is a population-based analysis based on secondary, aggregated data from the GBD 2021, which is currently the largest global health study. All estimates were derived from the publicly accessible GBD Results Tool and were not modeled by the authors. The GBD 2021, which uses over 328,000 data sources to generate more than 607 billion estimates, provides comprehensive assessments of the trends in 371 diseases and injuries across 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021 [14]. A detailed description of the data sources and data processing strategies employed in GBD 2021 has been provided elsewhere [10, 15]. In brief, a variety of data sources were used as input data in GBD 2021, including epidemiological surveillance data, disease registry data, scientific literature, health insurance claims, and clinical informatics data, etc. [10].

The Institutional Review Board Committee of the University of Washington approved the GBD 2021 (approval number: STUDY00009060). Since this study used de-identified data from GBD 2021, no additional ethics approval was needed.

Study Population and Data Sources

In GBD 2021, diabetes, which includes type 1 and type 2 diabetes, corresponds to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 codes E08-E08.11, E08.3-E08.9, E10-E10.11, E10.3-E11.1, E11.3-E12.1, E12.3-E13.11, E13.3-E14.1, E14.3–E14.9, R73-R73.9, and codes E08-E08.11, E08.3-E08.9, E10-E10.11, E10.3-E11.1, E11.3-E12.1, E12.3-E13.11, E13.3-E14.1, and E14.3-E14.9 in hospital/claim analyses. Blindness due to DR is defined as visual acuity in the better eye of < 3/60 or < 10% of the visual field around the central fixation point [10].

Burden data on DR-related blindness in the GBD 2021 were obtained from the official website of the Global Health Data Exchange (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/). Specifically, we comprehensively collected data on the burden of DR-related blindness across different years (1990–2021), sexes (male, female, both), age groups (20–95 years with a 5-year interval), and geographical locations (global, regional, and national levels), including prevalence rates and prevalent cases. In GBD 2021, 204 countries and territories worldwide are classified into 21 GBD regions based on their geographical proximity and epidemiological similarity. Table S1 provides a list of the regions and countries (or territories) involved in the GBD 2021. In this study, the prevalence rate is provided as the age-standardized rate with the corresponding 95% uncertainty interval (UI), whereas the number of prevalent cases is defined as the number of patients blind by the DR, with the corresponding 95% UI.

Moreover, the GBD 2021 reported the sociodemographic index (SDI) values of the 204 countries and territories. The SDI is a GBD-developed metric to compare health progress between countries (territories). It is calculated as the geometric mean of three components: lag-distributed income per capita, mean education for those aged 15 and older, and the total fertility rate under 25 [16]. In the GBD 2021, the SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with a value of 0 representing the theoretical minimum level of development and 1 representing the maximum [16]. On the basis of their SDI values, the 204 countries (territories) were divided into five SDI subgroups: high (e.g., USA, UK, and France), high-middle (e.g., Hungary, Malaysia, and China), middle (e.g., Mexico, Brazil, and Cuba), low-middle (e.g., Cambodia, Zimbabwe, and Sudan), and low (e.g., Liberia, Uganda, and Haiti). The classification criteria are shown in Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

In this study, we used the average annual percentage change (AAPC) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) to quantify the trend in the prevalence rate of DR-related blindness from 1990 to 2021. The AAPC was calculated via the joinpoint regression method. The potential number of joinpoints selected between 0 and 5 (determined by the Monte Carlo permutation test). If the value of the AAPC is greater than 0 and the P value is statistically significant (equivalent to the lower limit of the 95% CI being greater than 0), it is considered that there is a statistically significant increase in the prevalence rate during the study period. Conversely, if the value of the AAPC is less than 0 and the P value is statistically significant (equivalent to the upper limit of the 95% CI being less than 0), it is considered that there is a statistically significant decrease in the prevalence rate.

To understand the potential drivers of changes in the burden of DR-related blindness, we further performed a decomposition analysis. Through decomposition analysis, the overall differences in the burden of blindness were broken down into three key components: population growth, population aging, and epidemiological changes [17]. In addition, we applied a Bayesian age–period–cohort (BAPC) model to estimate the future burden of DR-related blindness from 2022 to 2035, as it is well-suited for capturing long-term trends in large-scale epidemiological datasets, such as those provided by GBD 2021 [18]. The BAPC model is a standard and robust method for analyzing disease trends, extending the classical age–period–cohort framework by incorporating Bayesian inference, which allows observed data to be integrated with prior distributions [19, 20]. It assumes that age, period, and cohort effects can be smoothly extrapolated based on historical patterns, thereby enabling forecasts of future disease burden [20]. However, as with most forecasting models, the BAPC model cannot account for unanticipated events or interventions, such as major health policy changes or medical innovations, which could affect future trends. Therefore, its projections may be sensitive to deviations from these underlying assumptions.

Given the substantial differences in age structures across countries/territories and over time, directly comparing crude prevalence rates may lead to misleading interpretations. In this study, we used age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPRs) to enable fair comparisons of the burden of DR-related blindness across countries, regions, and time periods [21]. Age standardization adjusts for demographic variations by applying a consistent reference age structure, thereby minimizing confounding due to population aging or differences in age structure. This is particularly important in global studies where demographic profiles can vary widely. By using ASPRs, we aimed to ensure that observed differences in DR-related blindness reflect the true epidemiological patterns, thus enhancing the validity and comparability of our findings.

The data analyses and visualizations were conducted in R software (version 4.3.3) and GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.1).

Results

Global Burden

Globally, the burden of DR-related blindness increased significantly from 1990 to 2021. Specifically, the global number of individuals blind due to DR increased by 326.0%, from 311,588 (95% UI, 231,163–409,198) in 1990 to 1,327,278 (95% UI 998,437–1,737,415) in 2021. Accordingly, the ASPR increased significantly from 7.59 (95% UI 5.67–10.00) per 100,000 population in 1990 to 15.28 (95% UI 11.53–19.91) per 100,000 population in 2021, with an AAPC of 2.30 (95% CI 2.15–2.45) (Fig. 1A, Table 1).

In terms of the type of diabetes, the global burden of DR-related blindness has increased for both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. From 1990 to 2021, the ASPR of DR-related blindness increased from 0.30 (95% UI 0.21–0.42) to 0.42 (95% UI 0.29–0.58) per 100,000 population for type 1 diabetes, and from 7.29 (95% UI 5.44–9.62) to 14.86 (95% UI 11.24–19.42) per 100,000 population for type 2 diabetes. During the study period, the number of blindness cases due to type 1 and type 2 diabetes increased by 172.8% and 332.7%, respectively. In 2021, 97.3% of blindness cases caused by DR occurred in patients with type 2 diabetes (Fig. 1B, C, Table 1).

Regional Burden

Regionally, Tropical Latin America reported the highest ASPR (43.50/100,000; 95% UI 32.40–55.97) of blindness because of DR in 2021, followed closely by Central Latin America (40.4/100,000; 95% UI 30.96–51.08). In contrast, the lowest ASPR was documented in Australasia (0.27/100,000; 95% UI 0.16–0.43). With respect to the number of cases, South Asia (373,469; 95% UI 272,541–484,662) and East Asia (221,485; 95% UI 162,216–297,592) had the most prevalent cases of DR-related blindness in 2021. Between 1990 and 2021, the ASPR significantly increased in 20 out of the 21 GBD regions. Among them, the fastest growth was observed in Oceania (AAPC = 4.19; 95% CI 4.02–4.36) and North Africa and Middle East (AAPC = 3.91; 95% CI 3.80–4.02). Only one region, High-income Asia Pacific, experienced a decline in ASPR (AAPC = − 0.33; 95% CI − 0.53 – − 0.12). In 2021, the ASPR of DR-related blindness was higher than the global average (15.28/100,000) in 6 regions (Fig. 2A, Table 1).

In terms of type 1 diabetes, Tropical Latin America had the highest ASPR (1.77/100,000; 95% UI 1.18–2.51) of DR-related blindness, whereas Western Europe had the most prevalent cases (9571; 95% UI 6956–13,065) in 2021 (Fig. 2B, Table S3). For type 2 diabetes, Tropical Latin America had the highest ASPR (41.73/100,000; 95% UI 31.18–53.96), whereas South Asia had the most blind cases (366,879; 95% UI 267,829–476,762) (Fig. 2C, Table S4).

National Burden

Nationally, India (359,779; 95% UI 262,032–467,153) and China (221,351; 95% UI 162,103–297,430) had the most prevalent cases, whereas the Marshall Islands (100.30/100,000; 95% UI 76.88–126.27) and Mauritius (84.89/100,000; 95% UI 64.14–109.24) reported the highest ASPR. In 2021, the ASPR of DR-related blindness was higher than the global average in 61 countries (territories) (Fig. 3A, Table S5).

In terms of type 1 diabetes, Italy (4.91/100,000; 95% UI 3.43–6.86) and Finland (2.03/100,000; 95% UI 1.44–2.76) reported the highest ASPR of DR-related blindness in 2021, whereas India (6305; 95% UI 4134–8911) and Italy (5279; 95% UI 3744–7413) had the greatest number of people blinded (Fig. 3B, Table S6). In terms of type 2 diabetes, the Marshall Islands had the highest ASPR (99.78/100,000; 95% UI 76.45–125.59), whereas India (353,474; 95% UI 257,527–459,587) and China (219,243; 95% UI 160,579–294,722) had the most people blinded (Fig. 3C, Table S7).

Burden by Sex, Age, and SDI

In both 1990 and 2021, the blindness burden due to DR was greater in females than in males. Specifically, regardless of the type of diabetes, the prevalence rate and prevalent number of DR-related blindness were greater in females than in males across all age groups (Fig. 4). For both sexes, the number of patients blind due to DR initially increased but then decreased with increasing age. In 2021, the peak ages for blindness due to DR among patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes were 50–54 years and 55–59 years, respectively. Compared with 1990, the number of patients and the prevalence of blindness due to DR, whether type 1 or type 2 diabetes, increased across all age groups in 2021 (Fig. 4).

In terms of the SDI, in 1990 and 2021, the middle SDI group had the highest number of patients who became blind due to DR, regardless of diabetes type (Fig. 4, Table S8). In 2021, for type 1 diabetes, the ASPR of blindness related to DR peaked in the high-middle SDI subgroup (0.51/100,000; 95% UI 0.37–0.70) and was lowest in the low SDI subgroup (0.21/100,000; 95% UI 0.14–0.30) (Fig. 5, Table S9). In contrast, for type 2 diabetes, the ASPR was highest in the middle SDI subgroup (19.62/100,000; 95% UI 14.63–25.70) and lowest in the high SDI subgroup (8.93/100,000; 95% UI 6.90–11.38) (Fig. 5, Table S10). We further examined the correlation between the SDI values of the countries (territories) and the ASPR in 2021 through Spearman regression analysis. For patients with type 1 diabetes, there was a significant positive correlation between the ASPR of DR-related blindness and the SDI value (Figure S1). For type 2 diabetes, the ASPR initially increased with increasing SDI but then decreased (Figure S2).

Burden Decomposition

The decomposition analysis revealed that the increased burden of DR-related blindness between 1990 and 2021 was jointly driven by population growth, population aging, and epidemiological changes (Fig. 6). Among these factors, population growth was the primary driver for the increased blindness burden among patients with type 1 diabetes, whereas epidemiological changes served as the main driver for the increase in blindness among patients with type 2 diabetes. For patients with both type 1 and type 2 diabetes, the increase in the number of blind patients by DR was greatest in the middle SDI group and smallest in the low SDI group (Fig. 6).

Burden Prediction

According to the BAPC predictive model, the global burden of DR-related blindness is expected to keep increasing between 2022 and 2035. By 2035, the global ASPR is projected to reach 16.1 per 100,000 population, whereas the number of patients is anticipated to increase to 1.95 million (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global, regional, and national burden of blindness due to DR from 1990 to 2021. Based on the latest data from the GBD 2021, our findings revealed a significant increase in the global burden of DR-related blindness during the past three decades. Notably, this increase was accompanied by great variations across regions, countries, and sociodemographic groups. These results highlighted the urgent need for targeted public health interventions to mitigate the growing impact of DR-related blindness, especially in the context of the global diabetes epidemic.

Between 1990 and 2021, the global burden of DR-related blindness increased substantially, with the number of prevalent cases increasing by 326.0%. This worrying trend coincides with the explosive increase in diabetes prevalence worldwide, which is driven by factors such as population ageing, urbanization, unhealthy dietary habits, sedentary lifestyles, and obesity [22]. As a common microvascular complication of diabetes, the increasing number of people with diabetes inevitably leads to an increasing burden of DR [23]. In 2021, an estimated 1.33 million people worldwide were blind due to DR, underscoring the profound impact of diabetes on global vision health. It also highlights the silent but devastating consequences of DR, which, despite being a preventable and treatable condition, remains the leading cause of blindness in the working-age population [24]. To address this growing burden, strengthening public health initiatives, improving access to diabetes care, and implementing widespread screening programs are critical. Early screening for DR, particularly vision-threatening DR, can significantly reduce the burden of vision loss. However, this process requires intensive labor and significant resources to meet the needs of an expanding diabetic population. Notably, artificial intelligence has emerged as a promising tool that has the potential to reduce the burden of DR-related vision loss while also lowering the costs and time associated with DR screening [25].

Regionally, South Asia and East Asia had the heaviest burden of blindness in 2021. The number of individuals blind by DR in these two regions accounted for 44.8% of the global total. This disproportionate burden possibly reflected the high prevalence of diabetes and limited access to healthcare resources in these regions [26, 27]. The large diabetic population in countries such as India and China further exacerbated this challenge, highlighting the urgent need for targeted interventions [28]. The regional disparities in the burden of DR-related blindness were also striking. In 2021, the ASPR of DR-related blindness in the region with the highest ASPR (Tropical Latin America) was 161 times greater than that of the region with the lowest ASPR (Australasia). In the GBD framework, Tropical Latin America included Brazil and Paraguay. Brazil, a large developing country, is home to the sixth largest population of individuals with diabetes globally but lacks national strategies or standardized workflows for DR screening within its public health system [29]. In Paraguay, nearly half of the diabetic patients visiting the endocrinology department are diagnosed with DR, and DR accounts for 43.8% of blindness cases among these patients [30]. For these countries, it is recommended to implement systematic screening programs and strengthen health education and communication efforts to diagnose and treat DR at an early stage, thereby reducing the blindness caused by DR.

At the national level, countries such as India and China emerged as hotspots for DR-related blindness, accounting for 43.8% of global blindness cases in 2021. The high prevalence of diabetes in India and China is clearly directly correlated with the significant burden of DR-related blindness in these countries. With the largest diabetic population globally, India and China face unique challenges in managing and preventing this vision-threatening complication [31]. As India and China continue to grapple with the rising tide of diabetes, addressing the associated burden of blindness must be a priority in public health strategies [32, 33]. The collaboration among governments, healthcare providers, and communities will be crucial in developing and implementing effective interventions to reduce the burden of DR-related blindness and improve the quality of life for individuals with diabetes.

Interestingly, we observed discrepancies between regional and national ASPRs for DR-related blindness. While Tropical Latin America exhibited the highest regional ASPR in 2021, the highest country-level ASPRs were observed in the Marshall Islands and Mauritius. These mismatches may reflect unique country-specific characteristics that are masked in regional aggregates. According to the International Diabetes Federation Diabetes Atlas, the estimated prevalence of diabetes among adults aged 20–79 years in 2021 was 25.1% in the Marshall Islands and 26.5% in Mauritius, ranking both among the countries and territories with the highest diabetes prevalence globally, compared to a global average of 10.5% [34]. The Marshall Islands, in particular, faces severe healthcare limitations, including a shortage of ophthalmic professionals and the absence of a national DR screening program. It also has one of the highest rates of diabetes-related amputations worldwide, driven by a combination of factors such as advanced disease progression related to lifestyle patterns, inadequate healthcare infrastructure, economic constraints, and cultural reluctance to seek timely medical care [35]. These challenges likely contribute to the high burden of diabetes-related complications, including DR. A recent review further highlighted the scarcity of eye care research and service coverage in Pacific Island countries and territories, including the Marshall Islands [36]. As a multiethnic island nation in the Indian Ocean undergoing epidemiological and economic transition, Mauritius also faces increasing pressure from DR. In a large population-based survey of over 6500 individuals in Mauritius, the prevalence of DR (nonproliferative or proliferative) was 14.8% among those with newly diagnosed diabetes and 44.3% among individuals with known diabetes [37]. A longitudinal follow-up study further reported that, in Mauritius, the 6-year incidence of DR among individuals with newly diagnosed diabetes at baseline was 19.1% [38]. These findings underscore the importance of complementing regional analyses with country-level assessments to uncover hidden public health hotspots and enable more targeted interventions.

The sex-based analysis revealed that females had a greater burden of DR-related blindness than males. This disparity may be attributed to several factors, such as higher diabetes prevalence and less access to health care in females, as well as different biological predispositions between sexes [39–41]. Further research is needed to understand the underlying causes of this difference. Our findings also highlight the important role of SDI in the burden of DR-related blindness. Notably, middle SDI countries had a greater burden of DR-related blindness than both low- and high-SDI countries did. In GBD 2021, the middle SDI group includes countries with moderate levels of income per capita, average educational attainment, and fertility rates that fall between those observed in high- and low-SDI countries, such as Mexico, Brazil, and Cuba. These countries often face a dual burden of disease: ongoing challenges from communicable diseases and a growing prevalence of noncommunicable conditions such as diabetes. Historically, diabetes was perceived as predominantly affecting affluent nations. Nevertheless, during the past few decades, the prevalence of diabetes in middle-income countries has increased dramatically, reaching levels comparable to those in high-income countries. Specifically, in 2021, the adult diabetes prevalence rates were 11.1%, 10.8%, and 5.5% in high-, middle-, and low-income countries, respectively [34]. Despite having a slightly lower prevalence of diabetes, middle-income countries often lack the comprehensive healthcare infrastructure and services available in high-income countries. They face significant challenges, including limited access to healthcare resources, inadequate diabetes management strategies, and insufficient coverage of DR screening and treatment services [42, 43]. These factors might contribute to the heightened burden of DR-related blindness in these countries.

In the decomposition analysis, population growth was identified as a key driver of the blindness burden among people with type 1 diabetes, whereas epidemiological changes emerged as the primary cause of the increased blindness burden for patients with type 2 diabetes. These findings highlight the need for targeted interventions to effectively mitigate the global impact of DR-related blindness.

The predictive analysis painted a worrying picture of the future burden of DR-related blindness. The projected increase in the ASPR and patient numbers by 2035 underscores the importance of addressing this issue now to prevent potentially catastrophic impacts on global vision health. Policymakers should continue investing in research, prevention, and treatment strategies to reduce the growing burden of DR-related blindness worldwide. However, it is important to note that these projections rely on the assumptions of the BAPC model, which cannot account for unanticipated developments such as policy reforms, medical breakthroughs, or sudden demographic changes. Therefore, the projected estimates should be interpreted with caution, particularly in the context of rapidly evolving public health systems.

This study has several limitations. First, in some underdeveloped countries, limited healthcare infrastructure may lead to misdiagnoses or underreporting of cases, potentially resulting in an underestimation of the burden of DR-related blindness. Second, the definition of blindness used in this study may not capture all cases of vision impairment due to DR. Third, the GBD 2021 estimates are derived from a wide range of data sources, including epidemiological surveillance, disease registries, and scientific literature. While the GBD framework incorporates robust modeling techniques and standardization procedures to enhance comparability across locations and over time, it remains dependent on the availability and quality of input data. In countries with limited surveillance systems or scarce ophthalmologic data, estimates may be extrapolated based on covariates or regional patterns, which can introduce bias or increase uncertainty. Therefore, country-level results are recommended to be interpreted with caution. Moreover, variations in diagnostic criteria, case definitions, and reporting practices for DR across countries may introduce heterogeneity into the underlying data, thereby affecting the comparability and accuracy of estimates. Lastly, this analysis focuses on population-level trends rather than individual-level risk factors or clinical outcomes. Therefore, findings should be interpreted as descriptive of epidemiological patterns useful for public health planning, rather than as precise clinical data. Despite these limitations, we believe the GBD data provide useful estimates for global comparisons, and our findings can provide important information for public health policies and intervention strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, the global burden of DR-related blindness has increased significantly from 1990 to 2021, with pronounced disparities observed across different regions, countries, and sociodemographic groups. These findings underscore the pressing need for targeted interventions in high-burden regions and countries. Future research should focus on identifying effective interventions and policies to address the already-coming and upcoming challenges.
