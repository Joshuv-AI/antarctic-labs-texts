Wear of interim resin materials manufactured by 3D printing and CAD/CAM milling following chewing simulation
Authors: Yelda Çiçek, Ceren Küçük
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRecently introduced novel additively manufactured interim resins require in vitro investigation of their mechanical properties. The objective of the study is to assess the wear rates of four different interim resin materials using a chewing simulator for 1.5 months of dynamic loading.MethodsFour interim resin materials were assessed: (1) Liquid crystal displays (LCD) 3D printed (n = 8), (2) Digital light processing (DLP) 3D printed (n = 8) (3) Conventional autopolymerizing bis-acrylic (n = 8), (4) CAD/CAM milled interim resin materials. The specimens underwent 30,000 cycles, approximately equivalent to 1.5 months of chewing simulation. The volumetric loss (mm3) and wear depth (mm) of each specimen was calculated. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to identify intergroup differences (α = 0.05).ResultsThe mean ± SD wear volume losses (mm3) following chewing simulation were 0.1394 ± 0.0810 for conventional resin, 0.1099 ± 0.0873 for milled resin, 0.0980 ± 0.1021 for DLP resin, and 0.0934 ± 0.0788 for LCD resin. Wear volume loss was not significantly different amongst interim materials (P > 0.05). The mean ± SD wear depths (mm) following chewing simulation were 0.4084 ± 0.01440 for conventional resin, 0.4343 ± 0.1115 for milled resin, 0.4546 ± 0.0349 for DLP resin, and 0.3954 ± 0.1051 for LCD resin. Wear depths were not significantly different amongst interim materials (P > 0.05).Conclusions3D printed and CAD/CAM milled resins had wear resistance comparable to conventional resin. Wear resistance after chewing simulation offers 3D printed resin a suitable interim restorative material for clinical use.

Background

Interim prosthetic treatment is an important part of managing prosthetic rehabilitation properly. It is widely agreed that interim restorations are only meant to be used for a short time, however extended use of interim restorations is often essential for various types of occlusal reconstructions and implant treatments [1, 2]. Therefore, the provisional material must have enhanced wear resistance, mechanical strength, aesthetic appeal, and marginal integrity [3, 4]. In general, conventional techniques are employed to fabricate interim restorations using bis-acryl or polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) due to their cost-effectiveness and simplicity of fabrication [5, 6]. However, these exhibit significant disadvantages, including severe shrinkage during polymerization, exothermic reactions at the curing process, water absorption, residual monomers, cracks and porosity, color alteration, and insufficient marginal integrity [5, 7].

Advancements in computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) approaches leads the fabrication of interim prostheses through additive 3-dimensional (3D) printing and subtractive milling methods [8]. CAD/CAM interim restorations are manufactured from preprocessed acrylic resin blocks and are said to provide greater stability in color and marginal accuracy compared to the conventional technique [4]. Moreover, subtractive manufacturing methods reduced the high polymerization shrinkage and high residual monomer problems associated with conventional PMMA-based interim materials [8]. Nevertheless, subtractive milling presents many disadvantages, including the wasting of milling burs and restorative materials, as well as challenges in fabricating complex forms [9]. More recently, techniques for three-dimensional (3D) printing have become increasingly popular. The ease of printing, reduced manufacturing time, less waste of raw material, sufficient surface roughness for interim restorations, and reduced costs of 3D printers have rendered this technology essential for prosthodontics [8, 10]. The additive manufacturing process begins with a virtual image generated by a 3D scanner or CAD software. It manufactures products using various additive manufacturing methods by incrementally adding small portions of material layer by layer [11, 12]. Liquid crystal display stereolithography (LCD) and digital light processing (DLP) are the leading 3D printing techniques used for the fabrication of interim restorations [3, 12]. The DLP procedure, involving photo-polymerization by ultraviolet light (UV), applied to a tank filled with light-curing resin composed of acrylic or epoxy, can be used for the fabrication of prostheses. Likewise to DLP, the LCD technique relies on photopolymerization; however, it employs liquid crystals rather than a light projector for imaging [5].

In recent years, advancements in restorative materials have profoundly impacted the field of reconstructive dentistry through digital dental systems [13]. However, studies regarding the wear behavior of novel interim materials following simulated chewing are lacking. This study aimed to assess the wear resistance of interim resin materials produced by the use of 3D printers (LCD or DLP) with those fabricated using conventional method (acrylic resin and bis-acryl resin). The hypothesis of this study was that the examined interim materials after the chewing simulation would not differ in terms of wear volume and depth.

Methods

Two 3D-printed interim restorative materials (LCD and DLP), 1 CAD/CAM milled (PMMA based) material, and 1 conventional material (bis-acryl resin) were evaluated for wear resistance. Specimens were obtained for the study as per Table 1. The sample size calculation was performed according to a study [3] by Myagmar et al. using a power analysis software program (G* Power Vesion 3.1.9.6). The minimum required number of specimens for each group was determined to be 5 with power of %95 and an α error of 0.05. Therefore, 8 specimens were fabricated for each group.

Materials used in the study

To manufacture the 3D printed and milled specimens (n = 32; 8 per group) rectangular parallelepipeds (15 × 10 × 10 mm; width × length × height) were designed using CAD software program (Cerec InLab 3.60; Sirona Dental Systems, Bensheim, Germany), and the design files were transmitted in the standard tessellation language (STL) format.

LCD-type 3D printed specimens (n = 8) were prepared with a light-cure biocompatible resin (Alias Temp C&B, Dokuz Kimya, Aydin, Turkey) using an LCD-type 3D printer (Phrozen Sonic 4 K device, Phrozen Tech., Hsinchu, Taiwan). The samples were produced at a printing orientation of 0o. The layer thickness was set to 50 μm. Following the 3D printing procedure, the residual monomer on the specimen’s surface was subjected to a 2-minute wash with 90% isopropyl alcohol by using an ultrasonic device (Cd-4820 Codyson, China). Following that, an UV LED device (LunaCure, Phrozen Tech., Hsinchu, Taiwan) was used to post-cure the specimens for 20 min.

DLP-type 3D printed specimens (n = 8) were prepared with a light-cure biocompatible resin (TempPrint GC Europe, Buckinghamshire, England) using a DLP type 3D printer (Asiga Max U.V device, Asiga Max, Asiga, Australia). The samples were produced at printing orientation of 0o. The layer thickness was set to 50 μm. Following the 3D printing procedure, the residual monomer on the specimen’s surface was subjected to a 2-minute wash with 90% isopropyl alcohol by using an ultrasonic device (Baku Bk-2400, Guangzhou Hanker Electronics Technology, Guangdong, China). Following that, an UV LED device (Asiga Flash, Asiga, Australia) was used to post-cure the specimens for 20 min.

Milled specimens (Group n = 8) were machined from the PMMA resin disk (Vita CAD-Temp; Vita Zahnfabrik, BadSackingen, Germany) by a 5-axis milling machine (Cerec inLab; Sirona Dental Systems).

For the fabrication of conventional interim resin specimens, a silicon mold was made and autopolymerizing bis-acrylic material (TempSmart, GC Europe, England) was injected into it. The mold was compressed by a glass slab under a 1.5 kg force for 3 min to eliminate excess material and ensure smooth surfaces, per the manufacturer’s recommendations for polymerization.

All samples were polished with 800 and 1200 grit silicon carbide sandpaper (Buehler, USA) under water cooling.

The two-body wear simulation was conducted using a dual-axis computer-controlled chewing simulator (CS-4.2, SD Mechatronik GmbH, Westerham, Germany). The specimens were fixed to the lower part of machine, while the antagonists (stainless steel hemispheres with a radius of 1.5 mm) were fixed in the upper part. The specimens were subjected to abrasion for 30,000 cycles, approximately equivalent to 1.5 months of chewing under a vertical load of 50 N, with a lateral movement of 0.6 mm at a frequency of 1.6 Hz. Specimens of each material were simultaneously subjected to thermal cycling during the wear simulation. The test chambers were flooded using distilled water with a temperature of 5 °C for 60 s and – after 12-second pause time to empty the chambers – with a temperature of 55 °C for 60 s. A total of 155 thermocycles were applied.

Each specimen was scanned with a 3D laser scanner (LAS-20, SD Mechatronik GmbH, Westerham, Germany) before and after the chewing simulation. The starting and ending points of the surfaces of the samples to be scanned were marked. The measurement step of the scanning was adjusted to 0.02 mm. The scanned data of specimens before and after the chewing simulation were saved in STL format and subsequently imported into a surface analysis program (Geomagic Control of 3D Systems, SD Mechatronik, Westerham, Germany) and superimposed. The superimposition contains two steps: alignment of prewear and postwear STL files and surface comparison to determine the amount of wear. The alignment achieved through best-fit algorithms using non-abraded areas as a reference. After alignment, the amount of volumetric loss (mm3) and wear depth (mm) were measured by comparing the STL files before and after the chewing simulation in such a way that the volumetric distances of the initial and final states of each sample to a particular plane were calculated separately. The accuracy of the analysis software has been validated within 0.5 mm. The parameters were the volume of wear, and maximum and mean wear depth.

Data analysis was carried out with a statistical software program (IBM SPSS Statistics, v28.0 for Windows; IBM Corp). The data did not show a normal distribution, so nonparametric tests were used. The Kruskal-Wallis test was used to identify intergroup differences (α = 0.05). Pairwise comparisons between groups were assessed using Dunn’s multiple comparisons test (α = 0.05).

Discussion

This in vitro study assessed the wear resistance of four different interim restorative materials after simulated chewing. The null hypothesis of this study was accepted as the findings indicated no significant difference in wear resistance between the evaluated interim materials. After the chewing test, 3D printed, milled, and conventional resins showed comparable wear volume loss.

The weakness of this study is that interim resin materials were tested for a simulated time span of 1.5 months, which is relatively short. Interim restorations are often applied for approximately 1.5 months. However, if the treatment involves extra root canal therapy or periodontal and implant surgery, interim restorations may require usage for over three months. This extended period of use may affect the wear behavior of the resin restorative materials used. However, it must be noted that approximately 40% of the ultimate wear of resin materials has been observed to occur within the first 10,000 cycles, which is equivalent to about 15 days of use [14]. Another limitation is that this study assessed the material’s wear volume loss using specimens with a rectangular parallelepiped shape instead of a crown shape. The outcomes for teeth may vary, as real clinical conditions represent complex, multidirectional forces on anatomically contoured teeth resulting from eating habits and chewing patterns [15]. Additionally, the material and shape of the antagonist significantly affect the wear in two-body wear simulators; enamel antagonists enable realistic contact simulation [16]. The use of a stainless steel antagonist does not accurately reflect clinical conditions and the different types of clinical contact conditions, such as opposing enamel or ceramic. Furthermore, it is essential to recognize that outcomes from in vitro studies could not be instantly applied to clinical settings. An accurate representation of the complicated oral environments of wear and mastication is impossible in vitro [17]. Despite the chewing simulator’s design to accurately mimic clinical conditions, the test failed to account for oral factors like saliva, non-axial loads, or acidic exposure [5]. Moreover, in the current study the printing orientation was set at 0°, despite existing literature indicating that orientation considerably affects wear behaviour [15, 18]. Incorporating various build angles may enhance experimental reliability. Finally, the existing data regarding the chemical composition, filler content, and mechanical properties of these materials remains limited. The increasing variety of 3D printing methods and materials complicates the classification and comparison of these materials [10]. The current study’s limitations include the quick development and marketing of novel 3D printing resins, which may result in resins with varying compositions and polymerization durations, potentially yielding interim restorations with differing properties.

3D-printed materials, being very new in the market, have to exhibit wear resistance that is at least equivalent to or superior to that of conventional composites. Inferior wear resistance may result in diminished stability of occlusal contacts due to the early emergence of flaws on abraded surfaces, potentially leading to a decreased lifespan of the entire interim restoration [19]. In line with the current study, the wear resistance of MMA-based 3D-printed interim resins was reported to exceed that of PMMA-based conventional and CAD/CAM milled interim resins [3, 9, 16, 20]. According to the results of these studies 3D-printed resins are applicable for short-term clinical interim restorations in terms of wear resistance. However, it is notable that all the evaluated 3D-printed materials were methacrylate-based. Recently, highly filled hybrid materials have been introduced for the additive manufacturing of definitive crowns, inlays, and bridges. The addition of filler to the resins increases the wear resistance significantly [16]. Therefore these material with high filler content can be recommended for long term provisional and definitive use in terms of wear resistance. Rosentritt et al. [21] evaluated the wear behavior of additively or subtractively fabricated resin-based composite molar crowns for interim and definitive restorations and concluded that 3D printed interim and definitive molar crowns demonstrated at least adequate in vitro performance and wear stability. In line with the current study, it was stated that no benefits of either manufacturing method were seen, reaffirming the applicability of both subtractively and additively manufactured crowns.

The addition of 3D printing to the prosthodontics workflow enables the digital fabrication of not only temporary restorations but also permanent crowns and fixed partial dentures. A narrative review of the literature on 3D printed resin composite, ceramic, and zirconia materials for definitive restoration was reported, emphasizing the mechanical, biological, and aesthetic aspects of these materials [22]. Several studies [23–27] have indicated favorable outcomes regarding the mechanical characteristics of 3D printed materials for definitive restorations. In addition to the mechanical properties, dimensional accuracy and fit have also been examined. Numerous studies [28–33] have demonstrated that 3D printed materials show significant promise in terms of dimensional accuracy and fit for replacing conventional fabrication methods and addressing the particular limitations of milling technology.

The integrating of filler into the resins notably improves wear resistance and is considered crucial importance. A material with increased filler amount is advised for prolonged interim treatment [16]. However, 3D printed materials need an appropriate viscosity according to the printing procedure [34]. As viscosity increases due to the addition of fillers, gravity and surface tension fail to create a uniform surface, leading to a failure of object printing [35]. Furthermore, extended storage of 3D printing resins in large containers may lead to precipitation of the fillers, resulting in an inhomogeneous distribution of components and decreased mechanical strength [16]. The fillers unable to prevent the composite matrix from wearing. Consequently, the wear resistance of the 3D-printed restorations depends critically on the filler distribution [20]. To ensure a homogeneous composition of all the ingredients, manufacturers suggest several mixing procedures like shaking, stirring, or specialized roller/tilt mixers, for up to one hour prior to using the resins, depending on the product. An alternative solution to this problem is the surface modification of the large fillers. An increased roughness inhibits the fillers from sinking. Also smaller fillers may be added to slow down the sinking of the larger fillers [16]. As a consequence of these knowledge, the filler volume content and manufacturer recommendations for mixing processes should be considered for 3D-printed materials. Kessler et al. [16]. evaluated the three-body wear of three 3D printed interim materials and one CAD/CAM-milled material, concluding that 3D-printed interim resin materials exhibit comparable wear resistance to previously developed interim materials. They additionally stated that the 3D printed material with 30% filler by volume showed statistically lower wear than the other 3D printed and milled materials, declaring as the addition of filler into the resins markedly enhances wear resistance and is considered the top priority. This finding was consistent with those of Firlej et al. [19], who revealed that interim resins with high filler content exhibited less wear without aging.

Using a mastication simulator, Myagmar et al. [3] evaluated the wear of DLP-printed, CAD/CAM-milled and conventional interim resin materials and found that during each cycle the 3D printed resin had a lower wear volume loss than the milled and conventional resins. The present study’s results indicate no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of wear volume loss. Earlier studies [3, 15] indicate that differences in material composition, manufacturing methods, and post-curing treatments could be responsible for variances in wear resistance outcomes. The different outcomes between the study of Myagmar et al. [3] and the current study may have been the consequence of these differences. Park et al. [9] also assessed the wear resistance of 3D printing resin materials in comparison to milled and conventional resin materials, reporting that the wear volume loss of the 3D printed resin did not differ significantly from that of the milled and conventionally made interim resin following 1.5 months of chewing simulation. These results align with the findings of the current study.

In their study, Wan et al. [15] assessed the wear resistance of additively manufactured resin under various printing parameters and post-polymerization conditions and reported that the wear resistance of the printed resin was markedly superior when printing orientation is 45 degrees in comparison to 0- and 90-degree printing orientations. On the contrary, Lee et al. [18] stated that for the fabrication of an interim crown with DLP 3D printers, a printing orientation of 0 degrees is recommended for achieving superior wear resistance compared to a 90-degree angle. In further studies, it may be helpful to evaluate the 3D printing orientation in terms of wear resistance. Studies involving a broader spectrum of materials with different filler volumes and matrix compositions, more extended simulation periods, more clinically relevant specimen geometries, and various antagonists can be performed to assess the wear resistance of innovative additively manufactured interim materials.

Conclusions

After simulating the clinical chewing phase, which is equal to a duration of 1.5 months, the 3D printed resin and the CAD/CAM milled resin demonstrated wear resistance comparable to that of the conventional interim resin. In terms of wear resistance, 3D printing technology has been determined to be appropriate for interim restorations.
