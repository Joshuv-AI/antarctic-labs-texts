Bridging the gap in traumatic brain injury (TBI) assessment: The cognitive and behavioral inventory (CBI)
Authors: Haewon Byeon
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The global burden of traumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a persistent public health crisis, leaving millions with invisible yet profound deficits. Yet, our current diagnostic toolkit remains surprisingly blunt. In the Indian context, clinicians have long relied on generic instruments like the Indian Disability Evaluation and Assessment Scale (IDEAS).[1] While IDEAS serves general psychiatric conditions well, applying it to TBI is often an exercise in frustration.[2] It frequently fails to capture the subtle, erosive cognitive and behavioral sequelae—such as frontal lobe paradoxes—that define the TBI experience. Consequently, patients often face inaccurate disability estimates, leading to clinically misguided rehabilitation plans and contentious legal battles over compensation.

Chacko et al.[3] address this diagnostic blind spot with the Cognitive and Behavioral Inventory (CBI). The CBI does not merely adapt existing metrics; it rebuilds the assessment framework around the specific pathology of brain injury. It targets the very symptoms generic tools ignore: executive dysfunction, impulsivity, and chronic irritability. These are not just medical symptoms; they are the practical barriers preventing a patient’s successful reintegration into society. By focusing on these high-impact domains, the CBI offers a granularity that generic scales simply cannot achieve.

The validation process demonstrates rigor. By correlating the CBI with gold-standard measures—the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Neuropsychiatry Inventory Questionnaire (NPI-Q)—the authors firmly ground their tool in established science. The results are compelling. Pearson correlation coefficients of 0.87 for cognition and 0.79 for behavior indicate strong construct validity. Perhaps even more significant is the inter-rater reliability (Intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.99). In a clinical landscape where subjective interpretation often clouds judgment, such reliability suggests that two different clinicians will arrive at nearly the same conclusion using the CBI. This consistency is paramount, particularly for forensic and insurance applications where reproducibility determines the fairness of a verdict.

Yet, we must temper optimism with necessary caution. As the authors frankly admit, the CBI currently measures impairment—the medical deficit—rather than disability—the functional limitation. This distinction matters. A score on a clinical test does not automatically translate to a percentage of lost earning capacity in a court of law. The immediate challenge for future research lies in mapping these raw scores to disability weights that align with India’s national guidelines. Furthermore, while the initial validation is robust, the sample size requires expansion. To ensure the CBI is truly representative, we need data spanning diverse socioeconomic and regional demographics across India, confirming that the tool holds up outside the initial study population.

Ultimately, the CBI offers a domain-specific precision that generic instruments cannot match. It moves us away from rough approximations and toward a granular understanding of the TBI patient’s reality. By quantifying the previously unquantifiable aspects of brain injury, this tool paves the way for fairer compensation and rehabilitation strategies that target the patient’s deficits. Chacko et al.[3] have provided a foundational instrument; the clinical community must now refine and deploy it.
