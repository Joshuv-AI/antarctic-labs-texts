Organic Photovoltaics of Regiorandom Copolymers Composed of Unsymmetric Diketopyrrolopyrrole with Different Alkyl Chain Lengths
Authors: Ryosuke Kamimura, Fumitaka Ishiwari, Akinori Saeki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP) is an authentic π-conjugated building unit that has been utilized as the electron acceptor of narrow-bandgap copolymers for organic photovoltaics (OPVs). The conventional DPP unit is symmetrically flanked with identical aromatic rings, whereas unsymmetric DPP with different aromatic units has remained mostly unexplored. Here, we report the synthesis and characterization of DPP copolymers composed of thiophene (Th) and pyridine (Py) as the unsymmetric configuration of side aromatics. The unsymmetric DPP unit is appended with three different lengths of alkyl chain and coupled with a Th donor to simplify the polymer structure, which is in contrast to the benchmark, high-cost benzodithiophene donor. With the elongation of the alkyl chain, a significant deepening of the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level by ∼0.3 eV is observed. Furthermore, the crystallinity as well as the HOMO levels are also subject to alteration by the processing solvents, yielding a maximum power conversion efficiency of 5.59% (blended with Y6 non-fullerene acceptor), much higher than those of IT4F-blended OPVs (∼1%). We provide a comprehensive evaluation of the bulk heterojunction morphology, polymer crystallinity, fluorescence quenching, and space-charge-limited current mobility, highlighting the effect of the alkyl chain length of unsymmetric DPP copolymers on the OPV performances.

Introduction

In the pursuit of a sustainable and renewable energy supply, − organic photovoltaics (OPVs) have gained attention as one of the next-generation solar cells owing to their light weight, low cost, and flexible characters. − In the past decade, the rapid development of narrow-bandgap polymers (NBP) and non-fullerene acceptor (NFA) has boosted the power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) to over 20%. − Following the established highly crystalline regioregular poly­(3-hexylthiophene), − the donor–acceptor (D–A) NBPs have emerged as a promising solution that allows fine-tuning of the bandgap and energy level via intramolecular charge transfer transitions. − The design strategy employed by NBPs exhibits a high degree of freedom in their building blocks, in particular, the A units including isoindigo, − thienopyrrolodione, − benzothiadiazole, − quinoxaline, − and diketopyrrolopyrrole (DPP). −

Among them, DPP is an authentic organic dye and a strong electron-withdrawing unit, which shows high crystallinity and high charge carrier mobility in its π-conjugated polymers. Generally, the DPP unit has two symmetrical thiophene (Th) , or furan rings , at both sides of DPP, which forms a planar π-plane and leads to 6–8% PCEs for polymer–fullerene OPVs. − As an extended modification, unsymmetric DPP polymers flanked with Th and thienothiophene (TT) rings were reported by Ji et al. in 2016, and other unsymmetric DPP polymers with Th and pyridine (Py) were subsequently developed. Nonetheless, the PCEs of these copolymers have remained at 6–8%, comparable to those of conventional symmetric DPP polymers. In these polymers, the electron donors of oligothiophene and alkylthiophene-flanked benzodithiophene (BDT) − are often coupled with DPP. The resultant unsymmetric, regiorandom DPP copolymers exhibit an optimal balance between solubility and crystallinity, together with a preferential face-on orientation of the polymer backbone suitable for OPVs. In addition, we documented the synthesis of unsymmetric DPP copolymers with phenyl (Ph) and Th or TT (i.e., Ph-DPP-Th or Ph-DPP-TT). These copolymers show a low PCE of 2.4% when they are blended with soluble fullerene (PC61BM). The phenomenon is attributed to their reduced crystallinity and diminished hole mobility. We further developed the unsymmetric DPP-based polymers with Py or fluorinated benzene (FBz). The Py ring is intended to reduce steric hindrance in comparison to Ph, while the FBz is intended to improve the open-circuit voltage (V OC) by deepening the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level. Consequently, an enhanced PCE of 3.75% (PC61BM as the acceptor) was achieved for the unsymmetric, random polymer composed of Py-DPP-Th and BDT. However, the utilization of the BDT donor leads to a comparatively elevated synthetic cost (Figure S1). Furthermore, the exploration of unsymmetric DPP polymers in the nonfullerene acceptor (NFA)-based OPVs has remained infrequent.

In this study, we report the synthesis and characterization of the unsymmetric Th-DPP-Py polymers, employing the simple Th unit as the donor. The side alkyl chains of the DPP unit are extended from 2-hexyldecyl (HD) and 2-octyldecyl (OD) to 2-decyltetradecyl (DT), yielding P1, P2, and P3, respectively (Figure ). In addition to the characterizations of bulk heterojunction (BHJ) OPV of these polymers blended with the benchmark NFAs (Y6 or IT4F), we conducted an extensive investigation of the morphological, photophysical, and electric properties using two-dimensional grazing-incidence wide-angle X-ray scattering (2D-GIWAXS), atomic force microscopy (AFM), surface free energy (SFE) by contact angle (CA) measurement, photoluminescence (PL) quenching, and charge carrier mobility by space-charge-limited current (SCLC). Although the PCE of 5.59% obtained for P2:Y6 is moderate, this is among the highest values in the non-BDT and unsymmetric DPP polymer NFA-OPVs.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterizations

The overall synthetic routes of P1, P2, and P3 are provided in Scheme . The unsymmetric DPP monomers were synthesized in 6 steps from thiophene derivatives 1. Notably, the yields of the alkylation reaction of compound 5 reached 41–44%, which is significantly higher than the typical range of 8–29%. , The improvement in this critical step in the overall yield is likely attributable to the suppression of byproducts at the reduced reaction time (4 h vs usually >12 h). The polymerization was implemented by Stille cross-coupling of a bis-stannylated Th with compounds 6a–6c. The detailed descriptions of the monomer and polymer syntheses and their characterizations are provided in the Supporting Information. The weight- and number-averaged molecular weights (M w and M n, respectively) and their dispersions (M w/M n) were evaluated by size exclusion chromatography (Figure S2), giving M n = 17.6 kDa and M w/M n = 3.5 for P1, M n = 20.1 kDa and M w/M n = 2.6 for P2, and M n = 71.8 kDa and M w/M n = 1.8 for P3 (Table ). The significant increase in the M n of P3 can be attributed to the increased solubility, owing to its longest alkyl chain length.

Photophysical and Electrochemical Properties

The photophysical and electrochemical properties of P1, P2, and P3 were evaluated by ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectroscopy and photoelectron yield spectroscopy (PYS, Figure S3) in thin films prepared from chloroform (CF) solutions. As shown in Figure a, the UV–vis absorption spectra of P1 and P3 are very similar, while that of P2 shows a blue shift in the maximum and decrease in the high-energy side vibronic peak (0–1 transition) compared with that of low-energy one (0–0 transition). Consequently, the optical bandgap energy (E g) of P2 is marginally smaller (1.56 eV) than those of P1 (1.53 eV) and P3 (1.52 eV) (Figure S4). Figure b displays the energy levels of the copolymers and NFAs, including the HOMO obtained from the PYS measurements and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels (=HOMO + E g) (Table ). Interestingly, the HOMO levels undergo a substantial shift from −5.47 eV for P1, to −5.58 eV for P2 and −5.77 eV for P3, suggesting a decrease in the effective conjugation length due to the increased twist of the polymer backbone bearing long alkyl chains. This is consistent with the marked decrease in crystallinity with increasing alkyl chains (vide infra). − A structurally optimized density functional theory (DFT) calculation of the polymer (three repeating units) exhibits the small dihedral angles at the DPP–Py bond (0°) and DPP–Th bond (2°) (Figure S5). This structural flattening may offer a partial explanation for the appearance of the shoulder peak. In contrast, the UV–vis absorption spectra of thin films prepared from chlorobenzene (CB) showed a divergent trend (Figure S6), where P1 exhibited the blue-shifted peak, while P2 and P3 were mostly identical. The intense 0–0 vibronic peaks observed in P2 and P3 suggest a more planar conformation than P1. The HOMO levels exhibited a slight shift toward the deep side by ∼0.1 eV: −5.57 eV for P1, −5.63 eV for P2, and −5.86 eV for P3 (Figures S7 and S3). The observations are associated with the more amorphous nature of CB-processed films than of CF-processed films, as discussed later in the 2D-GIWAX measurements.

Device Characterization

The inverted-type BHJ-OPV devices (indium tin oxide (ITO)/ZnO/active layer/MoO x /Ag) were fabricated from CB solutions with 5 vol % diphenyl ether as the solvent additive. The optimal PCE values were 1.30% for P1:IT4F, 1.49% for P2:IT4F, and 0.78% for P3:IT4F (Table ). These low PCEs suffered from the low short-circuit current density (J SC) and fill factor (FF), as is evident from the current density–voltage curves shown in Figure a. The external quantum efficiency (EQE in Figure b) spectra exhibit low values (∼0.3 at the maximum), and their shapes are similar to the superimposition of the photoabsorption spectra of the polymer and IT4F (the cutoff of the EQE is ∼850 nm). Subsequently, the BHJ-OPV devices of the polymer and Y6 were fabricated from CF solutions with 0.5 vol % 1-chloronaphthalene as the solvent additive. Although the PCEs of P1:Y6 and P3:Y6 remained at the low values of 2.73% (average 2.23 ± 0.44%) and 2.93% (average 2.03 ± 0.53%), respectively, P2:Y6 demonstrated a substantial enhancement in its PCE, achieving 5.59% (average 4.20 ± 0.75%) (Figure c and Table ). All the device parameters of P2:Y6 including J SC, V OC, and FF along with the EQE spectrum (Figure d) are the highest, whereas the FF is still not high (53.3%). Owing to the complementary photoabsorption of the polymer and Y6, the EQE spectra are extended to ∼950 nm, and the value is increased (∼0.48). The increased J SC and EQE maxima are partly associated with the augmented driving force for charge separation at the HOMO–HOMO and LUMO–LUMO energy offsets between P2 and Y6. The V OC values for P2:Y6 and P3:Y6 differ by only 0.01, although the HOMOs of P2 and P3 are largely different (−5.58 vs −5.77 eV, respectively, by 0.19 eV). This is likely due to the amorphous nature of the P3:Y6 blend (vide infra) and resultant V OC loss through the charge trap.

Film Crystallinity

The 2D-GIWAXS images of pristine films fabricated by CF revealed that P3 is amorphous without π–π stacking diffraction and orientation (Figure a, the profiles are provided in Figure S8). In contrast, P1 and P2 show weak π–π stacking and interlamellar diffractions in the out-of-plane (OOP) direction, indicating their more crystalline and face-on/edge-on-mixed features than P3, favorable for OPV. The π–π stacking distance (d π) and its coherence length (L π) calculated using Scherrer’s relation were 3.65 and 23.3 Å for P1 and 3.67 and 20.0 Å for P2, respectively (Table S1). These d π values are typical among the crystalline polymers (about 3.5–3.7 Å). We also analyzed the in-plane (IP) profiles of 2D-GIWAXS data and obtained the interlamellar d-spacing (d IL) and coherence length (L IL) of P1–P3 pristine films to be P1: d IL = 19.4 Å, L IL = 105 Å; P2: d IL = 21.4 Å, L IL = 115 Å; and P3: d IL = 23.9 Å, L IL = 122 Å. The increases in d IL and L IL are understood from the increase in the alkyl chain length. However, these L π values are small, and the number of interlamellar polymers (L IL/d IL) is almost the same (5.1–5.4) among the three polymers. Accordingly, the crystallinity of P1–P3 is inherently low, and therefore, its effect on E g is marginal. On the other hand, the pristine films fabricated using CB exhibited the clear edge-on orientation of P1 accompanied by the high-order lamellar diffractions of (100) and (200). P2 and P3 became completely amorphous, indicating a change in crystallinity and orientation by the processing solvent. The 2D-GIWAXS images of P1–P3:NFA blend films are shown in Figure b, where all of the Y6-blended films prepared from CF exhibit strong π–π stacking diffraction in the OOP direction and lamellar diffraction in the IP direction. They are readily ascribed to Y6, and there is no significant difference in either d π and L π. However, the face-on orientations of P2:Y6 and P3:Y6 are more distinct than those of P1:Y6, suggesting the better electron transport of the former. In contrast to the Y6-blended films, those blended with IT4F prepared from CB showed no orientation and mostly amorphous features. These crystalline features are consistent with the lower PCE of the IT4F-OPV than those of Y6-OPV.

Morphological Observation

The surface morphologies of blend films were observed by AFM as shown in Figure . All the Y6-blended films exhibited substantial aggregation (>50 nm) and average surface roughness (R a, 3–8 nm). The highest PCE and largest R a for P2:Y6 are generally contradicted, and therefore, the OPV device outputs are presumably governed by crystallinity and orientation of the polymer and Y6 and the resultant change in charge carrier mobilities (vide infra). Conversely, IT4F blend films exhibited minimal aggregation and low R a (∼1 and 2 nm), whereas their crystallinities are significantly low. CA measurements and SFE analysis were performed on the P1, P2, and P3 polymers (Figure S9 and Table S2). The SFE of the P2 film processed from CF was 20.3 mJ m–2, and the Flory–Huggins parameter (χ) of its blend with Y6 was 0.286, which was the largest value among the Y6 blends. However, this value remains lower than the χ parameter of the high-performing PM6:Y6 (0.502). Consequently, the optimal P2:Y6 system has considerable potential for further enhancement of the BHJ mixing through additional molecular modification. For the IT4F-blended films processed from CB, P2 showed the smallest χ parameter (0.0827) followed by 0.167 for P1 and 0.422 for P3. The χ parameter of PDCBT-Cl was 0.08, which shows the high PCE in combination with IT4F (PCE = 9.05%). The lower χ parameters of P1–P3:IT4F than P1–P3:Y6 are consistent with the higher polymer/NFA mixing of the former than that of the latter. However, the IT4F-blended films exhibited a loss of crystallinity and orientation upon the sacrifice of the well mixing process. This phenomenon could be a contributing factor to their low J SC values.

Charge Transfer Efficiencies and Charge Carrier Mobilities

In order to assess the efficiency of charge transfer and transport, we investigated PL quenching and SCLC charge carrier mobilities (Figures S10 and S11). In the blend film with Y6, the PL quenching efficiencies of P1, P2, and P3 (mainly polymers are excited) remained relatively low at 83%, 80%, and 60%, respectively. The low PL quenching showed the insufficient exciton diffusion to the donor/acceptor interface, as evidenced by the substantial aggregates observed in the AFM images. This phenomenon exhibited a similar tendency with the J SC values. The PL quenching efficiencies of P1, P2, and P3 in their IT4F blends (mainly NFAs are excited) were 76%, 50%, and 93%, respectively. The high PL quenching of P3:IT4F is consistent with its well-mixed morphology; however, its charge transport efficiency is probably low given its small J SC. AFM images and PL quenching indicate that while excitons can efficiently reach the interface in the CB-processed BHJ, their amorphous nature prevents charge transport. This assertion is corroborated by the SCLC hole mobilities (μh) and electron mobilities (μe) of the P1–P3:IT4F blends. The μhs were 1.0 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P1:IT4F, 5.1 × 10–4 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P2:IT4F, and 1.6 × 10–4 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P3:IT4F (Table ), indicating no trend with the alkyl chain length. The μes were largely comparable, with 9.3 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P1:IT4F, 4.3 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P2:IT4F, and 2.6 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P3:IT4F. Importantly the mobility balances (μe/μh) were 9.3, 0.084, and 6.2 for P1:IT4F, P2:IT4F, and P3:IT4F, respectively. The small μe on the order of 1 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 and the unbalanced μe/μh are in line with the low PCE values (mainly J SC). Although the μh values of P1–P3 blended with Y6 were similar to those of IT4F blends, the μe were partly increased to 3.7 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P1:Y6, 1.2 × 10–4 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P2:Y6, and 2.5 × 10–5 cm2 V–1 s–1 for P3:Y6. In particular, P2:Y6 showed the highest μh and μe values among the blend films. A significant difference is observed in the molecular weight and dispersity of P1, P2, and P3. However, P3, which has the largest molecular weight, exhibits low crystallinity along with low μh. Therefore, the impact of molecular weight and dispersity on solar cell performance is likely undominant. Most importantly, the well-balanced μe/μh values were obtained for P1:Y6 (1.8), P2:Y6 (1.1), and P3:Y6 (2.0), in accordance with the highest PCE of P2:Y6. Although P2 exhibited a lower PL quenching than the others, this blend showed the highest J SC, FF, and EQE due to its relatively high charge transport efficiency and relatively high crystallinity in the out-of-plane direction.

Conclusions

We synthesized and characterized a series of three Py-DPP-Th copolymers with different alkyl lengths, designated as P1, P2, and P3. These copolymers exhibit unique unsymmetric regiorandom configurations of the side aromatic rings. The PCEs of P1–P3:IT4F devices were 0.7–1.5%, due to the fact that P1–P3 had similar optical absorption spectra to IT4F, low crystallinity and orientation, and unbalanced μe/μh in all blend films. On the other hand, P2:Y6 devices showed an improved PCE of 5.59%, owing to the complementary optical absorption spectra to Y6, high crystallinity and face-on orientation of Y6, and high and balanced SCLC charge carrier mobilities. This study shows how different alkyl chains affect the self-assembly in the blend films and the OPV performance of DPP copolymers with unsymmetric molecular designs, thus expanding the library of conjugated polymers for organic electronics applications.

Experimental Section

General Measurement

Steady-state photoabsorption and photoluminescence spectroscopies were performed using Jasco V-730 ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) and Jasco FP-8300 spectrophotometers, respectively. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed by using a Jasco FT/IR-4700AC spectrometer. Photoelectron yield spectroscopy (PYS) measurements were performed using a Bunko Keiki BIP-KV202GD. The molecular weight (weight-averaged: M w; number-averaged: M n;) and polydispersity index (M w/M n) were measured using size exclusion chromatography (SEC, a Shimadzu Corp. LC-20AT/CBM-20A/CTO-20A/SPD-M20A) with polystyrene standards in hot chloroform (40 °C) as an eluent. 1H NMR (600 MHz) and 13C NMR (150 MHz) spectra were measured on a Bruker AVANCE-III spectrometer. High-resolution fast atom bombardment mass spectrometry (FAB-MS) was performed on a JEOL JMS-700. Chromatograms were recorded and analyzed by using a JASCO ChromNAV Ver.2 software system. Contact angle was measured by using an Asumi Giken CAME1. 2D-GIWAXS experiments were performed on the beamline BL46XU at the SPring-8 (12.39 keV, λ = 1 Å X-ray). The 2D-GIWAXS patterns were monitored with a 2D image detector (Pilatus 300 K). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) was performed by using a Bruker Innova AFM microscope. Thicknesses of the polymer and blend films were measured by using a Bruker Dektak XT surface profiler.

Device Fabrication

After cleaning an ITO/glass substrate, a ZnO layer was fabricated by spin-coating with a ZnO precursor solution (0.1 g mL–1 zinc acetate dihydrate and 0.028 g mL–1 ethanolamine in 2-methoxyethanol) followed by annealing on a hot plate at 200 °C for 30 min. On top of the ZnO layer, a BHJ layer (2000 rpm for 30 s for IT4F-based OPVs; 3000 rpm for 30 s for Y6-based OPVs) was spin-cast in a nitrogen glovebox. The optimized donor–acceptor (D–A) ratio was 1:1.5 for IT4F- and Y6-based OPVs. The optimized total concentration (D + A) was 16.0 mg mL–1. The optimized processing solvents for the IT4F and Y6 blends were chlorobenzene (CB):diphenyl ether (DPE) and chloroform (CF):1-chloronaphthalene (1-CN), respectively. Thermal annealing and solvent vapor annealing were examined but were found to be unsuccessful for these polymers (data are not shown). Subsequently, an anode (10 nm of MoO x and 100 nm of Ag) was deposited through a shadow mask by thermal evaporation in a vacuum chamber. The device configuration was ITO (120–160 nm)/ZnO (30 nm)/BHJ/MoO x (10 nm)/Ag (100 nm) with an active area of 7.1 mm2. The solar cell characteristics were measured using an ADCMT Corp. 6241A source meter unit under air mass 1.5 G solar illumination at 100 mW cm–2 (1 sun, monitored by a calibrated standard cell, Bunko Keiki SM-250 KD) from a 300 W solar simulator (SAN-EI Corp., XES-301S). The EQE spectra were recorded using a Bunko Keiki model BS-520BK equipped with a Keithley model 2401 source meter. The monochromatic light power for EQE measurements was calibrated by a silicon photovoltaic cell of a Bunko Keiki model S1337-1010BQ.

Space-Charge-Limited Current (SCLC)

The SCLC devices were fabricated in the same manner as the OPV devices. The hole-only device was ITO/PEDOT:PSS/active layer (100–200 nm)/MoO x /Ag, and the electron-only device was ITO/ZnO/active layer (∼200 nm)/C60/Al. The mobility was determined by fitting a current density–voltage curve by the Mott–Gurney law, J = 9ε 0 ε r μV 2(8L 3)−1, where ε 0, ε r, μ, V, and L are the permittivity of the free space, the dielectric constant of the material, the mobility, the voltage drop across the device, and the thickness of the active layer, respectively.
