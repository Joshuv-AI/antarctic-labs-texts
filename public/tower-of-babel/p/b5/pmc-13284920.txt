Pharmacologic Alteration of Meibum Lipid Composition Alleviates Dry Eye Phenotype in Awat2−/− Mice
Authors: Made Airanthi K. Widjaja-Adhi, Chloe Chung, Maryse Lapierre-Landry, Karina Chao, Eric Y. Lu, Jaclyn Swigris, Anna Walczak-Szeffer, Wiktor Golczak, Rony R. Sayegh, Michael W. Jenkins, Marcin Golczak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMeibomian glands (MGs) produce lipids that stabilize the tear film and maintain ocular surface homeostasis. Dysfunction of MG lipid secretion is a major cause of evaporative dry eye (EDE). The purpose of this study was to test whether pharmacologic modulation of meibum lipid biosynthesis could improve the physicochemical properties of meibum and thereby slow the progression of EDE in a mouse model of the disease.Methods Awat2−/− mice, an animal model of EDE, were treated systemically with ATR101 (nevanimibe), a selective sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1) inhibitor that suppresses cholesteryl ester (CE) synthesis. Meibum lipid composition, MG morphology, tear film breakup time, and corneal integrity were analyzed and compared with untreated controls to monitor disease progression.ResultsPharmacologic intervention in the CE biosynthesis in Awat2−/− mice altered the physicochemical properties of meibum, decreasing its melting temperature and increasing lipid fluidity. Treated mice exhibited reduced MG ductal obstruction and preservation of MG morphology. These changes were accompanied by prolonged tear film breakup time, improved tear film stability, and preserved corneal integrity compared with untreated Awat2−/− mice.ConclusionsPharmacologic intervention in meibogenesis corrects a pathogenic lipid imbalance in Awat2-deficient MGs and improves tear film and MG function. These findings identify lipid metabolic remodeling of meibum as a mechanistically grounded therapeutic strategy for obstructive MG dysfunction and evaporative dry eye.

Meibomian glands (MGs) are large sebaceous glands within the tarsal plates of the eyelids that secrete meibum, a complex mixture of lipids, via a holocrine mechanism. These meibum lipids form the outermost layer of the tear film, reducing its evaporation and stabilizing the air–liquid interface. Through these actions, MGs play essential roles in maintaining ocular surface homeostasis, ensuring optical quality, and protecting the eye from environmental stress and desiccation. Deterioration in the morphology or function of MGs, as well as changes in the quantity or quality of meibum they produce, results in the loss of these protective properties and manifests clinically as evaporative dry eye (EDE) disease.1 This common ocular disorder affects tens of millions of people worldwide, diminishing quality of life through acute and chronic symptoms that can lead to secondary ocular surface changes, visual impairment, and pain.2–5

The meibum discharge from the MGs is a complex mixture of lipids, many of which are synthesized exclusively by the MGs.6 Human meibum comprises esterified long-chain alcohols wax esters (WEs), cholesteryl esters (CEs), (O)-acylated ω-hydroxy fatty acids and their esters, and diacylated fatty α, ω-diols. Less abundant components include triacylglycerols, cholesterol, free fatty acids, sphingomyelins, and ceramides.7,8 Importantly, lipids isolated from mouse meibum revealed high resemblance to those obtained from human samples.8 Meibum lipids are synthesized de novo in situ instead of being sequestered by acinar cells from the systemic circulation.9 Also, the uniqueness of meibum lipid composition indicates the presence of specialized metabolic pathways not found in other cell types.8–10 Valuable insights into the metabolic pathways of meibogenesis are derived from recent studies on genetically modified mouse lines deficient in enzymes involved in lipid biosynthesis.11 These genetic approaches allowed for the direct investigation of the physiological role of particular enzymes and lipids, as well as providing valuable mouse models of meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD).

We developed a mouse model of MGD by inactivating the Awat2 gene (Awat2−/−).12 MGD in these mice begins with an altered meibum lipid composition that lacks WEs. Lipidomic analysis of Awat2−/− mice revealed that blockage of this specific metabolic pathway not only eliminates an entire lipid class but also induces a broader disruption of lipid homeostasis. The absence of WEs, which have a lower melting point than CEs, increases meibum viscosity, leading to ductal gland obstruction, glandular dilation, and progressive acinar atrophy.12 Similar pathologic features have been reported in humans and nonhuman primates with MGD.13,14 Thus, the Awat2−/− mouse recapitulates key aspects of human obstructive EDE, providing a valuable experimental model for elucidating disease mechanisms and developing targeted therapeutic strategies.

An emerging therapeutic approach that can be tested in mouse models of MGD involves pharmacologic manipulation of MG secretion.11,15 In this concept, the prevention of MG obstruction might partially recover tear film stability and avert the dire consequences of irreversible MG degeneration. We hypothesize that pharmacologically targeting specific metabolic pathways involved in meibogenesis can shift MG lipid homeostasis, restore meibum fluidity, and thus prevent the progression of EDE. The cause of MG obstruction and associated pathologies in Awat2−/− mice is the compensatory overproduction of CEs as a response to a metabolic blockage in WE biosynthesis.12 Therefore, the inhibition of CE overproduction constitutes a testable strategy aimed at improving the physicochemical properties of meibum, thus slowing the progression of the EDE phenotype in Awat2−/− mice, as schematically illustrated in Figure 1.

A strategy for treating evaporative dry eye by targeting lipid biosynthesis pathways. Metabolic alteration of meibum lipid, even without restoring native composition, improves meibum fluidity, prevents gland orifice blockage, and reduces tear film evaporation in Awat2−/− mice, a model of obstructive MGD. Therapeutic targets are selected based on the specific lipid profile of the meibum, with enzymes that regulate lipid balance serving as key candidates. In this model, SOAT1, an enzyme responsible for CE accumulation, is targeted. Inhibition of SOAT1 decreases CE concentration, rebalances meibum lipid composition, improves its physicochemical properties, prevents further ductal blockage, and thus provides therapeutic benefits in obstructive MGD.

To examine this hypothesis, we assessed the effect of ATR101 (nevanimibe), a selective inhibitor of sterol O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1), on the development of the EDE phenotype in Awat2−/− mice.16,17 Inhibition of SOAT1, the principal enzyme responsible for the biosynthesis of CEs in meibomian glands,18 improved meibum fluidity and slowed the progression of EDE. Slit-lamp examinations during treatment revealed fewer obstructed MG ducts and less viscous meibum, consistent with alterations in lipid composition. Treated mice also exhibited a prolonged tear film breakup time compared with untreated littermates, suggesting improved meibum secretion at the gland orifice and partial restoration of the tear film lipid layer, thereby enhancing tear film stability and preserving corneal integrity. Furthermore, alleviation of ductal obstruction reduced the rate of MG degeneration. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that pharmacologic targeting of the meibogenesis pathway can mitigate the progression of EDE symptoms in mice without restoring the native lipid composition of meibum. These results represent a novel therapeutic concept that might be applicable to human EDE, which often involves orifice blockage caused by increased meibum viscosity.

Methods

Animals

All mice used in this study were housed in a pathogen-free animal facility at the School of Medicine, Case Western Reserve University. Animals were maintained on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle under approximately 50 lux illumination, at a temperature range of 20°C to 26°C, with ad libitum access to food and water. Approval of all animal procedures and experiments was obtained from the Case Western Reserve University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The study adhered to the guidelines set forth by both the American Veterinary Medical Association Panel on Euthanasia and ARVO.

The experimental procedures outlined in this report involved female noncarrier Sox2-cre Awat2−/− (homozygous females) and male noncarrier Sox2-cre Awat2−/Y (hemizygous males). For simplicity, both sexes of mice were collectively designated as Awat2−/−, while their wild-type (WT) counterparts (female noncarrier Sox2-cre Awat2+/+ and male noncarrier Sox2-cre Awat2+/Y) were referred to as WT. Genotyping was conducted through genomic PCR using the methodology described in a previous publication.12

Our study included both male and female animals. As previously reported and confirmed again in the present study,12 the overall trends and magnitudes of the observed effects were comparable between sexes, with no statistically significant sex-specific differences detected for the primary outcome measures. Accordingly, to increase statistical power and streamline data presentation, we combined the data from male and female animals within each experimental group for analysis.

Slit-Lamp Biomicroscopy Examination

For corneal examination, mice were anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine cocktail (20 mg/mL ketamine and 1.75 mg/mL xylazine in sterile saline) administered via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 0.15 mL per 25 g of body weight. Corneal evaluation was subsequently performed using a slit-lamp biomicroscope (Zeiss SL 800, Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Jena, Germany). Each mouse was positioned in an animal holder aligned with the slit lamp's light path using an apochromatic lens. The ocular surface and eyelids were examined, and real-time, true-to-life color images were captured at 40× magnification using an LED light source with integrated slit illumination (cold-white and warm-white), optical filters (blue, green, and red), and an SL Cam Compact camera (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH).

Tear Film Breakup Time Test

To assess tear film stability, anesthetized mice were positioned on a slit-lamp biomicroscope. A small volume (∼1 µL) of sterile fluorescein sodium solution (1%) was carefully applied to the ocular surface using a micropipette. A uniform tear film was established following an initial spontaneous blink to ensure even distribution of the dye across the ocular surface. If anesthesia prevented spontaneous blinking, eyelids were gently closed using forceps to simulate a “forced blink.”

Examinations were conducted under blue-filter illumination, causing fluorescein to emit green fluorescence. Continuous video recording was initiated immediately prior to fluorescein instillation to ensure accurate capture of the starting point and continued until approximately one-third of the corneal surface was no longer covered by fluorescein staining. Tear film breakup time (TFBUT) was determined by frame-by-frame analysis of the recorded video and defined as the interval between complete fluorescein coverage of the corneal surface and the appearance of the discontinuity in the fluorescein layer. Trials in which a blink occurred during the observation period were excluded and repeated.

In Vivo Confocal Scanning Laser Microscope With Rostock Cornea Module

In vivo corneal imaging was performed using a confocal scanning laser microscope composed of the Heidelberg Retina Tomograph (Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany), coupled with the Rostock cornea module. Anesthetized mice were positioned in a custom mouse holder, and the cornea was imaged to assess epithelial morphology. Imaging was conducted using a 670-nm red laser diode, and backscattered light was detected by an avalanche photodiode through a confocal pinhole. Transverse images (384 × 384 pixels) were captured at a frame rate of ∼30 Hz. The cornea was optically coupled using a high-viscosity aqueous gel to fill the air gap between the microscope objective and a sterile, single-use polymethyl methacrylate TomoCap, Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany. This step minimizes refractive index mismatches along the optical pathway, which can otherwise generate back reflections, resulting in signal loss and potential ghost artifacts. An additional drop of ophthalmic gel was applied to the mouse eye to ensure proper optical contact and prevent corneal desiccation during imaging. The focal plane was adjusted manually using a mechanical z-feed (range ∼1.7 mm) and optically within an 80-µm range, enabling sequential imaging of individual corneal layers. Volumetric (z-series) scans were acquired at ∼2-µm intervals, with up to 40 optical sections collected over a maximum depth of ∼80 µm. The system provides a 400 × 400–µm field of view, a lateral resolution of 1 to 2 µm, and an axial resolution of ∼4 µm, allowing subcellular-resolution images of corneal epithelial layers, which were identified based on characteristic morphology, including bright cell borders, layer-specific cell size, and cellular organization of superficial, wing, and basal cells.

Tear Film Integrity Test

To assess tear film integrity, anesthetized mice were positioned under a stereomicroscope (Leica M205 C, Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) equipped with an LED ring light (66/80) and an LED control unit. Images were captured using an EC3 camera (Leica) and LAS 4.2 software. Tear film integrity was evaluated by analyzing the reflection pattern of the circular LED ring on the ocular surface. An intact and stable tear film produces smooth and continuous reflections, while distorted, irregular, or fragmented ring reflections indicate tear film instability or surface irregularities.

Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining and Imaging

The structural characteristics of mouse meibomian glands were evaluated by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of cryosections. Flash-frozen tissue cryosections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 minutes at room temperature, rinsed in 1× PBS to remove residual paraformaldehyde (four to five dips), and stained in hematoxylin for 3 minutes. Slides were then rinsed in tap water until streaking ceased, followed by immersion in tap water for 1 minute, bluing solution for 2 minutes, and tap water for an additional 1 minute. Sections were counterstained with 1% eosin for 6 minutes, briefly rinsed in water (four to five dips) to remove excess stain, and dehydrated sequentially in 50%, 70%, 95%, and 100% ethanol for 50 seconds each. Slides were then cleared in CitriSolv (Decon Laboratories, Inc., King of Prussia, PA) until streaking stopped and mounted with Cytoseal (Richard-Allan Scientific, Kalamazoo, MI) under a coverslip. Wide-field images of H&E-stained meibomian gland sections were acquired using a Zeiss AxioScan.Z1 slide scanner equipped with a Hitachi HV-F203 color camera and a 20×/0.8 Plan-Apochromat objective (Zeiss). Each image consisted of multiple tiles that were automatically stitched after acquisition using ZEN Blue software (Zeiss).

Collection of Mouse Meibum

For the retrieval of meibum samples, mice were anesthetized using a ketamine/xylazine cocktail and positioned in a slit-lamp bio-microscope for direct visualization of meibum secretion from the MG orifices. Meibum expression was performed in both Awat2−/− and WT mice using stainless steel meibomian gland expressor forceps applied gently along the eyelids. In Awat2−/− mice, expressed meibum exhibited a soft, paste-like consistency, consistent with lipid accumulation within the MG ducts, as previously described.12 In some cases, this material was directly collected using a microspatula. However, to ensure consistency across sample types, tear fluid from the lower eyelid meniscus, containing suspended meibum lipids, was also collected. In WT mice, meibum was more fluid and readily mixed with the tear film upon expression. Therefore, sample collection was primarily performed by harvesting tear fluid from the lower eyelid meniscus, which contains dispersed meibum lipids. For both Awat2−/− and WT mice, tear samples (containing meibum lipids) were collected from the lower meniscus using microcapillary tubes and transferred into glass vials containing hexane. When applicable (primarily in Awat2−/− samples), additional expressed material collected via microspatula was added to the same vial. Thus, all samples consisted of a mixture of tear film and expressed meibum components. The organic solvent and residual moisture were evaporated under a stream of nitrogen, yielding a dried extract containing both lipid and nonlipid constituents. The dried material was reconstituted in hexane to selectively dissolve nonpolar lipids, while insoluble components were allowed to precipitate. The hexane-soluble fraction was carefully separated and evaporated again under nitrogen to obtain the lipid fraction, which was subsequently weighed. Final samples were reconstituted in hexane to a concentration of ∼0.2 mg/mL, based exclusively on the mass of the residual hexane-soluble lipid fraction. Reconstituted samples were transferred to glass vials with crimped Teflon-lined caps and stored at −80°C until analysis. To obtain sufficient material, sample collection, particularly for WT mice, was repeated following a recovery period between sessions.

Removal of the MG Ductal Plug

Awat2−/− mice were anesthetized using a ketamine/xylazine cocktail. The mouse eyelids were warmed by applying a custom-made heat device for up to 2 minutes. The temperature of the heater was set to 42°C to 45°C, allowing for partial melting of the meibum lipid plugs obstructing the MG main ducts. The procedure was completed by gently squeezing the mouse's eyelids, repeated up to three times at 2-minute intervals to ensure complete unblocking of the MG ducts. The effectiveness of the procedure was verified by slit-lamp examination.

Determination of the Meibum Sample Melting Temperature

Meibum samples collected from Awat2−/− mice, vehicle-treated control, and ATR101-treated mice were placed on a custom-designed instrument consisting of a thermoelectric plate controlled by an ESP32 module. The temperature of the heating plate was gradually increased from 25°C to 65°C at a rate of 0.25°C per second. Samples were observed using a Leica M205C microscope. The temperature at which the first visible signs of melting appeared was defined as the melting onset, while the temperature corresponding to complete phase transition of the entire meibum sample was defined as the melting endpoint. As measurements were obtained under nonequilibrium heating conditions, the recorded temperatures represent dynamic melting parameters and may deviate from the thermodynamic equilibrium melting temperature due to thermal lag.

Determination of the Effect of SOAT1 Inhibition on Mouse Meibum

The analysis of changes in CEs and fatty acid (FA) composition was conducted through liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (LC/MS) using an LTQ Velos linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) coupled with an Agilent 1100 series HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The lipid analyses and quantifications were performed based on the previously published methodology.12 For quantification of CEs, the LC/MS was equipped with an atmospheric pressure chemical ionization source operating in positive mode with the following analyte-optimized settings: source voltage, 6 kV; capillary temperature, 300°C; vaporizer temperature, 220°C; sheath gas (nitrogen), 50 a.u.; and sweep gas, 0 a.u. Separation of nonpolar lipids occurred on a normal-phase Luna Silica column (250 × 4.6 mm, 10 µm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA) with a linear gradient of ethyl acetate in hexane (1% to 10%, v/v) over 15 minutes, followed by an isocratic flow of 10% (v/v) ethyl acetate in hexane for an additional 15 minutes at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. Quantification of CEs relied on the relationship between ion intensity peak areas of endogenous cholesteryl moiety fragment at m/z = 369.5 [M + H – FA – H2O]+ and an internal standard, deuterated cholesteryl-d7 palmitate (Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL) at m/z = 376.5 [M + H – FA – H2O]+.

For the determination of FA composition in meibum, 1 mL of hexanoic meibum lipid extract was hydrolyzed with 0.2 mL of 2 N NaOH in ethanol. After vortexing, the mixture was incubated at 80°C for 30 minutes, and the reaction was quenched by acidification with 0.2 mL of 2 M HCl. The samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes, the upper hexane layer containing liberated FAs was collected and evaporated under nitrogen, and the residue was reconstituted in a 1:1 (v/v) ethanol/isopropanol solution containing 0.1% formic acid (v/v). FA separation was performed on a Hypersil Gold reverse-phase HPLC column (50 × 2.1 mm; Thermo Scientific) using a linear gradient of the elution solvent composed of acetonitrile/isopropanol (1:1, v/v), starting from 50% (water) to 100% over 10 minutes, followed by an isocratic flow of 100% elution solvent for an additional 5 minutes. Both solvents contained 10 mM ammonium acetate. The column effluent was introduced into an LTQ Velos linear ion trap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with an electrospray ionization source operating in negative-ion mode with the following settings: source voltage, 5 kV; capillary temperature, 350°C; sheath gas (nitrogen), 45 a.u.; and sweep gas, 5 a.u. The instrument's working parameters were optimized using pentacosanoic acid (Cayman Chemical, Ann Arbor, MI). Relative percentages of individual FA chains in the mixture were determined from the ratio of the integrated areas of their corresponding ion intensity peaks to the total integrated ion signal of all detected FA species.

Quantitative PCR-Based Quantification of Marker Genes

Cornea and meibomian glands were isolated from mouse eyes and tarsal plates and submerged in RNAlater or Allprotect Tissue Reagent (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively, before storage at −20°C until use. For RNA isolation, two corneas were pooled per sample. Total RNA from corneas and meibomian glands was extracted using the RNeasy Fibrous Tissue Mini Kit and the RNeasy Lipid Tissue Mini Kit, respectively, according to the manufacturer's instructions (Qiagen). RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific).

Reverse transcription was performed using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (4368814; Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA), with up to 2 µg total RNA in a 20-µL reaction volume. Gene expression was quantified by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) using an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System with TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems) and TaqMan Fast Universal PCR Master Mix (2×), No AmpErase UNG, according to the manufacturer's instructions. TaqMan probes targeting the fatty acid–binding protein 5 (Fabp5; Mm00783731_s1), peroxisome proliferator–activated receptor gamma (Pparg; Mm01184322_m1), aldehyde dehydrogenase family 1 subfamily A3 (Aldh1a3; Mm00474049_m1), retinol-binding protein 1 (Rbp1; Mm00441119_m1), keratin 14 (Krt14; Mm00516876_m1), small proline-rich protein 1A gene (Sprr1a; Mm01962902_s1), and retinoic acid receptor beta gene (Rarb; Mm01319677_m1) were used. Gene expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh; Mm99999915_g1) as an endogenous control using the relative standard curve method.

Immunofluorescence Staining and Imaging

Freshly dissected eyes were embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (Sakura Tissue-Tek O.C.T. Compound 4583, Torrance, CA) in cryomolds and oriented appropriately. The molds were placed on a metal rack suspended over liquid nitrogen in a foam box, covered, and allowed to solidify. Once frozen, the blocks were transferred to dry ice and stored at −80°C until sectioning. Cryosections (5 µm thick) were cut using a cryostat (Leica), mounted onto Superfrost Plus slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific), air-dried, and stored at −20°C until staining.

For immunofluorescence staining, sections were equilibrated to room temperature, air-dried for 30 minutes, and washed twice in PBS for 5 minutes each. Sections were then permeabilized and blocked in a working solution containing 2% goat serum, 1% bovine serum albumin, and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for at least 1 hour at room temperature with gentle agitation (Rocker 25; Labnet International, Edison, NJ). Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted 1:50 in working solution, including antibodies against keratin 12 (AB185627; Abcam, Cambridge, United Kingdom) and keratin 14 (906004, Biolegend, San Diego, CA). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were rinsed and washed three times for 10 minutes each in PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (PBS-T), followed by three additional 10-minute washes in PBS. Sections were then incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies diluted 1:400 in working solution for at least 2 hours at room temperature in the dark. Secondary antibodies included goat anti-chicken (NL637; R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) for keratin 14 and goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Abcam) for keratin 12. After secondary antibody incubation, sections were rinsed and washed three times for 10 minutes each in PBS-T, followed by three washes in PBS.

Nuclear counterstaining was performed using Hoechst (1 µM) for 30 minutes, followed by three 5-minute washes in PBS-T and three additional 5-minute washes in PBS. Sections were then mounted with a coverslip using mounting medium (ProLong Gold Antifade Reagent; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Fluorescent images were acquired using an Olympus FV1200 laser scanning microscope (FV Fluoview, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with 405-nm, 473-nm, and 635-nm laser lines, using UPlan S-Apo 60× oil-immersion (NA 1.35) objectives.

Meibomian Gland Three-Dimensional Virtual Histology, Automated Segmentation, and Quantification

Intact eyelids were harvested and prepared according to a previously published workflow.19 Optical clearing was performed using LIMPID,20 and samples were mounted in sealed magnetic chambers to maintain full submersion during three-dimensional (3D) imaging. Volumetric confocal microscopy datasets were acquired across multiple overlapping tiles spanning the entire eyelid and mosaicked to generate large field-of-view reconstructions. Digital recoloring to histology-like contrast was performed using FalseColor-Python.21 We developed a deep learning network to segment meibomian gland ducts and acini from 3D nuclei and autofluorescence confocal images of intact, fixed, and optically cleared eyelids. This network was trained using digital labeling techniques we previously established, allowing for high-fidelity structural analysis.22 The final network training was performed on seven WT eyelids (from five mice) and three Awat2−/− eyelids (from two mice). The mean Dice score across all cross-validation folds was 0.87. To ensure segmentation accuracy across all experimental groups, including WT, untreated Awat2−/−, and treated Awat2−/− eyelids, all outputs from the deep learning network were manually reviewed. Any segmentation errors were corrected prior to final quantification to maintain data integrity. The maximum duct diameter was defined as the largest distance between the centerline passing through a duct and a point outside of the duct wall, multiplied by 2, and therefore includes the combined thickness of the duct lumen and duct wall. An average of 11 ducts per eyelid was observed and included in our final reporting.

Statistical Analyses

For most figures, results are presented as box-and-whisker plots, where the box represents the interquartile range and the horizontal line inside the box indicates the median. Figure legends specify the number of samples and independent experiments. Statistical significance between two groups was assessed using a two-tailed t-test following the normal distribution check by the Shapiro–Wilk test. The statistical calculations were performed using Excel (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA) or Origin (OriginLab, Northampton, MA) software. The thresholds for statistical significance were set at *P < 0.05, **P < 0.005, and ***P < 0.0005.

Results

Systemic Administration of SOAT1 Inhibitor Alters Meibum Lipid Composition

The primary cause of MG obstruction and associated pathologies in Awat2−/− mice is the overproduction of CEs, which arises in response to a metabolic blockade in WE biosynthesis.12 Therefore, pharmacologic inhibition of CE synthesis represents a rational and testable strategy to modify meibum lipid composition and alleviate the MGD phenotype of Awat2−/− mice. To this end, we selected ATR101 (nevanimibe), a competitive inhibitor of SOAT1,16,17 to target CE biosynthesis in the MGs of Awat2−/− mice. ATR101 (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ) was chosen based on its well-characterized pharmacodynamic properties,16 favorable half-life (∼10 hours), and its high selectivity and affinity for SOAT1 (kd ∼9 nM).23 In addition, ATR101 has demonstrated an acceptable safety profile in clinical trials for Cushing syndrome and congenital adrenal hyperplasia (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT03053271 and NCT03669549). Notably, although these trials reported insufficient efficacy for the primary endpoints, the favorable safety data support the potential for drug repurposing.

To assess the effect of ATR101 on meibum biosynthesis, 2- to 3-month-old Awat2−/− mice were selected. At this stage, MGs are still actively producing lipids, yet already exhibit ductal obstruction. Therefore, experimental time points capture the early, obstructive phase of MGD, characterized by qualitative alterations in meibum composition and impaired secretion from the MG orifices, rather than a reduction in lipid production. Awat2−/− mice were treated daily for 14 days by intraperitoneal injection with either the SOAT1 inhibitor ATR101 (10 mg/kg body weight) or vehicle (DMSO). On the first day of treatment, preexisting obstructions of the MG ducts were manually expressed by applying gentle pressure along the eyelids to expel meibum from the MG orifices, yielding the characteristic white, toothpaste-like lipid discharge (Fig. 2A). Additional meibum samples were collected from the same animals after 14 days of treatment and subjected to LC/MS-based lipidomic analysis. The main purpose of the lipid analysis was to validate the pharmacologic action of ATR101 in the MGs. As shown in Figure 2B, SOAT1 inhibition resulted in a significant reduction in CE levels in the meibum of Awat2−/− mice, reaching concentrations comparable to those observed in WT animals. This result confirms that the biological effects of ATR101 on meibum lipids are consistent with the expected mechanism of action of the drug. Notably, SOAT1 inhibition also indirectly altered the fatty acid (FA) composition of meibum lipids, as evidenced by a decrease in saturated FAs, particularly species longer than 22 carbons, and a concomitant increase in C20 to C24 unsaturated FAs (Fig. 2C). Remarkably, these changes in lipid profile had a pronounced effect on the physical properties of the meibum discharge. Meibum collected from ATR101-treated Awat2−/− mice exhibited increased transparency and enhanced fluidity at ambient temperature compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 2D). Consistent with these observations, ATR101 treatment induced a significant shift in the meibum melting profile, lowering the melting range from 43.0°C to 55.4°C in DMSO-treated mice to 36.7°C to 49.0 °C in ATR101-treated animals (Fig. 2E).

Enhanced Meibum Fluidity Preserves Corneal Integrity in Awat2−/− Mice

A primary effect of increased meibum fluidity is improved delivery and spreading of tear film lipids across the ocular surface, resulting in enhanced tear film stability. Following a 14-day treatment regimen with ATR101, this effect was readily observed by light-reflection imaging of Awat2−/− mice, which revealed a more uniform and stable tear film in ATR101-treated animals compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3A). This macroscopic improvement was accompanied by a significant increase in TFBUT, which was approximately threefold longer in mice treated with the SOAT1 inhibitor than in vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3B). Together, these findings indicate that modulation of meibum lipid composition and physical properties, resulting in enhanced meibum fluidity, substantially improves tear film stability in Awat2−/− mice.

To determine whether this improvement in tear film stability confers protection to the corneal surface, corneal integrity was assessed at the end of the same 14-day treatment period by fluorescein staining and slit-lamp examination. As shown in Figures 3C and 3D, vehicle-treated Awat2−/− mice exhibited progressive corneal epithelial damage, as evidenced by increased fluorescein staining over the treatment period. In contrast, ATR101-treated mice displayed significantly reduced fluorescein uptake, indicating partial protection against corneal surface deterioration. Consistent with these findings, confocal scanning laser microscopy of the cornea revealed improved preservation of epithelial cell morphology in ATR101-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4A). Vehicle-treated Awat2−/− mice exhibited increased reflectivity of superficial epithelial cells, consistent with squamous metaplasia and desquamation of the outermost corneal layers, indicative of epithelial stress or degeneration. In addition, basal epithelial cells in untreated Awat2−/− mice appeared enlarged relative to WT mice, suggesting altered epithelial homeostasis.24–26 Notably, this basal cell enlargement was less pronounced in ATR101-treated mice, indicating partial preservation of basal epithelial architecture and overall corneal epithelial integrity.

Krt12 and Krt14 serve as markers for corneal epithelial differentiation, defining distinct stages of maturation. Krt12 is primarily found in terminally differentiated suprabasal and superficial epithelial cells, whereas Krt14 is typically localized to basal epithelial progenitor cells, which maintain proliferative capacity.27–29 With age, there is a progressive loss of Krt14 from the mid-periphery, becoming progressively confined to the limbus, while all layers in control mice are Krt12-positive, with little or no Krt14 expression in the central cornea (Fig. 4B).27,28,30

Following epithelial stress or injury, as cells proliferate, restratify, and migrate onto the cornea from the limbal periphery, Krt12 expression is significantly downregulated, whereas Krt14 expression is markedly upregulated.31 In Awat2−/− mice, Krt14 is mislocalized to the superficial corneal layers (Fig. 4B), indicating heightened stress and repair activity. In this context, some basal cells lose Krt14 expression while others retain it, suggesting impaired basal cell renewal. In contrast, treated mice exhibit reduced Krt14 mislocalization to the superficial layer, with substantial Krt14 retained in the basal layer, thereby preserving renewal capacity.

Krt12 expression is diminished in the suprabasal layer and colocalizes with Krt14 in the superficial layer of untreated mice. However, in treated mice, Krt12 is retained in the suprabasal layers, with minimal colocalization in the superficial epithelium. These changes in keratin expression and distribution serve as sensitive biomarkers for corneal injury, epithelial remodeling, and repair. The decrease in Krt14 staining observed in SOAT1 inhibitor-treated Awat2−/− mice indicates attenuation of stress-induced epithelial remodeling, including squamous metaplasia and desquamation of the superficial epithelial layers (Fig. 4B).

Transcriptional profiling of corneal tissue using RT-qPCR supported these observations. Analysis of Krt14 mRNA expression revealed significantly elevated levels in untreated Awat2−/− mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 4C), consistent with previous findings that corneal epithelium shows enhanced Krt14 expression after wounding.31 Importantly, SOAT1 inhibition in Awat2−/− mice led to significant downregulation of Krt14, suggesting attenuation of stress-induced epithelial regenerative responses. This reduction likely reflects decreased compensatory epithelial renewal and a shift toward restoration of ocular surface homeostasis, secondary to improved tear film stability.

In addition, expression of the stress-associated epithelial marker Sprr1a was examined. Untreated Awat2−/− mice exhibited a marked increase in Sprr1a expression, exceeding 50-fold relative to WT controls. Sprr1a is a well-established marker of squamous differentiation and cornified envelope formation in the superficial corneal epithelium and is indicative of advanced epithelial remodeling under chronic stress conditions.32,33 Because pharmacologic intervention began at the onset of dry eye symptoms, the 14-day treatment period was likely insufficient to reverse established squamous metaplasia and superficial epithelial desquamation. Accordingly, no significant treatment-associated changes in Sprr1a expression were observed within this time frame.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that partial restoration of tear film function through pharmacologic reduction of CE accumulation is sufficient to mitigate corneal pathology and epithelial remodeling characteristics of evaporative dry eye disease.

Pharmacologic Intervention in Meibogenesis Delays the Onset of MG Central Duct Obstruction

To evaluate the effect of pharmacologic modulation of meibum composition on MG obstruction, 3-month-old, sex-matched Awat2−/− mice from multiple litters were randomly assigned to experimental or control groups. Mice in the experimental group received daily intraperitoneal injections of the SOAT1 inhibitor ATR101 at a dose of 10 mg/kg body weight for 14 consecutive days (Fig. 2A), whereas control animals received vehicle alone. Prior to the first injection, preexisting MG plugs were manually expressed and removed in both groups to establish a consistent baseline. Slit-lamp examinations of the ocular surface, MG orifices, and central ducts were performed every other day to monitor the kinetics of MG re-occlusion. In the control group, MG obstruction became apparent within 48 hours, whereas ATR101-treated mice remained free of detectable obstruction for up to 7 days, after which gradual replugging was observed despite continued treatment (Fig. 5A). Importantly, the severity of emerging MG obstruction in ATR101-treated mice was significantly reduced compared with controls, as evidenced by decreased dilation of the MG central duct, as measured by changes in MG terminal duct thickness (Fig. 5B).

Elimination of Central Duct Obstruction Preserves Meibomian Gland Secretory Function and Prevents Acinar Atrophy

As demonstrated by prior data, restoration of unobstructed meibum secretion is associated with improved corneal health in Awat2−/− mice. However, within the broader framework of MGD pathophysiology, it remains essential to determine whether pharmacologic intervention not only improves lipid delivery to the corneal surface but also preserves intrinsic MG function and prevents glandular degeneration, a hallmark of obstructive MGD. Two contrasting hypotheses have been proposed to explain the etiology of MG degeneration. The ductal-centric hypothesis posits that epithelial hyperkeratinization of the meibomian orifice and ductal epithelium leads to luminal obstruction, resulting in meibum stasis, increased intraductal pressure, cystic ductal dilation, and ultimately secondary acinar atrophy.35 In contrast, the meibocyte-centric hypothesis suggests that intrinsic dysregulation of meibocyte differentiation and renewal impairs lipid synthesis and meibum quality, leading directly to acinar atrophy independent of ductal obstruction.36 These theories differ fundamentally in the proposed sequence of events leading to glandular degeneration.

Pharmacologic modulation of meibum composition and fluidity in Awat2−/− mice provided a unique experimental platform to evaluate these competing models. Despite exhibiting features of obstructive MGD, the MG acini in 2-month-old Awat2−/− mice remained morphologically intact at baseline. To assess whether pharmacologic unblocking of the gland could prevent acinar degeneration, mice were treated with daily injections of ATR101 (10 mg/kg) or vehicle control for 14 days. Preexisting MG plugs were removed before the first injection in both groups. At the conclusion of the experiment, tarsal plates were harvested for histologic and molecular analyses.

As shown previously, persistent obstruction of the MG orifices and excretory ducts in Awat2−/− mice is associated with hyperkeratinization of the MG ductal epithelial layer and progressive degeneration of the acini (Fig. 7A).12 In contrast, histologic examination of MGs from Awat2−/− mice treated with the SOAT1 inhibitor ATR101 revealed substantial preservation of overall glandular architecture, including maintenance of acinar structure. To quantitatively assess this protective effect, serial histologic sections from individual eyelids were collected and analyzed to determine total acinar nuclear counts (Fig. 7B). ATR101-treated mice exhibited a statistically significant increase in acinar nuclei count compared with untreated controls, indicating preservation of acinar cellularity. Furthermore, RT-qPCR analysis of isolated MG tissue from treated and untreated groups demonstrated normalization of the expression of MG functional marker genes toward levels observed in WT control mice (Fig. 7C). Notably, expression of Pparg, a master regulator of meibocyte differentiation from basal progenitor cells, was significantly increased following ATR101 treatment, consistent with improved meibocyte maturation and lipid biosynthesis relative to untreated Awat2−/− mice. Concomitantly, expression of genes involved in retinoic acid signaling (Aldh1a3 and Rarb) was reduced to levels comparable to those in WT mice. A similar normalization trend was observed for Sprr1a and Krt14, indicating diminished activation of pathologic repair and hyperproliferative pathways that drive hyperkeratinization of the MG ductal epithelium.

Together, these findings demonstrate that sustained inhibition of SOAT1 and the resulting diminished lipid accumulation within MG ducts effectively protect against premature acinar degeneration in the Awat2−/− model. These results provide experimental support for a causal role of mechanical obstruction and intraductal lipid pressure in driving MG atrophy, consistent with the ductal-centric model of MGD pathogenesis.

Discussion

In this study, we present evidence supporting the concept that direct pharmacologic modulation of meibogenesis represents a viable strategy to alter the physicochemical properties of meibum and confer therapeutic benefit in EDE. Utilizing the well-characterized Awat2−/− mouse model, we demonstrate that this lipid imbalance leads to increased meibum viscosity, impaired lipid delivery to the tear film, spontaneous MG obstruction, and progressive corneal deterioration.12,37 This model enabled simultaneous evaluation of experimental therapeutic effects on both MG function and ocular surface integrity.

Systemic administration of ATR101, a selective SOAT1 inhibitor, significantly reduced CE accumulation in the meibum, resulting in increased fluidity and decreased opacity of the lipid secretion. Remarkably, this intervention prevented reobstruction of the MG orifice, partially restored tear film stability, and slowed the progression of corneal and acinar degeneration over the treatment period. These findings suggest that improving the fluidity of meibum, even without restoring its native composition, can provide measurable therapeutic outcomes in EDE. The efficacy of this approach is notable, given its departure from traditional strategies that aim to correct the underlying metabolic deficiency. Instead of restoring WE biosynthesis, our approach leverages pharmacologic inhibition of CE production to shift the lipid profile toward a more fluid state, thereby facilitating meibum outflow. While the resulting meibum composition deviates from the physiological norm, the reduced melting temperature and improved rheological properties appear to be the critical determinants of therapeutic benefit.

Importantly, this pharmacologic strategy also enabled the experimental evaluation of mechanistic hypotheses underlying MG degeneration. Our data indicate that relief of ductal obstruction through enhanced meibum fluidity is sufficient to preserve acinar morphology and function, supporting the ductal-centric model of MGD pathogenesis. Thus, small-molecule–based therapeutic strategies targeting meibum composition might serve not only as a symptomatic treatment but also as a disease-modifying intervention capable of preventing irreversible gland dropout.

Given the multifactorial nature of MGD and interindividual variability in meibum lipid composition, the choice of therapeutic targets will likely require personalization. Elevated CE content has been documented in the meibum of patients with MGD, with levels increasing with age and correlating with changes in meibum opacity and phase-transition temperature.38,39 Research by Borchman et al.40 indicated that the phase-transition temperature of meibum is higher in patients with MGD and is linked to changes in CE and WE FA composition rather than diminished major meibum lipid species. However, some studies have reported reduced or unchanged CE levels in certain MGD cohorts,41–43 underscoring the heterogeneity of the disease. These discrepancies highlight the importance of individualized lipid profiling for the development of targeted therapies.

Advances in cost-effective lipidomics and the expanding repertoire of pharmacologic agents targeting lipid biosynthetic enzymes offer a promising path toward precision medicine in EDE. Moreover, the availability of diverse animal models, including Awat2−/− mice and others recapitulating distinct aspects of MGD pathophysiology, provides critical tools for preclinical testing of meibogenesis-targeted therapies.11,44,45

Despite the high prevalence of EDE, therapeutic options remain limited. Current management strategies include conservative measures such as warm compresses to facilitate meibum expression, topical erythromycin ointment to reduce bacterial load at the lid margin, and oral doxycycline to suppress inflammation and modulate matrix metalloproteinase activity. Medications approved by the US Food and Drug Administration include cyclosporine A (Restasis) and lifitegrast (Xiidra), which modulate T-cell–mediated inflammation,46,47 and perfluorohexyloctane (Miebo), which stabilizes the air–liquid interface of the tear film.48 However, these approaches largely address secondary inflammation or tear film instability and do not address the underlying pathophysiological drivers of MGD or prevent glandular atrophy.

The failure to develop more effective treatments reflects both the biological complexity of MGD and the lack of mechanistic insight into how altered meibum composition drives orifice blockage and ductal obstruction, leading to cystic ductal dilation and compensatory acinar hypertrophy as the gland attempts to maintain lipid output. This knowledge gap not only impedes rational drug development but also hinders biomarker discovery and patient stratification for clinical trials. One of the limitations of this study is that the Awat2−/− mouse model does not fully recapitulate the multifactorial pathophysiology of human MGD. Instead, it represents a reductionist system in which WE biosynthesis is selectively disrupted, leading to a defined and reproducible alteration in meibum composition. This feature provides an advantage for isolating the contribution of lipid composition to tear film stability and meibomian gland function; however, it excludes other critical disease components. In particular, the model does not capture age-related glandular remodeling, or hormonal regulation that contribute to human MGD. Therefore, while the findings provide mechanistic insight into how altered lipid composition can drive functional changes in the ocular surface system, they should be interpreted within the context of this specific and limited aspect of MGD biology. Nevertheless, our findings support targeting meibogenesis as a novel, mechanism-based strategy for the treatment of obstructive MGD. By focusing on the biochemical pathways governing meibum composition and secretion, this approach holds promise for the development of next-generation therapeutics aimed at the root cause of EDE.
