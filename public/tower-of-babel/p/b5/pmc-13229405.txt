Xylem anatomy and pit architecture but not perforation-plate types shape hydraulic strategies against hypersaline and drought stress in eight woody mangroves
Authors: Yang Wei, Xin Jiang, Brendan Choat, Amy Ny Aina Aritsara, Kunfang Cao, Yajun Chen, Anna Lintunen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMangrove species mainly inhabit tropical and subtropical intertidal zones, where they suffer periodic tidal inundation. They display diverse perforation-plate types and pit structures; however, the extent to which these anatomical features contribute to their adaptation to coastal environments remains unclear. In this study, we quantified 27 traits, representing hydraulics, anatomy and pit characteristics, in eight woody mangrove species from Hainan, Southern China, including four simple-perforation (SI) and four scalariform-perforation (SC) plate species. All species exhibited high xylem embolism resistance (P50: −4.39 to −6.65 MPa) but relatively low hydraulic efficiency (Ks-max: 0.65 to 1.87 kg m−1 s−1 MPa−1). We found an overall trade-off between hydraulic safety and efficiency; species with greater embolism resistance had narrower conduits, thicker vessel walls and higher fractions of axial parenchyma. These species also exhibited more negative minimum water potentials (Ψmin: −2.90 to −3.98 MPa) and wider hydraulic safety margins (1.56 to 3.01 MPa) than species with lower embolism resistance. Although no significant differences were observed in hydraulic function or most xylem anatomy between SI and SC species, likely due to substantial within-group variations, pit architecture differed markedly. The SI species had smaller, more densely packed pits compared with SC species. Additionally, variations in pit aperture size and shape well explained interspecific differences in hydraulic safety but not hydraulic efficiency. Our findings highlight that enhanced hydraulic safety, rather than efficiency, underpins mangrove adaptation to hypersaline environment through reinforcement both anatomical and pit architecture. While perforation plate morphology influences pit architecture, it does not appear to affect hydraulic function directly. Further research, including a broader taxonomic range, is necessary to clarify how variations in perforation-plate types modulate hydraulic performance.Keywords: hydraulic safety, intertidal zone, parenchyma cell, salt tolerance, water transport

Introduction

Mangrove forests are critical components of tropical and subtropical coastal ecosystems, providing multiple valuable ecosystem services and functions, including global ‘blue carbon’ stocks, coastal protection and economic benefits (Kelleway et al. 2015, Rahman et al. 2021, Song et al. 2023, Lovelock et al. 2024, J.F.Zhang et al. 2024). These forests are distributed across intertidal zones along marine–terrestrial interfaces, where they suffer unique environmental challenges such as hypersaline conditions, high evaporative demand and periodic tidal inundation (Sippo et al. 2018). Despite their remarkable evolutionary adaptations to such harsh environments, however, increasing large-scale dieback events indicate a potential risk to mangrove communities posed by increasing drought frequencies and intensities. For example, the combination of hyper-salinity stress and extreme drought during the 2015–16 El Niño event caused catastrophic mortality in Australian mangrove populations (Duke et al. 2017, Lovelock et al. 2017, Gauthey et al. 2022). Consequently, mangrove forests are regarded as one of the most vulnerable ecosystems, and highly susceptible to climatic extremes (Laurance et al. 2011, Lovelock et al. 2015, Servino et al. 2018, Fazlioglu and Chen 2020).

Mangroves have evolved a suite of adaptations to mitigate water loss and tolerate salinity-induced osmotic stress. These include root-level salt exclusion, via well-developed Casparian strips and aquaporins (Parida and Jha 2010, Reef and Lovelock 2015), foliar salt secretion through specialized glands (Guo et al. 2023, Madhavan et al. 2024), efficient foliar water uptake (Bryant et al. 2021, Losso et al. 2024) and enhanced xylem embolism resistance via optimized anatomical structures (Robert et al. 2009, Jiang et al. 2021). To extract water from saline substrates, mangroves maintain xylem water potentials that are lower than those of the soil (Melcher et al. 2001, Jiang et al. 2021, Losso et al. 2024). For instance, the mangrove species Avicennia marina can sustain predawn water potential below −8.2 MPa, while its high xylem embolism resistance (P50 = −9.6 MPa) allows maintenance of hydraulic function even under extreme drought and salinity stress (Gauthey et al. 2022). However, how mangroves optimize xylem structural traits to withstand combined salinity and climate stressors remains unclear.

Global datasets reveal a functional trade-off between hydraulic safety and efficiency across angiosperms and gymnosperms (Gleason et al. 2016), but this fundamental relationship is poorly tested in woody mangroves. Xylem anatomical changes constitute a simple but effective hydraulic architecture, with reduced vessel diameters enhancing embolism resistance at the expense of conductive efficiency (Verheyden et al. 2005, Robert et al. 2009, Hacke et al. 2016, Jiang et al. 2017). In contrast, wider conduits usually improve hydraulic efficiency but increase vulnerability to embolism (Tyree and Zimmermann 2002, Lens et al. 2022). Xylem parenchyma cells also contribute by storing water and nonstructural carbohydrate (NSC) and potentially facilitating embolism repair through osmotic adjustments (Morris et al. 2016). Moreover, xylem ultrastructure such as perforation plate and intervessel pit morphology are tightly linked to their hydraulic efficiency and drought tolerance (Sperry et al. 2007, Lens et al. 2013, Hacke et al. 2016). In angiosperms, scalariform (SC)- and simple-perforation (SI) plates are two common vessel element junctions(Christman and Sperry 2010). The perforation plate at cell–cell connection and porosity of pit membranes constitutes the resistance of water transport along longitudinal and transverse axis (Ellerby and Ennos 1998). Previous study showed that species with SC plates have been reported to exhibit 340% higher vessel-level resistivity compared with species with SI plates (Christman and Sperry 2010). As a result, SI species exhibits fewer end bars and greater hydraulic efficiency compared with that with SC species (Baas and Baas 1976, Olson 2014, Medeiros et al. 2018), reflecting evolutionary shifts from cold-adapted scalariform to warm-adapted SI morphologies (Lens et al. 2016). Among mangroves, this divergence in perforation plates morphologies suggests a strong selection pressures for hydraulic safety in hypersaline habitats (Carlquist 1975, Christman and Sperry 2010). Intervessel pit membranes, which account for ~50% of total xylem flow resistivity in angiosperms (Wheeler et al. 2005, Choat et al. 2006), vary in thickness, porosity, pit-field fraction (FPF) and aperture size; all these together regulate hydraulic conductivity and embolism resistance, and mediate the hydraulic safety-efficiency trade-off (Choat et al. 2003, 2007, Wheeler et al. 2005, Jansen et al. 2009, Brodersen et al. 2014, Thonglim et al. 2021). Larger pit aperture size and higher FPF generally enhance conductivity (Lens et al. 2011, Jacobsen et al. 2016), while specialized structures such as vestured pits may reduce cavitation risks (Jansen et al. 2003, Choat et al. 2004, Jansen et al. 2004). Despite few anatomical studies in mangroves (Sobrado 2006, Schmitz et al. 2007, De Deurwaerder et al. 2016, Chowdhury et al. 2025), the integrated effects of these ultrastructural and anatomical adaptations on the hydraulic safety–efficiency continuum remain unresolved, representing a critical knowledge gap in our understanding of mangrove ecophysiology under climate change.

In this study, we investigated xylem hydraulic efficiency and safety, along with xylem anatomical features and intervessel pit characteristics, in eight mangrove species from two tropical mangrove forests. We specifically addressed the following questions:

(i) To what extent do the eight mangrove species differ in hydraulic properties, xylem anatomy and intervessel pit characteristics? We hypothesized that the mangroves would exhibit a trade-off relationship between hydraulic safety and efficiency, with species displaying greater hydraulic safety exhibiting lower efficiency, and vice versa.

Materials and methods

Study site and plant materials

This study was conducted in two protected mangrove ecosystems along the coast of Hainan Island, China, which contain the largest mangrove forests and highest mangrove diversity of mangrove species in the region. One site is situated in the Dongzhaigang Harbor National Nature Reserve (DZG, 19°57′17″N, 110°35′14″E), while the second site is located in the Qinglangang Harbor Provincial Nature Reserve (QLG, 19°37′35″N, 110°50′2″E). The two sites are ~44 km apart and share a tropical maritime monsoon climate. The annual mean temperatures in the DZG and QLG are 23.8 °C and 24.1 °C, respectively. Annual mean precipitation averages 1700 mm in the DZG and 1745.9 mm in the QLG, with around 80% of rainfall occurring during the wet season (from May to October), exhibiting distinct seasonality (Leng and Cao 2020).

Among the woody true mangroves with high important value, we selected eight common mangrove species from two primary perforation plates morphology: four with SI plate and four with SC plate (see detailed species information in Table 1). Due to the few woody true mangroves in two study sites, which limit the species selection and phylogenetic analysis although we try to contain more families. Branch samples for hydraulic measurements were collected from at least five individuals in the early morning before sunrise (4:00 to 6:00 a.m.) during September to December of 2018. To avoid open vessels and ensure measurement accuracy, branches ~1.5 to 2 m in length were cut from canopy, immediately sprayed with water and enclosed in black plastic bags with wetting towel inside to prevent desiccation. All samples were transported to the laboratory within 30 min.

Hydraulic measurements

We first determined the maximal vessel length (MVL) for each species prior to the other hydraulic measurements using an air-injection technique (Zimmermann and Jeje 1981). Branches (n = 5), ~1.5 m long, were cut and injected with 100 kPa compressed air gas from the distal ends, while the basal end was submerged in water. The branch was sequentially cut at a 1-cm interval from the basal end until a continuous stream of tiny bubbles appeared. The MVL was defined as the remaining branch length plus 1 cm.

Stem maximum sapwood-specific hydraulic conductivity (Ks-max, kg m−1 s−1 MPa−1) was determined using the steady-state flowmeter method (Sperry et al. 1988). A total of six branch samples per species were used for measurements. Branch samples were cut-off subbranches and trimmed to ~10% longer than the MVL. The remaining branchless segments were flushed using degassed water with 2 mM KCl and 20 mM NaCl solution (ultra-filtered at 0.2 μm) at 0.2 MPa over 1 h to remove embolism (Stuart et al. 2007). Flushed segments were connected to a pressure head (~3 kPa) with the same solution to measure a flushed flow rate using a liquid flow meter (Liqui-Flow L13; Bronkhorst, Ruurlo, the Netherlands). After Ks-max measurement, a 10-cm segment from the middle of each sample was collected, which was used for anatomy analysis and to determine the xylem wood density (WD, g cm−3).

The stem vulnerability curves (VCs) were determined using a bench dehydration method (Sperry et al. 1988). Branch samples (~1.5 × MVL) were harvested and transported to the laboratory for the further treatment. In the laboratory, all branch samples were cut-off 20 cm from the basal end and rehydrated over 12 h to ensure full rehydration. Branches were wrapped in vapor-saturated black plastic bags to suppress transpiration, then were air dried naturally over different time periods to achieve a wide range of xylem tensions. During the drying process, water potentials were periodically determined. Once a targeted water potential was reached, branches were bagged in vapor-saturated plastic bag again for ~ 2 h to allow xylem pressure to equilibrate across the branch. Two leaves from different positions of the branch were bagged with foil covered bags, the initial xylem water potential (Ψi) was assessed as the average of the two selected leaves. Branch samples remained sealed inside a plastic bag and the basal ends were recut underwater by 15 cm before immersion in deionized water to release xylem tension until water potential increased above −0.3 MPa (typically 1–3 h, depending on initial dehydration status) (Wheeler et al. 2013). For each species, a total of 20–30 PLC values were used to construct VCs. A Weibull model (Duursma and Choat 2017) was used to fitted the curves. Due to potential high native xylem tension and frequent drought stress, some VCs did not exhibit a typical s-shape. Following this, the xylem water potential at 12%, 50% and 88% loss of hydraulic conductivity (P12, P50 and P88) were generated from the fitted curves.

Xylem anatomy

Five 5-cm-long segments from five individuals were cut from the segment used for Ks measurements, and all segments were preserved in formalin/acetic acid/alcohol solution and transported to laboratory. Slices of 10 μm thickness were sectioned using a sliding microtome (SM2010R; Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany). To prevent wood deformation, a mixed solution of ethyl acetate and polystyrene was applied to the cross section prior to the sectioning. Those slices were then stained with Safranin and Alcian blue solutions. In total, 15 images at 50× magnification and 30 images at 400× magnification of per cross-section were captured using a light microscope (DM3000LED; Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Every species was manually counted from over 30 images, and the fractions of different xylem tissues, such as total vessel lumen area fraction (VAF), axial/ray parenchyma fraction (APF/RPF) and fiber fraction (FF), were calculated as their relative areas within each image (Figure S2 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). Vessel density (VD), individual vessel lumen diameter (Di), thickness of individual vessel wall (Tvw) and vessel circularity ratio (VCR) were manually counted from over 100 individual conduits using ImageJ software (Fiji-v1.52p, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Pit characteristics measurements

Approximately 1-cm long samples were obtained from the middle part of the segments after Ks-max measurements. Segments were placed in 70% ethanol and transported to the lab and dehydrated in an ascending series of ethanol solutions. After being air-dried, samples were fixed on aluminum sample holders with carbon adhesive tape, and sputter-coated with gold layer using a sputter coater (Model 108, Cressington, Watford, UK). Images were captured using a scanning electron microscope (SEM S-3400 N; Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at different accelerating voltage (5 kV, 10 kV). For per species, images that contain at least 100 individual pits from 20 fields were analyzed with ImageJ software (Fiji-v1.52p, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) to determine the following pit characteristics (see Table 2 for detailed definitions): pit membrane area (SPM), outer pit aperture area (SPA), diameter of the outer pit aperture along the longer and the shorter axis (DPAL and DPAS, respectively), ratio of long/short axis of outer pit aperture (RPA), pit-field fraction (FPF), outer pit aperture fraction (FOPA), number of outer pits per 100 μm in length (NOP) and outer pit frequency (OPF) (see Figure S1). Minimum xylem water potential and hydraulic safety margin

To evaluate the minimum stem water potential during the dry season, water potential measurements were conducted in March 2020, the driest period during the dry season. Five healthy, mature individuals of each species were selected, sun-exposed canopy twigs from each tree were tagged, wrapped with aluminum foil to avoid transpiration in the evening time prior to experiment day. The predawn (Ψpd) and midday (Ψmd) stem water potential measurements were performed at 5:00–6:00 a.m., and 1:00–2:00 p.m., respectively. Targeted sun-exposed terminal twigs were cut from the canopy and placed into a sealed plastic bag with moist paper towel to avoid water loss, then measured with a pressure chamber (Model 1505D; PMS Instrument, Albany, OR, USA) within 30 min. Statistical analysis

Stem VCs were fitted with a Weibull model using the fitplc package (Duursma and Choat 2017). We used a one-way ANOVA to compare the differences of traits among different species, and an independent t-test was used to compare the differences of traits between mangrove species with simple (SI) and scalariform (SC) perforation-plate. A principal component analysis (PCA) based on 16 representative hydraulic and anatomical traits was applied to test the coordination among traits in the eight mangrove species. Pearson correlation analysis was used to describe the relationships among all 27 hydraulic and structure traits. To contextualize hydraulic properties of mangrove species with global data for woody plants, we compiled data on Ks-max and P50 for woody plants (including 347 angiosperm and 84 gymnosperm species) sourced from published literature (Gleason et al. 2016). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.3, R Core Team).

Results

Trait variations of hydraulic properties, wood anatomy and pit architecture

The eight mangrove species exhibited relatively low variation in xylem embolism resistance (P50; CV = 16.30%), ranging from −4.39 MPa (B. sexangular) to −6.65 MPa (K. obovate) (Figure 1; Table S1 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). Minimum stem water potential during the dry season (Ψmin) ranged from −2.80 MPa (B. sexangula) to −3.98 MPa (C. tagal), with three species exhibiting a relatively narrow hydraulic safety margin (< 2 MPa) when using a commonly used hydraulic safety margins (HSM50). Even using a more conservative HSM88, one SC species showed value below 2 MPa, indicating potential hydraulic vulnerability. In contrast, hydraulic efficiency (Ks-max) varied substantially (CV = 40.9%), from 0.65 kg m−1 s−1 MPa−1 (K. obovate) to 1.87 kg m−1 s−1 MPa−1 (L. racemosa). These species also showed comparable, and relative high wood density (WD), ranging from 0.55 ± 0.01 g cm−3 to 0.70 ± 0.01 g cm−3 (CV = 8.14%).

Most xylem anatomical and pit structural traits varied considerably, except vessel circularity ratio (VCR) (Table S1 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). The SI species A. corniculatum had remarkable smaller mean hydraulic-weighted vessel diameter (Dh) and RPF, but higher vessel density (VD) values compared with others. Pit architecture differed across the species, with CV ranging from 23.38% to 92.76% (Table S1 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online).

When separated by perforation-plate types, SI and SC species were generally comparable in most hydraulic and anatomical properties, e.g., P50/88, Ks-max, Ψmin, HSM50/88, WD, Dh, VD, VWR, VCR, VAF, APF and FF, due to great within-group variation, except for MVL and RPF (Table 2; Figure S3 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). The SC species had significantly shorter MVL, but higher RPF compared with SI species. In contrast, SI and SC species differed significantly in most pit characteristics (Table 2). Specifically, SC species exhibited larger pit membrane area (SPM), outer pit aperture area (SPA), and wider hydraulic diameter of outer pit aperture (DPAh), but lower pit-field fraction (FPF), number of outer pits per 100 μm in length (NOP) and OPF than SI species (Figure S4 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). No differences were observed in ratio of long/short axis of outer pit aperture (RPA) and outer pit aperture fraction (FOPA) between two perforation-plate types.

Trait coordination between hydraulic performance, xylem anatomy and pit structure

A strong positive correlation was observed between P50 and Ks-max (R2 = 0.86, P < 0.001; Figure 2a). P50 correlated positively with Ψmin (R2 = 0.66, P = 0.014; Figure 2c) and negatively with HSM50 (R2 = 0.85, P = 0.001; Figure 2d). Surprisingly, embolism resistance increased with a decrease in Dh (P88: R2 = 0.88, P < 0.001; Figure 3a) and vessel circularity ratio (VCR) (P50: R2 = 0.85, P = 0.001; Figure 3c), while HSM50 was positively associated with vessel wall reinforcement (VWR: R2 = 0.50, P = 0.049; Figure 3b). Both P50 and Ks-max correlated negatively with APF (Figures 3d, Figure S5 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). No significant relationship was observed between P50 and pit membrane area (SPM: R2 = 0.01, P = 0.80; Figure 3e), while the relationship between P12 and FPF was marginally significant (R2 = 0.47, P = 0.061; Figure 3f). The hydraulic safety margin (HSM88) correlated negatively with the hydraulic diameter of outer pit aperture (DPAh: R2 = 0.52, P = 0.044; Figure 3g) and positively correlated to ratio of long/short axis of outer pit aperture (RPA: R2 = 0.51, P = 0.045; Figure 3h).

Discussion

Enhanced hydraulic safety over efficiency underpins mangrove adaptation to hypersaline environment

Our findings revealed that mangrove xylem evolution has converged on an exceptional hydraulic strategy characterized by markedly low water transport efficiency (Ks-max) coupled with high embolism resistance (P50) relative to a global dataset of woody species (Figure 2b). This enhanced hydraulic safety over efficiency implies that mangroves likely represent an adaptive response to the double selective pressures of chronic hyper-saline inundation and high evaporative demand in intertidal habitats. Such conservative hydraulic strategies (high resistance but low efficiency) in mangrove species is similar to xeromorphic adaptations in arid-zone flora, suggesting evolutionary convergence of plant hydraulic strategies across water-stressed ecosystems (Lens et al. 2016).

The observed trade-off between hydraulic safety and efficiency across the eight woody mangrove species aligns with theoretical predictions of hydraulic architecture evolution under persistent environmental stress. Mangrove species appear to optimize their hydraulic transport systems by increasing xylem embolism resistance at the expense of reducing hydraulic efficiency, in response to the combined pressures of hyper-salinity and drought. Their exceptionally high xylem embolism resistance, exceeding 78.36% of angiosperms and gymnosperms (Figure 2b), may reflect structural reinforcement mechanisms to withstand frequent tension-induced embolism caused by tidal fluctuations and hypersaline conditions. This long-term xylem tension might lead to a high native xylem embolism, which resulted that some VCs did not display a typical s-shape. On the other hand, their relatively low hydraulic efficiency is likely attributed to the anatomical constraints, such as narrow vessels and high parenchyma allocation, which enhance collapse resistance but limit axial water transport (Kiorapostolou et al. 2019, Lens et al. 2022). Notably, species with more negative P50 tended to exhibit lower minimum stem water potential during the dry season (Ψmin) and maintained wider HSM50 (Choat et al. 2012). This safety-enhancing evolutionary strategy in mangrove species under saline marine environments contrasts with the efficiency-optimized strategies observed in mesic forest trees which prioritize rapid growth during wet seasons (Liu et al. 2020). These findings thus highlight how habitat filtering shapes fundamental trade-off in vascular design among woody mangrove species.

Perforation plate morphology affects intervessel pit characteristics but not hydraulic functions

The transition in perforation plate morphology accompanied the changes in pit properties in woody mangroves. We found a significant difference in pit traits between SI and SC mangrove species, suggesting possible evolutionary implications of perforation plate types for xylem hydraulic function. Specifically, pit membrane area was negatively correlated with number of outer pits per 100 μm in length (NOP) or OPF (Figure S5 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online), indicating a potential trade-off between mechanical reinforcement and water conduction via modifications of pit wall area (Sano et al. 2011). Interestingly, we found that SI mangrove species had longer MVL and higher hydraulic conductivity potential (less resistance of water movement) as many studies show that longer vessels increase conducting capacity but also suffer greater cavitation risk (Comstock and Sperry 2000). However, SI mangrove species also have a smaller but more densely packed pits (Figure S4 available as Supplementary Data at Tree Physiology Online). According to relevant studies, smaller pit size and higher pit density can reduce the probability of air-seeding and enhance hydraulic safety, which just offsets the cavitation risk brought by longer vessels and lower perforation plate resistance (Carlquist 2001, Tyree and Zimmermann 2002). Consistent with Carlquist’s ‘evolutionary hypothesis’, the transitions from SC to SI plates likely enhances water transport efficiency at vessel level (Carlquist 1966, 2012). However, lower perforation plate resistance and higher pit-field fraction (FPF) in SI mangrove species reduced the water-flow resistance, with the result that SI mangrove species were assumed to be potentially more vulnerable to cavitation. This compensating structural trade-offs may reduce the probability of gas bubble propagation and counterbalance the cavitation risk during long-distance water transport, hence the expected divergence in hydraulic traits between SI and SC species was not clearly observed in mangroves. Moreover, mangroves may have evolved a ‘hydraulic safety priority’ adaptive strategy through the optimization of xylem structure (including pit architecture). This environmental-driven adaptive convergence weakens the potential hydraulic differences caused by inherent structural differences between different perforation plate groups (Choat et al. 2007, Christman and Sperry 2010). Another possible interpretation might be attributed to the limited number of mangrove species examined and considerable within-group variation of pit traits and hydraulic traits, which also weakens the overall divergence between groups. Together, these findings highlight that xylem hydraulic resistance in mangroves should be reconsidered from an integrated 3D perspective.

Across the eight mangrove species studies, hydraulic safety was closely linked to intervessel pit characteristics. Although FPF was not significantly correlated with P50, its marginal relationship with P12 suggests that higher FPF may increase the risk of embolism occurrence or propagation between adjacent vessels, which partly support the ‘pit area hypothesis’ (Wheeler et al. 2005). Notably, although L. racemosa possesses an obvious vestured pit, it exhibited a lower embolism resistance (P50 = −4.46 MPa) than the remaining three-quarters of studied mangroves. Indeed, vestures represent a hydraulic-safety mechanism that contribute to embolism repair by affecting the contact angle of the convex air-water interface within inter-vessel pits (Jansen et al. 2003); however, wider conduit diameter and larger pit membrane area in L. racemosa may also increase the risk of cavitation occurring, thus it did not display a relatively high hydraulic safety. Previous studies have shown that embolism resistance is strongly influenced by pit aperture shape, with narrower and more elliptical pit apertures conferring greater resistance (Lens et al. 2011, Scholz et al. 2013). In our study, embolism resistance showed no significant correlations with either pit membrane area (SPM) or outer pit aperture area (SPA); however, HSM88 was negatively related to hydraulic diameter of outer pit aperture (DPAh) and was positively related to ratio of long/short axis of outer pit aperture (RPA). These patterns indicate that smaller pit aperture size and more elliptical pit aperture shape may limit the propagation of air-seeding and thereby reduce mortality risk in mangrove species (Liu et al. 2023). Therefore, it would be necessary to estimate the hydraulic features of mangroves from multiple anatomical dimensions, including pit membrane thickness or porosity, which were thought to be important determinant for embolism resistance.

Structural determinants of hydraulic performance in mangrove species

In this study, embolism resistance (lower P50 and P88) and HSM50 of mangroves were associated with vessel properties (Figure 3), supporting the ‘vessel diameter hypothesis’ (Hacke et al. 2006, Sperry et al. 2006). Positive correlations between the mean hydraulic-weighted vessel diameter (Dh) and P88 suggest that larger conduits are more vulnerable to tension-induced implosion, thereby increasing the risk of irrevocable drought damage in mangroves. This finding is consistent with an earlier study which also showed that increasing Dh tightly correlated to higher conductive capacity and lower habitat salinity across three mangroves (Chowdhury et al. 2025). We also observed that conduits with narrower lumen and thicker walls better enhanced the strength of conduit and resisted mechanical loads under negative xylem tension, reflecting a carbon investment on vessel structure to reduce the mortality risk under extreme saline and drought stress, that is, higher carbon investment in vessel walls as an adaptive strategy to mitigate the risk of hydraulic failure under extreme hypersaline environments (Echeverría et al. 2022). Variation in embolism resistance was predominantly controlled by pit-level traits which are in turn correlated with vessel structures. This pattern may be indicative of differences in pit membrane characteristics, given that vessel wall thickness and pit membrane thickness are often correlated (Hillabrand et al. 2016). Interestingly, we found that embolism resistance was also associated with vessel circularity ratio (VCR), suggesting that more circular conduits provide greater mechanical reinforcement under high xylem tension. Nevertheless, the effect of conduit shape and pit geometry on hydraulic performance remain underexplored and need further investigation.

The amount of parenchyma cells was also associated with variation in hydraulic safety and efficiency across the eight mangroves. It has long been recognized that APF enhances hydraulic efficiency, while RPF contributes primarily to hydraulic safety (Janssen et al. 2020, Aritsara et al. 2021). In our study, however, APF was negatively correlated with both P50 and Ks-max, suggesting that a higher fraction of axial parenchyma cells may increase embolism resistance but reduce hydraulic conductivity. This could be explained by the fact that greater parenchyma abundance provides additional energy reserves for embolism repair, such as NSCs (Kiorapostolou et al. 2019, X.J.Zhang et al. 2024). Overall, these results highlight the important role of living cells in shaping the trade-off between embolism resistance and transport efficiency in woody mangroves.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that mangrove species prioritize hydraulic safety over efficiency, exhibiting high embolism resistance but low xylem transport efficiency. Enhanced hydraulic safety is associated with narrower vessels, thicker conduit walls and higher axial parenchyma fractions, as well as adjustment in pit traits, allowing these species to withstand lower minimum daily water potentials and maintain wider hydraulic safety margins. Although the structure of vessel perforation plate varied with pit traits, it was not linked with hydraulic efficiency or safety. These findings highlight the key linkages between hydraulic functions and xylem anatomy and pit structures underlying mangrove adaptation to hypersaline intertidal environments.
