International Practice Variation in Post‐Tonsillectomy Hemorrhage: A Survey Study of Pediatric Otolaryngologists
Authors: Gina M. Spencer, Claire A. Wilson, Jacob Davidson, Julie E. Strychowsky, Claire M. Lawlor, Hannah Burns, Eishaan K. Bhargava, James Fowler, M. Elise Graham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundA significant complication of pediatric tonsillectomy is post‐tonsillectomy bleeding (PTB). Management within and outside of the operating room (OR) is not standardized. We consolidated international similarities and differences in PTB management.MethodsThis cross‐sectional survey study was conducted from April 4 to May 16, 2024. It involved a 55‐item questionnaire distributed online to a sample of pediatric otolaryngologists from 22 countries, and is a part of larger study on tonsillectomy. The study achieved a 38.2% response rate (112 of 293). Eligibility included proficiency in English and currently practicing pediatric otolaryngology.ResultsA tonsil bleed was defined by 49.2% as any bleeding presenting to the hospital. In all, 58.3% signified taking patients for operative management in the presence of active bleeding. Operative criteria included persistent bleeding on presentation, repeated bleeding while admitted, severity, signs/symptoms of anemia or hemodynamic instability, or age less than 3 and non‐cooperative. When treating PTB within the OR, bipolar cautery is used by 60.6%. In all, 57.3% admit all patients experiencing a PTB for 23‐h observation versus 21.2% who admit a subset of patients for 23‐h observation. In all, 47.7% indicated that they have a formal monitoring program for bleeding rates in their institution/practice. Readmission rates are monitored by 60.7% of respondents.ConclusionThere are no standardized best practices for PTB. Our study confirmed moderate numbers of formal monitoring programs, and varying clinical decision rules when defining a tonsil bleed, readmitting a PTB, and management within or outside of the OR. Understanding global variation may facilitate development of future clinical practice guidelines.

Introduction

Tonsillectomy is one of the most common surgical procedures in the pediatric population [1], serving as a key treatment of recurrent throat infections and sleep disordered breathing (SDB) [2, 3]. However, there are significant risks inherent to this procedure, and practice variation in the management of complications. The most serious and potentially life‐threatening complication of pediatric tonsillectomy is post‐tonsillectomy bleeding (PTB) [1, 4]. Albeit rare, PTB may occur at any point most commonly within the first 2 weeks postoperatively. When occurring within the first 24 h after surgery, a bleed is deemed a primary hemorrhage and considered to be a direct consequence of surgery [5]. Secondary bleeding is defined as occurring more than 24 h after surgery, most often between 5 and 10 days, usually attributed to sloughing of the primary eschar as the tonsil bed heals by secondary intention [2]. It has been found rarely to occur upwards of 21 days after surgery [6]. A systematic review of 87 comparative studies found that PTB occurred in 4.2% of children who underwent extracapsular tonsillectomy (ECT) [7]. However, the incidence dropped to 1.5% when focusing on intracapsular tonsillectomy (ICT) [7].

Mortality rates from tonsillectomy complications vary significantly worldwide. In the United States, the mortality rate is 1 per 2360 in inpatient settings and 1 per 18,000 in ambulatory settings [8, 9], compared to 1 per 56,000 in Ontario, Canada, for example, in all settings [2]. Roughly one‐third of these deaths are attributable to PTB [2, 10].

The choice of whether to proceed with operative management of PTB is based on interpretation of both physical examination findings as well as experience of the physician [4]. American national guidelines recommend formally monitoring PTB rates at least annually [2], but its management within and outside of the operating room (OR) is not standardized. There is a wide variability in the definition and reporting of PTB, from observations made at home by the caregiver, to severe bleeds managed in the OR, necessitating blood transfusion and cautery under general anesthesia [2]. This heterogeneity makes it challenging to compare data accurately and establish comprehensive guidelines.

Bringing together international recommendations and practices for managing PTB is crucial for advancing care. Due to the variability in patient characteristics and surgical techniques, gaining insight into the range of PTB management strategies across multiple centers holds significant potential to inform pediatric treatment approaches. The objective of this survey study was to explore global commonalities and variances in how pediatric otolaryngologists view and treat PTB. Beyond highlighting areas for future investigation, our study can aid in informing future guidelines and improving outcomes for both children and their caregivers [11].

Methods

This study is a second part of a larger survey study encompassing comprehensive tonsillectomy practice patterns [12]. We utilized the Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E‐Surveys (CHERRIES) when reporting our methods and results [13].

Questionnaire Development

This cross‐sectional survey study received institutional ethics review board approval from the Western University Health Sciences Research Ethics Board (REB #124810). Using clinical practice guidelines and existing literature, survey questions were created. The development and revision of the survey involved a collaborative effort with five pediatric otolaryngologists, all fellowship‐trained, from several countries (Canada, United States, Australia, and England). This approach aimed to capture a wide spectrum of pediatric PTB management globally, as well as to determine usability and technical functionality of the online questionnaire.

The questionnaire was finalized at 55 items, explicitly focusing on the participants’ pediatric practices. Beyond demographic information, six domains of pediatric tonsillectomy practice patterns were examined: surgical techniques, admission criteria, pain control/management, diet, bleeding and complications, and follow‐up. No personal information was collected. The contents of this paper will focus specifically on bleeding and complications. Questions were presented in multiple formats, including multiple choice, select multiple options, and open‐text fields. The complete survey is included as Supporting Information. Randomization of items and adaptive questioning were not used.

From April 4 to May 16, 2024 (6 weeks), this anonymous online survey was available to be completed. Two reminders were sent at 2‐week intervals. The survey was hosted and managed using REDCap electronic data capture, a secure and encrypted online platform, hosted at Lawson Health Research Institute [14, 15]. The survey was presented on three pages and participants were unable to return to previous pages once they pressed submit for each section. REDCap does not permit collection of IP addresses to ensure anonymity. There was no compensation for participation, and completion of the survey was voluntary. Informed consent was implied by participants completion of the survey after viewing the Letter of Information. Questionnaires were not excluded based on the amount of time spent filling out the survey.

Participant Selection and Recruitment

The inclusion criteria required that members be pediatric otolaryngologists who can read and write English. An international WhatsApp group was used to recruit participants. There were 293 potential respondents, the identities of which were confirmed as pediatric otolaryngologists by group members or administrators before admission to the group. Direct recruitment in this fashion allows for accurate calculation of the response rate.

Statistical Analysis

Description of the study sample and the variables of interest were represented through descriptive statistics (percentages, median, interquartile range [IQR]). A small number of respondents (n = 20) did not complete all sections of the survey and were excluded only from the analyses of the portions left incomplete. The data were cleaned to ensure that duplicate responses to survey questions were not included in the analysis. A p value of < 0.05 was predetermined to indicate statistical significance, with odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported for significant comparisons. Analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant Demographics

The survey achieved a 45.1% overall response rate, with 132 of 293 eligible participants completing at least one‐third of the survey and 38.2% completing the full survey including all PTB questions (112 respondents). The largest proportion of respondents came from Europe (38.9%) and North America (23.6%). Overall, our participants represented a diverse range of continents. Twenty‐two countries were represented within these continents; however, only half of our respondents (54.5%) provided their country of practice. In terms of specific countries, most of our respondents were from Australia and the United Kingdom at 19.4% each.

In total, 57.6% practiced full‐spectrum general pediatric otolaryngology. The majority of participants practiced in an academic setting (87.1%), for 11–20 years (34.8%), but a range of years in practice were reported.

Defining a Tonsil Bleed

A tonsil bleed was most commonly defined by participants as any bleeding presenting to the hospital (49.2%) or any bleeding reported by parents (36.4%). Other respondents defined a tonsil bleed as any bleeding requiring admission or 23‐h observation (22.0%) and any bleeding requiring operative management (22.0%). Twenty‐three‐hour observation or admission is used similarly in this context based on country of practice and will be referred to as 23‐h observation throughout this paper.

Management of Tonsil Bleeds

When faced with a PTB, respondents highlighted their routine management practices (Figure 1). The majority (58.3%) signified that they take patients for operative management in the presence of active bleeding with specific criteria, whereas 2.3% take all active bleeding cases. While 57.3% of respondents admit all patients with a PTB for 23‐h observation, 21.2% admit only those meeting specific indications. A total of 17.4% of physicians monitor PTB in the emergency department (ER) while utilizing other routine management methods.

When discussing management tools used outside of the OR or within the emergency department, 79.5% of respondents highlighted intravenous (IV) fluids, 26.5% use direct pressure, 22.5% use vasoconstrictor‐soaked pledgets, and 20.5% suction clots. Silver nitrate is used with patients experiencing PTB in 17.4% of respondents and topical epinephrine is used by 12.1%. Of the 19.7% of respondents who identified other management employed outside of the operative room, 61.5% use tranexamic acid, 15.4% use a diluted hydrogen peroxide gargle, and 7.7% use bipolar cautery in awake patients. Many factors were identified that influenced the decision to manage bleeding in the OR. A majority of respondents (78.0%) identified bleeding severity to be the most important factor, followed by bleeding duration (56.1%). Patient age, specifically younger children, was selected by 22.0%, and increased geographic distance from home to the hospital by 12.1%.

In all, 12.5% of respondents indicated they routinely use antifibrinolytics (e.g., tranexamic acid) postoperatively, while another 13.4% indicated they routinely used these in a subset of patients, including patients with known or suspected bleeding disorders, patients with repeated bleeds, or patients with hypermobility.

Monitoring of Tonsil Bleeds

Fifty‐three respondents (47.7%) indicated that they have a formal monitoring program for bleeding rates in their institution/practice. While these monitoring programs varied, many respondents described reporting all bleeds for monthly morbidity and mortality meetings/audits, national registers of postoperative complications, as well as follow‐up phone calls postoperatively, occurring within 24–48 h postoperatively, and then at 2 weeks postoperatively. Of these 53 respondents who indicated there was a formal monitoring program, 59.6% knew the primary bleeding rate of their hospital, which ranged from 0.0% to 2.0%, while 75.5% knew their hospital's secondary bleeding rate, which ranged from 0.5% to 10.0%.

Readmission rates were monitored by 60.7% of respondents. Similar to monitoring programs for bleeding rates, common practices for readmission rate monitoring include reporting in monthly morbidity and mortality meetings/audits, national postoperative patient registries and complication databases, and tracking in electronic medical record systems.

Discussion

With over 500,000 cases performed each year in the United States, our cross‐sectional survey sought to better understand the international similarities and differences in management of PTB by pediatric otolaryngologists [2]. We acknowledge that clinical guidelines are periodically updated and may differ by region. This study references widely accepted guidelines, such as the 2019 AAO‐HNS Clinical Practice Guideline for Tonsillectomy in Children [2]; however, clinicians should consult their national or institutional recommendations for the most up‐to‐date guidance. The survey respondents had significant variation in defining a tonsil bleed, their management practices including indications of when to manage PTB operatively, and monitoring protocols. Understanding international differences in PTB management can be used to guide practice, create preventative approaches, as well as promote physician and patient awareness [16].

PTB cases are often defined as a patient who requires a return to hospital or emergency department for bleeding [17, 18]. This definition underestimates the actual rate, as it does not account for self‐resolving bleeding occurring in the home that may never present for care. The severity of the bleed affecting the definition is a concept reflected in our respondents, as 49.2% of our respondents defined PTB as any bleeding presenting to the hospital, compared to 22.0% believing it to be any bleeding requiring operative management.

In terms of readmission, 57.3% of respondents admit all patients experiencing a PTB for 23‐h observation versus 21.2% who admit a subset of patients for 23‐h observation. Of 826 presentations of secondary PTB in a children's hospital in Australia, 752 patients (91.0%) were admitted for a period of observation. Of these, 22 patients had another bleed, with 17 needing to be managed operatively [19]. A normal oropharyngeal examination at presentation signified a low risk of rebleeding and consideration for early discharge; however, patients presenting with an oropharyngeal clot should be observed [19].

Many respondents in our study likewise identified the presence of a clot as an indication for 23‐h observation post‐tonsillectomy, as the literature has shown that when able to visualize a blood clot in the tonsillar fossa, the pediatric patient is more likely to require a return to the OR for management than those without a blood clot [20]. This emergent management is also more likely to occur within 24 h of admission [20], representing a potential benefit in admitting a patient with resolved secondary PTB and blood clot on initial presentation for observation. 2012 clinical practice guidelines by the French Society of ENT and Head and Neck Surgery recommend that any onset of delayed hemorrhage necessitates readmission to a well‐equipped medical center [21]. Additionally, any clot occupying the tonsillar area should be removed to allow thorough exploration for active bleeding [21].

The literature states that age less than 3 years, comorbidities (such as anemia, cancer, and pulmonary hypertension), and postoperative neurologic complications were predictors for readmission following pediatric tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy [22]. The American 2019 Clinical Practice Guidelines deem age and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) severity to be associated with an increased risk to have a postoperative respiratory complication requiring intervention. In particular, children who are aged less than 3 years, have complicated medical histories, or have severe OSA benefit from inpatient hospital admission and monitoring after surgery [2]. The 2024 national guidelines written by the Australian Society of Otolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery claim that while the likelihood of postoperative haemorrhage in young children is comparable to that in older age groups, their smaller blood volume can significantly increase the severity and health consequences of any bleeding postoperatively [23]. To avoid readmission, the literature has found that early postoperative visits and 72‐h post discharge follow‐up phone calls are beneficial in patient education and early intervention [22, 24]. Our respondents described these follow‐up phone calls postoperatively as well, commonly occurring within 24–48 h postoperatively, and then at 2 weeks postoperatively.

If deciding to manage the bleed outside of the OR, chemical and electrical cautery, topical vasoconstriction, and pressure are common methods of bedside hemostasis [25]. These are traditional strategies used by our respondents. However, potential psychological trauma caused by these invasive measures is notable in the pediatric population [26], particularly when the success of these modalities is limited by trans‐oral access, patient gag reflex, visualization, and patient compliance [25]. New research supports the use of nebulized tranexamic acid (TXA) as an effective, noninvasive mode of hemostasis [25]. TXA administration via various routes—including intravenous, nebulized, and topical—has been shown to be an effective treatment for PTB in pediatric populations, with evidence demonstrating a reduced rate of return to the OR among those treated with TXA in the emergency department compared to those who were not [27]. When used prophylactically, TXA may also reduce rates of PTB and overall blood loss [28]. A recent study has shown that post‐tonsillectomy steroid use is not associated with an increase in operative intervention for postoperative hemorrhage [29].

While controversial, an operative approach has shown to be popular among American otolaryngologists, as two previous papers demonstrated that 50% and 67% of respondents would operate on their patients when presenting with an oropharyngeal clot without active bleeding [5, 30]. As with our respondents, these otolaryngologists also identified patient age, distance patient resides from hospital, bleeding severity, and bleeding duration to be the most important factors when considering operative management [5].

Statement 15b of 2019 Clinical Practice Guideline for Tonsillectomy in Children recommends that clinicians should determine their rate of primary and secondary PTB at least annually [2]. However, this becomes difficult when tonsillectomies are performed by physicians without access to robust reporting framework, unlike those who are employed by large institutions with access to quality reports from third‐party organizations [17]. Measuring PTB requires a substantial effort from these surgeons; even if the frequency of PTB can be determined, there are no established ranges by which to compare their rates [17]. This is reflected in our results, as only 47.7% of respondents indicated that they have a formal monitoring program for bleeding rates in their institution/practice. Statements 30‐32 in a recent paper on quality improvement conducted by a national Canadian expert panel state that physicians should calculate the rate of postoperative tonsillectomy ED visits, rate of postoperative tonsillectomy readmissions, and their rate of primary and secondary post‐tonsillectomy hemorrhage at least annually [31], mirroring the recommendations of the American guidelines.

A recent case series with chart review showed that among children with operative PTB events, the diagnosis of an occult coagulopathy was quite low. However, when separating children who had experienced multiple bleeding events, a significantly higher rate of coagulopathy diagnoses was revealed, increasing the rate from 1.2% to 13.0% [32]. In all, 8.7% of these children had Von Willebrand Disease (VWD) [32]. However, the authors stressed that obtaining coagulation panels in patients presenting with PTB is rarely useful. A portion of our respondents signified that they would administer antifibrinolytics such as TXA postoperatively if their patient had a bleeding disorder, such as VWD. Another study found that while anemia is the most common coagulopathy in pediatric PTB patients, only a small proportion require transfusions [33]. In a 2021 retrospective chart review, of 392 patients who were readmitted for PTB, anemia was commonly noted with a small percentage requiring transfusion, followed by thrombocytosis [33]. These patients typically present with abnormal vital signs, which are much more indicative of the need for intervention than coagulation test results. Physicians have argued that iron deficiency anemia should be corrected preoperatively to reduce intraoperative blood loss [34]. Systematic reviews of the literature found that occult coagulopathy continues to be a challenging and debated topic [35], and a systematic review in 2001 found that there was no difference in the rate of PTB between patients with abnormal coagulation studies and those with normal preoperative coagulation studies [36]. Given these findings, future guidelines should definitively address and guide appropriate laboratory work for improved care to patients while considering unnecessary costs on the institution.

There has been a recent shift by many surgeons toward ICT, as compared to the more traditional method of ECT [37]. The first part of this survey study reported elsewhere discussed our respondents practice patterns, revealing that in our cohort, 55.3% of respondents perform ICT, whether as their dominant method (20.5%) or with equal propensity with ECT (34.8%). Using this lens to examine PTB, a study evaluating pediatric tonsillectomies performed from 2004 to 2014 showed that the rate of PTB with ICT was 0.76%, compared to 2.3% in the extracapsular group [38]. This study was inspired by findings that demonstrated ICT leading to decreased postoperative bleeding, readmission for dehydration, and overall major complications [39]. Due to the small percentage of respondents in our study who knew their bleeding rates, we were unable to compare rates of PTB across ICT versus ECT within our survey respondents. Of note, other studies have suggested that the frequency of PTB across both ECT and ICT was similar; thus, a given technique cannot currently be called superior to another [7]. However, more recent literature reflects the growing popularity of the technique, with emerging evidence increasingly suggesting that ICT may significantly reduce rates of PTB and the need for subsequent operative intervention [40]. A retrospective study of 12,275 patients reported a PTB rate of 1.7% for ICT compared to 4.1% for ECT, with return to the OR at 0.08% for ICT versus 0.93% for ECT [40]. The frequency of bleeding can be hypothesized to be related to the unique anatomy of the tonsillar region, including the smaller caliber of intratonsillar blood vessels compared to those in the extracapsular plane [41]. Interestingly, clinical practice guidelines to date do not provide recommendations surrounding ICT [2]. Future research has been recommended to assess long‐term effectiveness of ICT in treating SDB and recurrent acute tonsillitis, as well as rates of regrowth [42]. Radiofrequency ablation is a popular tool for the “hot technique” of ICT, originally intended to reduce intraoperative bleeding and postoperative morbidity due to the hemostasis produced during tonsil dissection [2]. The 2021 Revised ENT UK Tonsillectomy Commissioning Guide states that there is emerging evidence of improved outcomes in postoperative bleeding rates and pain scores with tonsillotomy (intracapsular) over tonsillectomy (extracapsular), particularly in children [43]. More research is required to confirm “best practices” for pediatric tonsillectomy.

Strengths/Limitations

As with most survey‐based research, our study was susceptible to selection and response biases. While our response rate was high at over 40%, improving the overall generalizability, certain countries were underrepresented, specifically South American and Africa, potentially limiting the accuracy of our findings in capturing the full scope of practice patterns among pediatric otolaryngologists in their region. Another limitation of our survey was the potential for recall bias, especially concerning PTB rates in institutions lacking formal monitoring programs. Additionally, patient‐specific characteristics that influence decisions towards readmission and operative versus nonoperative management may further complicate accurate recollection. Content validity was provided through the survey's development by several experienced otolaryngologists from different countries. However, despite being broad and capturing many experiences, the survey was not formally validated. Our sample sizes in certain continents were too small to allow analysis of regional variation in PTB management between operative versus observational. We were also unable to compare rates of PTB between ICT and ECT given variation in responses.

Future Directions

This study has identified several key areas for further research. Future studies should explore how resource availability influences proper monitoring practices and investigate geographic differences in access to and knowledge of interventions like TXA, as well as intra‐capsular tonsillectomy. Research into the development of simpler monitoring tools could help make this recommended practice more feasible in under‐resourced countries. Additionally, research should examine whether management practices differ based on patient age, particularly between younger and older patients. Furthermore, investigating variations in the management of primary versus secondary PTB across regions could offer valuable insights into global practice patterns.

The development of global guidelines should be explored to enhance the standardization of tonsillectomy care. A modified Delphi consensus, conducted in collaboration with international societies, could facilitate this process. Incorporating diverse perspectives is essential to address factors such as access to new technologies, regional patient values, and geographic variations in care.

Conclusion

There are no standardized “best practice” recommendations for management of pediatric post‐tonsillectomy bleeds. Our study confirmed moderate numbers of formal monitoring programs and varying clinical decision rules when defining a tonsil bleed, deciding to readmit a PTB, and manage within or outside of the OR. Gaining insight into global variations can support the creation of future clinical practice guidelines aimed at improving patient care outcomes.
