Development and implementation of personalized postoperative care plans for gynecological oncology patients under the precision medicine paradigm: strategies to enhance patient participation and self-management capacity
Authors: Xifen Hu, Yun Han, Le Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate a personalized postoperative care pathway for gynecological oncology patients based on precision medicine principles, exploring effective strategies to improve patient engagement, self-management capacity, and long-term recovery outcomes.MethodsA prospective controlled trial was conducted, randomizing 80 postoperative gynecological cancer patients into an intervention group (n = 40) and a control group (n = 40). The control group received standard postoperative care, while the intervention group underwent a multi-module personalized care pathway incorporating the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), Patient Activation Measure (PAM), Shared Decision-Making (SDM) framework, and assessment of digital engagement and adaptability. Intervention components included collaborative rehabilitation goal-setting, digital platform tracking, stage-matched behavioral interventions, SDM support, health empowerment training, and remote psychological counseling. Scale-based outcomes were assessed over 6 months (PAM, SDM, and Health Empowerment Scale [HES]), while survival analysis (disease-free survival [DFS] and event-free survival [EFS]) was conducted over a 12-month follow-up period.ResultsThe intervention group demonstrated significantly superior outcomes in self-management capacity (PAM score: 71.77 ± 6.9 vs. 63.01 ± 7.2, P < 0.05), shared decision-making ability (SDM score: 72.73 ± 6.8 vs. 61.54 ± 7.1, P < 0.001), and health empowerment (HES score: 35.7 ± 3.2 vs. 29.6 ± 4.1, P < 0.001). Notably, 67.5% of intervention patients reached the “action” or “maintenance” stage of health behavior change, compared to only 35% in the control group. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significantly prolonged 12-month disease-free survival (DFS) in the intervention group (10.37 vs. 7.32 months, P < 0.05), with Cox regression identifying personalized care as an independent protective factor (HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.34–0.94, P = 0.028).ConclusionThe precision nursing-based postoperative intervention pathway significantly enhanced gynecological oncology patients’ self-management capacity, treatment engagement, and long-term survival outcomes, demonstrating strong feasibility and scalability. This study provides empirical support for transforming postoperative care into an integrated system of behavioral empowerment, digital support, and collaborative participation.

Introduction

Gynecological cancers (including cervical, ovarian, and endometrial cancers) represent a significant global health challenge, not only threatening patients’ lives but also posing persistent risks to their postoperative quality of life, psychological well-being, and social functioning. In 2022, there were 1,473,427 new cases and 680,372 deaths worldwide due to gynecological cancers [1]. According to the GLOBOCAN report, these cancers account for nearly 40% of all cancer cases and over 30% of cancer-related deaths among women globally [2]. Advances in surgical techniques, adjuvant therapies (including the evolving therapeutic landscape of ovarian cancer [3]), and early screening have enabled an increasing number of gynecological cancer patients to undergo curative surgery and achieve prolonged survival. However, traditional nursing pathways often fall short in addressing the multidimensional challenges encountered during postoperative recovery—such as functional impairment, chronic pain, emotional fluctuations, lifestyle adaptation, and treatment adherence management [4–7]. Particularly during the post-discharge phase, patients frequently face an “information gap” and lack of support in what is termed the “medical void,” resulting in rehabilitation behaviors that lack goal-directedness and continuity, ultimately compromising treatment efficacy and long-term prognosis [8, 9].

In recent years, the emergence of precision medicine has not only reshaped approaches to disease diagnosis and treatment but has also elevated expectations for nursing practice. It calls for care plans that are tailored to individual patient characteristics, biomarkers, behavioral preferences, and levels of social support. In contrast to traditional “standardized” or “protocol-driven” nursing, precision nursing emphasizes a dynamic and responsive closed-loop process—one that integrates “identification of differences + dynamic tracking + behavioral intervention + psychological empowerment.” This model steers nursing pathways toward greater personalization and interprofessional collaboration [10]. In postoperative oncology care, particularly for cancers such as breast and colorectal cancer, the application of precision nursing has shown promising results [11]. However, in the context of postoperative care for gynecological cancers, this approach is still in its early stages. There is a lack of standardized clinical pathways, limited instruments for assessing patient engagement and behavioral change, and no well-established framework for systematic implementation.

Moreover, research has confirmed that patient activation—defined as an individual’s knowledge, skills, and confidence in managing their own health—plays a crucial role in determining the quality of postoperative recovery and long-term survival outcomes [12]. Patients with higher activation levels are more likely to adhere to treatment regimens, recognize early warning signs, and adjust behavioral strategies when facing rehabilitation challenges. As a result, they tend to achieve better quality of life and psychological resilience [13]. Current nursing interventions, however, often fail to dynamically assess or reinforce patients’ behavioral intentions, remaining largely focused on information delivery and generalized recommendations rather than aligning with patients’ values, beliefs, or capacity for goal-setting. In this context, unidirectional and task-oriented approaches are no longer sufficient to meet the complexities of modern oncology rehabilitation. There is a pressing need to transition toward a model characterized by “multidimensional empowerment + behavioral stratification + digital support + active patient participation.”

In contemporary nursing practice, new metrics are increasingly being integrated into quality evaluations, particularly with the growing use of smart nursing technologies such as remote follow-up systems, mobile app-based management, and individualized behavioral assessments [14–15]. Nevertheless, existing precision nursing programs for postoperative gynecological cancer patients often lack systematic pathway design, evidence-based intervention strategies grounded in behavioral science, and longitudinal assessments of self-management capacity and survival outcomes [16–17]. Furthermore, the majority of nursing studies remain nurse-centered, failing to establish a collaborative care model that is truly “patient-led, nurse-facilitated, and technology-enabled” [19].

To address these gaps, this study, guided by precision medicine principles and integrating the Transtheoretical Model (TTM), Patient Activation Measure (PAM), Shared Decision-Making (SDM) framework, and digital literacy assessment frameworks, designed and implemented a modular, phased, and individualized postoperative nursing pathway. The intervention aims to enhance gynecological cancer patients’ engagement, self-management capacity, and long-term rehabilitation quality. For the first time, this research tightly couples behavioral change stage identification with nursing intervention content, incorporates digital platform support to establish dynamic tracking and real-time response mechanisms, and introduces psychologically empowering strategies from an “enablement-support” perspective, bridging the current gap in “patient engagement-behavioral adjustment-remote support” synergy. Through multidimensional quantitative assessments (e.g., PAM, SDM, and Health Empowerment Scale [HES] scores, and survival outcome analyses), this study seeks to provide empirical evidence and a pathway prototype for establishing a new paradigm of precision nursing in gynecological oncology.

Materials and methods

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 80 patients who underwent radical surgery for gynecologic malignancies (cervical, endometrial, or ovarian cancer) at the Gynecologic Oncology Center of Women and Children’s Hospital, Ningbo University, between January 2023 and June 2024 were enrolled in this study. All patients had pathologically confirmed stage I–III disease and were in stable postoperative condition.

Participants were randomized 1:1 into the intervention (n = 40) and control (n = 40) groups using a computer-generated random number sequence. To ensure balanced group sizes throughout the enrollment period, block randomization with a block size of 4 was employed. Allocation concealment was strictly maintained using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE) that were opened by an independent research assistant only after a participant had completed baseline assessments and met all eligibility criteria.

The mean age of participants was 49.8 ± 7.6 years. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, tumor type, FIGO stage, and adjuvant therapy regimens, were well-balanced between groups (all P > 0.05), as detailed in Table 1. All participants provided written informed consent; scale-based assessments were completed at 6 months, and survival outcomes were tracked through a 12-month follow-up period.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of study participants

A formal power analysis was conducted using G*Power (v3.1.9.7) to determine the adequacy of the sample size. Based on preliminary data for the primary outcome (PAM-13 score) and an anticipated effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.65, a total sample of 76 participants was required to achieve a power (1-β) of 80% with a two-sided significance level (α) of 0.05. Accounting for a projected 5% attrition rate, the final enrollment of 80 patients was determined to be statistically sufficient to detect significant differences in both behavioral measures and survival outcomes.

Data collection, procedural execution, and statistical analyses were performed by trained research personnel following standardized protocols to ensure consistency and reproducibility. The study was conducted in compliance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Women and Children’s Hospital, Ningbo University.

Intervention protocols

Conventional postoperative care

Patients in the control group received standard postoperative care during both their hospital stay and following discharge, in accordance with the institution’s established gynecological oncology nursing protocols. This conventional approach was primarily focused on postoperative safety management and fundamental recovery support. Crucially, the nursing team did not implement any additional precision interventions, behavioral assessments, or personalized, stratified management measures. The core components of this standard care pathway included the following four elements:

Postoperative vital sign monitoring and basic analgesia management

In line with early postoperative management protocols, nurses monitored patients’ vital signs (temperature, pulse, respiration, blood pressure, and consciousness) every four hours. For moderate to severe pain reported within the first 48 h post-surgery, analgesics were administered orally or intravenously based on assessments using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS). Nurses also documented the analgesic efficacy and any adverse reactions. While this component ensured baseline physiological stability and addressed acute physical discomfort, it did not proactively assess or engage with patients’ subjective experiences or their confidence in recovery.

Surgical site care, nutritional guidance, and complication prevention

Standardized daily care included inspecting surgical incisions, performing sterile dressing changes, and monitoring exudate to prevent infection or dehiscence. For patients experiencing early postoperative anorexia, general dietary advice—emphasizing high-protein, easily digestible foods—was provided; however, this was not accompanied by dynamic nutritional assessments or personalized guidance. To prevent complications such as deep vein thrombosis and constipation, nurses uniformly encouraged early ambulation, performed calf massage, and promoted regular bowel movements. These interventions were strictly protocol-driven and applied uniformly, without individualized adaptation based on patient-specific risk factors or needs.

Single-session pre-discharge health education

On the day of discharge, the attending nurse conducted a single, one-time verbal education session for the patient and their family. This session covered medication names and usage, follow-up schedules, postoperative rest and dietary recommendations, and guidance on avoiding strenuous activities. The information was delivered using standardized written materials and was not tailored to the patient’s cultural background, health literacy level, or personal preferences. This approach prioritized the passive transmission of information over interactive behavioral collaboration or reinforcement of comprehension.

Isolated post-discharge follow-up

Within one week of discharge, a designated nurse conducted a single telephone follow-up to assess the patient’s recovery status, including wound healing, pain levels, diet, and bowel function. The primary aim was to identify any signs of infection, fever, or delayed follow-up appointments. If no abnormalities were reported, no further contact was initiated, and subsequent care management was transferred to outpatient physicians. This isolated follow-up lacked continuity, did not proactively identify psychosocial stressors, and offered no personalized support or goal-oriented guidance to facilitate ongoing recovery.

In summary, the control group’s nursing pathway exemplified a “standardized and task-oriented” model, centered on nurse-driven execution and emphasizing procedural compliance and physiological monitoring. Within this framework, the patient assumed a passive role as a “recipient of information,” with minimal involvement in setting rehabilitation goals, participating in decision-making, or adapting their behavior. No digital tools, staged behavioral assessments, or empowerment-based interventions were employed. While this conventional approach effectively ensured basic postoperative safety, it fell short of actively fostering patient engagement in the rehabilitation process or cultivating long-term self-management capabilities.

Precision personalized nursing

The intervention group received a multifaceted, precision-nursing program comprising six core components, designed to transition postoperative care from a standardized model to a dynamic, patient-centered partnership. All data collected through these interventions were synchronized with patients’ electronic health records to ensure consistency and traceability.

Rehabilitation goal co-construction and dynamic tracking

During the first postoperative week, assigned nurses conducted structured interviews to collaboratively establish individualized rehabilitation goals with patients for months 3 and 6 post-surgery. These goals encompassed daily functional recovery, sleep duration, pain self-assessment, emotional regulation strategies, exercise plans, and dietary management. The agreed-upon objectives were formalized into a Personalized Rehabilitation Action Plan and uploaded to a dedicated nursing application. Patients logged their daily progress—including task completion and self-ratings—while nurses conducted weekly data reviews. This enabled the team to provide targeted reminders and make real-time adjustments in response to any deviations, thereby establishing a process of “dynamic co-construction.”

Digital remote nursing support

This intervention was supported by a customized nursing management app that integrated health diaries, validated self-assessment tools (e.g., PAM, HES), educational content, and video-based modules. Nurses monitored patient-reported outcomes—such as diet, sleep, pain levels, and medication adherence—in real time. If the system detected anomalies (e.g., sleep duration of less than four hours, pain scores exceeding 7, or three consecutive days of non-compliance), automated “nursing alerts” were triggered. These prompts initiated immediate nurse-led follow-ups via phone or video call, ensuring timely psychological and clinical support.

Stage-based personalized interventions guided by the Transtheoretical Model (TTM)

Using the Transtheoretical Model (TTM) as a framework, patients underwent monthly assessments to determine their behavioral stage concerning diet, exercise, medication adherence, and rehabilitation engagement. Based on the results, patients were stratified into one of five stages: pre-contemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, or maintenance. Tailored interventions were then delivered accordingly—for instance, motivational interviewing for those in pre-contemplation, structured video check-ins for patients in the action stage, and peer-support sharing sessions for those in maintenance. This approach ensured that nursing support was precisely aligned with each patient’s readiness to change.

Shared Decision-Making (SDM) and self-advocacy training

This module was designed to enhance patients’ capacity for shared decision-making through “micro-decision drills.” Nurses coached patients on the three-phase SDM model, which involves information exchange, preference articulation, and consensus-building. Pre-consultation role-playing exercises enabled patients to practice articulating their concerns and exploring treatment options, effectively transforming traditional, clinician-directed communication into a collaborative decision-making process.

Tiered self-management training driven by the Patient Activation Measure (PAM)

Patients were stratified into four activation levels based on the Patient Activation Measure (PAM-13). Those with low activation (Levels 1–2) received targeted interventions focused on disease literacy and adherence support. In contrast, patients with higher activation (Levels 3–4) engaged in advanced goal-setting, progress tracking, and app-based self-management activities. Monthly PAM reassessments were conducted to allow for dynamic adjustments to the training content, ensuring it remained responsive to the patient’s evolving capacity for self-management.

Psychological support and health empowerment focused on the Health Empowerment Scale (HES)

Monthly assessments using the Health Empowerment Scale (HES) evaluated patients’ self-efficacy, health literacy, and confidence in decision-making. In response, psychologist-supported group sessions, emotion journaling, and active listening techniques were employed to help patients navigate feelings of anxiety or helplessness. This fostered an adaptive psychological pathway—moving from “trust” to “control,” then to “expression” and “action”—thereby supporting the patient’s transition from a state of care-dependence to becoming an active “health self-manager.”

Structured follow-ups were conducted at four key intervals to ensure consistency and traceability across the intervention. In sum, this multidimensional, behaviorally adaptive approach fundamentally transformed postoperative care from a model of passive receipt to one of proactive co-management, fully realizing the principles of precision nursing.

Outcome measures

Self-management ability (patient activation)

The PAM-13 [18] was used to assess self-management ability. This scale consists of 13 items, each scored on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Total scores were converted to a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating greater self-management capacity. Based on score ranges, patients were classified into four levels (Levels 1–4), reflecting progressive stages from low awareness and passive compliance to proactive health maintenance. This measure predicts rehabilitation adherence and intervention responsiveness, addressing the under-quantification of patient self-regulation ability in nursing research.

Patient engagement (SDM)

The Shared Decision-Making Questionnaire (SDM-Q-9) [20] evaluated patients’ engagement in collaborative decision-making with healthcare providers. It comprises 9 items, each scored 0–5, with total scores converted to a 0–100 scale. Higher scores indicate greater patient involvement and autonomy in treatment decisions. This tool assesses whether interventions truly embody patient-centered care, emphasizing the synergistic relationship between engagement quality and clinical outcomes.

Digital engagement and adaptability

Digital adaptability was evaluated through app proficiency and usage frequency, measuring patients’ ability to search, comprehend, and apply digital health information, particularly in app-based follow-ups and telemedicine. Digital engagement was quantified by the frequency and proficiency of interaction with the nursing app. Higher interaction levels reflected better digital adaptability to smart nursing platforms (e.g., remote consultations, EHR interactions). As a cutting-edge metric in digital healthcare transformation, this assessment of digital engagement addresses the gap in quantifying patients’ adaptability to nursing interventions.

Readiness for behavior change (stage of change for health behavior)

Based on the TTM, a stage-based classification tool assessed patients’ readiness for behavior change in diet, exercise, and medication adherence. Patients were categorized into five stages: pre-contemplation, contemplation, action, maintenance, or relapse. Classification was determined via self-assessment and structured interviews, providing quantitative evaluation of behavioral motivation and guiding personalized nursing strategies. This approach innovatively integrates behavioral intention into intervention matching, enhancing precision in behavioral nursing.

Health empowerment

The Chinese version of the HES [21] measured patients’ sense of control, autonomy, and self-efficacy in health decisions. The 8-item scale uses a 5-point Likert system, with total scores ranging 8–40. Higher scores reflect greater empowerment, indicating patients’ ability to actively participate in care, thereby improving adherence and long-term recovery motivation. Validated in multiple clinical populations, this scale’s novel application in postoperative care shifts nursing focus from task completion to empowerment-driven outcomes.

Survival outcomes

Kaplan-Meier analysis compared 12-month survival curves between intervention and control groups, evaluating long-term prognosis. Primary endpoints included: Disease-Free Survival (DFS): Time from surgery to first recurrence or death. Event-Free Survival (EFS): Time from surgery to first recurrence, complication, progression, or death. Log-rank tests (significance threshold: P < 0.05) compared survival distributions. To adjust for confounders, a Cox proportional hazards model was constructed, incorporating personalized nursing as a covariate.

Statistical methods

Data entry and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0), while figures were generated using Python and R (version 4.2.0). All data underwent dual independent entry, followed by verification for consistency prior to analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, with the significance level set at P < 0.05. Variables were examined for missing values and outliers before analysis.

Continuous variables (e.g., scale scores, survival time) were presented as mean ± standard deviation ( x̄ ± s) if normally distributed; otherwise, appropriate non-parametric descriptors (median and interquartile range) were used. Categorical variables (e.g., stage distribution, empowerment levels) were expressed as frequencies (n) and percentages (%).

For primary outcome measures (PAM, SDM, and HES scores), comparisons between the control and intervention groups were conducted using independent samples *t*-tests. If data violated assumptions of normality or homogeneity of variance, non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests were employed instead. Categorical variables (e.g., behavioral stage distribution) were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests, as appropriate.

Longitudinal measurements of scale scores (e.g., PAM, SDM, HES) across multiple postoperative time points were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) to examine within-group trends over time and between-group differences, including interaction effects. For non-normally distributed longitudinal data, Friedman rank-sum tests were applied.

In exploratory analyses, Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate relationships among SDM, PAM, HES, app usage frequency, survival time, and quality of life. To examine potential causal pathways, structural equation modeling (SEM) or partial least squares path modeling (PLS-SEM) was used to construct chain relationship models (e.g., nursing behavior → patient engagement → self-management → survival outcomes).

Disease-free survival (DFS) and event-free survival (EFS) were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with between-group differences assessed via log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards regression models were further employed to analyze intervention effects on survival outcomes, with results presented as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual tests (all P > 0.05), and log-log survival plots confirmed that the hazard ratios remained constant over time. For patients lost to follow-up (< 5%), the last observation carried forward (LOCF) method was used for data imputation. Sensitivity analyses were conducted for key outcome variables (e.g., DFS) to verify result robustness.

Results

Changes in self-management ability

At the 6-month follow-up, the intervention group demonstrated significantly better self-management ability than the control group. As shown in Fig. 2A, the mean PAM-13 score in the intervention group was 71.77 ± 6.9, significantly higher than the control group’s 63.01 ± 7.2 (t = 4.93, P < 0.05). Additionally, 67.5% of intervention patients reached Level 3 or 4 activation, compared to only 32.5% in the control group, indicating that personalized nursing interventions enhanced patients’ proactiveness and confidence in health literacy, treatment decision-making, and lifestyle management. Further analysis (Fig. 2B) revealed a moderate positive correlation between PAM score improvement and collaborative goal-setting (r = 0.42, P < 0.05), suggesting that behavioral feedback activation may underlie the intervention’s effectiveness.

Improvement in Shared Decision-Making (SDM)

In SDM assessment the intervention group’s mean SDM-Q-9 score was 72.73 ± 6.8, significantly higher than the control group’s 61.54 ± 7.1 (t = 9.64, P < 0.001). Item-level analysis showed that the intervention group scored higher in “patient-provider information exchange,” “expressing preferences,” and “negotiating care plans,” confirming that SDM-guided interventions improved patient engagement. Notably, 85% of intervention patients “strongly agreed” they participated in decision-making, versus < 50% in the control group (Fig. 3B), supporting the real-world efficacy of integrating SDM into postoperative care.

App usage frequency also correlated strongly with PAM scores (r = 0.74, P < 0.01) (Fig. 4B), underscoring the role of eHealth literacy in enhancing patient participation and validating the “smart nursing tools” component.

Distribution of readiness for health behavior change

The intervention group exhibited more advanced behavioral stages. At 6 months, 67.5% were in “action” or “maintenance” stages, versus 35% in controls. From baseline to 6 months, 45% of intervention patients transitioned from “preparation” to “action” (Fig. 5A), confirming the intervention’s impact on behavioral sustainability. Daily adherence rates (e.g., diet/exercise tracking) were also higher in the intervention group (89% vs. 61%) (Fig. 5B), supporting the TTM-based stratified intervention strategy.

Improvement in health empowerment

The intervention group scored significantly higher on the Health Empowerment Scale (HES): 35.7 ± 3.2 vs. 29.6 ± 4.1 in controls (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6A). Differences were pronounced in “autonomy,” “health control,” and “provider engagement.” HES scores correlated strongly with PAM (r = 0.64, P < 0.01) (Fig. 6B), indicating that empowerment underpins self-management. These results validate the “empowerment education” model, shifting nurses’ role from instructors to facilitators.

Long-term survival outcomes

Kaplan-Meier analysis showed the intervention group had longer DFS: 10.37 months vs. 7.32 months in controls (Fig. 7A). The log-rank test confirmed statistical significance (P < 0.05), with fewer recurrence/progression events in the intervention group during months 3–9. EFS trends were similar (Fig. 7B). As shown in Table 2, multivariate Cox regression analysis, adjusting for baseline variables, identified the personalized intervention as an independent protective factor for DFS (HR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.34–0.94, P = 0.028). Clinical characteristics, including FIGO stage (P = 0.152) and tumor type (P = 0.485), did not significantly influence 12-month survival in this cohort. These findings demonstrate that precision nursing not only improves short-term behaviors but also enhances long-term oncological outcomes, marking a key advancement in nursing intervention research.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that a precision-based, individualized nursing pathway significantly enhances postoperative recovery for gynecological oncology patients. The results showed that the intervention group exhibited superior outcomes in self-management capacity, engagement levels, readiness for behavioral change, and long-term survival compared to the control group. These findings validate the effectiveness of an intervention pathway developed based on precision nursing principles, suggesting that the observed improvements stem from specific therapeutic components—such as stage-matched behavioral stratification and collaborative goal-setting—rather than a general “attention bias” resulting from increased clinical contact. Such advancements are necessary because traditional models of “passive care + one-time education” have demonstrated limited efficacy in this population, failing to sufficiently engage patients in proactive, long-term health management. Gynecologic oncology patients represent a population with high physiological burden, significant psychological stress, and varying levels of behavioral adherence, posing multiple challenges to their rehabilitation pathways [1, 18]. Consequently, as precision medicine continues to integrate into oncology, postoperative rehabilitation management must transition from “protocol uniformity” to “individualized differentiation.

First, regarding improvements in self-management capacity, the intervention group showed significantly higher PAM-13 scores, with a greater proportion reaching high activation levels (Levels 3–4), reflecting enhanced patient autonomy and health decision-making abilities during recovery. Consistent with Hibbard et al.‘s findings, elevated PAM scores correlate strongly with sustained health behaviors and treatment adherence [21]. This study further confirms that PAM is not merely a predictive indicator but a dynamic capability amenable to intervention. The observed positive correlation between the “goal co-creation + daily tracking” module and PAM improvement suggests that patients are more likely to establish positive behavioral feedback loops in settings with clear objectives and visible progress, aligning with the “behavioral context shaping” theory—that more structured support mechanisms yield higher activation levels [22].

In terms of medical decision-making engagement, the intervention group scored higher on the SDM-Q-9 scale than the control group, reflecting greater patient involvement in clinician interactions. This finding supports the positive impact of SDM on treatment satisfaction and trust. A key finding of this study was the positive impact of shared decision-making (SDM) interventions on patient outcomes. Corroborating the systematic review by Stacey et al. (2017), which established that SDM interventions improve patient comprehension, sense of control, and adherence [23], our study demonstrates the practical application of these principles in a postoperative setting. By implementing structured strategies such as “micro-decision training” and “pre-follow-up simulation,” we effectively enhanced patients’ ability to articulate their preferences and engage in consensus-building with clinicians. This approach directly mitigates the information asymmetry inherent in traditional, clinician-dominated models, fostering a more balanced and collaborative clinician-patient partnership. These results further underscore the applicability of SDM beyond complex treatment phases, demonstrating its feasibility and inherent value within postoperative care pathways.

Another significant finding was the marked improvement in patients’ readiness for health behavior change. Guided by the TTM, the intervention group demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of patients in the “action” and “maintenance” stages compared to the control group, which was accompanied by notably increased daily adherence to diet and exercise regimens. This indicates that stage-matched, personalized interventions not only effectively motivate initial behavioral change but also play a critical role in sustaining long-term adherence. Building upon Prochaska et al.‘s foundational TTM framework [24], this study refined the application of intervention matching by assigning specific, stage-appropriate tasks—such as motivational interviewing for those in pre-contemplation, structured behavior logging for patients in the action stage, and experience sharing for those in maintenance. This targeted strategy effectively avoided the common pitfall of “intervention misalignment,” where the support provided does not match the patient’s readiness to change. In contrast to uniform, one-size-fits-all education models, this nuanced approach is better suited to accommodate the dynamic progression of patient behaviors, offering a practical and effective example of integrating behavioral science theory into routine nursing pathways.

Furthermore, regarding digital engagement capacity, the strong positive correlation between app usage frequency and PAM scores suggests that digital tool proficiency directly enhances patients’ ability to access and respond to health information. Additional analysis revealed that features such as smart notifications, interactive questionnaires, and anomaly alerts provided a more “accessible, understandable, and user-friendly” digital support platform, thereby improving digital adaptability and care adherence [25]. This highlights digital empowerment as an indispensable “bridging tool” for future personalized nursing. Notably, the strong correlations observed between specific intervention modules and PAM scores provide evidence that the outcomes were driven by the functional content of the precision plan rather than mere increased interaction frequency.

Psychological support and health empowerment were critical supplementary dimensions. The intervention group showed significantly higher HES scores than the control group, with a positive correlation to PAM, suggesting that psychological well-being is a foundational driver of self-management capacity. Consistent with Molina-Mula et al. (2020), active listening, emotional support, and health belief reinforcement by nursing staff enhanced patients’ perceived “control” and “self-efficacy,” transforming them from passive recipients to active health managers [26]. This study introduced HES as a novel metric in postoperative care, clarifying the mediating role of psychological empowerment in the intervention chain and contributing to theoretical expansion.

Regarding long-term survival outcomes, the intervention group demonstrated improved disease-free and event-free survival compared to the control group. This suggests that the precision nursing pathway not only stabilizes short-term recovery behaviors but also contributes to better long-term oncological prognosis. By establishing a closed-loop feedback mechanism and consistent follow-up, the intervention effectively supports recurrence risk management over time. These results underscore the potential for nursing to evolve from a purely supportive role into a proactive interventional component of cancer care. These findings suggest that precision nursing not only improves short-term behavioral outcomes but may also yield meaningful mid-to-long-term clinical benefits. The structured follow-up and closed-loop feedback mechanisms employed in this study effectively stabilized postoperative health behaviors, thereby establishing a foundation for long-term recurrence risk management. These results further underscore the evolving role of nursing in empirical practice—from a traditionally supportive function to an increasingly interventional one.

In terms of clinical scalability, while this multidisciplinary model integrates nursing, psychology, and digital infrastructure, its practical feasibility is enhanced by capitalizing on existing hospital digital platforms and remote group-based psychological support, which streamlines resource allocation. Moreover, the significant improvement observed in 12-month DFS and EFS suggests that the initial resource investment required for such an intervention may be offset by reduced costs associated with managing long-term complications and recurrence. This indicates potential cost-effectiveness for implementation in routine clinical settings.

Limitations

Despite its contributions to pathway design, mechanistic insights, and intervention validation, this study has several limitations. First, as a single-center trial with a relatively limited sample size, the generalizability of findings may be constrained. Additionally, the intensive support provided to the intervention group may introduce a potential Hawthorne effect (attention bias). Future research should incorporate “attention-control” groups within multicenter settings to more rigorously isolate the specific therapeutic efficacy of the personalized nursing modules. Second, while survival data were collected up to 12 months, the study did not assess longer-term quality of life beyond the 6-month primary endpoint, nor did it capture rehospitalization rates or multi-year survival outcomes. Extended follow-up periods of 3–5 years are recommended for a more comprehensive evaluation of sustained intervention effects. Third, reliance on structured scales and app-based data may overlook nuanced behavioral indicators. Future iterations of this research could benefit from integrating biomarkers (e.g., heart rate variability, motion sensor data) and employing natural language processing techniques to enable more holistic and objective patient monitoring. Finally, app usability may present barriers for patients with limited health literacy or technical proficiency. To improve accessibility and equity, subsequent iterations of the digital platform should prioritize the development of simplified, age-friendly interfaces.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated a postoperative intervention pathway that integrates behavioral stratification, digital tools, and psychological empowerment within a precision nursing framework. The intervention significantly improved gynecologic oncology patients’ self-management capacity, engagement, and long-term survival expectations. The proposed pathway demonstrates innovation in its design, tool integration, and practical outcomes, thereby addressing a critical research gap in the “engagement-behavior-empowerment” triad of postoperative care. Future efforts should focus on optimizing the integration of multidisciplinary resources to develop scalable, cost-effective, and sustainable nursing pathways, ultimately advancing the transition from protocol-driven support to system-enabled empowerment.
