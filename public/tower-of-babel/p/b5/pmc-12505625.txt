CASC15 participated in the damage of vascular endothelial cells in atherosclerosis through interaction with miR-940
Authors: Tairan Li, Zhaolan Yang, Kun Zhang, Wei Song, Bing Liu, Shun Xiao, Mingjin Guo, Meng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the role of long non-coding RNA cancer Susceptibility 15 (CASC15) and microRNA (miR)-940 in atherosclerosis (AS) and to elucidate their potential mechanisms of action using an in vitro cell model of AS.MethodsHuman umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were subjected to oxidized low-density lipoproteins (ox-LDL) induction to establish an atherosclerotic cell model, and the expression levels of CASC15 and miR-940 in this model were evaluated. Cell viability and apoptosis were detected using the Cell Count Kit (CCK)-8 assay and flow cytometry, respectively. Quantitative real-time PCR was employed to quantify gene expression levels of CASC15, miR-940, and cell adhesion molecules intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)-1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)-1, while Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay was used to measure the protein levels of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α. Bioinformatics analysis was utilized to explore the target genes of miR-940 and their functional implications.ResultsThe expression of CASC15 and miR-940 in ox-LDL-induced HUVECs showed an increase in CASC15 and a decrease in miR-940. Notably, miR-940 is a downstream target gene of CASC15. CASC15 knockdown mitigated ox-LDL-induced autophagy flux impairment, thereby promoting autophagy, whereas decreased levels of miR-940 significantly inhibit autophagy. Inhibition of CASC15 alleviates endothelial dysfunction caused by ox-LDL primarily through promoting activity and reducing apoptosis and inflammation. Conversely, a decrease in miR-940 exacerbates endothelial dysfunction. The addition of an autophagy activator relieved endothelial dysfunction, highlighting the involvement of the CASC15/miR-940 axis in regulating autophagy, and its role in modulating endothelial function impairment through autophagy regulation.ConclusionInhibition of CASC15 alleviates ox-LDL-induced endothelial dysfunction, potentially through the activation of autophagy via the modulation of miR-940.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02211-7.Keywords: Atherosclerosis, CASC15, MiR-940, Autophagy

Background

The homeostasis of the cardiovascular system is maintained through a complex interplay of genetic and epigenetic mechanisms. Imbalances within this system can drive pathological processes, including atherosclerosis (AS). Vascular endothelial cells (VECs) serve as critical regulators in cardiovascular homeostasis [1]. Dysfunctions in VECs are a major driver of AS progression and adverse outcomes. Recent research has increasingly focused on autophagy’s role in modulating VEC dysfunction. Autophagy is a double-edged sword in VECs. When VECs sustain minor damage, autophagy is activated to clear damaged organelles and toxic substances. However, if the damage exceeds a critical threshold, it may lead to VECs’ death, facilitating lipid accumulation, plaque formation, and the onset of AS [2, 3]. Early studies suggest that macrophage autophagy can mitigate lipid deposition and foam cell formation, thereby preventing plaque development. During AS progression, autophagy may reduce the accumulation of inflammatory mediators, such as IL-1β, within plaques, enhancing plaque stability [4]. Current evidence predominantly shows that heightened autophagy in VECs improves stress tolerance and cell survival, ultimately benefiting cardiovascular health.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) are RNA molecules ranging from 200 to 100,000 nucleotides in length. They do not code for proteins and have once been considered biologically inert [5, 6]. However, recent studies have highlighted their involvement in the pathogenesis of AS. Ye et al. reported that lncRNA cancer Susceptibility 15 (CASC15) exacerbates the degradation of the fibrous cap by inducing inflammatory responses, thereby aggravating plaque rupture and advancing AS [7]. Initially, CASC15 was identified as abnormally expressed in tumors, exhibiting distinct roles across different tumor types [8]. Recently, extensive research has focused on its role of CASC15 in cardiovascular disease. Elevated CASC15 expression has been observed in acute ischemic stroke [9] and cardiac hypertrophy [10], yet its expression and role in AS remain poorly understood. LncRNAs exert their effects by binding to microRNAs (miRNAs), which are endogenous, non-coding, single-stranded small RNAs. These miRNAs specifically bind to the target gene regions, triggering their degradation or translational repression, thus regulating gene expression [11]. Previous studies have shown that certain miRNAs, like miR-185 and miR-126, play a role in plaque stability and AS progression [12]. Furthermore, miRNAs like miR-155 are implicated in autophagy regulation in tumor cells and macrophages [13, 14]. MiR-940, a highly conserved and extensively studied gene, is reduced in hypertension and cerebral infarction [15, 16]. However, research on the alterations and mechanisms of miR-940 in AS remains relatively scarce. Earlier, bioinformatics analysis revealed a target relationship between miR-940 and CASC15. However, it remains unclear whether they jointly modulated the progression of AS via target binding.

To accurately simulate the physiological state of VECs within an atherosclerotic environment, this experiment utilized human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) subjected to oxidized low-density lipoproteins (ox-LDL). The primary objective was to investigate the interaction between CASC15 and miR-940 and their relationship with autophagy in AS (the basic principle of this study is shown in Figure S1).

Materials and methods

Participants and samples

A total of 142 Subjects participated in this study, evenly divided between 71 control patients and 71 patients with AS. Based on 5% false positive error rate (two-sided, α = 0.05), power = 90%, β = 0.1, and dropout rate = 20%, PASS 11.0 software (NCSS, Kaysville, UT, USA) was estimate the sample size, and at least 70 subjects in each group should be recruited as the minimum sample size Limit. Ultimately, a total of 142 subjects were enrolled, with 71 in each group, Surpassing the minimum required sample size. The inclusion criteria for patients with AS adhered to the 2016 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the European Atherosclerosis Society (EAS) guidelines (2016 ESC/EAS) [17]. Plaque formation was defined as a carotid intima-media thickness (CIMT) greater than 1.3 mm, while carotid intimal thickening was identified as a CIMT between 0.9 mm and 1.3 mm. Patients with severe concurrent diseases, such as malignant tumors, myocardial infarction, or stroke, were excluded from the study. The control group consisted of individuals with a CIMT less than 0.9 mm and no history of cerebrovascular disease. This study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University, and all participants provided written informed consent. The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Helsinki Declaration for human research trials.

In this study, the AS patient cohort (38 males, 33 females) was aged 51 to 82 years. The control group (36 males, 35 females) was aged 50 to 87 years. Both groups had similar demographic characteristics. 5mL of fasting venous blood was drawn from participants in both groups. The serum was subsequently separated and preserved at −80℃ for future analysis.

Cell culture and treatment

HUVECs were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, USA), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Invitrogen, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA). The cultures were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37℃. Following established protocols, HUVECs at passages 4 to 8 were treated with 100 µg/mL oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for a duration of 24 h according to previous research [18]. This treatment was designed to emulate the atherosclerotic environment observed in vivo, thereby facilitating the development of an in vitro atherosclerotic cell model [19].

Cell transfection

Non-silencing scrambled control siRNA (si-NC) and specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) against CASC15 were purchased from GenePharma. MiR-940 inhibitor (cat. miR20004983-1-5) and miRNA inhibitor negative control (inhibitor NC, cat. miR2N0000001-1-5) were acquired from RiboBio. Intracellular CASC15 and miR-940 expression were modulated through cell transfection. Specifically, 20pmol of si-CASC15 and miR-940 inhibitor were each diluted in 50µL of OPTI-MEM medium, then mixed and designated as solution (A) Concurrently, 5µL of Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was mixed with 50µL of OPTI-MEM medium and incubated at room temperature for 5 min, and labeled as solution (B) The prepared solutions A and B were then mixed and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Following this incubation, the resulting mixture was added to the cells. The cells were Subsequently transfected, and gene expression was detected 48 h post-transfection.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was added to the cells, followed by a 5-min incubation at room temperature to facilitate cell lysis. After the cells were lysed, the resulting mixture was Subjected to multiple processes to ensure thorough disruption. RNA was then extracted and purified through sequential treatments with chloroform, isopropyl alcohol, and 75% ethanol. The concentration and purity of the RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 ultra microspectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). The MultiScribe Reverse Transcriptase kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) was used to reverse transcribe RNA into cDNA. Then, PCR was conducted using the SYBR green quantitative PCR kit (Takara, Japan) under the following conditions: pre-denaturation at 95℃ for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95℃ for 5 s, and annealing and extension at 60℃. After the PCR amplification, the relative gene expressions in each group were calculated using the 2−△△Ct method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) or U6 serving as the internal reference genes. This methodology was also applied for the analysis of intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM)−1 and vascular cell adhesion molecule (VCAM)−1 expression.

CCK-8 assay

HUVECs in the logarithmic growth phase were prepared as a cell Suspension with a concentration of 5× 104/mL. These samples were then aliquoted at 100µL per well into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. Following treatment according to the experimental design, at 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, the culture medium was discarded, and 10µL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime Inst Biotech, China) was added to each well. The cells were then incubated for an additional 2 h, and the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Flow cytometry assay

Cell apoptosis was detected using the Annexin V-fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) kit (Invitrogen, USA). Transfected cells were washed twice with pre-cooled PBS buffer, then digested with Ethylenediaminetertraacetic acid (EDTA)-free 0.25% trypsin. The cell Suspension was adjusted to 1× 106/mL with binding buffer. Subsequently, 5µL of Annexin V-FITC and 5µL PI (20 µg/mL) were added to the cell Suspension, gently mixed, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Apoptosis rates were quantified via flow cytometry.

Western blot

Western blot was used to determine protein levels. Initially, intracellular proteins were extracted following cell lysis with RIPA lysis buffer. The protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA kit. Subsequently, proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Bio-Rad, USA). To prevent non-specific binding, the PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature. After blocking, the excess liquid on the membrane was carefully blotted with filter paper before the membrane was incubated in a solution containing diluted primary antibodies. Specifically, the primary antibodies used are anti-LC3 (1:1000), anti-P62 (1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (1:3000). Following incubation with the primary antibodies, a second antibody was applied and incubated overnight at 4℃, with a Subsequent 1-hour incubation at room temperature. The membrane was then washed three times with TBST. Chemiluminescent detection was performed by applying an appropriate amount of enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent, and the membrane was exposed on the gel imaging analyzer with the PVDF side facing up. The image was captured and analyzed using LANE-1D to measure and record the gray values of the protein bands. The relative expressions of protein were normalized against the control group, with GAPDH as the internal reference. The complete uncropped gel and blot images are included in the supplementary files.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The cells from each group were collected and diluted with PBS buffer, adjusting the cell density to 1 × 106/mL. The cells were then Subjected to repeated freeze-thaw cycles to induce rupture, followed by centrifugation at 3000r/min for 20 min to obtain the supernatant. The concentrations of inflammatory factors of interleukin (IL)−1β, IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α in each group were assessed according to the instructions of the ELISA kit. The OD value of each sample at 450 nm was measured and normalized to determine the relative content.

Luciferase reporter gene assay

The 3’-UTR of CASC15 was amplified via RT-qPCR and subsequently cloned into the pmirGLO luciferase reporter vector. Site-directed mutagenesis was used to generate mutations into the miR-940 binding site of the CASC15 cDNA. The wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) luciferase constructs were then co-transfected with miR-940 mimics or inhibitors into ox-LDL-induced HUVECs using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). After 48 h, the cells were harvested, and luciferase activity was detected using the dual-luciferase assay system (Promega, USA). Renilla luciferase activity served as a normalization control.

Construction of lncRNA-miRNA-mRNA network

The lncBOOK, LncRNA SNP2 database, and GSE28858 dataset were employed to investigate the potential miRNAs binding to CASC15. The target genes of the predicted miRNAs were identified using the EVmiRNA, TargetScan, and DIANA databases. The results were visualized via Venn diagrams.

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis

At least three databases were used to identify miR-940 target genes, which were Subsequently Subjected to GO and KEGG pathway analyses. GO enrichment was conducted using DAVID 6.7 software to elucidate the cellular components, biological processes, and molecular functions of the targets. Meanwhile, KEGG pathway analysis was conducted to explore the signaling pathways linked to these target genes.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network construction

PPI networks were constructed using the STRING database to predict the interactions among proteins encoded by commonly differentially expressed mRNAs. A stringent threshold score of 0.7 was employed to ensure high-confidence predictions.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 and GraphPad Prism 7.0 were used for data analysis and graph rendering, respectively. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test, while one-way ANOVA was used for multiple-group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Identification of CASC15 target genes

As illustrated in Fig. 1A, CASC15 expression levels in the serum of AS patients were markedly elevated compared to the control group (P < 0.001). This suggests that abnormal CASC15 may be involved in the occurrence and progression of AS. Subsequently, a comprehensive analysis of the LncBOOK, LncRNA SNP2 databases, and the dataset GSE28858 related to the atherosclerotic miRNAs (Fig. 1B) identified 4 miRNAs—miR-940, miR-129-5p, miR-199a-3p, and miR-199b-3p—as potentially associated with CASC15. RT-qPCR was employed to assess the expression of these 4 miRNAs in the serum of patients with AS. The results indicated differential expression of these miRNAs in AS. Notably, miR-129-5p and miR-199b-3p were consistent with that of CASC15, showing an up-regulation in AS, whereas miR-199a-3p and miR-940 exhibited inverse trends relative to CASC15, with miR-940 being the most significantly downregulated. Thus, miR-940 was prioritized for further investigation (P < 0.01, Fig. 1C-F). We hypothesized that miR-940 may target CASC15 through direct binding in AS. To test this hypothesis, a luciferase reporter gene assay was conducted. The results revealed that the luciferase activity of the WT-CASC15 vector group varied with the transfection of miR-940 mimic and inhibitor, whereas no such variation was observed in the MUT-CASC15 group (P < 0.001, Fig. 1G).

The CASC15/miR-940 axis mediates ox-LDL-induced endothelial cell injury

In ox-LDL-induced HUVECs, CASC15 expression was significantly elevated, consistent with the trend in the serum of AS patients, confirming the successful establishment of the AS cell model. Transfection with si-CASC15 markedly downregulates intracellular CASC15 levels, indicating effective regulation of gene expression via cell transfection (P < 0.001, Fig. 2A). Transfection results indicated that inhibiting CASC15 increased miR-940 levels. However, this effect was abolished by adding miR-940 inhibitors, further substantiating the efficacy of cell transfection in regulating intracellular gene levels (P < 0.001, Fig. 2B). Additionally, ox-LDL reduced HUVEC viability and increased apoptosis. Down-regulation of CASC15 ameliorated these adverse effects, but the introduction of miR-940 inhibitors reversed this amelioration, restoring the inhibition of cell viability and increased apoptosis (P < 0.001, Fig. 2C-D). In terms of cellular inflammation, miR-940 inhibitors counteracted the anti-inflammatory effect of CASC15 silencing, resulting in increased production of inflammatory factors (P < 0.001, Fig. 2E). Similarly, the examination of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 levels revealed that si-CASC15 reduced the production of these factors and mitigated endothelial dysfunction, an effect that was reversed by miR-940 downregulation (P < 0.001, Fig. 2F).

Effect of CASC15 on ox-LDL-induced autophagy

As shown in Fig. 3A-B, ox-LDL treatment of HUVECs significantly increased intracellular LC3II and P62 protein levels compared to the control group, indicating impaired autophagic flux and autophagy inhibition. Inhibition of CASC15 expression markedly elevated LC3II levels while reducing P62 levels (P < 0.001), suggesting that CASC15 downregulation promotes autophagy activation (P < 0.001). Further, upon additional downregulation of miR-940, LC3II protein levels decreased once more, while P62 protein levels increased further, indicating that the loss of miR-940 impedes autophagy (P < 0.001). Rapamycin (Rapa), a classic autophagy agonist, was used as a positive control. Rapa treatment increased LC3II and decreased P62 levels (P < 0.001), consistent with its role in activating autophagy.

Rapa mitigated endothelial damage induced by miR-940 reduction

Cell viability and apoptosis experiments revealed that while miR-940 inhibitors abolished the beneficial effects of CASC15 reduction on cell viability and apoptosis, the administration of Rapa notably increased cell viability and decreased apoptosis (P < 0.001, Fig. 4A-B). Moreover, Rapa treatment down-regulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6) and adhesion molecules (ICAM-1, and VCAM-1) (P < 0.001, Fig. 4C-D), thereby mitigating inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. These findings highlight that autophagy activation via Rapa attenuates endothelial cell damage by improving viability, reducing apoptosis, and suppressing inflammation.

Discussion

In the cardiovascular system, VECs play a crucial role in maintaining vascular structure and function. VEC injury is a primary event in the initiation of AS. ox-LDL is a significant factor contributing to VEC injury, destabilizing endothelial cells and triggering inflammatory responses, including the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This cascade of events is instrumental in the development of atherosclerosis. Notably, ox-LDL serves as an inducer of VECs’ autophagy. Following a certain threshold of injury, VECs activate autophagy as a protective mechanism against damage. However, dysregulated or excessive autophagy can lead to VECs’ apoptosis, promoting lipid deposition and plaque formation. In this study, we exposed HUVECs to a certain concentration of ox-LDL to mimic the damage to VECs during AS progression. It was observed that ox-LDL impaired autophagy flux in HUVECs, accompanied by upregulation of CASC15 and downregulation of miR-940. These findings suggest a potential regulatory role between CASC15, miR-940, and the autophagic response in HUVECs.

In scholarly investigations, the expression profile of lncRNA genes in HUVECs treated with ox-LDL has been thoroughly analyzed, revealing that 298 lncRNAs were up-regulated while 234 were down-regulated [20]. These findings suggest that lncRNA abnormalities significantly affect the autophagy, proliferation, and inflammation of VECs. For example, MALAT1, a conserved lncRNA, influences endothelial cell proliferation during neonatal retinal angiogenesis [21]. MEG3 has been shown to exacerbate endothelial dysfunction in mice treated with a high-fat diet [22]. Our current study has identified an increased serum expression of CASC15 in AS patients compared to healthy individuals. This led us to hypothesize that abnormal CASC15 expression may contribute to AS development, indicating a potential link between AS and CASC15 dysregulation. Here, a cell experiment showed that the expression level of CASC15 in HUVECs treated with ox-LDL was significantly up-regulated, aligning with its expression trend in AS patients and other cardiovascular diseases. CASC15 has been found to function as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA), offering a neuroprotective role in the oxygen glucose deprivation/reoxygenation (OGD/R) cell model primarily through its interaction with miRNAs [9]. In this experiment, we predicted and confirmed a binding site between CASC15 and miR-940 through databases and luciferase experiments, and found that miR-940 was negatively regulated by CASC15. AS is recognized as a chronic inflammatory disease, where inflammation plays a pivotal role in its pathogenesis. After VEC injury, the immune and inflammatory responses are activated [23]. Under the condition of inflammation, cell degeneration and necrosis, leukocyte adhesion and exudation can promote the generation of ICAM-1 and VCAM-1, thus accelerating AS development [24]. Cell experiments demonstrated that specific transfection of si-CASC15 can alleviate ox-LDL-induced endothelial injury by enhancing cell viability and reducing apoptosis, inflammation, and adhesion factor expression. These results suggest that downregulating CASC15 could mitigate endothelial cell dysfunction. Further reducing miR-940 in cells reversed the protective effects of CASC15 reduction, indicating that CASC15 may act as an upstream repressor of miR-940. Consequently, decreased miR-940 levels might exacerbate endothelial dysfunction.

Autophagy, a highly conserved mechanism in eukaryotic cells, is crucial in the pathogenesis of AS. Moderate autophagy protects cells from oxidative stress, hypoxia, and inflammation, prevents cell apoptosis and necrosis, and thus delays AS onset and progression [25, 26]. Our study revealed that ox-LDL induces an increase in CASC15 and a decrease in miR-940 in HUVECs, leading to impaired autophagy flux and autophagy inhibition. LC3, a gene located on the surface of pre-autophagy vesicles and autophagic vesicle membranes, exists in type I when autophagy is not activated, and transitions to type II upon the initiation of autophagy [27]. LC3 levels correlate with the number of autophagy vesicles; hence, its expression intensity is positively associated with autophagy activity [28]. P62, which directly binds to LC3, partially enters autophagy and is continuously degraded during the process, showing a negative correlation with autophagy intensity [29]. To further explore autophagy, we detected the protein levels of LC3II and P62 in HUVECs under different conditions. The results showed that CASC15 downregulation upregulated LC3II expression while downregulating the expression of P62. Conversely, inhibition of miR-940 led to a reduction in LC3II levels and an increase in P62 expression. These findings suggest that CASC15 downregulation significantly promotes autophagy in ox-LDL-induced HUVECs, while reduced miR-940 inhibits autophagy.

Rapa, a well-known autophagy agonist, effectively activates autophagy in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and pluripotent stem cells [30]. During the course of our research, we observed that long-term exposure to ox-LDL resulted in autophagic flow arrest. Upon inhibiting the expression of CASC15, we noted a marked increase in the protein level of LC3II, a well-established marker of autophagosome formation, and a concomitant decrease in the protein level of P62, a substrate of autophagy. These findings strongly suggest that the downregulation of CASC15 promotes autophagy activation. However, subsequent experiments revealed that this autophagy-promoting effect was significantly attenuated by miR-940 inhibitors, leading to autophagy blockade. Based on these results, we hypothesize that miR-940 may act as a downstream effector of CASC15 in the regulation of autophagy. To further validate this hypothesis and confirm that CASC15 regulates autophagy by targeting miR-940, we introduced Rapa, a well-characterized autophagy inducer. By adding Rapa, we aim to reverse the blocked autophagy and provide more compelling experimental evidence for the proposed regulatory mechanism. Our study found that autophagy, which was weakened by miR-940 inhibitor, was significantly restored by Rapa. Our results confirmed that miR-940 may act as a downstream effector of CASC15 in autophagy regulation.

Furthermore, cell biological function studies corroborated these results. In this study, the addition of Rapa effectively counteracted the endothelial injury-promoting effects of CASC15/miR-940. While decreased miR-940 exacerbated endothelial function damage, Rapa further ameliorated this damage, demonstrating that CASC15/miR-940 regulates endothelial cell injury via autophagy modulation. With the advancement in genomics and biotechnology, the interaction between lncRNA and miRNA, along with their regulatory roles in gene expression regulation, will be further confirmed. Our current study indicated that miR-940 inhibitors can reverse the enhanced autophagy induced by CASC15 silencing in vitro, suggesting that CASC15 exerts its physiological effects through interaction with miR-940.

Many proteins involved in autophagy are regulated by miRNAs, which prompted us to delve deeper into the role of miR-940. Our bioinformatics analysis identified at least 80 possible downstream target genes for miR-940 across three independent databases. GO analysis showed that these target genes are predominantly associated with Golgi membranes and implicated in biological processes such as mitosis and the cell cycle. Additionally, KEGG pathway analysis indicated that these target genes are primarily located in the mTOR signaling pathway. mTOR, an atypical serine/threonine protein kinase, serves as a key regulatory factor in intracellular and extracellular signal networks. It is instrumental in various biological processes such as cell growth, proliferation, migration, autophagy, and apoptosis under the activation of many factors [31, 32]. The mTOR signaling pathway is notably linked to AS; for example, it contributes to AS through the autophagy of macrophages and the aberrant proliferation of vascular endothelial cells [33]. Further investigation is warranted to elucidate the specific role of mTOR in HUVECs within the context of this study. Advanced glycation end products have been shown to induce autophagy in HUVECs through the RHOA-mTOR signaling pathway [34]. Additionally, research indicates that rapamycin decreases CAV1 expression in hepatic sinusoidal endothelial cells, whereas 3-MA increases CAV1 levels, implying that CAV1 may undergo degradation via autophagy [35]. High-glucose conditions suppress CAV1 autophagic degradation by inhibiting the AMPK-mTOR-PIK3C3 pathway, subsequently promoting LDL endocytosis [36]. In the PPI network, we identified RHOA and Cav-1 as hub genes regulated by miR-940 in vascular endothelial autophagy during AS. These genes are all linked to the mTOR signaling pathway. Consequently, we hypothesize that the CASC15/miR-940 axis may be involved in autophagy-induced endothelial dysfunction by modulating the mTOR signaling pathway through CAV1 or RHOA. However, further investigation is warranted to confirm this speculation. This study has certain limitations. Although we used the autophagy agonist Rapa to restore autophagy inhibited by the downregulated miR-940, further clarification of this mechanism using autophagy inhibitors such as baffinolide A1 or chloroquine, or Beclin-1 gene knockout, is also necessary. Furthermore, this study still lacks the provision of more direct evidence to prove the role of CASC15 and miR-940 in autophagy through miR-940 overexpression or target gene rescue experiments, or add autophagic flux assays like mRFP-GFP-LC3. Finally, the failure to further explore and verify the specific signaling pathways and key targets involved in the CASC15/miR-940 axis is also a potential limitation of this study. In subsequent studies, we will focus on solving these problems.

Conclusion

In summary, ox-LDL induces elevated levels of CASC15 in HUVECs, concurrently impairing autophagy flux and leading to endothelial cell dysfunction. Inhibition of CASC15 promotes autophagy by up-regulating miR-940, thereby mitigating the endothelial damage caused by ox-LDL. This understanding of the underlying mechanisms offers a potential new target and foundation for the clinical treatment of AS.
