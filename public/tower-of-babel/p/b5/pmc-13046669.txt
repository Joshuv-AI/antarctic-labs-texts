Incidence, mortality, and factors associated with fat embolism syndrome in patients with long bone fractures at a trauma referral center in Bogotá, Colombia: 2016–2019 and 2022–2023
Authors: Oscar Yan Gavidia Bernal, Natalia Rocío Clavijo Lozano, Jhon Fredy Bello Cordero, Eduard Arturo Avila Tenjo, Juan Sebastian Lazaro, Diana Carolina Bermúdez Acosta, Claudia Sirley Quiroga Aguilera, Jeison David Gamba Valero, Laura Alejandra Garzón Bautista, Diana Lorena González Rico, Alejandra Parra Niño
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo estimate the incidence, mortality, and factors associated with the development of fat embolism syndrome (FES) in patients with long bone fractures treated at a trauma referral center in Bogotá, D.C., Colombia.MethodsRetrospective cohort study including patients >18 years with diaphyseal fractures of humerus, femur, tibia, or fibula, treated between 2016-2019 and 2022-2023. COVID-19 pandemic years (2020-2021) were excluded. Statistical analyses included univariate, bivariate, and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsAmong 3,475 patients with long bone fractures, FES incidence was 4.3% (n=148) with 5.4% in-hospital mortality. Multivariable analysis identified femoral fractures as the strongest risk factor (aOR=4.63; 95%CI:2.99-7.22; p<0.001), while early surgical fixation (<24h) demonstrated significant protection (aOR=0.42; 95%CI:0.25-0.71; p<0.001). Obesity independently increased FES risk (aOR=2.63; 95%CI:1.33-4.93; p=0.004). Most cases (97%) resulted from high-energy trauma, predominantly motorcycle accidents. Respiratory compromise was severe, with 93.9% of FES patients requiring advanced ventilatory support versus 16.8% in controls (p<0.001).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that early surgical fixation significantly reduces FES risk and identifies humeral fractures as an underrecognized risk factor. These findings support immediate implementation of early fixation protocols and expanded monitoring beyond femoral fractures in high-energy trauma patients.

Introduction

Fat embolism syndrome (FES) is a rare but potentially serious complication that can occur after major trauma, such as long bone fractures or invasive orthopedic procedures, such as total hip or knee arthroplasty, and, less frequently, in cases of burns or soft tissue injuries [1]. It has also been documented, although less frequently, after transplants[2, 3], cosmetic surgeries [4, 5], and even after treatment with acupuncture [6]. Its pathophysiology has been attributed to two main mechanisms: one mechanical, related to the direct entry of fat into the venous circulation due to increased intramedullary pressure, which facilitates its passage into the vessels without the need for an obvious vascular injury [7], and another biochemical, mediated by free fatty acids that induce endothelial toxicity, with systemic inflammatory consequences [8, 9]. These processes can cause edema, hemorrhage, and tissue damage in target organs, especially the lungs and central nervous system, during the early stages of the syndrome, even within the first 12–72 h after the trauma [1, 9–11]. In the absence of timely recognition, FES can rapidly progress to adverse outcomes [7].

Once the blood vessel is obstructed by fat emboli, an inflammatory response is triggered, which activates the coagulation cascade, leading to tissue damage and potentially resulting in acute respiratory distress syndrome. This imbalance can progress to a systemic inflammatory response syndrome or compensatory anti-inflammatory response, increasing the risk of sepsis [8, 12].

Clinically, the syndrome is characterized by a set of respiratory manifestations (hypoxemia, dyspnea, respiratory failure, and tachypnea), neurological symptoms (confusion, seizures, and focal deficits), and dermatological signs (petechiae on the chest, axillae, and conjunctivae). Neurological manifestations usually appear after respiratory manifestations, while dermatological signs tend to be the last to appear. Taken together, these symptoms typically develop between 24 and 72 h after the triggering event [1, 13].

Despite its clinical recognition for decades, the diagnosis of FES continues to be challenging because of the absence of validated criteria and the heterogeneous use of diagnostic scales, such as Gurd’s criteria or the Schonfeld index [14, 15].

The lack of standardization in diagnostic criteria has made it difficult to accurately estimate the incidence of the syndrome, which varies widely in the literature, ranging from 0.9% to 65%, depending on the type and location of the trauma [10, 15, 16]. Although the associated mortality has shown a downward trend in recent decades, decreasing from 7.9% to 0.7%, FES continues to represent a significant clinical risk, especially in young patients, those with diaphyseal fractures, or those with comorbidities such as obesity and diabetes mellitus [16, 17].

Currently, no specific treatment exists for fat embolism syndrome. Management focuses on comprehensive clinical support and treatment of the underlying cause, while the body resolves the process spontaneously [1]. In this context, reducing its incidence is a fundamental strategy to prevent serious outcomes, such as neurological compromise or death. Some interventions described in the literature include the use of corticosteroids and rapid and proper immobilization of fractures [18] to minimize the risk of the syndrome [10].

Although the use of corticosteroids is not yet part of standardized clinical guidelines and has not been widely evaluated in recent clinical trials, some studies have reported promising results. One such study observed a significant reduction in the incidence of FES in patients treated with corticosteroids [19]. Another study found a 78% reduction in the risk of developing the syndrome, with no increase in the incidence of infection [20]. These preliminary observations support the need to continue exploring the potential protective effects of corticosteroids as a preventive strategy.

The clinical impact of FES lies not only in its potential severity but also in the difficulty of early identification, which can delay key interventions in hospital settings. In polytraumatized patients, where symptoms may be attributed to multiple causes, FES can go unnoticed if not considered in the differential diagnosis. This highlights the need to establish monitoring protocols in trauma and emergency units, allowing for a systematic approach to cases with risk factors.

In the Colombian context, evidence on FES is limited and is mostly based on isolated case reports [3, 21]. The absence of systematic studies makes it difficult to understand the true magnitude of the syndrome, to accurately identify risk factors, and to assess the impact of potential interventions, such as early surgical fixation or the use of corticosteroids [19, 20, 22]. At the institutional level, generating local evidence on the frequency, clinical characteristics, and outcomes of FES is essential for designing more effective prevention and management strategies for FES. This is particularly relevant in high-complexity hospitals, where the volume of patients with high-energy blunt trauma is significant, and the time to surgical fixation may vary depending on the available resources and current care protocols. The present study was conducted at a high-complexity trauma center and, to the best of our knowledge, represents one of the largest published series estimating the incidence of FES in patients with long-bone fractures.

Consequently, the present study aimed to estimate the incidence, mortality, and factors associated with the development of fat embolism syndrome in patients with long bone fractures treated at a tertiary care hospital in Bogotá D.C., Colombia, during 2015–2019 and 2022–2023. The years 2020 and 2021 were excluded to avoid biases related to hospital care during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study design

This quantitative, observational, retrospective case series study was conducted at a single tertiary care hospital in Bogotá D.C., Colombia. The analysis period included 2015–2019 and 2022–2023. The interval between 2020 and 2021 was excluded because of the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which could introduce confounding biases related to respiratory symptoms and clinical outcomes.

Population and eligibility criteria

The population consisted of patients over 18 years of age who were admitted to the emergency department with a diagnosis of diaphyseal fracture of long bones (femur, tibia, fibula, or humerus) according to ICD-10 coding. Patients who were pregnant were excluded.

Data collection and sources of information

A retrospective database was constructed from electronic medical records, paraclinical records (laboratories, diagnostic imaging), and clinical-administrative documents. Case selection was performed by searching for ICD-10 codes, followed by manual validation of the development or non-development of fat embolism syndrome (FES) using the clinical criteria. Data were recorded in a standardized form developed in Excel, which included sociodemographic, clinical, evolutionary, therapeutic, and outcome variables of interest. The database was anonymized and safeguarded by the research team following institutional security protocols.

Definition of variables and outcomes

The development of FES was defined using the Schonfeld index, with a score of 5 or higher considered positive. The incidence was estimated as the number of FES cases among the total number of patients with long bone fractures. Mortality was defined as death from any cause during hospitalization in patients with FES. Additionally, variables related to clinical history (diabetes mellitus, obesity), trauma characteristics (type and cause of fracture), interventions (surgical fixation, administration of steroids, type of steroid, mechanical ventilation), clinical course (hypoxemia, ARDS, neurological sequelae, admission to chronic care unit), and final outcome (mortality or discharge).

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of the population characteristics was performed. For qualitative variables, absolute and relative frequencies were recorded. Quantitative variables were analyzed according to their distribution using the mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range after assessing normality. For the comparison of patients with and without FES, hypothesis tests were applied according to the nature of the variables: chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. The association between the timing of fixation (early vs. late) and FES was evaluated using binary logistic regression, estimating crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) for prespecified covariates. Propensity score-based methods (1:1 matching and inverse probability of treatment weighting, IPTW) were used to control for confounding. Additionally, a composite variable for criticality (altered consciousness, ARDS, mechanical ventilation, or combined long bone fractures) was incorporated into the adjusted models and stratified analyses. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1 with α = 0.05. The protective effect of early fixation (< 24 h) reported in the results (aOR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.25–0.71; p < 0.001) corresponds to the adjusted odds ratio obtained from the final logistic regression model, which was controlled for age, obesity, origin, fracture location (femur and tibia), and initial stabilization with a cast.

Ethical considerations

This study was classified as a no-risk investigation in accordance with Article 11 of Resolution 8430 of 1993 from the Ministry of Health of Colombia [23]. Secondary sources were used without any intervention involving the participants; therefore, informed consent was not required. The Institutional Ethics Committee approved the study, and the information was handled confidentially in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Belmont Report [24, 25]. The database was anonymized and accessible only to authorized researchers.

Surgical management

Patients who developed FES exhibited a characteristic pattern of initial management: 93.9% required initial stabilization with a cast, and 77.7% were scheduled for early surgical fixation (within the first 24 h). The high frequency of these interventions reflects the standard management protocol for severe high-energy fractures, which are precisely those carrying the highest risk of developing this syndrome and represent the most appropriate therapeutic response.

Respiratory support and use of steroids

In patients diagnosed with FES, the Schonfeld index had a mean of 9.7 ± 2.9 (95% CI: 9.2–10.2), with a distribution consistent with normality (Shapiro-Wilk test, p = 0.52). The development of FES was associated with severe respiratory compromise that required advanced supportive interventions (Table 3). The need for invasive mechanical ventilation was significantly higher in the FES group (35.1% vs. 2.4%; p < 0.001), as was the use of high-flow nasal cannulas (83.1% vs. 15.4%; p < 0.001). Considering the use of either respiratory support modality, 93.9% of patients with FES required some type of ventilatory assistance compared to 16.8% in the control group (p < 0.001). Multivariate analysis of independent risk factors

The multivariable logistic regression model identified independent predictive factors for FES development. Femoral fractures showed the strongest association, quadrupling the risk of FES (aOR = 4.63; 95% CI: 2.99–7.22; p < 0.001). Early fixation (within the first 24 h) demonstrated a robust protective operareffect, reducing the risk by 58% (aOR = 0.42; 95% CI: 0.25–0.71; p < 0.001). Conversely, obesity was associated with a significant increase in risk (aOR = 2.63; 95% CI: 1.33–4.93; p = 0.004). Closed reduction, understood as initial stabilization with a cast before definitive surgical correction, was associated with a higher risk (aOR = 2.78; 95% CI: 1.32–6.42; p = 0.011), likely reflecting its preferential use in more severe fractures. Age showed a modest protective effect (aOR = 0.98 per year; p = 0.001), whereas variables such as rural origin and tibial fractures did not reach statistical significance in the adjusted model. Discussion

The present cohort, composed of 3,475 patients with long bone fractures, identifies 148 cases of FES for an incidence of 4.3%, a figure that falls within the range (0.8% to 23%) reported in recent systematic reviews [16], and which probably reflects the high proportion of high-energy traumas in our population. This series represents one of the largest institutional cohorts reported in the contemporary literature and provides highly valuable data for the Latin American context, where detailed epidemiological information is scarce. Although studies based on Anglo-Saxon national registries, such as the National Hospital Discharge Survey (NHDS), have documented numerically larger cohorts (approximately 41,000 cases) [26], these cover periods of decades and heterogeneous populations. Similarly, more recent cohorts derived from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) and the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) report between 451 and 7,479 cases, respectively [27, 28]. In light of this situation, the magnitude and clinical details of our cohort not only provide statistical strength to our findings but also offer up-to-date and detailed evidence on the risk factors and management of this complication.

Our results show that femur fracture is the main risk factor for FES (aOR = 4.63; 95% CI: 2.99–7.22), a robust finding consistent with the established dogma in the literature. This strong association is pathophysiologically attributed to the large volume of the medullary canal and fat content of the femur, which acts as a substantial reservoir for embolism [14]. A notable secondary finding is the high frequency of tibial fractures among FES cases (49%) and their marginally significant association in the adjusted model (aOR = 1.55; p = 0.052), reinforcing their established clinical relevance. Furthermore, our cohort included four cases of FES in patients with isolated humeral fractures. While this does not establish humerus as an independent statistical risk factor in our model, it aligns with rare but documented case reports of FES originating from isolated humeral trauma [29, 30]. In the context of the high-energy polytrauma predominant in our cohort, these observations raise the consideration that significant kinetic force may contribute to embolization from other long bones. Future research is warranted to determine if specific clinical scenarios or injury mechanisms might better define any potential risk associated with humeral fractures.

We propose that in the context of high-energy trauma from traffic accidents (the predominant mechanism in 97% of our cohort), the transferred kinetic force is sufficient to cause the release of bone marrow even in bones with a smaller medullary volume but that are equally vulnerable, such as the tibia and humerus [5, 13]. This expanded paradigm of osteological risk has direct implications for triaging and monitoring protocols.

The epidemiological analysis identified a clearly defined high-risk patient profile: young male victims of high-energy traffic accidents, with motorcycle accidents as the predominant mechanism (80% of cases). This profile not only reflects the epidemiology of trauma [17] but also highlights the role of the injury mechanism as the main trigger of FES. Obesity emerged as an independent risk factor of great clinical relevance (aOR = 2.63; 95%CI: 1.33–4.93). The biological plausibility of this association is supported by the pathophysiology of OSA, given that excess adipose tissue not only increases intramedullary pressure [6], but also creates a baseline proinflammatory and prothrombotic state [13] that could potentiate the dysregulated response to tissue trauma and the formation of fat emboli. The combination of these factors—a young, male, obese patient who is a victim of high-energy trauma—outlines a patient phenotype that should not only trigger intensive monitoring protocols but also prompt consideration of additional prophylactic interventions to prevent VTE.

The risk showed a cumulative effect on the outcome. Patients with multiple fractures exhibited an incidence of FES of 4.9%, which was higher than that observed in single fractures (4.6%). This finding reinforces the notion that patients with polytrauma constitute a very high-risk population that requires early comprehensive management [16, 17]. Beyond a simple cumulative effect, the risk of FES arises from the complex interaction between the anatomical burden of trauma (multiple fracture sites), release of systemic inflammatory mediators, and the patient’s baseline physiological vulnerability. The presence of multiple fractures likely increases the total embolic load released into circulation and critically amplifies the second-phase systemic inflammatory response of FES, although further studies are needed to confirm the synergy of these mechanisms [7]. This underscores the importance of considering the total burden of injuries in risk assessment beyond the isolated location of a fracture.

The severity of respiratory compromise in FES was devastating: 93.9% of affected patients required advanced ventilatory support, in stark contrast to only 16.8% in the control group (p < 0.001). Particularly revealing is that among patients with FES, 83.1% needed high-flow nasal cannula and 35.1% required invasive mechanical ventilation, with significant overlap (36 patients required both modalities). This burden of respiratory support not only reflects the acute respiratory distress syndrome characteristic of FES but also explains the high percentage of ICU admissions and the substantial impact on morbidity and hospital costs [8].

The severity of this respiratory compromise, which explains the high percentage of ICU admissions, was objectively confirmed by a Schonfeld index of 9.7 ± 2.9, which not only quantifies respiratory impairment but also represents a finding of significant practical value. This value consistently exceeds the diagnostic cutoff point of five points [31], provides objective validation of the established clinical criteria, and serves as a quantifiable marker of severity. The strong correlation between this elevated index and the need for advanced ventilatory support (93.9% vs. 16.8% in controls) suggests its potential prognostic value for identifying patients who will require management in intensive care units. This objectification of respiratory severity reinforces the need for close monitoring in high-risk patients and provides a measurable parameter for assessing therapeutic responses in future studies.

The in-hospital mortality rate of 5.4% in patients with FES, although comparable to previous series [9, 11, 22], represents only the tip of the iceberg of the disease burden. Survivors often face prolonged rehabilitation and secondary disability due to long-term respiratory and neurological sequelae [10, 21], an often-underestimated aspect of the disease. The costs associated with managing these patients exponentially increase the resources required compared to those with fractures without these complications.

The most significant finding of our study is the powerful protective effect of early fracture fixation (< 24 h), which was associated with a substantially reduced odds of FES (aOR = 0.42; 95%CI: 0.25–0.71; p < 0.001). This finding is further contextualized by the significantly increased risk associated with initial cast stabilization (aOR = 2.78), a common temporary measure. The clinical implication of this contrast is clear: early definitive fixation within 24 h was associated with a 58% reduction in the odds of FES, while initial cast stabilization was linked to a nearly 3-fold increase in risk. This powerful contrast underscores that the window of time before definitive surgery represents a critical period of modifiable risk. While our study design reflects standard clinical practice where definitive fixation is the goal for all patients, the clear inverse relationship between the timing of fixation and FES risk provides compelling evidence that the early hours following trauma are a critical window for intervention. Our results provide high-level observational support for the principle of Early Definitive Care and suggest that the greatest benefit is achieved when fixation is accomplished within the first 24 h [12, 18].

However, it is crucial to clarify that the relationship between surgical duration and FES is not universal but rather dependent on the patient’s baseline clinical status. Our analysis, after controlling for critical variables, suggests that the benefit of early fixation is clearer in hemodynamically stabilized patients. In scenarios of extreme physiological instability, the intrinsic risk of the patient may mask this protective effect. Therefore, early fixation should be considered as the goal of an ongoing effort in management that first prioritizes the resuscitation and stabilization of polytrauma patients. The finding associated with initial stabilization with a cast (aOR = 2.78; 95%CI: 1.32–6.42; p = 0.011) corroborates that this procedure, while necessary, is an insufficient measure; patients require early definitive fixation to effectively reduce the risk of FES. This result indirectly reinforces the value of orthopedic damage control strategies in unstable patients [18].

The role of corticosteroids in this context remains unclear. Although their use was more frequent in the FES group (24% vs. 2.7%), our difficulty in determining the precise indication (rescue therapy vs. prophylaxis) and the exact timing of administration prevented us from evaluating their potential effect. This fundamental methodological limitation (confounding by indication bias) highlights the inherent difficulty in resolving this debate in observational studies. Although recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses suggest a promising prophylactic effect [19, 20], its routine implementation still requires support from prospective randomized clinical trials that define standardized administration protocols, establish optimal doses, and determine the ideal timing of administration in the high-risk populations identified here.

While the size of the cohort and the rigor of the analysis lend strength to our results, some limitations must be acknowledged. The observational nature of this study limits the ability to establish causality and does not rule out the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. Additionally, although clinical and surgical information were collected systematically, certain aspects, such as the exact timing of corticosteroid administration or variability in surgical technique among the treatment teams, could not be fully controlled. These considerations do not invalidate the findings but underscore the need for prospective multicenter studies to confirm and expand upon the associations identified.

Our findings have immediate and multi-level practical implications for the management of trauma. First, in triage and risk assessment, protocols must evolve to systematically include all long bone fractures (tibia and humerus) caused by high-energy trauma in FES risk stratification and not just those located in the femur. Identifying a high-risk patient profile (young male, obese, victim of a motorcycle accident) should trigger clinical alerts. Second, in surgical management, early fixation (< 24 h) in hemodynamically stable patients must be prioritized and standardized as the primary and most effective prevention strategy. Third, for unstable patients, a mandatory protocol of close respiratory monitoring (possibly in an intermediate care unit) should be implemented while the patient is resuscitated, with the explicit plan to achieve early definitive stabilization using damage control techniques [18] as soon as their physiological condition allows, turning the closed reduction from an endpoint into a transitional step. From a public health perspective, these results reinforce the crucial importance of comprehensive road-accident prevention strategies, given their overwhelming predominance as an underlying cause.

In conclusion, this study expands the etiological landscape of FES to include the tibia and humerus in the context of high-energy trauma, outlines a high-risk patient phenotype, and provides high-level evidence that consolidates early fixation as a fundamental preventive intervention for FES. Our findings advocate for a paradigm shift from a reactive, femur-centered approach to a proactive and comprehensive one based on the trauma mechanism and aimed at achieving prompt surgical stabilization of all long bone fractures. This proactive approach, based on the evidence presented in this study, has the potential to reduce the incidence of this devastating complication significantly.
