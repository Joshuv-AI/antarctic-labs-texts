Preoperative Embolization of a Spinal Metastasis With Obsidio Conformable Embolic: A Case Report
Authors: Aaron Higginbotham, Kevin Le, Lane Fry, Ari Williams, Frank A. De Stefano, Ernest J. Madarang, Koji Ebersole, Jeremy Peterson
Published: 2026

Metastatic spinal tumors often present with pain and neurological compromise due to cord compression. Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is characteristically hypervascular and commonly metastasizes to the spine,1 for which preoperative embolization is an established adjunct that can decrease operative blood loss and improve visualization.2,3 Obsidio Conformable Embolic (OCE), a shear-thinning hydrogel approved for peripheral embolization, offers conformable filling and nonadhesive properties that permit catheter retrieval.4,5 We report the first neurovascular application of OCE for embolization of a spinal metastasis.

Case Report

A patient in their 70s with prior clear cell RCC presented with worsening back and chest pain. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated a destructive T5 vertebral body mass with severe spinal canal compromise. Biopsy confirmed metastatic clear cell RCC, and magnetic resonance imaging showed marked cord compression (Figure [A and B]). Given the lesion’s hypervascularity and need for urgent decompression, preoperative embolization was performed.

Right common femoral artery access was obtained, and a 5F Cobra catheter was used for thoracic segmental angiography. Tumor blood supply originated from the right T4-T5 segmental arteries, with a mild contribution from T6. Vessel diameters measured 2 to 3 mm. A PX Slim microcatheter (0.025”, 0.635-mm internal diameter; Penumbra) was advanced under roadmap guidance. Radiculomedullary arteries (including the artery of Adamkiewicz) were assessed, given the risk of anterior spinal compromise; none were identified at T4 to T6.6

At T4, a significant tumor blush was observed (Figure [C]). Embolization began with Embosphere particles (300–500/500–700 µm; Merit Medical Systems), producing a substantial reduction in blush. OCE (Boston Scientific) was then infused slowly to complete vessel occlusion (Figure [D through F]). Because OCE is nonadhesive, the microcatheter was withdrawn and placed on a sterile table for reuse at the operator’s discretion. T5 embolization proceeded similarly: particle embolization was performed using a new PX Slim catheter, followed by OCE delivered through the previously used OCE-containing microcatheter, resulting in complete devascularization (Figure [G through I]). At T6, which demonstrated only mild tumor supply, OCE alone was delivered through the same microcatheter (Figure [J through L]). Completion angiography confirmed full occlusion of targeted vessels without reflux or nontarget embolization.

The following day, the patient underwent T4 to T6 laminectomy, tumor excision, and posterior spinal fusion (estimated blood loss, 500 mL). Postoperatively, the patient developed right lower-extremity weakness and paresthesia. Computed tomography angiography of the head/neck and magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed no stroke. Thoracic magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated no cord infarction or unintended arterial occlusion but persistent severe cord compression. A postoperative hematoma was evacuated; however, the patient developed progressive bilateral lower-extremity weakness and urinary retention with unchanged severe compression at T5 (Figure [M and N]). Although imaging showed no infarct, embolization-related ischemia cannot be fully excluded. Due to disease progression and declining functional status, the patient elected comfort-focused care.

Discussion

Preoperative embolization of hypervascular spinal metastases is a well-established strategy to mitigate surgical bleeding and improve operative conditions, traditionally using particles, coils, cyanoacrylates, and dimethyl sulfoxide–based liquid agents.7–10 OCE is a shear-thinning hydrogel that flows under injection and reforms into a solid in situ, enabling controlled delivery and durable occlusion.4,5 This may be advantageous in high-stakes anatomy where nontarget embolization is a concern. Its nonadhesive profile allowed microcatheter retrieval and sequential reuse, via direct roadmap guidance without microwire access. In addition, its radiopacity facilitates fluoroscopic visualization.9

Preclinical data suggest that OCE provides hemostatic efficacy comparable to coils with deliverability akin to liquid embolics.11 In peripheral applications, Pal et al5 reported 100% technical success across 16 procedures and Ahmed et al4 in 131 embolizations though neither series included spinal or neurovascular cases. Therefore, these results cannot be generalized to spinal segmental arteries, where inadvertent radiculomedullary occlusion can cause catastrophic ischemia, and collateral redundancy is limited. Both series provide technical parameters relevant to neurointerventional use: OCE is supplied premixed (1-mL syringe) and compatible with microcatheters ≥0.021” (0.533 mm), delivered slowly under continuous fluoroscopy.4,5

In this case, particle embolization was used initially at T4 and T5, consistent with standard practice for RCC metastases.8,9,12 OCE served as an adjunctive proximal pedicle embolization to eliminate residual blush after particle deposition, enabling further flow reduction. At T6, OCE alone achieved pedicle embolization. Prior series of embolized RCC spinal metastases report median intraoperative blood loss of 1500 (range, 300–8000) mL,12 while a recent meta-analysis of 1134 embolized cases demonstrated a mean reduction of ≈284 mL versus nonembolized controls, primarily in hypervascular tumors.7 Thus, the 500-mL blood loss observed in this case lies at the lower end of the reported range though this cannot be attributed to OCE alone due to multimodal embolization.

Postoperative neurological decline raised concern for ischemia; however, imaging showed no infarct or nontarget embolization. Nevertheless, the absence of imaging abnormalities cannot exclude microvascular injury or transient flow compromise. Persistent, severe spinal cord compression from infiltrative metastatic disease most plausibly explained symptom progression, and the patient elected comfort care.

This first reported use of OCE for spinal metastasis embolization demonstrates feasibility in neurovascular anatomy. Further investigation is needed to define safety and comparative outcomes versus traditional agents.
