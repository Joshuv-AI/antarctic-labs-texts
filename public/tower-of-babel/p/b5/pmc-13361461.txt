Determinants of Spousal Loneliness Among Caregivers for Patients with Proliferative Diabetic Retinopathy: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Tingting Wang, Zheyi Cai, Xueqiao Wei, Wei Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveProliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), the most severe form of diabetic retinopathy, imposes overwhelming caregiving demands on family caregivers, with spouses typically assuming the primary caregiving role. However, their psychosocial well-being, particularly loneliness—a major modifiable risk factor for adverse health outcomes—remains severely understudied. Guided by the relational model of stress, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of loneliness and identify its multilevel determinants among spousal caregivers of patients with PDR.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, a convenience sample of 239 spousal caregivers of PDR patients was recruited from four tertiary hospitals in Changsha, China, between March and August 2024. Participants completed validated standardized instruments measuring loneliness (6-item Short-Form UCLA Loneliness Scale, ULS-6), psychological resilience (Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, CD-RISC), dyadic coping (Dyadic Coping Inventory, DCI), and caregiver burden (Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview, ZBI). Hierarchical linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the independent associations of individual, environmental, and behavioral factors with loneliness.ResultsThe mean ULS-6 score was 14.89 ± 3.77, indicating a moderate level of loneliness among spousal caregivers; 10.0% reported severe loneliness. The final hierarchical regression model explained 41.6% of the variance in loneliness. Significant independent risk factors included severe patient cognitive impairment (β = 0.202, P = 0.004), longer disease duration (β = 0.177, P = 0.003), greater visual impairment (β = 0.349, P = 0.004), older caregiver age (β = 0.178, P = 0.011), living alone with the patient (β = 0.057, P = 0.008), poorer marital relationship quality (β = 0.355, P = 0.003), and higher caregiver burden (β = 0.242, P = 0.009). Conversely, higher psychological resilience (β = −0.217, P = 0.009) and more effective dyadic coping (β = −0.159, P < 0.001) were significant protective factors.ConclusionLoneliness is highly prevalent among spousal caregivers of PDR patients, driven by a complex interplay of patient illness severity, caregiver demographics, environmental conditions, and modifiable psychosocial resources. Couple-centered interventions targeting dyadic coping enhancement and psychological resilience building may effectively reduce loneliness and improve the overall well-being of this vulnerable caregiver population.Keywords: diabetic retinopathy, proliferative, caregivers, spouses, loneliness, resilience, psychological, dyadic coping, burden of care

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus has become a global public health crisis, with an estimated 537 million adults living with diabetes worldwide in 2021—a number projected to surge to 783 million by 2045.1 Diabetic retinopathy (DR), one of the most common and devastating microvascular complications of diabetes, affects approximately 34.6% of all diabetic patients globally, with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR)—the most advanced and vision-threatening stage—accounting for 7.5% of DR cases.1 PDR is characterized by progressive, irreversible retinal neovascularization and fibrosis, leading to severe visual impairment and even blindness in up to 50% of untreated patients within 5 years of diagnosis.1

The profound functional disability caused by PDR renders patients highly dependent on family caregivers for daily living, with spouses overwhelmingly serving as the primary unpaid caregivers.2 These spousal caregivers bear a multifaceted burden, including direct physical care, financial strain, household management, and emotional support, while simultaneously coping with the psychological distress of witnessing their partner’s gradual loss of vision and independence.3

The chronic and intensive nature of PDR caregiving places spousal caregivers at significantly elevated risk for adverse psychological outcomes, including anxiety, depression, and burnout.4 Among these, loneliness—defined as the distressing subjective experience of a discrepancy between desired and actual social relationships5—has emerged as a particularly insidious and underrecognized problem. Loneliness is not a trivial emotional state; it is an established independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease, immune dysfunction, cognitive decline, and premature mortality, with health risks comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day.6,7

While loneliness has been extensively investigated in caregivers of patients with dementia,8 cancer,9 and stroke, research focusing specifically on spousal caregivers of PDR patients remains extremely scarce. A systematic search of the literature revealed only a handful of studies examining general psychological distress in DR caregivers, with no published studies to date systematically investigating loneliness or its determinants in this population. This is a critical gap, as the unique trajectory of PDR—characterized by gradual, progressive vision loss and increasing patient dependency—creates a distinct context for caregiver loneliness. As the patient’s ability to participate in shared activities, social interactions, and communication declines, the spouse’s social world often contracts in parallel, leading to profound social isolation and the loss of the companionship that is central to marital relationships.5

The relational model of stress provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for understanding the multilevel determinants of caregiver loneliness in the context of chronic illness.10 This model posits that psychological adaptation to a chronic stressor (such as a partner’s PDR diagnosis) is not determined by a single factor but by the dynamic interaction of three interrelated domains: (1) Individual factors: Including patient illness characteristics (eg., disease severity, cognitive function) and caregiver demographics (eg., age, education), which shape the baseline demands of caregiving; (2) Environmental factors: Such as living arrangements, social support networks, and the quality of the marital relationship, which provide external resources to buffer stress; (3) Behavioral factors: Including coping strategies, caregiving burden, and psychosocial resources, which reflect how caregivers actively respond to caregiving demands.

According to this model, loneliness arises when the cumulative demands of caregiving exceed the caregiver’s available resources across these three domains. While previous studies in other caregiver populations have examined associations between loneliness and individual factors (eg., patient disease severity) or single psychosocial variables (eg., caregiver burden), no studies have integrated all three domains of the relational model of stress to examine loneliness in PDR spousal caregivers. In particular, the roles of two critical modifiable psychosocial resources—psychological resilience (the ability to adapt positively to adversity) and dyadic coping (the couple’s collaborative efforts to manage stress)—remain unexplored in this population. Understanding how these factors interact to influence loneliness is essential for developing effective, targeted interventions to support PDR spousal caregivers.

Therefore, guided by the relational model of stress, this cross-sectional study aimed to: (1) evaluate the prevalence and severity of loneliness among spousal caregivers of patients with PDR; and (2) identify independent determinants of loneliness across individual, environmental, and behavioral dimensions, with a particular emphasis on the protective roles of psychological resilience and dyadic coping, as well as the risk role of caregiver burden.

Methods

Study Design

This cross-sectional study was conducted and reported in strict accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.11 A multi-center facility-based convenience sampling approach was employed to recruit participants from four tertiary hospitals in Changsha, Hunan Province, China, between March 1 and August 31, 2024.

Study Sites

The study was conducted at four tertiary-level hospitals in Changsha, representing the major referral centers for diabetic eye disease in central Hunan Province: (1) The First Hospital of Hunan University of Chinese Medicine (endocrinology and ophthalmology departments) (2) Hunan Provincial People’s Hospital (endocrinology department) (3) Central South University Xiangya Second Hospital (ophthalmology department) (4) Changsha Central Hospital (endocrinology department).These hospitals serve a diverse patient population spanning urban, suburban, and rural areas of Hunan Province, with socioeconomic characteristics ranging from low-income to high-income groups. Under China’s basic medical insurance system, over 95% of local residents have access to basic healthcare coverage, but formal long-term care services for visually impaired patients remain limited, resulting in family members (predominantly spouses) bearing the vast majority of caregiving responsibilities.

Study Participants

Inclusion Criteria

(1) Being the legal spouse of a patient diagnosed with PDR according to the 2022 Chinese Medical Association clinical practice guidelines for diabetic retinopathy12 (2) Self-identifying as the primary unpaid caregiver, defined as providing ≥40 hours of care per week and having performed this role for a minimum of 3 consecutive months prior to study enrollment (3) Aged ≥ 18 years (4) Able to understand and complete the questionnaire (with assistance if needed) (5) Provided written informed consent.

Exclusion Criteria

(1) Having a severe physical illness (eg., terminal cancer, end-stage renal disease) or documented psychiatric disorder that would preclude survey completion (2) Having a diagnosis of cognitive impairment (Mini-Mental State Examination score <24) (3) The patient having a concurrent diagnosis of another severe chronic disease requiring intensive care (eg., advanced dementia, stroke with hemiplegia) (4) Participating in another psychosocial intervention study during the same period.

Sampling Procedure and Site Distribution

Eligible patients were first identified by attending endocrinologists and ophthalmologists at each study site. Trained research nurses then approached the accompanying spouses to explain the study purpose and procedures. All participants who met the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent were enrolled consecutively at each site.

The distribution of participants across the four study sites was as follows:

Site 1: 62 participants (25.9%); Site 2: 58 participants (24.3%) Site 3: 60 participants (25.1%) Site 4: 59 participants (24.7%).

Sampling Considerations: This multi-center convenience sampling approach was chosen due to practical constraints in accessing the broader population of PDR spousal caregivers. While this method may introduce some selection bias (eg., over-representation of patients who regularly attend tertiary hospitals), the inclusion of four geographically and institutionally diverse sites enhances the generalizability of our findings compared to a single-center study. Limitations regarding representativeness are further discussed in the Limitations section.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size was calculated specifically for hierarchical multiple linear regression analysis, which requires a minimum of 10 participants per independent variable to ensure stable parameter estimates.13 The full regression model included 23 independent variables (5 individual factors, 2 environmental factors, 3 psychosocial factors, and 13 demographic/clinical covariates), requiring a minimum sample size of 230 participants.

To account for an estimated 8% rate of invalid or incomplete questionnaires, we planned to distribute 250 questionnaires in total. A total of 250 questionnaires were distributed, and 239 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding a response rate of 95.6%, which exceeded the required sample size.

Data Collection Procedure

All research nurses involved in data collection completed a standardized 8-hour training program on study procedures, questionnaire administration, and ethical considerations prior to study initiation.

Prior to invitation, each eligible spouse was informed that participation is entirely voluntary and that declining or withdrawing will not affect the patient’s medical care in any way. After this explanation, written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Eligible spouses were invited to complete the questionnaire in a private, quiet room at the hospital to ensure confidentiality and minimize distractions. For participants with visual difficulties or low literacy levels, the research nurse read each question aloud in a neutral tone and recorded the participant’s verbal responses verbatim.

The time taken to complete each questionnaire was prospectively recorded by research nurses using a stopwatch, starting when the participant began reading the first question and ending when the last question was completed. For participants who required assistance with reading, the time was recorded from the start of the first question to the end of the last response. The average completion time was 25–30 minutes (range: 18–42 minutes).

After questionnaire completion, research nurses reviewed all responses for completeness and consistency. Any missing or ambiguous responses were clarified with the participant immediately.

Measures

All study instruments were administered in Chinese. For instruments originally developed in English, standard translation and back-translation procedures were followed, and cultural adaptation was performed to ensure suitability for the Chinese population. All instruments have been previously validated in Chinese samples, as detailed below.

General Information Questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire collected sociodemographic and clinical data on both the patient and the spouse. Patient variables included age, education level, monthly family income, cognitive impairment, disease duration, and visual impairment (revised from “visual decline” to conform to standard ophthalmological terminology). Spouse variables included age, sex, education level, living arrangement, marital relationship quality, and level of disease understanding.

Cognitive impairment: Assessed using a single-item Likert scale completed by the spouse: “How would you rate the patient’s current memory and cognitive ability?” (1=severe impairment, 2=moderate impairment, 3=mild impairment, 4=no impairment).

Visual impairment: Defined based on the patient’s most recent best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) measurement from medical records, categorized as: 1=severe visual impairment (BCVA < 0.05), 2=moderate visual impairment (0.05 ≤ BCVA < 0.3), 3=mild or no visual impairment (BCVA ≥ 0.3).

Living arrangement: Categorized as “living with the patient only” or “living with the patient and other family members (eg., children, parents)”.

Marital relationship quality: Assessed using a single-item Likert scale: “How would you rate the quality of your marital relationship over the past 6 months?” (1=dysfunctional, 2=general, 3=intimate and high quality).

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured using the 6-item Short-Form UCLA Loneliness Scale (ULS-6),14 which was translated into Chinese and validated by Li15 in 2012. The translation process followed standard forward-backward translation procedures: two independent bilingual translators translated the original English scale into Chinese, a third translator back-translated it into English, and a committee of five nursing psychology experts reviewed both versions to ensure semantic equivalence and cultural appropriateness.

The ULS-6 contains 6 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1=“never” to 4=“always”). Total scores range from 6 to 24, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness. In Chinese populations, scores are typically interpreted as: <12 = minimal loneliness, 12–18 = moderate loneliness, >18 = severe loneliness. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.809).

Resilience

Resilience was measured using the 25-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) Chinese version.16,17 The scale was translated using forward-backward translation and underwent extensive cultural adaptation to reflect Chinese cultural values of collectivism and family support.

The CD-RISC consists of three dimensions:

① Tenacity (13 items): Measures perseverance and adaptability in the face of adversity

② Strength (8 items): Measures confidence in one’s ability to cope with stress

③ Optimism (4 items): Measures positive outlook and hope for the future.

Items are rated on a 5-point scale (0=“not true at all” to 4=“true nearly all the time”). Total scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater resilience. In Chinese samples, scores <50 indicate low resilience, 50–70 indicate moderate resilience, and >70 indicate high resilience. Cronbach’s α for this study was 0.851.

Dyadic Coping

Dyadic coping was measured using the 37-item Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI) Chinese version.18,19 The translation and cultural adaptation process involved input from both Chinese and international experts in couple psychology to ensure that items accurately reflected dyadic coping behaviors in Chinese marital relationships.

The DCI Consists of Six Dimensions

① Stress Communication (8 items): Measures how partners communicate about stress. ② Supportive Dyadic Coping (10 items): Measures emotional and instrumental support between partners. ③ Delegated Dyadic Coping (4 items): Measures how partners take over responsibilities to reduce each other’s stress. ④ Common Dyadic Coping (5 items): Measures joint problem-solving and shared activities. ⑤ Negative Dyadic Coping (8 items): Measures ineffective or hostile coping behaviors. ⑥ Dyadic Coping Evaluation (2 items): Measures overall satisfaction with dyadic coping.

Items are rated on a 5-point scale (1=“very seldom” to 5=“very often”). Total scores range from 37 to 185, with higher scores indicating more effective dyadic coping. Cronbach’s α for this study was 0.879.

Caregiver Burden

Caregiver burden was measured using the 22-item Zarit Caregiver Burden Interview (ZBI) Chinese version.20,21 The scale was culturally adapted to reflect the Chinese context of family caregiving, with minor modifications to items related to social activities and family roles.

The ZBI consists of two dimensions:

① Personal Strain (12 items): Measures emotional and physical stress experienced by the caregiver

② Role Strain (6 items): Measures conflicts between caregiving and other life roles.

Items are rated on a 5-point scale (0=“never” to 4=“nearly always”). Total scores range from 0 to 88, with higher scores indicating greater burden. In Chinese caregiver populations, scores are interpreted as: 0–20 = no or mild burden, 21–40 = moderate burden, 41–88 = severe burden. Cronbach’s α for this study was 0.871.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Preliminary Assumption Testing

Prior to conducting inferential statistics, the following assumptions were tested:

① Normality: Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All variables (ULS-6, CD-RISC, DCI, ZBI scores) approximated a normal distribution (all P > 0.05). ② Homogeneity of variance: Levene’s test was used to assess variance homogeneity for t-tests and ANOVA. No significant violations were found (all P > 0.05). ③ Multicollinearity: Variance inflation factor (VIF) was calculated for all variables in the regression model. All VIF values were < 3, indicating no significant multicollinearity.

Descriptive and Inferential Statistics

Descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, frequencies, percentages) were calculated for all sociodemographic, clinical, and psychosocial variables.

Independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare ULS-6 scores across categorical variables. Post-hoc Tukey’s tests were performed for significant ANOVA results.

Pearson’s correlation coefficients were calculated to examine bivariate associations between continuous variables.

Hierarchical multiple linear regression was performed to identify independent factors associated with loneliness, with variables entered in three blocks guided by the relational model of stress: (1) Model 1: Individual factors (patient cognitive impairment, disease duration, visual impairment, spouse age, spouse education level). (2) Model 2: Environmental factors (living arrangement, marital relationship quality), added to Model 1. (3) Model 3: Behavioral factors (resilience, dyadic coping, caregiver burden), added to Model 2. The change in R2 (ΔR2) was assessed at each step to evaluate the incremental contribution of each block of variables.

Results

Participant Characteristics and Univariate Analysis of Loneliness

A total of 250 questionnaires were distributed, and 239 valid questionnaires were returned, yielding a response rate of 95.6%. All participants were Han Chinese, the predominant ethnic group in Hunan Province, China. The missing rate of individual items in the questionnaire ranged from 0.8% to 2.1%, with an overall missing rate of less than 2%. Complete case analysis was employed, excluding questionnaires with any missing responses, resulting in 239 participants included in the final analysis.

Among caregiver characteristics, older age (F=12.960, P<0.001, Cramer’s V=0.318), lower education level (F=8.573, P=0.003, Cramer’s V=0.261), living alone with the patient (t=−9.044, P<0.001, Phi=0.503), and poorer marital relationship quality (F=7.330, P<0.001, Cramer’s V=0.245) were also significantly associated with higher loneliness.

Loneliness

The mean total loneliness score was 14.89 ± 3.77 (range: 6–24), indicating a moderate level of loneliness overall. Of the participants, 34 (14.2%) reported minimal loneliness (score <12), 181 (75.7%) reported moderate loneliness (12–18), and 24 (10.0%) reported severe loneliness (>18). The mean item score was 2.24 ± 0.63.

Resilience

The mean total resilience score was 58.51 ± 8.79 (range: 25–87), indicating a moderate level of resilience. Among the three dimensions, the strength dimension had the highest mean item score (2.65 ± 0.66), followed by optimism (2.30 ± 0.80) and tenacity (2.20 ± 0.46). The tenacity dimension contributed the most to the total resilience score (48.9%), followed by strength (36.2%) and optimism (15.7%).

Dyadic Coping

The mean total dyadic coping score was 101.66 ± 16.54 (range: 58–152). Among the six dimensions, common dyadic coping had the highest mean item score (3.06 ± 0.47), followed by supportive dyadic coping (3.01 ± 0.23) and dyadic coping evaluation (3.10 ± 0.65). Negative dyadic coping had a mean item score of 2.76 ± 0.28, while stress communication had the lowest mean item score (2.30 ± 0.26).

Caregiver Burden

The mean total caregiver burden score was 58.20 ± 12.49 (range: 22–82), indicating a moderate to high level of burden. Role strain had a higher mean item score (3.20 ± 0.67) compared to personal strain (2.24 ± 0.58), suggesting that conflicts between caregiving and other life roles were the primary source of burden for participants.

In the final model (Model 3), all variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below 3, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The full model explained 41.6% of the variance in loneliness (Adjusted R2 = 0.384, F = 12.65, P < 0.001). The inclusion of environmental factors (Model 2) significantly improved the model over individual factors alone (ΔR2 = 0.068, P < 0.001), as did the addition of behavioral factors (Model 3, ΔR2 = 0.065, P < 0.001).

The strongest independent predictors of greater loneliness were poorer marital relationship quality (β = 0.355, P = 0.003) and greater patient visual impairment (β = 0.349, P = 0.004). Other significant individual risk factors included more severe patient cognitive impairment (β = 0.202, P = 0.004), longer disease duration (β = 0.177, P = 0.003), and older caregiver age (β = 0.178, P = 0.011). Living alone with the patient remained a significant environmental risk factor (β = 0.057, P = 0.008).

Regarding behavioral factors, higher resilience (β = −0.217, P = 0.009) and more effective dyadic coping (β = −0.159, P < 0.001) were significantly associated with lower loneliness, while greater caregiver burden (β = 0.242, P = 0.009) was associated with higher loneliness. Caregiver education level was not a significant predictor in the final model.

Novelty of the Results

This study represents the first systematic investigation of loneliness and its multilevel determinants among spousal caregivers of patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) in the Chinese population. The key novel findings include:

Discussion

This study is among the first to systematically examine loneliness and its determinants in spouses of patients with PDR, a uniquely vulnerable and understudied caregiver population. Our findings indicate that moderate to high levels of loneliness are prevalent, affecting nearly 90% of spouses. Crucially, the results strongly support the relational model of stress, demonstrating that loneliness is shaped by the dynamic interplay of individual factors (patient illness characteristics and caregiver demographics), environmental factors (living arrangements and marital relationship quality), and behavioral factors (resilience, dyadic coping, and caregiver burden). These findings align closely with our study objectives and provide important insights into the psychosocial needs of PDR caregivers globally.

The PDR-Specific Context of Loneliness: Cross-Cultural Comparisons with Broader Visual Impairment Caregiving Literature

The high prevalence of loneliness in our cohort (mean ULS-6 = 14.89) is comparable to levels reported in caregivers of patients with Alzheimer’s disease9 and enterostomy,22 confirming that caregiving for a chronic, debilitating condition is a significant risk factor for loneliness. However, our study highlights factors unique to the PDR context. The progressive and often unpredictable nature of vision loss was a dominant theme. Patient visual impairment emerged as one of the strongest predictors of spouse loneliness (β = 0.349). This finding resonates with research from diverse cultural settings.

From a mechanistic perspective, Moon et al23 demonstrated in a US longitudinal study that visual impairment is associated with cognitive decline, and this relationship is partially mediated by depression, loneliness, and lower social activity. Their work establishes loneliness as both a consequence of visual impairment and a pathway to further adverse outcomes—a bidirectional dynamic that likely operates in spousal caregivers as well. As the patient’s vision deteriorates, the spouse’s social world contracts, loneliness increases, and the cumulative stress may impair the caregiver’s own cognitive and emotional resilience.

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Pur et al24 synthesized evidence from multiple countries and found that 65% of family caregivers of patients with eye diseases report significant caregiver burden, with emotional distress and social isolation being common consequences. Although loneliness was not directly measured in all included studies, the review concluded that reduced social participation and loss of shared activities are key mechanisms linking patient visual impairment to caregiver psychological morbidity—a pathway consistent with our findings.

In Australia, Jin et al25 conducted a cross-sectional study of family carers of persons with age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and found that over 50% reported mild-to-moderate burden and more than 20% had depressive symptoms. Importantly, they identified patient dependency as the strongest predictor of caregiver burden (OR = 8.42). While their study did not specifically assess loneliness, the high prevalence of burden and depression suggests that loneliness likely co-occurs, as our study demonstrates. The Australian context shares similarities with our Chinese setting in terms of reliance on family-based care, highlighting the cross-cultural relevance of patient dependency as a driver of caregiver distress.

From Europe (United Kingdom), Vasileiou et al26 conducted a qualitative investigation of loneliness among informal caregivers. Through 16 semi-structured interviews, they identified three core themes: (1) contracted personal space and reduced social interactions, (2) relational deprivation and loss (eg., loss of conversation and shared interests with the care recipient), and (3) feelings of powerlessness and helplessness. Notably, spousal caregivers constituted the majority of their sample. Their findings directly mirror our quantitative results, particularly the strong protective effect of marital relationship quality (β = 0.355) and the risk associated with living alone with the patient. The relational deprivation theme—loss of companionship—is precisely what our measure of loneliness captured, and it aligns with the reduced dyadic coping we observed.

In the United States, Varadaraj et al27 analyzed data from the National Study of Caregiving and found that caregivers of older adults with self-reported vision impairment provided 36% more care hours per month compared to caregivers of those without vision impairment (111 ± 9.1 vs. 81.6 ± 6.2 hours), and had a 46% increased odds of experiencing significant emotional difficulty (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.04–2.03). Their large, population-based study reinforces our finding that visual impairment severity is a major determinant of caregiver loneliness, likely through the mechanism of increased caregiving time and emotional strain.

From Africa, Mashala et al28 conducted a cross-sectional study in the rural Limpopo province of South Africa among 253 informal caregivers of visually impaired individuals. They reported that 29.2% of caregivers had moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms and 28.1% had mild-to-moderate symptoms. Unmarried caregivers had higher odds of depression (OR = 2.3), and low household income was strongly associated with severe depressive symptoms (OR = 18.1). Although loneliness was not directly measured, depression and loneliness are closely correlated, and their findings highlight the additional vulnerability of caregivers with limited socioeconomic resources and those without the buffering effect of a spouse—consistent with our observation that living alone with the patient (without other family members) is a risk factor.

Taken together, these cross-cultural studies confirm that visual impairment in a family member consistently leads to caregiver psychological distress across high-income Western countries (Australia, UK, US) and low- and middle-income settings (South Africa, China). However, no prior study in any of these contexts has specifically examined the independent contributions of dyadic coping and resilience to loneliness in this population. Our findings add novel, modifiable targets that transcend cultural boundaries, while also inviting future cross-cultural validation.

Factors Influencing Loneliness According to the Relational Model of Stress

A key finding of this study is the independent protective role of resilience and dyadic coping, even after accounting for the considerable burden of care. According to the relational model of stress,11 loneliness arises when caregiving demands exceed available resources across individual, environmental, and behavioral domains. Our results operationalize this framework: resilience represents an individual-level behavioral resource that facilitates positive adaptation; dyadic coping captures the couple-level behavioral process of collaborative stress management.

Higher resilience (β = −0.217) was associated with lower loneliness. Resilient individuals likely possess better emotional regulation and problem-solving skills, enabling them to reframe their situation and maintain a sense of purpose and connection despite their circumstances.29 Within the relational model, resilience can be viewed as a key personal resource that buffers the impact of chronic stressors on psychological outcomes. This is consistent with the qualitative observations of Vasileiou et al,26 who noted that caregivers who maintained a sense of agency and found meaning in their role reported lower levels of loneliness.

More importantly, the significant negative association between dyadic coping and loneliness (β = −0.159, P < 0.001) underscores the importance of the couple as a unit in managing the PDR journey. Dyadic coping, which includes supportive behaviors, joint problem-solving, and delegated tasks, may enhance feelings of “we-ness” and perceived partner support.30 In the face of a shared stressor like PDR, couples who cope together are more likely to maintain emotional synchronization and a sense of shared reality, directly counteracting the feelings of isolation that define loneliness.31 From the perspective of the relational model, effective dyadic coping strengthens the environmental and relational resources available to the caregiver, thereby reducing the discrepancy between desired and actual social connectedness. This finding is clinically significant because dyadic coping is a modifiable skill that can be targeted through couple-based interventions.32 Healthcare providers should move beyond an individual-focused approach and consider assessing and enhancing the couple’s capacity to cope together.

Environmental and Individual Vulnerabilities

Consistent with prior research, environmental factors played a crucial role. Living alone with the patient was a significant risk factor (β = 0.057, P = 0.008), as these spouses lack the buffering effect of other household members for social interaction and practical support.33 Conversely, a higher-quality marital relationship was the strongest protective factor in our model (β = 0.355, P = 0.003). A strong, intimate relationship may provide an internal reservoir of emotional support that mitigates the loneliness arising from external social withdrawal. Older spouse age (β = 0.178, P = 0.011) was also a risk factor, likely reflecting age-related declines in physical stamina, shrinking social networks, and potentially fewer resources to cope with the intense demands of caregiving.10

Caregiver Burden as a Mediator?

Finally, greater caregiver burden was independently associated with higher loneliness (β = 0.242, P = 0.009). This aligns with the understanding that a heavy burden can be all-consuming, leaving little time or energy for social engagement and leading to exhaustion and a diminished sense of self.34–36 Within the relational model of stress, caregiver burden can be conceptualized as a proximal outcome of high caregiving demands and low resources, which in turn directly increases loneliness. The cross-cultural evidence from Jin et al25 (Australia) and Varadaraj et al27 (US) supports this pathway: both studies found that greater patient dependency—a key driver of burden—was strongly associated with caregiver emotional distress. It is plausible that burden mediates the relationship between patient factors (eg., visual impairment, cognitive decline) and loneliness—a pathway that future longitudinal research should test explicitly, potentially using structural equation modeling to examine mediating effects.

Synthesis: The Relational Model of Stress as an Organizing Framework

Taken together, our findings provide strong empirical support for the relational model of stress in the context of PDR caregiving. The model’s three domains—individual (patient cognitive impairment, disease duration, visual impairment, caregiver age), environmental (living arrangement, marital quality), and behavioral (resilience, dyadic coping, burden)—collectively explained 41.6% of the variance in loneliness. Notably, the addition of environmental factors (ΔR2 = 0.068) and behavioral factors (ΔR2 = 0.065) significantly improved model fit beyond individual factors alone, underscoring that loneliness is not merely a function of illness severity but is profoundly shaped by relational and coping resources. This multilevel framework distinguishes our study from prior work that examined only single predictors of caregiver distress.25,27,28 Future research should employ longitudinal designs to test causal pathways implied by the model, such as whether enhancing dyadic coping prospectively reduces loneliness, and whether the model operates similarly across different cultural contexts.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations must be considered. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Longitudinal studies are needed to understand the temporal dynamics of loneliness as PDR progresses. Second, the use of convenience sampling from a single Chinese city may limit the generalizability of our findings to other cultural or geographic contexts. Third, reliance on self-report measures may introduce response bias, and single-item measures for constructs like cognitive impairment and marital quality are less robust than multi-item scales. Future research should incorporate objective clinical data and more comprehensive assessments. Fourth, we did not assess the spouse’s own physical health or broader social support networks, which are likely important confounders. Despite these limitations, this study provides a critical first step in understanding the psychosocial needs of this overlooked caregiver population.

Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study of 239 spousal caregivers of patients with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), nearly 90% reported moderate to severe loneliness, with a mean ULS-6 score of 14.89 ± 3.77. Hierarchical regression analysis revealed that loneliness was independently predicted by greater patient visual impairment (β = 0.349), poorer marital relationship quality (β = 0.355), higher caregiver burden (β = 0.242), more severe patient cognitive impairment (β = 0.202), older caregiver age (β = 0.178), longer disease duration (β = 0.177), and living alone with the patient (β = 0.057). Conversely, higher psychological resilience (β = −0.217) and more effective dyadic coping (β = −0.159) were significant protective factors. The relational model of stress explained 41.6% of the variance in loneliness, confirming that loneliness is not merely a consequence of caregiving burden but is strongly shaped by the quality of the couple’s relationship and their ability to cope together.

These findings suggest that routine clinical care for PDR patients should include a psychosocial assessment of their spouses, and couple-centered interventions targeting dyadic coping enhancement and resilience building may reduce loneliness. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal directions cannot be definitively established. Alternative interpretations—such as pre-existing loneliness impairing dyadic coping, or caregiver burden and loneliness reinforcing each other bidirectionally—remain possible. Longitudinal studies are needed to test whether improving dyadic coping and resilience prospectively lowers loneliness, and to examine potential reciprocal effects. Despite these limitations, this study provides a critical foundation for understanding and addressing loneliness among this overlooked caregiver population.
