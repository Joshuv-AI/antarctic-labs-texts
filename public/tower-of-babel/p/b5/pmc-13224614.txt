Cyber-victimized and cognitively fogged? Cyberbullying victimization and executive function
Authors: Hongxiang Jia, Rui Suo, Jiale Wang, Luxing Zhou, Yinchen Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesCyberbullying victimization is closely associated with executive function in university students; however, its underlying mechanisms remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to investigate the longitudinal relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function, with a particular focus on the mediating role of anxiety and the moderating role of physical activity.MethodsA two-wave longitudinal survey was conducted among 1,928 Chinese university students. Self-report questionnaires were used to assess cyberbullying victimization, executive function, anxiety, and physical activity. Descriptive statistics, correlation analyses, and moderated mediation models were employed to examine the relationships among variables.ResultsCyberbullying victimization at T1 significantly and negatively predicted executive function at T2. Anxiety at T1 mediated this relationship, indicating that cyberbullying victimization increased anxiety, which in turn impaired executive function. Moreover, physical activity at T1 moderated the direct association between T1 cyberbullying victimization and T2 executive function.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that cyberbullying victimization impairs executive function through both direct and indirect (anxiety-mediated) longitudinal pathways. Crucially, the findings revealing that the cognitive dividends of physical activity are highly vulnerable to severe psychosocial stress. These results provide critical practical insights: while promoting physical activity is vital for baseline cognitive health, it cannot serve as an impenetrable shield. Effective campus interventions must fundamentally prioritize strict cyberbullying prevention and targeted anxiety management to safeguard university students’ cognitive development.

Introduction

In cognitive science and neuropsychology, executive function (EF)—also referred to as cognitive control—denotes a set of cognitive processes that support goal-directed behavior [1]. These processes regulate thoughts and actions through cognitive control, enabling individuals to select and successfully monitor behaviors conducive to achieving desired goals [2]. Core components of executive function include attentional control, cognitive inhibition, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, which collectively contribute to goal setting and problem-solving abilities [3].Executive function plays a vital role in everyday life. It is not only a key predictor of intelligence and academic achievement but also directly influences social adaptation and emotional regulation [4]. Executive functioning disorder (EFD) is a prevalent psychological health issue that significantly impacts individuals navigating the demands of higher education. Research indicates that young adults frequently face challenges related to EFD, which are often associated with academic difficulties, behavioral problems, and deficits in emotional regulation [5]. Moreover, impairments in executive function are closely linked to a range of mental health conditions, including anxiety and depression, highlighting the importance of targeted identification and intervention to improve psychological outcomes in university populations [6]. A growing body of empirical research has confirmed that executive function has profound implications for university students’ academic performance, emotional well-being, and social adaptability [7]. Consequently, exploring the factors and mechanisms that shape executive function is of considerable practical significance for improving students’ psychological health and academic success. This is particularly critical in light of the rising prevalence of mental health concerns among university populations. Understanding how executive function influences emotional regulation and social adjustment can provide strong support for the development of psychological interventions and educational practices.

The determinants of executive function are complex and multidimensional, encompassing both biological and social-environmental factors. Among these influences, cyberbullying victimization has recently been recognized as a salient social risk factor with profound implications for the academic, social, and emotional well-being of university students [8]. While extensive research has documented the severe emotional consequences of cyberbullying, such as depression and anxiety, emerging theoretical perspectives suggest that these adverse social experiences may also extend into the cognitive domain, potentially compromising core regulatory abilities like executive function [9]. It is crucial to contextualize the importance of examining cyberbullying—as distinct from traditional bullying—during this specific developmental period of emerging adulthood. In earlier developmental stages, such as middle and high school, traditional bullying and cyberbullying are often strongly correlated and lead to similarly detrimental psychological outcomes. Both forms share core characteristics, including intentional acts of harm, repetition, and a power imbalance. However, as individuals transition to university, the manifestation and impact of peer victimization undergo significant shifts. Traditional physical or verbal bullying typically declines in higher education settings, largely due to the unstructured nature of college campuses, increased physical independence, and greater social maturity [10]. Moreover, in the contemporary digital era, overt traditional bullying carries severe social and disciplinary costs. Perpetrators face the constant risk of being recorded, exposed online, and subjected to severe public condemnation or reputational ruin. Consequently, malicious peer interactions have largely migrated into the digital sphere, evolving into more covert and insidious forms. Conversely, with the rapid expansion of the internet, digital technologies and social media have become deeply and continuously embedded in university students’ daily lives for learning, communication, and professional networking. Furthermore, as emerging adults living away from direct parental monitoring, they rely heavily on these digital peer networks for social support and professional identity construction. As a result, cyberbullying emerges as a more pervasive and salient threat in this demographic [11]. Cyberbullying is typically defined as repeated and intentional acts of harm, harassment, or denigration carried out through electronic devices or other digital means [12]. Unlike traditional bullying, cyberbullying transcends physical boundaries and occurs 24/7, effectively stripping away the traditional “safe haven” of returning home. Furthermore, the openness, potential for an infinite audience, and anonymity of online environments make cyberbullying among university students more persistent, covert, and difficult to escape [13]. The perceived anonymity and diffusion of responsibility online often embolden perpetrators, facilitating collective aggression (or “mob attacks”) where identifying and holding specific individuals accountable becomes exceptionally challenging. Therefore, while traditional bullying wanes, cyberbullying poses an increasingly unique and significant threat to mental health and cognitive development for university students, warranting focused investigation as a pressing public health concern [14]. While direct empirical evidence linking cyberbullying victimization specifically to executive function in university populations remains scarce, recent studies have demonstrated that such victimization significantly disrupts students’ cognitive appraisals and psychological adjustments [9]. These severe psychological burdens and cognitive dysregulations continuously deplete limited cognitive resources, potentially undermining higher-order abilities like executive function [15]. From a social–psychological perspective, victims often exhibit maladaptive responses such as social withdrawal and heightened loneliness. These adverse social experiences reduce opportunities for collaborative learning and social engagement [16]. Because the development of executive function depends heavily on ongoing environmental interaction—particularly through social practice that supports planning, problem-solving, and reflection—such social deprivation may deprive individuals of critical cognitive scaffolding, thereby hindering the maturation of executive function [17]. In addition, cyberbullying frequently triggers intense negative emotional reactions in victims, including anger, shame, and sadness [18]. In the absence of effective emotion regulation strategies, these emotions may consume substantial psychological resources and interfere with executive processes such as sustained attention, task-switching, and impulse control [19]. Persistent difficulties in emotion regulation may further exacerbate dysregulation of cognitive processing, creating a vicious cycle. Neurobiological research lends additional support to these mechanisms. Studies indicate that prolonged psychological stress induced by cyberbullying can impair the functioning of the prefrontal cortex (PFC)—the neural substrate of executive function responsible for information processing, attentional control, inhibitory regulation, and goal-directed behavior [20]. Chronic exposure to social stress may lead to reductions in gray matter volume and weakened neural connectivity within PFC regions, ultimately diminishing the efficiency of higher-order cognitive regulation [21]. Taken together, these findings suggest that cyberbullying victimization may undermine executive function in university students through both socio-psychological and neurobiological pathways. On the basis of existing theoretical and empirical evidence, this study proposes the following hypothesis: H1: Cyberbullying victimization negatively predicts executive function in university students.

While cyberbullying victimization is known to trigger a cluster of comorbid internalizing problems (e.g., depression, low self-esteem) [22], this study specifically isolates anxiety as the primary mediator due to its unique cognitive pathology outlined by Attentional Control Theory. According to Attentional Control Theory [23], anxiety impairs processing efficiency because intrusive, threat-related thoughts (worry) actively compete for the limited cognitive resources of the central executive system. Unlike general psychological distress, the hyper-arousal and hyper-vigilance inherent in anxiety specifically hijack working memory and inhibitory control networks. Therefore, anxiety was selected as the focal mediator because its disruptive mechanisms are closely and theoretically linked to the impairment of executive function. Anxiety is one of the most prevalent psychiatric disorders worldwide, characterized by high incidence and early onset, particularly during adolescence and early adulthood. Due to the lack of timely and effective mental health interventions, anxiety has emerged as a significant public health concern in university populations [24]. Clinically, anxiety manifests as excessive worry about future events, heightened vigilance, and sustained activation of the autonomic nervous system [25]. University students exposed to cyberbullying often experience persistent feelings of fear, shame, and helplessness, which may trigger or exacerbate anxiety symptoms [26]. Cyberbullying can also undermine social support systems, leading to isolation in offline contexts, thereby weakening students’ sense of belonging and social confidence. Consistent evidence indicates that the absence of social support is a robust predictor of anxiety in university students [27]. Moreover, cyberbullying has been linked to a range of adverse psychological and behavioral outcomes, including heightened anxiety, depressive symptoms, anger, and declines in academic performance [28]. Cross-sectional studies have repeatedly shown that victims of cyberbullying report significantly higher levels of anxiety and depression than their non-victimized peers [29]. From a neurobiological perspective, cyberbullying represents a chronic social stressor that activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to prolonged elevations in cortisol secretion [30]. This sustained physiological stress may disrupt key brain regions involved in emotional regulation and higher-order cognition, particularly the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and the amygdala, thereby heightening vulnerability to anxiety [31].

Importantly, increasing attention has been directed toward the detrimental effects of anxiety on the development of executive function in young adults. Youth is a critical period for the accelerated maturation of executive function, yet chronic anxiety may act as a disruptive factor in this developmental trajectory [6]. At the neural level, anxiety has been shown to impair activity in the PFC, particularly the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), a core region supporting executive processes [29]. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies reveal that highly anxious individuals exhibit reduced PFC activation during executive tasks [32]. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies have shown that individuals with high levels of anxiety exhibit reduced activation in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) during executive tasks [33]. This reduction compromises core components of executive function, including attentional control, working memory maintenance, and task switching [34]. From a social–psychological perspective, anxious individuals also tend to display an attentional bias toward threat-related information, giving disproportionate weight to potential negative stimuli during information processing [35]. At the psychological level, anxiety may further undermine executive function through several mechanisms. First, according to attentional control theory, anxiety weakens the efficiency of the goal-directed attentional system while increasing the influence of stimulus-driven processing, making it more difficult for individuals to inhibit irrelevant information and maintain task focus [23]. Second, excessive worry, which is a central feature of anxiety, occupies limited working memory resources and reduces the cognitive capacity available for ongoing tasks [36]. Third, anxious individuals often engage in repetitive negative thinking and threat monitoring, which may impair cognitive flexibility and reduce their ability to shift efficiently between rules, strategies, or task demands [37]. Together, these processes may gradually erode the efficiency of executive control and contribute to poorer executive functioning. Although previous studies have suggested that executive dysfunction may also increase anxiety, indicating that the association between anxiety and executive function may be bidirectional [38], the present study conceptualized anxiety primarily as a mediator based on the specific theoretical framework linking cyberbullying victimization to later cognitive functioning. In this framework, cyberbullying victimization is regarded as a salient interpersonal stressor that may elicit anxiety symptoms, and anxiety, in turn, may undermine executive function through heightened threat monitoring, reduced cognitive flexibility, and depletion of regulatory resources. Therefore, in the context of the present study, anxiety was hypothesized as an intervening mechanism through which cyberbullying victimization may be associated with poorer executive function. Nevertheless, this mediation pathway should be interpreted as one theoretically grounded explanation rather than the only possible directional relationship. Taken together, these findings suggest that anxiety may play a pivotal role in linking cyberbullying victimization to impaired executive function. Therefore, we propose the following hypothesis: H2: Anxiety mediates the relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function in university students.

When examining the mechanisms through which cyberbullying victimization affects executive function, the role of physical activity cannot be overlooked. Physical activity, defined as voluntary bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure, encompasses both structured exercise and unstructured daily activities [39]. An increasing body of evidence demonstrates that engagement in physical activity is strongly associated with cognitive development, particularly executive function, during emerging adulthood—a critical period for continued brain maturation and the refinement of complex cognitive skills [40]. Conversely, insufficient physical activity has been shown to correlate with higher risks of executive functioning disorder (EFD), underscoring its role in maintaining cognitive health [41]. From a neurobiological perspective, physical activity optimizes brain structure and function to build an essential baseline cognitive reserve. First, it enhances the secretion of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which plays a pivotal role in neuroplasticity, synaptogenesis, and neuroprotection [42]. BDNF is especially crucial for the development of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) [43], the neural hub of executive function [44]. A lack of physical activity reduces BDNF levels [45], impairing both cognitive processing and emotional regulation. Second, empirical studies confirm that regular aerobic exercise significantly increases PFC blood perfusion and neural activation, optimizing executive processes such as task planning, attentional shifting, and error monitoring [46, 47]. In contrast, physical inactivity constrains this regulatory pathway, leaving the executive control regions vulnerable to cognitive depletion. At the social–psychological level, physical activity further fortifies this baseline cognitive advantage. It strengthens students’ sense of self-efficacy and perceived control, which in turn promotes persistence and adaptive regulation when facing cognitive demands [48]. Low self-efficacy and diminished goal persistence are widely recognized behavioral correlates of executive dysfunction. By improving self-efficacy and perceived control [49], physical activity significantly elevates the initial cognitive resources available to students. However, whether this exercise-induced cognitive advantage can withstand the severe psychosocial trauma of cyberbullying—or whether such intense stressors might overwhelm and “level” this advantage—remains a critical and underexplored empirical question [50]. Crucially, while previous literature has widely documented the benefits of physical activity in alleviating emotional distress, this study intentionally positions physical activity as a moderator of the direct pathway between cyberbullying victimization and executive function, rather than the mediated emotional pathway (i.e., via anxiety). This theoretical choice is grounded in the perspective of cognitive resource allocation. While physical activity serves as a general emotion-regulation tool, its neurobiological impact on the prefrontal cortex specifically fortifies an individual’s baseline cognitive reserve. By positioning the moderator on the direct pathway, we aim to isolate and test a specific cognitive interaction: how the direct cognitive load and resource depletion imposed by cyberbullying trauma compete directly against the baseline cognitive reserve established by physical activity, functioning independently of general affective distress. Drawing on this theoretical and empirical foundation, we propose the following hypothesis: H3: Physical activity moderates the relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function in university students.

Although prior studies have demonstrated the detrimental impact of cyberbullying victimization on psychological functioning, several important gaps remain. First, most existing research has relied on cross-sectional designs, which cannot establish the temporal ordering or potential causal mechanisms linking cyberbullying victimization and executive function. Second, prior work has primarily focused on emotional and behavioral outcomes such as depression, anxiety, or academic adjustment, while relatively little attention has been given to executive function—a core self-regulatory ability that underpins university students’ academic performance, decision-making, and mental health. Third, although anxiety is often examined as an outcome of cyberbullying victimization, its role as a mediator between victimization and cognitive processes has been underexplored. Finally, while accumulating evidence highlights physical activity as a protective factor against psychological distress, very few studies have investigated its moderating effect within the relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function from a longitudinal perspective. To address these gaps, the primary objective of the present study is to investigate the longitudinal relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function in university students. Specifically, grounded in Attentional Control Theory—which posits that stress-induced emotional dysregulation depletes limited cognitive resources—we aim to explicitly test the mediating role of anxiety in this relationship. Furthermore, we seek to explore the potential buffering role of physical activity as a moderator that may mitigate these adverse cognitive consequences. Based on these objectives and our theoretical framework, we propose a conceptual moderated mediation model (see Fig. 1) to systematically examine the mechanisms underlying executive function impairment and to inform targeted interventions.

Methods

Participants

This study employed a cluster sampling method to recruit university students from Chinese higher education institutions and conducted a two-wave longitudinal survey over a six-month period. Data were collected at two stages, with a six-month interval between measurements. The first stage (T1) was conducted in January 2025. At T1, participants retrospectively reported their experiences of cyberbullying victimization over the past six months, while their current levels of anxiety and physical activity were assessed simultaneously. Data collection was carried out using electronic questionnaires distributed in class groups. Prior to the survey, participants were informed of the research objectives, questionnaire content, data anonymity and confidentiality, as well as the intended use of the data. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. On average, participants completed the questionnaire within approximately 10 min.The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the authors’ institution, ensuring that all procedures adhered to ethical and legal standards. Strict compliance with these guidelines enhanced both the credibility of the study and the trust of participants. During data cleaning, questionnaires showing continuous identical responses, patterned response styles (e.g., wave-like patterns), or failed validity-check items were classified as invalid and excluded. A total of 2,331 valid questionnaires were obtained at the first stage.The second stage (T2) was conducted in July 2025, during which executive function was assessed. After data matching and further exclusion of invalid questionnaires, 1,928 complete and valid responses were retained, yielding an overall follow-up rate of 82.50%. Attrition was primarily due to student leave, suspension of studies, transfer to another major, or exclusion for careless responding. The final sample had a mean age of 18.97 years (SD = 1.120). To evaluate potential bias introduced by sample attrition, an attrition analysis was conducted. Independent samples t-tests were employed to compare the baseline (T1) characteristics of the retention group (n = 1,928) and the attrition group (n = 403). The results indicated no significant differences between the two groups regarding T1 cyberbullying victimization, T1 anxiety, and T1 physical activity. These findings suggest that the sample attrition was largely random and did not introduce systematic bias into the longitudinal analyses. Measures

Cyberbullying victimization

In prior studies, many researchers have employed single-item measures to assess cyberbullying victimization. Such measures typically begin with a definition of cyberbullying, followed by asking participants to report the frequency of their experiences [51–53]. Compared to multi-item scales, single-item measures are cost-effective, easy to administer, and efficient [54, 55]. Scholars have further suggested that when the construct is easily identifiable and comprehensible to respondents, single-item measures can be sufficiently valid [56]. Drawing on the classical definition of bullying [57] and previous research on victimization assessment [54, 58–61], this study developed a single-item measure of cyberbullying victimization for college students. Prior to responding, participants were presented with a detailed description of cyberbullying, including experiences such as being insulted, mocked, threatened, or having rumors spread online; receiving unpleasant audio materials; deliberate social exclusion in online settings; or having private information forwarded or disclosed without consent. It was emphasized that such behaviors did not include friendly teasing or conflicts among peers.

Participants were asked to indicate the frequency of cyberbullying victimization they had experienced during the past six months, using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (never) to 3 (often). Higher scores indicated a higher frequency of cyberbullying victimization. To provide better empirical context for this single-item measure, the response distribution in our final sample (N = 1,928) was as follows: 69.1% (n = 1,332) of participants scored 0, 25.1% (n = 483) scored 1, 5.2% (n = 100) scored 2, and 0.7% (n = 13) scored 3, indicating a positively skewed distribution typical of victimization experiences. This frequency pattern indicates a pronounced positively skewed distribution (Mean = 0.37, Median = 0, Mode = 0). Such a severe “floor effect” aligns well with theoretical and epidemiological expectations, as severe cyberbullying is a traumatic and relatively low-frequency negative life event within the general student population, making this skewed distribution highly typical and representative of authentic victimization experiences.

Anxiety

Anxiety was measured using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale-2 (GAD-2), which assesses symptoms over the past two weeks [62, 63]. The scale includes two items: (1) “Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge” and (2) “Not being able to stop or control worrying.” Responses were rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 4 (nearly every day), with total scores ranging from 2 to 8. In the present study, the GAD-2 showed good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.829).

Executive function

Executive function was assessed using the 10-item scale developed by Huang et al. [59], which captures three core dimensions: cognitive flexibility, inhibitory control, and working memory. Each item was rated on a 3-point Likert scale (1 = often, 3 = never), with all items positively scored. Higher total scores indicated stronger executive function. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.873), supporting its reliability and applicability among Chinese university students.

Physical activity

Physical activity was measured using a single-item self-report measure assessing the number of days during the past 7 days on which participants engaged in exercise or physical activity. Response options ranged from 0 to 7 days [64]. Although device-based measures, such as accelerometers, are often recommended because of their greater objectivity, their use is not always feasible in large-scale survey research due to high cost, low compliance with device wearing, and practical or logistical constraints [65]. Therefore, self-report measures remain widely used in epidemiological and behavioral studies. In this context, the 7-day recall was used to capture participants’ recent physical activity with reduced recall bias. This measure has been widely used in prior research [66]. To clarify the response distributions for this single-item variable, the distribution in our sample (N = 1,928) indicated that 5.8% (n = 111) of participants reported 0 days, 31.5% (n = 606) reported 1 to 2 days, 40.5% (n = 780) reported 3 to 4 days, and 22.3% (n = 431) reported 5 to 7 days of physical activity per week.

Data processing and analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0. To test for common method bias, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted. When the variance explained by the first factor is below 40%, common method effects can be considered negligible [67]. Next, descriptive statistics and correlation analyses were conducted to examine demographic characteristics and the distributions and interrelations of the study variables. Prior to hypothesis testing, all continuous variables were standardized. To test the research hypotheses, we used Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS. Specifically, Model 4 was applied to examine the mediating role of anxiety, and Model 5 was used to explicitly test the moderated mediation model, examining how physical activity moderates the direct effect within this mediation framework [68]. A bootstrap method with 5,000 resamples was employed to obtain robust parameter estimates and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), thereby improving the reliability of the inferences [69]. In addition, demographic variables including age, gender, grade level, only-child status, and parental education were included as covariates in all models. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Common method bias test

Given that all study variables were collected via self-reported questionnaires, the risk of common method bias was examined. We conducted Harman’s single-factor test using exploratory factor analysis of all measurement items. The results extracted four factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor accounting for only 19.29% of the total variance, which is well below the critical threshold of 40%. This finding suggests that common method bias was not a serious concern in this study, thereby enhancing the reliability of the data and supporting the robustness of subsequent analyses.

Descriptive analysis

Table 2 presents the descriptive statistics of the main study variables, including T1 cyberbullying victimization, T2 executive function, T1 anxiety, and T1 physical activity. Specifically, the means and standard deviations of these variables are reported across gender and only-child status groups, thereby providing important contextual information for the subsequent correlation and model analyses. Gender differences revealed that males reported significantly higher scores in cyberbullying victimization (t = 6.04, p < 0.001) and executive function (t = 3.01, p < 0.01) compared with females, while no significant gender difference was observed in anxiety (t = − 0.30, p > 0.05). For physical activity, males scored significantly higher than females (t = 5.88, p < 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were found between only-child and non-only-child students across any of the study variables (p > 0.05). Overall, gender differences emerged in T1 cyberbullying victimization, T2 executive function, and T1 physical activity, whereas only-child status did not significantly affect the key study variables.

Mediation model analysis

Prior to testing the full moderated mediation framework, we first examined the simple mediation model to establish the foundational indirect pathway. This step-by-step analytical approach allows for a clear and logical demonstration of the baseline mediating mechanism before introducing the complexity of the boundary condition. After controlling for demographic variables, results presented in Table 4 indicated that cyberbullying victimization significantly negatively predicted executive function (β = − 0.207, SE = 0.022, t = − 9.232, p < 0.001) and significantly positively predicted anxiety (β = 0.307, SE = 0.022, t = 13.989, p < 0.001). Further analysis revealed that, when anxiety was included in the model, the direct effect of cyberbullying victimization on executive function remained significant (β = − 0.079, SE = 0.021, t = − 3.663, p < 0.001), and anxiety itself significantly predicted executive function (β = − 0.417, SE = 0.021, t = − 19.636, p < 0.001).

Moderated mediation analysis

As shown in Table 6; Fig. 3, cyberbullying victimization significantly negatively predicted executive function (β = − 0.078, SE = 0.021, t = − 3.649, p < 0.001) and significantly positively predicted anxiety (β = 0.307, SE = 0.022, t = 13.989, p < 0.001). Physical activity also demonstrated a significant positive effect on executive function (β = 0.105, SE = 0.020, t = 5.153, p < 0.001), whereas anxiety exerted a significant negative effect on executive function (β = − 0.420, SE = 0.021, t = − 19.921, p < 0.001).Further interaction analysis revealed that the interaction between cyberbullying victimization and physical activity significantly predicted executive function (β = − 0.068, SE = 0.020, t = − 3.433, p < 0.01), indicating that physical activity moderated the relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function. The overall model demonstrated good fit, with explained variance for executive function and anxiety of 23.0% and 9.5%, respectively.

Discussion

This study examined the longitudinal relationship between T1 cyberbullying victimization and T2 executive function in university students, with a particular focus on the mediating role of T1 anxiety and the moderating role of T1 physical activity. The results indicated that T1 cyberbullying victimization significantly negatively predicted T2 executive function. Mediation analyses further revealed that cyberbullying victimization not only had a direct adverse effect on executive function but also indirectly impaired it by increasing levels of T1 anxiety. Moreover, moderation analyses demonstrated that T1 physical activity significantly moderated the relationship between T1 cyberbullying victimization and T2 executive function.

Consistent with prior research, the present study further confirms that T1 cyberbullying victimization negatively predicts T2 executive function [70]. From a social-psychological perspective, victims of cyberbullying often experience elevated feelings of helplessness and reduced self-worth. These negative psychological experiences can significantly undermine individuals’ beliefs in their own capabilities, known as self-efficacy [71]. Self-efficacy refers to one’s subjective belief in their ability to successfully complete specific tasks and plays a critical role in regulating motivation, task persistence, and goal-directed behavior [72]. Low self-efficacy not only diminishes students’ motivation when facing complex tasks but also reduces their engagement, flexibility, and initiative in planning, organizing, and executing behaviors, thereby hindering the maturation and development of executive function. Evidence suggests that the effective operation of executive function heavily relies on individuals’ cognitive evaluation of their abilities and their confidence and strategic choices in responding to external challenges [73], yet these psychological factors are often negatively affected in victims of cyberbullying [74]. From a neurobiological standpoint, the core neural mechanisms of executive function are closely linked to the dopaminergic system. Dopamine is involved not only in reward processing and motivation maintenance but also in regulating higher-order cognitive functions such as working memory, attentional control, and behavioral inhibition [75]. Key dopaminergic regulatory regions, including the nucleus accumbens and mesolimbic system, play pivotal roles in initiating motivation, maintaining goal-directed behavior, and facilitating reinforcement learning [76]. As a form of social exclusion, cyberbullying victimization is often accompanied by diminished self-worth and weakened social connectedness. Such social stressors may suppress dopamine secretion in the midbrain reward system, particularly within the nucleus accumbens [77]. Reduced dopaminergic signaling can result in insufficient motivation, impaired goal-directed behavior, and decreased reinforcement learning efficiency, ultimately compromising performance in planning, decision-making, and behavioral regulation—core domains of executive function [78].

This study confirmed the hypothesized mediating role of T1 anxiety in the relationship between T1 cyberbullying victimization and T2 executive function, a finding largely consistent with existing literature [79]. Extensive research has demonstrated that cyberbullying victimization is significantly associated with a range of adverse psychological outcomes, including depression and anxiety [80, 81]. Anxiety not only represents a common psychological consequence of cyberbullying but also serves as a critical mediator influencing cognitive functioning in college students [82]. Further studies indicate that college students who experience bullying exhibit heightened amygdala reactivity to socially threatening cues. Such hyperactivation amplifies negative expectations and fear responses in social contexts, thereby triggering or sustaining anxiety [83]. Concurrently, victims often endure persistent feelings of shame, fear, and helplessness, which, if not effectively regulated, can exacerbate anxiety levels [84]. From a social-psychological perspective, cyberbullying victims tend to adopt negative attributional styles, attributing bullying experiences to personal deficiencies or uncontrollable external factors. This self-blaming tendency reinforces feelings of helplessness and self-devaluation, increasing susceptibility to anxiety [85]. Anxiety itself exerts detrimental effects on executive function. Neuroscientific evidence shows that anxiety enhances amygdala responsiveness to threat-related information while weakening functional connectivity with the prefrontal cortex [86, 87]. This neural dysregulation reduces the efficiency of emotion regulation and cognitive resource integration, making individuals more vulnerable to emotional interference during task performance [88]. From a socio-cognitive standpoint, failures in emotion regulation are a key mechanism through which anxiety impairs executive function [89]. Individuals with high anxiety often lack effective regulation strategies, relying instead on maladaptive behaviors such as avoidance or rumination [90, 91]. Such maladaptive regulation increases cognitive load, disrupting the normal operation of executive function [92]. Taken together, converging theoretical and empirical evidence supports the current study’s findings: cyberbullying victimization positively predicts anxiety, which in turn impairs executive function in college students, thereby serving as a significant mediator in this relationship.

Consistent with our hypothesis, the results confirmed that physical activity significantly moderates the relationship between cyberbullying victimization and executive function. Building upon this significant interaction, the moderation analysis elucidated a complex and somewhat counterintuitive role of physical activity in this dynamic. Specifically, our simple slope analyses revealed that the negative association between cyberbullying victimization and executive function was significantly steeper for students with high levels of physical activity (b = -0.146) compared to those with low physical activity (b = -0.010). Technically, this indicates that a high level of physical activity exacerbates the rate of decline in executive function under the stress of cyberbullying. However, it is crucial to note that students with high physical activity possessed a substantially higher initial baseline (intercept) of executive function. Consequently, even when exposed to the highest levels of cyberbullying victimization, their absolute executive function scores remained superior to those of their inactive peers. Previous literature has widely documented physical activity as a robust resilience factor that typically buffers against general psychological distress and various adverse life events, including traditional peer victimization [93, 94]. However, when examining the unique and pervasive context of cyberbullying, our findings suggest a critical nuance. Rather than functioning as an impenetrable buffer, these findings suggest that the cognitive benefits of physical activity represent a ‘fragile cognitive advantage.’ As our data clearly illustrate, physical activity itself is highly beneficial, significantly elevating students’ baseline executive function in the absence of bullying. Yet, this exercise-induced cognitive dividend proves to be highly vulnerable. When confronted with the severe psychological trauma of cyberbullying, the protective effect of physical activity essentially fails. The cognitive advantage of highly active students is rapidly wiped out, causing their executive function to plummet toward the same impaired trough observed in inactive students (who exhibit a persistent ‘floor effect’). Ultimately, this steep decline starkly highlights the insidious and severe toxicity of cyberbullying—it is a psychosocial stressor so profound that it can completely dismantle the cognitive dividends accumulated through a positive, active lifestyle. From a social-psychological and cognitive perspective, this pronounced plunge among highly active students can be explained through several intersecting mechanisms. First, drawing on the contrast effect, highly active students typically possess higher social capital and are more deeply integrated into peer networks through their participation in sports [95]. Consequently, the profound social exclusion and reputation damage inherent in cyberbullying represent a drastic and traumatic deviation from their normal, positive social experiences. This stark contrast creates massive cognitive dissonance and intense emotional shock, whereas inactive students—who may already experience marginalization—exhibit a “floor effect” with less relative decline. Second, from a resource allocation perspective, the acute distress of cyberbullying forces a sudden reallocation of cognitive resources. Because highly active students possess a higher baseline of cognitive capacity, they may acutely perceive and deeply ruminate on the complexities of the online social threat. This intense rumination and hyper-vigilance hijack the prefrontal cortex, forcefully diverting their robust cognitive reserves away from task-oriented executive functions and toward threat-processing, which ultimately explains the remarkably steep trajectory of their cognitive impairment [96]. These findings powerfully underscore that while promoting physical activity is vital for baseline cognitive health, eradicating the root cause of the trauma—cyberbullying itself—is the only definitive way to safeguard university students’ cognitive development.

Interestingly, the correlation analysis revealed that physical activity was not significantly associated with anxiety, which is inconsistent with the majority of previous findings suggesting that higher levels of physical activity are typically linked to lower levels of anxiety among college students [97, 98]. Several potential explanations may account for this unexpected result. First, the relationship between physical activity and anxiety may depend on the intensity, frequency, and type of physical activity. In this study, physical activity was assessed using a general self-report measure, which might not have captured these nuances. For example, light or irregular activity may not be sufficient to produce significant mental health benefits, while moderate-to-vigorous and consistent exercise tends to have stronger effects on reducing anxiety [99]. Second, the timing of measurement may partly explain the weak association. Anxiety was measured at Time 1 and physical activity was also reported at the same wave, which may not fully reflect the long-term psychological benefits of exercise. The buffering effect of physical activity on anxiety might emerge more clearly in longitudinal changes rather than cross-sectional correlations [100]. Third, it is possible that contextual and individual factors (e.g., academic pressure, social stress, sleep deprivation) attenuate the expected protective effect of physical activity. During periods of high academic or social stress, even physically active students may still experience considerable anxiety, weakening the overall correlation [101]. Finally, the sample characteristics should also be considered. The participants were Chinese university students, who might engage in physical activity primarily for social or recreational purposes rather than stress management, thereby diluting the association between exercise and anxiety reduction [102]. Overall, although the present findings did not support a significant correlation between physical activity and anxiety, this does not necessarily contradict the theoretical framework of the study.

In summary, this two-wave longitudinal study examined the relationship between T1 cyberbullying victimization and T2 executive function among university students, elucidating the mediating role of T1 anxiety and the moderating role of T1 physical activity. The findings indicate that cyberbullying victimization not only directly predicts subsequent deficits in executive function but also exerts an indirect negative effect through heightened anxiety. Crucially, moderation analyses revealed that the cognitive benefits of physical activity represent a “fragile cognitive advantage.” While regular physical activity significantly elevates baseline executive function, this advantage is rapidly wiped out when students are exposed to severe cyberbullying. The steep cognitive plunge experienced by highly active students underscores the profound toxicity of online victimization, which can completely dismantle the cognitive dividends of a healthy lifestyle. Theoretically, this study extends the current literature in three significant ways. First, it expands the scope of cyberbullying research by positioning executive function as a core cognitive outcome during emerging adulthood, moving beyond traditional emotional and behavioral indices. Second, grounded in Attentional Control Theory, it clarifies the mediating mechanism of anxiety, demonstrating how emotional dysregulation depletes cognitive resources. Third, by challenging the traditional buffering hypothesis and introducing the concept of a fragile cognitive advantage, the study highlights the destructive “leveling effect” of cyberbullying. It integrates perspectives from health psychology and cyberbullying research to demonstrate how severe psychosocial trauma can neutralize the protective effects of positive health behaviors. From a practical standpoint, these findings suggest that university educators and mental health practitioners must remain highly vigilant regarding the severe cognitive harms of cyberbullying. While promoting physical activity remains a vital strategy for enhancing baseline cognitive health, educators must recognize that it is not an impenetrable shield against severe psychological trauma. Therefore, intervention programs cannot rely solely on promoting positive coping behaviors like exercise; they must fundamentally prioritize strict cyberbullying prevention and targeted anxiety management to eradicate the root cause of the distress. Comprehensive campus initiatives are strongly recommended to protect both the overall psychological well-being and the fragile cognitive assets of university students.

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the two-wave longitudinal design precluded the ability to control for baseline levels of the mediator (anxiety) and the outcome variable (executive function), limiting our capacity to definitively rule out reverse causality. For instance, individuals with pre-existing executive function deficits might struggle with emotion regulation, inadvertently making them more susceptible to cyberbullying. Furthermore, while we modeled anxiety as predicting impaired executive function, this relationship may be bidirectional. Future studies employing cross-lagged panel models with three or more measurement waves are warranted to establish strict temporal precedence and disentangle these reciprocal relationships. Second, regarding the temporal alignment in the moderation model, physical activity was assessed at T1 capturing the past 7 days, whereas cyberbullying victimization was assessed at T1 retrospectively covering the past six months. Although a 7-day recall is a validated proxy for habitual physical activity, a more rigorous design would ideally measure the stable lifestyle moderator strictly prior to the onset of the environmental predictor. Third, the measurement tools present certain constraints. Both cyberbullying victimization and physical activity were assessed using single-item measures. While preceded by comprehensive definitions to ensure content validity, this approach inherently lacks the psychometric sensitivity of multidimensional scales. Statistically, this reduced variance may attenuate the observed effect sizes, implying that the significant main and moderating effects identified in our models might actually represent conservative estimates of the true relationships. Additionally, reliance on self-report questionnaires may introduce recall bias, and future studies should incorporate objective measures or multi-informant reports. Fourth, regarding the mediation model, this study focused solely on anxiety without accounting for other comorbid mental health challenges commonly associated with cyberbullying, such as depression or general psychological distress. Given that depression also significantly impairs cognitive function, isolating anxiety without controlling for these comorbidities may introduce omitted variable bias. Future research should employ parallel mediation models to rigorously isolate the unique mediating pathways of specific emotional disorders. Fifth, statistical and control variable limitations should be noted. The assessment of common method bias was limited to Harman’s single-factor test; future research could employ confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) or marker variable techniques for a more rigorous evaluation. Additionally, we relied on PROCESS macro models, which provide limited overall model fit indices compared to structural equation modeling (SEM). Furthermore, key sociodemographic factors that might influence psychological outcomes, such as socioeconomic status and urban–rural background, were not collected and should be controlled for in subsequent studies. Finally, the sample consisted exclusively of Chinese university students, which may limit the external validity of the findings. Future research should examine whether these cognitive and emotional patterns generalize to more diverse cultural, educational, and age groups.

Conclusion

This longitudinal study demonstrates that cyberbullying victimization directly impairs university students’ executive function and exerts an indirect negative effect through heightened anxiety. Furthermore, our findings reveal that while physical activity elevates baseline executive function, it provides only a “fragile cognitive advantage.” Under the severe stress of cyberbullying, this advantage is rapidly wiped out, demonstrating how toxic online victimization can completely dismantle the cognitive dividends of a healthy lifestyle. Ultimately, because positive health behaviors cannot serve as an impenetrable shield against severe psychological trauma, campus interventions must move beyond simply promoting physical activity and fundamentally prioritize eradicating the root cause through strict cyberbullying prevention and targeted anxiety management.
