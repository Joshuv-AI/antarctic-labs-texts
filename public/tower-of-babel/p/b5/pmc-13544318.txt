Multi-omic analysis reveals that the CARNS1-mediated carnosine synthesis pathway modulates muscle metabolic responses to nutrient restriction in goats
Authors: Tianxi Zhang, Rong Wang, Rongzhen Zhong, Shaoxun Tang, Min Wang, Zhiyuan Ma, Xuefeng Han, Zhiliang Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeasonal feed shortages pose critical challenges to ruminant productivity, yet the mechanisms underlying muscle metabolic responses to varying degrees of restriction remain unclear. This study simulated feed shortage in goats using three dietary treatments: normal feeding (RF0), 25% restricted feeding (RF25), and 50% restricted feeding (RF50). A total of 30 male Xiangdong black goats with similar initial body weight (19.14 ± 2.75 kg) and age (4.0 ± 0.3 months) were randomly assigned into 3 groups (n = 10 per group) for an 87-d trial (7-d preliminary period followed by an 80-d experimental period). By integrating growth performance, muscle morphology, fatty acid and free amino acid profiles, and transcriptomic and metabolomic data, the multilevel responses of goat muscle to feed shortage were characterized. As anticipated, restricted feeding markedly impaired growth performance; in particular, the average daily gain in the RF50 group decreased markedly compared with the RF0 group (P < 0.05). Morphological analysis revealed that RF50 significantly reduced the cross-sectional area of longissimus lumborum muscle fiber (P = 0.017) without affecting fiber diameter or density (P > 0.05) compared with the RF0 group, suggesting that structural preservation of fiber number was prioritized over individual fiber volume under severe nutritional stress. Metabolically, RF50 induced accumulation of free amino acids (aspartate, glycine, valine, and lysine) and monounsaturated fatty acids (cis9-C16:1n-7 and cis9-C18:1 n-9), and depletion of polyunsaturated fatty acids (cis9,cis12-C18:2n-6 and cis9,cis12,cis15-C18:3n-3) compared with the RF0 group (P < 0.05). Transcriptomic analysis revealed suppression of protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, beta-alanine metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, and fatty acid degradation pathways under RF50 (P-adjust < 0.05); concordant downregulation of the beta-alanine metabolism and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathways was also observed in the metabolome. Trend clustering identified a nonlinear metabolic threshold, with RF25 inducing gradual adaptations while RF50 triggered abrupt metabolic reprogramming. Integrated multiomics analysis identified the beta-alanine metabolism pathway as a pivotal hub, marked by coordinated downregulation of CARNS1 and significantly reduced levels of carnosine and L-histidine in the RF50 group (P-adjust < 0.05). These findings suggest that severely restricted feeding disrupts muscle homeostasis via the CARNS1-mediated carnosine synthesis pathway, impairing fatty acid metabolism and protein turnover. The adverse effects of RF50 on muscle characteristics were more pronounced than those of RF25, indicating that maintaining feed intake at a minimum of 75% during seasonal feed shortages may prevent irreversible muscle damage and mitigate nutritional stress in goats.Keywords: Carnosine, Goat muscle, Restricted feeding, Metabolomic, Transcriptomic

Introduction

Amid global climate change and increasing volatility in agricultural production, feed scarcity has emerged as a critical constraint in livestock husbandry. Ruminants, particularly goats, inherently face nutritional challenges stemming from seasonal and regional feed shortages (Henry et al., 2018). Studies have demonstrated that seasonal feed deprivation not only induces > 30% body weight (BW) loss in ruminants but also compromises immune competence and markedly diminishes production performance (Parreira et al., 2020). When subjected to nutritional stress, metabolic pathways—including lipid transport, carbohydrate utilization, and protein turnover undergo remodeling within the organism (Smith et al., 2018). Such metabolic reprogramming may indirectly influence muscle characteristics through coordinated mechanisms involving intramuscular protein and lipid metabolism coupled with structural modifications of muscle fiber (Lehnert et al., 2006).

In recent years, significant progress has been made in research on the relationship between nutritional regulation and muscle characteristics in ruminants. Short-term nutritional restriction in calves may partially recover BW through compensatory growth mechanisms (Cabaraux et al., 2003; O'Shea et al., 2016). However, nutritional deficiencies during critical developmental stages, such as early growth stages, can cause irreversible damage to muscle tissue. This damage is manifested by muscle fiber atrophy, imbalances in amino acid metabolism, and alterations in fatty acid composition (Palma et al., 2016). In pregnant goats under nutritionally restricted conditions, the expression of liver energy metabolism genes, such as G6PC and PGC1A (the full names of the abbreviations of all genes and proteins can be found in Table S1), is altered, leading to enhanced gluconeogenesis (Chen et al., 2019). Concurrently, the allocation of nutrients to muscle tissue is reduced (Zhou et al., 2019). Furthermore, low-protein diets can significantly downregulate key genes, such as MYOD, which regulate muscle fiber differentiation in sheep, and decrease the deposition of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), thereby affecting meat flavor (Ramírez-Zamudio et al., 2022). Lv et al. (2025) demonstrated that long-term leucine supplementation in goats can mitigate the suppression of muscle protein synthesis induced by a low-protein diet through the regulation of intestinal amino acid transport (e.g., SLC7A5) and the mTOR pathway in muscle. Data from Li et al. (2025) suggest that carnosine may play a role in promoting muscle structural integrity in sheep under nutritional stress.

Currently, there is a lack of systematic analyses on how muscle characteristics dynamically respond to varying degrees of feed restriction. To address this, this study simulated restricted feeding by establishing three groups: normal feeding (RF0), 25% restricted feeding (RF25), and 50% restricted feeding (RF50). By integrating morphological analysis, fatty acid and free amino acid profiles, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, this study aimed to unravel the multiomics response patterns of goat muscles under restricted feed conditions and assess the effects of dietary stress on muscle characteristics. The findings of this research may have significant implications for optimizing feed resource utilization, developing precise supplementary feeding strategies, preventing irreversible muscle damage in goats, increasing economic efficiency in goat farming, and improving production management under extreme environmental conditions.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All procedures for animal experimentation were performed according to the guidelines for the care and use of animals approved by the Animal Care Committee, Institute of Subtropical Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Changsha, Hunan, China (No. ISA000263).

Animals, housing, and experimental design

A total of 30 male Xiangdong black goats with similar weight (19.14 ± 2.75 kg) and age (4.0 ± 0.3 months) were used in this experiment (n = 10 per group). These Goats were randomly divided into an ad libitum control group (RF0) and two restricted feeding groups receiving 25% restricted feeding (RF25) and 50% (RF50) of the control intake. The diet was formulated in accordance with the China National Feeding Standard of Meat-producing Sheep and Goats (Ministry of Agriculture of the People's Republic of China, 2004) to produce a pelleted total mixed feed. The detailed ingredients of the experimental diets are shown in Table 1. The total feeding trial period was 87 d, including 7 d of a preliminary period and 80 d of the experimental period. During the preliminary period, the ad libitum intake of the RF0 group was determined by daily feed refusal measurements, targeting 10% leftovers relative to daily supply. This intake level was then used to calculate the rations for the RF25 and RF50 groups, with weekly adjustments based on the RF0 group's average intake from the prior week. All goats were housed individually in metabolic cages (length, 152 cm; width, 84 cm; height, 140 cm) and were offered feed in equal portions at 07:00 and 17:00 daily. Throughout the entire experiment, the goats had free access to fresh water. The intake of feed by each replicate was recorded daily, and the average intake during the whole period was calculated. Body weight was recorded at the beginning and at the end of the experimental period, immediately before the morning feeding. The average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion rate were calculated.

Feed sample analysis

Analytical dry matter (method 930.15) was determined by drying at 135 °C for 2 h. Crude protein (CP; method 2001.11) was analyzed using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (Kjeltec 2300, FOSS, Hillerød, Denmark). Neutral detergent fiber (NDF; method 2002.04) and acid detergent fiber (ADF; method 973.18) were determined using a fiber analyzer (Fibertec 8000, FOSS, Hillerød, Denmark) according to the AOAC (2005). Calcium and phosphorus were analyzed using an inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (iCAP 7000 series, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) according to method 985.01 (AOAC, 2005). Starch was analyzed via enzymatic hydrolysis of α-linked glucose polymers (Rode et al., 1999), and absorbance was measured using a microplate reader (Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA).

Sample collection

After the experiment, the longissimus lumborum muscle of the goats was collected immediately following humane euthanasia. The associated anadesma was promptly removed via a sterilized scalpel. The muscle tissue was then rinsed with precooled sterile saline solution and divided into four portions. One piece, approximately 1 cm × 1 cm × 2 cm in size, was fixed in 10% neutral paraformaldehyde solution for histomorphometric microscopy. Another portion of muscle tissue was immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C for later RNA extraction, transcriptome, and metabolomics analysis. Additionally, two muscle samples were collected, stored at −80 °C, and subsequently freeze-dried for the determination of fatty acid and free amino acid profiles.

Determination of tissue morphology

The fixed muscle samples were embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and mounted on slides. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) according to routine protocols (Zhang et al., 2023a,b). After being mounted with a glass coverslip, the H&E-stained cross-sections were imaged via a microscope (BX51, Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The fiber diameter, density, and cross-sectional area of each sample were measured across the five fields using ImageJ (version 1.51; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Muscle fiber density refers to the number of fiber distributed in 1 mm2 of muscle (Deng et al., 2024).

Nutritional component analysis of the longissimus lumborum muscle

The longissimus lumborum muscle was freeze-dried via a freeze dryer (Alpha 2-4 LD plus, Martin Christ Gefriertrocknungsanlagen GmbH, Osterode, Germany) at −60 °C for 72 h. After freeze-drying, the samples were ground into a fine powder for subsequent nutritional component analysis. The medium- and long-chain fatty acid composition in the longissimus lumborum muscle was analyzed via gas chromatography (7890A, Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID) and a CP-Sil 88 fused silica open tubular capillary column, following the method described by Zhou et al. (2019). Fatty acid species were identified and quantified by comparing retention times with those of authentic standards and calculating peak area ratios relative to the standard peaks, with the results presented as percentages of total fatty acids. The free amino acid profile was determined using an L-8900 amino acid analyzer (Hitachi Global Inc., Hitachi, Ibaraki, Japan) following the method of Wu et al., 2022a, Wu et al., 2022b. Briefly, 1 g of freeze-dried powder sample was homogenized in 50 mL of 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid for 30 min. After vortex mixing and filtration, 2 mL of the clarified filtrate was transferred to a centrifuge tube and mixed with 2 mL of 8% sulfosalicylic acid. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 15 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was filtered through 0.22-μm syringe filters prior to instrumental analysis.

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was isolated from the longissimus lumborum muscle using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Universal RNA Extraction Kit (9767, Takara Bio Inc., Dalian, Liaoning, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. The isolated RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) to construct the cDNA library using an mRNA-Seq Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). After confirming and measuring the cDNA, the libraries were processed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

The process of filtering the raw sequencing data was performed as described previously (Zhang et al., 2023a,b), and the cleaned data were saved in FASTA format. HISAT2 (v2.04) was used to align the clean reads to the reference genome (Capra hircus, GCF_001704415.1_ARS1) (Kim et al., 2015). The clean reads were matched to the reference coding gene set using Bowtie (Langmead and Salzberg, 2012), and the expression level was quantified by calculating the transcripts per million (TPM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified via DESeq2.

The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs were performed by Phyper using the Hypergeometric test (Pan et al., 2021). Expression trend analysis of the transcriptomic data was performed using the R package Mfuzz, as the restricted feeding levels were equidistant (Kumar and Futschik, 2007).

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

The longissimus lumborum muscle samples from the RF0, RF25, and RF50 groups were analyzed using untargeted metabolomics via liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) technology by Beijing Novogene Technology Company Ltd. (Beijing, China). The analysis involved grinding the muscle samples in liquid nitrogen, extracting metabolites with 500 mL of 80% methanol-water, and then centrifuging the mixture. After dilution, the supernatant was collected for LC-MS analysis using a Hypesil Gold C18 column (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. To observe metabolic changes between groups, principal component analysis (PCA) was used for unsupervised data visualization. Orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was applied to maximize the separation between treatment groups and identify metabolites responsible for the discrimination. To ensure the robustness and predictive ability of the OPLS-DA models, it was calculated the goodness-of-fit (R2Y) and the predictive capability (Q2) for each comparison. The OPLS-DA and PCA were performed using the R package model. For the comparison of severe restriction (RF0 vs. RF50), the OPLS-DA model demonstrated strong predictive power (R2Y = 0.944, Q2 = 0.492), confirming a distinct and significant metabolic shift in the muscle metabolome. Furthermore, permutation tests (n = 200) were conducted on this model, where the low intercepts for R2 and Q2 (R2 intercept was < 0.4 and the Q2 intercept was < 0.05) confirmed the statistical robustness and lack of overfitting. Conversely, the model for mild restriction (RF0 vs. RF25) showed high fitting (R2Y = 0.961) but a negative predictive ability (Q2 = −0.435). This result is biologically interpreted as a subtle, sub-threshold metabolic variation that does not result in a distinct global profile separation, aligning with our conclusion of a metabolic threshold effect requiring severe stress to trigger comprehensive reprogramming. Differential metabolites (DMs) were identified through the Metabolome Database (BMDB) and the KEGG for further validation. Metabolic pathway distribution and metabolite set enrichment analysis were conducted using the MetaboAnalyst web server. Expression trend analysis of the metabolomic data was performed using the R package Mfuzz since the restricted feeding levels were equidistant. The number of clusters was set to 6 based on the minimum cluster centroid distance, determined using the cselection function to optimize cluster separation (Kumar and Futschik, 2007).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the R software (version 4.4.1). Prior to ANOVA, data were assessed for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances using Levene's test. The model used for data analysis was as follows:

Tukey's HSD post-hoc test was applied to determine the effects of restricted feeding on the goat growth performance, muscle morphology, free amino acid and fatty acid profiles, and metabolites, with a P-value < 0.05 considered significant. The DEGs were identified via DESeq2 with an absolute log2 (fold change) value > 1 and a threshold of P-adjust < 0.05, using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Based on OPLS-DA, significantly DMs were identified using a rigorous three-concurrent-criteria screening strategy to minimize false positives: metabolites were required to have a variable importance in projection (VIP) score > 1, an ANOVA P-value < 0.05, and a false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted P-value < 0.05, using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure (Heischmann et al., 2016; Sreekumar et al., 2009). A log2 (fold change) > 0 indicates upregulation of metabolites, while a log2 (fold change) < 0 indicates downregulation. Pathway enrichment analysis with significant levels was selected at P-adjust < 0.05.

Results

RF50 significantly decreased the growth performance and cross-sectional area of muscle fiber

Feed shortages in goats were simulated by reducing their feed intake (Fig. 1). As anticipated, restricted feeding markedly impaired goat growth performance; in particular, the ADG in the RF50 group declined precipitously, whereas the feed conversion ratio rose significantly (P < 0.05; Table 2). Morphological analysis revealed no significant differences in the fiber diameter or density of the longissimus lumborum muscle across the different restricted feeding levels (P > 0.05; Fig. 1 and Table 3). However, the cross-sectional area of muscle fiber was significantly smaller in the RF50 group than in the RF0 group (P = 0.017), whereas no statistical difference was observed between the RF25 and RF0 groups (P > 0.05).

RF50 altered the transcriptomic and metabolomic features of muscle

Transcriptomic profiling revealed significant differences in goat muscle under varying levels of restricted feeding. Specifically, 225 DEGs were identified between the RF0 and RF25 groups, with 102 upregulated and 123 downregulated; whereas 234 DEGs were detected between the RF0 and RF50 groups, including 105 upregulated and 129 downregulated genes (Fig. 2A). In GO enrichment analysis, the muscle organ development term within the Biological Process category was significantly enriched in both RF0 vs. RF25 and RF0 vs. RF50 comparisons (Fig. 2B and C). The KEGG downregulation pathway enrichment analysis showed that both the RF0 vs. RF25 and RF0 vs. RF50 comparisons were significantly associated with the protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum pathway (Fig. 2D and E). Notably, the RF0 vs. RF50 comparison also exhibited significant enrichment in pathways related to beta-Alanine metabolism, PPAR signaling, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 2E).

Untargeted metabolomic analysis demonstrated distinct metabolic profiles in goat muscle across different restricted feeding levels. Principal component analysis revealed a clear separation in metabolite composition between the RF0 and RF50 groups, whereas a weaker separation trend was observed between the RF0 and RF25 groups (Fig. 3A). MS2-based qualitative analysis identified 48 differential metabolites between the RF0 and RF25 groups, with equal numbers of upregulated and downregulated metabolites; in contrast, 67 differential metabolites were detected between the RF0 and RF50 groups, comprising 33 upregulated and 34 downregulated metabolites (Fig. 3B). The KEGG downregulation pathway enrichment analysis showed that both the RF0 vs. RF25 and RF0 vs. RF50 comparisons were significantly associated with the beta-alanine metabolism and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathways (Fig. 3C and D). Notably, the RF0 vs. RF50 comparison also exhibited significant enrichment in the PPAR signaling pathway and pentose phosphate pathway (Fig. 3D). Collectively, these findings indicate that variations in restricted feeding levels induce substantial alterations in the metabolic network of goat muscle, highlighting the metabolic pathways most responsive to nutritional stress.

Integrated trend analysis revealed that the CARNS1-mediated carnosine synthesis pathway plays a pivotal role in muscle homeostasis

Given the equidistant increments in restricted feeding levels applied in this experiment, trend clustering analyses were conducted on both the transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets to identify genes and metabolites with consistent expression patterns. As restricted feeding levels increased, genes and metabolites exhibiting a gradual decline from RF0 to RF25 followed by a sharp decrease at RF50 were predominantly grouped in cluster 3 (Fig. 4A and B). The KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of cluster 3 in the transcriptomic data revealed significant associations with pathways including beta-alanine metabolism, PPAR signaling pathway, aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis, fatty acid metabolism, and fatty acid degradation (Fig. 5A). Due to insufficient metabolites in cluster 3 of the metabolomic data for enrichment analysis, ANOVA was performed on differential metabolites between the RF0 and RF50 groups within this cluster. Results showed that metabolites L-histidine and carnosine, associated with the beta-alanine metabolism pathway, significantly decreased with increasing restricted feeding levels (P < 0.05; Fig. 5C). Combined transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses demonstrated that the gene CARNS1 within the beta-alanine metabolism pathway was significantly downregulated (Fig. 5B), accompanied by a significant reduction in the metabolites L-histidine and carnosine. These findings indicate that the beta-alanine metabolism pathway may serve as a critical node influencing goat muscle homeostasis under nutritional stress (Fig. 5D).

Discussion

Seasonal feed scarcity can induce BW loss in ruminants. To simulate such shortages, restricted feeding was imposed; as anticipated, this intervention significantly lowered the ADG of goats. Under feed shortage conditions, animals catabolize muscle lipids or proteins to sustain basal metabolism, leading to altered myofiber structure (Dou et al., 2023). In this study, restricted feeding was simulated in goats to investigate these effects. An RF50 treatment significantly reduced the cross-sectional area of the longissimus lumborum muscle without affecting myofiber diameter or density. This observation aligns with the survival strategy of goats, which maintain myofiber number at the expense of fiber size under nutritional stress (Parreira et al., 2020). Previous research in rabbits has demonstrated that severely restricted feeding diminishes muscle protein synthesis efficiency, thereby constraining myofiber hypertrophy (Li et al., 2023). Consistent with present findings of reduced myofiber cross-sectional area alongside stable density, skeletal muscle appears to minimize metabolic costs under nutritional deficit primarily by downregulating protein synthesis (Ahmadzadeh-Gavahan et al., 2023; Ron and Harding, 2012; Weinert, 2009). Notably, the RF25 group exhibited no significant changes, suggesting that the maintenance of muscle fiber morphology may be subject to a nutritional threshold effect. Specifically, catabolic programs are only activated when energy intake falls below the critical level required to sustain metabolic demands (Keogh et al., 2023). This indicates that maintaining feed intake at a minimum of 75% during seasonal feed shortages may help maintain muscle homeostasis in goats. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant downregulation of the protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum pathway, suggesting that cellular adaptation to energy restriction occurs through modulated protein folding. Additionally, GO analysis highlighted the muscle organ development term, indicating perturbations in myofiber differentiation regulatory networks, thereby indirectly influencing myofiber structure.

As restricted feeding intensified, the concentrations of free amino acids increased progressively in muscle tissue, indicating enhanced proteolysis to supply energy substrates under nutritional stress (Bell et al., 2016). Similar findings were reported by Sandri (2013), who demonstrated that nutritional deficiency activates the autophagy-lysosome system in muscle, accelerating myofibrillar protein breakdown and releasing free amino acids as both energy substrates and stress signaling molecules. Additionally, impaired replenishment of tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates may contribute to amino acid accumulation (Dyar et al., 2018; Zoppi et al., 2010). This observation aligns with the downregulation of the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway in the transcriptome and metabolome, suggesting that cells disrupt amino acid homeostasis through regulated translation mechanisms. Notably, the significant elevation of valine may reflect impaired catabolism of branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs). The concurrent elevation of lysine, an essential amino acid, indicates that muscle tissue prioritizes the retention of essential amino acids to sustain basal metabolic functions under nutritional stress (Mann et al., 2021).

In terms of fatty acid metabolism, the RF50 group exhibited a significant increase in monounsaturated fatty acid (MUFA), specifically cis9-C16:1n-7 and cis9-C18:1n-9, reflecting adaptive responses to negative energy balance (Wang et al., 2024). It is speculate that the accumulation of MUFA, attributed to their higher energy density and stability, may represent a strategy to minimize energy expenditure while sustaining basal metabolic demands (Li et al., 2024). Notably, PUFA, including cis9,cis12-C18:2n-6 and cis9,cis12,cis15-C18:3n-3, showed a dose-dependent decline, suggesting preferential PUFA catabolism under restricted feeding to meet energy requirements, thereby reducing tissue PUFA concentrations (Dai et al., 2025; Zhu et al., 2024). Research indicates that during starvation, hepatic and muscular PUFA reserves are mobilized and oxidized to supply energy (Imbard et al., 2025). The significant downregulation of fatty acid metabolism and degradation pathways in both transcriptomic and metabolomic data further indicates that the body consumes PUFA storage as a strategy to address insufficient energy intake. The downregulation of PUFA under nutritional stress may compromise muscle membrane integrity, a phenomenon potentially linked to dysregulation of the PPAR signaling pathway (Korbecki et al., 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). In this study, the downregulation of the PPAR signaling pathway observed in both the transcriptome and metabolome data further elucidates another reason for the reduced muscle fiber cross-sectional area. Collectively, these alterations in fatty acid profiles could influence muscle stability and nutritional quality (Yan et al., 2023).

Trend clustering analysis revealed that in muscle-related transcriptomic and metabolomic cluster 3, mild restricted feeding from RF0 to RF25 induced gradual changes in gene and metabolite profiles, whereas RF50 triggered abrupt adjustments. This finding suggests a critical threshold for nutritional stress in goat muscle at approximately 50% restricted feeding. While moderate restriction can be compensated for through metabolic adaptation, exceeding this threshold may result in irreversible muscle damage (Raposo de Magalhães et al., 2022). Therefore, during regional feed shortages, it is advisable to limit restricted feeding to within 25% and prioritize the supplementation of nutrients essential for maintaining muscle structural integrity and protein synthesis.

The beta-alanine metabolism pathway exhibited a pronounced, sharp decline pattern in both the transcriptome and metabolome of the RF50 group. The coordinated downregulation of key metabolites (L-histidine and carnosine) and the gene CARNS1 indicates that this pathway may serve as a critical node through which nutritional stress impacts muscle characteristics. Carnosine scavenges reactive carbonyls (e.g., malondialdehyde) and buffers pH to protect muscles from oxidative damage (Braga et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2024). During restricted feeding, carnosine depletion may cause oxidative stress accumulation and accelerate muscle protein degradation (e.g., via the ubiquitin-proteasome system) (Schön et al., 2023). In glucocorticoid-induced muscle atrophy models, carnosine suppresses ubiquitin ligase expression (e.g., atrogin-1) and maintains myotube diameter (Schön et al., 2023). Its downregulation may enhance muscle susceptibility to catabolic effects. Carnosine also regulates phosphocreatine recovery and glycolysis to maintain muscle energy homeostasis (Sharula et al., 2023). Its deficiency may exacerbate the energy crisis caused by restricted feeding. CARNS1 encodes carnosine synthase, the enzyme that catalyzes the synthesis of carnosine from beta-alanine and L-histidine (Kalbe et al., 2023; Riou et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2022a, Wu et al., 2022b). Under restricted feeding, downregulation of CARNS1 may represent a strategy to reduce energy expenditure and prioritize basal metabolic functions, thereby limiting carnosine production (Rahman et al., 2024). Studies in CARNS1 knockout mice have demonstrated significantly reduced muscle mass and disrupted protein homeostasis, underscoring the essential role of CARNS1 in maintaining muscle integrity (Rahman et al., 2024). L-histidine, an essential precursor for carnosine, also participates in histamine production and antioxidant defense mechanisms (Rahman et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2022a, Wu et al., 2022b). During restricted feeding, inadequate L-histidine availability likely constrains carnosine synthesis (Posa et al., 2023; Sajrawi et al., 2025). Supplementation studies further indicate that L-histidine can enhance muscle structure and function by upregulating CARNS1 and myofiber differentiation-related genes (Kalbe et al., 2023), suggesting that its downregulation under nutritional stress may exacerbate muscle atrophy.

Conclusion

This study revealed that RF50 markedly changed the muscle fiber cross-sectional area and the profiles of free amino acids and fatty acids. Integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses revealed that nutritional restriction disrupts muscle homeostasis via the CARNS1-mediated carnosine synthesis pathway. Specifically, downregulation of CARNS1 reduces carnosine synthesis, while L-histidine deficiency exacerbates this effect, collectively resulting in disrupted fatty acid metabolism, imbalanced protein metabolism, and increased risk of muscle atrophy.
