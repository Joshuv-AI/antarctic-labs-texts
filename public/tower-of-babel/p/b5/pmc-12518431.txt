Moral Intelligence: Construction of a Moral Intelligence Scale Developed in Poland
Authors: Monika Dacka, Czesław Noworol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoral intelligence is the ability to distinguish between right and wrong, to make ethical decisions, and to be guided by certain values in life. In view of cultural and social change and the ongoing crisis of values, moral intelligence is of particular importance today in various areas of human life and function. The purpose of this article is to introduce the topic of moral intelligence and its operationalization in order to construct the moral intelligence scale. The psychometric properties of the tool were also assessed. It was carried out in three stages. The first involved the creation of items based on the concept of moral intelligence. Next, exploratory factor analysis was conducted on a Polish sample of 433 people (218 women, 215 men; M = 27.23 years; SD = 4.66). In the third, the confirmatory factor analysis conducted on a Polish sample of 643 individuals (339 females, 304 males; M = 29.03 years; SD = 4.34) confirmed the five factors of the Moral Intelligence Scale. Moral intelligence has a specific structure. Analyses have shown that it is a complex construct consisting of five factors that have been recognized as: responsibility, honesty, forgiveness, conscience, and compassion.Keywords: Measurement, Scale development, Moral intelligence

Introduction

A growing challenge today is how intelligence and morality can work together to create a better world (Gardner, 1999). The modern world is characterized by selfishness, individualism, and consumerism and contributes to a decline in the popularity of moral ideals based on truth, honesty, or responsibility, which is evident in contemporary social, environmental, or political crises.

Intelligence is about adaptability; it is about making the world better not only for yourself but also for others (Sternberg, 2021) or to function effectively in the world, develop knowledge, and cope with everyday tasks (Beheshtifar et al., 2011), or “to solve problems or create products that are meaningful under specific cultural and environmental conditions” (Gardner, 1993, p. 15). According to Sternberg (2019, p. 5), “general intelligence is what enables an individual to adapt to his or her individual environment or set of environments.”

Morality is defined as a set of rules for action, a set of specific norms, a system of values created to regulate human behavior (Cevizci, 2009). It represents a form of social consciousness, a certain ideological relationship, and behavior (Aydin, 2001). “Morality is the measure proper to humanity. In it and through it, man realises himself when he does good; when he acts badly, he destroys the order of wisdom in himself and in the interpersonal and social range of his being” (Jan Paweł II, 1998, p. 124). Morality involves a system of rules that regulate a person’s social relations and the relationships they form in society. The essence of any morality is the search for respect for certain rules (Piaget, 1967). Morality consists of certain basic elements, which include: a set of moral values, mechanisms that motivate people, and ways of making moral decisions (Skitka, 2023). It is generally accepted by society and a specific group of people.

Intelligence and morality are undoubtedly complex constructs that influence human behavior and ethical decision-making. Nowadays, intelligence is perceived as a multidimensional construct (Ayoub et al., 2022). It encompasses many types of intelligence, e.g., social, emotional, practical, and analytical (González-Treviño et al., 2020).

The uniqueness of moral intelligence compared to the others lies in its focus on values and ethical principles. It is a reflection of the universal truths, principles, and behavior of individuals (Borba, 2008; Toprak & Karakus, 2018). According to Borba (2008), there are seven virtues that underpin moral intelligence. These include empathy, conscience, self-control, respect, kindness, tolerance, and honesty. Being a moral person is a complex, difficult, and lifelong process, as is developing moral intelligence (Lennick & Kiel, 2005).

Moral intelligence is sometimes defined as skills, knowledge, and the application of universal principles in solving individual and social problems (Sternberg, 2025). It gives meaning to a person’s life, enables the reinforcement of good behavior (Dacka, 2024; Farhan et al., 2015), and creates our “personal values, goals and actions” (Lennick & Kiel, 2011, p. 21) through experience and learning. Lennick and Kiel (2011) proposed honesty, responsibility, forgiveness, and compassion as four important elements of moral intelligence. This stems from the vision of the new millennium, which should not be about “simply to hone our different intelligences and use them appropriately, but to think about how intelligence and morality can work together to create a world in which a great diversity of people want to live” (Gardner, 1999, p. 4). It is worth mentioning some intelligence scales, i.e., the Yakut Moral Intelligence Scale (Yakut & Yakut, 2021). The scale consists of 20 items, forming four subscales: empathy, conscientiousness, self-control, and kindness. Moral Competency Inventory—MCI (Lennick & Kiel, 2011)—consists of 40 items, measuring four factors: honesty, responsibility, forgiveness, and compassion (10 questions each). Khampa Moral Intelligence Scale (2019) measures the moral intelligence of students and teachers. The scale consists of 62 items and includes five subscales: honesty, respect, kindness, conscientiousness, and self-control.

The purpose of this article is to present the issue of moral intelligence, to operationalize it, and to present the stages of construction of the moral intelligence scale. The motivation for constructing it is the perceived crisis of values, moral principles especially among young people. In the face of many intense socio-cultural changes, including the ongoing process of globalization, expansion of the cybernetic information space, and migration, moral intelligence is gaining importance. It is becoming important to be able to distinguish right from wrong, to be guided by and act in accordance with certain moral norms and standards.

Construction of the Moral Intelligence Scale

The phases of the development of the scale were processed on the basis of the framework created by Koenig and Al Zaben (2021). The first stage included the conceptualization of the issue of moral intelligence based on the literature on the subject. Moral intelligence is a relatively new research topic and is not yet fully understood. The starting point for the construction of the scale was the concept of moral intelligence by Lennick and Kiel (2011) as a four-dimensional phenomenon: honesty (telling the truth, keeping promises, and standing up for what is right); responsibility (the ability to admit one’s own mistakes and failures); forgiveness (a willingness to accept other people’s faults as well as one’s own); and compassion (active concern for others). There are various measures of moral intelligence, but there are no Polish scales to measure it. Hence, this new attempt to operationalize and measure moral intelligence is to develop the Moral Intelligence Scale that includes honesty, responsibility, forgiveness, conscience, and compassion.

Stages of Scale Construction

The first stage of scale construction involved developing statements to operationalize moral intelligence. Questions for the scale were developed based on a literature review on moral intelligence and the conduct of individual interviews with respondents. Therefore, a deductive approach (literature review) and an inductive approach (individual interviews with people on the topic of moral intelligence) were used to create the questionnaire, which follows the recommendations of Boateng et al. (2018). The goal of using both methods was to create a list of items that would more fully reflect the research question of moral intelligence (Koenig & Al Zaben, 2021). From a set of 140 questions, based on apparent validity and an initial pilot study with a target group of 12 participants and researchers who served as expert judges, 106 questions were ultimately selected for further development. Questions deemed too general, unclear, or ambiguous by participants and researchers were removed.

Scale Development

A questionnaire consisting of 106 items was developed. The scale had a 5-step response range from 1–strongly disagree—to 5–strongly agree. In the second stage of the study, a survey panel was conducted and results were obtained from 433 individuals. According to the classic guidelines of Comrey and Lee (1992), a sample size of more than 400 individuals is considered good for conducting factor analysis.

Extraction of Factors

The results obtained were subjected to an exploratory factor analysis to extract factors of moral intelligence. Loadings of no less than 0.35 were considered in the analyses. This value is within the range recommended in the literature (0.30–0.40) for samples exceeding 300 individuals (Hair et al., 2010). Adopting this criterion allowed for maintaining a balance between selection rigor and maintaining the theoretical consistency of the scale. A Varimax rotation was performed. This is an orthogonal rotation method that aims to maximize the variance of squared loadings of a factor across variables and make each factor load highly on a small number of variables and near zero on the rest. Varimax rotation redistributes the loadings so that each factor is defined by its own set of variables. VARIMAX rotation provides more stable and clearer results (Górniak, 1998). Five factors were derived from this, namely responsibility, honesty, forgiveness, conscience, and compassion, with a total of 43 items. In the next phase, the confirmatory factor analysis was carried out on a new sample of 643 people.

A test-retest reliability analysis conducted on a sample of 26 people with a seventeen-month interval between measurements showed high stability of the scale over time. Pearson-r correlation coefficients between subscales range from 0.90 to 0.99, with the exception of r = 0.85 for the Forgiveness subscale, indicating an explanation of 72.25% (Forgiveness) to more than 98% (Responsibility) of the common variance(Appendix 1). However, despite the small sample size, the results reflect the nature of the constructs. Responsibility is generally considered a stable trait, as people tend to have consistent views and behaviors related to this trait over time. However, forgiveness is a contextual trait and can be influenced by recent interpersonal experiences, emotional states or even cultural and situational factors.

Method

Participants and Procedure

Two samples of adult Poles allowed us to collect data for exploratory (EFA, Sample 1) and confirmatory (CFA, Sample 2) factor analysis. All respondents were born in Poland. In the first study panel, 433 people (218 f, 215 m) were examined; subjects ranged in age from 18 to 35 years (M = 27.23; SD = 4.66). Detailed information on the respondents is found in Appendix 2. The second study panel included 643 individuals (339 f, 304 m), aged 18–35 years (M = 29.03; SD = 4.34). Detailed information on the respondents is found in Appendix 3.

The Research Ethics Committee approved the project to Polish conditions. The research was conducted online using two research panels, with two different samples. The research was conducted in December 2022 and December 2023 by the National Research Panel. An invitation to participate in the study was sent by email by a panel member to individuals registered database who met certain criteria (e.g., age 18 to 35). The invitation sent to participants included information about the study’s topic, duration, anonymity, and scientific objective. The results of the first research panel were used to conduct an exploratory factor analysis (EFA), and the results of the second research panel were used to conduct a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

Measures

Measures Used in the First Panel

The questionnaire on moral intelligence (Dacka), consisting of 106 items, was used in the first panel. Subjects responded on a scale from 1—strongly disagree to 5—strongly agree. A number of the following measures were also used together: the Yakut Moral Intelligence Scale questionnaire (Yakut & Yakut, 2021), which contains four subscales: empathy, conscientiousness, self-control, and politeness; the Item-Personality Inventory TIPI (Gosling et al., 2003) in the Polish adaptation TIPI-PL (Sorokowska et al., 2014); the SAWS scale (Webster, 2003; Polish adaptation Brudek et al., 2023); Zimbardo’s Time Perspective Questionnaire (PS ZTPI) in the Polish adaptation by Przepiórka et al. (2016); the Heartland Forgiveness Scale (Thompson & Synder, 2003; Polish adaptation by Kaleta et al., 2016); the Scale of Self-value relationship areas (CSWS-PL) Crocker et al., 2003; Polish adaptation: Wojtaś et al., 2018); and the GRAT scale (Watkins et al., 2003; Polish adaptation by Tomaszek & Lasota, 2019).

Measures Used in the Second Panel

Moral Intelligence Questionnaire (Dacka) after the first panel study was reduced to 43 items. A number of the following measures were also used to determine content validity. Moral Intelligence Quotient (Code of Ethics Lennick & Kiel, 2005, in a cultural adaptation by Noworol, 2021) that examines ten factors of moral intelligence: integrity, honesty, courage, trustworthiness, responsibility, discipline and discretion, helpfulness, kindness, empathy, faith, and respect. Moral Codes Questionnaire (MFQ-PL) (Graham et al., 2011), Polish adaptation by Jarmakowski-Kostrzanowski & Jarmakowska-Kostrzanowska, 2016). The questionnaire is divided into two parts. The first part “Explained importance of moral concepts” asks directly how important a criterion is for the evaluation of a certain behavior. The second part “Moral evaluations” contains statements that do not refer directly to the “moral judgements.” The Dirty Dozen Scale (Jonason & Webster, 2010; Polish adaptation by Czarna et al., 2016). The scale consists of three subscales: psychopathy, machiavellianism, and narcissism. The HEXACO Personality Inventory-Revised (Ashton & Lee, 2009); Polish adaptation HEXACO-PI-R (Skimina et al., 2020) which is a shortened version of the HEXACO Personality Inventory, measuring honesty–humility, emotionality, extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, and openness to experience. Intensity of Religious Experience Scale IRES (Głaz, 2021) has two subscales: experience of God’s presence and experience of God’s absence. Light Triad Scale (Kaufman et al., 2019; Polish adaptation: Gerymski & Krok, 2019) consists of three factors: belief in people, humanism, and kantianism.

The Bidimensional Impression Management Index (BIMI) (Blasberg et al., 2014). The Polish adaptation (Bartczuk and colleagues, unpublished) consists of two factors: managing the impression of agency and managing the impression of community.

Results

Factor Analysis Approach

All data obtained in the study were converted into 100-point scales (Noworol, 2013, Formula 1.11, p. 30) in order to detect statistical dependencies and to facilitate the interpretation of the intensity of the measured phenomena. First, a factor analysis was carried out to determine the initial structure of the latent variables that make up five factors. The meaningful structure was then determined in the qualitative analysis. A confirmatory analysis was then conducted to determine the final version of the moral intelligence scale. In the model, seven of the predictors introduced proved to be significant (Table 3) for the influence on responsibility. The coefficient R2 = 0.688 allows us to conclude that the introduced predictors explain a total of almost 69% of the variance in the responsibility subscale. The adjusted R2 = 0.684 indicates a very good fit of the regression model that is confirmed by the analysis of variance (F(7,539) = 169.998; p < 0.01).

A summary of the stepwise regression for the responsibility subscale (Table 4) identifies the order of the variables that influence the responsibility subscale.

The variable honesty (telling the truth) R2 = 0.552, p < 0.001 has the largest effect on the dependent variable and explains 55.2% of the variance in the responsibility subscale. Including the care/harm variable in the model helps explain an additional 7% of the variance. Including the remaining variables conscientiousness and kantianism explains an additional 5.1% of the variance in the responsibility subscale. The variables consistent action (integrity, rightness), responsibility (taking responsibility for elections), and active concern for others (kindness and friendliness) appeared to contribute the least to explaining the variance of the subscale, allowing them to explain 1.5% of the variance.

The literature points to a number of explained influences of the predictors used in our research on responsibility in various studies. There is an impact of honesty on accountability as evidenced by research conducted among students during exam writing (Bucciol & Piovesan, 2011). Research among managers has shown a relationship between integrity and accountability (Rankin et al., 2008). Greater responsibility contributes to greater honesty (Hannan et al., 2010). Caring actions toward others who are vulnerable are associated with responsibility (Li et al., 2023). Conscientiousness influences responsibility in various aspects of life including those related to cognitive agility (Sutin et al., 2022), professional coping, or professional outcomes. Conscientiousness has been shown to influence accountability as manifested in goal-directed commitment and perseverance toward a goal (Liu et al., 2022).

The literature on the subject has shown the importance of the influence of some predictors used in our research on honesty in the conducted studies. There is a positive effect of authority on respect for hierarchies of values, norms of tradition, and honesty. Research has shown that respect for the rule is associated with honest behavior (Heck et al., 2018). Research indicates that higher consumer perceptions of review credibility are associated with honesty and caring (Yan & Hua, 2021). Bellucci and Park (2020) indicated that honesty may distort perceptions of trustworthiness. Recognizing the feelings of others can affect honesty. In situations of self-doubt, people with high honesty–humility may reduce their trust in others. A sense of commitment to other individuals or groups decreases honest behavior, while a sense of commitment to honesty norms increases honest behavior (Zickfeld et al., 2024). Openness to experience, together with honesty and humility, can shape the way people engage with social, political ideologies (Desimoni & Leone, 2014). Research indicates that religiosity can influence levels of honesty (Arbel et al., 2014). Spiritual beliefs may play a key role in how individuals perceive and respond to moral challenges (Hodgson & Carey, 2017). Regression calculations for the forgiveness subscale (Table 7).

The variable faith in people R2 = 0.41, p < 0.001 has the largest effect on the dependent variable and explains 41.3% of the variance in the forgiveness subscale. Including the recognizing the feelings of others (politeness, courtesy, humility, courtesy, empathy) variable in the model helped explain an additional 9% of the variance. Including the remaining variables recognizing spiritual needs (faith, respect), agreeableness, and presence of God in the model explained an additional 5.3% of the variance in the forgiveness subscale.

The literature shows the importance of the impact of emergent predictors in our research on forgiveness. Faith in people—especially in their goodness—affects forgiveness. People with strong beliefs in a Just world tend to be more forgiving of others because they perceive justice as an important principle that is associated with belief in the goodness of others and forgiveness (Strelan & Sutton, 2011). Recognition of others’ feelings can influence forgiveness, as indicated by research conducted on training programs focusing on the recognition and expression of feelings to increase the propensity to forgive (Kaya & Odacı, 2021). Agreeableness influences forgiveness; people with higher agreeableness are more likely to forgive others because they tend to be more compassionate, just toward others (Kaleta & Mróz, 2021). Also, the influence of faith on forgiveness is discernible in the research. Individuals with strong religious beliefs who perceive kindness in others are inclined to forgive (Fox & Thomas, 2008). Recognition of spiritual needs (faith, respect) affects forgiveness. Individuals with higher spiritual awareness are more likely to forgive others and themselves (Mróz et al., 2021). Belief in God can influence forgiveness of self and others. Believers who engage in religious activities are more forgiving of themselves and others (Fincham et al., 2020). A positive relationship with God, believing that God forgives, is fostering forgiveness of others (Escher, 2013).

Regression calculations for the conscience subscale (Table 9).

In the model, seven of all introduced predictors turned out to be significant (Table 9). The coefficient R2 = 0.679 allows us to conclude that the introduced predictors explain a total of 68% of the variance in the conscience subscale. The adjusted R2 = 0.674 indicates a very good fit of the regression model. The goodness of fit is confirmed by analysis of variance (F(7,539) = 162.485; p < 0.01).

A stepwise regression summary for the subscale (Table 10) identifies the order of variables that affect the conscience subscale.

The variable caring–honesty R2 = 0.517, p < 0.001 has the greatest influence on the dependent variable and explains 52% of the variance in the conscience subscale. Including the active concern for others (kindness and friendliness) models helped explain an additional 10% of the variance in this subscale. Including the remaining variables honesty (telling the truth), humanism, conscientiousness, and helpfulness (helping others), and kantianism in the model explained an additional 5.6% of the variance in the conscience subscale.

The literature on the subject has shown the importance of the influence of some predictors used in our research on honesty in the conducted studies. Concern for others and honesty significantly influence the functioning of conscience. The research indicated that the principle of moral concern for others was related to charity. It contributed to helping people in need (Bekkers & Ottoni-Wilhelm, 2016). When a person does not provide support to others, feelings of guilt may arise (Vaish & Grossmann, 2022). Conscientiousness indirectly affects conscientiousness. Conscientiousness as indicated by research is related to self-conscious emotions, especially guilt (Tangney & Dearing, 2002). A person’s sense of guilt may contribute to displaying more conscientiousness-compliant behavior (Roberts et al., 2009). Humanism and kantianism influence moral consciousness, in which conscience plays an important role (Khasuev et al., 2022).

In the model, five of all introduced predictors turned out to be significant (Table 11). The coefficient R2 = 0.616 allows us to conclude that the introduced predictors explain a total of 61% of the variance in the compassion subscale. The adjusted R2 = 0.612 indicates a very good fit of the regression model. The goodness of fit is confirmed by analysis of variance (F(5,541) = 173.157; p < 0.01).

A stepwise regression summary for the compassion subscale (Table 12) identifies the order of variables that affect the compassion subscale.

The variable care/harm R2 = 0.45, p < 0.001 has the greatest influence on the dependent variable and explains 45% of the variance in the compassion subscale. Including the helpfulness (helping others) variable in the model helped explain an additional 12% of the variance in this subscale. Including the remaining variables humanism, consistent action variable (integrity, righteousness), active concern for others (kindness, friendliness) in the model explained an additional 4.5% of the variance in the compassion subscale.

The literature demonstrates the importance of the impact of the predictors used in our research on compassion in the studies conducted. Caring associated with not hurting others, responding to their suffering, harming them, helping them influences forgiveness. Compassion is linked to empathy, taking the perspective of others, and tolerance of others’ suffering as studies have shown (Gilbert, 2015). Compassion activates neural circuits linked to prosocial behavior and the intrinsic reward system which can foster engagement with and empathy toward others (Kim et al., 2009). Helpfulness reinforces altruistic behavior contributing to an increase in the propensity to help others. Research indicates that individuals with higher levels of empathic concern are more likely to help others, to feel compassion for them (Jazaieri et al., 2018). The primary goal of humanism becomes the pursuit of truth, ethical treatment of people, social responsibility, and justice, which is reflected in the promotion of a compassionate and just society (Croft, 2021). Consistent actions, and in it especially honesty, can influence compassion as studies show (Danielsen & Cawley, 2007).

Discussion

The purpose of this article is to present the construction and psychometric properties of the Moral Intelligence Scale. The article presents the basics of the tool and the rationale for its construction. The Moral Intelligence Scale consists of 38 items and five subscales: responsibility (8 items), honesty (7 items), forgiveness (9 items), conscientiousness (7 items), and compassion (7 items). Validation of the Moral Intelligence Scale including the extent to which the scale measures moral intelligence, i.e., the ability to distinguish between right and wrong, to be guided by moral principles and standards and by declared values, was developed in several stages. The first involved the creation and development of Moral Intelligence Scale items in the form of statements relating to the moral intelligence under investigation. The second involved conducting an exploratory factor analysis. In the third step, the relevance of the scale was assessed using a confirmatory factor analysis (see results in Fig. 1 and Table 1). In the next step, a reliability analysis was carried out (see results in Table 2). In a further step, stepwise regressions were carried out for the individual subscales (Tables 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12). The validation process performed confirmed that the 38-item Moral Intelligence Scale is a valuable and reliable psychometric tool.

Moral intelligence is a complex research construct that includes responsibility, honesty, forgiveness, conscience, and compassion. The confirmatory factor analysis shows that the factor loadings of the items are strong, and all goodness-of-fit indices, including the RMSEA, indicate that the proposed five-factor model of moral intelligence satisfactorily confirms construct validity [Appendix 4 contains the Moral Intelligence Scale]. The regression analyses performed yielded good results and confirmed criterion validity.

Limitations

The analysis carried out gives rise to some comments. During the validation of the instrument, exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses were carried out, resulting in a scale with 38 items. The present study is dominated by Roman Catholics. The analysis of the instrument was carried out on a sample of 18–35-year-olds. Ongoing analyses should be extended to include a group in a broader age range.

Conclusions

The Moral Intelligence Scale questionnaire can be used to examine people in early adulthood. It provides a broader perspective on their moral functioning, their approach to the distinction between right and wrong, the existence of and orientation toward social rules and norms. Especially today, in the face of a perceived moral crisis, young people’s knowledge and attitudes toward morality and the way they assess themselves can broaden the perspective for analyzing their morality and the values they choose to adopt.
