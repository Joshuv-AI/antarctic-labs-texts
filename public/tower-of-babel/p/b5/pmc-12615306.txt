Development and validation of prognostic model based on distribution and density of CD3+ and CD8+ infiltrating lymphocytes in breast cancer: A multicenter cohort study
Authors: Xu Zhang, Zheng Wang, Yiwei Tong, Xi Sun, Weiqi Gao, Xiaocun Fei, Yujie Tang, Juanying Zhu, Zhi'an Li, Kunwei Shen, Xiaosong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic value of infiltrating lymphocytes distribution and their densities in breast cancer is unknown. This study aimed to develop a tumor immune microenvironment-based model to predict prognosis in early breast cancer patients.MethodsDensities of CD3+ and CD8+ lymphocytes in the core of tumor (CT) and invasive margin (IM) regions were quantified. A combined score of CD3+ and CD8+ infiltrating lymphocytes in both CT and IM regions was performed to construct the prognostic immunoindex (PII) as follows, low-PII (score 0), intermediate-PII (score 1 to 2), and high-PII (score 3 to 4). The PII was developed in the training cohort and verified in the internal and external validation cohorts.ResultsA total of 1194 patients were included. In the training cohort, a higher 5-year DFS was observed in the high- or intermediate-PII groups compared with the low-PII group (95.1 %, 89.1 %, 83.7 %, respectively, P = 0.008). Patients with a high PII also had the best 5-year OS of 97.5 %, compared to 96.4 % and 92.7 % in the intermediate- and low-PII groups (P = 0.016), which was also found in the internal (P = 0.011 for DFS, P = 0.013 for OS) and external validation cohort (P = 0.010 for DFS, P = 0.143 for OS). PII was shown to be an independent favorable prognostic factor for DFS (P < 0.001) and OS (P = 0.001), especially in HER2-positive (P = 0.009) and triple-negative (P = 0.007) breast cancer patients.ConclusionThe PII model can predict survival in breast cancer patients, which might provide a novel risk classification to guide further individualized treatment.Keywords: Breast cancer, Prognosis, Immunoindex, Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes

Background

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy in women and one of the major causes of cancer-related deaths [1,2]. Classic clinicopathological factors such as tumor size, lymph node status and molecular subtype could provide important but incomplete prognostic information [3]. Over the past two decades, new methods, including gene expression assays, have emerged for survival prediction and risk stratification, but their accuracy and clinical application remain inconsistent and limited [4,5]. Thus, additional strategies and novel models of biomarkers are warranted to improve outcome prediction over the current staging system.

The crosstalk between tumor and its microenvironment plays an essential role in tumor development [6,7]. In particular, immune cell infiltration in tumor has been proven of great clinical importance in certain malignancies, including colorectal and lung cancer [[8], [9], [10]]. The presence of cytotoxic and memory T cells was associated with a low incidence of tumor recurrence. Several studies have also analyzed the level of immune infiltration in breast cancer and explored its prognostic value [11,12]. It was demonstrated that an increased stromal tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) level in breast cancer could predict better response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy regardless of molecular subtype, and was associated with a survival benefit in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and human epidermal growth factor 2 (HER2)-positive breast cancer [11]. However, most of previous studies focused on patients with TNBC. The prognostic value of immune infiltration in breast cancer remains unclear.

Besides the quantity of immune cell infiltration in cancer, data from single-cell sequencing and spatial transcriptome revealed that the location and functional orientation of various immune cell subtypes can further provide information regarding treatment response and prognosis [[13], [14], [15]]. Several scoring systems, including Immunocore in colorectal cancer, were constructed to reflect the characteristics of the immune contexture of tumor based on the assessment of lymphocyte subgroups in different tumor regions. It has been proved to be a prognostic marker and a predictive marker for response to preoperative chemoradiation therapy, also a reliable risk factor for recurrence and a complement of traditional TNM staging system in patients with colorectal cancer [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]].

Therefore, we aimed to construct a novel scoring system based on immune cell infiltration and location in tumor, named Prognosis Immunoindex (PII), and evaluate its value to predict survival in breast cancer patients.

Methods

Study design and patients

Breast cancer patients treated at the three large breast cancer centers from January 2009 to December 2013 were retrospectively reviewed. Female patients receiving primary radical surgery with histologically proven TNM stage I-III invasive breast cancer were included in this study. Patients who received preoperative treatment were excluded. Postoperative treatment plans for all included patients were determined through a multidisciplinary consultation to ensure consistency. Patients undergoing breast-conserving surgery and those with metastatic axillary lymph nodes were recommended postoperative radiotherapy. All HER2-positive breast cancer and TNBC patients were recommended adjuvant chemotherapy, with HER2-targeted agents for HER2-positive cases. Patients with positive hormone receptor were recommended endocrine therapy. All patients have informed consent to use tumor tissue and clinicopathological data for research purposes. Histopathologic, immunohistochemical and immunological evaluation were conducted on surgical specimens of primary breast tumors. Demographic, diagnostic, clinicopathological, treatment and follow-up information were retrieved from Shanghai Jiao Tong University Breast Cancer Database (SJTU-BCDB). After stratification by age, tumor stage, and molecular subtype, patients were randomly assigned to a training and internal validation cohort at a ratio of 1:1.

The external cohort of breast cancer patients meeting the same inclusion and exclusion criteria from other breast cancer centers was collected. All patients had intact pathological specimens and complete clinical and follow-up information.

Clinicopathological and follow-up data

Histopathologic evaluation of surgical specimens was conducted in the Department of Pathology, Ruijin Hospital. Histological type and grading were determined from the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) sections of the primary tumors by at least two independent experienced pathologists. TMN stage was decided according to AJCC 8th Edition Staging System [21]. The expression of hormone receptor (HR), including estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), HER2 and Ki-67 were evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC) in surgical specimens. The criteria for categorizing ER, PR and HER2 status were based on the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) and the College of American Pathologists (CAP) guideline recommendations [22,23].

Follow-up information was completed by specialized nurses at regular intervals. The endpoints to evaluate clinical outcomes in this study were disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). DFS was calculated from primary surgery till local or distant disease recurrence, onset of other malignancies, or death from any cause. OS was calculated from primary surgery till death from any cause. The last follow-up was completed by July 2023.

Quantification of CD3+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, PD-L1, and TILs

Tissue microarray (TMA) was constructed from paraffin sections of surgical specimens and was processed for IHC staining of CD3 and CD8 according to the following protocol. Antigen retrieval with Tris base buffer (pH = 8) for 60 min, and incubation with primary antibody against CD3 (Abcam, ab16669, 1:100) and CD8 (Abcam, ab101500, 1:100), then revelation with DAB reagent and counterstaining with hematoxylin. Expression of programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) was also detected in TMA using a primary antibody (Abcam, ab205921, clone 28-8, at 1:30 dilution) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Digital images of the stained TMA sections were obtained at 20 × magnification.

Positive CD3 and CD8 staining lymphocytes were separately counted in the core of tumor (CT) area, which was the area within tumor cell nests, and the invasive margin (IM) area, defined as ≤1 mm-wide region centered on the border of malignant cells from host normal tissue from each sample. PD-L1 status was evaluated by combined positive score (CPS), defined as the proportion of tumor cells with positive membranous staining of PD-L1, associated lymphocytes and macrophages with positive membranous/cytoplastic staining of PD-L1 compared to total tumor cells. CPS≥1 % was regarded as PD-L1 positive. The densities of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells (positive cell counts per mm2) in CT and IM regions, as well as CPS for PD-L1, were determined independently by pathologists after satisfactory external validation for accuracy and internal quality control for consistency. The densities of the immune cells stained in each section were expressed as the mean density of the three most infiltrated areas.

The mean of positive-staining cell number was used as the cutoff value to determine high (score 1) or low (score 0) infiltration of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells, respectively. The PII, ranging from score 0 to score 4, was calculated by summing up all infiltration scores of CD3+ and CD8+ in both CT and IM regions.

TILs were evaluated on TMAs as the percentage of stromal immune cell infiltration within tumor borders according to the guidelines of the International TIL Working Group [24]. TILs counts were categorized into low (TILs ≤10 %) or high (TILs >10 %) groups.

Statistical analysis

Univariate Chi-square test and multivariate logistic regression with odds ratio (OR) were used to assess the association between clinicopathological characteristics and PII. Survival was compared between groups using a log-rank test and in the form of Kaplan–Meier curves. A Multivariate Cox proportional hazard model was applied to identify prognostic factors for DFS and OS. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, TX). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient and clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 836 tumor samples from patients with stage I–III breast cancer were collected for the PII development. Sixty patients were excluded after IHC quality control, and four patients were excluded after clinical data quality control. Finally, 772 patients with qualified TMA sections, clinicopathological and follow-up data were included in further analyses for developing the PII scoring system (Fig. S1). The enrolled population was assigned to the training and internal validation cohort at a ratio of 1:1, matched according to age, tumor stage, and molecular subtype. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients were well-balanced between the two cohorts (Table S1). The median age was 56 years (ranging from 23 to 93 years). In addition, 410 (53.1 %) patients had stage I breast cancer, 290 (37.6 %) had stage II disease, and 71 (9.3 %) had stage III disease. In terms of molecular subtype, 497 (64.4 %) patients were HR+/HER2-, 151 (19.6 %) patients were HER2+, and 124 (16.0 %) patients were TNBC. Systemic treatments were omitted in 8 of 151 HER2-positve cases (5.3 %) and 8 of 124 TNBC cases (6.5 %). The rest of TNBC and HER2-positive breast cancer patients received adjuvant chemotherapy, and all HER2-positive patients were treated with HER2-targeting agents. All patients with positive hormone receptors were given endocrine treatment. The external validation cohort contained 422 patients after quality control. The median age was 51 years (ranging from 27 to 84 years). This cohort included 134 cases (31.8 %) of stage I, 217 (51.4 %) of stage II, and 71 (16.8 %) of stage III. HR+/HER2-disease were 231 (54.7 %) cases, HER2+ disease were 99 (23.5 %) cases, and TNBC were 92 (21.8 %) cases.

Immune cell infiltration and construction of PII

Densities of CD3+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes were assessed in CT and IM regions on TMA (Fig. 1, Fig. S6). The mean density of CD3+ T cells was 238/mm2 (95 % confidential interval [CI] 201–276/mm2) in the CT regions and 312/mm2 (95 % CI 253–372/mm2) in the IM regions. The mean density of CD8+ T cells was 161/mm2 (95 % CI 138–184/mm2) in the CT regions and 169/mm2 (95 % CI 135–203/mm2) in the IM regions. Mean density was used as the cutoff value to determine high (score = 1) or low (score = 0) density of T cells.

A combined evaluation of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells in the tumor CT and IM regions was performed to calculate PII in each patient. The PII was calculated from CT-score (0, 1, or 2), determined by summing up the density scores of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells in CT regions, and IM-score (0, 1, or 2), determined by summing up the density scores of CD3+ and CD8+ T cells in IM regions. The percentage of patients with PII = 0, PII = 1, PII = 2, PII = 3, and PII = 4 was 51.6 %, 13.1 %, 15.4 %, 9.7 %, 10.2 %, respectively. Patients were stratified into three PII categories for further analyses: low-PII (score 0, n = 398, 51.6 %), intermediate-PII (score 1 to 2, n = 220, 28.5 %), and high-PII (score 3 to 4, n = 154, 19.9 %).

Association between PII and clinicopathological factors

Correlations between PII and clinicopathological factors were listed in Table S3. Both in the training and internal validation cohorts, PII was associated with ER (P < 0.001 and P = 0.016), PR (both P < 0.001), HER2 (P = 0.037 and P < 0.001), Ki-67 (both P < 0.001), PD-L1 status (both P < 0.001), and TILs (both P < 0.001) expressions.

The relationships between tumor stage, molecular subtype, TILs, and PD-L1 with the PII category were further analyzed (Fig. S2). The distribution of PII was similar among stage I, stage II, and stage III breast cancer (P = 0.132). Regarding molecular subtype, patients with low-PII were more common in the HR+/HER2-group. Compared with HR+/HER2-patients, a significantly higher percentage of high-PII patients was found in the HER2+ (30.5 % vs 12.5 %, P < 0.001) and TNBC (37.1 % vs 12.5 %, P < 0.001) groups. Meanwhile, A significantly higher percentage of positive PD-L1 status was found in high-PII group compared with intermediate-PII group (76.0 % vs. 29.8 %, P < 0.001) or low-PII group (76.0 % vs. 12.1 %, P < 0.001). A higher level of TILs was observed in high-PII group than intermediate- or low-PII groups (all P < 0.001, Fig. S3A).

Multinomial logistic regression showed that the distribution of molecular subtype (P < 0.001), PD-L1 status (P < 0.001), and TILs (P < 0.001) had a significant difference among PII categories (Table 1). Intermediate-PII group was set as the reference. Compared with the intermediate-PII group, high-PII patients were more likely to have HER2+ breast cancer (Odds ratio [OR] 2.47, 95 % CI 1.21–5.06; P = 0.013) and TNBC (OR 5.54, 95 % CI 1.99–15.41; P = 0.001). Positive PD-L1 status (OR 0.43, 95 % CI 0.26–0.72; P < 0.001) and high level of TILs (OR 0.17, 95 % CI 0.12–0.31; P < 0.001) were less common in low-PII patients. While high-PII patients were more likely to have positive PD-L1 status (OR 4.70, 95 % CI 2.70–8.20; P < 0.001) and high level of TILs (OR 6.13, 95 % CI 3.11–12.11; P < 0.001) than intermediate-PII group.

Prognostic value of PII in breast cancer patients

The median follow-up time for included patients was 91.4 months (ranging from 44.5 to 134.0 months) in the training cohort, 89.8 months (ranging from 34.3 to 134.9 months) in the internal validation cohort, and 41.7months (ranging from 6.2 to 107.6 months) in the external validation cohort. In the training cohort, 68 (17.6 %) DFS events were found, including 8 loco-regional recurrences, 6 contralateral breast cancers, 27 distant metastases, and 7 other malignancies. Among 29 cases (7.5 %) of all-cause deaths, 20 patients died from breast cancer. In the internal validation cohort, 66 cases (17.1 %) had DFS events, including 10 loco-regional recurrences, 8 contralateral breast cancers, 17 distant metastases, and 8 other malignancies. Among 37 cases (9.5 %) of all-cause deaths, 20 patients died due to breast cancer. In the external validation cohort, 40 cases (9.4 %) of DFS events were recorded, including 13 distant metastases and 7 loco-regional recurrences. Ten patients died from breast cancer out of all 14 all-cause deaths (3.3 %).

The 5-year DFS significantly differed among the low-, intermediate-, and high-PII groups in the training cohort (83.7 %, 89.1 %, and 95.1 % respectively; P = 0.008), internal validation cohort (84.8 %, 88.0 %, and 94.2 % respectively; P = 0.011), and external validation cohort (83.4 %, 91.9 %, and 96.6 % respectively; P = 0.010) (Fig. 2). Tumor size (P < 0.001), lymph node metastasis (P < 0.001), TNM stage (P < 0.001), ER status (P = 0.019), PR status (P = 0.018), and PII category (P < 0.001) were related to DFS in univariate analyses (Table S4). In multivariate analysis, PII category (P < 0.001) and ER status (P = 0.024) were identified as independent prognostic factors for DFS (Table 2). Compared with patients in the low-PII group, those with intermediate- or high-PII had significantly better DFS (intermediate-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.62, 95 % CI 0.42–0.93, P = 0.022; high-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.32, 95 % CI 0.17–0.58, P < 0.001).

The 5-year OS for the low-, intermediate-, and high-PII patients was also significantly different in both the training (92.7 %, 96.4 %, and 97.5 % respectively; P = 0.016) and internal validation (90.2 %, 95.4 %, and 98.5 % respectively; P = 0.013) cohorts. The PII category was apt to relate with OS in external validation cohort (5 year-OS 92.9 %, 96.8 %, and 98.1 % for low-, intermediate-, and high-PII patients respectively; P = 0.143). Age (P = 0.004), histological grade (P = 0.048), tumor size (P < 0.001), lymph node metastasis (P = 0.001), TNM stage (P < 0.001), ER status (P = 0.003), PR status (P = 0.014), and Ki-67 (P = 0.038) expression were associated with OS in univariate analyses (Table S4). Multivariate analyses showed age (P = 0.002), PII category (P < 0.001), and ER status (P = 0.025) were independent prognostic factors for OS (Table 2). Patients with intermediate- or high-PII had significantly higher OS than low-PII patients (intermediate-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.50, 95 % CI 0.28–0.91, P = 0.024; high-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.15, 95 % CI 0.05–0.49, P = 0.002).

Prognostic value of PII according to molecular subtypes

The prognostic value of PII was further tested according to molecular subtypes. For TNBC subgroup, PII was significantly associated with DFS (P = 0.007) and OS (P = 0.005; Fig. 3). Intermediate- or high-PII patients had higher DFS (intermediate-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.58, 95 % CI 0.21–1.63, P = 0.307; high-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.13, 95 % CI 0.03–0.57, P = 0.007) and OS (intermediate-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.41, 95 % CI 0.12–1.47, P = 0.173; high-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.08, 95 % CI 0.01–0.61, P = 0.015) compared with low-PII patients. The PII also showed prognostic value for DFS in the HER2+ subgroup (P = 0.016). Intermediate- or high-PII patients had higher DFS than low-PII patients (intermediate-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.57, 95 % CI 0.26–1.28, P = 0.175; high-PII vs low-PII: HR 0.24, 95 % CI 0.08–0.70, P = 0.009). For patients with HR+/HER2-tumor, there was a trend of association between PII and disease outcome (DFS: P = 0.058; OS: P = 0.061).

Prognostic value of PII according to PD-L1 status

Similar prognosis was observed in patients with positive or negative PD-L1 status in the overall population (5-year DFS: 83.8 % and 88.9 %, P = 0.580; 5-year OS: 90.9 % and 95.3 %, P = 0.081; Fig. S4). The PII showed prognostic value regardless of PD-L1 status (Fig. S5). For patients with PD-L1 positive status, low-, intermediate-, and high-PII groups had significantly distinct 5-year DFS (59.1 %, 82.8 %, and 94.7 % respectively; P < 0.001) and 5-OS (71.4 %, 92.1 %, and 98.3 % respectively; P < 0.001). For patients with PD-L1 negative status, the 5-year OS was significantly different among low-, intermediate-, and high-PII groups (94.2 %, 97.4 %, and 97.3 % respectively; P = 0.032). The 5-year DFS was apt to differ among the three PII groups in PD-L1 negative patients (87.4 %, 90.1 %, and 94.6 % respectively; P = 0.079).

Discussion

In this study, we developed a prognostic scoring system based on the density and location of infiltrating lymphocytes in breast cancer. Then, we evaluated and validated its prognostic value in a large cohort of breast cancer patients. The PII model was shown to be an independent favorable prognostic factor for both DFS and OS in breast cancer patients, indicating this immune cell infiltration-based scoring system could provide additional risk stratification information to tailor further treatment.

Although powerful, the AJCC classification system fails to provide complete prognosis information. Heterogeneity in the clinical outcomes among patients with the same histologic tumor stage is often observed. In recent years, several scoring systems based on immune infiltration of tumor emerged as a complement for predicting survival and treatment response. Moreover, the location of infiltration immune cells in cancer was getting more interest in predicting tumor invasion, treatment response, and prognosis. Immunoscore, calculated from quantification of CD3 and CD8-positive lymphocytes in tumor immune microenvironment, has been established as a prognostic marker and decision-maker reference regarding adjuvant therapy in colorectal cancer. [7,[25], [26], [27]]. Higher CD3 and CD8-positive lymphocyte infiltration was reported to be associated with a higher pathological complete response rate in breast cancer, while its prognostic value has not been evaluated [28]. Similarly, our study constructed a revised PII scoring system based on the quantification of CD3 and CD8-positive T cells both at the CT and IM regions of TMAs, which correlated with disease prognosis in breast cancer patients. The PII offered a convenient and direct reflection of immune cell infiltration with a simple IHC staining in TMA. TMA has been proved as a reliable substitution for whole H&E-stained slides to identify tumor-related markers [29]. Although the role of TMA in assessing immune infiltration of tumor was still in debate, tissue cores used in this study contained both CT and IM regions to diminish the heterogeneity of breast cancer tissue. The feasibility and reproducibility of PII make it operable and propagable in clinical practice.

The distribution of PII and its correlation with clinicopathological factors in breast cancer has not been reported before. Any factors directly or indirectly affecting immune cell infiltration will also have an influence on PII. According to our results, PII was significantly associated with molecular subtype, PD-L1 status, and TILs in breast cancer patients. Considering PII is a direct measure of T-cell infiltration of tumors, the relationship between PII and TILs is almost parallel. The level of TILs was higher in TNBC and HER2+ breast cancer than in luminal-HER2-negative tumors [11]. This study had similar results that a higher percentage of high-PII patients was observed in TNBC or HER2+ subgroup compared to HR+/HER2-subgroup. It is possible that TNBC and HER2+ breast cancer have more oncogenic driver mutations, which leads to a higher neoantigen load and triggers T-cell enrichment and immune response [30].

The prognostic value of PII was explored in a training cohort. Generalization and reproducibility of its prognostic value were verified in the internal and external validation cohorts. According to our results, PII could divide patients into three categories with significant differences for DFS and OS. High-PII patients were related with the best survival, while those with low-PII had the worst. PII was also demonstrated as an independent prognostic factor in breast cancer patients. A series of studies have reported similar results in colorectal cancer [16,20,31]. With the increase of PII, a longer DFS and OS were observed for colorectal cancer patients after primary surgery. A significant association between a low PII and a shortened survival in urothelial carcinoma of the bladder was also reported. The PII, together with T stage, were demonstrated as independent prognostic parameters [32]. In the metastatic cancer settings, prolonged survival of metastatic colorectal cancer patients was significantly associated with high PII of metastases.

According to subgroup analysis, the prognosis value of PII varied across molecular subtypes. For TNBC and HER2+ breast cancer, higher PII was significantly associated with better survival. The relationship between immune cell-based score and prognosis accords with previous report [28]. The PII could not predict prognosis in HR+/HER2-tumors, which was significantly lower than that in TNBC or HER2+ tumors, indicating PII might not play an important role in anti-tumor procedure for HR+/HER2-tumors. In another aspect, the role of immune cell infiltration was distinct across molecular subtypes. The presence of many immune cell subtypes in TNBC, including T cells, was associated with improved prognosis. In contrast to TNBC, the presence of T cells in HR+/HER2-breast cancer was not abundant and not prognostic. In addition, T-cell-related mRNA signatures using Cibersort were also found prognostic only in TNBC and HER2+ breast cancer [33].

Furthermore, immune infiltration of tumor had a close relationship with some known immune checkpoints such as PD-L1, CTLA-4, and LAG3 [34,35]. Previous studies reported that higher PD-L1 expression was associated with high levels of TILs in breast cancer [36]. A higher PD-1 expression was also observed in colorectal cancer patients with high Immunoscore [37]. In this study, we found a higher percentage of positive PD-L1 status in high-PII patients. Although PD-L1 showed no prognostic value for the entire population, we found that PII showed greater prognostic value in patients with PD-L1 positive status than those with PD-L1 negative status. Low-PII patients with high PD-L1 expression had the worst prognosis according to our results. PD-L1 acts as an immunosuppressive molecule targeting immune cells and is negatively associated with prognosis. We suppose that most immune cell infiltration was suppressed in this group, thus limit the anti-tumor effect from lymphocytes. Except for effector T cells, various immune cells were enriched in high-PII tumors. Other immunoregulatory cells might play a role in boosting immune response, thus bypassing the suppression from PD-L1 [38]. Although there is insufficient evidence to explain the effect on the prognostic value of PD-L1 from PII, it is implied that PII might be a possible marker to predict response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Further studies are warranted to explore the role of PII in tumor immune response and immunotherapy.

Although this study provides substantial evidence on the application of PII in the prognosis of breast cancer, it has several limitations. Firstly, selection bias was inevitable in this retrospective study. Therefore, well-designed prospective clinical trials are necessary to ensure highly qualified specimen and robust statistical analysis. Moreover, the value of PII for predicting treatment response was not evaluated in this study. Associations between PII and response to neoadjuvant or systemic therapy could be explored in further studies. Thirdly, the methodology for evaluating PD-L1 CPS score has limitations. The antibody for PD-L1 used in this study was from Abcam instead of DAKO 22C3, which is not an FDA/NMPA-approved companion diagnostic assay. Consequently, caution is warranted when applying these results in real-world clinical practice or comparing them with similar studies. In a future perspective, more molecular biomarkers and signatures characterized by single-cell sequencing, spatial transcriptome, and multiplex immunofluorescence assays to reveal the TIME of cancer might be added to this IHC-based PII to achieve better prognostic and predictive values.

In conclusion, we developed and validated a prognostic scoring system based on the distribution and density of CD3+ and CD8+ infiltrating lymphocytes in tumor to better predict survival in breast cancer patients, which might facilitate further risk classification to guide individualized therapy.
