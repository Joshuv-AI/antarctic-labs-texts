Comparison of progress test performance between PBL and TBL curricula
Authors: Ahmad S. Alamro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProblem-based learning (PBL) and team-based learning (TBL) are two widely implemented educational strategies in medical education. Although both are aimed at enhancing student engagement and clinical reasoning, limited research has compared their long-term outcomes by using curriculum-independent measures such as progress testing.MethodsThis retrospective comparative study examined progress test performance among medical students enrolled in PBL vs. TBL tracks at the same university. From de-identified data, student scores were analyzed across five academic years, stratified by gender, discipline, body system, dimension of care, and physician activity.ResultsTBL students scored higher in early years, whereas PBL students showed a steeper knowledge trajectory and outperformed their TBL peers in senior years. The PBL students consistently achieved higher scores across most disciplines, organ systems, and care dimensions, particularly in chronic care, preventive care, diagnosis, and psychosocial domains. Analysis by gender indicated slightly higher performance among women than men in both tracks.ConclusionCurricular structure critically shapes students’ patterns of knowledge acquisition. The findings suggested that PBL and TBL support learning in different ways across the curriculum, with each demonstrating strengths at different stages of student progression. These results highlight the potential value of designing blended curricula drawing on the complementary features of both approaches to optimize medical education outcomes.

Introduction

Problem-based learning (PBL) and team-based learning (TBL) are widely adopted instructional strategies, each with distinct pedagogical philosophies.1,2 Both approaches are rooted in constructivist learning theory, wherein learners actively construct knowledge through engagement, social interaction, and integration of new information with prior understanding.3 PBL encourages learner-driven exploration of complex, open-ended problems through small group collaboration and independent study, thereby fostering autonomy and integrative thinking.4,5 TBL, in contrast, uses a highly structured format involving pre-class preparation, readiness assurance testing, and application-focused team tasks that emphasize immediate feedback and accountability.4,5 Despite broad implementation across global medical schools, no clear consensus exists regarding whether one approach more effectively supports the development of sustained, functional medical knowledge essential for safe and competent clinical practice.7

The literature comparing PBL and TBL has largely focused on short-term outcomes, such as student satisfaction, engagement, and performance in curriculum-aligned modular assessments.8 These evaluations, although valuable, often do not capture longitudinal knowledge growth or the durability of learning beyond the confines of specific course content. Furthermore, many comparative studies have methodological limitations due to cross-institutional differences in curriculum design, faculty expertise, and student population characteristics; consequently, the true effects of pedagogical strategy are difficult to isolate.2 Curriculum-independent tools such as progress test (PTs), which assess knowledge expected of graduating physicians across all training levels,9 might offer a more objective measure of cumulative knowledge. However, no prior study has compared PBL and TBL by using the same standardized PT within the same institutional context, thus resulting in a substantial evidence gap in the discourse on curriculum effectiveness.

This study took advantage of an opportunity to compare two distinct medical education approaches, PBL and TBL, delivered simultaneously across two campuses within the same institution. Despite differences in teaching style and structure, students from both tracks took the same annual PT, thus enabling a fair, curriculum-neutral comparison. The aim of this study was to understand whether the design of a curriculum alone might shape long-term knowledge development, as well as to analyze performance trends over five academic years across core disciplines, body systems, care dimensions, and physician activity domains, to provide evidence supporting informed decision-making among curriculum committees, educational policymakers, and faculty developers regarding instructional design and best practices in curricular models.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study used a retrospective comparative design to examine and compare the performance of medical students enrolled in two curricular tracks, PBL or TBL, on a standardized PT administered at both schools.

Setting and curricular structure

The study was conducted at Qassim University, KSA, which operates two undergraduate medical programs using PBL or TBL approaches, delivered on separate campuses. Although both programs are designed to achieve the same graduate outcomes and are aligned with national medical competency frameworks, they differ in several structural and contextual aspects beyond instructional methods.

The two programs were established at different points in time and evolved independently, thus influencing their curricular development and organizational culture. Differences also existed in faculty composition and staffing models, as well as in curricular sequencing and emphasis across program phases.

The PBL curriculum was organized primarily around student-led inquiry, and included small-group tutorials conducted twice weekly and a strong emphasis on self-directed learning. In contrast, the TBL curriculum was structured around pre-class preparation, individual and team readiness assurance tests, and team-based application exercises. Immediate feedback was its core instructional strategy.

Despite these differences in program origin, faculty composition, and curricular structure, students at both campuses were exposed to the same academic calendar, institutional policies, and assessment regulations, and had equivalent access to learning resources, including clinical placements, simulation centers, and library facilities.

Progress test

The PT used in this study was a comprehensive, multiple-choice assessment designed to evaluate the breadth and depth of medical knowledge expected of a final-year graduate. The test included 200 single-best-answer MCQs covering all medical disciplines. The PT was administered annually to all students in both curricular tracks and was considered curriculum-neutral, because it assessed knowledge expected of graduating physicians independently of local course sequencing or instructional design.

Participants

All final-year students enrolled in either the PBL or TBL curriculum who completed the PT during the 2022–2023 academic year were included in this study. Their performance data were retrieved retrospectively, and included PT results in years 1–5, thus enabling longitudinal analysis of knowledge development across both curricular tracks.

Data collection

De-identified student performance data were extracted from the institutional assessment database. The data included individual PT scores (both overall and by single disciplines), body system-based categories, and two Saudi Medical Licensing Examination themes (dimensions of care and expected physician activities). The students’ scores were mapped to their year levels to enable a year-by-year comparison of performance trends between curricula.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics was used to summarize student characteristics and average PT scores across curricular tracks and year levels. For comparison of mean PT scores between PBL and TBL students at each academic year, either independent samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests were used when assumptions of normality were not met. Normality for each subgroup was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. If the assumption of normality was violated, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied as a nonparametric alternative. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and all analyses were performed in SPSS version 20.0.

Performance across medical disciplines

Table 2 shows a year-by-year comparison of average PT scores across 24 medical disciplines for students enrolled in PBL and TBL curricula. In the first academic year, TBL students showed a statistically significant difference in disciplines such as community medicine, family medicine, microbiology/immunology, ophthalmology, pharmacology/therapeutics, and pediatrics, thus suggesting early benefits in community- and foundational-science subjects. However, PBL students outperformed their TBL peers in key disciplines including dermatology, pathology, and anatomy, thus indicating early strengths in clinical and integrative content areas. A shift in performance emerged in the second year, in which PBL students achieved significantly higher scores in anatomy, ENT, general surgery, internal medicine, pathology, pharmacology, and physiology. These disciplines reflect integration of basic and clinical knowledge, consistently with the cognitive scaffolding promoted by PBL. Whereas TBL retained a lead in disciplines such as dermatology and orthopedics, PBL showed improvement in more disciplines overall. By the third and fourth years, PBL students had established statistically significant difference in multiple core clinical subjects, including emergency medicine, internal medicine, ENT, and general surgery, and performance gaps had widened in favor of PBL in microbiology/immunology, pharmacology, and physiology. TBL students maintained advantages in selected domains such as biochemistry, dermatology, and pediatrics in year 3, and psychiatry in year 4. Performance by body system

Table 3 provides a comparative overview of student performance in PT scores across 13 body system domains in years 1–5 for both PBL and TBL curricula. In year 1, students in the PBL track outperformed those in TBL in most systems, particularly the cardiovascular, gastrointestinal (GIT), genitourinary, and women's health systems. The differences were statistically significant in areas such as dermatology (PBL: 28.0 vs. TBL: 14.9) and GIT (PBL: 6.3 vs. TBL: 3.4). However, TBL students demonstrated a notable advantage in pediatric growth and development (TBL: 22.8 vs. PBL: 9.3), thus suggesting a possible early benefit of TBL in developmental topics. In the second year, PBL vs. TBL students continued to demonstrate statistically significant differences in performance in most body systems, including GIT, genitourinary, nervous system, respiratory, women's health, and otolaryngology; the most pronounced differences were observed in GIT (PBL: 30.6 vs. TBL: 22.1) and respiratory system (PBL: 27.8 vs. TBL: 7.5). TBL maintained stronger scores in dermatology (TBL: 42.3 vs. PBL: 26.1) and pediatrics (TBL: 19.0 vs. PBL: 11.9), thereby continuing the observed trend in community-oriented content. In year 3, the performance gap began to converge. TBL students maintained statistically significantly greater performance in dermatology and the genitourinary system, whereas PBL students continued to have greater performance in ophthalmology and the respiratory system. Interestingly, the performance across most systems remained closely aligned, thus highlighting a transitional point in curricular impact. The fourth year revealed broader PBL advantages. Statistically significant differences were observed in the musculoskeletal (PBL: 40.3 vs. TBL: 28.4), GIT (PBL: 48.9 vs. TBL: 34.5), and respiratory (PBL: 49.3 vs. TBL: 44.3) systems. Whereas TBL students maintained higher scores in dermatology, ophthalmology, and pediatrics, PBL students outperformed them in integrated and complex clinical systems, thus indicating stronger synthesis of prior knowledge. In year 5, PBL students demonstrated significantly greater performance in nearly all systems, including cardiovascular (PBL: 48.9 vs. TBL: 38.1), GIT (PBL: 45.3 vs. TBL: 34.5), respiratory (PBL: 61.6 vs. TBL: 44.3), and women's health (PBL: 47.7 vs. TBL: 37.4). Discussion

This study explored how two pedagogically distinct curricula, PBL and TBL, shape medical students’ longitudinal knowledge acquisition, as measured with a curriculum-neutral PT. The findings indicated a compelling trajectory in which TBL students had an early lead in test performance, whereas PBL students demonstrated stronger cumulative growth and eventually outperformed their peers in later years across most areas of the PT.

The findings reaffirmed that curriculum structure is not merely a vehicle for delivering content but a dynamic force that shapes how knowledge is constructed, retained, and applied. TBL's early lead, most evident in first-year scores, reflected the pedagogy's structured readiness-assurance process, in which individual and team tests are followed by immediate, targeted feedback. This rapid feedback loop enables learners to identify misconceptions at the point of need, calibrate their understanding with respect to that of their peers, and consolidate accurate concepts before errors are reinforced.10 Consequently, cognitive load is decreased, accountability is promoted, and factual retention is bolstered during the critical transition from secondary to self-regulated learning.4,5,9 These scaffolds are likely to explain why TBL students showed initial gains in foundational subjects such as biochemistry and medical ethics. In contrast, PBL's spiraling, inquiry-driven process, wherein learners cyclically activate prior knowledge, identify gaps, and revisit increasingly complex problems, produced a steeper long-term learning curve yielding durable, transferable knowledge that emerged in later years on longitudinal assessments. The present findings contrast with more recent findings suggesting that TBL might offer modest benefits in theoretical knowledge test performance (standardized mean difference = 0.37, p < 0.05).12 Schmidt and Rotgans4 have argued that PBL fosters integrated knowledge structures, and its benefits become apparent in complex clinical reasoning and cumulative assessments such as the PT. Therefore, curriculum is not a passive container for content but an active mediator influencing the timing, depth, and durability of learning.

The superior performance of PBL students in disciplines such as internal medicine, surgery, and public health was noteworthy. These areas often require integrative thinking and problem-solving, which are competencies closely aligned with the PBL process. The cases used in PBL are likely to provide repeated exposure to clinical reasoning across systems, thereby building mental models that enhance knowledge recall.10,11 In contrast, TBL's strengths in structured recall and teamwork might be less evident in individualized, knowledge-heavy tests such as the PT.12,13

Examination of performance by body systems and care dimensions indicated a consistent pattern in which PBL students appeared to internalize information more broadly and deeply than TBL students. In particular, the strong results in chronic care, preventive care, and psychosocial aspects suggested educational effects extending beyond factual recall. These domains require contextual understanding, empathy, and longitudinal thinking—qualities nurtured through the reflective and narrative nature of PBL tutorials.3,14

The use of a curriculum-independent PT added methodological strength to this study. Unlike internal block examinations, which might favor one curricular model over another, the PT enables a fair comparison of knowledge acquisition across educational designs.9 More importantly, it reflects curriculum effectiveness through a longitudinal and competency-based lens; this consideration is essential as medical schools move toward outcomes-focused education.16

However, the limitations of using knowledge-based tests as the sole proxy for curriculum effectiveness must crucially be recognized. Skills such as collaboration, professionalism, and clinical judgement, which are hallmarks of TBL, might not be fully captured in written assessments. Therefore, although PBL students might excel in knowledge domains,15 TBL students might exhibit strengths in areas not reflected in these data.5,6

This study has several notable strengths that enhance its credibility and relevance. Foremost, the use of a curriculum-neutral PT allowed for an equitable comparison of student knowledge acquisition, regardless of instructional model.17 This choice mitigated potential biases arising from internal assessments aligned with specific curricular content. The inclusion of data across five academic years enabled descriptive comparison of performance patterns across successive cohorts within both PBL and TBL frameworks, and the findings reflected observed trends over time.9 Moreover, the dataset's rich scope encompassed performance across physician activities, dimensions of care, disciplines, and body systems, thus offering a multidimensional view of student competence. Conducting the study within a single institutional context further strengthened its internal validity by controlling for external institutional variables such as faculty qualifications, admission criteria, and available learning resources.

Despite the study's strengths, several weaknesses and limitations should be considered in interpreting the findings. A primary concern is the non-random allocation of students to PBL vs. TBL curricula, which might potentially have introduced selection bias. Moreover, students might have had differing motivation, learning styles, or academic aptitude before entering the program, which might have influenced performance independently of curricular exposure. Moreover, the unequal group sizes between cohorts might have affected the robustness of statistical comparisons, particularly in subgroup analyses.

Although the study was conducted within the same institutional context, no controls existed for other influential factors such as variations in teaching quality, faculty experience, student engagement, or access to supplementary learning resources. Subtle differences in how instructors structured discussions, provided feedback, managed time, or scaffolded learning might possibly have influenced students' experiences and outcomes. In addition, variability in students’ prior exposure to active learning strategies, self-directed learning readiness, and peer interaction patterns might potentially have contributed to the observed effects. Notably, both programs were delivered within the same university operating under established regulatory and institutional frameworks, thus ensuring alignment of program outcomes and the equitable delivery of core curricular content.

Importantly, both curricula were developed in accordance with the national medical curriculum framework (SaudiMEDs), which provides standardized outcome domains and competency expectations. This alignment helped ensure that, despite differences in instructional approaches, the intended learning outcomes and coverage of foundational content remained comparable across programs.

Another limitation was the exclusive reliance on quantitative data derived from a cognitive-based PT. Although this tool is effective in assessing knowledge acquisition and clinical reasoning, it does not capture essential non-cognitive competencies such as teamwork, professionalism, or communication skills—domains in which TBL may hold a comparative advantage. Furthermore, the study did not incorporate qualitative perspectives from students or educators, which might have provided richer insight into how curricular design shapes learning experiences. The generalizability of the findings might also be limited, because institutional, cultural, and curricular variations across other medical schools might potentially yield different outcomes. Finally, because the study did not follow the same students over time, individual learning trajectories could not be modeled with growth-curve or mixed-effects analyses. Future confirmatory research should use multivariate, longitudinal designs and, where feasible, including controls for differences in curriculum implementation.

Nevertheless, the current findings have several practical implications. Curriculum designers should consider blending the cognitive depth of PBL with the structured efficiency of TBL to create more balanced programs. Faculty development efforts should enable educators to incorporate the strengths of each educational approach, by focusing not only on delivery but also on cultivating reasoning, empathy, and systems thinking. Students might benefit from being informed of the learning philosophy underlying their curriculum, to allow them to adapt their study strategies accordingly.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that curriculum structure influences students’ knowledge development, as measured with a curriculum-neutral PT. Whereas TBL students showed an early performance advantage, PBL students displayed stronger gains in later years and achieved higher performance at senior levels. These findings suggest that PBL and TBL might support learning in different ways at different stages of the curriculum. The early success of TBL learners underscored the value of structured preparation and accountability mechanisms in foundational phases, whereas the later gains observed among PBL students might reflect the benefits of active inquiry, contextual learning, and collaborative problem-solving. Rather than positioning one approach as superior to the other, these results highlight the potential merit of blended curricular models that draw on the complementary strengths of both PBL and TBL. Future research should investigate how these curricular differences relate to broader outcomes, including clinical competence, communication skills, and professional behaviors.
