Objective Assessment of Bone Age in Orthodontics: A Scoping Review
Authors: Prachi Gaonkar, Ajay Kubavat, Shailesh Shenava, Puneet Batra, Chinmaya Hawaldar, Abhay Paul
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEstimation of skeletal maturation using radiographs is often used in clinical orthodontics as it is one the crucial factors in achieving a successful treatment outcome. In Orthodontics, we commonly use hand wrist radiographs, lateral cephalograms and sometimes orthopantomogram (OPG) as an objective diagnostic evaluation into the skeletal maturity of an individual. However, the techniques employed are subjective and account for inter-operator errors. With the advent of technology objective methods have been developed and documented in literature but have not gained much importance yet. This article aims at discussing the recent objective methods of bone age assessment in orthodontics.

INTRODUCTION

The maturational status of a person affects the clinical decisions for growth-modification operations such as myofunctional therapy, extraoral orthopedics, orthognathic surgeries, extraction versus non-extraction, etc.

Estimating skeletal growth is crucial for the success of orthodontic therapy.

Since the development of technology, objective methods have been written about but not used. The primary objective of this article is to introduce objective techniques for assessing cervical bone age within the context of orthodontic practice.

These recent automated methods deserve attention as they enable more accurate growth estimation and quantification.

AUTOMATED SKELETAL BONE AGE ASSESSMENT

Automated skeletal age assessment techniques combine biology and technology. Artificial intelligence (AI) is used by automated systems. The rapid development of deep learning technology has enabled automated bone age assessment systems. Artificial neural networks (ANNs) are a significant area of AI. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), a specialized type of ANN, excel at image classification and segmentation. This capability has been leveraged successfully in bone age assessment.[1]

Automated skeletal bone assessment using hand wrist radiographs

The BoneXpert software, introduced in 2008, utilizes a radiographic image of the left hand to assess the skeletal age of 13 specific bones. Subsequently, it utilizes either the Greulich-Pyle (GP) method or the Tanner-Whitehouse (TW) method to calculate the skeletal age for each bone.[2] The software’s ability to accurately estimate bone age diminishes when fewer than eight bones are included in the analysis. This is especially true in instances where the image quality is suboptimal or the bone morphology exhibits significant deviations from the expected pattern.

AI-based systems like VUNO Med-BoneAge,[3] HH-boneage.io,[4] and MediAI-BA[5] have received Korea FDA approval. VUNO Med-BoneAge provides comparable images and probability-ranked bone ages. HH-boneage.io and MediAI-BA utilize the TW3 method, identifying regions of interest in specific bones to calculate maturity scores and predict bone age.

Objective assessment of skeletal age using a formula-based method on lateral cephalograms

There were formula-based techniques created for the objective measurement of bone age before AI technology was used on lateral cephalograms.

Mito et al.[6] pioneered the research on formula-based bone age evaluation on Japanese girls.

Caldas et al.[7] included both male and female participants of the Brazilian population in their study. To develop two distinct formulas, specifically tailored for males and females.

Female cervical vertebral bone age = 1.3523 + 6.7691 × AH3/AP3 + 8.6408 × AH4/AP4

Male cervical vertebral bone age = 1.4892 + 11.3736 × AH3/AP3 + 4.8726 × H4/AP4

In a study by Kumar et al.,[8] they discovered that the formula developed by Mito et al.[6] was reliable only on North Indian females. They pointed out the need to develop a formula to evaluate skeletal age in males as well.

Chandrasekar et al.[9] validated a formula within the Asian South Indian population.

The formula-based approaches have drawbacks. Every study is cross-sectional and ignores the validation of the formula at various maturational phases. Laborious and prone to error if calculated manually. According to Caldas et al.,[7] the next step was to create software that could automatically determine the cervical vertebral bone age for various ethnic groups.

Automated skeletal bone age assessment using lateral cephalogram

A semi-automated method for classifying growth patterns was introduced by Baptista et al.[10] utilizing software, three Naive Bayes-based pattern classifiers were created and evaluated.

Kök et al.[11] compared seven common AI classification algorithms. They found that the accuracy values of the kNN and Log. Regr algorithms were the lowest. NB and SVM-RF-Tree techniques both offer variable degrees of accuracy. ANN might be the best approach for figuring out CVS.

According to Amasya et al.[12] the ANN model produced the greatest results for the cervical vertebrae morphology classifier models, while the LR model produced the best results for the CVM stage classifier models.

An AI system was created by Zhou et al.[13] AI proved to be a valuable and trustworthy tool for determining cervical vertebral maturity, showing good agreement with human examiners.

Machine learning techniques were used by Kim et al.[14] to estimate the hand-wrist skeletal maturity indicator from cervical vertebral scans. To forecast the Fishman SMI, an ensemble machine learning approach was applied. This approach helps to avoid overfitting, a common issue when training models on small datasets.

Atici et al.[15] introduced a groundbreaking deep CNN equipped with CNN, MobileNetV2, ResNet101, and Xception Deep Learning (DL) models, group of configurable directional edge enhancers.

Liao et al.[16] employed the Grad-CAM technique to identify the specific regions within the image that were most influential in the model’s prediction process. They integrated the concept of label distribution learning into the loss function to enhance the model’s performance.

Li et al.[17] created a fully automated CVM classification system based on four CNN models—VGG16, GoogleNet, DenseNet161, and ResNet152. The ResNet152 model outperformed the other three models for CVM classification.

Seo et al.[18] developed a deep-learning method for precise bone age calculation using the DeepLabv3 + Inception-ResNet-v2 architecture.

A deep CNN was created by Khazaei et al.[19] using ConvNeXtBase-296 architecture in determining pubertal development spurts based on CVM staging.

CONCLUSION

Both cervical bone age assessment and the development of AI are areas of ongoing research. To further improve AI models for this purpose, future training will necessitate access to a large and diverse dataset collected from multiple institutions.

Crucially, comparative studies are needed to directly compare the performance of deep-learning models with human experts.

Ultimately, the successful integration of AI in healthcare requires a cautious approach, with a clear understanding of its limitations.
