Energy Intake During the Transition Period Triggers Substantial Impacts on Body Weight, Milk Production Efficiency, Milk Composition and Blood Biochemical Constituents in Fat‐Tailed Ewes
Authors: Amir Mousaie, Mohammad Reza Ghaedifard, Rouhollah Azizpour, Ahmad Ali Sadeghi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe transition period poses a considerable metabolic challenge for dairy ruminants, primarily due to the heightened energy requirements. This study aimed to assess the effects of varying energy intake (EI) levels on the performance and metabolic status of fat‐tailed sheep. A total of 24 single‐bearing pregnant ewes were randomly assigned to three experimental groups with eight replications each: (1) a control group with EI in accordance with their estimated energy requirements (CON); (2) a group receiving approximately 60% of the CON intake (0.6CON) and (3) a group provided with 120% of the CON intake (1.2CON). The experimental period spanned from 2 weeks prior to parturition to 2 weeks following parturition. The findings indicated that the 0.6CON group exhibited significantly lower intake levels of metabolizable energy (ME), neutral detergent fibre (NDF) and non‐fibre carbohydrates (NFC) compared to the other groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, the energy restriction (ER) resulted in a notable reduction in the body weight of ewes at lambing and on postpartum days 7 and 14 (p < 0.05). It is important to highlight that milk production ranged from 660 to 1500 g/day, demonstrating a significant difference between the ER group and those provided with higher energy levels. In addition, the EI had a pronounced positive impact on the production of fat (FCM), energy (ECM) and total solids (TSCM)‐corrected milk (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the 0.6CON group showed diminished yields of fat, protein, lactose and solid non‐fat (SNF) (p < 0.05), whereas the 1.2CON group displayed enhanced production efficiency (p < 0.05). The ER resulted in a significant reduction in blood glucose and phosphorus levels, while concurrently causing an increase in urea and non‐esterified fatty acids (NEFA) concentrations (p < 0.05). However, no significant effects were observed on circulating levels of triglycerides, cholesterol, aspartate transaminase, alanine transaminase, calcium, total protein, globulin or β‐hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA). It can be concluded that EI during the transition period has a substantial impact on the lactation performance and metabolic status of fat‐tailed ewes.

Introduction

With the rapid increase in the global population, there has been a corresponding upsurge in the demand for animal proteins (Ismail et al. 2020). This burgeoning population is the primary factor contributing to the growth of domestic animal numbers, exerting immense pressure on the livestock sector (Udo and Budisatria 2011). The efficiency of animal production plays a vital role in fulfilling global food requirements. Various environmental and nutritional challenges have resulted in undernourishment among animals, particularly in tropical areas, which has negatively impacted their health and productivity (Faryabi et al. 2023). Small ruminants, particularly sheep, are emerging as favourable livestock options for tropical and subtropical regions due to their smaller body size, lower maintenance energy requirements and greater resilience to harsh climatic conditions compared to larger animals (Valizadeh 2014). In many parts of Asia and certain African countries, fat‐tailed sheep are recognized as valuable genetic resources because of their superior adaptability to environmental and nutritional stresses. It is important to highlight that the majority of sheep are reared within pastoral or agro‐pastoral systems, which typically experience low‐cost feeding and frequent feed shortages (Doreau et al. 2003; Valizadeh 2014; Mousaie 2021; Faryabi et al. 2023). In this context, inadequate nutrition during the transition period can have detrimental effects on the health and productivity of ewes. Research indicated that feed restriction during late gestation had a more pronounced impact on ewe health than during early or mid‐pregnancy (Roca Fraga et al. 2018). As a result, various stressors, particularly energy restriction (ER), lead to non‐compensatory losses for farmers. In Iran, sheep production predominantly relies on pastures, which often face serious nutritional and reproductive challenges. Due to their seasonal oestrus cycles, many pregnant ewes experience ER and negative energy balance during late gestation and early lactation, coinciding with the winter months when feed shortages are prevalent (Valizadeh 2014). Insufficient energy intake (EI) in pregnant ewes can lead to several adverse outcomes, including intrauterine growth retardation, the development of pregnancy toxaemia, decreased birth weight in lambs and a reduction in both the quantity and quality of colostrum and milk produced (Vonnahme et al. 2011). Nevertheless, research examining the impact of EI on the health and productivity of fat‐tailed ewes during the transition period remains scarce. Additionally, the production efficiency of fat‐tailed dairy sheep has not been extensively investigated.

The Kermani fat‐tailed sheep represents a distinct ecotype within the Baluchi breed, recognized as the predominant indigenous sheep breed in Iran and several surrounding countries. Previous studies have suggested that fat‐tailed sheep may demonstrate enhanced resilience to nutritional and environmental stressors (Valizadeh 2014; Seifalinasab et al. 2022). Evidence indicated that the adipose tissue of fat‐tailed sheep exhibits significant plasticity and an enhanced capacity for energy storage and mobilization. They tend to experience a lower loss in body weight (BW) compared to thin‐tailed animals in the face of adverse nutritional conditions (Xu et al. 2023). Nonetheless, the potential variations in hepatic metabolic processes—such as the ability of hepatocytes to perform fatty acid beta‐oxidation and the rate of ketone body production—between fat‐tailed and thin‐tailed sheep in different physiological and nutritional conditions have yet to be thoroughly investigated. Moreover, there is insufficient information regarding the nutritional requirements, specifically the energy needs, of fat‐tailed sheep around parturition. To address this gap, we hypothesized that variations in EI during the transition phase can significantly influence the metabolic responses and overall productivity of ewes. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the impacts of varying energy consumption, both prior to and following parturition, on BW variations, milk production and composition, metabolic blood profiles and birth weights of lambs in Kermani fat‐tailed ewes.

Materials and Methods

Animals, Experimental Procedures and Samples’ Analysis

The present study was conducted at the research station within the Department of Animal Science at the University of Jiroft, Iran. All animal care practices and sampling procedures adhered to the guidelines established by the Iranian Council of Animal Care (1995). A total of fifty fat‐tailed Kermani ewes were synchronized for oestrus using progesterone‐containing vaginal sponges (Esponjavet, HIPRA, Spain) for 14 days, followed by an intramuscular administration of 400 IU of Pregnant Mare Serum Gonadotropin (PMSG). The ewes were then exposed to rams for 72 h. Pregnancy was confirmed using ultrasound imaging conducted with a Mindray DP 6600 system (China), as outlined by Mousaie et al. (2017). A total of 24 ewes (with an average BW of 40.1 ± 0.9 kg), confirmed to be single‐pregnant, were selected 4 weeks before the expected date of parturition. The animals were subjected to a 2‐week acclimatization phase, during which their feed consumption was meticulously recorded. This period was implemented to allow the ewes to adjust to the experimental environment. During this time, the animals had unrestricted access to a standard diet, thereby maintaining consistency across conditions. This methodology enabled a thorough assessment of the specific impacts related to varying levels of EI during the transition phase. Following this 2‐week adaptation period, the ewes were assigned to one of three experimental treatments, with eight ewes allocated to each experimental group. The following treatments were implemented from 2 weeks prior to parturition and continued to 2 weeks postpartum: (1) a control group receiving the estimated energy requirements for ewes (CON); (2) ewes receiving approximately 60% of the CON energy level (0.6CON) and (3) ewes receiving 120% of the CON energy level (1.2CON). The diets employed in the experiment (Table 1) were formulated utilizing the Small Ruminant Nutrition System Software (SRNS, version 1.9.4468). These diets were specifically designed and offered to maintain equivalent protein intake across the groups while systematically altering the levels of metabolizable energy (ME) consumption. We implemented ER through two approaches: dietary ME dilution by incorporating wheat straw and limit feeding by reducing the quantity of feed offered. ER was achieved through the dilution of dietary ME by the inclusion of wheat straw and limit feeding, which involved reducing the feed quantity offered. To achieve the targeted EI while ensuring sufficient protein levels, wheat straw was added to the 0.6CON (the energy‐restricted group) to lower the ME of the diet. Additionally, a controlled feed allowance was implemented for the 0.6CON and 1.2CON groups, ensuring that they consumed 60% and 120% of the EI of the CON group, respectively. To accomplish this, the daily dry matter intake (DMI) for the control group was carefully monitored, allowing for the appropriate adjustment of feed given to the 0.6CON and 1.2CON groups. Throughout the course of the trial, each animal was housed in separate pens measuring 1.5 × 1.5 m2, situated within a secure barn environment. Each pen was furnished with individual containers for both drinking and feeding.

Feed samples were composited and assessed for their chemical composition. The dry matter (DM) content was determined through oven drying at 65°C for 72 h, and ash content was quantified subsequent to the combustion of the samples in an electric furnace (Fan Azma Gostar, Co., Tehran, Iran) at 550°C for 6 h. Ether extract analysis was conducted using a Soxhlet extractor (Behr EF, Germany), whereas crude protein levels were quantified using the micro Kjeldahl method (Behr S5, Germany; method 984.13, AOAC 2002). Additionally, the feed analysis included measurements of neutral detergent fibre (NDF) following the method of Van Soest et al. (1991). Blood samples were obtained from subjects following an overnight fasting period, during which they had unrestricted access to water. This sampling was conducted on Day 14 postpartum via jugular venipuncture. Immediately after collection, the samples were placed on ice and underwent centrifugation at 3500 g for 15 min at a temperature of 4°C to facilitate serum harvesting. The resultant serum was stored at −20°C until subsequent analysis. Serum concentrations of glucose, urea, total protein, albumin, globulin, triglyceride and cholesterol as well as aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), were evaluated using an autoanalyser spectrophotometer (Biotecnica BT3500, Italy), employing analysis kits and protocols provided by Pars Azmoon Co., Tehran, Iran. The concentrations of serum non‐esterified fatty acids (NEFA) and β‐hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA) were determined using an autoanalyser spectrophotometer (Biotecnica BT3500, Italy), following established protocols and utilizing specific analytical kits (Randox Ransod kits, Randox Laboratories Ltd., Antrim, United Kingdom).

Milk production was assessed as outlined in existing literature (Ünal et al. 2007; Burgos‐González et al. 2018). Specifically, the oxytocin + hand milking technique was employed for all ewes on the last day of the experiment (Day 15 postpartum). Prior to milking, the lambs were separated from their mothers and placed in a pen previously provided for this purpose. Each ewe was administered an intravenous injection of oxytocin (Vetocin, Aburaihan Pharma Co., Tehran, Iran) at 8:00 AM, followed by milking and the provision of their daily feed. The milk obtained from this process was subsequently discarded. Six hours later, following the same order of oxytocin administration and milking procedure, the ewes received a second injection of oxytocin at the same dosage and were subsequently milked until their udders were completely emptied. The weight of the milk collected during this milking session was recorded as the 6‐h milk yield and was then extrapolated to estimate the total production over a 24‐h period. All ewes were milked by a single skilled operator to ensure consistency. The collected milk samples were analysed for true protein, fat, lactose, solid non‐fat (SNF) and milk urea nitrogen (MUN) concentrations utilizing an automated Infrared Fossomatic 4000 Milk Analyser (Foss North America, Brampton, ON, Canada).

Calculations

The production of fat‐corrected milk (FCM), energy‐corrected milk (ECM) and total solids‐corrected milk (TSCM) was computed on the basis of the following equations (Tajaddini et al. 2021): (1) FCM4%g/day=0.4×milkproductiong/day+15×fatg/day (2) ECMg/day=milkproductiong/day×38.3×fatg/kg+24.2×proteing/kg+16.54×lactoseg/kg+20.7/3140 (3) TSCMg/day=12.3×goffat+6.56×gofnon−fatsolids−0.0752×gofmilk

The change in body weight (BWC) from parturition (BW d0) to 14 days postpartum (BW d14) was calculated as follows: (4) BWCkg=BWd14−BWd0

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted utilizing SAS software (SAS, 9.3; SAS Institute 2011). The assessment of residual normality was conducted using normal probability plots and boxplots, whereas the examination of variance homogeneity involved generating plots of residuals against predicted values. A mixed model approach was adopted for data analysis, incorporating fixed effects of treatment along with a random effect for the animals within each treatment group. For repeated measures, such as BW and DMI data, mixed models were employed that accounted for fixed effects of treatment, time and the interaction between treatment and time, while also including a random effect for the animal within the treatment × time framework. Mean comparisons were executed using the least significant difference (LSD) test. Results are presented as least squares means ± standard error (SE). Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05, with trends noted at 0.05 < p < 0.10.

Results

Feed Intake and BW Variations

In the present study (Table 2), as anticipated, the feed intake observed in the 0.6CON group was significantly lower than that of the CON and 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, the intake of ME displayed statistically significant differences among the various experimental treatments (p < 0.05). Although protein intake remained consistent across all groups, the 0.6CON group demonstrated a lower intake of NDF and non‐fibre carbohydrate (NFC) relative to the CON and 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). In terms of BW changes noted throughout the experimental period, sheep in the CON and 1.2CON groups successfully maintained their BW up to 2 weeks postpartum. Conversely, the 0.6CON group exhibited a marked weight loss during the postpartum period, resulting in lower BW for these animals compared to the CON and 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). The ER led to a remarkable weight loss in ewes (p < 0.05). Specifically, the weight fluctuations from parturition to 14 days postpartum were recorded as +0.9 kg for the CON group, −2.8 kg for the 0.6CON group and +0.8 kg for the 1.2CON group.

Blood Metabolic Parameters

The blood constituents of lactating ewes are reported in Table 3. As documented, the ER implemented during the transition period resulted in a notable reduction in blood glucose levels (p < 0.05), which was accompanied by an increase in urea concentration (p < 0.05). Notwithstanding these alterations, the experimental interventions did not produce any substantial effects on the circulating levels of triglycerides, cholesterol, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), calcium, total protein or globulin. However, ewes in the 1.2CON group showed decreased serum albumin levels when compared to their counterparts in the 0.6CON group (p < 0.05). Although the 0.6CON group exhibited elevated levels of blood BHBA, no statistically significant differences were detected among the various animal groups in this respect. Nonetheless, ewes that underwent ER demonstrated markedly increased concentrations of NEFA in their blood relative to both the CON and the 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). Additionally, a lower quantity of blood phosphorus was recorded in the 1.2CON group than in the 0.6CON group (p ≤ 0.05).

Milk Production and Composition

The results of this study highlighted a significant positive association between EI and milk production in fat‐tailed ewes (Table 4). Notably, ewes classified in the energy‐restricted group (0.6CON) exhibited reductions in both milk yield and FCM (at 4%) when compared to their counterparts in the CON and 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the trends observed in ECM production paralleled those of FCM, indicating that higher EI corresponded to improved ECM output, with the 1.2CON group achieving the highest ECM levels and the 0.6CON group the lowest (p < 0.05). Moreover, ewes in the 0.6CON group showed reduced TSCM relative to the CON and 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). Although the 1.2CON group had a lower milk fat percentage and the 0.6CON group displayed decreased percentages of milk protein and lactose, these variations did not reach statistical significance across the different treatment groups. However, the yields of fat, protein and lactose were significantly lower in ewes undergoing ER (0.6CON) compared to the CON and 1.2CON groups (p < 0.05). Likewise, the percentage of milk solids‐not‐fat (SNF) was significantly lower in the 0.6CON group compared to the CON group (p < 0.05), whereas the overall production of SNF increased in response to higher EI (p < 0.05). In terms of production efficiency evaluated as FCM/DMI and ECM/DMI, ewes in the 1.2CON group demonstrated superior production efficiency relative to those in the 0.6CON group (p ≤ 0.05). Additionally, the difference between the 1.2CON group and the CON group showed a tendency toward statistical significance (0.05 < p < 0.1). Notably, there was no significant difference in production efficiency between the 0.6CON and CON groups.

Discussion

The impacts of varying energy consumption levels on the productive and physiological responses of Kermani ewes during the transition period have not been extensively studied. The results of this study have the potential to offer significant insights into the nutritional needs of ewes, thereby supporting farmers in enhancing their feeding strategies.

This study demonstrated that the DMI recorded in the 0.6CON group was significantly lower than that of both the CON and 1.2CON groups due to the implementation of limit feeding. The diets were formulated to ensure comparable protein intake among the experimental animals, as supported by the provided data. However, the ME, NDF and NFC intake were reduced in the ER group compared to those receiving higher energy allowances, a consequence of their decreased DMI. The DMI for the 0.6CON group was adjusted on the basis of the ad libitum feed intake of the CON group, achieving approximately 60% of the intake observed in CON animals, as intended in the study design. The results indicated that ewes receiving the lowest energy (0.6CON) exhibited a reduction in BW, particularly following parturition. In contrast, animals belonging to the CON and 1.2CON groups demonstrated weight gain after lambing. Specifically, from parturition to Day 14 of lactation, the changes in BW were −2.8 kg for the 0.6CON group and +0.9 kg and +0.8 kg for the CON and 1.2CON groups, respectively. The weight loss in the 0.6CON ewes may progressively worsen due to increasing milk production, which may place these animals into a state of negative energy balance with potentially detrimental effects on their health and productivity (Tsiplakou et al. 2020; Martens 2023). As will be elaborated upon in subsequent sections, these ewes exhibited lower milk yield and blood glucose levels, along with elevated circulating urea and NEFA, when compared to those receiving a higher EI. During the dry season, the scarcity of pasture can lead to substantial weight loss in animals, with some losing up to 40% of their BW, a phenomenon termed seasonal weight loss (Palma et al. 2016). In the early stages of lactation, the somatotropic axis plays a critical role in nutrient allocation, modulating the secretion of specific hormones and leading to elevated blood levels of growth hormone. This results in a prioritization of nutrient partitioning toward milk production while simultaneously promoting the mobilization of body tissues (Chagas et al. 2008). However, the degree to which lactating ewes are capable of mobilizing their body reserves remains a subject of debate. Notably, it appears that fat‐tailed sheep have a greater capacity to mobilize energy reserves, primarily in the form of fat stored in their tails, when compared to conventional breeds. In addition, a recent study (Mokhtari et al. 2025) demonstrated that lipogenesis and lipolysis of the fat‐tailed sheep adipose tissue would be influenced by metabolite and hormone instability during the transition period and feed restriction. The lessening of adipose tissue lipogenesis and lipolysis might be a haemostatic response to cope with the energy insufficiency for foetal growth and the onset of lactation and also prevent the induction of inflammation, metabolic disorders and infectious diseases during feed restriction or in the transition period.

Blood metabolites have the potential to serve as effective indicators for predicting and preventing metabolic deficiencies, thereby averting serious or even irreversible harm resulting from various nutritional and physiological conditions. The observed lower glucose levels and elevated urea and NEFA concentrations in the 0.6CON group suggest a disruption in energy metabolism among ewes in the transition period. During periods of feed restriction, energy balance is altered as a result of decreased blood glucose levels, leading to an increased reliance on fatty acid utilization as the primary source of energy (Gao et al. 2008). The concentration of glucose serves as a crucial indicator of energy metabolism in ruminants (Yang et al. 2022). Although the reference range for blood glucose levels in sheep has been established at 50–80 mg/dL (Kaneko et al. 2008), it tends to be lower in lactating ruminants. This reduction is attributed to the heightened demand for glucose by the mammary glands (Lunesu et al. 2023). Throughout late gestation and early lactation, ewes often encounter glucose deficits and may develop ketosis due to an energy shortfall, coupled with increased catabolism of amino acids. The energy deficiency can also adversely affect the synchronization of energy and protein necessary for optimal ruminal microbial protein synthesis, potentially leading to elevated blood urea concentration (Mousaie et al. 2017; Faryabi et al. 2023; Desta 2024). Circulating urea levels serve as a key indicator of protein metabolism, reflecting the primary nitrogenous waste generated from protein catabolism. Changes in blood urea concentration can provide insights into an animal's biological response to variations in protein or EI, influenced by both dietary nutrient availability and the physiological state of the animal (Carcamo et al. 2019). The heightened serum urea in ER ewes (59 mg/dL compared to the reference range of 17–49 mg/dL documented by Kaneko et al. 2008) most likely stems from ERs, decreased microbial protein synthesis and increased tissue protein catabolism. The lower dietary energy supply—particularly from fermentable organic matter and non‐fibre carbohydrates, resulting from the reduced proportion of grains and lower NFC intake in the 0.6CON group—may have intensified the mismatch between energy and protein synchronization in the rumen. This could lead to increased protein degradation and a rise in ammonia production, ultimately entering the liver for urea synthesis. Given that the CP intake was comparable among the experimental groups, these facts suggest that the ratios among nutrients in the diet are more important than the daily intake of nutrients in controlling blood urea concentration (Cannas et al. 1998). The circulating concentrations of total protein and globulin did not significantly respond to EI. The blood total protein (7.1–7.73 g/dL) was within the reported reference range for sheep (6–7.9 g/dL; Kaneko et al. 2008). However, the concentration of blood albumin exhibited a downward trend, with the 1.2CON group demonstrating lower albumin concentrations compared to the 0.6CON group. Notwithstanding the observed decrease, the albumin levels in all experimental groups remained within the reference range established for adult sheep (2.6–4.2 g/dL; Carcamo et al. 2019). The likely mechanism through which dietary energy affects blood albumin remains inadequately understood, particularly concerning the impact of sufficient versus restricted EI. Nevertheless, it is reported that in certain species, total plasma protein decreases during lactation, primarily due to a reduction in albumin levels (Kaneko et al. 2008). The reduction in blood albumin levels noted in the 1.2CON group may be associated with a heightened milk yield and increased synthesis of milk proteins in these ewes. Both triglyceride and cholesterol levels remained consistent across the various experimental groups, indicating that the treatments applied did not exert significant effects on these parameters, consistent with findings from other researchers (Carcamo et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2022). Although the blood calcium levels were lower in the 1.2CON group, no statistically significant differences were observed between the experimental groups. However, the concentration of circulating phosphorus exhibited a significant response to the experimental treatments. The response of sheep's blood phosphorus levels to varying EI during the transition period has not been extensively studied. Nonetheless, the lower blood phosphorus levels identified in the energy‐restricted group (0.6CON) compared to those of the 1.2CON group of this study may be associated with decreased feed intake and elevated plasma corticosteroid concentrations. Although we did not specifically measure corticosteroids such as cortisol, some research suggested that nutritional and physiological stressors can lead to an increase in corticosteroid levels, reducing intestinal absorption of phosphate and increasing renal phosphate excretion (Grünberg 2014; Mousaie et al. 2014). In contrast, the greater intake of more easily digestible concentrate in ewes receiving the 1.2CON treatment may have led to elevated blood phosphorus levels in these animals.

The indicators of metabolic profiles play a crucial role in the early detection of energy metabolism disorders in animals. Among the most reliable metrics for assessing ewes’ energy status are the concentrations of NEFA, BHBA and glucose, particularly during the peri‐parturient period (Prodanović et al. 2010; Ospina et al. 2010). Ewes that are experiencing ERs (ER ewes) display elevated levels of NEFA, indicating a compromised metabolic state. Energy deficits during lactation lead to the mobilization of fat from body reserves, which results in increased NEFA concentrations in the bloodstream and an augmented synthesis of milk fat in the mammary tissue (Reddy et al. 2023). Plasma BHBA levels exceeding 1.6 mmol/L are typically indicative of severe undernutrition, with BHBA concentrations often surpassing 3.0 mmol/L in ewes affected by pregnancy toxaemia (Duehlmeier et al. 2011). Consequently, the physiological decline in insulin sensitivity observed in ewes during late gestation further exacerbates disruptions in maternal glucose supply. In response to reduced glucose availability, the mobilization of maternal triglycerides occurs, resulting in elevated plasma NEFA levels. Incomplete breakdown of NEFA leads to an increase in plasma BHBA concentrations. Elevated levels of BHBA may inhibit hepatic gluconeogenesis, contributing to exacerbated maternal hypoglycaemia (Schlumbohm and Harmeyer 2003). Notably, it has been observed that NEFA concentrations tend to exhibit a stronger correlation with milk production compared to BHBA levels (Ospina et al. 2010). According to literature, feed restriction invariably increases NEFA levels in blood, whereas increments in BHBA are less consistent (Leduc et al. 2021; Barrio et al. 2023). In the present study, the documented rise in blood NEFA alongside a decline in milk production among ewes subjected to ER reinforces the previously established correlation between blood NEFA levels and lactation performance. However, a specific concentration threshold for NEFA and BHBA that would predict detrimental effects in ewes has not yet been established. Furthermore, potential differences in the metabolic responses of fat‐tailed versus non‐fat‐tailed sheep regarding fat mobilization and their adaptation to negative energy balance around parturition remain inadequately explored. Research indicated that fat‐tailed sheep exhibit a significantly lower percentage of weight loss (ranging from 18.85% to 26.05%) when faced with feed shortages, in stark contrast to thin‐tailed sheep, which experience weight reductions between 30.27% and 48.15%. This finding implies that fat‐tailed sheep retain a superior energy reserve. Additionally, the seasonal fluctuation in adipocyte size within the tail fat of fat‐tailed sheep is less pronounced than in their thin‐tailed counterparts. This suggests that the enhanced energy storage capacity of fat‐tailed sheep is primarily due to a greater quantity of adipocytes, which provides a buffer against variations in nutritional and environmental conditions. A meta‐analysis has identified a significant set of differentially expressed genes associated with fat‐tailed sheep. In addition, variations in the expression of these genes were noted between fat‐ and thin‐tailed sheep (Hosseini et al. 2023). However, the potential responses of these gene expressions to different nutritional plans and their subsequent effects on tail fat deposition and fat metabolism have yet to be investigated. In the present study, the observed concentration of BHBA in the blood of ewes (0.81–1.16 mol/L) remains below the critical threshold of 1.6 mol/L, which indicates severe undernutrition (Duehlmeier et al. 2011). Furthermore, the absence of significant differences in blood BHBA levels across various experimental treatments may be explained by the relatively mild nature and short duration of the ER or by the increased resilience and adaptability of fat‐tailed sheep, which may optimize the utilization of NEFA through an efficient fatty acid catabolism, consequently resulting in a lower production rate of BHBA. However, the hepatic metabolic processes, specifically the capacity of hepatocytes for fatty acid beta‐oxidation and the rate of ketone bodies production in fat‐tailed sheep compared to thin‐tailed ones, warrant further investigation.

Elevated levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) have been proposed as a potential indicator of pregnancy toxaemia in ovine species. During periods of physiological stress that result in liver cell dysfunction, it is common to observe an increase in the concentrations of both AST and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) enzymes (Hefnawy et al. 2010). The serum quantity of AST in sheep has been established within a reference range of 60–280 U/L (Kaneko et al. 2008). The AST levels recorded across all treatment groups in this study consistently remained within the normal range. Previous research has demonstrated that significant fat mobilization can lead to excessive fat deposition in the liver, resulting in hepatocyte damage and subsequent release of liver enzymes, including AST and ALT. Therefore, it can be deduced that the fat mobilization observed in the ER group was moderate and did not negatively affect liver function. Additionally, this may imply that fat‐tailed sheep have a distinctive ability to mobilize fat without impairing liver functionality (Zarrin et al. 2021).

ER is a prevalent concern for small ruminants across various countries. This issue is particularly pronounced during certain times of the year, more specifically the winter months, which coincide with late gestation and lactation periods. During these times, animals often encounter a shortage of energy‐rich food sources, such as grains. Additionally, economic factors may contribute to energy deficiency, as the high cost of feed can lead to reduced availability of adequate nutrition for these animals (Doreau et al. 2003). Sheep milk production constitutes a significant economic enterprise in numerous countries. Ovine milk is utilized in the manufacturing of various dairy products, including cheese and yogurt (Cannas et al. 1998). In the present study, the augmentation of EI in experimental animals was associated with increased levels of milk production, including FCM, ECM and TSCM. As mentioned earlier, the EI of ewes in each treatment group exhibited variations compared to the other groups. The data gathered revealed a robust association between the EI of lactating ewes and their milk production capabilities. These findings underscore the significance of nutritional management for dairy ewes during the early stages of lactation. Typically, during this period, ewes experience a negative energy balance, necessitating that much of the energy derived from body reserves be supplemented by protein sourced from the digestive process. Research has demonstrated that in circumstances where nutrient availability is constrained or when the physiological demands on the animal are elevated, ewes will tap into their body reserves to fulfil their metabolic requirements (Kenyon et al. 2014). According to the obtained data, the 0.6CON group exhibited greater weight loss, accompanied by increased levels of NEFA in the bloodstream, along with reduced circulating glucose levels. These findings propose enhanced fat mobilization from adipose tissue, indicative of a certain degree of negative energy balance. It has been argued that NEFA concentrations generally have a significant negative association with milk production (Ospina et al. 2010). Fluctuations in BW following parturition are shaped by the interactions between genotype and environmental factors, which dictate the allocation of body reserves toward lactogenesis (Chagas et al. 2008). Given that body reserves are likely to change in ewes throughout gestation, it is important to recognize that pre‐lambing body condition has a positive correlation with both milk yield and the duration of lactation (Hossamo et al. 1986). Campion et al. (2016) indicated that ewes receiving a high‐energy diet during late gestation were able to sustain superior milk production during the first 6 weeks of lactation. Our results suggest that when ewes receive an adequate energy supply, less reliance is placed on body reserves for milk production compared to those subjected to ER. Moreover, ER may negatively influence milk yield through potential underdevelopment of the mammary glands (Muñoz et al. 2009). Research demonstrated that 70% of udder development in ewes occurs within the final 4 weeks of gestation (Tygesen et al. 2007). Ensuring adequate nutrient provision, especially energy, during the final trimester of gestation is crucial for the development of the mammary alveolar system (Mousaie et al. 2017; Tsiplakou et al. 2020). Available literature strongly indicates that nutritional input plays a significant role in mammary development; specifically, underfeeding during periods of NEB can lead to the upregulation of genes associated with programmed cell death in mammary tissue. This process ultimately decreases the number of mammary epithelial cells and results in diminished milk production (Dessauge et al. 2011; Tsiplakou et al. 2020). It has been highlighted that nutrient restriction in the final stages of gestation and early lactation can negatively affect the development of mammary glands in ewes. Notably, significant impacts have been documented after just 3 days of restriction (Mellor and Murray 1985). Additionally, ER may alter the microbial composition of the reticulo‐rumen, with research showing that bacterial concentrations decline with reduced EI (Doreau et al. 2003). However, the specific impacts of these changes on milk yield remain inadequately characterized. In summary, the observed similarity in protein intake and its adequacy alongside lower ME intake coupled with elevated blood NEFA and reduced circulatory glucose concentration within the ER group suggests that energy availability is a primary determinant contributing to the decline in milk production. Furthermore, it appears that fat‐tailed sheep also necessitate sufficient energy levels to fully express their milking potential, as the energy reserves stored in their fat tails may not wholly contribute to milk production. The comparative response of fat‐ and thin‐tailed sheep to ER around parturition has not been studied. Nonetheless, in thin‐tailed sheep of the Lacaune breed, negative energy balance during early lactation resulted in a significant increase in fat mobilization and BHBA synthesis, accompanied by a reduction in milk production (Bouvier‐Muller et al. 2016).

Milk metabolites hold significant promise as valuable biomarkers because they represent the final products of various biological processes and physiological mechanisms related to production efficiency and reflect the nutritional status of the animals (Marina et al. 2024). In this study, we demonstrated that ER led to a reduction in the percentage of SNF in milk, as well as decreased yields of fat, protein, lactose and SNF. Although the comparisons did not reach statistical significance, numerical values indicated that milk protein percentages in ewes from the CON and 1.2CON groups were higher than those in the 0.6CON group, whereas no difference in milk fat percentages was documented. As discussed, protein intake was similar across the experimental groups. Consequently, this observation is likely a result of increased energy consumption, which facilitated the retention of glucogenic amino acids during the gluconeogenesis process. This mechanism led to a greater availability of amino acids for milk protein synthesis (Mackle et al. 1999). It is worth noting that the higher milk yield and productive efficiency of 1.2CON ewes were not associated with a dilution effect on milk fat and protein concentrations. This finding aligns with the conclusions drawn by other studies (Toral et al. 2021; Barrio et al. 2023). Regarding the MUN, the researchers demonstrated a significant association between MUN levels and both dietary CP content and daily CP intake (Cannas et al. 1998). Given that there was no variation in CP intake across the different energy levels, the observed consistency in milk MUN was anticipated.

Concerning the efficiency of milk production (FCM or ECM/DMI), the current data revealed that the animals in the 0.6CON group exhibited lower efficiency in converting feed into milk compared to their counterparts in the 1.2CON group. In particular, the ewes that were provided with the highest level of energy demonstrated a productivity efficiency that was approximately 1.7 times greater than that of those receiving the lowest EI. Enhanced feed efficiency in dairy ruminants indicates a greater ability to convert feed nutrients into milk and its components. As a result, variations in feed efficiency are anticipated to be partially associated with physiological alterations in the mammary gland as well as the quantity and quality of nutrients provided (Doreau et al. 2003; Suárez‐Vega et al. 2023). Additionally, it appears that the EI of the CON group might have been insufficient to meet the energy demands necessary for ewes to attain their optimal milk production potential, as there was no observed difference in milk efficiency between the CON group and the 0.6CON animals. Consequently, providing sufficient energy through high‐quality feed ingredients is a critical determinant of lactation performance in sheep. The complexity and the costs of calculating feed efficiency on a large scale are a challenge for dairy sheep (Marina et al. 2024), so this information may offer valuable insights for the industry. The increased milk production—up to 2.3 times more—and the enhanced feed‐to‐milk efficiency observed in ewes receiving a daily ME intake of 2.82 Mcal (1.2CON), compared to those consuming only 1.43 Mcal (0.6CON), can offer substantial economic advantages for farmers. This improvement not only promotes better health and growth rates in lambs but also enhances the availability of milk for dairy production.

Although the body of literature addressing the effects of nutritional restriction on fat‐tailed ewes during late gestation and its subsequent impact on lamb birth weight is relatively sparse, existing studies indicate that proper nutritional management of ewes during this critical period is essential for optimizing lamb birth weights. It has been demonstrated that failing to meet the ewe's essential nutritional requirements during gestation can lead to a decrease in lamb birth weight of up to 22% (Roca Fraga et al. 2018). In the present study, no significant effect of dietary energy level on lambs’ birth weight was observed, consistent with the research that also reported minimal or no influence of nutritional interventions during the very late stages of pregnancy (Cranston et al. 2017). For instance, McGovern et al. (2015) found that adjusting the energy nutrition of twin ewes to 80%, 100% or 120% of their ME requirements in late gestation did not affect either the birth weight or organ weight of newborn lambs. The absence of significant effects on lambs’ birth weight may be attributed to several factors. The findings suggest that, alongside the brief period of ER prior to parturition, there may be a compensatory mechanism in animals with adequate body reserves, such as fat‐tailed sheep breeds. The observation that ewes in the ER group experienced greater weight loss post‐parturition compared to those in the other groups may lend support to this hypothesis. Thus, it can be speculated that the negative impacts of ER on the birth weight of lambs may be less pronounced in fat‐tailed breeds that possess adequate energy reserves. Furthermore, the relationship between the ewe's weight and lamb's birth weight remains contentious. Some studies have reported that the body condition of ewes does not have a significant effect on the birth weight of lambs (Corner‐Thomas et al. 2015; Cranston et al. 2017), whereas others have found a positive correlation between the dam's body reserves and the birth weight of her offspring (Roca Fraga et al. 2018). Taken together, these findings suggest that short‐term maternal ER during late pregnancy may primarily impact milk production rather than directly affecting the lamb's birth weight. However, the question remains whether a longer term ER would impair the lambs’ birth weight in fat‐tailed sheep, which would require further investigations.

The study's potential limitations included the relatively brief duration of the energy nutrition programme implemented, particularly during the prepartum phase, as well as the exclusive focus on single‐bearing ewes. Future research with larger sample sizes should investigate the long‐term effects of varying EI on aspects such as milk production and the birth weight of fat‐tailed sheep, while also assessing potential metabolic adaptations and direct fat mobilization in fat‐tailed animals compared to their thin‐tailed counterparts. It would also be beneficial to investigate the response of twin‐bearing sheep to different energy quantities during the transition period and to examine any differences from the responses observed in single‐bearing animals, which could provide valuable insights for researchers and practitioners in the field. Additionally, conducting further blood sampling, especially during the prepartum period, and comparing it with the postpartum phase, may improve the understanding of metabolic profile changes during the transition period in sheep.

Conclusion

This study explored the short‐term impacts of varying EI levels on specific productive and physiological performance indicators in pregnant fat‐tailed sheep during the transition period, which comprises the 2 weeks preceding parturition and the 2 weeks following it. The results revealed that a daily EI of 2.82 Mcal of ME significantly enhanced milk yield in ewes (1.2CON group), whereas the predicted ME requirement of 2.40 Mcal of ME per day did not enable the sheep (CON group) to optimize their milk production potential. Additionally, ER, characterized by a daily intake of 1.43 Mcal of ME, adversely impacted the metabolic and productive performance of the sheep (0.6CON group), as evidenced by substantial weight loss, diminished milk yield and abnormal blood metabolite levels. Notably, the nutritional plan implemented in this study did not significantly influence the birth weight of the offspring.
