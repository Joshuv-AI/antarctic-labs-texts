Unraveling glutamine metabolism in ovarian carcinoma: signaling cross-talk, positron emission tomography utility, and therapeutic boundaries
Authors: Jiajia Tong
Published: 2026

I read with great interest the original study by Hélage et al. titled “Glutamine metabolism drives tumor aggressiveness but not chemoresistance in ovarian carcinoma cell lines” (1). Through systematic analysis of metabolic phenotypes in three ovarian carcinoma cell lines (ES-2, TOV-21G, OVCAR-3) and primary ascites cells, this study is the first to confirm that enhanced glutamine metabolism is closely associated with tumor aggressiveness but does not affect chemosensitivity to cisplatin and paclitaxel. This work provides important evidence for metabolic mechanism research and clinical translation in ovarian cancer, especially offering a new perspective for the diagnosis and treatment of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F-FDG PET)-negative subtypes such as clear cell carcinoma. However, to further deepen the mechanistic depth and clinical applicability of the study conclusions, the following points deserve in-depth discussion:

First, the cross-talk mechanism of signaling networks by which glutamine metabolism regulates tumor aggressiveness deserves further elaboration. The study confirmed that the high aggressiveness of TOV-21G cells is associated with overexpression of glutaminase, GDH1, and c-Myc, as well as activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 pathway. However, the interaction between glutamine metabolism and other pro-invasive signaling pathways remains to be refined. On one hand, the study mentions that JAK1 mutation may be involved in the metabolic phenotype of TOV-21G, while Yang et al. (2) found that the JAK1/STAT3 pathway can enhance ovarian cancer cell invasion by upregulating the glutamine transporter SLC1A5. It is thus worth exploring whether glutamine metabolism further amplifies the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 signal through STAT3 phosphorylation. On the other hand, glutamate produced by glutamine metabolism can mediate paracrine signaling via mGluR1 (1), and it remains unclear whether this signal forms a positive feedback loop with the PI3K/AKT/mTORC1 pathway to continuously drive tumor invasion. Clarifying the interaction of these signaling networks will help reveal the core regulatory nodes by which glutamine metabolism drives aggressiveness.

Second, the differential diagnostic and prognostic value of 18F-fluoroglutamine PET among ovarian cancer subtypes requires in-depth verification. The study proposes that 18F-fluoroglutamine PET can identify glutamine-dependent tumors, especially suitable for clear cell carcinoma negative on 18F-FDG PET. However, metabolic heterogeneity among different ovarian cancer subtypes may affect the clinical application of this probe. As the most common subtype, does high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma have lower glutamine metabolism levels than clear cell carcinoma? Fasoulakis et al. (3) pointed out significant differences in metabolic characteristics among ovarian cancer subtypes, so the detection sensitivity of 18F-fluoroglutamine PET for serous carcinoma needs to be clarified. In addition, glutamine consumption of primary ascites cells (platinum-resistant serous carcinoma) is lower than that of cell lines, suggesting potential deviations in metabolic phenotypes between clinical samples and cell lines. Larger-scale clinical samples are needed to verify the prognostic value of 18F-fluoroglutamine PET for patients with chemoresistance.

Third, the impact of glucose concentration on the association between glutamine metabolism and chemosensitivity warrants further investigation. The study found that glutamine concentration does not affect chemosensitivity in medium with 11 mM glucose, which is similar to the hyperglycemic state caused by glucocorticoids during clinical chemotherapy. However, some patients may experience blood glucose fluctuations (e.g., diabetic patients) in clinical practice. It is necessary to clarify whether, under hypoglycemic conditions, glutamine can affect oxidative stress levels by regulating glutathione synthesis, thereby changing chemosensitivity. Mathews et al. reported that hypoglycemia combined with low glutamine can significantly reduce tumor cell survival (4), but the study did not explore the synergistic effect of glucose-glutamine metabolism on chemosensitivity. In addition, the high chemoresistance of OVCAR-3 cells is unrelated to glutamine metabolism, and it remains to be confirmed whether this is related to the high expression of P-glycoprotein or DNA repair genes in these cells (1). Clarifying the independence between metabolic microenvironment and drug resistance mechanisms can provide a theoretical basis for combining chemotherapy with metabolic targeted therapy.

Fourth, the safety and benefit boundary of glutamine supplementation in clinical chemotherapy need to be defined. The study suggests that glutamine supplementation may alleviate iatrogenic damage of chemotherapy without enhancing chemoresistance. However, clinical application needs to clarify the beneficiary population and dosage. On one hand, Nan et al. (5) confirmed that glutamine can enhance anti-tumor immunity by regulating Th1 lymphocyte polarization, so it is worth exploring whether glutamine supplementation can further improve chemotherapy efficacy by improving the immune microenvironment. On the other hand, there are differences in glutamine metabolism levels between clear cell carcinoma and serous carcinoma patients, so it is necessary to consider whether to individually adjust the supplementary dosage according to the tumor metabolic phenotype (e.g., 18F-fluoroglutamine PET results). In addition, it remains unclear whether long-term glutamine supplementation can promote tumor recurrence during chemotherapy intervals by enhancing the glutamine reserve of tumor cells. Answering these questions is crucial for the clinical application of glutamine supplementation.

In conclusion, Hélage et al.’s study lays an important foundation for metabolic mechanism research and clinical translation in ovarian cancer, though there are limitations such as primary ascites cells derived from only one patient and the lack of verification with clinical tissue samples, which need to be addressed in subsequent studies. In-depth discussion on the above issues, along with verifying conclusions by expanding the number of primary samples, conducting in vivo experiments, and applying single-cell metabolomics technology, will help further clarify the targets and clinical application value of glutamine metabolism in ovarian cancer, and promote the optimization of combined strategies of metabolic targeted therapy with chemotherapy and immunotherapy.
