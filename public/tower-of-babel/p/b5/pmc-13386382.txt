Identification and Characterization of Key Hazardous Substances in Precocious Puberty Children: An Internal Exposure Assessment in China’s Typical Coastal Cities
Authors: Xu Li, He Zhang, Xueqin Zhao, Feng Ren, Jing Jin, Yihou Peng, Cuicui Guo, Yubing Dai, Rong Cao, Ningbo Geng, Jiping Chen, Lidong Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Epidemiological studies indicate associations between exposures to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) with reproductive disorders (e.g., early puberty). However, the scientific evidence remains limited, particularly in studies on clinically diagnosed precocious puberty (PP), and the specific pollutant drivers underlying this condition are poorly characterized. Identifying the key hazardous substances contributing to PP thus represents a critical research priority. This study conducted a population-based case–control study to profile the internal exposure to organic pollutants. Using a combination of liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) and solid-phase extraction, we performed suspect screening and nontargeted analysis with high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (liquid chromatography-/GC-Orbitrap HRMS). Nontargeted analysis revealed a broad spectrum of EDCs, including polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, phthalate esters (PAEs), organophosphate esters (OPEs), phenols, amides, and acrylates. Differential analysis between cases and controls further highlighted several pollutant classes, such as organochlorine pesticides (OCPs), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs), per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS), phenols, and chlorinated paraffins. These pollutants were significantly elevated in the PP group. These findings provide new internal exposure data and methodological support for environmental health research. They also offer a scientific foundation for targeted source–control strategies to reduce children’s exposure to harmful pollutants, supporting the broader public health objectives of “Healthy China 2030”.

Introduction

Precocious puberty (PP), defined as the onset of secondary sexual characteristics before age 8 in girls and 9 in boys, results from premature activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. It is categorized into central and peripheral forms, both associated with accelerated bone maturation and psychosocial challenges. The incidence of PP is rising in China, particularly among girls, consistent with global epidemiological patterns. − Diet and environmental pollutants are recognized contributors. , High-calorie diets and hormone-residue foods may disrupt endocrine function, while endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) may interfere with hormonal signaling, such as bisphenol A, phthalates, and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs).

Epidemiological studies indicate the association between EDC exposures with reproductive disorders such as early puberty. To date, 109 chemicals have been identified as known or potential EDCs, including pesticides and industrial compounds, with over 1800 substances showing estrogenic activity in high-throughput screens. Exposure to organochlorine pesticides (OCPs), flame retardants (FRs), pentachlorophenol, and other estrogen-mimicking chemicals (e.g., phenols) has been epidemiologically linked to PP. − These compounds can alter hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis regulation by mimicking or antagonizing natural hormones. However, despite evidence from wildlife and in vitro studies, the relationship in humans remains inadequately characterized, particularly the specific pollutant drivers are poorly characterized. This is mainly attributed to the exposure complexity, genetic variability, and methodological limitations in human studies.

Environmental health studies increasingly link disease to single or combined environmental factors, yet evidence remains limited for PP. Specifically, the key causative pollutants, their exposure sources, and biological mechanisms have been poorly characterized. Identifying these critical agents is thus a central challenge in the field. More than 300 environmental pollutants have been detected in the human body, including persistent organic pollutants, heavy metals, pesticides, and emerging contaminants (e.g., microplastics and perfluoroalkyl substances). Once internalized, these compounds can undergo metabolic activation (e.g., oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, and conjugation), potentially forming more bioactive metabolites. This metabolic complexity, combined with the vast number of candidate chemicals, makes pinpointing the specific drivers of PP akin to finding a needle in a haystack.

High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) has advanced human biomonitoring by enabling targeted, suspect, and nontargeted screening (NTS) for comprehensive exposure assessment. − While targeted methods quantify predefined chemicals, suspect and NTS approaches uncover unexpected or unknown contaminants. Nevertheless, most biomonitoring studies still rely on targeted analysis, with limited suspect or NTS research revealing plasticizers, pesticides, halogenated compounds, and personal care products in human samples. In the specific context of PP, suspect and NTS remain scarcely applied, and key causative pollutants have yet to be identified. Two major challenges impede progress in this area. − First, sample preparation must balance selectivity and sensitivityremoving matrix interferents while retaining trace-level contaminants amid abundant endogenous compounds, which often differ in concentration by 3–5 orders of magnitude. Second, a multidimensional analytical system integrating LC-Orbitrap HRMS and GC-Orbitrap HRMS is urgently needed to effectively identify relevant chemicals from over 100 million registered substances. Such a platform will be essential to pinpointing key hazardous exposures affecting PP.

This study conducted a population-based investigation on internal exposure to organic pollutants, employing a case–control study design combined with dual HRMS analysis (LC-Orbitrap HRMS and GC-Orbitrap HRMS). Through the integration of liquid–liquid extraction (LLE) and solid-phase extraction (SPE), we conducted comprehensive suspect screening and nontargeted analysis to systematically identify hazardous substances. Notably, this approach enabled the discovery of differential pollutant profiles between case and control groups, revealing specific key pollutants associated with PPa finding that advances beyond conventional exposure assessments. By providing novel internal exposure data and a refined methodological framework, this study contributes significant empirical and analytical support to environmental health research. Furthermore, it establishes a scientific basis for developing targeted source–control strategies aimed at reducing children’s exposure to harmful pollutants, thereby aligning with and supporting the public health objectives of “Healthy China 2030.”

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Methods

Dichloromethane (DCM) and n-hexane (pesticide residue grade) were obtained from Baker (Ihilianapolis, NJ, USA). Chromatographic grade acetonitrile (ACN) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Lewis, MO, USA). Ultrapure water was produced by Millipore System (Massachusetts, USA). 13C-labeled di-n-amyl phthalate (13C DnAP, 1000 μg/mL) was purchased from ANPEL Laboratory Technologies, Inc. (Shanghai), and 13C6 4-tert-butylphenol (100 μg/mL) standards were purchased from Alta Scientific Co., Ltd. Packing materials included anhydrous sodium sulfate (Damao Chemical Reagent Factory, Tianjin, China), sodium chloride (Aladdin, 99%), Florisil (LGC, 60–100 mesh), and C18 (Fuji Silysia Chemical Ltd.). Prior to use, some packing materials needed pretreatment in the following steps. Florisil was activated at 650 °C for 2 h and then stored in a desiccator. Anhydrous sodium sulfate was ultrasonically washed twice for 10 min, followed by oven-drying at 250 °C for 2 h. Sodium chloride was dried at 300 °C for 2 h.

Sample Collection

Sample collection was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University and Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (No. 2021-100 and 086B00), and the study was carried out in accordance with The International Code of Medical Ethics of the World Medical Association for experiments involving humans. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture using BD Vacutainer serum tubes (BD Biosciences, USA) with a clot activator and gel separator. After centrifugation at 3500 r/min for 10 min at 4 °C within 2 h, the serum sample was transferred immediately to polypropylene tubes and stored at −80 °C until analysis. A total of 923 serum samples were collected from the clinical laboratory of a local hospital from November 2022 to June 2024 in Dalian (n = 309) and Guangzhou (n = 614), respectively. These serum samples were obtained from children who underwent a physical examination and clinical diagnosis. They can be divided into three categories, namely, physical examination (n = 307) as the control group and peripheral PP (n = 322) and central PP (n = 294) as the case group.

Sample Pretreatment

Pooled Samples

To reduce interindividual variation, a representative pooled serum sample was prepared by combining every 100 individual serum samples. For each batch, pooled samples were prepared for both the case group and the control group. When the number of case group samples was fewer than 100, equal-volume aliquots were combined to prepare a 1 mL pooled sample. Based on the differences in sampling time and sample processing time, the pooled samples were divided into two batches (P1 and P2). Each batch included pooled samples from the control group (CON), peripheral PP group (PPP), and central PP group (CPP). Within the same batch, samples were further categorized by the geographic collection region into the Dalian (DL) group and Guangzhou (GZ) group.

Extraction and Phase Separation

One milliliter of thawed serum and 1 mL of ultrapure water were transferred into a 30 mL centrifuge tube and mixed thoroughly; 4 mL of acetonitrile (ACN) was added and vortexed for 2 min. The mixture was incubated for 1 h to ensure complete protein precipitation. Subsequently, 1.6 g of anhydrous sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) and 0.4 g of sodium chloride (NaCl) were added to the mixture. Then, the mixture was vortexed vigorously to promote phase separation and then centrifuged, finally collecting the ACN phase. For liquid–liquid extraction, 2 mL of n-hexane was added to the ACN phase, vortexed for 1 min, and centrifuged to separate the organic layers. The extraction was repeated twice. All n-hexane fractions were combined and concentrated to approximately 0.5 mL under a gentle nitrogen stream. The residual hexane and ACN phase were subjected to further cleanup steps.

Cleanup

For the hexane phase, 0.5 g of Florisil was loaded into an SPE column and activated with 15 mL of n-hexane and 15 mL of hexane/DCM (1:1, v/v). Loading the hexane phase into a Florisil column, the target compounds were sequentially eluted with 10 mL of n-hexane and 10 mL of hexane/DCM (1:1, v/v). The eluates were evaporated to near dryness under a mild stream of nitrogen. Finally, 10 μL of 13C DnAP (100 ng/mL) was added as an injection internal standard prior to GC-Orbitrap MS analysis. For the ACN phase, 0.5 g of the C18 sorbent was packed into an SPE column and activated with 15 mL of ACN. Loading the ACN phase onto the C18 column, the target was eluted with 10 mL of ACN. The eluate was evaporated to 1 mL under a gentle nitrogen stream, and then 10 μL of 13C6-4-tBP (100 ng/mL) was added as an internal standard prior to liquid chromatography (LC)-Orbitrap MS analysis.

Instrumental Analysis

GC-Orbitrap MS Analysis

The samples were analyzed using a GC Q-Exactive MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). In detail, 1 μL of concentrated extract was injected into an Agilent TG-5 SILMS column (15 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm) in split mode, with the ratio set at 5:1. Electron ionization (EI) and chemical ionization (CI) were all used for analysis. The injection temperature was set at 270 °C (EI) and 280 °C (CI), and the purge flow was 5.0 mL/min. The column temperature (for EI) was ramped with the following program: an initial temperature of 40 °C, holding for 1 min, ramping from 20 °C/min to 200 °C, and finally ramping from 10 °C/min to 310 °C, holding for 5 min. Helium was used as a carrier gas with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The column temperature (for CI) was ramped with the following program: an initial temperature of 100 °C, holding for 2 min, ramping the temperature from 20 °C/min to 160 °C (holding for 2 min), and finally ramping the temperature from 30 °C/min to 310 °C (holding for 10 min). Helium was used as a carrier gas with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min and methane as the reagent gas for CI with a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. The ion source temperature was set at 250 °C, and the transfer line temperatures were set at 260 °C (EI) and 220 °C (CI). The mass spectrometer ran at the following specifications: full MS scan (50–750 m/z for EI, 200–750 for CI) at 60,000 resolution (at m/z 200). The automatic gain control was set to 1 × 106.

UPLC-Orbitrap MS Analysis

An ultraperformance LC (UPLC, Vanquish Flex, Thermo Scientific, USA) system coupled with a high-resolution mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 120, Thermo Scientific, USA) comprising an electrospray ionization (ESI) source was used for both suspect and nontarget analysis. Separation was performed in gradient elution mode with a temperature-controlled (at 40 °C) chromatographic column (Acclaim RSLC 120 C18, 2.1 × 150 mm, 2.2 μm particle size, Thermo Scientific, USA). Two binary LC mobile-phase compositions were used for linear gradient elution running in positive and negative (ESI+ and ESI–) modes (i.e., 2.5 mmol/L NH4Ac-water [A] containing 0.1% formic acid [v/v %] was paired with methanol [B]). The same linear gradient elution program of the LC method was applied to both mobile-phase compositions, which was composed of the following steps within the total run time of 15 min. In the Orbitrap MS system, parameters for the analysis were the following: sheath gas and auxiliary gas were all N2 with flow rates of 45 and 8 psi, respectively. Spray voltages were 3400 and 2800 V for ESI+ and ESI– modes, respectively; vaporizer and ion transfer tube temperatures were 350 and 320 °C. More details are provided in Table S1.

Development of the Regional Database for Compound Screening

A total of 487 organic compounds belonging to different families were selected as target contaminants to construct a regional database for suspect analysis (Figure S1). The selected compounds included pesticides (n = 88), polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs, n = 111), benzenes and derivatives (n = 22), organophosphate esters (OPEs, n = 33), phthalate esters (PAEs, n = 18), phenols and derivatives (n = 18), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) and derivatives (PAHs, n = 22), polybrominated diphenyl ethers (PBDEs, n = 20), and other industrial chemicals (n = 173), such as synthetic colors, plasticizers, surfactants, and FRs, all of which have recently emerged as a new class of environmental contaminants and raised increasing concerns. The chemicals in the regional database cover a wide range of polarities and molecular weights ranging from 79 to 642 m/z. The database includes additional compound information, such as compound name, chemical formula, CAS number, isotopic pattern, and MS/MS fragments (with relative abundance >10%).

Screening Strategy

Following the establishment of the regional chemical database, a tiered analytical strategy was implemented to prioritize hazardous contaminants in serum samples. As illustrated in Figure , the analytical workflow incorporates two complementary approaches: (1) comprehensive NTS and (2) suspect screening-assisted differential analysis. NTS was used to characterize prominent EDCs with significant signal intensity differences in both case and control groups, feasible for the typical confirmation of major EDCs in human serum. Subsequently, differential screening was performed to systematically identify and prioritize case-specific hazardous pollutants from the pre-established database.

Full-Scan Mass Spectra for Suspect and Nontarget Screening Using GC-Orbitrap MS

High accurate mass spectrometric data of individual compounds were obtained using GC Q-Exactive MS (in EI+ and CI– modes) and TraceFinder (version 4.1, Thermo Scientific, USA) to obtain detailed information on real chromatographic peaks, MS spectra, accurate masses, retention times, and performing National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) library match searches (with the instrument response exceeding the baseline threshold of 105) for NTS. Screening parameters were as follows: deconvolution threshold of 100,000, extracted ion chromatogram peak apex overlap setting >95%, match >500 (60%), and quality error <10 ppm. Screening of suspicious compounds was also performed using TraceFinder software analysis, conducting regional database searches and identifying compounds by matching accurate mass (mass error ≤5 ppm), retention time (window within 0.5 min), and ion ratio (tolerance ≤50%). Only features with significant differences in peak area between the disease group and the control group were retained.

Full-Scan and MS/MS Spectra for Nontarget Screening Using LC-Orbitrap MS

High accurate mass spectrometric data of individual compounds were obtained using an LC-Orbitrap Exploris 120 MS system (in ESI+ and ESI– modes) and Compound Discoverer (version 3.3, Thermo Scientific, USA) to obtain detailed information on real chromatographic peaks, MS spectra, accurate masses, retention times, precursors, and MS/MS fragment ions of individual compounds. These data can be efficiently used in the identification and confirmation process for suspects and nontarget screening. A tolerance of 5 ppm was selected as a mass filter. In contrast to suspect screening, nontarget screening is used without prior information for the compound to be detected. Accurate masses were obtained from the chromatographic peaks in HRMS using the average spectrum function of the Xcalibur software. For the screening of unknown contaminants, the selection of masses was based on the intensity and presence of MS/MS information. The possible molecular formulas were generated based on the measured exact masses. All potential formulas with a confidence >90% were considered with a filter containing C, H, O, N, P, S, Cl and F elements. The online databases mzCloud, ChemSpider, and PubChem were used for deducing the possible names and structures matched with the empirical formulas and exact masses via the Compound Discoverer software by comparing the experimental results with their theoretically predicted fragments.

Results and Discussion

Application of Nontarget Screening to Identify the Major Chemicals

Comparison of Major Chemical Residuals in Case–Control Groups via GC-Orbitrap MS

Nontarget screening by deconvolution was performed in two batches of samples from GZ and DL city, and the major compounds were identified and classified into endogenous and exogenous compounds. As shown in Sankey diagrams (Figure ), a limited variety of endogenous compounds were identified, with squalene, oleic acid, isopropyl myristate, and cis-vaccenic acid being the predominant ones. In contrast, a greater diversity of exogenous compounds was detected, and they were classified into several categories, including fatty acid esters (FAEs), PAHs and their derivatives, phenols, acrylates (ACRs), ketones, OPEs, PAEs, amides etc., which are mainly used as pesticides, dyes, plasticizers, antioxidants, softeners, and rubber additives, as well as flavors and preservatives, and other food additives. Among these hazardous chemicals, according to ECHA’s endocrine disruptor assessment list, major EDCs in the case–control group included phenols (e.g., 4-tert-butylphenol, 2-tert-butyl-4-methoxyphenol, bisphenol A, bisphenol B, bisphenol S), OPEs (e.g., triphenyl phosphate), PAEs (e.g., dicyclohexyl phthalate), ACRs (e.g., tridecafluorooctyl acrylate), parabens (e.g., propylparaben, butylparaben), and ketones (e.g., bicyclo[2.2.1]­heptan-2-one), demonstrating these objects be paid much attention.

Besides the aforementioned results, there was some variability between screening results of the two batches (Figure A,B for DL; Figure C,D for GZ). For samples from the same region but different batches, the identified exogenous species were similar, but their quantities varied significantly, indicating substantial heterogeneity among samples that greatly influenced the screening results. On the other hand, for samples from the same batch but different regions (Figure A,C for P1; Figure B,D for P2), there were some important findings as follows. (1) Differences in exogenous species: For example, citrate esters (CIT-E) were detected in GZ samples. (2) Variations in hazardous substance profiles: FAEs were more diverse in GZ samples. (3) Distinct exposure sources: GZ samples primarily contained exogenous substances from daily life sources (e.g., food additives, cosmetics, and plasticizers), whereas DL samples showed higher contributions from industrial raw materials and pesticides. These findings are consistent with the development levels and urban functions of the two regions.

Identification of Key Hazardous Substances Based on Differential Analysis

Figure A,B reveals distinct geographical clustering in batch P1 samples from GZ and DL cities, consistent with PCA results. Marked differences in endogenous metabolites were observed between case and control groups, including upregulated 5-androsten-3β,17β-diol-3-sulfate and testosterone sulfate and downregulated bile acids (deoxycholic acid and glycochenodeoxycholic acid) and steroid sulfates (4-pregnen-17α,20β-diol-3-one-20-sulfate and pregnenolone sulfate). Exogenous contaminants also showed region-specific profiles: PP cases from GZ exhibited pronounced upregulation of the OPEs (e.g., tris­(2-butoxyethyl) phosphate, tris­(2-ethylhexyl) phosphate), PAEs (e.g., monobutyl phthalate, diisobutyl phthalate, bis­(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate), and phenols (e.g., pentachlorophenol, 2,4-dinitrophenol, 4-nitrophenol, 2,2′-methylenebis­(4-methyl-6-tert-butylphenol) etc., particularly in the ZSX subgroup. In contrast, DL cases were characterized by elevated levels of PFAS, such as perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), 1H-perfluoroheptane, and perfluoro-1-octanesulfonic acid (PFOS). Additionally, cortisone showed mild upregulation, whereas 4-indolecarboxaldehyde and 2,5-di-tert-butylhydroquinone (BHQ) were slightly downregulated.

Batch P2 analysis further confirmed exposure disparities (Figure C,D). ESI+ mode detected upregulation of long-chain phthalates (e.g., toluyl isooctyl phthalate, diisodecyl phthalate, butyl cyclohexyl phthalate) and bisphenol analogs (e.g., BPF), 2-phenyl propanamide and 5-methoxylsalycylic acid, while ESI– mode revealed broad upregulation of four endogenous metabolites (e.g., 5-Androsten-3β,17β-diol-3-sulfate, deoxycholic acid, testosterone sulfate, homogentisic acid) and 12 exogenous pollutants, including six PFAS (e.g., perfluoro-1-hexanesulfonic acid, perfluoro-1-octanesulfonic acid, perfluorooctanoic acid, perfluorodecanoic acid), phthalate metabolites (e.g., mono­(2-ethylhexyl) phthalate, monobutyl phthalate), and phenolic derivatives (e.g., phenol, 4-phenolsulfonic acid, 4-methylphenol). These findings collectively demonstrate coexisting disruptions in metabolic pathways and pollutant exposure profiles in PP cases.

Application of Suspect Screening to Identify the Differential Hazards

Among the major pollutants present both in case and control groups, most of them had similar concentrations and are not considered as key hazardous. However, only those present in the case group with higher concentration than the control group should be paid much attention, especially those that were not detected in control groups. Such differential compounds might have the potential to become key hazards.

Case–Control Samples from a Typical Southeastern Coastal CityGuangzhou

Figure B,C shows significantly higher concentrations of diheptyl phthalate (DHP), dibutyl phthalate (DBP), and short-chain chlorinated paraffins (CPs) (C10–11Cl6–9) in the case group compared to controls, which were suggested as key hazardous EDCs. DHP exhibited the most pronounced elevation, particularly in the CPP subgroup. Suspect compounds further identified as the key differential hazards (Figure A,C) included 1,3,5-trichlorobenzene (1,3,5-TCB), 2,4-Di-tert-butylphenol (2,4-DTBP), mesitylene (Mes), CPs (C12–14Cl7–9), and PCB 180. These pollutants represent multiple exposure sources such as plasticizers (e.g., DHP, DBP), FRs (CPs, PCB180), and UV stabilizers (e.g., 2,4-DTBP), the raw materials for dye (1,3,5-TCB) and flavor (e.g., Mes) products. Batch P2 analysis revealed a broader spectrum of pollutants (Figure D–G), including previously identified phthalates (e.g., DBP), newly detected phthalates (e.g., DCHP, DPP, DNOP, DMP, DIBP), and additional OPEs (e.g., TCEP, TCPP, TEHP, TCIPP, TDCPP). Suspect screening also highlighted anthraquinone derivatives (2-AN), PAHs, and their derivatives (e.g., phenanthrene, pyrene, dibenzofuran, 2-methylnaphthalene, 9-chlorofluorene) as potential critical hazards.

Public Health Implications and Potential Exposure Source

This study utilizes nontargeted and suspect screening techniques, including high-throughput and HRMS, to systematically identify EDCs associated with PP. From a public health perspective, the findings provide a priority pollutant control list to inform targeted regulations (e.g., restrictions on harmful chemicals in children’s products) and advance evidence-based exposure intervention strategies. Furthermore, this work establishes a scientific basis for developing an early exposure warning system rooted in biomonitoring, empowering families and communities to reduce exposure risks, and enhancing public awareness of environmental health issues. In terms of exposure source analysis, region-specific pollution profiles (e.g., elevated plasticizers and FRs in Guangzhou and pesticides and their intermediates in Dalian) offer direct evidence for identifying priority pollution sources in different areas. These insights help clarify major exposure pathways, including the oral intake of contaminated food, the inhalation of dust, and dermal contact with household chemicals. Such information supports the construction of multipathway exposure models and contributes to the ongoing refinement of environmental monitoring systems. Ultimately, these results promote both source reduction and individual protective measures, facilitating a shift from passive environmental monitoring to proactive public health intervention and providing a scientific foundation for safeguarding children’s health.

Conclusion

Serum samples from pediatric control groups in typical northern and southern coastal cities were analyzed using GC-Orbitrap- and LC-Orbitrap-based nontargeted screening to characterize the chemical exposures. Candidate compounds were selected based on detection frequency and concentration differences between the disease and control groups. This approach identified structurally diverse compounds with widespread national use and potential human health concerns, including benzenes and derivatives, phenols, PAHs and their derivatives, alkyl acid esters, and PAEs and OPEs. Comparative analysis between northern and southern cities revealed distinct regional differences in compound types and abundances, with clear geographical clustering observed among samplesa finding that warrants further attention.

In summary, this study delineates the distribution characteristics of organic pollutants in serum from pediatric control subjects across coastal cities, identifies key exogenous hazards relevant to disease prevention, and provides a scientific foundation and methodological framework for protecting the health of population. While this nontargeted screening provides a valuable snapshot of potential exposures, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The present findings are constrained by a moderate sample size and the substantial influence of interindividual variability within the cohorts. Although we employed pooled, multibatch sampling to mitigate this issue, these factors may still affect the precision and generalizability of the results. The semiquantitative analysis of nontargeted screening limits direct risk assessment. To conclusively validate these initial findings, our future work will focus on conducting a rigorous, quantitative analysis of the prioritized contaminants in each individual sample, which is essential for verifying exposure levels and strengthening the epidemiological associations.
