Problematic screen exposure and oral health in preschool children: a comparison study
Authors: Şeyma Mustuloğlu, Özlem Tezol, Sıddıka Songul Yalcın
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesRecent studies have highlighted the association between problematic screen exposure (PSE) in preschool children and various health issues. This study aims to determine the relationship between PSE and oral/dental health as well as parafunctional oral habits in this population.MethodsHealthy children aged 3 to 6 years who visited the pediatric dentistry outpatient clinic were included in the study. PSE was evaluated using the parent-reported ‘Seven-in-Seven-Screen-Exposure Questionnaire’. An intraoral examination was conducted to record the number of decated, missing, and filled teeth, along with scores from the International Caries Detection and Assessment System II (ICDAS II), as well as dental plaque and gingival indices.ResultsA total of 210 mother–child pairs were included in the study, comprising 70 pairs with PSE and 140 pairs without PSE. The sociodemographic characteristics, frequency of daily tooth brushing behaviors, use of children's toothpaste, and regular dental examinations were statistically evaluated between the PSE and non-PSE group (p > 0.05). Additionally, there was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of having abnormal oral habits (p > 0.05). In the PSE group, the median scores for gingival and plaque index were significantly higher (p = 0.011 and p = 0.006, respectively). The median number of decayed teeth was significantly higher in the PSE group (p = 0.039).ConclusionsTurkish preschool children with PSE were found to have a significantly higher likelihood of experiencing poor oral health. Consequently, it is essential to conduct oral health examinations for Turkish preschool children exhibiting PSE. The study may have clinical implications for the evaluation of preschool children with problematic media use within dental practice.Keywords: Problematic screen exposure, Preschoolers, Oral habits, Oral health

Introduction

Tooth decay typically occurs when simple carbohydrates, such as sucrose, are fermented by oral microorganisms [1]. It is considered a behavioral and lifestyle disease that can be prevented through a sugar-free diet and daily removal of plaque via brushing with fluoride toothpaste. Despite being preventable, primary tooth decay in early childhood remains one of the leading causes of pain and suffering among infants and young children [2]. Previous studies have reported that lifestyle factors, including skipping breakfast and smoking by parents, frequent snacking, physical inactivity and inadequate sleep in children, are associated with increased prevalence of caries [3]. Both parental and child lifestyle choices significantly influence the development of caries during childhood [3]. Moreover, parafunctional oral habits, such as thumb sucking, onychophagia (nail biting), and lip biting –often explained by psychological or behavioral theories—can result in alterations to dental tissues, bones, muscles and growth patterns in children [4]. Considering that healthy primary dentition is essential for the establishment of healthy permanent dentition [5], it is critical to analyze all risk factors that may impact oral and dental health, as well as healthy occlusal development in young children.

Today, preschool children have unprecedented access to varios digital media devices, including televisions, computers, laptops, smartphones, tablets, and other handheld devices [6]. This exposure can quickly lead to habitual screen use among preschoolers, with screen time often increasing as children seek additional entertainment experiences [7] Consequently, the factors influencing developmental, behavioral, and health concerns related to screen media use in preschoolers have increasingly become critical issues in recent years [6]. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends limiting media use among children under five years of age concerning time, content, and parent–child interaction due to concerns such as obesity, decreased sleep duration, behavioral difficulties, and delays in cognitive, linguistic, and social-emotional development [6]. According to the AAP and World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, children under two years should have no screen exposure aside from video calls with family members. However, children aged 1.5 to 2 years may engage in viewing high-quality educational content together for less than one hour per day when accompanied by an adult. For children aged 2 to 4 years, screen time should be kept to under one hour daily. For those aged 5 and older, recreational screen time should not exceed 2 h per day [6, 8, 9]. Given the vulnerabilities of the pediatric population and the potential adverse effects of media use, PSE may significantly contribute to the implantation of cariogenic bacteria, underdeveloped host defense systems, behavioral patterns related to nutrition and oral hygiene, as well as parafunctional oral habits.

Studies have demonstrated a relationship between media use -specifically television and gaming- and the prevalence of dental caries in school-aged children and adolescents [3, 10]. Additionally, Yilmaz and Avci [11] reported an association between daily screen exposure time and oral health in preschool children. However, to date, no studies have specifially investigated the relationship between PSE and oral health, including the presence of parafunctional oral habits in preschoolers. The “Seven-Seven Screen Exposure Questionnaire” is a validated tool designed to identify young children and their families exhibiting abnormal patterns of screen use. This tool assesses not only excessive screen time but also other aspects of screen use usage, such as independent and indefinite use, use before bedtime and during meals, screen exposure in children under the age of two, and consumption of low-quality programming [12].

This study aims to adress an existing research gap by investigating the relationship between PSE in children aged 3 to 6 years and their current oral health, which includes factors such as poor oral hygiene, gingivitis, and dental caries, as well as parafunctional oral habits. By elucidating these associations, the study seeks to inform the development of improved guidelines and interventions that promote healthier screen use habits, ultimately enhancing oral health outcomes and reducing the prevalence of parafunctional oral habits among preschool children.

Materials and methods

Study approval

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of Mersin University (Protocol No: 2022/227) and adhered to all procedures in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration. Additionally, the study protocol complied with the STROBE guidelines. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from parents, along with verbal assent from children involved in the study.

Study population

The descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted from May 2022 to January 2023 at the Pediatric Dentistry Department of the Faculty of Dentistry, –- University. The study included 210 children aged 3 to 6 years (36 to 71 months), who presented for routine dental examinations in the primary dentition stage. Eligible participants did not have any chronic systemic disease and/or acute infection and were willing to participate. Children exhibiting acute severe illness, disabilities, systemic diseases, or neuropsychiatric conditions were excluded from the study (Fig. 1).

Data collection

The data were collected using a structured and pre-tested questionnaire. Initially, a questionnaire consisting of 24 items was administered face-to-face to the mothers of the children, addressing sociodemographic factors, anthropometric measurements, oral health care habits, and parafunctional oral habits. Prior to the main study, a pilot study was conducted with a draft version of the questionnaire, which involved 15 children and their attending the same department. The feedback received from the pilot study facilitated the finalization of the questionnaire. Additionally, the “Seven-in-Seven Screen Exposure Questionnaire (SEQ)” was utilized to assess the screen exposure (SE) status of the children, with administration conducted face-to-face with their parents. Developed by Yalçin et al., the SEQ is specifically designed to evaluate PSE in preschoolers [12]. This questionnaire consists of seven items, divided into three categories: three items on 'screen exposure rules,' two on 'screen exposure during daily routines,' and two on 'screen exposure conditions.' It evaluates factors such as daily screen time, viewing with parents, setting screen limits, screen use during meals, screen exposure in the hour before bedtime, the age at which screen exposure began, and the content of the screens. Daily screen time was classified based on overall average duration; 0: < 1 h; 1: 1–2 h; 2: > 2 h. Shared screen use with parents was scored as follows: 0: always; 1: sometimes; 2: rarely ever. Screen limits were scored based on both the presence of limits and the child’s adherence: 0 for limits set and followed, 1 for no limits, and 2 for limits set but not followed. Screen exposure during meals and before bedtime was recorded as binary variables: 0 for no exposure and 1 for exposure. The age of onset for screen exposure was scored as follows: 0 for ≥ 24 months, 1 for 18–23 months, 2 for 12–17 months, and 3 for < 12 months. Screen content was scored: 0 for age-appropriate content only, 1 for one type of inappropriate content, and 2 for multiple types of inappropriate content. The total PSE score, ranging from 0 to 13, was calculated by summing the scores from the three categories, with higher scores indicating more problematic screen exposure. A cut-off point of 7 was established for identifying PSE. The SEQ was originally developed in Turkish, and the original Turkish version was utilized in the present study [12]. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the questionnaire’s reliability was found to be 0.58 in our study.

Antropometric data, specifically weight and height measurements, were collected for each child. Body mass index was calculated using the formula kg/m2. The WHO's child growth standards were employed to derive anthropometric Z-scores. With a mean of 0 and a normal range of -2.0 to + 2.0, the clinical interpretation of these Z-scores is straightforward. They are utilized to assess nutritional status and to identify malnutrition in preschool-aged children [13].

Subsequently, intraoral examinations were performed under the illumination of a dental unit. A pediatric dentist (Ş.M.), with eight years of experience and trained to use the relevant indices, recorded the presence of dental caries, plaque index (PI), and gingival inflammation on a data collection form.The diagnosis of dental caries was madeusing a dental mirror and a ball-ended explorer (WHO 973/80-Martin), according to codes and criteria determined by the World Health Organization (WHO). Dental caries were evaluated and reported using the decayed (d), missing (m) and filled (f) teeth (dmft) and surfaces (dmfs) indices, which quantify the number of affected teeth or surfaces in primary dentition [14]. Additionally, ICDAS-II was employed for a more comprehensive evaluation of dental caries, encompaasing both initial and cavitated lesions as well as their respective stages [15]. For ICDAS coding, each tooth was dried with a compressed air syringe and assigned a single code reflecting the highest surface score. The maximum ICDAS score per child was utilized for analyses [16]. Furthermore,, patients' oral hygiene status was assessed using the plaque index (PI), which ranges from 0 to 3 (indicating no plaque, thin plaque, moderate plaque and heavy plaque, respectively), and gingival health was evaluated using the gingival index (GI), also scored from 0 to 3 (representing no inflammation, mild inflammation, moderate inflammation and severe inflammation, respectively), employing a periodontal probe (Williams Periodontal Probe, Hu-Friedy) [9].

Sample size calculation and statistical analysis

The minimum sample size was determined to be 138 subjects for the non-PSE group and 69 subjects for the PSE group. This calculation was based on a comparison of the mean dmft scores in Turkish preschool children exposed to recommended screen time (8.5 ± 4.9) versus excessive screen time (10.52 ± 4.86), as reported by Yilmaz and Avci [11]. The analysis utilized a 95% confidence interval, an 80% statistical power, and a 2:1 ratio of sample sizes, as calculated using the "OpenEpi" program (https://www.openepi.com/SampleSize/SSMean.htm). Furthermore, intra-rater agreement was assessed using data collected from 10 children who were not part of the main study, measured across two periods with a 7-day interval. The resulting intra-rater kappa values exceeded 0.80 for all scales utilized, indicating a high level of agreement.

Statistical analyses were conducted the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21.0 (IBM SPSS Corp.; Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of the data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and histogram visualizations. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations and medians with interquartile ranges (IQR, 25th-75th percentiles), while categorical variables are reported as numbers (n) and percentages (%).Independent group comparisons were performed using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test. The relationships between continuous variables were analyzed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p values < 0.05 deemed to indicate statistical significance.

Results

This study included a total of 210 mother–child pairs. The median age of the children was 63.5 months (IQR: 54–69), with 58.6% being male. Among the participants, 70 children were identified as having PSE development, while 140 exhibited non-PSE development. Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences between of the groups concerning age, gender, breastfeeding status, and anthropometric z-scores (p > 0.05). None of the children were classified as malnourished. The prevalence of pacifier and feeding bottle use did not differ significanbetween the groups (p > 0.05). High birth order and level of parental education related to primary schooling were more frequently reported in the PSE group (p < 0.05). Additionally, the PSE group had a lower monthly family income, with median amounts of $370 compared to $435 in the non-PSE group (p = 0.043). There was no significant differences between the groups in relation to toothbrush ownership, daily toothbrushing habits, usage of children’s toothpaste, and regular dental check-ups (p > 0.05). According to maternal reports, 121 children (57.6%) adhered to the recommended screen time of ≤ 2 h per day, while 89 children (42.4%) exceeded this guideline with excessive screen time (> 2 h per day). Excessive screen time was significantly more prevelent in the PSE group (75.7% vs. 25.7%, p < 0.001). The median seven-in-seven SEQ scores for the PSE and non-PSE groups were 8 (IQR: 7–9) and 3 (IQR: 2–5), respectively (p < 0.001). A total of sixty-nine children (49.3%) with PSE and thirty children (42.9%) with non-PSE exhibited at least one parafunctional oral habit (p = 0.379). No significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding parafunctional oral habits (p > 0.05). Notably, none of. the children demonstrated excellent oral hygiene, nor were any free of gingivitis, or exhibiting healthy tooth surfaces. The median scores of plaque index and gingival index were significantly higher in the PSE group (1.5 vs. 1.2, p = 0.006 and 1.25 vs. 0.8, p = 0.011, respectively). Additionally poor oral hygiene was more prevalent in this group (32.9% vs. 15.9%, p = 0.011), while mild gingivitis was less common (42.9% vs. 60.7%, p = 0.035). The frequencies of good or fair oral hygiene and moderate or severe gingivitis did not differ statistically between the groups (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the median number of decayed teeth was significantly higher in the PSE group (5 vs. 4, p = 0.039). However, there were no significant differences in max ICDAS, dmft, and dmfs scores between the PSE and non-PSE groups (p = 0.163, p = 0.098, and p = 0.104, respectively). Discussion

This study utilized the “Seven-in-Seven Screen Exposure Questionnaire” a tool designed to assess PSE among Turkish preschool children in accordance with American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommendations [12]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the relationship between parafunctional oral habits, oral/dental health status, and PSE in preschool children. The results demonstrated that preschool children with PSE exhibited a significantly higher prevalence of poor oral health and a higher mean number of decayed teeth compared to their non-PSE counterparts. Furthermore, within the PSE group, a higher birth order and lower levels of parental education were prevalent, while monthly family income was also found to be lower. These findings align with existing literature, which indicates that a high PSE score is associated with factors such as low parental education level, non-worker mothers, single-parent or extended family structures, families with two or more siblings, households comprising five or more members, and rural residence [12]. Notably, PSE in children may disrupt daily routines, postpone vital needs, and diminish both quality and quantity of sleep [12, 17, 18]. Such adverse effects resulting from PSE can significantly impact the oral health of affected children.

Problematic media use in children is defined as excessive engagement that interferes with the child's daily functioning and may lead to addictive behaviours, beyond merely the number of hours spent in front of the screen (e.g., exhibiting strong resistance to limits on media use) [19, 20]. In this study, 89 children (42.4%) were found to have excessive screen time, defined as more than 2 h per day, with 70 children classified as experiencing PSE. Notably, exposure to excessive screen time was significantly more prevalent in the PSE group. A broader perspective, which includes factors such as parent–child co-viewing of screens and parental implementation of screen limits, is essential for understanding the emergence of these results in the context of evaluating problematic screen use.

Young children dependent on their caregivers to maintain optimal oral health; however, a neglectful parenting style can lead to inadequate oral hygiene, poor dietary management, and a lack of timely treatment when necessary [21]. Therefore, in this study, we focused on problematic screen use, which reflects the overall functioning of preschool children beyond merely assessing total daily screen time or the types of media consumed. Our findings demonstrated that a significant relationship between PSE and oral health. Specifically, the median plaque index and gingival index scores, along with the mean number of decayed teeth, were higher in the PSE group. Moreover, in this study, children with PSE were more likely than their non-PSE counterparts to brush their teeth only once or twice a week and to do so independently, seek dental care solely when experiencing toothache, and present with extensive lesions, as categorized by the ICDAS. Our previous study indicated that in Turkish school-age children, medium to high levels of problematic media use was associated with decreased daily tooth brushing, and lower utilization of age-appropriate toothpaste, as well as increased gingivitis and the prevalence of caries and activity compared to children with low problematic media use [22].

Previous studies on media use and oral health have predominantly focused on the screen time and media exposure of children and adolescents. Asaka et al. [3] an association between screen time and dental caries in children aged 6 to 12 years. Yilmaz and Avci [11] found a correlation between poor oral and early exposure to screens, as well as long-term screen use, in Turkish preschool children. Their findings also indicated that preschool children with more than two hours of daily screen time had higher dmft scores. Furthermore, a study examining the impact of video games on oral/dental health and oral habits revealed that Turkish children aged 6 to 12 who engaged in daily video gaming exhibited a higher prevalence of severe gingivitis and advanced caries lesions; playing video games for two or more hours daily significantly increased the risk of severe gingivitis [23]. Tsuchiya et al. [24]. reported a type-specific negative effect of screen viewing on tooth brushing behavior in children aged 6 to 15 years, noting an association between excessive video game play (over two hours per day), and decreased brushing frequency (less than twice daily), while no such association was found with television viewing. Additionally, Doitchinova et al. [25] reported a correlation between extended television viewing and the development of dental caries in children aged 6 to 12 years. Problematic internet use in adolescents -defined as excessive and disruptive non-essential use- has also been linked to poor toothbrushing habits, gingival bleeding, tooth pain, and a lack of dental checkups [25, 26]. Consistent these findings, the current study demonstrates a significant correlation between daily screen time and various oral health indicators, including plaque index, gingival index, max ICDAS, as well as dmft and dmfs scores. These results suggest that family-based interventions aimed at reducing PSE may positively impact oral health by promoting appropriate screen time along children. Furhermore, considering the existing literature primarily addresses school-age and adolescent populations, this study’s focus on the preschool period -a critical developmental stage where early signs of future oral health issues may emerge- provides a valuable contribution to the literature.

Parafunctional oral habits are recognized as potential etiological factors that can disrupt dentofacial development [27]. Thus, the early detection and elimination of the causative factors associated with these habits are critical. Although this study found no significant relationship between parafunctional oral habits in preschool children and PSE, its significance lies in being the first of its kind to investigate this association. A previous study has reported that as problematic media use increases among school-age children, the prevalence of oral parafunctional habits also increases; furthermore, moderate to high levels of problematic media use have been linked to these habits [22]. Future studies should re-examine the relationship between PSE and parafunctional oral habits in preschool-aged schildren.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design is insufficient for establishing a temporal association between PSE, oral habits, and oral health status. Second we did not collect data on dietary habits, particularly regarding the consumption of cariogenic foods or beverages during periods of media use. However, the normal weight of the child participants—indicating the absence of underweight, overweight, or obesity- may help mitigate this limitation. Therefore, future multicenter studies that incorporate additional comprehensive variables representing Turkish preschool children are warranted.

Conclusion

PSE in preschool-age children is associated with poor oral and dental health Establishing a positive child-parent relationship, implementing rules regarding screen use, and efectively managing screen time can contribute to improved oral health this age group. Pediatricians, family physicians, pediatric dentists, and parents should be aware of the potential implications of PSE on oral and dental health and prioritize preventive oral health measures for preschool children. Furthermore, future interventions that promote self-regulation and self-care skills in preschoolers, along with enhancing parents' general parenting practices, may be effective in preventing problematic screen use and improving oral health outcomes.
