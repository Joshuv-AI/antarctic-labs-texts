A giant squamous cell papilloma with high fluorodeoxyglucose uptake mimicking squamous cell lung cancer: A case report
Authors: Ryohei Kamada, Hidenori Mizugaki, Shohei Mizobuchi, Ken Kuwahara, Noriyuki Yamada, Yasushi Mizukami, Hajime Asahina, Hirofumi Adachi, Hiroshi Yokouchi, Yoshihiro Matsuno, Satoshi Oizumi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBronchial papillomas have a median diameter of 15 mm at diagnosis and are classified as benign tumors; however, they may occasionally demonstrate increased fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake, requiring differentiation from malignant tumors. A 64-year-old man was referred to our hospital after chest computed tomography (CT) performed to evaluate a cough during a routine workplace health checkup revealed a 60-mm mass in segment 6 (S6) of the right lower lobe. The serum levels of carcinoembryonic antigen, cytokeratin 19 fragment, and squamous cell carcinoma antigen were elevated. FDG positron emission tomography/CT demonstrated intense FDG uptake in the right S6 mass, with a maximum standardized uptake value of 23.21. Bronchoscopy revealed a tumor protruding into the right B6 bronchus. Direct biopsy of the endobronchial lesion revealed a papilloma-like lesion composed of stratified mature squamous epithelium. Although no definite malignant features were identified, the possibility of secondary hyperplastic/dysplastic changes associated with neighboring malignancies, such as squamous cell carcinoma, could not be completely excluded. To evaluate the lesion, a CT-guided percutaneous lung biopsy was performed, which revealed similar histological findings without evidence of malignancy. As malignancy could not be definitively excluded, diagnostic and therapeutic surgery, consisting of uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic right lower lobectomy with bronchoplasty and ND2a-1 lymph node dissection, was performed. Histopathological examination revealed a squamous cell papilloma measuring approximately 46 mm in diameter, with no evidence of malignancy. Introduction

Bronchial papilloma is a benign tumor characterized by papillary epithelial proliferation and fibrovascular stromal cores. Bronchial papillomas have a median diameter of 15 mm at diagnosis and are subclassified into the following three categories: (i) squamous cell papilloma, (ii) glandular papilloma, and (iii) mixed squamous cell and glandular papilloma [1].

Bronchial papillomas can present as solitary or multiple lesions. Solitary lesions most commonly arise in the central bronchi; however, rare cases of tumors originating from the peripheral bronchioles have also been reported. Solitary endobronchial papillomas account for 0.38% of all lung tumors and 7% of all benign epithelial and mesenchymal lung tumors [2].

Although papillomas are classified as benign, they occasionally demonstrate high fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) uptake on FDG positron emission tomography (PET)/computed tomography (CT), necessitating their differentiation from malignant tumors [3,4]. Additionally, malignant transformation has been reported in squamous cell papillomas and mixed squamous cell and glandular papillomas [5,6].

Herein, we report a case of squamous cell papilloma measuring 60 mm in diameter, with high FDG uptake on FDG-PET/CT, which required differentiation from squamous cell carcinoma of the lung. Furthermore, we discuss the challenges associated with ruling out malignancy and explore treatment strategies that take these considerations into account.

Case report

A 64-year-old man, a former smoker with a 40 pack-year history, was referred to our hospital for evaluation after chest CT performed at another hospital for the investigation of a cough during a routine workplace health checkup revealed a 60-mm mass in segment 6 (S6) of the right lower lobe.

Laboratory tests revealed elevated serum tumor marker levels (carcinoembryonic antigen, 11.1 ng/mL; cytokeratin 19 fragment, 4.2 ng/mL; and squamous cell carcinoma antigen, 15.4 ng/mL). Chest radiograph revealed a mass lesion in the right pulmonary hilum with no silhouette sign in the mediastinum (Fig. 1A). Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a 60-mm mass in the right lower lobe and enlargement of lymph nodes #4R and #7 (Fig. 1B). FDG-PET/CT showed intense FDG uptake in the right lower lobe mass, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 23.21 and moderate uptake in lymph nodes #4R and #7, with an SUVmax of 4.02 (Fig. 1C).

B: Contrast-enhanced computed tomography image shows enlargement of lymph nodes #4R and #7 and a 60-mm mass in the right lower lobe.

C: Fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography/computed tomography image shows intense FDG uptake in lymph nodes #4R and #7, with a maximum standardized uptake value (SUVmax) of 4.02, and the right lower lobe mass, with a SUVmax of 23.21.

Bronchoscopy revealed a tumor protruding into the right B6 bronchus. Direct biopsy of the endobronchial lesion revealed a stratified mature squamous epithelium proliferating in an exophytic papillary architecture with fibrovascular cores. Mild nuclear enlargement was observed predominantly in the basal and parabasal layers. Histopathological findings suggestive of malignancy, such as an increase in nuclear chromatin levels, cellular pleomorphism, and a loss of polarity, were not observed (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, the possibility of secondary hyperplastic/dysplastic changes associated with neighboring malignancies, such as squamous cell carcinoma, could not be completely excluded. Endobronchial ultrasonography-guided transbronchial needle aspiration of lymph nodes 4R and 7 revealed no evidence of malignancy. To evaluate the lesion, a CT-guided percutaneous lung biopsy was performed, which revealed similar histological findings without definite evidence of malignancy.

Histopathological findings of the tumor in the right B6 segment of a direct biopsy using bronchoscopy

Hematoxylin and eosin staining shows a stratified squamous epithelium proliferating in an exophytic papillary architecture with fibrovascular cores without definite evidence of malignancy.

Despite these findings, malignancy could not be ruled out considering the large tumor size, elevated FDG uptake, and increased levels of tumor markers. Therefore, diagnostic and therapeutic surgeries, consisting of a uniportal video-assisted thoracoscopic right lower lobectomy with bronchoplasty and ND2a-1 lymph node dissection, were performed (Fig. 3). Gross examination of the resected right lower lobe revealed a well-demarcated mass measuring 46 × 35 mm impacted within a dilated bronchus (Fig. 4A). Histopathological examination revealed a tumor composed of exophytic papillary proliferation, predominantly of stratified mature squamous epithelial cells with fibrovascular stromal cores, in addition to limited glandular epithelial cell coverage (<10%) (Fig. 4B and C). No stromal invasion or malignant features were identified, leading to the diagnosis of giant squamous cell papilloma. The dissected lymph nodes were not involved in the tumors.

The postoperative course was uneventful and the patient was managed with radiological follow-up.

Discussion

Solitary endobronchial papillomas account for 0.38% of all lung tumors and 7% of all benign epithelial and mesenchymal lung tumors [2]. A single-center report summarizing solitary endobronchial papillomas diagnosed by bronchoscopy demonstrated a male-to-female ratio of 3:1, with 65.6% of patients being former smokers [5]. The mean age at presentation was 56.9 ± 14.3 years for male patients and 53.3 ± 14.4 years for female patients. Histological analysis revealed squamous cell papilloma in 65.6% of patients, glandular papilloma in 18.8%, and mixed squamous cell and glandular papilloma in 15.6%. According to Inamura et al. [7], the mean tumor size is 27 mm (range, 7–90 mm) for squamous cell papillomas, 14 mm (range, 5–40 mm) for glandular papillomas, and 18 mm (range, 3–30 mm) for mixed squamous cell and glandular papillomas. Malignant transformation has been reported in 10.8% of squamous cell papillomas and 25% of mixed squamous cell and glandular papillomas, whereas no cases of malignant transformation in glandular papillomas have been reported [5].

FDG-PET/CT has been increasingly used to evaluate pulmonary nodules and masses; however, bronchial papillomas may also demonstrate increased FDG uptake. Masunaga et al. [8] reported glucose transporter type 1 expression by immunohistochemical staining in mixed squamous cells and glandular papilloma with high FDG uptake, suggesting that high FDG uptake in mixed papillomas warrants caution regarding malignant transformation. Although the present case involved a squamous cell papilloma with high FDG uptake and no malignant findings, further studies are required to clarify this hypothesis. Furthermore, the mechanism underlying FDG uptake remains unclear. The hypoxic microenvironment of a papilloma may promote glycolysis via glucose transporters, thereby contributing to FDG uptake; however, this hypothesis warrants further investigation.

A literature review of FDG-PET/CT findings in solitary endobronchial papillomas in Japan reported SUVmax values ranging from no uptake to 12.95 in squamous cell and glandular papillomas [3]. Additionally, a report summarizing mixed squamous cell and glandular papillomas in Japan has described SUVmax values up to 15.17 [4]. As increased FDG uptake can be observed, solitary endobronchial papillomas can mimic malignant tumors. Moreover, no imaging findings specific to solitary endobronchial papillomas have been reported, complicating the differential diagnosis.

The present case involved a squamous cell papilloma presenting as a 60-mm mass on CT with increased FDG uptake (SUVmax of 23.21) on FDG-PET/CT. This lesion was larger and demonstrated higher FDG uptake, compared with other squamous cell papillomas. In addition to elevated tumor marker levels, differentiation from malignant tumors is required.

Bronchoscopic biopsies are frequently performed to diagnose bronchial papillomas. However, in small biopsy specimens, the differential diagnosis of squamous cell papillomas from other mature papillary squamous epithelial lesions, such as well-differentiated squamous cell carcinoma, verrucous carcinoma, or pseudoepitheliomatous squamous cell hyperplasia, may sometimes be difficult. Thus, as in the present case, when the entire lesion cannot be evaluated, especially in large, metabolically active tumors, complete exclusion of malignancy may be difficult, which is another factor that complicates the differential diagnosis.

There are no established standard treatments for solitary endobronchial papillomas. Pulmonary resection has been performed [[3], [4], [5],7,9], although some cases have been diagnosed preoperatively and treated with bronchoscopic resection [10]. As bronchial papillomas are benign tumors, observation may be considered; however, there are limited reports regarding their natural course under observation, and careful follow-up is required. Nitanda et al. [6]. Reported a case in which a 22-mm pulmonary nodule suspected to be a solitary endobronchial papilloma, based on bronchoscopic pathology, enlarged to 43 mm over 8 months, necessitating surgical resection. Histopathological examination revealed a pulmonary pleomorphic carcinoma arising from mixed squamous cells and glandular papilloma. This report suggests that, even when a preoperative diagnosis of bronchial papilloma is made, rapid tumor growth warrants careful attention owing to the possibility of malignant transformation. Furthermore, this case indicates that when a large mass is already present at initial presentation and the growth rate is unknown, aggressive surgical resection may be appropriate, even if the findings suggest a bronchial papilloma. In this case, the pathological findings did not allow the definitive exclusion of squamous cell carcinoma. Because the tumor was large and its growth rate was unclear, the potential for malignancy required careful attention, and pulmonary resection was considered an appropriate treatment option.

In conclusion, we report a case of squamous cell papilloma that required differentiation from a malignant tumor. Our experience suggests that, although squamous cell papilloma is a benign neoplasm, it can present as a large mass with high FDG uptake, necessitating careful differentiation from malignancy. Given the difficulty of excluding malignancy based on biopsy specimens alone and potential for malignant transformation, treatment strategies should be carefully considered.
