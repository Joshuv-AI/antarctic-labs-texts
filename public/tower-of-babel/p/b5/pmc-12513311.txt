Establishing a lifeline: the first publicly-managed ambulance dispatch system and advanced cardiac life support (ACLS) resuscitation in Somaliland, East Africa
Authors: Yacin Mohamed Abdillahi, An-Fu Lee, Chien-Te Pan, Matthew Heui-Min Ma, Wen-Chu Chiang
Published: 2025

To the Editor

Developing emergency medical services (EMS) in resource-limited and fragile settings is a critical step towards reducing preventable deaths. In Somaliland, a country facing a high burden of trauma, obstetric emergencies, and an escalating cardiovascular disease burden, a new partnership with Taiwan has established the nation's first public ambulance system. This system, which has been preparing for operation since early 2025, is based at Hargeisa Group Hospital.

The immediate impact and challenges of this new system were starkly illustrated by its first two critical cases. On 29 May 2025, the ambulance call centre responded to its first call (supplementary video at: https://www.facebook.com/share/v/1ArUv66E23/), transporting a 40-year-old man with fever, who was subsequently diagnosed with fulminant malaria and sadly died, highlighting the profound burden of endemic diseases and the limitations of available tertiary care.1

Just five days later, on 3 June, the system demonstrated its life-saving potential. A 45-year-old man who collapsed was brought to the emergency department in cardiac arrest, with no detectable pulse or blood pressure. The newly-trained Somaliland ACLS team initiated resuscitation. Following ACLS protocols, the team administered adrenaline and intravenous fluids, performed endotracheal intubation, and provided ventilator support. This intervention led to the return of spontaneous circulation. The patient was stabilised and later discharged from the hospital, marking the first successful resuscitation from cardiac arrest by a public ACLS team in Somaliland.

This achievement is built on two strategic pillars that offer a replicable model for similar contexts. First, the project prioritised local adaptation. Following an assessment of Somaliland's health infrastructure, a hospital-based, physician-led EMS model, similar to the Franco-German system, was adopted. This approach leverages existing hospital doctors, contrasting with Taiwan's own fire-based, paramedic-led Anglo-American model.2, 3

Second, sustainability is driven by a robust 'train-the-trainer' programme. A cohort of 16 Somaliland 'seed trainers', trained in Taiwan, is now cascading this knowledge to over 200 local emergency care providers. This task-shifting approach is vital for creating a self-sufficient workforce in a resource-constrained environment.4

By delivering a tangible, life-saving public service, this initiative not only improves health outcomes but also strengthens the resilience of the local health system. This partnership between Taiwan and Somaliland exemplifies a novel approach to health system development, operating effectively through bilateral cooperation. As neither Taiwan nor Somaliland is a member of the WHO, the collaboration operates outside traditional global health governance structures.5 This model, grounded in local adaptation and sustainable training, offers valuable lessons for the development of emergency and resuscitation care in other fragile settings globally.
