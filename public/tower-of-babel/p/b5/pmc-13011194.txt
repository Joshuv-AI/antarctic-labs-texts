Automated Tooth Detection and Caries Identification in CBCT With Deep Learning
Authors: Surong Chen, Weiwei Wu, Pan Chen, Gengyu Zhou, Yan Yang, Bowen Shen, Jingyu Hu, Jingzhi Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDeep learning networks have achieved significant progress in caries diagnosis, but automated localization and numbering of carious teeth in cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images remain underexplored. This study aimed to develop a two-stage deep learning framework for tooth detection, numbering, and caries identification in CBCT images, providing a technical basis for automated analysis in support of opportunistic caries screening.MethodsThis retrospective study included CBCT images from 65 eligible patients. Axial slices were used for model development. For tooth detection, seven classification schemes were designed, and YOLOv3 was compared with Cascade R-CNN. For caries identification, three classification networks (DenseNet169, MobileNet_V2, and ResNet50) were trained and evaluated. Detection performance was evaluated using mean average precision (mAP) and average precision (AP). Identification performance was assessed using balanced accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC), and area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR).ResultsYOLOv3 achieved superior detection performance compared with Cascade R-CNN across all classification schemes (P < .0001). DenseNet169 outperformed the other networks for caries identification. Despite class imbalance, it achieved a balanced accuracy of 0.7414 and an MCC of 0.6074, with high specificity (0.9828) and NPV (0.8976). The integrated two-stage framework showed acceptable overall performance.ConclusionThe proposed two-stage framework showed promising performance in detecting, numbering, and identifying caries in CBCT images, supporting the feasibility of adjunctive opportunistic screening on scans acquired for non-caries indications.Clinical RelevanceThis framework may help prioritize clinician review of CBCT scans for suspected carious lesions, and clinical utility requires external multi-center and prospective validation.

Introduction

Dental caries is a chronic, destructive disease of the dental hard tissues caused by bacterial infection, and it remains highly prevalent worldwide.1 According to reports from the World Health Organization, the treatment coverage for dental caries is extremely low, with more than one third of the world’s population living with untreated caries.2 Owing to its subtle early symptoms, caries is often neglected by patients. If not treated in a timely manner, caries may gradually extend to the dental pulp and periapical tissues, eventually resulting in tooth loss.3 Therefore, early detection and timely intervention are critical for preventing disease progression and preserving the affected teeth.

Visual examination and probing are commonly used methods in clinical practice for caries diagnosis; however, their diagnostic outcomes can be influenced by factors such as the location of the lesion and the clinician’s experience.4 Imaging techniques are essential auxiliary tools, partially compensating for the limitations of traditional methods. Nevertheless, 2-dimensional (2D) imaging modalities such as periapical, bitewing, and panoramic radiographs are susceptible to the superimposition of anatomical structures, image distortion, and limited resolution.5 These limitations may lead to the underestimation of the extent and severity of carious lesions.

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), as a 3-dimensional (3D) imaging technique, has been widely applied in dental implantology, apical surgery, the diagnosis of complex endodontic lesions, and digital orthodontics.6 Authoritative guidelines, such as those from the AAOMR and SEDENTEXCT,7,8 advise against prescribing CBCT as a routine modality for caries detection because of radiation dose considerations. Accordingly, the present study focuses on opportunistic screening of CBCT scans already acquired for other justified clinical purposes. Given its high spatial resolution and 3D visualization, CBCT may enable incidental identification of existing carious lesions on these scans, thereby increasing the diagnostic value of available imaging without additional radiation exposure.9 However, interpreting CBCT images is often time-consuming owing to the large volume of complex radiographic information. Moreover, in busy clinical settings, factors such as distraction, fatigue, or insufficient experience may affect clinicians’ interpretation of carious lesions, increasing the risk of missed or incorrect interpretation.10

With the rapid advancement of computer technology, deep learning (DL), particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), has significantly driven the development of intelligent applications in the healthcare field. CNNs are highly effective in processing medical images and automatically extracting critical features, exhibiting excellent performance in various computer vision tasks.11 In the field of dentistry, CNNs have been widely applied to the analysis of oral radiographic images and can assist clinicians in accurately diagnosing various dental diseases.12,13 Although many studies have investigated the automatic detection, classification, and segmentation of carious lesions using CNNs, relatively little research has focused on the precise localization of caries and the recognition of tooth numbering in CBCT images.14, 15, 16 Accurate alignment of tooth localization and numbering is crucial for all dental conditions, including caries, particularly with the extensive application of artificial intelligence into dental imaging diagnostics. It serves not only as a prerequisite for accurate diagnosis but also as an essential source of clinical information for subsequent treatment planning.17

In recent years, several studies have investigated automated tooth detection and numbering systems in dental images using object detection networks. For example, Shi et al. employed the YOLOv3 network to localize and number teeth in orthopantomograms (OPGs), achieving an overall mean average precision (mAP@50) of 97.5%.18 Ghorbani et al. developed a YOLOv8-based automated system to identify mixed dentitions in occlusal view oral photographs and demonstrated strong performance.19 Kılıç et al. utilized an AI-based algorithm for detecting and numbering deciduous teeth in pediatric panoramic radiographs.20 Ozudogru et al. employed the nnU-Netv2 algorithm for automatic tooth segmentation and numbering in mixed dentition from CBCT scans,21 while Du et al. developed a mini-VGG-based model to extract multi-level tooth regions and applied YOLOv3 for object detection and proportional localization of individual teeth.22 These advances highlight the capability of AI to identify teeth in dental images and pave the way for improving clinical workflows.

The overall framework of our research is to develop an automated pipeline for caries analysis in CBCT images. The process architecture comprises two major steps: (1) automatic tooth detection and numbering from axial CBCT slices to establish anatomical correspondence, and (2) caries identification and classification for each detected tooth. Although Chen et al. developed a CBCT-based caries classification scheme and trained corresponding classification models, these models relied on tooth images cropped from predefined regions of interest.23 To overcome this limitation, the present study integrates tooth detection, numbering, and caries identification into a unified pipeline, providing a technical basis for automated analysis of CBCT volumes acquired for non-caries indications to support opportunistic caries screening. In addition, classification schemes commonly used for tooth detection in CBCT images include 4-class, 7-class, 8-class, 28-class, and 32-class systems.22,24, 25, 26, 27, 28 Among these, the 4-class, 7-class, and 8-class schemes cannot provide localization or numbering of individual teeth. While the 28-class and 32-class schemes offer more detailed tooth localization, most studies simplify them into 4-class or 8-class detection tasks, or apply clustering methods based on tooth centroids, followed by post-processing to infer full tooth numbering. These procedures are relatively complex. Therefore, this study additionally introduces 14-class and 16-class classification schemes, and evaluates model performance across all seven classification schemes to optimize tooth detection for future clinical evaluation.

Accordingly, this study aimed to develop a two-stage deep learning framework based on CBCT images that integrates tooth detection, numbering, and caries identification. Appropriate classification schemes for tooth detection were explored to optimize detection performance. The proposed framework may support opportunistic caries screening as an adjunctive tool.

Materials and methods

Dataset

In this retrospective study, CBCT images from 200 patients, acquired between June and December 2022, were randomly selected from the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology at Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, China. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee (IRB No. TJ-IRB20230939). The requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee due to the retrospective nature of the study. The 2024 Checklist for Artificial Intelligence in Medical Imaging (CLAIM) guideline was followed,29 and the detailed information is reported in Supplementary Table 6. All CBCT scans were acquired using a KaVo 3D Exam scanner (KaVo Dental, Germany) with the following parameters: tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 5 mA, voxel size of 0.25 mm. The images were exported and anonymized from the database in Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format, and then sequentially numbered.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) permanent dentition; (2) caries clearly identifiable on CBCT-derived multiplanar reconstructions (MPR) in at least 2 orthogonal planes with adequate image quality; and (3) caries characterized by either discontinuity of the enamel surface with a discrete radiolucency, or a radiolucency crossing the enamel-dentin junction (EDJ) and extending into dentin. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) limited visualization of teeth or carious lesions due to severe imaging artifacts caused by restoration or filling materials; (2) caries that occurred after root canal therapy; (3) teeth with extensive coronal destruction (>50% structural loss); and (4) radiolucencies visible on only a single slice or lesions with ambiguous radiographic features.

A total of 65 eligible patients were included in the CBCT image dataset, with 10% of the scans obtained from the Department of Endodontics. The dataset was randomly split into training (70%), validation (20%), and test (10%) sets. Dataset partitioning was performed strictly at the patient level, such that each patient was assigned to a single subset and no patient contributed data to more than one subset. All derived axial slices and cropped tooth regions of interest (ROIs) inherited the corresponding patient-level assignment for both the tooth detection and caries identification stages. Data augmentation and oversampling were applied only within the training set, and all augmented samples remained within the training subset of the originating patient.

Data annotation and classification schemes

Based on the axial slice range containing carious lesions, relevant image slices for each patient were extracted in order and saved in JPG format, with 640 × 640 pixels. The training, validation, and test sets contained 1103, 375, and 157 axial slices, respectively. The characteristics of the dataset used in this study are in Supplementary Table 1. The Dark-Label software (version 2.4, https://github.com/darkpgmr/DarkLabel) was applied to label bounding boxes of detection object in the axial slices based on seven classification strategies. Detailed descriptions of these strategies are presented in Supplementary Table 2. The initial data annotation was independently performed by two dentists, each with over 4 years of clinical experience. Prior to formal labeling, both annotators received standardized training on the annotation tools and criteria, followed by a calibration exercise using five cases. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s Kappa,30 yielding a value of K = 0.586 (95% CI, 0.485–0.688), indicating moderate agreement.31 Disagreements were subsequently reviewed by a senior endodontist with over 10 years of experience. Final labels were assigned by consensus following discussion among the three clinicians, with the senior endodontist serving as adjudicator when disagreements persisted. Annotations were saved in Extensible Markup Language (XML) files. XML files are commonly used to store annotation information in the PASCAL VOC format, which includes bounding box coordinates and class labels for the target objects. These annotations, along with the image slices, were then used to construct seven independent datasets for tooth detection.

Development of the deep learning models

A two-stage framework combining tooth detection and caries identification models was developed in this study. The pipeline comprises two components: the first stage for localizing and numbering teeth in axial CBCT slices, and followed identifying whether each detected tooth has caries. Tooth detection model

During the training phase, image slices and the corresponding XML files from seven datasets were inputted into the tooth detection model, respectively. In this study, YOLOv3 and Cascade R-CNN networks were employed and trained separately on each dataset.32,33 The outputs of the tooth detection model included tooth numbering, the coordinates of the predicted bounding boxes, and prediction scores ranging from 0 to 1. Pretrained weights from the ImageNet dataset were used to initialize the model parameters to accelerate convergence. Data augmentation techniques, such as random distorting, random cropping and random horizontal flipping, were applied to the training images. These networks were trained for 300 epochs with a batch size of 2. The learning rate was initially set to 0.000125 and linearly warmed up over the first 4000 iterations, starting from 0.001 times the base learning rate. Then, the learning rate was decayed by a factor of 0.1 at the 200th and 266th epochs, respectively. The Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) optimizer was used with a momentum coefficient of 0.9. Further, L2 regularization was applied to mitigate overfitting.

The axial image slices from the test set were employed to evaluate the performance of the tooth detection model. The primary evaluation metrics were average precision (AP) and mean average precision (mAP), calculated based on an Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5. IoU is defined as the ratio of the intersection area to the union area between the predicted bounding box and the ground truth bounding box. AP represents the area under the precision–recall (P–R) curve for each tooth class at this specific IoU threshold, and mAP is calculated as the mean of AP values across all tooth classes. Caries identification model

Individual tooth images were cropped from the original slices using a customized script, based on the bounding boxes and tooth numbering generated by the tooth detection model. These cropped images with an assigned tooth number were subsequently used as inputs for the caries identification model. The dataset was redistributed into a training set with 17,501 samples, a validation set with 5767 samples, and a test set with 2368 samples (Supplementary Table 1). Since the number of non-carious axial slices was approximately ten times that of carious slices in the training set, data augmentation techniques such as random horizontal flipping and random rotation were applied to reduce the risk of overfitting. Finally, the number of carious slices in the training set was increased to 15 882, as shown in Supplementary Table 1. Moreover, the augmented images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and subsequently fed into the models.

Three CNN architectures, DenseNet169,34 MobileNet_V2,35 and ResNet50,36 were trained using transfer learning to identify carious lesions. To improve training stability and convergence, a learning rate scheduler combining linear warmup and cosine annealing decay was applied. The learning rate increased linearly from 0.0001 to a base value of 0.001 over the first 3 epochs, and then decayed following a cosine function to a minimum of 1 × 10⁻⁶ over the remaining training epochs. The SGD optimizer was employed with a momentum coefficient of 0.9 and a weight decay of 0.005. Training was conducted for 80 epochs with a batch size of 8, utilizing cross entropy (CE) loss as the loss function. To reduce overfitting, early stopping with a patience of 4 epochs was applied. To mitigate class imbalance, dynamic oversampling and real-time data augmentation were performed during each training epoch.

Model performance was comprehensively evaluated using standard metrics derived from true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN). These included accuracy, precision (Positive Predictive Value, PPV), recall (Sensitivity), specificity, negative predictive value (NPV), and the F1-score. To visualize identification performance, confusion matrices and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC-ROC) was calculated.

To address class imbalance, balanced accuracy and the Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were employed. Additionally, precision-recall curves and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUC-PR) were utilized to evaluate the trade-off between precision and recall. The Brier score was calculated to evaluate model calibration, and calibration curves were plotted for visual assessment. Detailed definitions and formulas for all metrics are provided in the Supplementary Materials. To enhance clinical applicability, a post-processing rule was implemented in which a tooth was considered carious only if caries was predicted in three or more consecutive axial slices, thereby enabling tooth-level classification. Finally, non-parametric bootstrap resampling (n = 1000 iterations) was applied at the patient level to quantify sampling variability and derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Experimental setup

All models were trained and evaluated on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU (NVIDIA, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Experiments were conducted using the Python programming language (version 3.7.0, Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) and the PaddlePaddle 2.4 deep learning framework with CUDA and cuDNN libraries. Detailed experimental configurations, including hardware specifications, data augmentation strategies, and training hyperparameters for the networks, are summarized in Supplementary Table 5.

Application of two-stage framework and statistical analysis

In the constructed two-stage framework, tooth numbering and bounding boxes were first obtained using the tooth detection model. Considering the bilateral symmetry of homonymous teeth in axial slices, a label mapping strategy was applied to align the detected numbering with the complete tooth sets (eg, teeth detected under 14-class and 16-class schemes were mapped to the corresponding 28-class and 32-class systems). Subsequently, axial slices of each mapped tooth were extracted based on the predicted bounding boxes. A tooth was considered correctly detected if more than 90% of its slices were accurately predicted; otherwise, it was regarded as a detection failure. In the second stage, image slices of each successfully detected tooth were separately input into the caries identification model to determine the presence or absence of carious lesions.

To ensure a rigorous end-to-end evaluation and prevent performance inflation due to the exclusion of difficult-to-detect teeth, a penalization mechanism was introduced. Under this protocol, the total number of ground truth teeth (all teeth in the test set during the tooth detection stage) served as the denominator for caries identification metrics. Consequently, missed detections and localization failures (i.e., teeth that did not enter the identification stage) were counted as end-to-end errors, thereby penalizing the pipeline for failing to include difficult cases in the identification stage. An overall penalized end-to-end accuracy was then calculated, and the results were stratified by tooth region. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software (version 25.0 for Windows, SPSS Inc.). Non-parametric bootstrap resampling at the patient level (n = 1000 iterations) was used to derive 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Furthermore, Chi-square tests were applied to compare the performance of different networks in tooth detection and caries identification tasks. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to establish a comprehensive method for the automated detection, numbering, and identification of dental caries, thereby facilitating efficient opportunistic screening in clinical practice. While previous research has successfully classified carious lesions in CBCT images using deep learning,23,37 caries localization often relied on manually preprocessed data and lacked critical spatial information about tooth position. Therefore, the present study developed a two-stage deep learning framework capable of automatically detecting teeth, assigning tooth numbers, and identifying caries in CBCT images. Our findings suggested the feasibility of this automated approach for localizing and identifying caries in patients undergoing CBCT for other clinical indications. Taken together, these results highlight the model’s potential utility as an adjunctive tool for caries screening, or as a triage support tool to prioritize scans with suspected carious lesions for timely clinician review.

Accurate tooth detection and numbering are fundamental steps in dental examination and facilitate the maintenance of standard clinical records. In this study, seven classification schemes for tooth detection were designed and evaluated. The results indicated that the 4-class classification scheme achieved the best detection performance but was limited in localizing individual teeth. In contrast, the other schemes provide more detailed tooth positioning. Specifically, the 7-class and 8-class schemes required spatial judgment across four quadrants, whereas the 14-class and 16-class schemes only required left–right discrimination, which likely reduced quadrant-related misclassification and improved detection performance. Meanwhile, their mAP values remained comparable to those of the 7-class and 8-class schemes. Although the 28-class and 32-class schemes could directly localize individual teeth, they were more susceptible to confusion due to the morphological similarity of contralateral homologous teeth, resulting in decreased mAP values. These findings are consistent with previous studies. For example, Yasa et al. reported that a 24-class model trained on bitewing radiographs achieved a detection precision of 86.9%, whereas 12-class models trained separately for the left and right sides improved precision to 92.9%.38 Similarly, Alsolamy et al. found that two independent 12-class models improved precision by approximately 6%–7% compared to a single 24-class model.39 Therefore, based on these results, either the 14-class or the 16-class scheme is recommended for tooth detection tasks.

In this study, YOLOv3 demonstrated superior overall performance compared with Cascade R-CNN, likely due to its pyramid feature structure and multi-scale detection mechanism, which may improve detection of small objects.32 Moreover, YOLOv3 and Cascade R-CNN achieved mAP scores of 0.9087 and 0.9055 under the 4-class classification scheme, respectively. These results exceed those reported by Li et al.24 Although Miki et al. achieved an accuracy of 0.91 using a model trained on single-tooth ROIs extracted from axial CBCT slices under the 7-class scheme, their model lacked the capability for tooth localization.25 The 14-class and 16-class classification schemes proposed in this study outperformed the 28-class or 32-class schemes. In addition, these schemes could localize individual teeth similarly to the 28-class or 32-class schemes through a label mapping strategy. A similar approach was applied to improve the 4-class scheme. Jang et al. assigned tooth numbers based on the sequential arrangement of box center coordinates, and their model eventually achieved an overall mAP of 80.67%.27 Overall, the tooth detection model developed in the present study shows potential for supporting automated localization in CBCT-based opportunistic screening, and further studies are warranted to optimize its performance.

Analysis of single-tooth detection revealed that both networks exhibited a performance dip when detecting the last maxillary tooth. Compared with other schemes, the YOLOv3(14-class scheme) showed minimal performance fluctuations. In addition, YOLOv3 demonstrated greater robustness and required less training time for tooth detection than Cascade R-CNN. After applying label mapping to convert detection outputs into tooth numbering, Cascade R-CNN appeared more susceptible to several factors: (1) morphological similarity among different molars in certain axial slices; (2) substantial morphological variations in molars across axial slices, which complicates feature extraction, particularly in slices containing only the cusps; and (3) influence from adjacent teeth, which can propagate sequential numbering errors. These findings highlight the need to expand the dataset with more molar-region images to improve performance in future studies. Furthermore, both networks showed the lowest performance in detecting maxillary third molars. Possible reasons include the limited number of axial slices containing third molars in the dataset, their irregular morphology, and positional inconsistency within the maxilla due to restricted eruption space, which in turn exacerbates morphological irregularity. Given the difficulty of maintaining oral hygiene and limited visibility during treatment, long-term caries management outcomes for maxillary third molars are often suboptimal, which may limit the clinical utility of detailed caries assessment in these teeth. Therefore, the YOLOv3 (14-class scheme) appears to be a more reasonable choice.

In the caries identification task, DenseNet169 demonstrated the best overall performance, particularly under class imbalance. It achieved the highest balanced accuracy (0.7414), MCC (0.6074), and AUC-PR (0.7815), which provide a more informative assessment than standard accuracy when positive samples are limited. Beyond identification performance, calibration of predicted probabilities is important for supporting risk stratification for clinician review. DenseNet169 achieved the lowest Brier score (0.0821) with a relatively stable, monotonic trend. In contrast, MobileNet_V2 and ResNet50 showed distinct calibration deviations in the 0.6–0.8 probability range, suggesting potential overconfidence in that interval (Supplementary Figure 1C). Moreover, the analysis of confidence intervals (CIs) further supported the stability of DenseNet169’s probability estimates, with a narrow 95% CI for the Brier score (0.0643-0.1018). All three networks also showed consistently high specificity for identifying healthy teeth (95% CI: 0.9583-1.0000), confirming a low false-positive risk. Although sensitivity and AUC-PR fluctuated due to data constraints, the favorable calibration and specificity suggest that the proposed approach, particularly DenseNet169, may be suitable as an adjunctive tool for screening and triage support, pending external and prospective validation.

Regarding the model’s moderate sensitivity, retrospective analysis showed that missed cases were concentrated in two settings. First, missed detections frequently occurred in posterior teeth, especially near the occlusal surface, where fissures, cuspal morphology, and adjacent structures can generate heterogeneous radiolucency patterns and confound both annotation and model inference. Second, small or early lesions that were visible on only 1 or 2 axial slices were filtered by the 3-slice rule. Clinically, false negatives may lead to lesion progression, potentially increasing the likelihood of more extensive restorative intervention or even pulpal involvement. However, false positives primarily increase the verification burden on clinicians and may prompt unnecessary concern or additional imaging review, with a theoretical risk of overtreatment if not verified. Based on the current error profile, the main practical consequence of our model was the possibility of missed subtle lesions. Given that the intended role of the system is adjunctive screening and triage support instead of autonomous diagnosis, model outputs should prompt focused clinician review rather than replace clinician interpretation. Therefore, model-negative predictions in posterior regions should not be interpreted as excluding disease when clinical suspicion persists, and model-positive outputs should be used to prioritize review within the CBCT volume rather than directly guide treatment decisions.

Importantly, while the consecutive-slice threshold can affect model sensitivity, its determination depends on acquisition parameters, particularly voxel size. The sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Figure 2) indicated that, at a voxel resolution of 0.25 mm, thresholds of ≥1 or ≥2 slices (0.25 or 0.5 mm) were more susceptible to partial-volume effects and image noise, resulting in reduced specificity and PPV. Conversely, a higher threshold (≥5 slices; 1.25 mm) increased specificity but tended to miss shallow lesions, reducing sensitivity. Overall, the ≥3-slice threshold (0.75 mm) provided the most balanced performance between noise suppression and lesion detection in this setting. Given that the consecutive-slice threshold depends on voxel size and slice sampling, it should be adjusted across CBCT protocols to maintain a comparable physical continuity requirement (approximately 0.75–1.0 mm). Moreover, to mitigate residual false negatives related to the selected threshold, the model should be used within a human-in-the-loop workflow, in which clinicians re-evaluate anatomically complex posterior regions and subtle lesions that may be suppressed by a strict decision rule.

In addition, all three networks exhibited reduced performance in identifying caries in the molar region. This is likely attributable to the anatomical complexity of molars and the lack of 3-dimensional spatial continuity inherent in 2D axial slices. Despite these challenges, adopting a 2D approach represents a strategic trade-off between volumetric context and deployment feasibility. While 3D data provide comprehensive spatial information, processing full volumes often requires down-sampling or patch-based strategies to meet computational and memory constraints. These steps can reduce the visibility of subtle density changes relevant to early lesions. By focusing on 2D axial slices, our models preserve high in-plane resolution, supporting detection of subtle intensity and texture differences. Furthermore, 2D architectures are well established and benefit from extensive pre-training on ImageNet. This supports feature learning in limited medical datasets and reduces computational burden. Additionally, 2D models enable faster inference and lower hardware requirements, making them better aligned with clinical workflows that require efficient processing. To partially reintroduce spatial continuity, we applied the rule that a tooth is classified as carious only when positive predictions occur across at least three consecutive axial slices. This acts as a spatial consistency constraint that reduces isolated false positives and links 2D predictions to volumetric continuity. Even so, we suggest that AI-generated findings in the molar region be cross-referenced by clinicians using the original 3D views to confirm identification.

Due to the relatively poor detection performance observed under the 28-class and 32-class schemes (Figure 2A), our comparative analysis focused on the 14-class and 16-class schemes. It is worth noting that in the absence of an end-to-end penalization mechanism, the Cascade R-CNN-based pipeline demonstrated misleading performance inflation. Specifically, within traditional evaluation frameworks, the caries identification accuracy of the Cascade R-CNN-based pipeline appeared superior to that of YOLOv3. However, this apparent superiority was driven by selection bias, as Cascade R-CNN was prone to missed detections and localization errors in anatomically complex posterior teeth. Consequently, these difficult cases were excluded from the subsequent caries identification stage, artificially reducing the difficulty of the identification stage. In contrast, with the introduction of a rigorous end-to-end penalization mechanism, teeth failing to enter the identification stage due to missed detections and localization errors were directly counted as end-to-end errors, thereby effectively mitigating this inflation. Although the accuracy of the YOLOv3-based pipeline also declined under this penalized metric, it demonstrated superior end-to-end robustness compared with Cascade R-CNN due to its stronger detection performance. Given the small test sample size and inter-patient heterogeneity, the confidence intervals for the performance metrics were relatively wide for some measures. Nevertheless, these findings reinforce the positioning of this work as a preliminary technical feasibility study.

This study has several limitations. First, the dataset was relatively small and derived from a single center and a single CBCT scanner. Although ROI-based training increased slice-level samples, the limited patient cohort and class imbalance may constrain robustness and generalizability to other devices and populations. Despite data augmentation, sensitivity and AUC-PR remained suboptimal. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Future work should use repeated patient-level splits or cross-validation and perform external multicenter validation on heterogeneous datasets to obtain more stable estimates of generalizability.40

Second, the label mapping strategy based on bilateral symmetry (used under the 14-class and 16-class schemes) depends on the performance of the tooth detection model. Improving direct 28-class and 32-class detection performance may reduce reliance on this mapping. In addition, the 2D architecture, while computationally efficient, loses 3D spatial continuity. This limitation is particularly challenging in slices with overlapping maxillary and mandibular teeth and in complex molar regions. While our post-processing rules partially compensated for this, subtle lesions may therefore be missed. Future studies should explore 3D-CNNs or multi-view fusion to improve anatomical context with feasible computational demands. For external DICOM data, identical preprocessing (including windowing and normalization) is required to match training inputs. Potential domain shift from scanner- or center-specific DICOM export settings and preprocessing differences should be considered during external validation. The consecutive-slice threshold may also require adjustment across CBCT protocols to maintain a comparable physical continuity requirement (approximately 0.75–1.0 mm).

Third, we acknowledge the lack of an external validation cohort and prospective comparison with clinicians.41 Therefore, clinical utility (such as time efficiency or workflow impact) remains to be quantified, and these preliminary findings should be interpreted with caution. Consistent with the ALARA principle and current guidance, CBCT is not recommended for primary caries diagnosis; thus, this tool is intended only for opportunistic screening on CBCT scans acquired for non-caries indications, with deployment adhering to authoritative radiographic guidelines. Finally, this study focused on tooth detection, numbering, and caries identification. Future work will integrate caries severity classification to enable a more comprehensive pipeline and support further evaluation of clinical utility.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this exploratory retrospective study suggests the feasibility of a two-stage deep learning framework for the automated detection, numbering, and identification of dental caries in CBCT images. The combination of YOLOv3 (under the 14-class scheme) and DenseNet169 achieved promising preliminary results on the internal held-out test set. Although clinical utility has not been assessed in the present study, the proposed framework provides a technical basis for future adjunctive screening and triage support. Future work should validate these findings in larger, multi-center prospective cohorts and incorporate caries severity grading to support further evaluation of clinical utility.
