Predicting global distribution of Caryopteris under climate change using an optimized MaxEnt model: insights into ecological adaptation and conservation
Authors: Yang Liu, Feihong Liang, Wei Liu, Meng Zhu, Mili Liu, Qi Feng, Yizhong Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is reshaping plant species distributions, posing challenges for drought-adapted taxa with restricted native ranges. This study employed an optimized MaxEnt ecological niche model, using GBIF occurrence records and a suite of bioclimatic and UV-B radiation variables, to project the current and future potential distribution of Caryopteris Bunge under four climate scenarios (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585). The model exhibited relatively high predictive performance, forecasting a consistent expansion of highly suitable habitats across all future pathways, with the SSP585 scenario projecting a 24.41% increase by 2090. A northeastward shift of the habitat centroid suggests potential expansion into temperate and high-altitude ecosystems. UV-B radiation (28.4%), diurnal temperature range (23.0%), and precipitation seasonality (10.2%) were the most influential environmental predictors.However, increased fragmentation—especially in Central Asia and inland Australia—may reduce population viability.Conservation gap analysis revealed that, although the spatial extent of protected areas remains relatively stable under future climate scenarios, a large portion of highly suitable habitats, especially in East Asia and the Middle East, remains outside protected zones, indicating insufficient protection coverage in projected future hotspots. These findings provide critical insights for adaptive conservation planning and ecological restoration in arid and semi-arid regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09391-z.Keywords: Global change ecology, Caryopteris, MaxEnt model, UV-B radiation, Conservation

Introduction

Climate change is reshaping global plant distributions, particularly in drought-prone and ecologically fragile regions. Rising temperatures, altered precipitation regimes, and intensified environmental stress threaten not only individual species but also the stability of entire ecosystems [1–3]. Understanding how climate-sensitive taxa respond to these changes is essential for informing biodiversity conservation and adaptive ecosystem management [4, 5].

Although range shifts have been widely documented for mesic-adapted species—such as Fagus sylvatica in Europe and Quercus species in North America [4] —global-scale analyses of drought-tolerant and ecologically plastic genera remain scarce. This knowledge gap limits our ability to assess the resilience and future viability of such species under climate change.

Caryopteris Bunge, a drought-tolerant genus in the Verbenaceae family, is native to temperate Asia and parts of Europe. It commonly inhabits arid mountain slopes, grasslands, and degraded river valleys, thriving in nutrient-poor soils due to its tolerance to drought, cold, and other abiotic stressors [6]. Beyond its ecological plasticity, Caryopteris has ornamental, medicinal, and restoration value, owing to its antioxidant-rich secondary metabolites [7]. It also contributes to sand fixation, soil stabilization, and desertification control, making it a valuable species for ecological restoration in arid and semi-arid regions [8].

Naturalized populations of Caryopteris have been reported in Europe, North America, and Australia, where the genus is now established in gardens, urban landscapes, and disturbed wildlands [9]. These populations exhibit relatively stable ecological niches, suggesting partial climatic adaptation and supporting their inclusion in ecological niche modeling.

To capture its full ecological amplitude, this study adopts a genus-level modeling approach incorporating both native and naturalized occurrence data. This strategy is consistent with recent modeling practices for drought-tolerant genera such as Thymus and Artemisia [10, 11].

Despite the practical and ecological relevance of Caryopteris, its potential response to future climate scenarios remains poorly understood. Existing studies have seldom explored how its distribution may shift under projected climate trajectories [12, 13]. To address this gap, we employed an optimized MaxEnt ecological niche model to project the current and future distribution of Caryopteris under four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585) across four future periods: 2030s, 2050s, 2070s, and 2090s. Species occurrence data were obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) and combined with 19 bioclimatic and topographic variables derived from CMIP6. In addition, ultraviolet-B (UV-B) radiation—an often overlooked but ecologically relevant factor for drought-adapted plants—was included as a predictor due to its effects on plant morphology, physiology, and secondary metabolite accumulation [14, 15].

Rather than predicting precise species-level distributions, this study aims to identify broad climatic trends, key environmental drivers, and potential shifts in macroecological suitability. By clarifying these climatic patterns, the study provides a scientifically grounded basis to inform future research, horticultural management, and climate-resilient conservation planning in arid and semi-arid landscapes.

Materials and methods

Data collection

Species occurrence data

Occurrence records for Caryopteris Bunge were obtained from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF, http://www.gbif.org, accessed on March 19, 2025) [16], yielding 3,056 distribution points. Using ArcGIS 10.4 the “CoordinateCleaner” package in R 3.6.3 [17], duplicate entries and those located in marine environments, capital cities, or national administrative centers were removed. To reduce spatial redundancy and model overfitting, ENMTools v1.3 (https://www.example.com/enmtools, accessed on March 19, 2025) [18] was employed to retain only one occurrence per 5 × 5 km grid cell. This spatial filtering minimizes autocorrelation and sampling bias, a common issue in ecological niche modeling. The 5 km resolution balances data reduction with ecological representativeness [19, 20]. After filtering, 906 unique occurrence points were retained (Fig. 1). Due to the limited number of high-quality records for individual species and the genus’s disjunct global distribution, a genus-level modeling approach was adopted. This improves prediction stability and aligns with practices used for other drought-tolerant genera with sparse data [10, 11].

Data processing and model calibration

Selection of bioclimatic variables

To minimize multicollinearity [26], Spearman’s rank correlation analysis was performed on variables extracted at all 906 occurrence points using R 3.6.3 (Fig. 2). Pairwise coefficients were calculated with base R functions. Jackknife tests in MaxEnt 3.4.4 assessed individual variable contributions. For correlated pairs (|r| > 0.7), the variable with higher model contribution was retained [18], resulting in 10 final predictors (Table 1).

Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was not applied, as it assumes linearity and multivariate normality, which are not required by MaxEnt’s machine learning framework [27]. Eliminating variables based on VIF could remove ecologically relevant predictors.

MaxEnt model optimization

MaxEnt’s default settings are optimized for a range of taxa but remain sensitive to sampling bias [28]. Model complexity is influenced by the feature combination (FC) and regularization multiplier (RM). We used the “kuenm” package in R 3.6.3 [29] to optimize RM (0.1–4) and FC (LQPTH) combinations in 0.1 steps. A total of 1,160 candidate models were tested (Table S1), and the final model was selected based on the lowest corrected Akaike Information Criterion (delta AICc) [30]. The kuenm workflow was selected because it allows comprehensive evaluation across multiple calibration criteria (e.g., AICc, omission rate) and is well suited for datasets where spatially structured cross-validation is not feasible due to fragmented occurrence patterns [31].

Model settings and evaluation

MaxEnt 3.4.4 was run using the standalone desktop GUI version with the following settings: logistic output format; random seed enabled; 25% test data; 500 iterations; 10,000 background points; and 10 replicate runs [18]. Background points were sampled randomly across the study area, consistent with standard practice [11]. A global background was selected to ensure representation of the full climatic gradient across the study region, as buffer-based background selection was unsuitable due to the fragmented and geographically discontinuous distribution of Caryopteris.

Each replicate used 75% of occurrences for training and 25% for testing. Although spatial cross-validation methods (e.g., block or checkerboard partitioning) are gaining popularity in SDMs, we adopted random subsampling paired with replicate runs and random seed, following recent MaxEnt protocols [11, 32]. This strategy yields consistent model performance across replicates while maintaining model simplicity.

Model evaluation relied on the area under the curve (AUC) and true skill statistic (TSS). AUC values were categorized as follows: 0.5–0.7 (poor), 0.7–0.9 (good), and 0.9–1.0 (excellent) [33]. The classification threshold was selected using the maximum sum of sensitivity and specificity (maxSSS), which balances omission and commission errors effectively [11]. Since the GUI version does not compute TSS directly, we calculated it manually in R. Binary habitat suitability maps were generated using the maxSSS threshold, and confusion matrices were built by comparing predicted presence–absence with test data. TSS was then computed as TSS = sensitivity + specificity – 1, following standard practice [34]. Performance was categorized as excellent (> 0.8), good (0.6–0.8), moderate (0.4–0.6), or poor (< 0.4) [35].

In addition to AUC and TSS, we calculated the Boyce Index using the continuous logistic suitability output for the current-climate model to provide an additional presence-only evaluation metric. RMSE was not used because it is not statistically informative for presence-only suitability predictions, whereas the Boyce Index was incorporated as an additional evaluation metric [36]. Prediction uncertainty was quantified by calculating the standard deviation of suitability values across 10 replicate runs for the SSP585–2090 scenario, using the calc() function in the R raster package and visualized [27].

Habitat suitability classification

Predicted suitability was reclassified in ArcGIS 10.4 using natural breaks into four classes: unsuitable (0–0.135), low (0.135–0.306), moderate (0.306–0.477), and high suitability (0.477–0.880) [37]. This enhances readability and supports interpretation by land managers [38].

Habitat area and centroid shift analysis

Changes in suitable area across time periods were assessed using the “Distribution Change Between Binary SDMs” tool in SDMTools [39], with categories: 0 (unsuitable), -1 (gain), 1 (stable), 2 (loss). Centroid shifts were calculated to trace changes in the geographic center of suitable habitat over time [40].

Protected area overlay and gap analysis

Global protected area data were retrieved from the World Database on Protected Areas (WDPA, https://www.protectedplanet.net/en/thematic-areas/wdpa?tab=WDPA, accessed on April 1, 2025) [41]. Potential habitat coverage within protected areas was assessed in ArcGIS 10.4 [42]. The analysis included both terrestrial and marine protected zones to account for indirect conservation benefits and land-sea ecological connectivity. Spatial overlays were used to differentiate and quantify protected coverage. This approach aligns with emerging standards for integrated conservation planning [43].

Results and analysis

Model optimization and accuracy assessment

A comprehensive optimization process evaluated 1,160 combinations of feature classes (FC) and regularization multipliers (RM) via cross-validation. The optimal model, with FC = Q and RM = 0.1, achieved an omission rate of 4.19% and the lowest delta AICc value (0), satisfying the predefined criteria (Fig. 3a). This model demonstrated strong predictive performance, with an average test AUC of 0.804 and an average TSS of 0.7938 (Table 2; Fig. 3b), indicating high model reliability. Additionally, the standard deviation across 10 replicate runs was low, reflecting stable spatial predictions (Fig. 3c). In addition to AUC and TSS, the Boyce Index for the current-climate model was 0.445, reflecting moderate discriminative ability consistent with the presence-only nature and sampling heterogeneity of the dataset.

Key environmental predictors

The MaxEnt model outputs- including regularized training gain, test gain, permutation importance, and Jackknife test results—clearly identified the dominant environmental factors influencing the distribution of Caryopteris Bunge (Figs. 4 and 5; Table 1). The Jackknife test (Fig. 4) highlighted the unique contribution of each variable when used independently or omitted from the model. The five most important predictors were minimum monthly average UV-B radiation (UVB-4), diurnal temperature range (Bio2), elevation (DEM), precipitation seasonality (Bio15), and soil texture (ST), contributing 28.4%, 23.0%, 12.1%, 10.2%, and 10.0%, respectively, for a cumulative contribution of 83.7%. Their corresponding permutation importance values were 24.6%, 32.8%, 1.6%, 14.5%, and 5.8%, totaling 79.3%.

Current distribution under contemporary climate

Under current climatic conditions, the potential distribution area of the Caryopteris Bunge covers approximately 99.45 million km². Within this area, highly suitable habitats constitute 11.29% (17.18 million km²), moderately suitable areas 38.31% (38.10 million km²), and low-suitability areas 44.40% (44.16 million km²) (Fig. 6; Table 3).

The genus is primarily distributed across eastern and central Asia, with species extending into Southeast Asia and the Himalayas. In China, Caryopteris incana is the most widespread, thriving in elevations from several hundred to 2,000 m and occurring along forest edges, shrublands, dry gravel slopes, and roadsides. Caryopteris divaricata primarily inhabits high-altitude regions in southwestern China—such as Sichuan, Yunnan, and Guizhou-and extends to northern India and Nepal. Caryopteris trichosphaera, endemic to China, is confined to cold, arid regions along the eastern margin of the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau (e.g., southeastern Tibet and western Sichuan) above 3,000 m, favoring dry valleys and stony slopes.

In Japan, Caryopteris is largely represented by cultivated varieties, especially Caryopteris × clandonensis (bluebeard), widely used ornamentally. Natural populations also occur in parts of the Korean Peninsula and southern Mongolia, adapted to temperate continental climates. The genus has significant potential for ecological restoration in arid regions and ornamental use due to its drought and cold tolerance and synchronized flowering.

Although native to Asia, horticultural expansion has introduced Caryopteris hybrids into Europe, the Americas, and Australia, where they are naturalized in some regions. This broadens the genus’s ecological amplitude and confirms its restoration and ornamental value globally.

Projected future distribution under climate scenarios

Under future emission scenarios (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585), the potential suitable habitats of Caryopteris Bunge remain concentrated in East Asia, Central Asia, and temperate regions. Although the overall suitable area remains relatively stable, the area classified as highly suitable consistently increases, highlighting the species’ adaptability (Figs. 7 and 8; Table 3).

Protection status and conservation gaps

The world’s largest protected areas are located in remote regions like Greenland, while potential habitats for Caryopteris in East Asia, Central Asia, Southern Europe, and the Middle East are often under fragmented protection.

Overlay analysis between the WDPA database and model-predicted high-suitability zones (threshold > 0.6) revealed that only ~ 9.7% of such areas are currently protected. Although Caryopteris is terrestrial, adjacent marine and coastal zones were included to reflect broader ecological benefits such as watershed integrity and landscape connectivity [44].

In East Asia, protection drops below 8.5%, leaving areas like the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau and northern Iran largely unprotected.

While highly suitable habitats for Caryopteris are predicted to expand under future climate scenarios (Fig. 7), their overlap with existing protected areas—shown under current conditions in Fig. 10—remains limited. This spatial mismatch highlights a critical conservation gap: many of the newly emergent or persistently suitable habitats lie outside the current protection network. These findings underscore the need to strengthen and adapt existing conservation strategies to safeguard the long-term survival of Caryopteris under climate change.

Discussion

Major findings and ecological implications

This study employed an optimized MaxEnt model to evaluate the potential geographic distribution of Caryopteris Bunge under current and future climate scenarios (SSP126, SSP245, SSP370, and SSP585). Our results revealed a consistent expansion in highly suitable habitats across most scenarios, even as areas of low suitability experienced minor reductions. Notably, under the extreme SSP585 scenario, the total suitable habitat area is projected to increase by 24.41% by 2090. This expansion is accompanied by a systematic northeastward migration of the habitat centroid, with markedly improved suitability in the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau, the Mongolian Plateau, and Southern Europe. These patterns highlight the species’ sensitivity to warming trends and its strong potential for range expansion [8, 45].

From an ecological perspective, the observed centroid shift implies that Caryopteris is progressively colonizing temperate and high-altitude ecosystems. This trend appears to be driven by rising temperatures alleviating climatic constraints in formerly cooler regions, thereby opening up new suitable habitats at higher latitudes and elevations.

These migration patterns are consistent with the well-documented phenomenon of poleward and upslope movement in response to climate change [46, 47]. Together, these findings underscore the ecological resilience and adaptive potential of Caryopteris, enabling it to exploit newly emergent niches under changing conditions.

Moreover, our model identified UV-B radiation (28.4% contribution), diurnal temperature range (Bio2, 23%), and precipitation seasonality (Bio15, 10.2%) as the top bioclimatic predictors. UV-B radiation, in particular, exerts a dual influence: while excessive levels can cause photoinhibition and oxidative stress, moderate levels promote photomorphogenesis and stimulate the synthesis of protective secondary metabolites, thereby improving stress resilience and photosynthetic performance [48, 49]. The high importance of UV-B radiation suggests that Caryopteris may possess specific physiological adaptations—such as cuticle thickening, strong antioxidant systems, and potential root–microbe symbioses—that enhance its ecological plasticity under environmental stress [50, 51].

Despite the overall expansion of suitable range, our projections also indicate increasing habitat fragmentation in arid and semi-arid regions, especially Central Asia and inland Australia. While these areas become climatically suitable, colonization may be hindered by topographic complexity, substrate heterogeneity, uneven water availability, and historical isolation [52, 53]. This fragmentation poses dual risks: limited dispersal and gene flow may reduce genetic diversity [54], while weakened landscape connectivity could diminish population resilience to extreme climate events [55]. Thus, although climate change may create new opportunities for expansion [56], the structural and functional stability of Caryopteris populations remains vulnerable.

Comparison with previous studies

In contrast to earlier studies predicting substantial habitat loss for moisture-sensitive species under climate change [57, 58], our findings support the “climate winners” hypothesis [59, 60]. This hypothesis suggests that drought-tolerant species like Caryopteris Bunge may actually benefit from global warming. For instance, the documented northward migration of Artemisia annua [61] mirrors the centroid shift observed for Caryopteris in this study. Similar adaptive responses have been reported in Mediterranean aromatic shrubs such as Thymus vulgaris, which demonstrate resilience and adaptability under warming trends [62].

Moreover, although our projections indicate an increase in highly suitable habitats for Caryopteris, they also reveal a troubling stagnation in the spatial coverage of protected areas in these zones. As shown in Fig. 10, important regions such as the Iranian Plateau and northwestern China experience significant increases in climatic suitability but remain largely outside protected areas. This mismatch between future habitat projections and existing conservation networks echoes concerns raised in prior research [63, 64], reinforcing the need to enhance spatial coverage and connectivity of protected areas to safeguard Caryopteris and associated species.

Limitations and uncertainties

Although the optimized MaxEnt model provides valuable macroecological insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the occurrence dataset is strongly biased toward East Asia, particularly China, while verified records from Central and Western Asia remain sparse, which may lead to underestimated climatic suitability in these regions [65]. Second, only climatic and coarse topographic variables were included; important non-climatic drivers—such as soil properties, land use, disturbance, and biotic interactions—are known to influence habitat quality but were unavailable at comparable global resolution [66, 67]. Third, the 2.5′ (~ 5 km) resolution and the clustering of native occurrences may still introduce geographic bias despite the 5 × 5 km thinning procedure. More comprehensive, taxonomically verified records outside Asia will be required for future multi-scale modeling.

Several methodological constraints should also be noted. The strong imbalance and taxonomic heterogeneity of the dataset—dense, species-level identifications in East Asia but sparse, often genus-level records elsewhere—preclude reliable niche similarity or equivalency tests. Spatially structured cross-validation is also infeasible because effective spatial blocking requires geographically continuous and environmentally comparable data partitions, which do not exist for this fragmented global distribution. Ensemble or BART-based approaches were not adopted because pseudo-absence uncertainty would be substantial under presence-only and spatially biased sampling.

The genus-level modeling framework introduces additional uncertainty. Species-level SDMs could in principle capture finer ecological differentiation, but such an approach is not feasible for Caryopteris at present due to uneven taxonomic resolution and insufficient species-level occurrences outside East Asia. Species-level models would therefore be dominated by a single well-sampled species, producing unstable climatic envelopes. In contrast, a genus-level model provides greater statistical stability and aligns with the macroecological objectives of this study.

Similar approaches in drought-tolerant genera such as Thymus and Artemisia have shown reliable performance when models are rigorously calibrated [10, 11].

We also recognize potential concerns regarding the integration of native and non-native occurrences. While non-native records may broaden estimated climatic tolerance, naturalized populations in Europe and North America appear to be in quasi-equilibrium with local climates and thus offer complementary information on realized climatic limits. Their influence is moderate, as indicated by the Boyce Index (0.445) and clustering patterns.

Consequently, the reported evaluation metrics represent relative rather than fully spatially independent estimates of model performance. Future projections—particularly under high-emission scenarios—remain sensitive to divergence among GCMs and precipitation-related predictors [68]. Moreover, SDMs that rely exclusively on climatic variables may overestimate range expansion because they do not incorporate dispersal constraints, species interactions, or landscape structure [69]. Taken together, the model should be interpreted as a macroecological climatic envelope rather than a precise species-level prediction. As more balanced, taxonomically verified global datasets become available, species-level niche analyses, spatial cross-validation, and ensemble or mechanistic frameworks will further improve predictive realism.

Conclusion

This study offers a comprehensive assessment of the potential distribution dynamics of Caryopteris Bunge under multiple future climate scenarios, integrating an optimized MaxEnt ecological niche model with diverse environmental datasets. Our results project a substantial expansion of highly suitable habitats across all future scenarios, with the SSP585 pathway indicating an increase of up to 24.41% by 2090. The predicted northeastward shift of the species’ habitat centroid suggests a potential migration toward temperate and high-altitude ecosystems.

Notably, UV-B radiation, diurnal temperature range, and precipitation seasonality emerged as key climatic drivers influencing the species’ distribution. The incorporation of UV-B as a predictive variable represents a novel advancement in species distribution modeling, particularly for drought-adapted taxa.

Despite the projected expansion, our findings also underscore critical ecological and conservation challenges. Habitat fragmentation—especially in arid regions such as Central Asia and inland Australia—may constrain natural dispersal and threaten long-term population viability. Moreover, the limited spatial congruence between the current high-suitability habitats of Caryopteris and existing protected areas highlights a critical conservation gap in the present protection network. This spatial mismatch calls for strategic reassessment and expansion of protected areas to ensure long-term habitat security. Our conservation gap analysis further reveals substantial deficiencies in protected area coverage across current critical habitats, particularly in East Asia and the Middle East. While our modeling framework offers relatively high predictions, it is important to acknowledge uncertainties inherent in species occurrence data and climate projections, warranting continued refinement.

To address these challenges, we recommend establishing ecological corridors to enhance habitat connectivity, cautiously implementing assisted migration in climatically suitable but currently unoccupied areas, and developing long-term monitoring programs in ecologically vulnerable regions. Future research should also incorporate genomic data and explore biotic interactions to deepen our understanding of adaptive responses under climate change. Overall, this study enhances our understanding of the ecological resilience of drought-tolerant species under global change and provides a robust scientific foundation for promoting nature-based solutions and sustainable conservation strategies in arid and semi-arid ecosystems.
