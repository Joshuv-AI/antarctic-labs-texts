Biological age acceleration and bradyarrhythmia: evidence from clinical and epigenetic perspectives
Authors: Zheng-Qi Song, Lu-Jie Huang, Ke Liu, Sheng-Ke Wu, Jia-Rui Chen, Yan-Zhen Pei, Yi-He Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo date, effective preventive and therapeutic strategies for early-stage bradyarrhythmia remain limited.MethodsWe first calculated two well-established biological aging (BA) measures (Klemera-Doubal Method [KDM] and Phenotypic Age [PhenoAge]), with BA acceleration defined as the residual from regressing each BA measure on chronological age. Introduction

Bradyarrhythmias encompass a broad spectrum of rhythm and conduction disorders characterized by pathological slowing of the heart rate and/or impaired impulse propagation, imposing a substantial clinical and healthcare burden worldwide. The development of bradyarrhythmia can reduce cardiac output and precipitate syncope, progressive heart failure, and even sudden death [1–3]. To date, permanent pacemaker implantation remains the cornerstone and most widely accepted therapy for end-stage bradyarrhythmia. However, device therapy is associated with peri-procedural and long-term complications, including infection, hematoma or bleeding, pneumothorax, and lead-related mechanical failure, which contribute to morbidity [4–6]. Hence, implementing early preventive strategies to reduce the risk of bradyarrhythmia is of great importance.

Biological aging reflects the progressive accumulation of cellular and physiological deficits that increase vulnerability to internal and external stressors. Compared with chronological age, biological age may more precisely capture inter-individual differences in disease susceptibility and thereby provide a more informative framework for explaining heterogeneity in health outcomes [7]. In recent years, a growing number of clinical measure-based algorithms have been developed to estimate biological age [8]. Among these, the Klemera-Doubal method biological age (KDM-BA) and phenotypic age (PhenoAge) have demonstrated robust predictive validity for morbidity and mortality across diverse cohorts [9, 10]. Both indices are derived from routinely collected clinical measures (e.g., C-reactive protein and albumin), facilitating their application in large-scale population research. Previous cohort studies have shown that KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration are associated with an increased risk of multiple cardiovascular diseases, including atrial fibrillation, stroke, heart failure, and coronary heart disease [11–14]. However, studies investigating the association between these two biological age measures and bradyarrhythmia risk remain limited.

Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation (DNAm), are widely regarded as highly accurate molecular readouts of biological aging [15, 16]. Growing evidence also implicates epigenetic mechanisms in susceptibility to bradyarrhythmia [17, 18]. Of note, a recent study suggests that GrimAge, a DNAm-based biological age metric, may be associated with incident bradyarrhythmias [18]. Nevertheless, there are still limited studies investigating the relationship between aging-related DNAm CpG sites and bradyarrhythmias, hindering a deeper understanding of the molecular pathways that drive these associations.

To address these gaps, we integrated evidence from observational and epigenetic perspectives to comprehensively investigate the associations between biological age acceleration and bradyarrhythmias. First, we conducted a prospective cohort study in the UK Biobank to evaluate the associations of KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration with incident bradyarrhythmias. Second, we performed epigenetic Mendelian randomization (MR) and colocalization analyses to prioritize aging-related DNAm CpG sites associated with bradyarrhythmias, thereby providing novel insights into downstream epigenetic mechanisms linking biological age acceleration to bradyarrhythmia risk.

Method

Prospective cohort study from UK biobank

The workflow of the prospective cohort study is illustrated in Fig. 1. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines, and the completed checklist is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

The workflow of the prospective cohort study

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large, population-based cohort comprising over 500,000 participants recruited from 22 assessment centres across the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010, with ongoing prospective follow-up. Details of the study design and data collection have been described previously [19]. Of the 501,956 participants initially enrolled, we excluded individuals with incomplete data required to calculate biological age measures (n = 124,358), missing covariates (n = 65,397), prevalent cardiovascular disease at baseline (n = 13,050), and pre-existing bradyarrhythmias or pacemaker implantation at baseline (n = 937). The final analytic sample included 298,214 participants (Fig. 1).

Assessment of biological age and biological age acceleration

We applied two widely validated biological aging algorithms: KDM-BA [20] and PhenoAge [21], both of which have been extensively used in UK Biobank studies [13, 22]. PhenoAge was calculated from chronological age and nine circulating biomarkers (alkaline phosphatase, albumin, C-reactive protein, creatinine, glucose, mean cell volume, red cell distribution width, white blood cell count, and lymphocyte percentage). KDM-BA was estimated using systolic blood pressure (SBP), forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1), and seven laboratory measures (alkaline phosphatase, albumin, blood urea nitrogen, C-reactive protein, creatinine, glycated hemoglobin, and total cholesterol). KDM-BA and PhenoAge were calculated using the “BioAge” R package [23]. The included biomarkers and their corresponding UK Biobank data field IDs are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

To quantify deviations of biological age from chronological age, biological age acceleration was defined as the residual from regressing each biological age estimate (KDM-BA or PhenoAge) on chronological age. Positive residuals indicate biological age acceleration, whereas negative residuals indicate decelerated aging [22]. Acceleration measures were subsequently standardized to z-scores (mean = 0, SD = 1) to facilitate comparisons of effect sizes across metrics [13].

Assessment of covariates

Potential confounders and modifiers were collected as covariates, including age (years), sex (male or female), ethnicity (non-white or white), Townsend Deprivation Index, education level (unknown, college, or other levels), household income (<£18,000; £18,000 to £30,999; £31,000 to £51,999; £52,000 to £100,000; >£100,000), body mass index (< 18.5; 18.5–25; 25–30; ≥30) [24], physical activity (inactive, active) [25], smoking status (never, previous, or current), drinking status (never, previous, or current), and healthy diet score [26]. Detailed definitions of each covariate are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Assessment of outcome

The primary outcome was incident bradyarrhythmia, defined as sick sinus syndrome (SSS), distal conduction disease (DCD; including atrioventricular block [AVB], left bundle branch block [LBBB], and right bundle branch block [RBBB]), or pacemaker implantation. Secondary outcomes included incident SSS, DCD, LBBB, and severe conduction disease (defined as third-degree AVB or pacemaker implantation). Diagnoses were ascertained through linkage to hospital inpatient records using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes. Pacemaker implantation was identified using the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Surgical Operations and Procedures, Fourth Revision (OPCS-4) procedure codes. Detailed code lists for diagnoses, pacemaker implantation, and baseline cardiovascular disease history are provided in Supplementary Table S4. Follow-up time was defined from baseline to the earliest of incident bradyarrhythmia, death, loss to follow-up, or the censoring date (March 31, 2023).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as counts (percentages), and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (Q1-Q3). Group differences between participants who developed bradyarrhythmia during follow-up and those who did not were evaluated using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables. Associations between biological age acceleration and incident bradyarrhythmia were quantified using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) estimated. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, Townsend Deprivation Index, education, and household income. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for body mass index, physical activity, smoking status, drinking status, and healthy diet score. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no evidence of violation was observed (P > 0.05) [27]. Dose-response associations between biological age acceleration and incident bradyarrhythmia were further examined using restricted cubic spline models with three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of biological age acceleration [28].

Subgroup analyses were performed across strata of age, sex, body mass index, physical activity, smoking status, and drinking status. Effect modification was evaluated by including multiplicative interaction terms between biological age acceleration and each stratification factor. Several sensitivity analyses were undertaken to assess robustness: (i) excluding bradyarrhythmia events occurring within the first two years of follow-up to mitigate potential reverse causation; (ii) applying Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models to account for death as a competing risk; and (iii) handling missing covariates using multiple imputation by chained equations (n = 5) implemented with the R package “mice”.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.0, with UK Biobank data processing facilitated by the FastUKB R package [29]. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05.

Epigenetic mendelian randomization study

Identifying the mQTL of aging-related DNA methylation

Consistent with a previous study[30], we obtained 8,540 aging-related CpG sites reported by Marttila et al. [31] and retrieved their corresponding cis-methylation quantitative trait loci (cis-mQTLs) from the GoDMC Consortium database [32]. Independent cis-mQTLs were selected at genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10−8) and pruned for linkage disequilibrium using clumping (r2 < 0.001 within a 10,000 kb window). Finally, 3,853 CpG sites with 4,688 cis-mQTLs were identified (Supplementary Table S5).

GWAS summary statistics of bradyarrhythmia

Genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics for three bradyarrhythmia-related phenotypes were obtained from a recent large-scale GWAS meta-analysis by Weng et al. to proxy bradyarrhythmia [33], which included 9,511 cases and 1,249,043 controls for SSS, 37,798 cases and 1,263,549 controls for DCD, and 28,899 cases and 1,275,332 controls for pacemaker implantation.

Epigenetic mendelian randomization analysis and colocalization analysis

The genome build used for both the exposure and outcome GWAS datasets was based on GRCh37/hg19. Prior to the epigenetic MR analysis, exposure and outcome summary statistics were harmonized to ensure consistency of effect alleles. For palindromic SNPs (A/T or C/G), variants were retained only when the strand orientation could be confidently inferred from allele frequency information. Ambiguous palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies or missing allele frequency data were removed to avoid potential strand misalignment. The resulting independent cis-mQTLs were then used as genetic instruments to assess the causal relationship between aging-related DNA methylation CpG sites and bradyarrhythmia risk. When only one independent cis-mQTL was available as an instrumental variable (IV), causal effects were estimated using the Wald ratio method. If two or more independent cis-mQTLs were available as IVs, we applied the random-effects inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method [34]. For multiple testing correction, we used false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment, with PFDR < 0.05 considered statistically significant. For CpG sites that remained significant after FDR adjustment, we further conducted Bayesian colocalization analysis to determine whether CpG methylation and bradyarrhythmia were driven by the same causal variant within the locus [35, 36]. Bayesian colocalization was evaluated under five hypotheses: H0 (no causal variant for either trait), H1 (causal variant for the CpG site only), H2 (causal variant for bradyarrhythmia only), H3 (distinct causal variants for the two traits), and H4 (a single shared causal variant affecting both traits). Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within ± 500 kb of each CpG site were extracted for colocalization testing [30]. Strong evidence of colocalization was defined as a posterior probability of H4 (PP.H4) > 0.80.

All epigenetic MR and colocalization analyses were implemented using the MendelR [37]. R package in R version 4.4.0 and the Cloud-Based GWAS Platform[38].

Construction of gene-gene interaction networks and enrichment analysis

To elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying CpG-mapped genes associated with SSS, DCD, and pacemaker implantation, respectively, we constructed gene-gene interaction networks using GeneMANIA network analysis (https://genemania.org/) [39]. The networks integrated evidence from co-expression, physical interactions, co-localization, genetic interactions, and shared protein domains. Subsequently, enrichment analysis was performed on both the query genes and their functionally related interacting genes to identify potentially involved biological processes.

The median age at baseline was 57 years, and 54.9% of participants were female. Compared with participants who did not develop bradyarrhythmia, incident bradyarrhythmia individuals were older and more likely to be male and obese. Meanwhile, they also exhibited significantly older biological age and greater biological age acceleration.

Observational association between biological age acceleration and bradyarrhythmia

As presented in Table 2, after adjusting for potential confounders in Model 2, per 1-SD increase in PhenoAge acceleration was significantly associated with a 13% higher incidence of bradyarrhythmia (HR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.11–1.15, P < 0.001), while per 1-SD increase in KDM-BA acceleration was associated with an 11% higher risk of bradyarrhythmia (HR = 1.11, 95% CI 1.09–1.13, P < 0.001). In categorical (quartile) analyses, compared with the lowest quartile (Q1), the HRs for the highest quartile (Q4) were 1.27 (95% CI 1.21–1.34, P < 0.001) for PhenoAge acceleration and 1.36 (95% CI 1.29–1.43, P < 0.001) for KDM-BA acceleration in Model 2. Similar results were observed in Model 1 (Table 2). In dose-response analyses, restricted cubic spline models showed nonlinear positive associations of KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration with bradyarrhythmia under Model 2 adjustment (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinearity < 0.001). Of note, we observed a significant increase in bradyarrhythmia risk only when KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration were above zero, whereas no significant change in risk was observed when they were below zero (Fig. 2). When further examining the associations of KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration with the incidence of SSS, DCD, LBBB, and severe conduction disease, we observed results similar to those for bradyarrhythmia (Supplementary Table S6 and Supplementary Figure S1).

Identification of aging-related DNAm CpG sites for bradyarrhythmia

In the epigenetic MR analysis, a total of 3, 8, and 5 aging-related DNAm CpG sites were identified to be associated with SSS, DCD and pacemaker implantation, respectively. (PFDR < 0.05) (Fig. 4A–C and Supplementary Table S10). Among these, 9 DNAm CpG sites mapped to 8 genes and were supported by strong colocalization evidence (PP.H4 > 0.80) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S11). Specifically, cg11410859 (SCN5A), cg18249173 (TBX20), cg07185587 (AKAP6), cg16555537 (TRIP6), cg10459018, cg03473532 (MKLN1), and cg13654588 (PRLHR) were associated with a reduced risk of bradyarrhythmia-related phenotypes (OR < 1; PP.H4 > 0.80; PFDR < 0.05). In contrast, cg25286333 (RNF207) and cg02447380 (RAB42) were associated with an increased risk of bradyarrhythmia-related phenotypes (OR > 1; PP.H4 > 0.80; PFDR < 0.05).

Gene-gene interaction network construction and enrichment analysis

As shown in Supplementary Figure S2, GeneMANIA-based gene-gene interaction networks were constructed using CpG-mapped genes identified from the epigenetic MR analysis for DCD, pacemaker implantation, and SSS. In DCD, the network was mainly enriched in cardiac electrophysiological and contractile processes, including cardiac muscle contraction, action potential, regulation of membrane potential, and sodium ion transport (Supplementary Figure S2A). For pacemaker implantation and SSS, the enriched pathways showed considerable overlap, particularly in cytoskeleton- and actomyosin-related processes, including contractile actin filament bundle, actomyosin, and actin filament bundle. Notably, pacemaker implantation also showed enrichment in calcium-handling processes, such as sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ion transport and regulation of cytosolic calcium ion concentration, whereas SSS was additionally associated with cell-substrate adhesion and cell leading edge-related processes (Supplementary Figure S2B and Supplementary Figure S2C).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to integrate clinical and epigenetic evidence to comprehensively evaluate the associations among biological age acceleration, underlying epigenetic pathways, and bradyarrhythmia risk. In the prospective cohort analysis, both KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration were associated with an elevated risk of bradyarrhythmia, and dose-response analyses further showed a significantly increased risk when acceleration values exceeded 0. In the epigenetic MR analysis, we prioritized 9 aging-related DNAm CpG sites mapping to 8 genes implicated in bradyarrhythmia, such as cg11410859 (SCN5A) and cg18249173 (TBX20).

Previous population-based studies have shown that bradyarrhythmias are strongly associated with advancing age and cardiometabolic risk factors, including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia [17, 40–42]. Therefore, reliance on chronological age alone is insufficient to fully capture an individual’s true risk of bradyarrhythmia. In contrast, the biological age measures KDM-BA and PhenoAge integrate cumulative multisystem damage and functional decline, thereby providing risk information beyond chronological age [20, 21]. Previous studies have suggested that acceleration in KDM-BA and PhenoAge may serve as risk factors for atrial fibrillation [11] Similarly, in our prospective cohort, each 1-SD increase in KDM-BA acceleration and PhenoAge acceleration was associated with an 11% and 13% higher risk of bradyarrhythmia, respectively. Notably, the dose-response analysis indicated a marked increase in bradyarrhythmia risk when acceleration values exceeded zero, suggesting that individuals in an advanced biological aging state may have a substantially elevated susceptibility to bradyarrhythmia. When comparing their composition, KDM-BA and PhenoAge share several common clinical indicators (C-reactive protein, albumin, alkaline phosphatase, and creatinine) but emphasize distinct biological domains. KDM-BA incorporates indicators such as FEV1, systolic blood pressure, glycated hemoglobin, and total cholesterol, thus placing greater weight on cardiovascular and metabolic aging. By contrast, PhenoAge more strongly captures inflammatory/immune aging, including measures such as white blood cell count, lymphocyte proportion, mean corpuscular volume, and red cell distribution width [43] These components and their corresponding pathways have been linked to the development and progression of bradyarrhythmias [41, 44–46]. Although other biological aging measures have also been proposed in relation to bradyarrhythmias, such as leukocyte telomere length and DNAm-derived GrimAge [18, 47], quantifying these measures remains challenging, which may limit their application in large population studies. Importantly, both KDM-BA and PhenoAge can be derived from routinely available clinical chemistry and complete blood count data, making them more accessible than telomere length or DNAm-based aging markers and thereby more feasible for population-level screening, risk stratification, and early preventive interventions.

In our subsequent epigenetic MR and colocalization analyses, we identified 9 aging-related CpG sites mapped to 8 genes (SCN5A, TBX20, AKAP6, TRIP6, MKLN1, PRLHR, RNF207, and RAB42) that were significantly associated with bradyarrhythmia-related phenotypes. Although direct evidence linking these specific aging-related CpG sites to bradyarrhythmias remains limited, DNAm can modulate gene expression, raising the possibility that these CpGs influence bradyarrhythmia susceptibility through regulation of their mapped downstream genes [48]. Specifically, cg11410859 (SCN5A) was associated with a reduced risk of DCD. Prior studies have reported that human SCN5A loss-of-function variants are related to AVB and clinically relevant pacing/capture problems, underscoring the pivotal role of SCN5A in bradyarrhythmia susceptibility [49–51]. In contrast, cg25286333 (RNF207) was associated with an increased risk of DCD, consistent with functional evidence that RNF207 regulates cardiac excitation by modulating hERG abundance/trafficking. Previous basic research also unveiled that knockdown of RNF207 in zebrafish prolonged action potential duration and was accompanied by slowed conduction velocity and occasional 2:1 AVB [52] cg18249173 (TBX20) was inversely associated with DCD, concordant with GWAS evidence implicating loci near TBX20 in PR-interval and related conduction traits [53]. Meanwhile, experimental data also showed that adult cardiomyocyte TBX20 deletion disrupted electrophysiological gene programs and induced arrhythmias [54] cg07185587 (AKAP6) showed a strong protective association with pacemaker implantation, a clinically relevant marker of severe conduction disease. Mechanistically, AKAP6 scaffolds PKA-RyR2 signaling and has been implicated in sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium leak and fibrosis-related remodeling, thereby plausibly contributing to the development and progression of conduction delay and block [55] cg16555537 (TRIP6) was associated with a reduced risk of pacemaker implantation and SSS, in line with prior GWAS supporting TRIP6 in heart-rate/pacemaker regulation [56]. For cg03473532 (MKLN1), cg13654588 (PRLHR), cg02447380 (RAB42), and cg10459018 (unmapped), plausible links to bradyarrhythmia susceptibility remain limited and warrant further mechanistic and tissue-specific functional studies. Collectively, our findings highlight potential intervention targets that connect aging to bradyarrhythmias from an epigenetic perspective and may inform future precision prevention and therapeutic development.

The main strength of our study lies in the integration of clinical and epigenetic evidence, which provides a multidimensional perspective on how biological aging may contribute to bradyarrhythmia. From a clinical perspective, we demonstrated that two readily measurable biological aging indicators, PhenoAge and KDM-BA, were significantly associated with the risk of bradyarrhythmias, supporting their potential utility for population-level screening, risk stratification, and early preventive interventions. From a translational and precision medicine perspective, we identified nine aging-related CpG sites mapped to eight genes associated with bradyarrhythmia-related phenotypes, providing new insights and potential directions for future epigenetic-based therapeutic development. Several limitations should also be noted. First, our analyses were restricted to individuals of European ancestry, and the generalizability of these findings to other populations requires further evaluation. Second, biological age in the UK Biobank cohort was calculated solely using baseline clinical biomarkers. Therefore, we were unable to capture longitudinal changes in biological aging. Third, the mQTL data used in the epigenetic MR analysis were derived primarily from peripheral blood. Although blood-based biological aging indices and DNA methylation markers are valuable because of their accessibility and potential utility for non-invasive risk assessment, and previous studies have suggested that blood-derived DNAm-based aging measures or methylation patterns may exhibit a certain degree of consistency or conservation across tissues, they may not fully reflect epigenetic regulation in cardiac conduction tissues [21, 57]. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of tissue-specific regulatory mechanisms. Future studies are warranted to validate the tissue specificity and functional relevance of the prioritized aging-related DNAm CpG sites and mapped genes in disease-relevant cardiac tissues. Fourth, although a nominal interaction was observed between PhenoAge acceleration and physical activity, this association did not remain significant after FDR correction; therefore, this subgroup finding should be interpreted cautiously and requires validation in future studies. Fifth, as the primary analyses were conducted using a complete-case approach, selection bias arising from non-random missingness of covariates cannot be entirely excluded. Nevertheless, the results from multiple imputation-based sensitivity analyses were materially consistent with those of the complete-case analyses, supporting the robustness of our main findings.

Conclusion

In summary, we found that biological age acceleration was associated with a higher risk of bradyarrhythmias, and aging-related DNA methylation may contribute to this association. These findings provide novel insights to inform prevention and therapeutic strategies.
