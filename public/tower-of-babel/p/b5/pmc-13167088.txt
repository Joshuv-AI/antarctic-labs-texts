Tumor necrosis factor-alpha-mediated inflammatory bone loss: Pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic potential of its inhibitors
Authors: Wen-Ying Lin, Yu-Jen Pan, Kuang-Ting Yeh, Kuei-Ying Su, Kameshwara Kumar Dharini, Ing-Ho Chen, Wen-Tien Wu, Ming-Der Lin, Cheng-Huan Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBone remodeling preserves skeletal integrity through the balanced actions of bone-resorbing osteoclasts and bone-forming osteoblasts, regulated by mechanical, endocrine, and immune signals. Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), a key pro-inflammatory cytokine, disrupts this balance by enhancing osteoclast differentiation and activation in synergy with receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand, leading to net bone loss, microarchitectural deterioration, and increased fracture risk. Epidemiological studies consistently demonstrate that TNF-α-mediated inflammatory diseases are associated with systemic reductions in bone mineral density (BMD) and heightened fracture risk. Patients with rheumatoid arthritis have approximately twice the prevalence of osteoporosis compared to the general population. Similarly, individuals with ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, psoriasis, or inflammatory bowel disease often present with generalized BMD loss affecting both axial and appendicular skeletons. Notably, these skeletal deficits are observed even in patients without overt joint destruction, underscoring the systemic nature of TNF-α-driven bone pathology. Biological TNF-α inhibitors – etanercept, infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab, and certolizumab pegol – were primarily developed to control inflammation but have also demonstrated potential skeletal benefits, such as preservation or modest improvement in BMD and favorable modulation of bone turnover markers. This review synthesizes mechanistic and clinical evidence on TNF-α-mediated bone loss across diverse inflammatory diseases, evaluates the bone-protective potential of TNF-α inhibitors, and highlights shared pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic considerations to guide future research.

INTRODUCTION

Bone tissue provides structural support and is continuously remodeled by osteoblasts and osteoclasts to maintain growth, repair, and integrity. Disruption of this delicate balance – leading to conditions such as osteopetrosis (excessive bone formation) or osteoporosis (excessive bone resorption) – significantly compromises skeletal health. Chronic systemic inflammation is a significant contributor to the perturbation of bone homeostasis, inhibiting osteoblast function, promoting osteoclast-mediated resorption, and accelerating bone loss [Figure 1].

Osteoblasts originate from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), multipotent progenitors capable of differentiating into osteoblasts, chondrocytes, and adipocytes [1]. Their lineage commitment is primarily governed by Wingless-related integration site (WNT) signaling. This process is further modulated by osteoblast- and osteocyte-derived WNT antagonists, such as Sclerostin (SOST) and Dickkopf-1 (DKK-1), whose elevated levels are associated with suppressed bone formation. Committed osteoprogenitors express early markers such as Bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP) and Collagen type I (COL1A). BALP hydrolyzes pyrophosphate to phosphate, enabling hydroxyapatite deposition, while COL1A provides the matrix scaffold; its synthesis releases Procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (PINP), a specific serum biomarker of bone formation. In late differentiation, Osteocalcin (OCN) binds hydroxyapatite, stabilizes mineral deposition, and serves as a robust marker of active bone formation and mineralization.

Osteoclasts are multinucleated cells specialized for bone resorption. They originate from hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) of the monocyte/macrophage lineage, with their differentiation driven by Macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) and Receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) [1]. M-CSF, via its receptor Colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (c-Fms), activates Extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERK) and Serine/threonine kinase (AKT) signaling to promote precursor survival, proliferation, and differentiation. RANKL, produced mainly by osteoblasts and osteocytes, binds Receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB (RANK) to activate Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) and Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) pathways, inducing the expression of Nuclear factor of activated T cells 1 (NFATc1), the key regulator of osteoclastogenesis. Osteoprotegerin (OPG), a decoy receptor secreted by osteoblasts, inhibits RANKL; the RANKL/OPG ratio thus critically determines osteoclast formation. Mature osteoclasts form a ruffled border and sealing zones to create resorption lacunae, where they secrete hydrochloric acid, Tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP), and Cathepsin K (CTSK) to dissolve mineral matrix and degrade type I collagen. Collagen breakdown releases C-terminal telopeptide (CTX-I) and N-terminal telopeptide (NTX) – widely used biochemical markers of bone resorption. Mature collagen cross-links, such as pyridinoline (PYD) and deoxypyridinoline (DPD), are released during collagen degradation and excreted in urine as specific markers of bone resorption.

Chronic inflammation is widely recognized as a multifactorial driver of osteoporosis, in which various cytokines and signaling pathways converge to disrupt bone homeostasis [2]. Among these, Tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) plays a central role, extensively interacting with Interleukin-6 (IL-6), RANK, and WNT signaling. In bone, TNF-α suppresses osteoblast differentiation, reducing osteoprogenitor proliferation and inducing apoptosis in mature osteoblasts. Concurrently, it promotes osteoclastogenesis via NF-κB activation, increased RANKL/OPG ratio, and enhanced survival. In this review, we summarize current clinical evidence linking inflammation to osteoporosis and highlight the therapeutic potential of biological TNF-α inhibitors (TNFi) in preventing inflammation-related bone loss.

TUMOR NECROSIS FACTOR-ALPHA-MEDIATED UNCOUPLING OF BONE FORMATION AND RESORPTION

Under physiological conditions, bone formation and resorption are tightly coupled to maintain skeletal integrity. However, in an inflammatory milieu, TNF-α acts as a central mediator to uncouple these processes. By divergently regulating the differentiation and survival of the osteoblastic and osteoclastic lineages, TNF-α drives a rapid net loss of bone mass. This section delineates the distinct signaling pathways responsible for these opposing effects.

TNF-α acts as a potent inhibitor of osteoblastogenesis. In osteoblast precursors, including MSCs and pre-osteoblasts, TNF-α suppresses their differentiation primarily by activating the ubiquitin–proteasome system, which targets key osteogenic regulators for degradation [3]. Mechanistically, this inhibition is driven by two converging pathways: the degradation of SMAD family member 1/5 (SMAD1/5), which antagonizes the canonical Bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) signaling required for lineage commitment [4], and the direct degradation of RUNX family transcription factor 2 (Runx2), the master transcriptional regulator of osteoblast differentiation [3]. The consequent depletion of Runx2 prevents the expression of downstream targets, such as Osterix (Osx), resulting in a comprehensive arrest of the osteoblastic differentiation program [34]. Furthermore, TNF-α interferes with Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signaling, further constraining the differentiation potential [5]. Interestingly, while chronic, high-dose TNF-α exposure is inhibitory, low doses or transient signaling are crucial for osteogenesis and fracture healing, partially by recruiting MSCs, highlighting a complex dose-dependent role [6]. In addition to these differentiation defects, TNF-α induces autophagy in osteoblasts. While initially cytoprotective, prolonged exposure under chronic inflammatory conditions shifts this balance toward apoptosis, thereby reducing the viable osteoblast pool [7].

TNF-α potentiates osteoclastogenesis primarily by priming osteoclast precursors to RANKL-mediated signaling. Upon binding to TNF receptor superfamily member 1A (TNFR1), TNF-α activates the NF-κB and MAPK signaling cascades [8]. These pathways play a dual role: they directly drive the transcription of functional osteoclast genes, such as TRAP and CTSK, and critically, induce the upregulation of RANK expression. This increased receptor density sensitizes precursor cells to RANKL, enabling differentiation even in the presence of suboptimal RANKL concentrations. Beyond these direct actions, TNF-α exerts indirect effects by modulating osteoblast-lineage cells through distinct signaling mechanisms. While TNF-α stimulates RANKL and M-CSF production via NF-κB and MAPK signaling to favor osteoclastogenesis, it concurrently suppresses the expression of OPG – the soluble decoy receptor for RANKL – by inducing DKK-1 to inhibit the canonical WNT signaling pathway requisite for OPG transcription [9]. By simultaneously upregulating RANKL and downregulating OPG, TNF-α drastically increases the RANKL/OPG ratio in the bone microenvironment. This imbalance effectively removes the natural physiological brake on osteoclastogenesis, thereby accelerating bone resorption. Furthermore, TNF-α prolongs the lifespan of mature osteoclasts by preventing apoptosis, a process mediated at least in part through the activation of the Phosphoinositide 3-kinases (PI3K)/AKT and ERK pathways [10].

In conclusion, TNF-α exerts a dual catabolic effect on bone remodeling: it concurrently inhibits osteoblast differentiation and induces apoptosis, while promoting osteoclast differentiation and prolonging survival. Collectively, these actions underscore TNF-α as a pivotal therapeutic target in the context of osteoporosis and inflammatory bone loss.

TUMOR NECROSIS FACTOR-ALPHA UPREGULATION AND SYSTEMIC BONE LOSS IN CHRONIC INFLAMMATORY DISEASES

In clinical settings, musculoskeletal (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, ankylosing spondylitis, and psoriatic arthritis) and extra-skeletal inflammatory diseases (e.g., psoriasis, inflammatory bowel diseases) are often characterized by elevated levels of TNF-α, which is typically accompanied by a decrease in bone mineral density (BMD) and alterations in bone turnover markers [Table 1]. The subsequent sections will delineate the specific skeletal alterations, bone turnover marker profiles, and TNF-α levels associated with these conditions.

Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune inflammatory disease characterized by symmetric synovitis and progressive joint destruction. Circulating and synovial TNF-α and IL-6 levels are elevated and correlate with disease activity [11]. Beyond articular involvement, RA is associated with a substantially increased risk of systemic bone loss. Compared with healthy controls, patients with RA show lower total hip BMD as well as reduced trabecular volumetric BMD (vBMD) and cortical surface BMD, with decreases of 8.2%–8.4% at the total hip, 9.1%–9.7% in trabecular bone, and 8.0%–8.8% in cortical bone [12]. In line with these deficits, the pooled prevalence of osteoporosis in RA is 27.6%, nearly twice that of the general population [13]. RA is also associated with a higher fracture risk (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR]: 1.52, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.13–2.06), corresponding to a 52% increase relative to controls [14].

This systemic bone loss is accompanied by characteristic alterations in bone turnover biomarkers. On the resorption side, the RANKL/OPG ratio is increased – primarily driven by elevated RANKL with little change in OPG – particularly in women [15]. Notably, even during clinically inactive phases of RA, urinary resorption markers (PYD, DPD, NTX, and CTX-I) remain significantly elevated [16]. On the formation side, serum PINP and BALP are reduced, consistent with suppressed osteoblast activity [1517]. This reduction in bone formation may be mediated, at least in part, by increased WNT pathway inhibitors (DKK-1 and SOST), which restrain osteoblast differentiation and reduce osteoblast numbers.

Ankylosing spondylitis

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease predominantly affecting the axial skeleton. It is characterized by osteoproliferation in paravertebral ligaments and cortical bone regions, leading to progressive spinal ankylosis and restricted mobility. Paradoxically, concurrent trabecular bone loss within vertebral bodies compromises skeletal strength and increases fragility [18]. Consistent with these pathological changes, meta-analytic data indicate that patients with AS have significantly lower BMD than healthy controls at lumbar spine L2–L4 (weighted mean difference [WMD] −0.07 to −0.08, 95% CI −0.11 to −0.04) [19]. Notably, lumbar spine BMD may be overestimated in advanced AS due to syndesmophytes and ligamentous ossification; thus, hip/femoral neck BMD or quantitative computed tomography (QCT)-derived vBMD measures may better reflect true bone loss. Clinically, this translates into a markedly higher risk of vertebral fractures (odds ratio [OR] 5.42, 95% CI 2.50 to 11.7) [20], along with a higher incidence of osteoporosis (incidence rate ratio 2.17, 95% CI 1.83 to 2.57), particularly among women, older adults, and individuals with metabolic comorbidities [21]. Reduced BMD is also evident at extra-spinal sites, including the femur (WMD −0.09, 95% CI −0.12 to −0.07) and hip (WMD −0.06, 95% CI −0.08 to −0.05) [19], consistent with a 40% increased risk of non-vertebral fractures (OR 1.39, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.73) [20].

The immunopathogenesis of AS is strongly linked to the IL-23/IL-17 axis [22], which sustains inflammation and contributes to pathological bone remodeling, whereas TNF-α further amplifies immune activation and disease activity. Serum TNF-α levels are elevated and correlate with disease activity [23], and increased TNF-α expression is also observed in inflammatory infiltrates within the sacroiliac joints [24]. Mechanistically, TNF-α can facilitate bone resorption by enhancing osteoclastogenesis and matrix degradation, accompanied by reduced OPG and a consequent increase in the RANKL/OPG ratio, even in the absence of marked changes in RANKL [25]. Consistent with heightened resorption, urinary NTX levels are increased and correlate with inflammatory activity and impaired physical function in AS [26].

Psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis

Psoriasis is a chronic systemic inflammatory disease characterized by keratinocyte hyperproliferation and immune cell infiltration in the skin, leading to erythematous, scaly plaques. Approximately 30% of patients with psoriasis develop psoriatic arthritis (PsA), which can cause irreversible joint damage if untreated [27]. Beyond cutaneous and articular manifestations, accumulating evidence indicates that psoriatic disease is associated with an increased risk of low bone mass, osteoporosis, and fractures. High-resolution peripheral QCT (HR-pQCT) studies have reported reduced vBMD in patients with psoriatic disease (including PsA) compared with controls (mean difference −14.90, 95% CI −22.90 to −6.89), with more pronounced effects in Asian cohorts and measurements at the distal radius [28]. Consistently, a large national cohort study showed a modest but significant increase in osteoporosis risk among patients with psoriasis (aHR 1.09, 95% CI 1.05–1.13) [29]. Moreover, fracture risk appears to vary with disease severity: vertebral fracture risk was increased in mild psoriasis (aHR: 1.17, 95% CI: 1.03 to 1.33) and was more pronounced in severe psoriasis (aHR: 2.23, 95% CI: 1.54 to 3.22) [30]. In PsA cohorts, osteoporosis prevalence has been reported to range widely (1.4%–68.8%) and fracture prevalence from 12% to 40%, with low BMD observed in most studies, underscoring substantial heterogeneity across study populations and assessment methods [31].

Increased TNF-α expression has been detected in psoriatic skin lesions and serum, as well as in the synovial fluid of patients with PsA [323334]. Alterations in bone turnover markers further support dysregulated bone remodeling. Patients with psoriasis exhibit reduced levels of the bone formation marker PINP [35], whereas PsA is associated with an increased number of circulating osteoclast precursors and elevated resorption- and inflammation-related factors, including serum RANKL, Matrix metalloproteinase-3 (MMP-3), and CTX-I, together with reduced OCN, consistent with enhanced bone resorption and progressive structural damage [3637].

Inflammatory bowel diseases

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), comprising Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic immune-mediated disorder of the gastrointestinal tract that arises from an aberrant immune response to the gut microbiota and typically follows a relapsing–remitting course. Beyond intestinal manifestations, IBD is associated with systemic skeletal complications, including low bone mass, osteoporosis, and increased fracture risk. HR-pQCT studies have demonstrated significantly lower total, cortical, and trabecular vBMD of ultra-distal radius in IBD patients than in controls (−8.3% total vBMD, −6.6% cortical vBMD, and −8.9% trabecular vBMD), accompanied by microarchitectural deterioration (e.g., reduced cortical thickness), with more pronounced deficits in CD than in UC [38]. A 2025 meta-analysis of more than 417,000 patients estimated an osteoporosis prevalence of 12.2% and higher odds versus non-IBD controls (OR: 1.64, 95% CI: 1.24 to 2.16) [39]. Consistently, vertebral fracture risk is increased in both CD and UC, with hazard ratios of 1.59 (95% CI: 1.31–1.92) and 1.26 (95% CI: 1.14–1.41), respectively, versus controls [40].

Bone turnover profiles in IBD further indicate an imbalance favoring resorption. Serum OCN is reduced in both CD and UC [41]. In CD, bone formation markers – including BALP, PINP, and OCN – are decreased, whereas the resorption marker CTX-I is elevated [42]. TNF-α expression is increased in serum, stool, and inflamed colonic mucosa in IBD [434445]. TNF-α levels correlate with clinical and endoscopic disease activity indices, including the Crohn’s Disease Activity Index (CDAI), the Ulcerative Colitis Disease Activity Index (UCDAI), and endoscopic inflammation grade in UC [44]. In pediatric IBD, fecal TNF-α rises during active inflammation and returns toward baseline in remission, supporting its utility as a noninvasive biomarker of disease activity [45].

EFFECTS OF BIOLOGICAL TUMOR NECROSIS FACTOR-ALPHA INHIBITORS ON BONE PRESERVATION IN CHRONIC INFLAMMATORY DISEASES

Inhibiting TNF-α is a standard strategy for controlling inflammation and is increasingly recognized for preserving bone health. While all biological TNFi – including monoclonal antibodies (infliximab, adalimumab, and golimumab), TNFR2 extracellular domain–Fc fusion protein (etanercept), and PEGylated Fab’ fragment (certolizumab pegol) – restore bone homeostasis by neutralizing TNF-α, their structural differences result in distinct target specificity, binding affinity (KD), in vitro neutralization potency (IC50/IC90), half-life, and cellular mechanisms [Table 2].

Beyond neutralizing soluble TNF-α (sTNF-α), full-length IgG1 monoclonal antibodies – infliximab, adalimumab, and golimumab – bind bivalently to transmembrane TNF-α (tmTNF-α). This interaction triggers cell cycle arrest and caspase-dependent apoptosis in osteoclast precursors via two primary mechanisms: Fc-mediated effector functions (antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity [ADCC] and complement-dependent cytotoxicity [CDC]) and Fc-independent reverse signaling [46]. These actions effectively deplete the osteoclast precursor pool, thereby suppressing bone loss and erosion. In contrast to full-length IgG1 monoclonal antibodies, etanercept forms less stable complexes with tmTNF-α and exhibits a weaker capacity to induce apoptosis [47]. In line with this difference in tmTNF-α binding affinity and complex stability, adalimumab has been shown to inhibit osteoclastogenesis more effectively than etanercept under conditions of chronic TNF-α exposure [48]. Finally, certolizumab pegol, distinguished by its lack of an Fc region, does not induce reverse signaling or CDC. Nevertheless, it remains a potent inhibitor of bone erosion, functioning solely through the efficient neutralization of sTNF-α and the subsequent blockade of RANKL induction [49]. Collectively, these mechanistic data more robustly support an anti-osteoclast pathway, whereas direct effects on the osteoblast lineage, especially survival, are less clearly established.

Across indications, biochemical changes in bone metabolism (bone turnover markers) can be detected within days to weeks after TNFi initiation, whereas structural outcomes (e.g., erosion scores and BMD) generally require months to years; the earliest reported timepoints for these endpoints are summarized in Supplementary Tables 1-3. The following sections summarize the clinical evidence for each specific inhibitor, focusing on its capacity for bone preservation.

Etanercept (Enbrel®)

Etanercept (Enbrel®) is a recombinant fusion protein comprising two extracellular domains of human TNFR2 linked to the Fc portion of human IgG1 [50]. By selectively binding sTNF-α and, to a lesser extent, tmTNF-α, it prevents receptor engagement and downstream inflammatory signaling without inducing complement- or antibody-dependent cytotoxicity. Approved for RA, AS, psoriasis, and PsA, etanercept was originally developed to control inflammation but has also shown potential benefits for bone metabolism. Its effects on bone remodeling appear context-dependent, varying with disease type, treatment duration, and concomitant methotrexate (MTX, a disease-modifying antirheumatic drug) use.

In RA, etanercept – particularly when combined with MTX – has been shown to decrease bone resorption markers (CTX-I, PYD, and DPD) and increase formation markers (PINP and BALP), supporting a shift toward balanced bone turnover [1551].

In AS, 12-week etanercept therapy was associated with increased BALP and OCN levels, together with reductions in M-CSF and MMP-3, suggesting early modulation of bone turnover and inflammatory osteoclastogenic signaling [52]. In another study, one-year combination therapy with MTX increased serum PINP but did not significantly change lumbar spine (L2–L4) or femoral neck BMD, indicating a potential stabilizing rather than clearly anabolic skeletal effect over this timeframe [53]. Over 2 years, modest BMD gains were reported at the lumbar spine (L2–L4) and left hip (total proximal femur), whereas CTX-I did not change significantly [54].

In PsA, 2-year treatment significantly slowed radiographic progression by reducing bone erosion, as evidenced by improvements in the modified Sharp erosion score [55].

Overall, current evidence supports a bone-protective effect of etanercept in RA, whereas findings in AS and PsA are less consistent and primarily limited to surrogate outcomes such as bone turnover markers or radiographic progression.

Infliximab (Remicade®)

Infliximab (Remicade®) is a chimeric IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting TNF-α, composed of approximately 75% human and 25% murine sequences [50]. It binds to both sTNF-α and tmTNF-α with high affinity and, through its Fc region, can trigger CDC and ADCC, leading to apoptosis or elimination of TNF-α–expressing cells [56]. It was first approved by the FDA in 1998 for CD and later for RA, AS, psoriasis, PsA, and UC. While originally developed for inflammation control, it has also been associated with favorable changes in bone metabolism across multiple inflammatory diseases, with effects varying by disease type and treatment duration.

In RA, infliximab generally preserves axial BMD over one to two years and reduces bone resorption markers. On the biochemical level, infliximab markedly suppresses bone resorption, with serum CTX-I reduced as early as 6 weeks [575859]. Combined therapy with MTX further lowers urinary NTX and DPD within 6 months [60]. In terms of bone formation, serum OCN levels increase by week 14 and remain elevated for up to 24 months [5759], whereas BALP and PINP show inconsistent changes during follow-up [5860].

In AS, infliximab appears to exert more pronounced anabolic effects on the axial skeleton. Lumbar spine (L1–L4) and hip BMD increase within 6–24 months [5961], accompanied by early rises in BALP within 2 weeks and sustained OCN elevation through 24 months [5961]. These changes suggest rapid activation and long-term maintenance of osteoblast activity and bone matrix mineralization. Interestingly, PINP – a marker of type I collagen synthesis—decreased by week 14 [62], possibly reflecting a shift from active collagen deposition toward mineralization as inflammation subsides. CTX-I decline was generally delayed, becoming significant only after 24 months [5961], suggesting that suppression of bone resorption occurs more gradually, likely due to the involvement of other pro-resorptive pathways beyond TNF-α.

In PsA, data are limited but suggest protection against peripheral bone loss. In the Induction and Maintenance Psoriatic Arthritis Clinical Trial 2 (IMPACT 2) trial, infliximab reduced hand bone loss and slowed radiographic progression, particularly bone erosion [6364].

In CD, infliximab generally improves bone metabolism, though the effects appear to vary between adults and children. In adults, one-year therapy increased BMD by 2.4% at the lumbar spine (L2–L4), 2.8% at the femoral trochanter, and 2.6% at the femoral neck [65]. Bone formation markers rose consistently – OCN and PINP by weeks 6 and 30 [41], BALP by week 4 [66] – and in fistulizing or refractory disease, BALP, PINP, and OCN increased simultaneously by week 8 post-treat [42]. Bone resorption also decreased, with CTX-I reduced within 14–42 days [67], though some studies reported unchanged CTX-I and NTX [4166].

In pediatric CD, OCN increased by week 6 and remained elevated through week 30 [68], with BALP and PINP rising after 10 weeks [69]. However, changes in bone resorption markers were modest, showing only slight increases in urinary DPD and CTX-I [69]. Notably, not all cohorts improved – some showed no lumbar spine (L2–L4) BMD, BALP, OCN, or CTX-I gains after one year [6870].

Collectively, infliximab exerts consistent bone-protective effects in RA, AS, PsA, and adult CD, primarily by suppressing bone resorption and enhancing osteoblast activity, with AS showing the most pronounced anabolic response. However, responses in pediatric CD are more variable, underscoring the influence of age, disease context, and treatment timing.

Adalimumab (Humira®)

Adalimumab (Humira®) is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting TNF-α [50]. Like infliximab, it binds both sTNF-α and tmTNF-α with high affinity and, via its Fc region, can lead to apoptosis or clearance of TNF-α–expressing cells. It was first approved by the FDA in 2002 for RA and has since gained indications for AS, psoriasis, PsA, CD, and UC. Emerging evidence suggests that adalimumab may attenuate inflammatory bone loss by promoting bone formation.

In RA, 1-year combination therapy with adalimumab and MTX effectively prevented further bone loss of the lumbar spine (L1–L4) or femoral neck, indicating stabilization of systemic BMD [71]. Another study in RA women treated for 15 months showed a significant decrease in serum CTX-I and a concurrent increase in PINP, suggesting a net anabolic shift in bone remodeling [15].

In psoriasis without arthritis, six months of adalimumab failed to significantly alter trabecular bone score (TBS), suggesting that in the absence of joint inflammation, short-term therapy may have limited effects on bone microarchitecture [72].

In CD, adalimumab increased serum OCN and PINP at one and three months, whereas CTX-I remained unchanged, indicating an enhancement in bone formation without a parallel reduction in resorption in short-term treatment [73].

Collectively, available data support a potential bone-protective effect of adalimumab – most consistently observed in RA and CD – primarily through stimulation of bone formation. However, evidence in AS, psoriasis, PsA, and UC remains sparse, underscoring the need for further long-term, disease-specific studies to clarify its skeletal benefits.

Certolizumab pegol (Cimzia®)

Certolizumab pegol (Cimzia®) is a polyethylene glycol–conjugated antigen-binding fragment (PEGylated Fab) of a humanized monoclonal antibody that lacks an Fc region [50]. The absence of an Fc domain eliminates Fc-mediated immune effector functions such as CDC and ADCC, thereby reducing the risk of certain immune-related adverse events. In addition, PEGylation increases molecular stability, prolongs serum half-life, and enhances tissue penetration. Initially approved for CD in 2008, certolizumab pegol has since gained indications for RA, AS, psoriasis, and PsA.

In RA patients, combination therapy with certolizumab pegol and MTX led to a rapid and sustained modulation of bone turnover. Within one week of treatment, serum CTX-I levels significantly decreased, while PINP levels increased, and both markers remained stable for the following two months [74]. This early and persistent shift toward reduced bone resorption and enhanced bone formation suggests that certolizumab pegol may not only prevent inflammatory bone loss but also promote bone repair in chronic inflammatory conditions.

Golimumab (Simponi®)

Golimumab (Simponi®) is a fully human IgG1 monoclonal antibody that exhibits higher binding affinity and neutralizing capacity than infliximab and adalimumab [50]. It was approved by the FDA in 2009 for RA, AS, and PsA, with the indication for UC added in 2013. It demonstrates greater in vitro binding affinity and is administered less frequently – typically once monthly by subcutaneous injection or via intravenous infusion – while adalimumab is usually given every two weeks. Despite the bone-related effects of golimumab in AS, PsA, and UC remaining underexplored, evidence from clinical imaging and biomarker studies suggests that golimumab may exert bone-protective effects in RA.

In a randomized controlled trial, golimumab in combination with MTX significantly reduced bone erosion scores at weeks 12 and 24 compared with placebo plus MTX, as assessed by the Rheumatoid Arthritis Magnetic Resonance Imaging Scoring system [75]. Consistent with these imaging results, a 12-month biomarker study reported a 53.12% increase in serum PINP levels and a 4.97% increase in CTX-I in RA patients receiving golimumab plus MTX [76]. This disproportionate change indicates a net anabolic shift in bone remodeling. Nevertheless, the small sample size (N = 9) limits the generalizability of these findings, underscoring the need for confirmation in larger patient cohorts.

MOLECULAR MECHANISMS OF TUMOR NECROSIS FACTOR-ALPHA INHIBITORS IN MODULATING BONE REMODELING IN CHRONIC INFLAMMATORY DISEASES

Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors suppress osteoclastogenesis via modulating receptor activator of Nuclear factor-κB ligand/Osteoprotegerin ratio

Upon binding to TNFR1 or TNFR2, TNF-α recruits TNF receptor-associated factors (TRAFs), activating the NF-κB pathway, which induces NFATc1 – a key regulator of osteoclast differentiation – along with pro-inflammatory cytokines and bone-resorptive genes. Although TNF-α alone has weaker osteoclastogenic potency than RANKL, it acts synergistically with RANKL and contributes to osteoclastogenesis [8].

Among chronic inflammatory diseases, RA has the most extensive clinical studies demonstrating that TNFi effectively suppresses TNF-α signaling. For example, infliximab therapy reduced serum TNF-α, TNFR1, and TNFR2 levels within 2 weeks, with sustained effects for up to 1 year [77]. Regarding the RANKL/OPG axis, findings on its modulation by TNFi are more variable. In a 15-month study, patients receiving adalimumab or etanercept in combination with MTX showed increased OPG and a reduced RANKL/OPG ratio [15]. By contrast, shorter treatments with adalimumab (12 weeks) or infliximab (14 weeks) generally showed no significant effect [7879]. Nevertheless, other studies reported that infliximab (14 weeks) or etanercept (6 months) reduced RANKL and the RANKL/OPG ratio, without concomitant changes in OPG levels [5157].

Beyond RA, clinical studies investigating the modulatory effects of the RANKL/OPG ratio by TNFi in other chronic inflammatory diseases are limited. In AS, etanercept therapy produced no effect on the RANKL/OPG ratio across studies [525354], but a decline in serum M-CSF levels after 12 weeks suggested suppression of osteoclast differentiation [52]. In PsA, etanercept reduced circulating osteoclast precursors within two weeks; however, their levels remained higher than those of healthy controls after six months, indicating a partial inhibition of osteoclastogenesis [80]. In CD, infliximab treatment decreased OPG without altering RANKL levels [81]. Taken together, these clinical studies highlight disease-specific differences in TNFi-mediated modulation of bone remodeling, indicating that the magnitude and consistency of this effect are influenced by the specific agent used, treatment duration, and disease context.

Beyond direct Tumor necrosis factor-alpha blockade: Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors modulate osteoblastogenesis through the Wingless-related integration site signaling pathway

A proposed mechanism by which TNFi supports bone formation is modulation of the WNT signaling pathway. Several clinical studies report that TNFi therapy reduces circulating WNT antagonists – most notably DKK-1 and SOST – thereby relieving inhibition of WNT-dependent osteoblast differentiation and function. In RA, certolizumab pegol combined with MTX induced a rapid decline in serum DKK-1 and SOST within 1 week, and this suppression was sustained for at least 2 months [74]. In AS, adalimumab treatment for 12 weeks markedly decreased serum DKK-1 levels [82]. In contrast, in a one-year study of etanercept combined with MTX, DKK-1 was unchanged, and SOST increased [53], suggesting that WNT-pathway responses may differ across TNFi agents and/or disease contexts. Overall, available data support WNT disinhibition – particularly via reduced DKK-1 – as a plausible contributor to TNFi-associated osteoblast activity; however, the evidence remains limited and heterogeneous, especially in PsA and IBD, where direct data on WNT modulation by TNFi are still sparse.

Modulation of inflammatory cytokine networks by Tumor necrosis factor inhibitors in bone homeostasis

In addition to its direct effects, bone loss mediated by TNF-α signaling can also result from the orchestration of pro-inflammatory cytokine networks that amplify bone resorption. TNF-α signaling upregulates several cytokines – including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17, and IL-23 – all of which promote osteoclast differentiation through synergistic and reinforcing mechanisms [83]. For example, TNF-α and IL-6 synergistically induce NFATc1, IL-1β, and MMP-3 expression, and IL-1β further enhances TNF-α/IL-6–driven osteoclastogenesis [84]. In osteoblasts, these cytokines collectively stimulate RANKL expression, contributing to excessive osteoclast formation [8586], while IL-23 increases RANK expression in osteoclast precursors [87]. Moreover, TNF-α and IL-6 suppress WNT signaling in synovial fibroblasts, thereby impairing osteoblast function [88]. Together, these effects destabilize bone homeostasis and shift the balance toward bone resorption.

Because TNF-α drives cytokine networks that enhance bone resorption, TNFi counteract pathological bone loss by blocking TNF-α and its downstream effects. In patients with RA, combination therapy with infliximab or etanercept plus MTX significantly reduced serum IL-6 levels, though IL-1β levels remained largely unchanged [8990]. Infliximab also led to early and sustained reductions in IL-6, IL-17, and IL-23, with changes evident as early as two weeks and persisting for up to one year [77]. Similar IL-6 reductions were observed in patients with PsA and psoriasis after 14–16 weeks of TNFi treatment [91], and in patients with CD and UC following six weeks of infliximab therapy [41]. These findings indicate that the bone-protective actions of TNFi are not limited to direct TNF-α suppression but also encompass broader regulation of cytokine networks that collectively drive pathological bone loss.

COMPARATIVE SAFETY AMONG TUMOR NECROSIS FACTOR INHIBITORS

TNFi are highly effective and generally well tolerated. Most adverse events are mild – such as injection site reactions, headache, rash, nausea, and upper respiratory infections – and rarely require treatment discontinuation. However, serious but rare complications include reactivation of latent infections (especially TB and HBV), opportunistic infections, and hepatotoxicity – particularly with infliximab (Remicade® prescribing information). The risk of malignancy remains uncertain: while meta-analyses report no overall increase [92], some observational studies suggest drug-specific associations with lymphoma or leukemia [93]. Other rare events reported in TNFi prescribing information include hematologic disorders, demyelinating disease, worsening heart failure, thrombotic events, and paradoxical psoriasis.

Among the five approved TNFi, etanercept is associated with the most favorable safety profile, exhibiting low rates of infection and treatment discontinuation [94]. In contrast, infliximab carries higher risks of infection, infusion reactions, hepatotoxicity, and malignancy, but remains useful for rapid symptom control [949596]. Adalimumab strikes a balance between efficacy and safety, though it is linked with slightly higher infection rates than etanercept [94]. Golimumab and certolizumab pegol are generally well tolerated – golimumab as monotherapy, and certolizumab, particularly during pregnancy [9497]. However, some data suggest that certolizumab pegol may carry higher infection and discontinuation rates compared to adalimumab [94].

Anti-TNF agents are well established in the management of inflammatory autoimmune diseases, where the benefits clearly outweigh the risks, and improved bone health may be considered an additional advantage. In contrast, their potential use in primary osteoporosis warrants greater caution. Evidence from the anti-TNF Therapy Against Congestive Heart Failure (ATTACH) trial [98] suggests a dose-dependent safety profile, with low-dose infliximab showing more favorable outcomes than high-dose treatment. Thus, if TNFi are to be repurposed for osteoporosis, lower dosing strategies and the choice of agents with more favorable safety profiles should be considered. In addition, baseline inflammatory markers such as TNF-α or C-reactive protein (CRP) may influence treatment response and deserve further investigation.

CONCLUSIONS AND PERSPECTIVES

TNF-α serves as a central mediator of bone loss in both musculoskeletal and extraskeletal inflammatory diseases, disrupting the remodeling balance by increasing osteoclast activity while impairing osteoblast function. Biological TNFi not only suppresses inflammation but also critically modulates bone metabolism. These agents function predominantly via antiresorptive mechanisms, although their anabolic effects remain variable across different agents and disease contexts [Supplementary Tables 1-3].

Notably, while TNFi treatment consistently improves BMD, current clinical evidence does not support a definitive reduction in fracture risk [5499]. This discordance between BMD improvement and the lack of immediate fracture reduction reflects a time-dependent dissociation. While anti-inflammatory therapies effectively arrest bone loss and increase mineralization, the restoration of biomechanical competence – which depends on the repair of trabecular microarchitecture (e.g., thickness and connectivity) – requires a longer duration of remodeling. Consequently, the fracture-preventive benefits of TNFi may be underestimated in existing clinical trials due to limited follow-up periods. Future studies must be designed with extended observation windows (exceeding 2 years) and prioritize fracture incidence as a primary endpoint to fully capture the long-term structural benefits of inflammation suppression. Furthermore, advanced metrics, such as TBS or HR-pQCT, should be utilized to better assess the recovery of biomechanical competence.

Clinicians and researchers must recognize that achieving the full osteoprotective potential of TNFi requires sustained remission and sufficient time for bone remodeling. To bridge this gap, future therapeutic strategies should explore the potential of combining TNFi with osteoanabolic agents. The rationale lies in their complementary mechanisms, where TNFi suppresses TNF-α and the downstream inflammatory cytokines that induce anabolic resistance, thereby creating a permissive environment for osteoanabolic agents. This concept is supported by a case-control study [100], finding that RA patients showed reduced anabolic response to romosozumab compared to non-RA patients. This finding suggests that inflammatory disease-related factors may partially limit bone anabolic efficacy. Therefore, by directly stimulating osteoblast activity to accelerate microarchitectural repair, such adjuvant therapies could overcome the temporal lag in structural recovery, offering more robust protection against vertebral fractures in high-risk patients. Nevertheless, the field awaits definitive trials to confirm whether combination therapy translates into a significant reduction in fracture rates among patients with chronic inflammatory diseases. To optimize such strategies, integrating precision medicine – specifically dual-biomarker panels tracking both bone turnover and inflammation – will be essential for guiding clinical decision-making. In parallel, novel therapeutic avenues – including selective TNF receptor modulation, bone-targeted delivery systems, and emerging biologics such as IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors – warrant further exploration to optimize long-term skeletal health.
