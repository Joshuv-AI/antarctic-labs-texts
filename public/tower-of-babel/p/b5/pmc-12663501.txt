Quality attributes, metabolomics and microbial community dynamics of naturally fermented Xiaohuang ginger (Zingiber officinale cv. Xiaohuang): A ready-to-eat flavoring food
Authors: Jiajia Wang, Hongru He, Lianbing Lin, Yicen Lin, Feng Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGinger, a popular dietary condiment, is rarely consumed directly due to its intense pungency. Pickled Xiaohuang ginger, a ready-to-eat flavoring food, has become prevalent in Southwest China, yet its quality traits, metabolite profiles, and microbial dynamics remain largely unexplored. In this study, we extensively examined the physicochemical properties, flavor characteristics, metabolome, and microbial dynamics (encompassing both bacterial and fungal communities) during a 42-day natural fermentation of Xiaohuang ginger. Our findings revealed significant increases in organic acids, flavonoids, polysaccharides, gingerol, curcumin, and lactic acid bacteria populations during fermentation (all P-values <0.05). At the species level, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Pichia fermentans, and Kluyveromyces hubeiensis were found to be dominant in pickled Xiaohuang ginger. Notably, integrated network analysis identified nine keystone microbial species (four bacteria and five fungi) that orchestrate critical physicochemical transitions, metabolite accumulation, and flavor development. Collectively, this study offers valuable insights for the development of ginger-based ready-to-eat flavoring foods.Keywords: Ginger, Fermentation, Flavor, Metabolome, Microbial dynamics

Introduction

Ginger possesses a distinctive aroma and pungent taste. It is rich in nutrients, including dry matter, lipids, soluble sugars, crude protein, cellulose, starch, and over 100 other components (Zeng et al., 2023). In addition, it is widely used as a seasoning agent and culinary ingredient and provides numerous health benefits, such as warming the body against cold conditions, fortifying spleen function, and soothing cough symptoms (Adebayo et al., 2021). These unique functions of ginger make it a prime example of foods with dual uses, namely medicinal and edible purposes. Also, it falls within the category of foods that share a common origin with medicine, providing both nutritional and health benefits (Ademosun et al., 2021).

Chinese Xiaohuang ginger, a high-quality variety, has gained popularity among consumers owing to its notable oil content, sufficient spiciness, delicate texture, bright yellow color, strong aroma, and unique flavor (Zeng et al., 2023). Nonetheless, the inherently strong and pungent taste of ginger renders it unsuitable for direct ingestion. Fermented Chinese Xiaohuang ginger, a ready-to-eat delicacy, has gained immense popularity in Southwest China owing to its distinctive flavor. However, to the best of our knowledge, the quality attributes, metabolite profiles, and microbial community dynamics of fermented Xiaohuang ginger have not yet been thoroughly investigated.

Microorganisms play a crucial role in developing flavor compounds during kimchi fermentation (Wu et al., 2015). Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and yeast are the most representative probiotics due to their beneficial properties and fermentation activity. They serve as excellent leavening agents to enhance food flavor and nutritional quality (Cai et al., 2022). Typically, LAB produce flavor compounds primarily through lipid breakdown or protein hydrolysis. For example, Lactobacillus species (such as Lactobacillus acetolerans and Lactobacillus sakei) form the core flavor microbial community in many Chinese fermented vegetables, including Jiangxi yancai, Sichuan paocai, and Dongbei suancai. These bacteria can produce volatile esters (including benzoic acid ethyl ester, isopentyl formate, and ethyl octanoate) and terpenes (including linalool, γ-terpinene, and anethole), contributing to the pleasant aroma and flavor of kimchi (Xiao et al., 2020). The Lactiplantibacillus plantarum 423 promotes the production of aldehydes and phenols in rice and wheat bran (Wang et al., 2020). In traditionally fermented radish, the overwhelmingly dominant genus Lactobacillus was strongly linked to 14 flavor compounds, encompassing four volatiles, nine free amino acids and one organic acid (Rao et al., 2020). These findings align with the broader context of microbial contributions to flavor development in traditional vegetable fermentations.

Studies have shown that yeast can also significantly contribute to the overall aroma characteristics of wine by producing higher alcohols, esters, and other flavor-active compounds (Xu et al., 2021). The principal flavor components in kimchi are alcohols, esters, ketones, hydrocarbons, FAAs, sugars, and organic acids (Shang et al., 2022). Recent studies have increasingly focused on elucidating the intricate link between microbial diversity and the flavor profile of pickled vegetables, as exemplified by the studies on the fermentation of radish (Rao et al., 2020) and red pepper (Xu et al., 2021). Given the paramount importance of harnessing microbial resources for industrial applications (Xu et al., 2021), gaining a deeper understanding of the key microorganisms involved in the ginger fermentation process and their interplay with flavor-generating metabolites is crucial.

In this study, we explored the intricacies of pickled Xiaohuang ginger, a popular ready-to-eat flavored food in Southwest China. Using ginger samples from Luoping County, Yunnan Province, we conducted a 42-day natural fermentation process. Through integrated metabolomic profiling and microbial succession analyses, our objectives were threefold: to investigate the physicochemical and flavor properties of pickled Xiaohuang ginger, to examine microbial community dynamics and associated metabolite changes during fermentation, and to evaluate the relationship between key functional microbiota and quality factors.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

The standard products (oxalic acid, malic acid, shikimic acid, lactic acid, acetic acid, maleic acid, citric acid, succinic acid, fumaric acid, propionic acid, curcumin, gingerol, and L-2-chlorophenylalanine) used for quantification were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)-grade methanol, glacial acetic acid, and acetonitrile were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). All other reagents were analytical grade and purchased from DaMao (Tianjin, China).

Preparation of ginger samples

Xiaohuang ginger samples were freshly collected from Luoping County, Qujing City, Yunnan Province, China, located at 24.88°N and 103.53°E. These samples were used as the raw material for our study. The fermentation process in this study aligns with commercial-level production methods. Briefly, the ginger was uniformly sliced into thin pieces with a thickness of 0.3 ± 0.1 cm and then naturally air-dried at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) in a well-ventilated environment until the surface moisture had evaporated. Then, the samples were immersed in a sterilized kimchi jar and fermented in darkness at 25 ± 3 °C, utilizing a homemade brine containing 40 g/L of table salt. For sampling purposes, we systematically collected specimens from three vertical positions, ranging from the top to the bottom of the kimchi jar and from four horizontal positions, extending from the side to the center. Then, these samples were thoroughly mixed. At specific intervals during the fermentation process—on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42—we obtained at least three biological replicate samples of ginger. All collected samples were stored at −80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Enumeration of LAB

Throughout the fermentation process, on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42, samples of the fermentation broth were systematically collected. For the enumeration of LAB, a 1 mL aliquot of the fermentation broth was used. This broth was serially diluted, and triplicate portions of the diluted broth were inoculated onto 3 M™ Petrifilm LAB Count Plates, procured from 3 M™ Microbiology, Saint Paul, MN, USA. These inoculated plates were incubated in a thermostatic incubator at a temperature of 36 ± 1 °C for 48 h. Afterwards, the microbial counts were recorded and expressed as colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL), providing a quantitative measure of the LAB population throughout the fermentation process.

pH, titratable acidity (TA), and nitrite

The pH of the fermentation broth was measured using a calibrated pH meter (FE28, Mettler Toledo, Shanghai, China). After the sample was thoroughly homogenized, 5 g of the resultant homogenate was extracted and subsequently diluted to a final volume of 100 mL with deionized distilled water, ensuring uniform dispersion and dilution of the sample constituents. Then, the resultant solution was carefully filtered, and a standardized 0.1 mol/L sodium hydroxide solution was used for titration of the filtrate, proceeding until the pH of the solution reached the predetermined endpoint of 8.1. The volume of sodium hydroxide solution consumed during the titration process was accurately recorded, enabling the calculation of the titratable acidity. This parameter, indicative of the acidic constituents present in the sample, was expressed as a percentage (w/w) of lactic acid content. A commercially available Food Nitrite Content Assay Kit (Boxbio, Beijing, China) was used to determine the nitrite content in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. All measurements were conducted in triplicate to ensure accuracy.

Hardness

The hardness of the samples was accurately determined using a digital fruit hardness tester (AIPLI GY-4, Quzhou, China). Prior to the hardness analysis, the ginger pieces were precisely cut into uniform cubic shapes, each measuring 2 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm. A probe with a diameter of Φ 3.5 mm was employed for the testing.

Colorimetric parameters

The surface color changes in Xiaohuang ginger were effectively measured using a Hunterlab Agera colorimeter (HunterLab, Reston, VA, USA). This device enabled the determination of three fundamental color coordinates: L* (representing whiteness and brightness), a* (indicating a transition from green to red), and b* (denoting a shift from blue to yellow). To ensure accuracy, all measurements were replicated three times, and the resulting average values were calculated.

Total flavonoids, sugars, and organic acids

The contents of total flavonoid, total sugar, glucose, and polysaccharide in the fermented Xiaohuang ginger were accurately measured using assay kits specifically designed for these analytes, sourced from Grace Biotechnology in Suzhou, China. For reliability, all measurements were replicated three times, and the results were accurately calculated based on the respective standard curves for total flavonoid, total sugar, glucose, and polysaccharide contents. The results are presented in a standardized format, expressed as mg/g (w/w), to facilitate comparison and interpretation.

Organic acids were extracted as follows: First, 0.05 g of the sample was accurately weighed and transferred to a suitable container. Then, 500 μL of a 0.2 mol/L aqueous solution of metaphosphoric acid was added. The mixture was vigorously vortexed until it became homogenized. Afterward, it was sonicated for 30 min at 4 °C to ensure complete extraction. Then, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane. Finally, the filtered sample was analyzed using a Shimadzu liquid chromatography system (LC-20 CE, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) equipped with a UV detector (SPD-M40, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan). The chromatographic separation and detection of organic acid content were triplicated to ensure accuracy. The results were expressed in terms of μg/g (w/w), following the methods outlined by Shang et al. (Shang et al., 2022).

Soluble protein and FAAs

The soluble protein content in the fermentation broth was accurately determined using the BCA protein assay kit sourced from Grace Biotechnology in Suzhou, China. All measurements were triplicated to ensure reproducibility. The samples were prepared for FAA measurement as follows: briefly, 1.0 g of sample powder was subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic extraction using 2 mL of 0.1 mol/L HCl for 30 min. Then, the mixture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was retained. This extraction step was repeated, and all supernatants were combined. After the supernatants were freeze-dried, the extracts were dissolved in water to make a final volume of 1 mL. Subsequently, 500 μL of 0.1 mol/L phenyl isothiocyanate (PITC) acetonitrile solution and 500 μL of 1 mol/L triethylamine acetonitrile solution were added to the mixture. The mixture was vortexed for 1 min and incubated at 25 °C for 1 h. To further purify the sample, 250 μL of 20 % acetic acid and 2 mL of hexane were added, vortexed for 1–2 min, and allowed to stand for 5 min. After the sample was centrifuged, the lower layer was extracted and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic phase filter. Finally, the amino acid contents were determined through chromatographic analysis using a Shimadzu liquid chromatography system (LC-20 CE, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) equipped with a UV detector (SPD-M40, Shimadzu Corporation, Japan).

Gingerol

The samples were initially dried at 70 °C for 72 h. Subsequently, 0.25 g of ground sample was ultrasonically extracted with 20 mL of 75 % methanol at 50 °C and 450 W output amplitude for 40 min. After sample extraction, the mixture was thoroughly filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane to ensure purity. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: Supersil AQ-C 18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 5 μm; Elite, Dalian, China); flow rate: 1.0 mL/min; injection volume: 10 μL; column temperature: 35 °C; detection wavelength: 280 nm; run time: 12 min. The mobile phase consisted of a mixture of acetonitrile and water, with a volume ratio of 47:53. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate under these conditions.

Curcumin

The sample was dried at 70 °C for 72 h. Accurately weighed 1.0 g of ground sample, which was then ultrasonically extracted using 6 mL of 90 % ethanol at 50 °C and 450 W output amplitude for 30 min. The resultant suspension was filtered using a 0.45 μm membrane prior to injection for chromatographic analysis. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: a Supersil AQ-C18 column was used at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min, an injection volume of 10 μL, a column temperature of 35 °C, and a detection wavelength of 425 nm. The run time was set to 6 min, using a mobile phase comprising acetonitrile and 0.5 % glacial acetic acid in a 65:35 ratio. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate under these specified conditions.

Metabolomics analysis

The sample (50 mg) was weighed into a low-temperature tissue grinder (Wonbio-96c, Wonbio, Taizhou, China), and 400 μL of an extraction solution (methanol: water, 4:1, v/v) was added. The mixture was homogenized for 6 min and subjected to low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min. The supernatant was then collected and ready for LC-MS/MS analysis, which was conducted using the Thermo Fisher Scientific ultra-high performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC)-Q Exactive HF-X platform, integrating with Fourier transform mass spectrometry. The chromatographic parameters were as follows: the ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm; i.d., 1.8 μm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) was used, with an injection volume of 3 μL and a column temperature of 40 °C. The mobile phase A comprised 95 % water +5 % acetonitrile (containing 0.1 % formic acid), and mobile phase B comprised 47.5 % acetonitrile +47.5 % isopropanol +5 % water (also containing 0.1 % formic acid). Mass spectrometry settings included an S-Lens voltage of 50, a scanning range of m/z 70–1050, a heated capillary temperature of 425 °C, and a capillary temperature of 325 °C. Sample ionization was achieved using electrospray, and mass spectral data were collected in both positive and negative ion scanning modes.

The raw data were processed using Progenesis QI software (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) to generate a data matrix encompassing retention times, mass-to-charge ratios, and peak intensities. Metabolite annotation ensued by querying the Human Metabolome Database (HMDB, http://www.hmdb.ca/), Metlin (https://metlin.scripps.edu/), and homemade Majorbio databases. The annotated matrix data were subsequently uploaded to the Majorbio Cloud Platform for comprehensive bioinformatic analysis. Metabolites demonstrating variable importance in projection (VIP) scores exceeding 1, adjusted P-values below 0.05, and absolute fold-change values greater than 1 were classified as differentially accumulated metabolites. To further explore the intricate relationships between these metabolites and the microbial communities, Cytoscape software (version 3.9.1) was used to visualize their network.

High-throughput sequencing

Total genomic DNA from the brine was extracted using a bacterial/fungal DNA extraction kit (Genstone Biotech, Beijing, China). For bacteria, the full-length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primer pair 27F (5’-AGRGTTYGATYMTGGCTCAG-3′) and 1492R (5’-RGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′); for fungi, the ITS rRNA gene was amplified using primers ITS1F (5’-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS2R (5’-GCTGCGTTCTTCATCGATGC-3′). PCR products were checked using a 2 % agarose gel and further purified using a PCR Clean-Up Kit (Yuhua, China). The purified products were quantified using Qubit 4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and DNA samples were sequenced on the PacBio Sequel IIe System (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA) by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), utilizing the third-generation high-throughput sequencing platform. Raw sequencing reads were quality-controlled using the FASTP software (version 0.20.0), and paired-end reads were merged using FLASH software (version 1.2.7). Operational taxonomic unit (OTU) clustering was conducted at a similarity level of 97 % using the UPARSE software (version 7.1). Taxonomic annotation of OTUs was performed based on Silva database comparison using the RDP classifier (version 2.11).

Statistical analysis

The data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The physicochemical properties of Xiaohuang ginger during the fermentation process were thoroughly analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2). The obtained results were statistically evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance (for comparisons of three or more groups) or Student's t-test (for comparisons between two groups). Statistical significance was determined at a significance level of α = 0.05 (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Changes in physicochemical properties during fermentation

pH, TA value, and nitrite content

In this study, Chinese Xiaohuang ginger underwent a natural fermentation process lasting 42 days, with samples collected on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42 for measurement (Fig. 1A). Generally, pH and TA values are important factors that influence the growth of microorganisms and the accumulation of metabolites during the fermentation process of kimchi (Wu et al., 2015). As shown in Fig. 1B, the starting pH of fermented Xiaohuang ginger was 4.33, which significantly decreased to 3.20 after 21 days of fermentation (P < 0.001). The TA value correspondingly increased during the fermentation process (the TA value was 0.49 % at day 0, which increased to 5.35 % at day 21) and eventually stabilized (the TA value was 5.00 % at day 42; Fig. 1C). Similar trends were also observed during the fermentation process of Chinese sauerkraut (Xiong et al., 2012). As LAB grew, high concentrations of organic acids were produced, thus decreasing the pH and increasing TA (Wu et al., 2015). The LAB count in this study is shown in Fig. 1D. The initial count was 1.48 log CFU/mL, which increased to 7.49 log CFU/mL on day 14 of fermentation and stabilized at 6.81 log CFU/mL after 35 days of fermentation. Generally, when the pH is below 3.80 and the TA content is higher than 0.50 %, the growth of most spoilage bacteria in food is inhibited, and the pickled kimchi can be safely consumed (Chen et al., 2019). Therefore, the Xiaohuang ginger in this study, after 42 days of fermentation, was considered mature.

The accumulation of nitrite can pose a risk to food safety and human health (Ding et al., 2018). In this study, the nitrite content of fresh Xiaohuang ginger was approximately 4.49 mg/kg, which increased to 16.65 mg/kg after 7 days of fermentation (Table 1). This phenomenon may be attributable to the reduction of nitrate and production of nitrite on the surface of fresh Xiaohuang ginger by non-acid-producing microorganisms (Wu et al., 2015). In the later stages of fermentation, the nitrite content gradually decreased and reached its lowest value (3.80 mg/kg) after 42 days of fermentation. We proposed that the initial high peak in nitrite content was closely related to the reduction of nitrate by microbial nitrate reductase. As the fermentation progressed, the environment became anaerobic, facilitating the growth of LAB. The increase in lactic acid production lowered the pH, thus weakening the activity of nitrate reductase and reducing the nitrite content (An et al., 2021). Additionally, LAB caused high TA content and low pH in the fermentation liquid, inhibiting the growth of bacteria responsible for reducing nitrate to nitrite. Some LAB strains were also able to produce nitrite reductase and degraded nitrite (Xiong et al., 2019). In compliance with China's national health standard for nitrite levels, which is set at 20.00 mg/kg, the pickled Xiaohuang ginger in this study, following a 42-day fermentation process, has been deemed safe for consumption.

Hardness

Hardness is a significant indicator that directly reflects the texture of fermented foods and influences consumer choice (Shang et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1E, the initial hardness of fresh Xiaohuang ginger was 412.14 N, and this value significantly decreased as fermentation progressed (P < 0.001). After 21 days of fermentation, the hardness of pickled Xiaohuang ginger stabilized at 237.06 N, indicating that fermentation markedly reduced the hardness of Xiaohuang ginger.

Many microorganisms can secrete pectinase to break down pectin in plant cell walls, thereby softening plant tissues. For example, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum produces polygalacturonase during fermentation, while Pichia generates pectinase, cellulase, and amylase. These enzymes all contribute to reducing the hardness of pickles (Ye et al., 2022). Additionally, Pearson correlation analysis in this study showed that hardness was significantly correlated with pH (R > 0.96, P < 0.05). Thus, the reduced hardness of fermented Xiaohuang ginger may be also linked to pH changes during fermentation, weakening cell wall strength and adhesion.

Color

Table 1 shows the color changes observed in the fermented Xiaohuang ginger over 42 days. The initial values of the L*, a*, and b* were approximately 78.74, 3.47, and 49.93, respectively. After the fermentation, a significant decrease (P < 0.05) in brightness (L*), redness (a*), and yellowness (b*) was observed, indicating that the color of the fermented Xiaohuang ginger was darker than that of fresh Xiaohuang ginger. Furthermore, the total color difference (ΔE*) of the fermented Xiaohuang ginger was relatively high, reaching 15.41 after 7 days of fermentation. The high ΔE* value indicates a significant change in the color of Xiaohuang ginger during fermentation. This change can be attributed to the acidity produced through lactic fermentation and structural changes due to moisture migration during high-salt fermentation, both of which affect the color of the ginger (Bekhit et al., 2018).

Changes in flavor during fermentation

Sugars and organic acids

Sugars and organic acids were the main attributes that contributed to the sweet and sour tastes of kimchi. The changes in glucose, polysaccharides, and total sugars in Xiaohuang ginger during natural fermentation were detected, which are shown in Fig. 2A–2C. During the fermentation for 42 days, the glucose content significantly decreased from 9.92 to 1.10 mg/g (P < 0.001). Glucose is considered the primary energy and carbon source available for LAB strains (Hashemi et al., 2017), and its content gradually decreased as the fermentation progressed. The polysaccharide content significantly increased from 79.68 to 156.17 mg/g during fermentation (P < 0.001). Studies have shown that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and yeast can produce extracellular polysaccharides during fermentation (Cai et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2020). Additionally, we also hypothesized that the increase in polysaccharides in fermented samples resulted from the release of mannoproteins and glucans during maturation and alcohol fermentation (Ducasse et al., 2010).

Organic acids influence the sour taste of kimchi. In this study, a total of 10 organic acids, namely oxalic acid, malic acid, shikimic acid, lactic acid, acetic acid, maleic acid, citric acid, succinic acid, fumaric acid, and propionic acid, were quantitatively detected in the fermented Xiaohuang ginger lasting 42 days (Table 2). Overall, the total content of organic acids in the fermented Xiaohuang ginger significantly increased from 4315.79 to 9392.25 μg/g (P < 0.05). This finding is consistent with the increase in the number of LAB during the fermentation and most LAB possess the ability to produce organic acids, especially lactic acid and acetic acid. Meanwhile, many fungi also accumulate organic acids, increasing their content (Xu et al., 2021). The main organic acids in fresh Xiaohuang ginger at day 0 were lactic acid (1566.71 μg/g) and oxalic acid (1257.34 μg/g), consistent with the results of a study on pickled radishes (Chen et al., 2019). Succinic acid content was below the detection limit in fresh Xiaohuang ginger. The contents of other acids, such as malic acid, shikimic acid, maleic acid, citric acid, fumaric acid, and propionic acid, were relatively low, ranging from 1.59 to 241.44 μg/g. During the 42-day fermentation period, the lactic acid content showed a significant upward trend (P < 0.05), rising from 1566.71 μg/g to 7056.22 μg/g by day 35, before experiencing a minor decline to 6476.97 μg/g by day 42. Conversely, the malic acid, citric acid, fumaric acid, and propionic acid contents diminished over the fermentation process. Also, after 14 days of fermentation, citric acid and fumaric acid became undetectable. This reduction can be attributed to the fact that in the later fermentation stages, certain organic acids, including lactic acid, function as carbon sources for microbial growth (Zaunmüller et al., 2006).

Soluble proteins and FAAs

During fermentation, LAB can secrete peptidases that break down soluble proteins into various FAAs (Wu et al., 2015). In this study, soluble proteins significantly decreased with fermentation time (P < 0.001; Fig. 2D). We detected 19 FAAs in Xiaohuang ginger: Asp, Glu, Asn, Gln, His, Arg, Tyr, Val, Met, Ile, Leu, Phe, Trp, Lys, Ser, Gly, Thr, Ala, and Pro (Table 3). In fresh Xiaohuang ginger, the major FAAs were Gln (44.10 μg/g), Glu (44.57 μg/g), Ala (53.22 μg/g), Asp (57.87 μg/g), and Asn (66.07 μg/g), with Trp at a relatively low level (4.74 μg/g). In the fermentation process, the contents of Glu, Tyr, Gly, and Pro showed a marked increase with time (P < 0.05). In contrast, the levels of Ile, Val, Phe, Ser, and Thr remained relatively stable throughout fermentation. Overall, the total FAA content experienced a reduction (Fig. 2E). Based on taste, FAAs can be classified into three categories: umami (Asp, Glu, Asn, and Gln), bitter (His, Arg, Tyr, Val, Met, Ile, Leu, Phe, Trp, and Lys), and sweet (Ser, Gly, Thr, Ala, and Pro). By day 42 of Xiaohuang ginger fermentation, the levels of umami, sweet, and bitter FAAs had all decreased slightly (Fig. 2F). Interestingly, similar trends have also been observed in the fermentation of bitter gourd (Shang et al., 2022). FAAs are crucial for LAB growth, and their decline during fermentation is likely due to utilization by LAB (Chen et al., 2019). They can also be converted by LAB into clove and floral compounds (An et al., 2021; Xiao et al., 2020).

Total flavonoids, gingerol, and curcumin

Flavonoids, gingerol, and curcumin are beneficial to human health (Wang et al., 2024). For example, gingerol has pharmacological activities such as antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anticancer effects. Fig. 2G–2I show the changes in total flavonoids, gingerol, and curcumin contents during the fermentation process. Compared to fresh Xiaohuang ginger (day 0), the contents of these three substances significantly increased in pickled Xiaohuang ginger after a 42-day fermentation (all P < 0.05). Specifically, the concentrations of flavonoids, gingerol, and curcumin on day 42 were 16.61 mg/g, 0.97 mg/g, and 1.84 μg/g, respectively.

Dynamics of metabolites during fermentation

The flavor and texture of kimchi are mainly related to the metabolites produced by microorganisms during the fermentation process (Jeong et al., 2013). In this study, the UHPLC-QE-MS-based metabolomics analysis revealed a total of 12,204 peaks in positive and negative ion modes. According to first and second-level mass spectrometry data, a total of 2213 metabolites were identified by searching databases including Metlin, HMDB and self-built Majorbio libraries. In the fermentation process, the main metabolites at the superclass level were lipids and lipid-like molecules (30.27 %), organic acids and derivatives (17.62 %), organoheterocyclic compounds (11.73 %), benzenoids (10.87 %), and phenylpropanoids and polyketides (10.21 %) (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Lipids and lipid-like molecules were the most abundant, with 596 matched metabolites in total. At the class level (Supplementary Fig. S1B), the main metabolites included carboxylic acids and derivatives (16.10 %), prenol lipids (11.02 %), fatty acyls (9.95 %), steroids and steroid derivatives (6.20 %), phenols (3.05 %), and flavonoids (2.64 %). The subclass level featured amino acids, peptides, and analogues (15.03 %), carbohydrates and carbohydrate conjugates (4.47 %), fatty acids and conjugates (4.01 %), sesquiterpenoids (2.18 %), methoxyphenols (2.13 %), terpene lactones (1.68 %), and flavonoid glycosides (1.63 %) as the main metabolites (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed that ginger samples from different fermentation time points formed distinct clusters (Fig. 3A). Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS-DA) further confirmed these results (Fig. 3B), indicating remarkable differences in metabolic profiles across various fermentation stages. Accordingly, the fermentation process can be divided into three phases: early (0–7 d), middle (7–21 d), and late (21–42 d). A total of 1472 differentially accumulated metabolites were identified in pickled Xiaohuang ginger (Supplementary Fig. S2A). These metabolites were mainly enriched in phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, alpha-linolenic acid metabolism, flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, sphingolipid metabolism, and flavonoid biosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. S2B). More specifically, in the early stages of fermentation, metabolite profiles showed marked divergence, particularly evident between day 7 and day 0, where 563 metabolites increased while 301 decreased. From the start (day 0) to the end (day 42) of fermentation, 535 metabolites increased, while 401 decreased (Fig. 3C–3H). This trend aligns with the fermentation process of Tibetan tea pile (Wang et al., 2024). Additionally, 328 differentially accumulated metabolites were commonly identified across all comparisons (Fig. 3I), including 79 lipids and lipid-like molecules, 61 organic acids and derivatives, 16 flavonoids, 8 phenols, and 53 amino acids, peptides, and analogs (Supplementary Fig. S3). These metabolites are likely to influence the quality and flavor of fermented Xiaohuang ginger, and the top 50 most accumulated substances are presented in Fig. 3J. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that the 328 shared differentially accumulated metabolites were primarily enriched in pathways such as flavone and flavonol biosynthesis, flavonoid biosynthesis, citrate cycle (TCA cycle), and tryptophan metabolism (Fig. 3K).

The metabolic shifts in Xiaohuang ginger during fermentation were remarkable and followed a distinct trajectory, akin to that seen in pickled chayote (Shang et al., 2022). Using the Short Time-series Expression Miner (STEM) clustering algorithm, we identified five metabolic profiles exhibiting significant changes in metabolite patterns (all P < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 4A. The most substantial cluster, cluster 29 (n = 80, P = 2.2e-31), exhibited a progressive upregulation of metabolites throughout the fermentation process; its metabolites were primarily enriched in pathways like arginine and proline metabolism, tryptophan metabolism, lipoic acid metabolism, cofactor biosynthesis, and fatty acid biosynthesis (Fig. 4B). Conversely, cluster 0 (n = 136, P = 6.5e-72) demonstrated a pronounced downregulation pattern; its metabolites were mainly enriched in processes such as lysine degradation, the citrate cycle (TCA cycle), glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, purine metabolism, and the biosynthesis of tropane, piperidine, and pyridine alkaloids (Fig. 4C). These findings highlight the dynamic metabolic changes occurring in Xiaohuang ginger during fermentation.

Microbial community dynamics during fermentation

Bacterial communities

Based on 16S rDNA full-length sequencing with the third-generation PacBio SMRT platform, a total of 949,722 high-quality reads with an average length of 1484 bp were recovered, which aggregated into 52 OTUs. The species richness and diversity indices, including Sobs, Shannon, Simpson, Chao, and ACE values, were analyzed (Zhu et al., 2017). As shown in Fig. 5A, these indices suggested a significant reduction in species diversity of the bacterial community with increasing fermentation time (all P < 0.05). Similar results were also reported by Xu et al. in their study of fermented chili peppers (Xu et al., 2021). Notable differences were observed in the distance of bacterial community structure during the fermentation process. Samples from days 0, 7, and 14 of fermentation could be clearly distinguished from samples in other groups, while samples from days 21, 28, 35, and 42 were mixed together and could not be clearly distinguished (Fig. 5B–5C). These findings suggested that the fermentation process markedly transformed the bacterial communities present in fresh Xiaohuang ginger, with particularly considerable changes observed in samples from the early and middle stages of fermentation.

A: Bacterial alpha diversity indices; B: Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) of bacterial communities; C: Non-metric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) of bacterial communities; D: Fungal alpha diversity indices; E: PCoA of fungal communities; F: NMDS of fungal communities; G: Taxonomic composition of bacterial communities at genus level; H: Taxonomic composition of bacterial communities at species level; I: Taxonomic composition of fungal communities at genus level; J: Taxonomic composition of fungal communities at species level.

Furthermore, we classified high-quality sequencing reads at the level of genus and species to study the structure and dynamic succession of bacterial communities during the fermentation process of Xiaohuang ginger. At the genus level, a total of 33 bacterial genera were identified, among which the dominant bacterial groups included Lactobacillus, Leuconostoc, Acinetobacter, Raoultella, Erwinia, Pseudomonas, Rahnella1, and Pantoea (Fig. 5G). In the early stage of fermentation, the main bacterial genus in the fermentation liquid was Leuconostoc (Fig. 6A). However, Leuconostoc rapidly decreased after 14 days of fermentation, while the genus of Lactobacillus dominated in the later stages of fermentation. Indeed, pioneer microbiota like Leuconostoc are commonly found in the early stages of fermentation (e.g., kimchi, silage), where they rapidly acidify the environment to form a physical barrier and inhibit spoilage organisms. Stable-phase bacteria such as Lactobacillus dominate the mid-to-late stages (e.g., yogurt, mature kimchi), adapting to lower pH conditions to gain a competitive advantage (Shang et al., 2022). In this study, we propose that during the mid-to-late fermentation stages, three major selective pressures—environmental acidification, oxygen depletion, and nutrient transformation—combined with Lactobacillus's metabolic efficiency, acid tolerance, and antimicrobial strategies, enable it to gradually replace initial microbiota and become the dominant bacteria. This dynamic succession is a critical mechanism underlying the stable quality (e.g., flavor, safety) of fermented foods (Jeong et al., 2013).

Taxonomic profiling of dominant bacterial communities (A: bacterial genera; B: bacterial species) and fungal communities (C: fungal genera; D: fungal species) in pickled Xiaohuang ginger in the 42-day fermentation process .

At the species level (Fig. 5H), 47 bacterial species were identified in total. Among these species, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Leuconostoc citreum, Raoultella terrigena, Rahnella sp. strain Y9602, and Pantoea rwandensis were the dominant ones. In fresh Xiaohuang ginger, Leuconostoc citreum was the main species (Fig. 6B). As fermentation proceeded, the population of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum showed a significant increase (P < 0.05), highlighting its crucial role in Xiaohuang ginger fermentation. Consistently, it is also predominant in other reported fermented products (Jung et al., 2024). Additionally, a study has demonstrated that Lactiplantibacillus plantarum in kimchi can greatly inhibit the growth of spoilage bacteria and foodborne pathogens (Xiong et al., 2019). In light of these findings, we propose that the presence of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum in fermented Xiaohuang ginger could be instrumental in combating pathogenic bacteria (Hu et al., 2017).

Fungal communities

An average of 633 fungal OTUs were identified. The α-diversity indices, including Sobs, Shannon, Simpson, Chao, and ACE, were calculated and are presented in Fig. 5D. Notably, all these indices revealed a consistent downward trend of fungal diversity in the fermented Xiaohuang ginger (all P < 0.01). Moreover, both the species richness and evenness of the fungal communities in the fermented Xiaohuang ginger decreased over time. This trend is consistent with findings from studies on fermented chili peppers (Xu et al., 2021).

In addition to bacteria, fungi also play an important role during fermentation, contributing to the unique flavors of pickled vegetables (Liang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2015). During the fermentation of Xiaohuang ginger, the fungal community exhibited notable differences (Fig. 5E–5F). Both principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) showed distinct variations among samples from different fermentation stages, demonstrating that fermentation dramatically altered the fungal community composition in Xiaohuang ginger.

At the genus level, we identified a total of 269 fungal genera. Fig. 5I illustrates the major fungal genera in fermented Xiaohuang ginger, which include Pichia, Kluyveromyces, Wallemia, Xeromyces, Hannaella, Cladosporium, Vishniacozyma, and Acremonium. Considerable differences were found in the abundance of these genera between the 0–7 d and 14–42 d of fermentation (Fig. 6C). Specifically, Wallemia, Xeromyces, Hannaella, Cladosporium, Vishniacozyma, and Acremonium were predominant in the early stages and their abundance declined after 14 days of fermentation. Conversely, the relative abundances of Pichia and Kluyveromyces, the two most notable fungal genera during fermentation, increased significantly in the middle and late stages (P < 0.05). Interestingly, Pichia was also identified as the main fungal genus in many other pickled vegetables such as Chinese sour bamboo shoots (Guan et al., 2020).

At the species level, a total of 396 fungal species were identified. The major species included Pichia fermentans, Kluyveromyces hubeiensis, Xeromyces bisporus, Cladosporium delicatulum, Acremonium stromaticum, Vishniacozyma sp., and Hannaella sp. (Fig. 5J). Notable differences in fungal species composition were observed at different fermentation stages (Fig. 6D). Vishniacozyma sp., Acremonium stromaticum, Xeromyces bisporus, and Cladosporium delicatulum predominated in the early fermentation stage (0–7 d), whereas Pichia fermentans and Kluyveromyces hubeiensis became dominant in the middle and late stages (14–42 d). This aligns with prior studies indicating that yeast is the most important fungal community in Sichuan pickles (Guan et al., 2020).

Integrated analysis of bacterial and fungal communities, physicochemical properties, and flavor-related metabolites

The production of flavor compounds during food fermentation is closely linked to microbial activity. To explore this relationship, we used Pearson correlation analysis to assess the connection between core microbial communities and the quality attributes of fermented Xiaohuang ginger. The results were visualized using Cytoscape (Fig. 7). Microorganisms with a correlation coefficient |R| > 0.6 and P < 0.05 were identified as key functional microbes closely associated with critical metabolites in the fermentation process. Four bacterial species (Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, Leuconostoc citreum, Lactococcus lactis, and Weissella cibaria) and five fungal species (Pichia sp., Pichia fermentans, Kluyveromyces hubeiensis, Vishniacozyma sp., and Hannaella sp.) showed significant correlations with physicochemical properties and metabolites. These included 10 physicochemical attributes, 8 organic acids, 8 FAAs, and 12 key metabolites. Notably, bacterial communities exhibited stronger correlations with flavor characteristics compared to fungal communities.

Bacterial metabolic coordination

Lactobacillus species serve as critical biocatalysts in flavor development through their production of fruity esters, organic acids, and FAAs, as evidenced in kimchi fermentation studies (Markkinen et al., 2021). Our analysis corroborates the essential microbial role in flavor modulation (Rao et al., 2020), revealing that LAB collectively exhibited strong negative correlations with glucose and total FAAs (R < −0.67), alongside significant positive correlations with polysaccharides and organic acids (R > 0.68). Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, the dominant LAB species, demonstrated multifaceted flavor regulation, showing positive correlations with acetic acid (R > 0.61), valine (R > 0.61), behenic acid (R > 0.79), and flavonoids (quercetin and kaempferol, R > 0.6), while strongly suppressing gingerol (R < −0.65), curcumin (R < −0.76), and tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates (citric acid: R < −0.99; fumaric acid: R < −0.99). In contrast, Leuconostoc citreum displayed inverse metabolic preferences, negatively associated with acetic acid (R < −0.63), valine (R < −0.62), and flavonoids (R < −0.62) but positively correlated with gingerol (R > 0.64), curcumin (R > 0.75), and organic acids (citric acid: R > 0.99; fumaric acid: R > 0.99). Lactococcus lactis exhibited curcumin-promoting (R > 0.61) and organic acid-enhancing activities (citric acid: R > 0.98; fumaric acid: R > 0.97), while suppressing acetic acid (R < −0.65), isoorientin (R < −0.6), and phenolic compounds (quercetin: R < −0.65; kaempferol: R < −0.68). Weissella cibaria further demonstrated dual regulatory roles, positively modulating sweet FAAs (Asn, Gln) and organic acids (malic, citric, fumaric, oxalic acids; all R > 0.62), while exhibiting an inverse relationship with dipeptides (threonylphenylalanine, histidine glutamate), phenylpropanoids (isoorientin, quercetin, kaempferol), and lipid derivatives (all R < −0.67). Generally, these metabolic signatures align with LAB's established capacity to hydrolyze proteins into flavor-active FAAs and generate organic acids via homolactic/heterolactic fermentation, mechanisms critical for kimchi sourness (Jeong et al., 2013) and rice wine aroma enhancement (Wang et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2018).

Fungal metabolic coordination

Fungal communities exhibited complementary metabolic networks during Xiaohuang ginger fermentation. Pichia species demonstrated positive correlations with polysaccharides, total organic acids, and lipid-like molecules (R > 0.61), while showing negative associations with gingerol and bitter FAAs (tryptophan, lysine; R < −0.61). Interestingly, Pichia fermentans and Kluyveromyces hubeiensis synergistically promoted lipid synthesis (R > 0.65) but suppressed organic acid accumulation (R < −0.65), indicating niche-specific metabolic partitioning. Conversely, Vishniacozyma species displayed strong positive correlations with gingerol, curcumin, and organic acids (R > 0.71), alongside negative correlations with lipid derivatives (R < −0.70). Hannaella species further enriched this functional diversity, positively regulating curcumin, creatine, organic acids (citric, fumaric, oxalic acids; R > 0.60), while suppressing acetic acid, behenic acid, threonylphenylalanine, histidine glutamate, phenylpropanoids and polyketides, and benzenoids (R < −0.6). These interspecies metabolic interactions highlight fungi's roles in modulating lipid-phenolic equilibria and oxygen-sensitive compounds, collectively shaping the fermentation ecosystem's flavor complexity.

Microbial regulation, texture modulation and flavor formation

Yeasts constitute a pivotal fungal group that drives nutrient synthesis and characteristic flavor development in fermented foods through their specialized metabolic regulation (Xu et al., 2021). In the Xiaohuang ginger fermentation system, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum and Leuconostoc citreum (LAB), alongside yeasts such as Pichia fermentans, emerged as primary architects of flavor biosynthesis. Microbial community dynamics exerted significant control over critical metabolites, particularly organic acids (such as lactic acid and acetic acid) and amino acids, which directly govern the textural and sensory properties of fermented products (Wu et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2021). This biochemical regulation manifested in distinct physicochemical shifts: LAB displayed a robust inverse correlation with pH (R < −0.88) and a strong positive association with total acidity (R > 0.76), paralleled by Pichia's negative pH correlation (R < −0.61) and TA promotion (R > 0.67). Such pH reduction—a hallmark of carbohydrate microbial metabolism and acidogenesis (Liang et al., 2019)—not only inhibits pathogens but also enhances ecological stability by modulating microbial diversity (Jung et al., 2022). The principal LAB-derived acids (lactic and acetic acids) serve dual roles as flavor enhancers and preservatives (Wang et al., 2022), mirroring their pH-modulating functions in cassava fermentation and aroma optimization in Shanxi vinegar (Oguntoyinbo & Dodd, 2010; Zhang et al., 2017).

Notably, LAB demonstrated significant negative correlations with ginger hardness and nitrite content, a pattern similarly observed for L. citreum, L. lactis, Vishniacozyma sp., and Hannaella sp. This aligns with Chen et al.'s findings that LAB inoculation accelerates fermentation kinetics while suppressing nitrite accumulation in mixed vegetable fermentations (Chen et al., 2020). Of particular significance, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum-dominated fermentation markedly enriched aromatic compound profiles (Chen et al., 2023), confirming LAB's dual capacity for rapid acidification and flavor potentiation. Collectively, these mechanisms elucidate how LAB consortia drive Xiaohuang ginger fermentation through competitive acidogenesis and metabolic specialization, ultimately shaping its distinctive flavor characteristics.

Conclusion

This study investigated the interlinked dynamics of quality attributes, microbial community structure, and metabolome during the spontaneous fermentation of Chinese pickled Xiaohuang ginger—a traditional ready-to-eat flavoring food. Progressive fermentation was characterized by a decline in pH and elevation in TA, accompanied by a reduction in texture firmness and a marked proliferation of LAB. By day 7, notable darkening of the ginger's color occurred, coinciding with the accumulation of key flavor metabolites. These metabolites encompassed umami-conferring FAAs such as glutamic acid, sweet-tasting FAAs like proline and glycine, organic acids including lactic acid and acetic acid, as well as bioactive compounds such as polysaccharides, total flavonoids, gingerol, and curcumin. Microbial community analysis revealed a dominance of bacterial taxa (e.g., Lactobacillus) and yeast genera (Pichia, Kluyveromyces). At the species level, Lactiplantibacillus plantarum emerged as the predominant bacterial species, while Pichia fermentans and Kluyveromyces hubeiensis were the most prevalent fungal species. Correlation network analysis further identified nine core microbial species whose abundances were strongly associated with critical quality attributes of fermented Xiaohuang ginger. These findings offer comprehensive insights into the metabolic, physicochemical, and flavor transformations linked to microbial community succession during Xiaohuang ginger fermentation. Future research will focus on isolating and functionally characterizing these core microorganisms to validate their roles in flavor biosynthesis, thereby promoting the advancement of ready-to-eat ginger-derived flavoring foods.
