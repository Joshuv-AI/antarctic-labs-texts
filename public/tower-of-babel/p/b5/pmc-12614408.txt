Effects of anaesthesia and surgery on sleep–wake timing and subjective sleep quality in children: an observational study
Authors: Arjen J G Meewisse, Sharon J Meijer, Kee Fong Choi, Stefan Kanters, Kim B Meekel, Pia Burger, Reinoud J B J Gemke, Dirk Jan Stenvers, Jeroen Hermanides, Mark L van Zuylen, Jorinde Polderman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntroductionSurgery and anaesthesia can cause disruption of the circadian timing system and diurnal sleep–wake rhythm. However, it remains unknown how sleep–wake rhythm in children is affected by anaesthesia and surgery, and if anaesthesia without a surgical procedure disturbs sleep–wake cycles.MethodsWe recruited children aged 1–11 y undergoing anaesthesia for an elective surgical procedure or magnetic resonance imaging. The Children's Chronotype Questionnaire, Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire and a daily sleep diary from three days before until seven days after anaesthesia were used to assess sleep–wake rhythm and sleep quality.ResultsIn total, 117 patients (median (IQR [range]) age 4 (2–6 [1–11]) y, 56 (47.9%) female) were included. The midpoint of sleep did not change on the night after anaesthesia when compared with three nights before (estimated median time difference 8 min, 95%CI ‐15–1, p = 0.12, n = 114). Median (IQR [range]) midpoint of sleep at baseline was 01.42 (01.22–02.19 [00.10–03.15]) for the surgical group and 01.22 (01.03–02.07 [00.15–04.02]) for the magnetic resonance imaging group. The midpoint of sleep did not shift on the night after anaesthesia for either group (surgical group: 01.37 (01.07–02.15 [23.00–03.12]), estimated median time difference ‐15 min, 95%CI ‐27–0, p = 0.07, n = 58; magnetic resonance imaging group: 01.30 (01.07–02.07 [23.45–05.00]), estimated median time difference 0 min, 95%CI ‐9–9, p = 0.90, n = 56), and there was no between‐group difference (estimated median time difference: ‐15 min, 95%CI ‐30–0 min, p = 0.07). Subjective sleep quality was unaffected.DiscussionWe found no disruption of sleep–wake rhythm in children after anaesthesia and surgery. Further research is needed to see whether more invasive procedures and accompanying anaesthesia pose a risk of disrupting sleep–wake rhythms in children.Keywords: circadian rhythm, paediatric anaesthesia, peri‐operative sleep quality, sleep–wake rhythm

Introduction

Good sleep is essential for development in children [1, 2] and aids recovery following surgery [3, 4, 5]. Sleep and sleep–wake timing are partially regulated by the circadian timing system [6]. Many other physiological functions that are involved in peri‐operative care, for example, haemodynamics, haemostasis, immune system and metabolism, also have a diurnal rhythm regulated by the circadian timing system [6, 7, 8]. An earlier study by our group found that surgery and anaesthesia negatively affect the diurnal rhythm of sleep–wake timing and sleep quality in adults [9]. However, a recent scoping review of six articles, with two studying children and focusing on sleep quality, found conflicting results [10]. As such, it remains unknown how day‐to‐day sleep–wake timing is affected by anaesthesia and surgery in children.

The endogenous sleep–wake rhythm mimics a sinusoidal curve, where the timing of the peak (also called ‘phase’) varies between individuals [6, 11]. These individual differences reflect a person's ‘chronotype’, which typically changes over the life course [6]. In children, prenatal circadian rhythmicity seems to be maternally regulated, while the child's circadian timing system develops gradually during the first months and consolidates in the first year [12, 13, 14]. Young children tend to have early chronotypes (i.e. going to bed early and waking up relatively early), which shifts towards a later chronotype during adolescence and a gradual shift back towards an earlier chronotype in adulthood [15]. Sleep–wake rhythm in children differs from that of adults, for example, children need more sleep than adults [16].

Factors that influence the circadian timing system are called ‘zeitgebers’ [6]. These synchronise the circadian phase of a biological system with the external environment. Preclinical research has shown that anaesthesia can function as a zeitgeber [17], potentially disturbing the circadian timing system and sleep. In a previous study of adults undergoing surgery, we found a phase advance of sleep–wake timing and an associated decrease in postoperative sleep quality [9]. Some studies have researched postoperative sleep or sleep during hospitalisation in children with objective and subjective sleep measures, with conflicting results [10, 18, 19, 20, 21]. However, none of these studies evaluated the first night after the procedure and none studied sleep–wake rhythm specifically.

We therefore aimed to investigate the influence of anaesthesia on sleep–wake rhythm and sleep quality in children. Based on our findings in adult patients [9], we hypothesised that the sleep–wake rhythm would phase advance on the night after anaesthesia with an associated decrease of sleep quality. Furthermore, we wished to explore whether the phase advance of sleep–wake rhythm was primarily associated with anaesthesia or surgery, by comparing patients receiving general anaesthesia for a surgical procedure with those receiving general anaesthesia for magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).

Methods

We performed a single‐centre prospective observational cohort study in a tertiary care centre. Informed consent was obtained from parents/carers before inclusion. The study was approved by the local ethics committee.

Patients were screened at the pre‐operative assessment and, if eligible, were approached and recruited by telephone discussion with their parent/carer. Subsequently, parents/carers were sent the first set of questionnaires three days before anaesthesia and the second set seven days after anaesthesia, with both sets being sent by email.

We included patients aged 1–11 y, who underwent general anaesthesia for > 30 min for either an elective surgical procedure or MRI. Patients aged < 1 y were not included as diurnal rhythms are not stable during infancy, and patients aged > 11 y were not included as puberty could influence diurnal rhythms and sleep [12, 13, 14]. To reduce the influence of known disturbers of the circadian timing system and sleep, we did not study patients with any of the following: severe sleeping disorder; ASA physical status ≥ 4; undergoing cardiac surgery; development of postoperative delirium; scheduled for post‐procedure paediatric intensive care unit (PICU) admission; severe developmental disorder (estimated IQ < 85); or were patients whose parents/carers were unable to communicate in Dutch (i.e. the language of all the information/consent forms and questionnaires).

Baseline sleep parameters (i.e. midpoint of sleep, sleep duration, sleep on‐ and offset times, sleep latency/inertia, time spent napping and use of alarm clock (or family member) for waking up) were established with the Children's Chronotype Questionnaire [22]. As there is no validated Dutch version to date, the original (English) Children's Chronotype Questionnaire was translated into Dutch according to the World Health Organization guidelines [23] (online Supporting Information Appendix S1). The questionnaire consists of: 16 items on chronotype and sleep timing (adapted from the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire [24]), calculating chronotype by midpoint of sleep on free days corrected for sleep deficit; 11 questions on morningness‐eveningness (adopted from the Morningness‐Eveningness Questionnaire [25]); and one question asking parents directly to assess their child's chronotype on a 5‐point scale. Midpoint of sleep as measured by the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire has a strong correlation with the Morningness‐Eveningness Questionnaire [26] and the chronotype measured by the Children's Chronotype Questionnaire, that is, the midpoint of sleep, has been validated with actigraphy and timing of melatonin onset in children [27]. As non‐compliance was high during the first months of the study, the questions on morningness‐eveningness were dropped to shorten the questionnaire.

Night‐to‐night sleep was measured with a sleeping diary, with similar questions on sleep timing as the Children's Chronotype Questionnaire (online Supporting Information Appendix S2). The sleeping diary was filled out for the three nights before and the seven nights after anaesthesia.

The Dutch version of the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire [28] was used to determine subjective sleep quality. This contains 33 items on sleep that are scored with ‘rarely’, ‘sometimes’ or ‘usually’. Sleep quality is assessed over eight domains, which are bedtime resistance (six items); sleep anxiety (four items); sleep duration (three items); sleep disordered breathing (three items); parasomnias (seven items); daytime sleepiness (eight items); night awakenings (three items); and sleep onset delay (one item). Some items are part of multiple domains but are only used once in the calculation for total score. Higher scores indicate a lower sleep quality. The last question is a self‐assessment of subjective sleep quality on a scale of 0–10. The Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire was filled out twice. First, three days before the procedure to establish a baseline on sleep quality in the preceding month and second, seven days after the procedure, using a modified version to measure sleep quality in the week after anaesthesia.

Relevant patient and surgical characteristics were collected from the electronic patient record including age; sex; ASA physical status; risk assessment of the procedure (risk of cardiac complications for non‐cardiac surgery) [29]; pre‐operative morbidity and medication use; and relevant data from during and after the procedure. We also collected data on neurodiversity (i.e. autism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) and the use of pre‐operative over‐the‐counter sleep promoting medication (most notably, melatonin) from the electronic patient record. Lastly, we reported the duration of hospital stay for each patient, including whether the patient stayed in the hospital on the night before the procedure. All patient rooms on the paediatric unit in our facility are one‐ or two‐bedded rooms, where parents can stay with their child.

Our primary outcome was the phase shift of the midpoint of sleep observed on the first night after anaesthesia for all patients (surgical and MRI), when compared with the midpoint of sleep three nights before surgery, which we called Δ‐NAA (night after anaesthesia). We also performed a sub‐analysis for patients aged < 4 y and patients aged ≥ 4 y, as the Children's Chronotype Questionnaire has only been validated for children aged > 4 y. As secondary outcomes, we assessed Δ‐NAA for surgical vs. MRI patients; postoperative subjective sleep quality in the week after surgery or MRI compared with pre‐intervention (Δ‐Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire), including all subscales of the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire; Δ‐Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire compared between surgical and MRI patients; the factors that were associated with Δ‐NAA, focusing on factors that are known to influence sleep or postoperative recovery (age, sex, ASA physical status), chronotype, type of anaesthesia used for induction (propofol only vs. sevoflurane usage (with or without propofol), duration of anaesthesia, use of esketamine or corticosteroids and Δ‐Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire [6, 30, 31, 32]); and changes in sleep–wake timing for all 10 nights of the sleeping diary (i.e. midpoint of sleep, sleep duration, sleep on‐ and offset time, sleep latency/inertia and proportion of patients napping).

We used nQuery version 8.5.1 (GraphPad Software DBA Statistical Solutions, Boston, MA, USA) to calculate the required sample size, drawing on our previous work which found a standard deviation of 62 min for the phase shift of midpoint of sleep after surgery in adult patients [9]. With a sample size of 100 patients, we had 80% power to detect an effect size of 17 min at a significance level of 0.05, using a paired t‐test. Accordingly, we aimed to include 50 patients in the surgical group and 50 patients in the MRI group.

All analyses were done using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). All data were visually assessed for normality. For our final analysis, we only included patients with sufficient data, that is, for whom the first set of questionnaires and > 60% of the sleeping diary were filled out.

We compared sleep variables at two different time‐points within the same patients using paired t‐tests or Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests, depending on normality, and at the same time‐point between groups using Student's t‐tests or Mann–Whitney U‐tests. We used Spearman's ρ to analyse the association of Δ‐NAA with various factors. Lastly, we used repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for analysing midpoint of sleep, nightly sleep duration and sleep on‐ and offset times, and Friedman's ANOVA for the proportion of patients napping, sleep latency and sleep inertia. Bonferroni corrections were used in the ANOVA analyses to compare all individual nights with baseline.

Results

We recruited a total of 306 participants from March 2022 until May 2024. Of these, 117 patients were available for the final analysis, with a median (IQR [range]) age of 4 (2–6 [1–11]) y, and 56 (47.9%) were female. We included 60 patients in the surgical group and 57 in the MRI group (Fig. 1). Compared with the three nights before anaesthesia, the median (IQR [range]) midpoint of sleep did not shift on the night after surgery or MRI (01.37 (01.15–02.07 [00.10–04.02]) vs. 01.36 (01.07–02.07 [23.00–05.00]), estimated median time difference ‐8 min, 95%CI ‐15–1, p = 0.12, n = 114). For patients in the surgical group, the midpoint of sleep at baseline was 01.42 (01.22–02.19 [00.10–03.15]) and was 01.37 (01.07–02.15 [23.00–03.12]) on the night after anaesthesia (estimated median time difference ‐15 min, 95%CI ‐27–0, p = 0.07, n = 58). For patients in the MRI group, the midpoint of sleep at baseline was 01.22 (01.03–02.07 [00.15–04.02]) and on the night after anaesthesia, it was 01.30 (01.07–02.07 [23.45–05.00]) (estimated median time difference 0 min, 95%CI ‐9–9, p = 0.90, n = 56). When comparing median (IQR [range]) Δ‐NAA between surgical and MRI patients, we found no difference (‐15 (‐45–18 [‐142–170]) min vs. 0 (‐19–15 [‐105–180]) min, respectively; estimated median time difference ‐15 min, 95%CI ‐30–0, p = 0.07).

The midpoint of sleep three nights before anaesthesia compared with the night after anaesthesia did not shift for patients aged < 4 y (01.29 (01.10–02.00 [00.15–04.02]) vs. 01.37 (01.07–02.09 [23.45–05.00]), estimated median time difference ‐4 min, 95%CI ‐15–12, p = 0.72, n = 53), nor for patients aged ≥ 4 y (01.40 (01.17–02.15 [00.10–03.15]) vs. 01.35 (01.01–02.07 [23.00–03.07]), estimated median time difference ‐15 min, 95%CI ‐23–0, p = 0.06, n = 61).

Subjective sleep quality did not deteriorate after surgery or MRI (n = 106), with a median (IQR [range]) pre‐procedure Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire score of 9 (5–13 [0–30]) and a post‐procedure Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire score of 8 (5–14 [1–50]) (estimated median difference 0, 95%CI ‐0.5–1, p = 0.61). On the Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire subscale scores and self‐assessment of sleep quality, post‐procedural sleep quality was similar to before the procedure, except for the subscale ‘sleep duration’, where post‐procedural scores were worse. Total and subscale Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire scores can be found in online Supporting Information Table S4. We found no difference in the median (IQR [range]) Δ‐Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire between patients in the surgical group and the MRI group (0 (‐2–2 [‐5–23]) vs. 0 (‐3–3 [‐10–11]), respectively; estimated median difference 0, 95%CI ‐1–2, p = 0.85).

Higher phase delay (Δ‐NAA) was associated with male sex (Spearman's ρ 0.289, p = 0.002). We did not find any other associations between Δ‐NAA and other factors, most notably with midpoint of sleep on free days corrected for sleep deficit, total time under anaesthesia and Δ‐Children's Sleep Habits Questionnaire. Discussion

In this study, we found no post‐procedural sleep–wake rhythm phase shift in children. Subjective sleep quality also did not deteriorate in the week after surgery or MRI requiring anaesthesia. Phase shift of the midpoint of sleep on the night after the procedure and sleep quality did not differ between patients who received anaesthesia for surgery or who received anaesthesia for MRI. We found that phase delay in children was associated with male sex, but not with chronotype or deterioration of sleep quality. Finally, we found that the midpoint of sleep, sleep duration and sleep offset time did vary over the study period when analysing the night‐to‐night differences of all 10 nights, but these variations were minor, resolved quickly and therefore probably have minimal clinical relevance.

Our results are not in line with those found in adult patients, who experience a clear phase advance of midpoint of sleep on the night before and two nights after surgery [9]. Although the patterns of peri‐operative sleep–wake rhythms found in this study are quite like those found in adults before and after elective surgery, the amplitude of the deflections is smaller and not significantly different from baseline. There are several potential explanations for this. First, young children need more sleep and have sleep–wake rhythms that are mostly guided by their parent/carer, possibly resulting in a more stable sleep–wake rhythm. Additionally, the procedures that the patients in our current study underwent were almost all non‐invasive or minimally invasive, whereas in our adult study [9] procedures were more invasive. This supports the hypothesis that the physical stress of surgery, including more severe postoperative pain and hospitalisation, might be a larger contributor to the diurnal phase shift of sleep–wake rhythm than anaesthesia. Lastly, our sample size could not detect phase changes < 17 min, although it is questionable whether these small changes would have any clinical or patient‐centred relevance.

We did not find a negative effect of anaesthesia or surgery on subjective sleep quality in the week post‐procedure. Several studies have been conducted on the subject, and results have been conflicting. One recent study did find sleep disturbances, both objectively and subjectively measured, in hospitalised children, although the night after surgery requiring anaesthesia was deliberately not measured, and all patients in this study were hospitalised [21]. Objective sleep measures seemed to deteriorate more after longer hospitalisation. They also found large discrepancies between subjective and objective sleep measures [21]. The differences between parent‐reported sleep and sleep measured objectively might explain why we did not find any sleep disturbances in our study. Another study measured sleep by actigraphy in a population of children undergoing low‐risk elective surgery (ENT/urology), similar to our population. They found no differences when comparing three 7‐day periods, measured at baseline (before surgery), the week after surgery and during a follow‐up period 3 months after surgery. They also found no differences in sleep when comparing their subjects with a healthy control group [20]. However, this study compared averages of actigraphy measurements over seven nights and, as a result, was not designed to detect subtle night‐to‐night changes in diurnal sleep–wake rhythm that disappear within a week. Two other studies using actigraphy found disturbances in the rest‐activity rhythm of children after surgery, with relatively more night‐time activity [18, 19]. However, both studies studied more invasive procedures and included nights spent on the PICU, where sleep disturbance is generally worse [3, 18]. More invasive procedures also tend to cause more postoperative pain, which seems to be the largest contributor to sleep disturbance during hospitalisation [21]. One study in children undergoing plastic surgery suggested that general anaesthesia led to worse sleep quality and a higher incidence of sleep disorders, with a higher risk of sleep disturbance in children undergoing multiple procedures [33], although day‐to‐day sleep–wake timing was not measured in this study.

Parents are often concerned about postoperative sleep disturbance due to anaesthesia. Our results indicate that these disturbances seem to be negligible after minor procedures. We observed the largest disruptions in sleep–wake rhythm were the night before the procedures, where sleep duration was shorter and sleep offset time was earlier. This, in conjunction with the result from the study from Burger et al. [21] that sleep disturbance increases during (prolonged) hospitalisation, could indicate that peri‐procedural sleep disruption is mostly the result of the hospital admission or the logistics of undergoing outpatient surgery, rather than the procedure itself or anaesthesia.

This study has several limitations. First, studying sleep in young children by self‐report from parents enables a larger number of participants, but is also limited by large discrepancies between subjective and objective sleep measures found in other studies [21]. Another note on the subjective measures we used is that the Children's Chronotype Questionnaire has not been validated in Dutch or for children aged < 4 y. To date, there is no questionnaire or other subjective way to measure chronotype in children aged 0–4 y. However, as younger children require more sleep than older children, we found that hypothesis‐generating data for this group were too important not to include in this study. In a sub‐analysis, we also did not find a difference between children aged < 4 y vs. those aged ≥ 4 y, although our study did not have an adequate sample size for this analysis. A last note on the questionnaires used is that, to increase compliance, we dropped the Morningness‐Eveningness Questionnaire part of the Children's Chronotype Questionnaire as the study progressed. However, an earlier study has shown that the Morningness‐Eveningness Questionnaire and midpoint of sleep as measured by the Munich Chronotype Questionnaire have a strong correlation [26], which led us to believe that measuring both would be redundant. Second, sending the questionnaires by e‐mail resulted in many patients with insufficient data for analysis, which we also found in earlier questionnaire studies with non‐hospitalised patients [9]. This could have introduced bias, as patients lost to follow‐up might have more sleep issues, resulting in their parents dropping out because of this, or may have had less sleep issues, resulting in their parents losing interest in following through with the study. However, we also found that there were no differences in the demographics of patients lost to follow up vs. patients included in our final analysis, except for a higher proportion of female patients in our final analysis. Third, almost all the patients in our surgical group underwent minor ambulatory surgery, and thus, our data cannot be extrapolated to patients undergoing major surgery. However, the pre‐anaesthetic sleep patterns found in our study are comparable with those found (at home) in earlier studies [21, 22], meaning that our results are most likely representative of a population of relatively healthy children undergoing minor procedures. Finally, with our small sample size, we could not control for all possible confounders in this study, such as the use of volatile vs. propofol anaesthesia; esketamine and steroid use during anaesthesia; use of pre‐medication directly before surgery; anxiety in children; and parents sleeping in the same room as their child during the trial, which could all influence diurnal rhythmicity and sleep in children [34]. With some of these factors, such as volatile vs. propofol anaesthesia and esketamine/steroid use, our sample size was too small to control for these factors properly. However, we found that esketamine/steroid use and pre‐operative hospital admission were more common in the surgical group, while we did not find differences between patients receiving anaesthesia for surgery vs. for MRI. We found that certain other factors, such as anxiety before induction and use of pre‐medication, were not noted accurately enough in the electronic patient record to control for these factors. We did collect data on neurodiversity and sleep‐altering medication from the electronic patient record and found a very low incidence of both these potential confounders. However, we should note that not all neurodiverse children would have been diagnosed at this young age and that not all sleep‐altering medication, especially over‐the‐counter products (e.g. melatonin) would be reported sufficiently in our electronic patient record.

In contrast with adult patients [5], the circadian timing system (including melatonin or cortisol serum rhythms) in children who require anaesthesia with and without surgery has not yet been studied. Future research in children should combine sleep–wake rhythm measures with other indicators of circadian phase. As the effects of anaesthesia alone or minimally invasive procedures on sleep–wake timing seem to be small, future research should also focus mainly on the relation between sleep and children undergoing more invasive (major) surgical procedures that also require longer postoperative hospital admission.

In conclusion, we found that, in contrast with adults [9], sleep–wake rhythm in children did not shift after minor surgical procedures requiring anaesthesia or MRI, and post‐procedural sleep quality did not deteriorate, which is reassuring for concerned parents. Further research is needed to see if the circadian timing system in children is completely unaffected by surgery and anaesthesia, or whether more invasive surgery causes a diurnal phase shift of sleep–wake rhythm and other sleep disturbances.
