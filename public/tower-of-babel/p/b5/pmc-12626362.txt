Case Presentation

A 31-year-old man presented to the dermatology clinic with a pink nodule on his head for 4 months. The nodule was initially the size of a bean and gradually increased to thumb-sized. It was smooth, lightly eroded, medium textured and had a moist feel [Figure 1]. Histopathological examination revealed a tumour mass consisting of numerous nested cells in the dermis that reached deep into subcutaneous tissue [Figure 2]. The tumour cells were positive for a range of melanocyte markers, including SOX10 [Figure 3]. Ten years ago, a biopsy of his back revealed melanoma, which received surgical resection and interferon. However, he did not get regular check-ups after the surgery.

Answer: Cutaneous metastatic malignant melanoma.

Discussion

Based on these clinical and histopathological findings, a diagnosis of cutaneous metastatic malignant melanoma was established. Eight months ago, he had received ineffective chemotherapy for a pulmonary nodule, which was considered ‘lung cancer sarcoma’. The diagnosis of cutaneous metastatic malignant melanoma and genetic testing of the pulmonary nodule corrected the diagnosis to lung metastatic malignant melanoma, with the tumour cells being BRAF V600E positive. The patient was then treated with dabrafenib, a BRAF inhibitor. He recovered well and is still being followed up.

Malignant melanoma is a group of malignant tumours originating from neural crest melanocytes that can metastasize to any system, most often to the skin and lungs. Metastatic malignant melanoma had a high mortality rate and the median overall survival for patients with metastases was 5.3 months. Its 5-year overall survival ranges between 5 and 19%, depending on the location and number of metastases and associated systemic changes (survival decreased if LDH values were elevated).[1] Cutaneous metastatic malignant melanoma presents clinically as solitary or clustered nodules, skin colour or red, and is highly susceptible to clinical misdiagnosis due to its lack of features.

Definitive diagnosis of metastatic malignant melanoma relies on the history of previous malignant melanoma and histopathological examination. The patient in question underwent surgery and chemotherapy at another hospital a decade ago, and no objective documentation (e.g., pathology reports) is available from that procedure. Our conclusion regarding the existence of prior malignant melanoma was based on the patient’s self-reported medical history and a thorough clinical evaluation during follow-up. Immunohistochemical techniques help to define the source of tumour cells. Histopathological features of cutaneous metastatic melanoma include thinning of the epidermis, widening of the papillary dermis by aggregates of atypical melanocytes, flanking epidermal collarette, lack of inflammation, monomorphism, and fibrotic stroma.[2] However, in rare cases, especially deeply invasive primary melanomas, these features are also present. While primary melanoma is treated with wide excision, metastatic malignant melanoma is often treated with systemic therapy. Approximately 50% of melanomas harbour activating BRAF mutations, and BRAFV600E is the most common.[3] The application of targeted therapies such as BRAF and MEK inhibitors dabrafenib and trametinib can significantly increase the median survival of patients. These emerging biologics have emerged as promising treatment options.[4]
