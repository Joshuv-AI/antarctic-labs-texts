Sos1 deficiency ameliorates oncogenic KRAS-mediated hematopoietic stem cell exhaustion and myeloid progenitor expansion
Authors: Maoshuo Yang, Lanlan Liu, Yaqing Miao, Yongxin Jia, Sijia Tian, Limei Wang, Fabao Liu, Xiaona You
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractConstitutive KRAS activating mutations are prevalent in hematopoietic malignancies. Our previous study showed that the deficiency of Sos1 prolongs the survival of KrasG12D/+ mice. However, whether Sos1 deletion ameliorates oncogenic Kras-mediated hematopoietic defects remains unknown. Here, we found that Sos1 deletion restored KrasG12D-mediated hematopoietic stem cell (HSC) and multipotent progenitor (MPP) exhaustion by maintaining quiescent HSC and MPP pools. Sos1 knockout attenuates hyperactivation of ERK signaling in KrasG12D/+ HSCs and MPPs. Additionally, the loss of Sos1 reduced the frequency and colony-forming capability of myeloid progenitors in KrasG12D/+ mice, resulting in a less severe myeloproliferative neoplasm phenotype. Moreover, Sos1 knockout prolonged the survival of KrasG12D/+ mice in a cell-autonomous manner. In general, cells with Sos1 deletion remained sensitive to MEK and JAK inhibition, suggesting that combined Sos1 inhibition and other therapies could be a promising strategy for the treatment of oncogenic KRAS-driven leukemia.Keywords: Sos1, Kras, Hematopoietic stem cell, Quiescent, ERK signaling

Introduction

Dysregulated RAS signaling is pervasive in all human cancers. Mutations in cancer-associated RAS impair the hydrolysis of GTP, resulting in the accumulation of the active RAS isoform (RAS-GTP) and hyperactivation of downstream signaling pathways such as RAF/MEK/ERK [1,2]. Mutations in KRAS and NRAS are particularly common in childhood leukemia and often occur during disease onset, progression, malignant transformation, and relapse [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Despite the high mutation rate of NRAS, KRAS mutations are closely associated with accelerated disease progression and poor prognosis in some types of childhood leukemia [7]. Juvenile myelomonocytic leukemia (JMML) is particularly aggressive in children with heterozygous KRAS mutations and often necessitates urgent bone marrow transplantation [8]. KRAS mutations, rather than NRAS mutations, are closely linked to poor prognosis in relapsed childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) [9]. Therefore, in-depth research into the pathogenic mechanisms induced by KRAS mutations in hematological malignancies and the exploration of new therapeutic targets have significant theoretical and clinical value.

SOS1 is the major RAS guanine nucleotide exchange factor (RAS-GEF) expressed in many types of cells [10]. The catalytic domain (SOS-cat) of SOS1 consists of an REM domain and a Cdc25 homologous domain. A crystallographic study of the SOS-cat domain indicated that the allosteric site binds to RAS-GTP and the catalytic site binds to inactive RAS (RAS-GDP) [11]. Our previous study reported that KRAS-GTP can cross-activate WT-Ras by binding to SOS1, enabling leukemic cells to further activate the ERK signaling pathway through this positive feedback mechanism [12]. Additionally, SOS1 serves as a node in the RAS signaling pathway regulation: the activation of the RAS pathway leads to SOS1 phosphorylation, reducing its GEF activity and thereby suppressing RAS signaling [13]. This negative feedback loop is one of the reasons why single agents, such as MEK/ERK inhibitors, show limited efficacy. Therefore, the combined inhibition of SOS1 and MEK may be more effective in treating hematologic malignancies driven by oncogenic KRAS.

Our previous study showed that KRASG12D binds to SOS1 and activates RAS signaling. Sos1 deletion improves KRASG12D-induced myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) phenotypes [12]. However, whether Sos1 deficiency ameliorates KRASG12D-mediated hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell (HSPCs) defects remains unknown. Here, we demonstrate that Sos1 knockout restores KrasG12D-induced hematopoietic stem cell (HSCs) depletion and prevents the expansion of myeloid progenitors (MPs), thereby prolonging the survival of KrasG12D/+ mice in a cell-autonomous manner. Our study elucidates the mechanism underlying the critical role of Sos1 in KRASG12D-induced hematological malignancy and provides a rationale for targeting the interaction between Sos1 and oncogenic Kras in cancer treatment.

Materials and methods

Mice

The mouse lines were maintained in a pure C57BL/6 genetic background (N > 10). The mating strategy was the same as previously reported [12]. CD45.1+ recipient mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. Six to eight week old mice (20–25 ​g, both male and female) were used. All animal experiments were conducted according to the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee at UW-Madison.

Flow cytometric analysis

For the lineage analysis of different hematopoietic tissues [14], myeloid progenitor (MP) in the bone marrow and spleen [15], and bone marrow and spleen HSC analysis [16] were performed as previously described. The cells were stained and analyzed using a FACSCalibur (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) or LSR II (BD Biosciences). Antibodies against B220 (RA3-6B2), CD19 (eBio1D3), Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), CD45.2 (104), Thy1.2 (53-2.1), Mac-1 (M1/70), CD8 (53-6.7), CD4 (GK1.5), CD3 (145-2C11), cKit (2B8), IgM (II/41), Sca-1 (D7), IL7Rα (A7R34), CD34 (RAM34), and TER119 (TER-119) were purchased from eBioscience (San Diego, CA, USA). FcγRII/III (2.4G2) was purchased from BD Biosciences. CD150 (TC15-12F12.2) was purchased from Biolegend (Sand Diego, CA, USA).

Flow cytometry analysis of p-ERK1/2 and p-Stat5

Total bone marrow cells were deprived of serum and cytokines for 2 ​h and then stimulated with or without cytokines as previously described [17]. ERK1/2 and STAT5 phosphorylation levels were analyzed as described previously [17]. Surface antigens were detected using FITC-against Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), B220 (RA3-6B2), CD4 (GK1.5), CD3 (145-2C11), CD8 (53-6.7), TER119 (TER-119), and PE-conjugated anti-c-Kit (2B8) antibodies (eBioscience).

Flow cytometric analysis of phosphorylated ERK1/2 in HSCs and MPPs was conducted as previously described [18]. Briefly, Sca1+ cells were enriched in BM using biotin beads. And then CD150+CD41− and CD150−CD41− cells were sorted. The sorted cells were subjected to flow cytometric analysis of p-ERK and p-Stat5.

Primary antibodies against p-ERK (Thr202/Tyr204; Cell signaling Technology), APC-donkey anti-rabbit F(ab’)2 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA), and Alexa 647-p-Stat5 (BD Biosciences) were used.

Cell cycle analysis

Cell cycle analysis was conducted as previously described [19]. First, HSCs and MPPs were labeled using the following antibodies: Biotin-CD41 (eBioMWReg30), B220 (RA3-6B2), CD48 (HM48-1), TER-119 (TER-119), and Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), followed by APC-CD150 (TC15-12F12.2), PE-Cy7 Streptavidin, PE-Kit (2B8), and PerCP Cy5.5-Sca1 (D7). Lin− Kit+ Sca1+ (LSK) and MP cells were labeled by antibodies: Biotin-B220 (RA3-6B2), CD4 (GK1.5), Gr-1 (RB6-8C5), CD3 (145-2C11), CD8 (53-6.7), CD19 (eBio1D3), CD127 (A7R34), TER-119 (TER-119), IgM and APC-Kit (2B8), PerCP Cy5.5-Sca1 (D7). The stained cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA), permeabilized, and labeled with FITC-Ki67 (BD Biosciences) and DAPI (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). All antibodies used were purchased from eBioscience unless specified otherwise.

Colony forming analysis

One million control, KrasG12D/+ or KrasG12D/+; Sos1−/− splenocytes were plated in duplicate in MethoCult M3234 semi-solid medium (StemCell Technologies, Vancouver, BC, Canada) supplemented with or without purified mGM-CSF or mIL-3 (PeproTech, Cranbury, NJ, USA) using a 6 ​mL Luer lock syringe attached to a 16-gauge blunt-end needle. Colonies were counted 7 days after culture.

Splenocytes transplantation and drug treatment

Spleen cells were isolated from KrasG12D/+ and KrasG12D/+; Sos1−/− mice. 2 ​× ​106 splenocytes (CD45.2+) along with 2.5 ​× ​105 BM cells (helper cells, CD45.1+) were injected into lethally irradiated CD45.1+ mice, as previously described [20]. Peripheral blood was collected every four weeks after treatment to monitor the development of hematopoietic malignancies.

Bone marrow cells from moribund KrasG12D/+ and KrasG12D/+; Sos1−/− mice were cultured in SFEM medium (StemCell Technologies) containing 10% FBS and 0.2 ​ng/ml mouse GM-CSF (PeprotTech). Cells were cultured in 6-well plates in a medium containing DMSO and/or MEK (AZD6244, ChemieTek, Indianapolis, IN, USA) and JAK inhibitors (CYT387, ChemieTek) for 2 ​h, and p-ERK1/2 and p-STAT5 were detected using Phosflow.

Data analysis, statistics, and reproducibility

The Prism 9.4.1 software (GraphPad, USA) was used for statistical analyses, and data are shown as mean ​± ​SD. Experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times. Statistical testing was performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test and/or ANOVA, assuming significance at P ​< ​0.05.

Results and discussion

HSCs and MPPs in KrasG12D/+; Sos1−/− mice are more quiescent than those in KrasG12D/+ mice

To determine whether Sos1 deficiency could restore KRASG12D/+-mediated hematopoietic defects, we generated KrasG12D/+; Vav-Cre (Kras) and KrasG12D/+; Sos1fl/fl; Vav-Cre mice (Kras; Sos1−/−). Vav-Cre (C) mice served as controls (Fig. 1A). Consistent with the previously reported acute myeloproliferative neoplasm (MPN) phenotype, Kras mice displayed expanded myeloid compartments (Mac1+Gr1– monocytes and Mac1+Gr1+ neutrophils) at age 6 weeks. The numbers of neutrophils in various hematopoietic tissues were lower in Kras; Sos1−/− mice compared to those in Kras mice. This finding is consistent with previously reported lower white blood cell counts and less splenomegaly in Kras; Sos1−/− mice [12], indicating that the loss of Sos1 suppresses MPN progression in KrasG12D/+ mice (Fig. 1B). Since mutant HSCs serve as MPN-initiating cells in KrasG12D/+ mice, we examined the HSC compartment in 6 weeks old mice. Consistent with a previous study [21], the frequency and number of HSCs in the BM of KrasG12D/+ mice were significantly lower than those in controls, whereas the HSC frequency and number in KrasG12D/+ spleens were much higher. Notably, the KRAS-mediated HSC depletion phenotype in the BM was rescued in Kras; Sos1−/− mice (Fig. 1C). Similarly, the decreased MPP compartment in KrasG12D/+ BM cells was restored by Sos1 knockout (Fig. 1D). However, due to the milder splenomegaly [12], the numbers of HSCs and MPPs in the spleen of Kras; Sos1−/− mice were lower than those in Kras mice, despite a slightly higher percentage (Fig. 1C and D).

To investigate whether the loss of Sos1 affects oncogenic KRAS-mediated HSC proliferation, cell cycle analysis of BM cells was performed. Our results showed that both HSCs and MPPs in the BM of KrasG12D/+ mice were more proliferative than those in control mice, resulting in the exhaustion of the HSC and MPP pools. Interestingly, increased frequencies of G0 phase-HSCs and MPPs were observed in the BM of Kras; Sos1−/− mice, suggesting that loss of Sos1 maintains a quiescent HSPC pool, thereby preventing HSPC exhaustion (Fig. 1E and F). These results revealed that loss of Sos1 rescued hematopoietic abnormalities in KRASG12D/+-mediated hematopoietic malignancies.

Sos1 function is indispensable for KRASG12D-mediated downstream ERK1/2 signaling

Since ERK1/2 signaling is the main downstream target of KRAS, we investigated whether the loss of Sos1 affects ERK signaling in HSCs and MPPs. Consistent with our previous publication [21], ERK1/2 signaling in KrasG12D/+ HSCs and MPPs was more hyperactivated than in control cells. As expected, the hyperactivated ERK1/2 signaling of HSCs and MPPs was suppressed in Kras; Sos1−/− mice, although remained higher than that in control cells, which is consistent with the high KRAS-GTP level after Sos1 deletion [12] (Fig. 2A and B). HSPCs are tightly regulated by cytokines; therefore, we investigated whether the loss of Sos1 disturbs cytokine-induced ERK1/2 signaling activation. Interleukin-3 (IL-3) is a major early hematopoietic growth factor that plays a crucial role in hematopoietic regulation. Indeed, IL-3-stimulated ERK activation was significantly reduced in Kras; Sos1−/− HSCs and MPPs compared to that in Kras cells (Fig. 2A and B). Our data indicate that Sos1 knockout rescues HSC and MPP exhaustion, at least partially, by modulating ERK1/2 signaling.

Sos1 deficiency reduces colony-forming capability of KrasG12D/+ myeloid progenitor cells

Our previous study showed that granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF)-stimulated ERK1/2 and STAT5 activation were greatly reduced in myeloid progenitors (MPs) and myeloid precursors (MPres) in Kras;Sos1−/− mice compared with those in Kras mice [12]. However, whether the loss of Sos1 affects the Lin-c-Kit+ Sca-1+ (LSK) and MP compartments in Kras mice remains unknown. Therefore, we analyzed the LSK, MP, granulocyte-macrophage progenitor (GMP), and megakaryocyte-erythroid progenitor (MEP) compartments. The manifestation of MPN phenotypes is primarily driven by the rapid proliferation of MPs. Consistent with the milder splenomegaly of Kras;Sos1−/− mice, the numbers of LSKs, MPs, GMPs, and MEPs were significantly reduced in the spleen, despite comparable percentages in Kras and Kras;Sos1−/− mice (Fig. 3A–D). Similar to HSCs, the percentages of LSKs and MPs in the BM of Kras;Sos1−/− mice were higher than those of Kras mice. Cell cycle assays revealed increased quiescence in LSKs and MPs from Kras;Sos1−/− mice compared to those from Kras mice (Fig. 3E and F). Correspondingly, the notable increase in the frequency of MPs in the spleen was confirmed by colony forming assays, where Kras cells formed more colonies than control cells with or without cytokine stimulation, while Kras;Sos1−/− cells formed fewer colonies than Kras cells, indicating a reduced percentage of functional MPs in the spleen of Kras;Sos1−/− mice (Fig. 3G). Taken together, our results demonstrate that Sos1 deficiency decreases the proportion of proliferative and functional MPs, thereby contributing to the less severe MPN phenotype observed in KrasG12D/+ mice.

Sos1 deficiency prolongs the survival of KrasG12D/+ mice in a cell-autonomous manner

We used a splenocyte transplantation approach to study whether Sos1 affects KRASG12D-induced leukemogenesis in a cell-autonomous manner. An equal number of Kras or Kras; Sos1−/− splenocytes (CD45.2+) were transplanted into lethally irradiated recipient mice (CD45.1+), along with helper cells (CD45.1+). The survival of recipients transplanted with Kras; Sos1−/− cells was much longer than that of recipients transplanted with Kras cells (Fig. 4A). Since the HSC frequency in Kras; Sos1−/− splenocytes was comparable to that in Kras splenocytes (Fig. 1A), all recipients displayed a similar contribution of CD45.2+ (donor-derived) cells in the peripheral blood (Fig. 4B). Our previous studies showed that both Kras and Kras; Sos1−/− mice develop MPN. Consistent with this, our transplantation experiments demonstrated that MPN and T-ALL occurred in almost all Kras and Kras; Sos1−/− transplant recipients (Fig. 4C–E). Overall, our data suggested that Sos1 deficiency plays a cell-autonomous role in inhibiting leukemogenesis in KrasG12D/+ mice.

Combined MEK and JAK inhibition decreases GM-CSF-stimulated ERK1/2 and STAT5 signaling in both Kras and Kras; Sos1−/− cells

Our data suggest that Sos1 is indispensable for the activation of cytokine-stimulated ERK1/2 and STAT5 signaling in oncogenic KRAS-induced leukemogenesis. Moreover, we previously reported that a combination of MEK and JAK inhibitors can abrogate oncogenic NRAS-induced MPN [16]. Therefore, we investigated whether cells with Sos1 deletion remained sensitive to MEK and JAK inhibition. We used AZD6244 and CYT387 to treat the BM cells of Kras and Kras; Sos1−/− mice. The results showed that the combination of JAK and MEK inhibitors significantly reduced GM-CSF-stimulated ERK1/2 and STAT5 signaling activation in both Kras and Kras; Sos1−/− cells (Fig. 5A and B), which is consistent with our previous finding that combined treatment can prolong the survival of Kras; Sos1−/− mice [12]. From a clinical perspective, these findings indicate that the combination of Sos1 inhibition and other therapies could be a promising strategy for treating oncogenic KRAS-driven leukemia. However, further studies are required to test the potential clinical implications of this therapeutic strategy.

Although several SOS1 inhibitors and degraders have been developed [22,23], available SOS1 inhibitors are scarce. In addition, SOS1 inhibitors targeting its catalytic domain may affect physiological MEK/ERK signaling in normal tissues/cells, leading to significant toxicity. Hence, the identification of new targets and establishment of viable targeting strategies remain imperative. The allosteric site on SOS1 can specifically bind to high concentrations of KRAS-GTP in cancer cells, thereby cross-activating wild-type NRAS and HRAS to activate the downstream ERK signaling pathways. Targeting the SOS1 allosteric site may effectively inhibit cancer cell growth with minimal toxicity to normal cells containing low levels of KRAS-GTP. Our future focus will be to explore new inhibitors targeting the Sos1 allosteric site.

Conclusion

Previously, we reported that Sos1 deletion attenuated the hyperactivation of wild-type NRAS and HRAS, thereby ameliorating KRAS-induced MPN phenotypes and prolonging the survival of KrasG12D mice [12]. However, whether Sos1 knockout can rescue KRAS-mediated defects in hematopoietic compartments remains unclear. In this study, we demonstrate that Sos1 deletion rescues oncogenic KRAS-mediated HSC exhaustion by maintaining a quiescent HSC pool. In both the HSC and MPP compartments of Kras mice, Sos1 deletion significantly downregulated ERK signaling. Previous studies have demonstrated that robust Ras/ERK signaling can shift HSC self-renewal toward differentiation, leading to HSC exhaustion [16]. A similar mechanism may apply to HSCs in Kras mice. Thus, we attributed the rescue of oncogenic Kras-mediated HSC exhaustion to the inhibition of ERK signaling caused by Sos1 deletion. Additionally, the loss of Sos1 reduced the frequency and colony-forming capability of KrasG12D/+ MPs, contributing to a less severe MPN phenotype. Moreover, Sos1−/− prolongs the survival of KrasG12D/+ mice in a cell-autonomous manner. Our data elucidated the mechanism by which Sos1 deficiency ameliorates KRAS-induced MPN (Fig. 6). Notably, cells with Sos1 deletion remained sensitive to MEK and JAK inhibition, suggesting that targeting SOS1 could be combined with other targeted therapies for the treatment of KRASG12D-driven leukemia. Our findings provide a strong rationale for targeting the SOS1-KRAS-GTP interaction to treat oncogenic Kras-driven cancers.
