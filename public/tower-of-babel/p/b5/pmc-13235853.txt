Pulmonary Artery Sarcoma Study: A Multi-Centre European Society of Thoracic Surgeons Working Group Evaluating Diagnosis, Treatment, and Outcomes
Authors: Olivia Lauk, Bianca Battilana, Ulrike Held, Claudia Lourenço Rodrigues, Martina Haberecker, Kathrin Chiffi, Tom Verbelen, Marc de Perrot, Bedrettin Yildizeli, Lanuti Michael, Bernhard Moser, Stefan Schwarz, Hussein Nasser Awada, René Horsleben Petersen, Isabelle Opitz

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPulmonary artery sarcoma (PAS) is a rare and aggressive malignancy often mimicking pulmonary embolism, leading to diagnostic delays and poor outcomes. This study aims to describe the clinical characteristics, diagnostic pathways, treatment modalities, and outcomes of PAS patients in a multicentre cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included patients from 7 institutions between 1994 and 2024. Clinical, radiological, histological, and treatment data were collected via a standardized REDCap-based platform. All patients underwent either multimodal treatment or single therapy approach, including systemic therapy, surgery, and/or radiotherapy. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank testing were performed to assess overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS).Results84 patients from 7 international centres were analysed. The mean age was 54.4 years, and 43.8% of patients were female. The most common presenting symptoms were dyspnoea (81.2%) and chest pain (50.0%). Pulmonary embolism was the most frequent initial misdiagnosis (49.1%). Histologically, PAS was diagnosed in 46 cases. Tumours were bilateral in 58.1% of patients with surgery only. Most commonly, endarterectomy was performed in 59.5%. After a median follow-up time of 39 months, 19 were excluded as they either had no treatment information or received monotherapy only. Additionally, 11 patients were excluded due to missing date of diagnosis and 17 due to missing overall follow-up date. There was moderate evidence that OS differed between treatment groups (75th quantile: 34 months for multimodal [95% CI, 26 to not reached] vs 11 months for surgery only [95% CI, 7-21]; log-rank P = .022). There was no evidence that PFS differed between treatment groups (median 19 months for multimodal [95% CI, 14-39] vs 16 months surgery only [95% CI, 11-21]; log-rank P = .69).ConclusionsThis study represents the largest reported PAS cohort to date and underscores the diagnostic challenges and poor prognosis associated with this disease. Our findings offer evidence that multimodal treatment provides significant prognostic benefits. However, further prospective studies are warranted to establish standardized diagnostic and therapeutic protocols.

Introduction

Primary pulmonary artery sarcoma (PAS) is an exceedingly rare (0.8-3 cases per year in expert centres) and aggressive malignancy of mesenchymal origin, first described in 1923 during autopsy.1 The disease typically presents with non-specific cardiopulmonary symptoms—such as dyspnoea, chest pain, cough, and haemoptysis—that closely mimic pulmonary embolism, often leading to delayed diagnosis with considerable variation, with reported median and mean delays ranging from 3 to 9 months.2–6 Imaging modalities such as CT angiography, cardiac MRI, and PET/CT are crucial in differentiating PAS from acute and chronic thromboembolic disease, with characteristic findings such as the “wall eclipsing sign” and increased FDG (Fluorodeoxyglucose) uptake aiding in identification.7 Nevertheless, definitive diagnosis requires histopathological confirmation. Pulmonary artery sarcoma is associated with rapid clinical deterioration and poor prognosis, with median overall survival (OS) reported between 6.6 and 36.5 months depending on disease stage and treatment approach.8,9 At the time of diagnosis, 30%-50% of patients already present with metastatic disease, most commonly to the lungs. The tumour predominantly arises in the right pulmonary artery, often involving the pulmonary valve, resulting in significant haemodynamic compromise.10,11 Management of PAS is complex and demands a multimodal and individualized treatment strategy.8,9 Surgical resection remains the mainstay of therapy, with approaches including lobectomy, or pneumonectomy and pulmonary tumour endarterectomy, the latter requiring cardiopulmonary bypass.12–15 Neoadjuvant chemotherapy may be considered for selected patients with favourable performance status.16 Complete surgical resection is associated with improved survival but is technically demanding and should be attempted only in high-volume, specialized centres.17,18 Due to the lack of standardized treatment protocols, multidisciplinary decision-making within dedicated sarcoma tumour boards is strongly recommended.

Given the rarity of PAS and the heterogeneity of treatment approaches, data are scarce and primarily limited to single-centre retrospective reports. In response, we have initiated an European Society of Thoracic Surgeons (ESTS) working group and launched a multicentre international study (PULS study) to establish a shared database aiming to collect and analyse surgical and multimodal treatment outcomes in patients with PAS. By pooling institutional experiences, we aim to improve the management of PAS, refine treatment strategies, and ultimately contribute towards the development of future standardized clinical guidelines for this orphan disease.

Methods

ESTS Working Group

The ESTS Pulmonary Artery Sarcoma Working Group (PULS) was established in 2023 to coordinate this study across multiple centres. A detailed description of the consortium and its structure is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Text S1).

Ethical statement

The study was approved by the local ethics committee of the leading site in Switzerland (BASEC-Nr. 2021-00882) and by the respective boards of all participating centres. Due to the retrospective design, informed consent was waived at most sites (Supplementary Text S2).

Patients and methods

Inclusion criteria were patients with histologically confirmed PAS diagnosed between 1994 and 2024. Data were collected via a REDCap-based platform and included demographic details, clinical presentation, imaging, histology, treatment, and survival outcomes (Figure 1).

The Study Cohort and Treatment Stratification of Patients With Pulmonary Artery Sarcoma (PAS) From 7 International Centres (1994-2024). Abbreviation: PFS, progression-free survival.

Treatment modalities were categorized according to clinical intent and timing. Surgical procedures included tumour endarterectomy, pneumonectomy, debulking, and, in selected cases, lobectomy, depending on the extent and location of the tumour. Surgical procedures were performed at high-volume, specialized centres with expertise in complex pulmonary vascular and oncologic surgery.

Multimodal treatment was defined as surgery combined with either chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy as neo- and/or adjuvant adjacent therapy, as well as chemotherapy combined with radiotherapy (n = 1).

Adjuvant therapy encompassed systemic chemotherapy. The choice of systemic agents was centre-specific and based on institutional protocols or clinical judgement. In general, therapeutic agents included doxorubicin, ifosfamide, docetaxel, gemcitabine, paclitaxel, dacarbazine, and other treatments.

Outcomes

The primary endpoint was OS, defined as the time from diagnosis to death from any cause, serving as the main criterion to evaluate the most effective treatment approach. In addition, median OS will be reported and compared across predefined treatment subgroups, including neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery, surgery alone, surgery with adjuvant therapy, and systemic therapy alone. The secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), assessed through imaging (CT, PET-CT, or MRI of the chest) during structured 6-monthly follow-up visits. Progression-free survival was defined as the time from diagnosis to either documented disease progression or death, whichever occurred first.19 Patients without an event were censored at the time of last follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and clinical variables. Continuous variables were reported as means with SDs; categorical variables were presented as absolute and relative frequencies. Missing data were documented separately for each variable.

Time-to-event analyses were conducted to assess the impact of treatment modality on patient outcomes. Patients without an event were censored at the date of last follow-up.

Patients were grouped according to whether they received surgery only or multimodal treatment, which combines surgery with neoadjuvant/adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were used to visualize survival probabilities over time. Median follow-up times were estimated using the reversed KM method, and survival times were reported separately for each treatment group, whenever possible. Differences between groups were tested using the log-rank test.

Time-to-event analyses and the estimation of a treatment effect of multimodal as compared to surgery-only treatment were limited by the small numbers of patients with follow-up and other missing values in potential confounders. KM curves are only shown as descriptive. Further, an unadjusted hazard ratio for the multimodal vs surgery-only treatment group was estimated. We also evaluated whether the inclusion of the variable age in the model would change the treatment effect estimate.

All analyses were performed with R version 4.4.3 (2025-02-28), in combination with dynamic reporting for reproducibility.

Additionally, 11 patients were excluded due to missing date of diagnosis, and 17 due to missing overall follow-up date (Figure 1).

Adjuvant chemotherapy with doxorubicin and ifosfamide was administered in 60% in the multimodal group, and 53.3% received radiotherapy. A minority received neoadjuvant treatment (20%).

In 11 patients, time of diagnosis was not available. The median follow-up time across the remaining patients was 39 months (Figure S3).

Overall survival

The 75th quantile of OS time was 11 months (95% CI, 7-21) in the surgery-only group and 34 months (95% CI, 26 to not reached) in the multimodal treatment group. Overall, patients treated with surgery only appear to have lower survival probabilities over time compared to those receiving multimodal treatment. However, the confidence bands are notably wide and overlapping due to the limited number of events observed in each group, reflecting the uncertainty in the estimates. The results of a log-rank test reveal a P-value of .022, indicating moderate evidence against the null hypothesis of no difference between the 2 treatment groups (Figure 2). We found that the treatment effect estimate did not change when age was added to the model.

Progression-free survival

A total of 32 patients experienced disease progression or death, while 5 did not show signs of progression during the observation period. The median PFS time was 16 months (95% CI, 11-21) in the surgery-only group and 19 months (95% CI, 14-39) in the multimodal treatment group. The results of a log-rank test revealed a P-value of .69, indicating no evidence against the null hypothesis of no difference between the 2 treatment groups (Figure 3).

Discussion

This international, multicentre cohort represents, to the best of our knowledge, the largest known collection of patients diagnosed with PAS to date (Table S1).2,17,20–34 Our study confirms the diagnostic challenge and poor prognosis of PAS. The heterogeneity in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches across centres calls for standardized care pathways and prospective registries. Its frequent clinical mimicry of pulmonary embolism continues to be a critical barrier to timely diagnosis and effective treatment, as this was the initial diagnosis in 49% of our patients in this cohort. The main symptom patients presented with was dyspnoea in 81%, followed by cough and chest pain with 51% and 50%, respectively. Pulmonary artery sarcomas are rare, highly malignant tumours that originate from the pulmonary artery and are frequently misdiagnosed due to nonspecific clinical and radiological features; 38% of the diagnosis were obtained through surgery, which shows the limited possibilities to obtain diagnosis otherwise for this disease, as well as the limited significance for imaging modalities. According to the literature, while advanced imaging modalities—particularly PET-CT and cardiac MRI—can support differentiation from thromboembolic disease, a high degree of clinical suspicion remains essential.32,33 The identification of the wall-eclipsing sign and increased FDG uptake are reported to be consistent findings that should alert clinicians of PAS, especially in cases of non-resolving or chronic embolism.32,33

Surgical treatment remains the cornerstone of therapy.12,15,34 In our cohort, various surgical approaches were applied, ranging from endarterectomy to pneumonectomy, depending on the extent and location of disease. Despite aggressive treatment strategies, long-term outcomes remain poor, with high rates of recurrence and limited OS.9,17,33 In our multi-institutional and international cohort, radical surgical resection appears to show survival benefit, underscoring the importance of early and aggressive management. Adjuvant therapy showed an uncertain impact, highlighting the lack of standardized treatment protocols and the need for further research.

There are currently no dedicated European or international society guidelines specifically for the management of PAS. Due to the extreme rarity of this malignancy, management recommendations are based on expert consensus, case series, and extrapolation from general soft tissue sarcoma guidelines.9,17,33 According to the current literature, complete surgical resection with negative margins is the mainstay of treatment for PAS, as this offers the best chance for prolonged survival.2,9,12–15,17,18

Adjuvant chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy may be considered, particularly in cases with incomplete resection or high risk of recurrence, but there is no consensus on specific regimens or their efficacy due to the lack of prospective data. Radiotherapy was typically administered for palliative or adjuvant purposes. While it provided symptomatic relief in selected cases, its effect on long-term survival remains unclear and was not evaluated as a standalone modality in this analysis.

In general, multimodal therapy (surgery plus [neo-]adjuvant treatment) is associated with improved survival compared to single-modality therapy in our cohort. The choice of chemotherapeutic agents is generally guided by protocols for soft tissue sarcomas, as outlined in international expert opinion documents.35

European policy recommendations for sarcoma care stress the importance of multidisciplinary management in specialized centres, access to clinical trials, and individualized treatment planning for all sarcoma patients, including those with rare subtypes like PAS.36,37

In summary, management should be individualized, with surgical resection as the cornerstone, and adjuvant therapy considered on a case-by-case basis in a multidisciplinary setting. No formal society guidelines exist for this entity, and treatment decisions should be made in expert centres with sarcoma experience.

In our cohort, the majority of cases were intimal sarcomas (83%, n = 39), with leiomyosarcoma (12.8%, n = 6) and primary pulmonary myxoid sarcoma (2.1%, n = 1) representing rarer subtypes. Pulmonary artery leiomyosarcomas represent a significant proportion of PASs.10,11,34,38 These tumours are characterized by spindle cell morphology and immunohistochemical expression of smooth muscle markers, including desmin, α-smooth muscle actin, and caldesmon, which help distinguish them from other sarcoma subtypes.38–40 Several case series and reports suggest that patients with pulmonary artery leiomyosarcoma may experience longer survival compared to other histological variants.26–29 Unfortunately, our subgroup was too small for proper statistical evaluation to undermine this finding.

In general, our outcomes strengthen the concept that a multimodal approach, incorporating surgery, (neo-)adjuvant therapy, and careful follow-up, may offer the best outcomes. However, clinical variability in treatment pathways across institutions underlines the urgent need for centralized treatment algorithms and prospective registries.

Limitations

Being retrospective, the study is subject to selection and information bias. Missing data and heterogeneity in treatment protocols limit the generalizability. The rarity of the disease also restricts statistical power for subgroup analyses. The heterogeneity of treatment strategies across centres and time periods further complicates direct comparisons. Unfortunately, as our data go back over 30 years, a lot of therapeutic details, such as treatment allocation for surgery alone, were not recorded in a standardized manner. Lastly, while imaging and pathology were reviewed based on site-specific standards, a centralized radiological and histopathological review was not yet performed, which may affect diagnostic uniformity.

Conclusion

Pulmonary artery sarcoma remains a rare, aggressive disease that is frequently misdiagnosed and associated with poor outcomes. Early identification through advanced imaging techniques and improved clinical suspicion is essential.

The PULS collaboration presents the largest dataset on PAS to date. Radical resection improves outcomes and should be pursued when feasible. There is an urgent need for treatment to be centralized in centres of excellence with a dedicated sarcoma team. Future prospective studies are needed to refine diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

This study also includes a prospective component, and we are eager to see whether this prospective addition will provide more timely and clinically relevant results at a defined future timepoint.
