Epilepsy and systemic autoimmune diseases: A bidirectional two‐sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ruoshi Tian, Wenlong Yu, Jiaqing Hu, Yan Yang, Xirui He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEpidemiological data indicates that individuals with epilepsy exhibit an elevated susceptibility to autoimmune disorders, and conversely, those with systemic autoimmune diseases (SAD) demonstrate a heightened predisposition to epilepsy. Although an increasing number of publications support this association, the causal direction remains undetermined. This investigation offers evidence supporting a causal relationship between these conditions through a bidirectional two‐sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis.MethodsBidirectional two‐sample MR analyses were performed to investigate the causal relationships and directionality between various epilepsy subtypes and eight SAD. The analysis utilized genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary data for the following conditions: type 1 diabetes, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), rheumatoid arthritis, coeliac disease, multiple sclerosis (MS), ulcerative colitis, and Crohn's disease.ResultsIn this study, causal analyses were performed for different epilepsy subtypes and SAD disorders. In the Inverse‐Variance Weighted (IVW) model, the MR study indicated that focal epilepsy increased the risk of IBD [odds ratio (OR): 1.15, confidence interval (CI): 1.02–1.30, p: 0.027] and MS [OR: 1.25, CI: 1.07–1.47, p: 0.004]. Childhood absence epilepsy increased the risk of MS [OR: 0.25, CI: 0.07–0.90, p: 0.034]. Reverse analyses showed that SLE increased the risk of myoclonic epilepsy in adolescents [OR: 1.01, CI: 1.00–1.01, p: 0.042], and IBD [OR: 0.99, CI: 0.99–1.00, p: 0.013], and celiac disease [OR: 0.99, CI: 0.99–1.00, p: 0.021] reduced the risk of generalized epilepsy. The results demonstrated homogeneity, and the MR‐Egger directions were concordant.SignificanceThis study shows that epilepsy is a causal risk factor for both IBD and MS, providing new insights into the prevention of SAD in people with epilepsy.Plain Language SummaryThis paper employs a bidirectional two‐sample MR analysis to investigate the genetic correlation between epilepsy and autoimmune diseases, revealing potential associations between the onset of epilepsy and MS as well as IBD. Further exploration in this area requires support from clinical data.

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a major neurological condition affecting approximately 70 million people worldwide. 1 , 2 Epilepsy is most common in young children 3 and the elderly. 4 It not only increases the financial burden on families 5 and reduces their well‐being 6 but also increases the risk of premature death for patients. 7 Recent increases in mortality 8 and disability rates associated with epilepsy 9 have had a significant impact on patients' quality of life. 10 Additionally, the risk of suicide, 11 depression, 12 and anxiety in people 13 with epilepsy is several times higher than in the general population. It is estimated that over 50% of people with epilepsy are estimated to have underlying comorbidities, 1 such as type 1 diabetes 14 and peptic ulcer disease. 15 These comorbidities significantly increase the risk of mortality in people with epilepsy. 16 Mothers with autoimmune system disorders have an elevated risk of epilepsy in their offspring, which can result in increased financial pressure on the family unit and deterioration in the quality of life experienced by the family. 17 The administration of certain pharmaceutical agents, including phenytoin sodium, to patients diagnosed with epilepsy has been demonstrated to exacerbate pre‐existing immune disorders. 18

Neuronal antibodies (e.g., NMDA and LGLL), 19 neuroinflammatory responses, 20 and systemic immune activation 21 are increasingly recognized as causes of immune dysregulation and potential co‐morbid mechanisms for epilepsy and systemic autoimmune diseases (SAD). 22 There is growing evidence for a potential link between immune dysregulation and seizures. 23 , 24 Notably, some cases of epilepsy manifest before the onset of SAD. 25 However, the majority of seizures occur within the first 1–2 years following an SAD diagnosis. 26 A study revealed a 2.5‐fold elevated risk of autoimmune diseases in individuals diagnosed with epilepsy. 27 The American Rheumatism Association has highlighted the association between epilepsy and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE). 28 Patients with multiple sclerosis (MS) are predisposed to dysmetropic epilepsy, and the likelihood of recurrent seizures and epilepsy diagnosis has increased significantly. 29 However, there is a paucity of research examining the association between MS and various forms of epilepsy. 30 However, the findings regarding the association between epilepsy and rheumatoid arthritis (RA) remain controversial. 31 , 32 Current research into the relationship between epilepsy and SAD remains inadequate. Misdiagnosis is an important issue; for example, hypoglycemia in people with Type 1 diabetes may be mistaken for seizures. 33 , 34 Therefore, it is crucial to identify whether immune disease‐induced epilepsy or hypoglycemia or lupus encephalopathy is involved in a patient's treatment, and correct medication will prevent recurrent seizures or even exacerbation of epilepsy in the patient. Furthermore, the potential association between epilepsy and cryptic celiac disease‐positive antibodies has not been confirmed. 35 Duodenal biopsies have not shown this association, so the possibility remains unproven. Further research is needed to better understand the relationship between epilepsy and SAD.

A substantial body of research has previously been dedicated to the examination of the impact of SAD on the risk of developing epilepsy. However, a paucity of research has been dedicated to investigating whether epilepsy can act as a counteragent to SAD. This unidirectional perspective has the consequence of limiting a comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms by which these two conditions co‐occur. Furthermore, preliminary studies analyzed SAD and epilepsy in their totality, without distinguishing the specific associations between the different subtypes of the two diseases. This resulted in mechanism explanations that are prone to generalization. This study aimed to explore the potential causal relationship between epilepsy and SAD using bidirectional two‐sample MR analysis. The MR method, similar to randomized trials, is employed to investigate causal relationships between modifiable exposures or risk factors and disease. 36 , 37 If the hypotheses from the MR analysis are valid and the assumptions regarding genetic variation in the instrumental variables are plausible, it is possible to determine whether a causal relationship exists between the exposure or risk factor and the disease. Bidirectional two‐sample MR analyses can effectively study the potential impact of SAD on epilepsy risk and vice versa.

METHODS

Study overview

The aim of this study was to investigate the causal relationship between SAD and epilepsy using a bidirectional two‐sample MR analyses approach. Eight diagnostic subtypes of SAD were selected, and their genome‐wide association study (GWAS) summary statistics were retrieved from publicly available databases. The initial study adhered to the ethical standards established by the relevant institutional review board, and informed consent was obtained from the participants. Instrumental variable selection

To ensure accurate results in the bidirectional two‐sample MR analyses of epilepsy with comorbid SAD, the most appropriate instrumental variables are selected based on three criteria (IVs): (1) to identify IVs strongly associated with exposure, increase the number of SNPs, and improve statistical efficacy, a significance threshold of p < 5.0 × 10−6 was applied. (2) To identify independent variables unassociated with potential confounders, chain imbalances (r 2 < 0.001, kb = 10 000) were removed. (3) The most robust instrumental variables were selected by calculating their F‐statistics, excluding those with values below 10. 48

MR analysis

The inverse variance‐weighted (IVW) MR method is the primary approach for identifying potential associations between SAD and epilepsy. Several additional tests were performed to assess the stability of the IVW results. These included the MR‐PRESSO, the weighted median, the heterogeneity test, the MR‐Egger regression heterogeneity test, and the Cochrane Q test. 44 , 45 MR‐PRESSO is a software program that was developed to ensure the reliability of MR analysis results. It detects outliers and corrects for pleiotropic bias in MR studies. Multiplicity bias is defined as the phenomenon by which genetic variants exert an influence on results not only through exposure factors but also via alternative pathways. Heterogeneity tests were used to evaluate the consistency of results across studies or datasets; high heterogeneity signified substantial differences between studies, necessitating further exploration to ascertain the plausibility of the studies and the sources of these differences. Specifically, the Cochrane Q test was used to evaluate the heterogeneity of the SNPs. A p‐value of <0.05 in this test indicated significant heterogeneity. 46 Directed pleiotropy of genetic tools was detected using MR‐Egger regression. The leave‐one‐out sensitivity test is used to exclude SNPs with anomalies that could affect the reliability of the results. 47 The association between exposure and outcome was confirmed by examining similar trends in IVW, weighted median, and MR‐Egger analyses.

DISCUSSION

This bidirectional two‐sample MR analyses aimed to clarify the potential causal relationship between SAD and epilepsy. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive bidirectional study of SAD and epilepsy, covering eight types of each. Epilepsy subtypes affect MS differently. Documented cases show that focal epilepsy increases the risk of MS, whereas childhood absence epilepsy is associated with a decreased risk of MS. Diabetes increases the risk of epilepsy, while IBD and coeliac disease reduce it. Despite the relatively weak association, probably due to the adjustment for many confounding factors, our study is supported by strong evidence from other research.

There is consistent evidence that focal epilepsy increases the risk of developing MS. 49 A large population‐based retrospective analysis found an increased risk of epilepsy in people with MS, 50 with some having had epilepsy before MS symptoms. 51 These findings suggest that epilepsy may be a potential cause of MS. However, the existing literature is scarce and lacks a comprehensive description of the typology of epilepsy and the risk of developing MS in individuals with epilepsy. To clarify the association between epilepsy and MS, bidirectional two‐sample MR analyses were used to simulate a randomized controlled trial, thus avoiding potential confounding factors and reverse causality seen in observational studies. Cortical damage may underlie both epilepsy and MS. 52 Seizures are often associated with hippocampal sclerosis, 53 and hippocampal damage may trigger MS. 54 Seizures significantly affect cortical structural integrity by triggering neuroinflammation. It is well established that cortical damage is closely associated with the onset of MS. 55 Furthermore, the activation of neuroinflammatory pathways in epilepsy may either induce MS or play a pivotal role in its pathogenesis. 56 While most current clinical studies consider epilepsy to be a risk factor for MS, they do not specify which subtype of epilepsy occurs. The present study found that childhood absence epilepsy was a protective factor. It has been established that childhood absence epilepsy is predominantly associated with genetic mutations in the GABAA receptor (GABAR) β 3 subunit gene (GABRB3), resulting in neuronal hyperexcitability due to diminished GABAA receptor functionality. 57 Interestingly, studies have found that downregulating the GABAB receptors of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells protects against demyelination and oligodendrocyte loss. 58 , 59 The finding of the article that childhood absence epilepsy is a protective factor in MS and may be associated with GABA receptor down‐regulation suggests that the targeted modulation of GABA receptor signaling could be an innovative therapeutic strategy for various disorders, including epilepsy and demyelination.

The relationship between IBD and epilepsy remains limited. A meta‐analysis failed to establish a correlation between the two conditions 60 ; however, our study identified IBD as a protective factor for epilepsy. In this study, we explored the potential reasons for the discrepancy between the results of our study and those of previous studies. (1) The effect of IBD on epilepsy is more strongly linked to environmental factors. 61 IBD results in dysbiosis of the intestinal flora, 62 disruption of the mucosal barrier, 63 and the induction of epilepsy. This is achieved by affecting the central nervous and immune systems through the gut microbiota‐brain‐gut axis and inflammatory factor. 64 , 65 (2) A study has demonstrated that Bacteroidetes exerts a protective effect against generalized epilepsy with tonic‐clonic seizures. 66 This bacterium is not only a significant component of the gut microbiome but also serves as a biomarker of zinc status. 67 It is hypothesized that Bacteroidetes may influence epilepsy by modulating zinc levels, thereby contributing to its protective effect. 68 The results of this study support the existing literature on the increased risk of epilepsy in patients with SLE. 69 (3) Evidence has emerged indicating a correlation between gut microbes and epilepsy, with patients diagnosed with epilepsy often exhibiting gastrointestinal symptoms that can result in underdiagnosis of IBD. This phenomenon, termed a “protective artifact” has been observed, leading to the underdiagnosis and undertreatment of these comorbid conditions. 64

SLE has been shown to increase the risk of epileptogenesis through several mechanisms. (1) Structural changes in the brain have been proposed to be associated with the development of seizures. 70 Several studies have shown that SLE can lead to structural brain alterations, reduced subcortical white matter integrity, hippocampal damage, and cerebral atrophy through direct neuronal damage and immunopathogenesis. 71 , 72 (2) Studies have shown that specific autoantibodies are associated with seizures in SLE patients. The presence of anticardiolipin, 73 anti‐phosphate antibody (APL), and anti‐Sm antibody has been linked to a higher risk of seizures. 69 , 74 (3) A potential genetic link between SLE and epilepsy has been suggested. A potential genetic locus for susceptibility to seizures has been identified in chromosome 15q22, involving a three‐SNP variant of the TREX1 gene. 70 , 75 The findings of this study suggest that coeliac disease may serve as a protective factor against epilepsy. However, this conclusion is complicated by evidence from a meta‐analysis 76 and several population‐based studies, 77 , 78 which have shown an increased risk of epilepsy among individuals with coeliac disease. Consequently, it is unlikely that the observed association between celiac disease and epilepsy can be explained by a common genetic aetiology. 79 Diagnosing celiac disease is challenging due to its non‐specific symptoms that often develop over several years. 80 As a result, epilepsy is often underestimated in patients with celiac disease. A bidirectional two‐sample MR study identified celiac disease as a protective factor for migraine, a finding that contrasts with the majority of current studies. 81 This discrepancy highlights the complexity of the relationship between celiac disease and neurological disorders, which requires further investigation. In addition, the association between coeliac disease and epilepsy remains underexplored and warrants more comprehensive research. 82

This study has several notable strengths. It used a bidirectional two‐sample MR analyses design, an effective method for minimizing confounding variables and reverse causation. Our approach was based on the most comprehensive pooled data from available GWAS databases. Based on the results, we provide genetic evidence for a bidirectional association between SAD and epilepsy. Furthermore, various MR techniques were used to minimize potential bias in the study. Sensitivity analyses showed no evidence of heterogeneity, horizontal pleiotropy, or outliers, confirming the validity and robustness of the findings.

This study has inherent limitations. First, SNPs associated with known confounders were excluded from the Phenoscanner analysis; however, potential unidentified confounders may still be present, which is an unavoidable limitation of this approach. Secondly, most GWAS data come from European populations, so caution is needed when applying these findings to other ethnic groups. More studies in non‐European populations are needed to improve our understanding. Thirdly, the lack of subtyping, classification, and severity data for different epilepsy subtypes in SAD diseases from existing public databases prevents the estimation of the association between epilepsy and SAD subtypes or severity. It is imperative to be cognizant of the risk factors, which include dietary habits, vitamin intake, sleep patterns, and trauma, as they play a pivotal role in the early prevention of the disease. Therefore, our findings only partially explain the causal relationship between SAD and epilepsy. In light of the study's limitations, the following solutions are proposed to enhance the study's scope by expanding the sample size to include patients diagnosed with autoimmune epilepsy, thereby facilitating the identification of subtype‐specific risk loci. The establishment of a multicenter, prospective cohort is imperative to facilitate the systematic documentation of the following: SAD type, epilepsy subtype, antibody profile, treatment response, and cognitive function.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, our study suggests a potential causal relationship between diabetes mellitus and increased susceptibility to epilepsy, whereas IBD and celiac disease appear to have a protective effect against epilepsy. Our findings provide strong new evidence that epilepsy may be a causal determinant of MS risk. Our findings reveal a complex interaction between SAD and epilepsy. Further research is needed to explore potential common mechanisms between these two conditions in more detail.
