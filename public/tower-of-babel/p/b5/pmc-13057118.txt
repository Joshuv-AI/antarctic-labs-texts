Optimized T-shaped resonator via local enhancement model integration within a cell for enhanced Rydberg-atom receiver sensing
Authors: Bo Wu, Zhanshan Sun, Di Sang, Yi Liu, Qiang An, Yi Lin, Yunqi Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRydberg atom with resonators is regarded as promising atomic receivers to surpass conventional receivers’ sensitivity. However, previous research has presented diverse open resonator structures, focusing on design yet lacking in theoretical guidance. Here we bridge this gap by elucidating the fundamental principles of local enhancement in open resonators, which guides us to adopt positive countermeasures aimed at maximizing the electric-field local enhancement ability and avoid blindly modifications. To overcome limitations of injection-type cylindrical resonators, which require external antennas and lack mobility, we propose an enclosed T-shaped resonator (TSR) integrated within a vapor cell for direct C-band coupling. Simulations show that the enhancement factor rises from 27 times of the basic parallel-plate resonator (PPR) to 57 times of the TSR at the same resonant frequency. An experimental validation was performed with an TSR. The TSR has 32.5 dB at 7.83 GHz electric-field local enhancement ability and an electrical size of 0.18 λ.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering; Electronics, photonics and device physics; Optical metrology

Introduction

Enhancing reception of weak electromagnetic (EM) waves is crucial across various domains, encompassing detection1, cosmological studies2, and meteorology3,4. The radar equation indicates that enhancing reception of weak EM waves is primarily achieved by boosting transmission power, antenna gain, and sensitivity. When sensitivity is limited, enlarging the parabolic antenna aperture is a common approach to improve weak signal detection. However, this results in higher costs, larger sizes, and greater technical complexity. Consequently, maximizing receiver sensitivity is essential. Currently, advanced microwave (MW) receivers utilize a superheterodyne structure with a local oscillator (LO) field, as shown in Fig. 1a, achieving high sensitivity levels. However, their theoretical sensitivity is fundamentally limited by electronic thermal noise, and enhancing sensitivity remains challenging. Therefore, there is an urgent need to overcome the sensitivity limitation of MW receivers based on electronics by studying other technologies.

Schematics of the basic conventional receiver and Rydberg-atom-based receiver.

Over the last decade, atomic MW detection technology has offered an approach to high-sensitivity MW reception, transcending traditional electronic systems5,6. This approach capitalizes on the distinctive attributes of Rydberg atoms, which exist in highly excited states with large electric dipole moments. These atoms are particularly susceptible to disturbances from electric (E)-field components in MW signals, leading to transitions between Rydberg energy levels. The Rydberg atomic MW detection technology offers several advantages. First, the Rydberg atomic MW E-field measurement sensitivity can break through the traditional limit7,8. Particularly, H. Fan et al.9 reported on the measurement sensitivity limit with −220 dBm/Hz. Second, a single atomic receiver has ultra-wideband measurement capability10. Third, the probe size is not constrained by the Chu limit11. It has great potential for applications in a diversity of fields, such as communication signals12,13, stereo reception14, imaging15 and so on.

Two sketches compare an atomic receiver with a conventional receiver in Fig. 1a and b. Traditional receivers necessitate components such as reception antennas, filters, low-noise amplifiers (LNA), mixers, and demodulators. The signal source activates the transmitting antenna to generate current and radiate EM waves. The EM waves travel to the conventional receiver, driving the free electrons in the receiving antenna to generate current, forming an electrical signal for subsequent circuit processing. The traditional antenna functions as an energy converter between EM waves and high-frequency currents. The atomic receiver is mainly composed of three key components: vapor cells, lasers, and photodetectors (PD). The vapor cell contains cesium atoms, which are excited to the Rydberg state by the probe and coupling lasers. The probe laser’s transmission signal is detected on a PD, where the Stark shift can be observed. Rydberg atoms eliminate the need for additional filters and mixers. Compared with traditional receivers, the noise caused by these devices can be reduced. Traditional antennas capture electromagnetic wave energy through their apertures and direct it to receivers for further processing. In the realm of atomic receivers, the process does not involve energy absorption. The atomic receiver is capable only of reception and does not function as a transmitter.

To date, no experiment has demonstrated that atomic receiver sensitivity surpasses that of traditional receivers. Therefore, it remains elusive how to further improve sensitivity. Previous research proposed the use of multi-photon through homodyne readout to mitigate photon shot noise16. Additionally, experiments have investigated three-photon excitation to eliminate Doppler-broadening17. In the microwave domain, studies explored superheterodyne systems18,19 and local E-field enhancement techniques20–26. Effective microwave interactions occur with atoms within the laser beam’s path in the atomic vapor cell, typically just a few hundred microns in diameter. Consequently, by precisely controlling the enhancement of the microwave electric field in this localized region, the detection capability for microwave signals is enhanced, which in turn improves the sensitivity of the entire atomic receiver. The motivation for the usage of resonators stems from the resonant cavity’s ability to locally enhance the electric field. By positioning the resonator in the region where the two lasers overlap with the cell, the detection-sensing capability is enhanced. The yellow and red boxes illustrate the resonator’s connection scheme within the atomic receiver, as shown in Fig. 1c. Current local enhancement strategies utilize cylindrical or rectangular resonators in the C and X bands. However, being injection-type27, they required an external antenna to couple electromagnetic waves to transmission lines, thereby compromising mobility and flexibility in practical applications. This enhancement method does not permit direct coupling with EM waves. In addition, a notable example of E-field local enhancement is the parallel plate resonator (PPR), which has been employed at 2.1 GHz to achieve a 17-fold E-field enhancement24. For a parallel plate resonator in the high-frequency band, a compact physical size is required, necessitating placement within, rather than outside, the vapor cell. This approach presents several challenges. Firstly, traditional parallel plate resonators have a limited enhancement factor, which requires further improvement. Secondly, miniaturization limits the vapor cell space, necessitating a reduction in volume. Thirdly, the structure’s surface area must be controlled to prevent excessive adsorption that could decrease the atomic number, as this could result in reduced sensitivity. At the same time, these studies propose numerous resonant structures for local E-field enhancement and have been published extensively on the topic. However, they focus solely on design without providing analytical models.

In this work, we propose a T-shape resonator (TSR), which is enclosed in a vapor cell to directly couple between the electromagnetic wave and the resonator in the C band. Our research proposed a model for E-field intensity in the gap of a parallel plate resonator to avoid blind modifications. To start with, we study theoretically potential factors that may influence the E-field enhancement. These factors include gain, gap distance, wavelength, and resistance. Subsequently, one representative symmetrical parallel plate resonator example is chosen to validate the proposed model, with the agreement between the finite-element simulation and the model. Based on the proposed model, the enhancement factor can be enhanced by reducing the gap distance, boosting the structure’s gain, and elevating the impedance at the gap port to guide the actual design. We strive to optimize the resonators and emphasize the advances of Shape's subtle design. Based on the proposed model, the enhancement factor can be optimized by improving impedance, which can be accomplished by diminishing capacitance and amplifying inductance. Therefore, TSR is optimized based on the original parallel-plate resonator. Finite element simulation results show that at the same resonant frequency, the enhancement factor rises from 27 times the basic PPR to 57 times the TSR. The volume of the TSR is reduced to 13% of the PPR, and the surface area is reduced to 18% of the PPR. An experimental validation was performed with an TSR. The TSR has 32.5 dB at 7.83 GHz, electric-field local enhancement ability, and an electrical size of 0.18λ. We further discuss the cost of incorporating a metal resonator using thermal noise theory. Additionally, thermal noise can be mitigated through the use of materials possessing higher conductivity. This offers insights for the design and optimization of parallel plate resonators in atomic receivers.

Results

Rationale behind specific design choices

The following analysis is the motivation to choose the C, X-band. Currently, the application of the parallel-plate resonator is primarily in the L, S, and C bands. The application form is that the structure is outside, and the vapor cell is inside. This approach is effective at L, S, and C bands, but struggles to scale to shorter wavelengths. In the C, X-band, either a closed rectangular cavity or a cylindrical cavity is utilized to enhance the E-field-sensing ability of the atomic receiver. Aiming at the challenge of high-frequency usage, this paper proposes putting the structure inside the vapor cell in Fig. 2a. A general cesium atom vapor cell is shown in Fig. 2b.

The design goal is to obtain an optimized parallel-plate resonator with a high enhancement factor in the C, X-band. With a fixed resonant frequency, the wavelength is constant. Considering the small aperture size in the C, X-band, achieving a high receiving gain is challenging. Therefore, the effective receiving area is difficult to further improve. On the basis of the “Methods” section analysis, the enhancement factor can be improved by increasing the impedance. When the structural resonance is at the same frequency point, the larger the inductance, the smaller the capacitance, and the larger the impedance according to the Supplementary Information in the “Methods” section. Therefore, we increase impedance by reducing the structure capacitance based on the initial parallel-plate resonator in Fig. 2c. At low frequencies, it is feasible to directly incorporate lumped elements to adjust overall capacitance or inductance. Conversely, at high frequencies, adjustments are made by modifying the structure itself. For detailed calculations of the resonant frequency of the PPR structure, please refer to the Supplementary Information section on Estimation of Resonance Frequency. The capacitance reduction method is considered from two perspectives. The total capacitance in parallel is the gap capacitance plus the surface capacitance. We can either fill the gap capacitance or the surface capacitance. Starting with the definition of capacitance, methods for reducing these two capacitances are discussed respectively. The way to reduce the Cgap is to sharpen the port into a butterfly shape28. It should be acknowledged that this is not a novel method. The determination formula of the parallel plate capacitor is given by C=εrS4πkd029, where εr is the relative dielectric constant, S is the effective overlap area, k is the electrostatic force constant, and d0 is the plate distance. The surface capacitance Csurf can be minimized by reducing S. To maintain the local E-field enhancement effect of the beam through the path, we propose a T-shaped resonator. Both the parallel-plate resonator (PPR) in Fig. 2c and the T-shaped resonator (TSR) resonate in Fig. 2d at 8 GHz. The path of the probe laser and coupling laser is described. The optimized effective overlap area of TSR is 20.76% of the PPR. For more detailed manufacturing information, please see the Supplementary Information Manufacturing Process section.

Thermal noise

For Rydberg atom-based receivers employing optical detection schemes, the fundamental sensitivity limit should be adjusted by quantum projection noise and the shot noise. However, it should be noted that when additional metal structures are added, additional metal thermal noise will be introduced. At this time, it is necessary to evaluate whether the size of the metal thermal noise will affect the atomic system. The mechanism of thermal noise is different from shot noise, so this is the cost of introducing a metal structure. For a given parallel plate resonator, thermal noise contributions must be minimized by careful selection of the external quality factor32. High thermal noise occurs when the resonator is critically coupled and therefore impedance matched to the input port33. Therefore, when considering the limit of the atomic receiver, it is necessary to consider the thermal noise caused by the metal structure. Consequently, the TSR resistor thermal noise analysis is proportional to physical temperature and resistor value. Thermal noise in resistors existed independently of the existence of voltage or current in the conductor. An ultra-high precision resistance meter was used to measure the resistance of the TSR and PPR samples. Table 1 provides a thermal noise comparison among the proposed TSR samples and PPR. Oxygen-free copper has lower resistivity than stainless steel, resulting in a smaller measured resistance. When both TSR and PPR were made of stainless steel 420, the PPR resistance was 1.23 mΩ lower than that of TSR. This occurs because a larger effective overlap area corresponds to smaller resistance. Experimental setup

Figure 6a provides the experimental setup overview, which includes 852 nm laser frequency locking component, 509 nm laser frequency locking component and an experimental measurement component. The DL Pro laser controller features a PID feedback control system that enables its frequency locking unit to achieve frequency locking via external input feedback signals. The LO and weak SIG MW were generated by two signal generators producing output power of up to 25 dBm. Because a larger output power was required, a 45 dB power amplifier was employed. These two signals were synthesized by a two-way MW resistive power divider (RPD). The combined signal was radiated by a broadband ridged horn antenna. We utilized Fraunhofer distance far-field condition, which was calculated as 2D2/λ where D was 5.6 cm and the wavelength was 3.75 cm at 8 GHz. Hence, we positioned the antenna 33 cm away from the vapor cell. In the laser overlap area, a T-shaped resonator integrated cesium vapor cell was 10 mm long and 1 mm thick at room temperature. The probe and the coupling laser were counter-propagated and overlapped to create the electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT). After detection by a PD, electric signal was connected to a spectrum analyzer and an oscilloscope.

In order to better understand the nature of the interaction, we conducted the following discussion. When the microwave frequency is far detuned, the AC Stark shift is indeed mainly the displacement of the lower-frequency component. When the microwave frequency and energy level resonance are added, the spectrum exhibits Autler–Townes splitting, and the higher frequency peak can be detected. L. Zhang et al.35 reported on the separation between the two peaks, and in particular their relative height by varying the detuning of the radio-frequency electric field. When the MW frequency lies below the energy-level resonance frequency, the lower-frequency signal component exhibits greater intensity. Conversely, when the microwave frequency exceeds the resonance frequency, the higher-frequency component becomes more prominent. When the frequency detuning is >20 MHz, only the lower-frequency component or the higher-frequency component will be shifted. The Rydberg state selected in this paper is 50S1/2, and the energy level transition and resonance frequency that can occur are respectively 50S1/2 → 50P3/2, 32.73 GHz and 50S1/2 → 49P3/2, 35.15 GHz based on the ARC calculator36. The microwave frequency we applied is 8 GHz in this paper, which is much lower than 32.73 and 35.15 GHz. Therefore, only the lower-frequency component peak can observe the frequency shift.

Figure 7b illustrates the changes in the enhancement factor with microwave frequency. It is evident that the resonator reaches its highest measured E-field enhancement at 7.83 GHz and demonstrates a narrow frequency response. The resonant frequency shift is explained by two main factors, from 8 GHz in simulation to 7.83 GHz in experiments. Firstly, the vapor cell’s inner surface exhibits nonzero conductivity because of alkali-metal atom adsorption37. Secondly, miniaturized designs inevitably face performance variations or degradation due to manufacturing and installation errors. Fabrication imperfections in the resonator can also lead to a decrease in the resonant frequency.

It transforms the focus from spectral frequency shifts measurement to difference frequency signal amplitudes measurement. We further clarified the specific contribution of the proposed TSR design. From a principle perspective, the way traditional antennas receive electromagnetic waves differs from Rydberg-based quantum sensors. Traditional antennas capture the power density in space through their effective aperture area, with received power being the product of power density and effective area. In contrast, the measurement area is the Rydberg atom space enveloped by coupling and probe laser beams, where the laser-microwave-atom coherence effect occurs. By enhancing the electric field intensity to be measured in this overlapping area, the measurement sensitivity can be improved. If a local resonant enhancement structure is added around the vapor cell to compress the electric field intensity at the aperture into an overlapping region of laser beams and the vapor cell, measurement sensitivity can be enhanced. Therefore, the most critical improvement that the TSR brings to the performance of Rydberg-based quantum sensors is that the TSR locally enhances power density in the Rydberg atomic region. The second improvement is spatial filtering. The traditional parabolic antenna has aperture gain and a narrow beam. It can realize spatial filtering, so as to improve the external signal-to-noise ratio. However, we generally believe that the gain of the Rydberg atom is 1.5, which is almost non-directional. At this time, when the signal-to-noise ratio of the external signal is very weak, even if the sensitivity of the Rydberg atom is higher than that of traditional electronic receivers, the signal-to-noise ratio of the signal to be measured is very low and cannot be detected. At present, we have always wanted to replace the traditional electronic receiver with the Rydberg atomic receiver. The high sensitivity of the atomic receiver reduces the requirement for the aperture of parabolic antennas. However, only when the signal-to-noise ratio of the signal to be measured is high enough is high sensitivity useful. Therefore, we think it is necessary to improve the directivity by adding a TSR to achieve spatial filtering. And due to the narrow-band bandpass characteristics of the resonant structure, the out-of-band noise can also be filtered out.

Discussion

To summarize, we proposed an E-field enhancement model that builds a bridge between the electromagnetic properties of parallel plate resonators, such as wavelength, resistance, gain, and E-field strength at the gap. Based on the proposed model, the enhancement factor can be optimized by improving impedance, which can be accomplished by diminishing capacitance and amplifying inductance. Therefore, TSR is optimized based on the original parallel-plate resonator. Specific optimization IEEE gain of TSR is enhanced from 1.23 to 1.39 dB. Additionally, there is an improvement in real part impedance from 1555 to 6230. The TSR has 32.5 dB at 7.83 GHz E-field local enhancement ability and electrical size of 0.18λ. The volume of the TSR is reduced to 13% of the PPR, and the surface area is reduced to 18% of the PPR. Additionally, we employ a TSR enclosed within a vapor cell, effectively addressing the challenges associated with deploying parallel-plate resonators in high-frequency bands. Results show that integrated resonators not only enhance E-field measurement but also improve miniaturization and mobility in practical applications. Additionally, we explore the limitations on sensitivity imposed by thermal noise. To mitigate this, we compared the thermal noise reduction capabilities of a TSR made from stainless-steel 420, oxygen-free copper, and oxygen-free copper with silver plating materials. In the future, we will aim to integrate various enhancement technologies, including multi-photon16,43, Doppler-free17, and repump44, with local enhancement structures to boost the sensitivity of atomic receivers.

Methods

This paper establishes a mathematical model of the local enhancement structure covering the effective aperture based on antenna-receiving principles, and verifies the accuracy of the model using a PPR structure. According to the derivation in the Supplementary Information, it can be found that adjusting the capacitance and inductance to increase the impedance at the gap port effectively enhances the electric field. The E-Field local enhancement model is derived in detail in parameters in the supplementary information, and an example validation is performed using a classic resonant structure. Additionally, a performance analysis is conducted: TSR versus conventional antennas. Finally, theoretical calculations of the estimation of resonance frequency and the spatial distribution model of E-field, charge density, and current density are also conducted.
