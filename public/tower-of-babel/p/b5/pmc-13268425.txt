Assessing the influence of air pollution on cholelithiasis formation and blood lipid levels: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Zekang Xu, Changqi Yu, Yunxiang Feng, Jianming Wang, Zhengdong Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study used a two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) method to explore the potential causal relationship between air quality, cholelithiasis, and its related risk factor, blood lipids. This study used MR in European and East Asian groups, utilizing publicly available genome-wide association study data. The main method was inverse-variance weighting, with additional quality checks including weighted median, MR-Egger, simple, and weighted models. The Cochran Q test assessed heterogeneity. MR-Egger regression and MR-PRESSO checked for gene pleiotropy, with sensitivity tests using leave-one-out analysis. No statistically significant association was observed between air pollution and cholelithiasis in both European and East Asian populations for all pollutants analyzed, including PM2.5 (European: P = .745, East Asian: P = .209), PM2.5 to 10 (European: P = .737), PM10 (European: P = .258, East Asian: P = .977), nitrogen dioxide (European: P = .986, East Asian: P = .893), and nitrogen oxides (European: P = .902, East Asian: P = .843). However, significant correlations between air pollution and blood lipids were identified. In European populations, PM2.5 was significantly correlated with triglycerides (β = 0.428, odds ratio = 1.534, 95% confidence interval = 1.058–2.225; P = .024). In East Asian populations, PM2.5 showed a significant association with low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol (β = 0.050, odds ratio = 1.051, 95% confidence interval = 1.006–1.098; P = .026). Importantly, no evidence of heterogeneity or pleiotropy was detected in these associations, bolstering the credibility of our findings. Our study found no clear causal link between air pollution and cholelithiasis. However, we observed significant correlations between PM2.5 and triglycerides in Europeans, and between PM2.5 and LDL cholesterol in East Asians. These results suggest that PM2.5 might affect triglyceride and LDL cholesterol metabolism differently in various populations, potentially raising the risk of cholelithiasis indirectly.

1. Introduction

Cholelithiasis represents a prevalent health concern, with notable variations in its prevalence across different populations. Ultrasound studies have revealed an average prevalence of cholelithiasis ranging from 10% to 15% in European adults to 3% to 5% in African populations.[1] Notably, in China, the prevalence of cholelithiasis is escalating, with rates ranging from 5% to 11%.[2] Alarmingly, a considerable proportion of individuals with cholelithiasis remain asymptomatic. Specifically, 76.7% of men and 72% of women are unaware of their condition, and 87.0% of men and 84.9% of women exhibit no symptoms.[3,4] However, despite its often asymptomatic nature, complications necessitating surgery arise in approximately 1% to 2% of patients annually.[5] In the United States, cholelithiasis is the leading inpatient diagnosis among gastrointestinal disorders.[6] Gastroesophageal reflux disease incurs the highest annual direct costs at $9.3 billion, followed by cholelithiasis at $5.8 billion.[7] With the global incidence of cholelithiasis on the rise, elucidating its etiology assumes paramount importance.

The development of cholelithiasis involves multiple factors, with critical roles played by bile acid and lipoprotein metabolism in its formation.[8] Epidemiological evidence has elucidated the association between obesity, metabolic syndrome (characterized by hypercholesterolemia, hypertriglyceridemia, low-density lipoprotein [LDL] cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol), and the development of cholelithiasis, with cholelithiasis often regarded as a complication of metabolic dysregulation. Specifically, elevated triglyceride levels are implicated in cholelithiasis pathogenesis through disturbances in lipid homeostasis, influencing bile composition and predisposing to cholesterol crystallization.[9–11] In a similar manner, elevated LDL cholesterol levels interfere with cholesterol metabolism, causing the liver to secrete excess cholesterol into bile. This process increases cholesterol saturation, promotes crystal formation, and ultimately leads to the development of cholelithiasis. Animal models have provided further insights, demonstrating the potential preventive role of lipid regulation in cholelithiasis.[12,13] It is significant that cholelithiasis and dyslipidemia have several overlapping risk factors, such as obesity, high-fat diets, and sedentary lifestyles.[14–16] Simultaneously, recent studies suggest a connection between prolonged exposure to atmospheric particulate matter (PM) and dyslipidemia, illustrating that PM exposure can alter lipid profiles and increase the risk of dyslipidemia.[17–19]

In today industrialized societies, air pollution presents a major issue, posing serious threats to human health. Harmful substances such as PM2.5, PM10, nitrogen dioxide (NO2), and nitrogen oxides (NOx) are commonly found in the atmosphere and have a notable impact on respiratory health. Numerous studies have also highlighted their wider health effects.[20,21] Research by de Bont and colleagues found a significant link between long-term air pollution exposure and increased risks of cardiovascular death and diseases, including stroke, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease. Fine and coarse particles (PM2.5 and PM10), as well as NOx, are identified as major contributors to these health issues.[22] Furthermore, Glencross et al delineated the pro-inflammatory immune response incited by ambient air pollutants, characterized by oxidative stress, toll-like receptor activation, and dysregulated intracellular signaling pathways, culminating in inflammation and tissue damage.[23] Notably, investigations probing the association between cholelithiasis and air pollution have suggested a potential link, particularly implicating chronic exposure to ambient PM in heightened cholelithiasis risk among Chinese adults, with dyslipidemia potentially mediating this relationship.[24] Wang et al found that exposure to PM2.5 causes a decrease in the expression of the Farnesoid X receptor, a crucial nuclear receptor activated by bile acid, playing a vital role in lipid metabolism. Farnesoid X receptor modulation by PM2.5 may precipitate hepatocellular steatosis, elucidating a mechanistic pathway underlying PM2.5-induced hepatic pathology.[25–27] Nonetheless, the precise association between cholelithiasis and environmental factors, particularly air pollution, remains enigmatic, warranting further investigation in the present study.

Airborne pollutants infiltrate the human body through diverse routes, instigating inflammatory reactions and oxidative stress, thereby potentially inducing cellular injury and metabolic derangements.[28] This research aims to investigate the impact of air pollution on cholelithiasis and its related risk factors, with a specific emphasis on lipids. Breathing in fine PM endangers respiratory health and can enter the bloodstream, affecting both the development of cholelithiasis and lipid metabolism.[29] This expanded viewpoint sheds light on the interaction between environmental factors and the development of cholelithiasis.

To investigate the potential connection between air pollution and cholelithiasis, publicly accessible genome-wide association study (GWAS) data were used. This research incorporated exposure factors like PM and nitrogen compounds. The outcome variables were cholelithiasis and associated lipid parameters, allowing for the assessment of causal links between air pollution and cholelithiasis. In addition, comprehensive analyses were performed on European and East Asian cohorts to investigate geographic differences in the association between air pollution and cholelithiasis. This varied method enables the inclusion of different geographical and demographic factors, as well as possible genetic and environmental impacts. These stratified analyses provide detailed insights and enhance the understanding of how air pollution impacts cholelithiasis. As a result, the findings from this study hold global significance, offering crucial insights that can inform future scientific investigations. Furthermore, these outcomes offer a vital foundation for creating and improving public health policies aimed at addressing the impact of air pollution on gallstone disease. This research not only broadens our knowledge but also supports policymakers in formulating effective strategies to protect public health.

2. Research methods

2.1. Design

This study is based on 3 key assumptions: the instrumental variables (IVs) are associated with air pollution (exposure), they do not affect cholelithiasis or its related risk factor, blood lipids (outcome), through confounding variables, and their impact on the outcome happens exclusively through air pollution.[30] Air pollution, including various forms of PM and nitrogen compounds, is the primary exposure factor, with relevant single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) used as IVs. The study focuses on cholelithiasis and its related lipid parameters as outcome variables. Figure 1 provides a schematic representation. 2.2. Data sources

Data sources are detailed in Table 1. We selected air pollution – including PM2.5, PM2.5 to 10, PM10, NO2, and NOx – as the exposure. All GWAS summary statistics were derived from UK Biobank, a prospective cohort that recruited over 500,000 participants aged 40 to 69 years in the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010, providing extensive phenotypic, genetic, and genome-wide genotyping data.[31,32] Air pollution exposure estimates were based on land-use regression models applied to participant residential addresses, representing the annual average concentration for the year 2010.[33] No long-term follow-up air pollution data were available (cross-sectional exposure assessment). Key covariates adjusted for in the GWAS included age, sex, body mass index, smoking status, socioeconomic factors, and principal components for ancestry correction.

We selected gallstone disease and related lipid traits (triglycerides, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and HDL cholesterol) as outcomes, with GWAS summary statistics sourced from the IEU OpenGWAS database.[34] Maintained by the MRC IEU at the University of Bristol, the IEU OpenGWAS platform (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) is a publicly available repository standardizing tens of thousands of GWAS summary datasets globally and supporting MR and other genetic analyses. 2.3. Instrumental variable selection

As illustrated in Figure 2, a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10−8 was utilized for the exposure, in line with hypothesis (1). Among the factors considered, only PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and NOx from European datasets had sufficient SNPs.

Previous studies have shown that using linear regression with a screening threshold of P < 5 × 10−6 for each genetic variant diminishes the likelihood of weak instrument bias in MR studies.[35,36] We modified the genome-wide significance level to P < 5 × 10−6 for the remaining exposures to find sufficient SNPs as IVs linked with this degree of significance. PM2.5 to 10 (East Asia) was excluded due to insufficient SNP yield, even after reducing the gene-wide significance threshold.

To exclude SNPs in linkage disequilibrium (LD), r2 < 0.001 and kb > 10,000 were set at the time of IV extraction. Palindromic SNPs were subsequently excluded to ensure consistency in allele effects between exposure and outcome. We then computed the R2[37] and the F-statistic[38] for each SNP. The R2 represents the overall degree of explanation of all genetic variants for the target exposure, and the F-statistic assesses the strength of the IV. The F-statistic of each of our selected SNPs is >10, indicating that the selected genetic tools are strong predictors of exposure.[39] Detailed SNP information, R2, and F-statistic values are provided in Tables S1,S2,S3,S4,S5,S6,S7,S8,S9,S10 and S11, Supplemental Digital Content. By examining the PhenoScanner database, SNPs associated with sex or age were excluded to eliminate potential confounding factors. The selected SNPs will serve as IVs for this study.

2.4. Genetic instrumental variable analysis

The causal relationship between air pollution, cholelithiasis, and lipids was assessed utilizing the inverse-variance weighting (IVW) method, initially proposed by Burgess et al in 2013 and now widely adopted as the primary analytical approach in MR studies.[40,41] This method computes the exposure-outcome causal effect for each genetic variant via Wald method, followed by aggregating these effects using the inverse variance as weights to derive the overall effect estimate. Furthermore, to enhance result reliability and accuracy, we employed weighted median, MR-Egger, simple, and weighted models to validate the causal association between exposure and outcome variables.[42]

2.5. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using leave-one-out analysis and the MR-PRESSO outlier detection method to identify and mitigate the influence of influential SNPs that could introduce heterogeneity or bias. The leave-one-out approach systematically evaluates the impact of each individual SNP on the overall causal estimate, while MR-PRESSO detects and corrects for outliers potentially distorting results. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed via the MR-Egger intercept test (threshold P < .05), and heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran Q statistic within the IVW framework (P < .05 indicating significant heterogeneity). In the presence of heterogeneity, random-effects IVW was applied as the primary estimator.

To improve the robustness and interpretability of our analyses, we explored the possibility of incorporating reinforcement learning techniques, such as the Q-learning models recently applied to optimizing complex systems.[43] The basic idea would be to recast IVs selection as a sequential decision-making task: the current set of IVs and their level of heterogeneity (e.g., Cochran Q statistic) serve as the state, adding or removing SNPs acts as possible actions, and the reward function is shaped to reduce heterogeneity while keeping the causal estimates stable. This model-free, iterative approach holds promise for identifying more reliable IV combinations, particularly in settings with weak instruments or pleiotropy, where it could help lower estimation variance and bias. Because our main analyses were based on GWAS summary statistics, we stuck to established statistical methods. Still, conceptual parallels from similar applications in network synthesis suggest that reinforcement learning strategies of this kind might boost robustness by 10% to 20% under heterogeneous conditions. Full implementation and rigorous head-to-head comparison would depend on access to individual-level data or well-designed simulated datasets – an avenue we consider worthwhile pursuing in future methodological work.

2.6. Statistical analysis

The implementation of MR was performed using the “TwoSampleMR,” “MendelianRandomisation,” and “gwasglue” libraries within the R statistical framework, version 4.2.3.

3. Analysis results

3.1. Screening of SNPs associated with exposure factors

In this study, to investigate the causal relationship of air pollution between cholelithiasis and dyslipidemia, we conducted a rigorous selection of SNPs. Firstly, we systematically screened SNPs that showed significant associations with environmental exposure factors from publicly available GWAS. Then, we applied stringent selection criteria to ensure the genetic independence of the selected SNPs: the threshold for LD was set at r2 < 0.001, and the physical distance was defined as kb = 10,000. This filtering strategy helps exclude SNPs with significant LD, thereby reducing potential interference from multicollinearity on causal effect inferences. To ensure the validity of the IVs, we require that the F-statistic for the selected SNPs must reach or exceed 10. This threshold is set to guarantee that these SNPs can sufficiently explain the phenotypic variation of the exposure factor, thereby avoiding the use of weaker IVs, which is crucial for ensuring the accuracy of causal effect estimation. To ensure the robustness and scientific quality of the causal effect analysis results, this study conducted rigorous data preprocessing on palindromic sequences during the data merging process.

3.2. Air pollution and cholelithiasis

MR studies were conducted to assess the causal impact of air pollution components on cholelithiasis within both European and East Asian populations. After excluding IVs with chain imbalance, the number of SNPs per pollutant ranged from 5 to 23 for Europeans (Table 2) and from 3 to 6 for East Asians (Table 3). To analyze the causal effect of air pollution on cholelithiasis, this study employed the IVW random-effects model as the primary analytical method, supplemented by multiple MR approaches, including MR-Egger regression, the weighted median method, the simple mode method, and the weighted mode method. In both study populations, IVW analysis failed to demonstrate a significant association between air pollution and cholelithiasis (Tables 2 and 3). Specifically, the P-values obtained using the IVW method were as follows: PM2.5 (Europe: P = .745, East Asia: P = .209), PM2.5 to 10 (Europe: P = .737), PM10 (Europe: P = .258, East Asia: P = .977), NO2 (Europe: P = .986, East Asia: P = .893), and NOx (Europe: P = .902, East Asia: P = .843). In addition, none of the other 4 MR methods revealed a clear causal relationship between air pollution and gallstones. Figures 3 and 4 present the results of the scatter plot analysis. In these figures, 5 regression lines show no obvious trends, with relatively flat slopes, some even presenting negative values. Figures 5 and 6 display the forest plot analysis, where the confidence intervals (CIs) for both individual IVs and their summary estimates all cross zero.

3.3. Air pollution and blood lipids

The causal effect analysis between air pollution and dyslipidemia primarily employed the IVW random-effects model. To ensure the robustness and reliability of the findings, we also applied multiple MR methods, including MR-Egger regression, the weighted median approach, the simple mode method, and the weighted mode method. In the European population, the results of the IVW analysis showed: PM2.5 (total cholesterol levels: P = .491, LDL: P = .975, HDL: P = .136), PM2.5 to 10 (total cholesterol levels: P = .735, LDL: P = .417, HDL: P = .754), PM10 (total cholesterol levels: P = .619, LDL: P = .991, HDL: P = .979), NO2 (total cholesterol levels: P = .745, LDL: P = .392, HDL: P = .241), and NOx (total cholesterol levels: P = .924, LDL: P = .772, HDL: P = .426). Similarly, in East Asian populations, evidence of a causal relationship between the following air pollution components and blood lipids was not found in replicated MR studies: PM2.5 (triglycerides: P = .282, total cholesterol levels: P = .053, HDL: P = .647), PM10 (triglycerides: P = .841, total cholesterol levels: P = .346, HDL: P = .187), NO2 (triglycerides: P = .406, total cholesterol levels: P = .853, HDL: P = .352), and NOx (triglycerides: P = .774, total cholesterol levels: P = .318, HDL: P = .575). Furthermore, results from the other 4 MR analytical approaches also failed to reveal a significant causal relationship between air pollution and abnormal blood lipid levels.

Using Tables S3,S4,S5,S6,S7,S8,S9,S10 and S11, Supplemental Digital Content, we identified 6 SNPs strongly associated with PM2.5 exposure (rs77205736, rs6749467, rs1372504, rs12203592, rs77255816, and rs1537371) as IVs in individuals of European ancestry, and 3 SNPs (rs11067476, rs7630570, and rs80151214) in those of East Asian ancestry. IVW analyses revealed a significant effect of PM2.5 on triglycerides in the European cohort (β = 0.428, P = .024; Table 4), whereas in the East Asian cohort PM2.5 exerted a significant effect on LDL-cholesterol (β = 0.050, P = .026; Table 5), suggesting that PM2.5 may play a role in different ethnicities through different lipid pathways.

3.4. Quality control

To ensure the robustness and accuracy of MR analysis results, we conducted a rigorous quality control evaluation when investigating the causal relationships between air pollution and cholelithiasis as well as dyslipidemia. Specifically, we focused on assessing the heterogeneity and horizontal pleiotropy of the analysis to guarantee the reliability of the research findings. The horizontal pleiotropy test results (Tables 2–5) indicated that the P-values of all intercept terms were >.05. Some analyses showed statistical heterogeneity, and we adjusted the results using the IVW random-effects model. The MR-Egger regression and weighted median method further validated the consistency and stability of the IVW results. This study systematically evaluated the robustness of the causal association between air pollution and cholelithiasis as well as dyslipidemia through a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis.

In the sensitivity analysis of PM2.5 on triglycerides (Europe) and LDL (East Asia), the CIs of effect estimates for all genetic instruments were consistently located to the right of the null line, with all genetic instrument variables demonstrating consistent positive effect directions. In all analyses, the removal of any single genetic IV did not significantly alter the direction or magnitude of the causal association. The comprehensive analysis results demonstrate that the causal association between air pollution and cholelithiasis as well as dyslipidemia established in this study possesses strong methodological robustness. The statistical inferences were robust to the inclusion or exclusion of individual SNPs (Figs. S1,S2,S3,S4,S5,S6,S7,S8,S9,S10,S11,S12,S13,S14,S15 and S16, Supplemental Digital Content).

4. Discussion

Despite the growing body of evidence implicating environmental factors in various health conditions, the epidemiological evidence establishing a link between air pollution and cholelithiasis remains sparse and inconclusive. Furthermore, the precise mechanisms by which PM contributes to the development of cholelithiasis are not yet fully understood. In a study examining the effects of extended metal pollution exposure on human health through the analysis of gallstones,[44] it was found that airborne particulate pollutants, particularly in regions with frequent mining activities, can enter the bloodstream through inhalation and deposit in the gallbladder, gradually accumulating to form gallstones. This finding suggests a plausible mechanism by which PM exposure could increase the risk of cholelithiasis. The rationale behind this link is that the body eliminates some of these inhaled pollutants through bile excretion,[45] resulting in their accumulation in the gallbladder over time.

To our knowledge, only a handful of studies have investigated the association between cholelithiasis and air pollution. The first epidemiological study on this topic was conducted by Nie et al using a cross-sectional study using initial data from the Chinese Multi-Ethnic Cohort.[24] The study comprised 72,893 participants aged 30 to 79 years, hailing from 5 southwestern provinces of China and representing 7 distinct ethnic groups. The participants’ prolonged average exposure to PM was evaluated using validated spatio-temporal models. The association between ambient PM and cholelithiasis risk was evaluated through unconditional logistic regression. Furthermore, mediation analyses were performed to determine if dyslipidemia influenced the link between PM and cholelithiasis. Higher levels of ambient PM were positively associated with an increased risk of cholelithiasis. The odds ratios (95% CI) per 10 μg/m3 increase in PM were 1.17 (1.06–1.28) for PM1, 1.10 (1.05–1.15) for PM2.5, and 1.07 (1.04–1.10) for PM10. Mediation analyses indicated that dyslipidemia significantly influenced the relationship between PM and cholelithiasis, with mediation ratios of 5.37%, 9.13%, and 7.66% for PM1, PM2.5, and PM10, respectively. The study suggests that extended exposure to ambient PM may increase the risk of cholelithiasis among Chinese adults, with dyslipidemia possibly mediating this association. Subsequent investigations indicate that this correlation could be notably pronounced in various subpopulations, such as men, alcohol consumers, and centrally obese individuals.

Contrary to the findings of Nie et al, our MR study did not reveal direct genetic evidence linking air pollution to cholelithiasis. However, when examining the causal link between air pollution and lipid levels, a notable association was found between PM2.5 exposure and triglyceride levels in a European population. In addition, a significant causal link was identified between PM2.5 exposure and LDL cholesterol levels in East Asian populations.

It’s worth noting that Nie et al employed a cohort study with a cross-sectional design, which does not establish causality between exposure and outcome but rather indicates an association. Limitations of their study include reliance on self-report and physical examination of participants, dependence on survey data, and limited detail on the adequacy of covariate controls. Adjustments for factors like age, sex, ethnicity, and degree of urbanization might not have been comprehensive enough to eliminate the influence of other potential variables. In contrast, our study represents the first exploration of the causal relationship between air pollution and cholelithiasis using MR methods.

Compared to conventional observational studies, MR study employs genetic variants as IVs, effectively mitigating confounding and reverse causation to yield more robust evidence for causal inference. To ensure the reliability of our findings, we rigorously selected IVs strongly associated with the exposure (all F-statistics > 10) and conducted cross-validation using multiple MR methods, including IVW, MR-Egger regression, and the weighted median estimator. No evidence of horizontal pleiotropy was detected, further supporting the high reliability of our MR analytical results.

While our analyses did not reveal direct causal relationships, further investigation into the connection between air pollution and abnormal blood lipid levels may uncover potential mediating mechanisms. Earlier research has demonstrated a notable association between blood lipid irregularities and the risk of cholelithiasis. Zhang et al[46] conducted a multi-center study and found that elevated triglycerides and LDL cholesterol levels increase the risk of cholesterol supersaturation in bile, leading to gallstone formation. Similarly, Wang et al[47] performed a population-based analysis in a large Chinese cohort, identifying high levels of triglycerides and LDL cholesterol as significant risk factors for gallbladder stones. These results emphasize the significance of lipid management in lowering the occurrence of gallbladder stones, proposing lipids as potential mediators in the formation of cholelithiasis.

In our research, although we sought to clarify the causal relationship between air pollution and cholelithiasis, our results did not show a direct connection. However, upon refocusing the inquiry on the causal association between air pollution and blood lipids, a novel insight emerged: PM might influence the metabolic pathways of triglycerides or LDL cholesterol in specific demographics. This is consistent with earlier epidemiological research, which has similarly identified a link between air pollution exposure and adverse changes in lipid profiles.[48–51] In a Chinese multi-ethnic cohort study encompassing 67,305 participants,[52] the relationship between ambient air pollution and blood lipids was investigated using a satellite-based spatio-temporal model to estimate the average concentrations of various air pollution particles over 3 years. Ultimately, extended exposure to air pollutants was discovered to correlate with lipid levels and the risk of dyslipidemia. Certain experimental models have suggested that higher exposure to PM2.5 may result in increased levels of triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL cholesterol.[53–55]

By conducting an in-depth analysis of the causal relationship between air pollution and lipid profiles, our objective was to pinpoint potential biological mechanisms clarifying the effects of air pollution on cholelithiasis. The variations observed in the associations between European and East Asian populations in our findings are likely caused by genetic and environmental differences among these groups. In addition, differences in environmental conditions and air pollution levels between regions might also explain these disparities. Variations in atmospheric makeup, climate conditions, and emission sources between European and Asian areas can lead to significant differences in air pollution exposure among populations in these regions.

This highlights the complex interactions between environmental, genetic, and biological factors, requiring more extensive research to clarify how air pollution affects cholelithiasis. Moreover, disparities in data collection methods and criteria across regions may impede study comparability. In the East Asian cohort, the study results were predicated on a genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10−8 due to an insufficient number of SNPs reaching this threshold. Furthermore, the comparatively small sample sizes in the GWAS dataset for each pollutant can present difficulties for the study representativeness and generalizability. To gain a more complete understanding of this matter, future studies should include data from various regions and populations to provide a thorough perspective.

In conclusion, this paper explored the correlations between air pollution, cholelithiasis, and lipid profiles, which may offer a more nuanced comprehension of the collective impact of environmental factors on health. Medical professionals should consider environmental factors, like air pollution levels, in their risk evaluations for lipid metabolism disorders and cholelithiasis. It is recommended to regularly monitor blood lipid levels in people exposed to elevated levels of PM2.5. Preventive actions, such as lifestyle changes and limiting outdoor activities during high pollution times, should be advised for patients, particularly those in heavily polluted regions. An integrated, multi-disciplinary approach involving family physicians, cardiologists, and public health experts is essential for effectively tackling the impact of air pollution on lipid metabolism and the associated risk of cholelithiasis development.
