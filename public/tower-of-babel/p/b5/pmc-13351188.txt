Ultrasound-responsive nanodroplets activate the Hippo pathway via ROS-MST1-YAP cascade for breast cancer therapy
Authors: Yuye Fu, Yading Zhao, Lu Guo, Dandan Shi, Xiao Sun, Mengmeng Shang, Xiaoxuan Wang, Rui Liu, Shuting Huang, Xiaoyue Zhang, Jie Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

The absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) contributes to its aggressive clinical behavior, making it the most formidable subtype[1]. No benefit is derived from endocrine treatment or HER2-targeted agents in patients with TNBC. As a result, patients with TNBC often develop early distant metastases. Systemic chemotherapy continues to be a main clinical approach; however, it is associated with substantial toxicity and frequently leads to the development of chemoresistance, ultimately contributing to poor patient outcomes[2]. In this context, the development of innovative therapeutic strategies capable of overcoming drug resistance and efficiently inhibiting tumor progression and metastasis is urgently required to improve the clinical outcomes of TNBC patients.

Evidence suggests that the Hippo pathway serves as a key intrinsic safeguard against tumor progression, while also playing a crucial role in the regulation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)[3], [4], [5]. Dysregulation of Hippo signaling contributes to proliferation and metastasis across multiple cancers[6], [7]. The pathway comprises the mammalian sterile 20-like kinase 1 (MST1)-large tumor suppressor kinase 1 (LATS1) signaling axis and the downstream transcriptional coactivators yes-associated protein (YAP) and its paralog transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ)[8], [9], [10]. In TNBC, YAP is often aberrantly activated and accumulates in the nucleus, where it drives the transcription of pro-proliferative, anti-apoptotic, and EMT-related genes, thereby driving tumor growth and metastasis[11], [12], [13]. Therefore, restoring Hippo pathway function represents an attractive therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

Fluvastatin (FLU) has recently been reported to exhibit antitumor activity independent of its cholesterol-modulating effects[14]. Mechanistically, FLU can inhibit the isoprenoid modification of YAP, thereby impeding its nuclear translocation and suppressing downstream oncogenic output—functionally counteracting Hippo pathway dysregulation from the “downstream” end[15], [16], [17]. However, exploiting the antitumor potential of statins typically requires high-dose administration, which inevitably causes severe systemic side effects such as hepatotoxicity and myopathy. In addition, as FLU primarily targets YAP activity, it fails to restore the upstream MST1-LATS1 kinase cascade, which is essential for comprehensive reactivation of Hippo signaling and durable suppression of tumor progression. Collectively, these inherent drawbacks highlight the urgent demand for a precision delivery strategy to enhance intratumoral FLU accumulation and reduce off-target toxicity, alongside a synergistic therapeutic approach that concurrently activates upstream Hippo kinases to complement YAP inhibition.

Multifunctional nanodroplets (NDs) have recently gained attention as targeted delivery vehicles that also serve as nanoscale ultrasound contrast agents (UCAs) [18], [19]. Upon ultrasound (US) irradiation, liquid-core NDs undergo acoustic droplet vaporization (ADV) to form gas-core microbubbles, enhancing US imaging contrast[20], [21]. As a liquid perfluorocarbon, PFH forms nanoscale droplets with substantially smaller particle sizes than conventional gas-cored microbubbles. Benefiting from its unique liquid-core structure, PFH-based nanodroplets exhibit superior structural stability, prolonged systemic circulation duration, and improved cellular internalization efficiency. Upon ultrasound irradiation, PFH undergoes ADV and transforms into microbubbles in situ. The resulting microbubbles oscillate and eventually rupture under continuous ultrasonic stimulation, which thereby enhances cavitation effects and triggering the release of loaded drugs in a controlled manner. This unique “delivery-before-activation” property makes PFH highly suitable for ultrasound-responsive drug delivery systems. Concurrently, ultrasound-targeted microbubble destruction (UTMD) increases local vascular and cellular membrane permeability, improving drug uptake[22], [23]. Previous studies have demonstrated that MST1 can be activated by reactive oxygen species (ROS)[24], whereas UTMD induces localized ROS generation through acoustic cavitation[25], [26], [27]. Based on this mechanistic link, we hypothesized that UTMD may activate MST1 via ROS generation, thereby contributing to the upstream reactivation of the Hippo pathway in a non-invasive and safe manner. This effect, unprecedented in prior literature, may provide a novel conceptual paradigm. Building on the above rationale, we therefore propose an “upstream–downstream clamp” strategy that concurrently employs UTMD to activate MST1 and utilizes NDs to release FLU for YAP inhibition, thus combating Hippo pathway dysregulation and potentially achieving substantial anti-tumor effect.

Albumin exhibits excellent intrinsic biocompatibility and versatile drug-binding capability, making it a favorable biomaterial for drug delivery. The tumor accumulation and cellular internalization of albumin-based nanocarriers are mediated by multiple intrinsic mechanisms, including gp60-dependent transcytosis, macropinocytosis, and specific interactions with tumor-associated albumin-binding proteins[28], [29], [30]. Consequently, albumin-based nanocarriers can preferentially deliver encapsulated drugs to tumor sites via a combination of nanoscale size–mediated passive targeting and albumin-associated accumulation. Despite the widespread application of albumin as a versatile drug delivery carrier, current research on albumin-based nanodroplets remains limited. In particular, few studies have simultaneously integrated ultrasound responsiveness, tumor-targeted delivery, and precise signaling pathway modulation within a single nanosystem. To further advance this promising direction, we herein fabricate an intelligent ultrasound-responsive nanoplatform by encapsulating PFH and FLU within a bovine serum albumin (BSA) shell. Under externally focused ultrasound, the PFH core undergoes a liquid-to-gas phase transition, enabling on-demand drug release[31]; in parallel, UTMD-induced cavitation elevates intracellular ROS, which may contribute to MST1 activation and subsequent Hippo signaling reactivation. Simultaneously, released FLU suppresses YAP activity downstream, cooperatively driving comprehensive Hippo pathway activation. Unlike conventional drug delivery systems, the key innovation lies in the integration of a mechanistically designed therapeutic strategy. Specifically, ROS produced by UTMD may activate the upstream Hippo kinase MST1, whereas fluvastatin suppresses the downstream effectors YAP. This creates a synergistic regulatory pattern for the Hippo pathway featuring upstream activation coupled with downstream inhibition within a unified ultrasound-responsive delivery platform. To the best of our knowledge, this nanomaterial-based strategy for synergistically regulating the Hippo pathway has not been reported to date. This theranostic strategy holds promise for integrating contrast-enhanced US imaging, dual (endogenous/exogenous) targeting, and mechanism-guided combination signaling control to inhibit proliferation, promote apoptosis, reverse EMT, and ultimately impede TNBC growth and metastasis while mitigating systemic toxicity.

Materials and methods

Materials

FLU was obtained by MCE (New Jersey, USA). PFH (C6F14) and lecithin were obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China). BSA, Tween-20, CCK-8, JC-1, anti-Ki67 antibody, RNA extraction kits and TUNEL kits were purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Matrigel was purchased by Corning (New York, USA). N-Acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC) was obtained from Macklin. Mevalonic acid is obtained by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). Live/dead cell viability/cytotoxicity kit and EdU-488 proliferation kit were purchased by Beyotime (Shanghai, China). Anti-MMP2, anti-MMP9, anti-BAX, and anti-BCL-2 antibodies were obtained by Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Anti-E-cadherin, anti-N-cadherin, and anti-β-Actin antibodies were purchased from Boster (Wuhan, China). Anti-YAP, anti-phospho-YAP (p-YAP), anti-Cleaved Caspase3, anti-MST1, anti-LATS1, anti-phospho-LATS1 (p-LATS1), and anti-TAZ antibodies were obtained by Cell Signaling Technology (Boston, USA). Anti-phospho-TAZ (p-TAZ) were purchased from ImmunoWay Technology (San Jose, CA, USA).

Cell culture

Mouse breast cancer 4 T1 cells were supplied by ATCC. Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin and grown in 37 °C at standard humidified incubator with 5% CO2. FLU was used at 10 μg/mL. Ultrasound irradiation parameters were set as follows: acoustic intensity of 1.0 W/cm2, duty cycle of 60%, irradiation time of 30 s, and frequency of 1.0 MHz. The therapeutic ultrasound stimulation was conducted using a dedicated laboratory therapeutic ultrasound device (WED-300; Honda Hi-Tech, Shenzhen, China).

Animal

BALB/c mice (female, 4–5 weeks; Vital River, China) are inoculated subcutaneously on right flank with 4 T1 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells). Animal treatments are approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (DWLL-202500171).

Preparation of NDs and FLU-NDs

NDs and FLU‑NDs were fabricated by a two-step homogenization-emulsification process. Briefly, lecithin (0.15%, w/v), PFH (10%, v/v), and Tween‑20 (0.2%, v/v) were mixed to form the oil phase. The mixture was sonicated on ice using a probe sonicator (UP250, Scientz, China) at 150 W for 5 min (10 s on / 10 s off). BSA (0.15%, w/v) and Tris (2‑carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP, at a 5:1 M ratio to BSA) were dissolved in deionized water. The BSA solution (with or without FLU) was mixed with the pre‑emulsion and subjected to a second sonication for 5 min under the same conditions. During ultrasonication, BSA undergoes partial conformational unfolding and adsorbs onto the PFH-water interface, forming a stabilizing protein shell through interfacial adsorption and hydrophobic self-assembly. The resulting nanodroplets were collected by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm, washed with PBS to remove unencapsulated FLU, and stored at 4 °C.

Characterization of NDs and FLU-NDs

Morphology was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL, Japan) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Zeiss, Germany). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) was employed for assessing size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI). UV–Vis spectrum was obtained using a UV–Vis spectrometer (UV2600i, Shimadzu, Japan). The stability was determined through dynamic size measurements conducted in PBS, RPMI 1640, and RPMI 1640 with FBS at defined intervals. MFLU refers to total input mass of FLU.

After identifying the optimal encapsulation efficiency of freshly prepared FLU-NDs, their encapsulation efficiency was subsequently compared with that of FLU-NDs obtained after lyophilization. In vitro release was profiled by dialysis at 37 °C. To assess ultrasound sensitivity, FLU-NDs were placed in dialysis bags with or without US exposure; 1 mL aliquots were collected at predefined intervals and replaced with equal volumes of PBS. Released FLU was quantified by UV–Vis.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) imaging

The diagnostic ultrasound imaging is performed using an ultrasound device (GE LOGIQ E9, USA) equipped with 9 L probe. For in vitro ultrasound imaging, PBS or FLU-NDs solutions at different concentrations were injected into a dropper immersed in water for imaging. The imaging parameters were as follows: center frequency 9.0 MHz, dynamic range of 60 dB and mechanical index (MI) 1.0/0.7/0.5/0.3. For anesthetized tumor-bearing mice, a 200 μL dose of PBS or FLU-NDs is administered intravenously, and probe is positioned for imaging. The main imaging settings were: 9 L linear-array probe, center frequency 9.0 MHz, dynamic range of 60 dB and MI 0.4.

In vitro tumor-targeting capability

Cellular uptake of NDs by 4 T1 is visualized via fluorescence microscope and quantified by flow cytometry. 4 T1 are treated by FITC-labeled NDs, with or without ultrasound stimulation, for 2 h. Then, they are labeled by DAPI and imaged. For flow cytometry, parallel samples were digested and washed after 2 h for quantitative analysis.

In vivo tumor-targeting capability and biodistribution

When tumors reached approximately 200 mm3, mice received tail-vein injections of FITC-labeled FLU-NDs. Whole-body imaging was performed at predetermined time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h) using an IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Mice were euthanized at the specified time points, and tumors along with major organs were harvested for ex vivo imaging. At 24 h post-injection, tumors were excised, fixed, dehydrated, sectioned, stained with DAPI, and visualized via fluorescence microscopy.

In vitro cytotoxicity

Cytotoxicity of NDs toward 4 T1 cells at varying concentrations was assessed by CCK-8. The microplate reader (TECAN, Switzerland) determined optical density value at 450 nm.

Hemolysis assay

Whole blood was collected from female BALB/c mice by venous puncture, followed by centrifugation to isolate red blood cells (RBCs). The RBCs were washed three times with PBS and then incubated with nanoparticles at different concentrations. PBS served as the negative control, while ddH2O was used as the positive control. All samples were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The supernatant was collected, and its optical density (OD) was measured at 545 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis was calculated using the following formula:Hemolytic rate (\%) = (ODsample - ODnegative) / (ODpositive - ODnegative) × 100%

The liquid–gas phase transition ability

The FLU-NDs suspension was dropped onto a glass slide and incubated on a constant temperature heating plate at 25 ℃ and 37 ℃ respectively. The morphology of the FLU-NDs was observed and recorded under an optical microscope immediately after incubation. Add 500uL of FLU-NDs suspension to a 24-well plate and perform ultrasonic irradiation (1.0 W/cm2, 30 s). After irradiation, quickly transfer the sample onto a slide and observe under an optical microscope to record the morphology of the FLU-NDs. Compare with the unirradiated sample to evaluate its ability to trigger liquid–gas phase transition via ultrasound.

4 T1 cells were seeded into six-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. After 4 h of incubation, 10 µM DCFH-DA was added in the dark, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were washed three times with PBS and examined using a fluorescence microscope.

Zeta potential is a key indicator for predicting the stability of the dispersion system. The Zeta potential of NDs is −36.3 mV, whereas that of FLU-NDs is −14.6 mV (Fig. 2F). Given the high hydrophobicity of FLU, it likely partitions into the lipophilic inner core of the oil-in-water nanodroplets instead of adhering to the particle outer surface. The hydrodynamic diameter remains unaltered post FLU encapsulation, indicating that the decrease in absolute Zeta potential may result from altered core microenvironment and interfacial charge distribution, which could partially shield or reorient surface negative groups and reduce the effective charge density at the shear plane[32]. In addition, UV–Vis spectroscopy confirmed the successful loading of FLU into the nanodroplets, which was validated by the appearance of FLU-specific characteristic absorbance peak (Fig. 2G). Next, we further evaluated the stability of FLU-NDs (Fig. 2H and Fig. S1A) and NDs (Fig. S1B, S1C) in PBS, RPMI 1640, and RPMI 1640 supplemented with FBS. The size of both NDs and FLU-NDs remained below 450 nm over a period of 5 days, indicating good stability under physiological and culture conditions. Owing to their small particle size and favorable stability, these nanodroplets may efficiently extravasate through the leaky tumor vasculature and accumulate within tumor tissues via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect[33], [34].

After determining the optimal FLU content (1:1 (w/w) to BSA) by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, the LE is 26.61% ± 0.79%, and the EE is 79.82% ± 2.35% (Fig. S2A). No statistically significant difference in EE was observed between freshly prepared FLU-NDs and lyophilized FLU-NDs (Fig. S2B). The release curve analysis demonstrated that the release rate of FLU was slow in the absence of ultrasound stimulation, indicating stable drug loading. Notably, the dense early-time profile (15, 30, 45, 60, 75, 90 min) clearly demonstrates that the ultrasound-treated group (US + ) exhibits a significantly faster and sharper release rate compared to the non-ultrasound group (US-) within the very first few minutes to hours post-irradiation (Fig. S3). While the non-sonicated control group only underwent passive leakage of approximately 40% over 8 h, the cumulative release from sonicated FLU-NDs was significantly enhanced, reaching 89.85% ± 4.89% over the identical incubation period (Fig. 2I), confirming the on-demand release capability of FLU-NDs and aligning with the mechanism of acoustic droplet vaporization and cavitation-assisted release from the PFH/BSA matrix. In summary, these data support an ultrasound-triggered on-demand release mode, which can facilitate drug accumulation at the tumor site, improve delivery efficiency, and reduce the systemic off-target effect.

Ultrasound imaging performance

To evaluate imaging capability, we acquired ultrasound images both in vitro and in vivo under greyscale and CEUS. We systematically examined the effects of MI and FLU-NDs concentration on signal intensity. With increasing MI and concentration, the ultrasound imaging performance improved correspondingly (Fig. 2L, M). Time-intensity curve (TIC) analysis (Fig. S4) showed that, relative to PBS, FLU-NDs produced markedly elevated CEUS signals in vitro after US irradiation. Triggered by US stimuli, PFH-laden nanodroplets undergo liquid-to-gas phase transition, a physical process termed ADV. This transition not only enhances ultrasound contrast imaging but also enables localized drug release by UTMD, thereby demonstrating their therapeutic potential[35], [36], [37]. In vivo, the echo intensity of tumors following PBS injection remains extremely low, while FLU-NDs are significantly enhanced after ultrasound stimulation, enabling clear delineation of tumor boundaries and visualization of tumor size (Fig. 2J, K). This enhanced ultrasound contrast imaging holds substantial clinical value, as it enables early detection of lesions, noninvasive and real-time visualization of pathological sites, and effective assessment of therapeutic responses[38].

In summary, these results show that FLU-NDs can be utilized as an efficient US contrast agent to enhance ultrasound imaging performance and support the integrated theranostic strategy, so as to enhance the precision and manageability of therapeutic interventions.

Tumor targeting capability and biosafety

Given the potential involvement of albumin-binding proteins in enhanced tumor uptake, we were able to directly visualize the uptake of NDs by tumor cells. As shown in Fig. 3A and 3B, cellular uptake of NDs was markedly higher than that of the control group. Upon ultrasound stimulation, the targeting efficiency of NDs was further enhanced, with pronounced accumulation observed within tumor cells. These results indicate that ultrasound-induced sonoporation significantly promotes intratumoral accumulation of NDs, consistent with prior reports[39]. To further determine whether the intrinsic targeting ability of NDs was associated with BSA, competitive inhibition experiments were performed (Fig. 3C, D). BSA was added 2 h prior to NDs incubation to preoccupy the albumin-binding receptors. In vivo, we used BALB/c tumor-bearing mice to evaluate the tumor targeting behavior of NDs. As shown in Fig. 3F, real-time fluorescence imaging at different time intervals after tail intravenous injection shows that the signal is mainly located in the tumor and reaches a peak in 4 h (Fig. 3E). Therefore, in the follow-up experiment, we carried out ultrasound treatment 4 h after the injection of NDs. Tumor-related fluorescent signals last for up to 24 h, indicating that NDs can stay in the tumor for a long time, thus promoting drug penetration. After the mice were sacrificed, we collected tumors and major organs, and observed residual fluorescence in the liver and kidneys − which may be attributed to the uptake of the liver's reticular endothelial system and the removal of the kidney[39], [40] (Fig. 3G, H). Frozen tumor sections show obvious green fluorescence around the nucleus (Fig. 3I), verifying the robust tumor-targeting and deep penetration capacity of the NDs.

To enable the in vivo application, biosafety of the nanocarriers must be thoroughly evaluated. We therefore utilized the CCK-8 assay to assess the cytotoxicity of NDs. Experimental findings revealed that following 24-hour co-incubation with varying doses of NDs, the viability of 4 T1 remained above 90.65% (Fig. S5). In addition, we performed a hemolysis assay (Fig. S6), and the findings were in accord with those of the CCK-8 assay. These findings illustrate that the FLU-NDs exhibit outstanding biological compatibility and serve as a viable drug delivery vector.

Liquid-gas phase transition

The liquid-to-gas phase transition capability of FLU-NDs was evaluated by observing morphological change under ultrasonic irradiation. Optical microscopy revealed that the size of FLU-NDs increased significantly post ultrasound exposure relative to untreated counterparts (Fig. 3J), confirming that ultrasound effectively induced phase transition. Particle size distribution analysis further corroborated these morphological changes, showing that the hydrodynamic diameter of the particles shifted markedly to 1.681 µm only upon sonication (Fig. 3K). Notably, FLU-NDs maintained stable dimensions at both 25 °C (room temperature) and 37 °C (physiological temperature) without significant fluctuation (Fig. 3L), which was also evidenced by the particle size distribution of the NDs (Fig. 3M). Such favorable intrinsic stability stems from the fact that thermal energy under physiological ambient conditions is far below the vaporization threshold of the PFH core (boiling point: 56–57 °C), rendering it insufficient to trigger the liquid-to-gas phase transition. Consequently, FLU-NDs retain their structural integrity during systemic circulation and tumor accumulation, effectively preventing premature drug leakage. More importantly, upon ultrasound stimulation, the liquid PFH undergoes a rapid phase transition into gas, accompanied by drastic volume expansion. This process irreversibly ruptures the albumin shell of nanodroplets, creating temporary or permanent micro-fissures and pores. This physical structural disruption accounts for the drug release phenomena observed in our release kinetics: although the active sonication is brief, the structural damage to the carrier matrix allows the surrounding media to penetrate easily, driving the remaining drug molecules inside the core to continuously diffuse out along the concentration gradient over the subsequent hours. Therefore, the ultrasound-triggered drug release, UTMD-mediated biological effects, and contrast-enhanced imaging can be regulated by external sonication, which highlights the exogenous controllability and spatiotemporal precision of our therapeutic strategy.

UTMD combined with FLU-NDs activates the Hippo pathway

We first quantified the protein levels of core Hippo signaling components via western blot analysis. Notably, MST1 expression in the combinatorial treatment group was upregulated 2.34-fold compared with control group (Fig. 4A, B), an effect that may stem from ultrasound-enhanced ROS generation. In line with MST1 upregulation, the upstream key kinase LATS1 was also markedly elevated in the combination treatment group (Fig. 4A, C). This upregulation effectively suppressed the downstream effectors, as evidenced by the distinct downregulation of both YAP and TAZ expression, which was accompanied by a prominent elevation in their respective phosphorylated counterparts (p-YAP and p-TAZ) (Fig. 4A, D, E). We further assessed subcellular localization of YAP using immunofluorescence microscopy after various treatments (Fig. 4F). As is widely recognized, cytoplasmic retention followed by ubiquitination characterizes inactive YAP, whereas activated YAP preferentially undergoes nuclear translocation to associate with TEAD transcription factors[41]. Consistently, cells receiving the combined treatment displayed the weakest YAP fluorescence and minimal overlap between YAP signals and nuclear staining, demonstrating robust inhibition of YAP abundance and transcriptional activity.

To further evaluate Hippo pathway activation, real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR was employed to assess the mRNA abundance of the representative Hippo pathway-regulated target genes. The results showed (Fig. 4G) that the average mRNA expression levels of target genes promoting cell proliferation and negatively regulated by the Hippo pathway, such as CTGF, CYR61, NRP1 and AxL, were reduced to a certain extent. Meanwhile, the mRNA expression level of RASSF4, a pro-apoptotic target gene positively regulated by the Hippo pathway, was significantly increased. These findings are consistent with Hippo pathway activation, suggesting suppression of tumor cell proliferation and metastatic potential. In summary, the results support the notion that FLU-NDs combined with US may promote Hippo pathway activation, accompanied by inhibition of YAP-associated downstream oncogenic signaling.

Mechanistic basis of Hippo pathway activation induced by UTMD

Having verified that the combined therapy synergistically activates Hippo signaling, we next explored the potential mechanism of UTMD to activate. Previous evidence shows that oxidative stress can stimulate the Hippo signaling pathway through multiple mechanisms and affect key components, especially MST1[42], and it is known that UTMD can trigger oxidative stress by increasing intracellular ROS level[43]. As revealed in Fig. S8, the FLU-NDs + US group produced the highest level of intracellular ROS, followed by the NDs + US group. The analysis of flow cytometry further confirmed this trend (Fig. 5D, E). Meanwhile, mitochondrial ROS production was also evaluated, yielding consistent findings with the above observations (Fig. S9). In order to ascertain whether UTMD operates in the upstream of Hippo activation by producing ROS, we pretreated the cells with ROS scavenger NAC; after NAC drug treatment, the expression of MST1 was significantly reduced (Fig. 5B, S10A). In summary, these results show that under combined treatment, ROS may be involved in the upstream activation of the Hippo signaling cascade. As mentioned earlier, Hippo activation will destroy mitochondrial integrity, thus impairing the activity of mitochondria to remove ROS, thus intensifying the accumulation of ROS[44], [45]. As an important second messenger, ROS plays a pivotal role in enhancing Hippo pathway activation. Notably, NAC pretreatment largely attenuated this effect, further supporting a potential association between ROS generation and Hippo pathway activation. Moreover, the higher ROS levels observed in the FLU-NDs + US group compared to the NDs + US group suggest that, in addition to ultrasound-mediated UTMD, the released FLU may also contribute to oxidative stress induction, which is consistent with previous literature reports[46], [47]. FLU acts as a classic HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor. It suppresses tumor growth by depletion of MVA pathway intermediates, rather than direct action on HMGCR. We conducted rescue experiments to eliminate the possibility of non-specific Hippo pathway regulation. As shown in Fig. 5C and S10B, compared with control group, YAP expression was decreased in the free FLU group (p < 0.05) yet restored upon MVA supplementation. In contrast, NAC addition did not significantly change how FLU regulated YAP. Together, these data indicate that FLU primarily suppresses YAP by depleting cellular MVA, while UTMD acts independently via ROS-MST1 activation. The convergence of these complementary mechanisms-upstream MST1 activation by UTMD-induced ROS and downstream YAP inhibition by FLU-accounts for the potent, cooperative activation of the Hippo pathway seen in the FLU-NDs + US group.

We next sought to investigate the potential mechanisms through which Hippo pathway activation restrains tumor proliferation and metastatic progression. At the molecular level, pronounced changes in apoptotic regulators were detected, as combinatorial treatment markedly downregulated anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 while concomitantly upregulating the BAX and Cleaved Caspase-3 (Fig. 5A, F-H). This observation is consistent with the reported role of mitochondria as key downstream effectors of Hippo signaling in breast cancer[48]. Structural changes in mitochondria following different treatments were examined by electron microscopy (Fig. 5K). Relative to the control and NDs groups, intact mitochondria were substantially diminished across treatment groups, with the combined therapy inducing striking mitochondrial injury, such as swelling, broken cristae and damaged outer membrane. Furthermore, we examined changes in mitochondrial membrane potential across different groups. Compared with the control group, decreased red fluorescence and increased green fluorescence were observed following FLU-NDs combined with US treatment, indicating a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5L). It is generally accepted that mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilization and membrane-potential collapse constitute early and irreversible checkpoints in apoptotic cell-death cascades[49], [50]. Transmission electron microscopy further revealed classical apoptotic bodies in cells receiving combination treatment, confirming robust induction of apoptosis (Fig. S7). Numerous studies have proven the Hippo pathway is critical to governing mitochondrial function and sustaining cellular homeostasis. These findings support the potential involvement that the Hippo-mitochondrial axis as a promising and therapeutically actionable target in breast cancer intervention.

Tumor metastasis is tightly linked to EMT, a cellular reprogramming process whereby epithelial cells adopt a more motile, mesenchymal-like phenotype[51]. Canonical EMT features include the downregulation of epithelial markers (e.g., E-cadherin) accompanied by upregulation of mesenchymal markers (e.g., N-cadherin). As illustrated in Fig. 5I and 5 J, US combined with FLU-NDs substantially enhanced E-cadherin expression while simultaneously suppressing N-cadherin levels. These results suggest that Hippo pathway activation may be associated with EMT inhibition, consistent with previous reports[52]. Together, these in vitro data reveal that reactivation of Hippo signaling is accompanied by apoptotic responses and suppression of EMT-related phenotypes in tumor cells.

Antitumor and anti-metastatic capacities in vitro

To compare the cytotoxic capacities of distinct treatments on 4 T1 cells, live-dead staining of cells was performed using Calcein-AM and PI, where green fluorescence denotes viable cells and red fluorescence labels dead cells. Consistent with good biocompatibility, the NDs group showed minimal cell death. By contrast, the FLU-NDs combined with US group exhibited the strongest red fluorescence and the weakest green fluorescence, indicating the maximum number of dead cells and the minimum number of viable cells (Fig. 6A). These results demonstrate that this combination treatment induced the most pronounced cytotoxic response. We next quantified viability by CCK-8. Consistent with the live/dead staining result, cell viability was lowest in the FLU-NDs + US group, significantly lower than every other group (Fig. 6E), demonstrating that UTMD potentiates the anti-cancer effect of FLU-NDs. Anti-proliferative activity was assessed by EdU. Among all treatment groups, the FLU-NDs + US group exhibited the smallest fraction of EdU-incorporated nuclei (Fig. 6B, C). In the wound healing scratch assay, FLU-NDs + US treatment group displayed the lowest migration at 24 h (12.26%±0.55%), with the scratch gap remaining largely unchanged, whereas the NDs + US and free FLU group showed higher closure rates of 40.46% ± 6.56% and 29.19% ± 4.52%, respectively (Fig. 6D, G). In vivo antitumor efficacy

Given the encouraging in vitro results, subsequently, we assessed the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanism of FLU-NDs combined with UTMD in BALB/c mice with established 4 T1 tumors (Fig. 7A). On day 12, mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed (Fig. 7B). The tumor sizes between the Control and NDs groups were comparable, indicating negligible intrinsic antitumor activity of blank NDs. In contrast, the combination group (FLU-NDs + US) yielded the smallest tumors by both volume and weight (Fig. 7C, D). Mean tumor weight in the combination group was 0.05 ± 0.01 g, markedly reduced compared with the FLU group (0.15 ± 0.02 g) and the NDs + US group (0.31 ± 0.01 g), corresponding to approximately 66–84% reductions relative to monotherapies. In tumors in the FLU-NDs + US group, YAP expression was markedly downregulated, which is congruent with the activation of the Hippo pathway observed in vitro. All blood cell counts and serum biochemical indicators stayed within normal physiological ranges.

To conclude, the combined treatment achieved potent tumor inhibition in vivo while maintaining a favorable safety profile, highlighting the potential of FLU-NDs as a promising ultrasound-responsive nanoplatform for future translational applications.

Tumor metastasis constitutes a sophisticated biological process encompassing numerous genes, steps, and stages, in which MMP2 and MMP9 act as “opening pioneers” playing a crucial part. The activity of MMP2 and MMP9 increases, and the ability of tumor cells to degrade the basal membrane (mainly type IV collagen) is enhanced, resulting in the enhanced ability of tumor invasion and metastasis. Therefore, in most cancers, high MMP2 and MMP9 expression correlates with enhanced invasive capacity, higher metastatic potential, and unfavorable clinical prognosis. To further dissect the molecular alterations underlying the anti-metastatic activity of combination therapy, we detected MMP2 and MMP9 expression via IHC (Fig. 8E) and performed quantitative analysis of the staining signals (Fig. S14). The expression levels of MMP9 and MMP2 in the FLU-NDs + US group were significantly reduced, which was associated with the reduced metastatic burden observed in vivo. Although these findings suggest a potential role of MMP2 and MMP9 in the anti-metastatic effect of the combined treatment, the underlying molecular mechanism requires further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we successfully developed FLU-NDs with a PFH core and BSA shell loaded with FLU. FLU-NDs exhibited endogenous tumor targeting via albumin-associated accumulation, exogenous ultrasound-triggered rapid drug release, favorable ultrasound imaging performance, and good biocompatibility. Under UTMD, FLU-NDs generated abundant ROS within the tumor region, thereby activating the upstream Hippo pathway kinases MST1. In parallel, UTMD-induced FLU release effectively suppressed YAP activity, synergistically restoring the tumor-suppressive function of the Hippo pathway. This coordinated modulation of both upstream and downstream signaling further enhanced tumor cell apoptosis and markedly reversed EMT. As shown by both in vitro and in vivo experiments, the combination of FLU-NDs and UTMD exerted a marked inhibitory effect on the proliferation and metastasis of TNBC. Despite the encouraging therapeutic outcomes observed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Our findings suggest an association between UTMD-induced ROS generation, activation of the ROS-MST1-YAP signaling cascade, and Hippo pathway reactivation. However, because ROS scavenger studies, pathway inhibition experiments, and rescue assays were not performed, a direct causal relationship cannot be definitively established. In addition, although FLU-NDs combined with UTMD significantly reduced pulmonary metastatic burden, the molecular mechanisms underlying this anti-metastatic effect remain unclear and were not directly investigated in the present study. Future studies incorporating mechanistic validation and metastasis-focused investigations will be necessary to further elucidate the biological basis of these observations. Nevertheless, our findings support the potential of FLU-NDs as an ultrasound-responsive theranostic platform and provide a promising strategy for Hippo pathway-targeted cancer therapy.
