Synthetic capillary pressure modeling with dipole sonic waves for enhanced reservoir characterization
Authors: Janner Galarza-Alava, Jorge Mendoza-Sanz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a mathematical framework for generating synthetic capillary pressure curves using dipole sonic log information and a fractal model of the pore structure. The approach estimates key petrophysical properties such as porosity and elastic constants by simulating the deformation behavior of porous media under effective stress conditions through boundary element methods. Pore geometries are modeled using a combination of Euclidean shapes and fractal distributions, which allow for realistic simulation of elastic responses and pore volume variability. A central issue addressed is the non-uniqueness problem, where different pore structures can produce similar elastic properties. To mitigate this, the model incorporates a fractal pore population constrained by scaling laws derived from empirical studies. This enables a more consistent and physically meaningful interpretation of sonic log responses. The methodology was validated using field information from the Tapi–TTT Oilfield in the Oriente Basin of Ecuador and further evaluated in the Las Piedras Formation in western Venezuela. In both settings, the synthetic capillary curves showed strong agreement with laboratory measurements. This framework offers a scalable and replicable alternative to core-based methods, providing a robust tool for characterizing reservoirs in formations where core data are unavailable or laboratory techniques are impractical.Keywords: P-wave, S-wave, Fractal, Cores, Sonic logs, Petrophysical propertiesSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Accurate reservoir characterization depends on reliable estimates of petrophysical properties such as porosity, permeability, and capillary pressure, among others1. Traditionally, these properties are derived from laboratory analysis on core samples. However, core data are often unavailable due to geological or operational constraints, leading to uncertainties in modeling the flow and mechanical behavior of subsurface fluids2,3.

Capillary pressure curves are commonly obtained through laboratory techniques such as mercury intrusion, centrifugation, and porous-plate methods, each with well-known limitations related to alteration of wettability, long equilibration times, high operational costs, and the requirement of high-quality core material4,5. Although indirect estimates from NMR logs or empirical capillary pressure correlations have been developed, their performance often depends on regional calibration and may not be generalized in different lithologies5,6.

In this context, well logs-especially dipole sonic logs–have emerged as a valuable indirect source of elastic and structural information about the subsurface. These logs capture travel times of the P and S-waves, which can be translated into elastic moduli and, when combined with suitable physical models, allow the estimation of additional petrophysical parameters such as capillary pressure7,8. The main challenge in this approach is the non-uniqueness of solutions: different pore structures may yield similar elastic responses, complicating the interpretation of sonic data9,10.

To mitigate this ambiguity, recent studies have incorporated concepts from fractal geometry into pore-scale modeling. Sedimentary rocks–such as sandstones, carbonates, and shales–have been shown to exhibit fractal characteristics on multiple scales, as observed by electron microscopy and image analysis11–13. These fractal features influence the elastic and hydraulic behavior and provide a more accurate description of pore size distributions than Euclidean approximations. Thus, Fractal-based models offer a pathway to constrain pore populations and improve the physical realism of pore-size characterization14,15.

Based on these advances in fractal-based pore characterization, recent studies have also explored the possibility of inferring capillary behavior directly from elastic or acoustic measurements, using the sensitivity of pore deformation to fluid–solid interactions and internal pore topology16,17. However, most sonic-log-based formulations still rely on empirical Vp–Vs transforms, oversimplified pore geometries, or data-driven regressions with limited physical interpretability.

In a previous work, Mendoza18 demonstrated that boundary element methods, combined with fractal distributions, can be used to characterize the number, shape and spatial distribution of pores in sedimentary rocks, as well as to derive their elastic constants and porosity from laboratory measurements. Subsequently, Mendoza and Floricich19 extended this approach by incorporating dipolar sonic log data acquired in the field to estimate rock elastic constants within the same fractal distribution framework.

Building on these results, the present study advances the methodology by identifying and formulating mathematical relationships that enable the derivation of pore throat radii from a distribution of pore shapes. This step effectively transforms a geometric distribution on a pore-scale into a representative capillary pressure curve of the analyzed rock. The proposed integrated framework allows for the direct construction of synthetic capillary pressure curves from elastic responses–without relying on laboratory measurements, empirical regional calibrations, or machine-learning training–while explicitly addressing the long-standing issue of non-uniqueness in sonic-based petrophysical inversion.

The novel mathematical model is validated using data from the Tapi–TTT oil field in the Oriente Basin of Ecuador and further tested in the Las Piedras Formation in western Venezuela. These case studies demonstrate the adaptability of the method to varying geological settings and data quality. The approach holds promise for application in fields where the core data are limited or absent, offering a replicable, physically grounded, and scalable alternative to traditional capillary pressure estimation. Moreover, the integration of fractal geometry helps to resolve non-uniqueness issues inherent in traditional petrophysical models, reinforcing the physical credibility of the results.

Methods

Calculation of porosity and elastic constants

To develop a topological model of the pore space, it is essential to define the most representative pore geometries for the rock type under analysis. These geometries are typically obtained by microscopic examination of thin sections of rock samples. Once representative geometries are selected, their surfaces are digitized with high precision to ensure that consecutive points can be connected by straight lines without causing significant distortions9. The digitalization of these surfaces serves as a fundamental input parameter for the numerical algorithm used to model pore deformations under applied stresses. In this study, the boundary element method20–22 is used for pore deformation calculations due to its computational efficiency and high accuracy. The algorithmic framework and mathematical formulation employed in this study were described in detail by Mendoza (1998a)18. The full derivation of the equations used here is provided in the Supplementary Material (S.1).

As previously described, implementing the numerical algorithm requires discretizing the pore shape into interconnected straight-line segments. The non-uniqueness in the compressibility calculation of porous materials extends to Young’s modulus25, affecting all elastic constants since only two are independent23. It might appear that the scaling issue arises solely from the numerical method used to compute elastic constants. However, discretizing the surface of a pore with linear elements is a mathematical approach to characterize the shape of the pore, independent of the deformation method used. In fact, any pore surface can be subdivided into infinitesimally small elements, yielding the same results. Similar conclusions for sharp-edged pores, modeled using continuous functions to represent their surfaces, have been reported24. The advantage of this technique lies in its applicability to irregular surfaces, which is characteristic of real sedimentary rocks.

Pore models

To build a topological and realistic model of the pore space of sedimentary rocks, it is necessary to define the types of pores to use based on the shapes observed in thin sections or high-resolution images of the porous state. Although simple pore geometries are three-dimensional, a plane strain convention is adopted, meaning that stress is applied to cross sections while the transverse axis remains rigid. This assumption is valid for sandstones and carbonates, composed of materials that exhibit elastic resistance under small stresses18,26. This corresponds to the initial stage of a stress-strain curve, where only pore deformation occurs while the rock matrix remains unchanged. A complete description of pore types used in this work is presented in Supplementary Material (S.2).

Fractal dimension and pore topology

Evidence suggests that the rock–pore interface in sedimentary samples, such as carbonates, shales, and sandstones, exhibits statistically fractal geometries13. These patterns have been characterized through fractal measurements using scanning electron microscopy, fracture surface analysis, and thin-section techniques11,12.

Reported fractal dimension (D) values for sandstones, carbonates, and shales typically range from 2.27 to 2.89 in most sedimentary samples, with the exception of novaculite, a thermally metamorphosed microcrystalline quartz sandstone. In sandstones, D values generally fall within the range of 2.70 to 2.8011. Katz and Thompson (1985) used a D value of 2.78 for a clean sandstone with porosities of approximately 15%. We adopted this value for our calculations, as both the rocks reported in the literature and those analyzed in this study correspond to well-consolidated sandstones with very low clay content and porosities between 15% and 23%. D values lower than 2.78 are commonly associated with tight gas sands, which are characterized by low permeability and porosity; therefore, they are not considered in this study.

The fractal nature of the rock–pore interface is supported by geological evidence. Thin-section images of sedimentary rocks frequently exhibit fine structures representing authigenic material growing within the pore space12. A nucleation and crystal growth model was proposed to explain the fractal behavior of pore walls, suggesting that authigenic material follows this nucleation pattern during growth11.

Notably, samples exhibiting extensive fractal behavior contained significant amounts of cement and interstitial material, whereas those with minimal cement and clays showed weak fractal characteristics12. This supports the hypothesis that crystal growth within the pore space influences fractal behavior.

Euclidean and fractal pores

Not all sedimentary rock pores exhibit fractal distributions12. Experimental observations indicate that fractal feature lengths L exist within a specific range , where is limited by the resolution of the measuring instrument. Pores with sizes greater than do not follow fractal distributions and are classified as Euclidean pores. These structures are rigid and voluminous, exhibiting minimal deformation under external stress compared to fractal pores.

Euclidean pores typically include circular, star-shaped, and diamond-shaped pores, which contribute to the high porosities observed in sandstones18. However, their rigidity prevents them from explaining changes in compressibility and porosity under external pressure.

Conversely, fractal structures include tapered and pseudo-star pores, which have small internal volumes and high deformability. These highly flexible pores account for significant variations in elastic properties such as compressibility and Young’s modulus observed in many sedimentary rocks. However, they alone cannot explain the high porosities measured in these formations.

A successful model of sedimentary rock pore topology must combine both Euclidean and fractal pores. The fractal pore distribution is determined by the chosen fractal dimension, while the Euclidean pore distribution is dictated by the rock’s dominant internal volume. This hybrid approach provides a more accurate representation of the porous state in sedimentary formations.

Derivation of capillary pressure curves

From a cumulative porosity and pore volume distribution in Fig. 2, a capillary pressure curve can be derived through a mathematical procedure involving the conversion of porosity into fluid saturation and perimeters into equivalent pore throat radii. Initially, cumulative porosity is transformed into wetting or non-wetting phase saturation by considering that total porosity corresponds to 100% saturation. This is achieved by applying the relationship between fluid saturation and the volume of fluid occupying the pores relative to the total volume of the pore of the rock, allowing the construction of a cumulative saturation curve as a function of porosity referenced to a known baseline.

Subsequently, this curve can be reinterpreted as a capillary pressure curve through the conversion of the pore perimeters into equivalent throat radius. Although pore systems were historically modeled as interconnected cylindrical tubes, later studies demonstrated the complex geometry of the pore space, which led us to introduce the concept of the equivalent throat radius - defined as the maximum inscribed radius within an irregular pore cross section, as illustrated in Fig. 3.

Study area

To evaluate the validity and applicability of the developed mathematical model, it is essential to contrast it with real data from a specific geological formation. In this context, the selection of a study area represents a key step, as it provides the natural setting required to test the model under actual subsurface conditions.

The Tapi–TTT Oilfield is part of the Shushufindi asset, located in Block 57 of the Oriente Basin in northern Orellana Province, Ecuador. As illustrated in Fig. 5, these fields span approximately 7.87 and include 29 operational wells30,31. The well TAPI-001I, highlighted in the figure, was selected as the study well for developing the proposed model.

The Tapi–TTT Oilfield is bounded by two NE–SW-trending structural highs: the Tapi High, located to the northwest, and the larger TTT High, to the southeast. The Tapi structure is interpreted to have developed between the Coniacian and the Lower Eocene over a basement high composed of metamorphosed gabbro32. It has also been proposed that the structure formed over a volcanic island, where the Napo Formation exhibits onlap-type deposits32,33. The field features a fault system with two principal faults: the Tapi Fault and the TTT Fault. These faults generate surfaces to the east and antithetic faults to the west34,35.

The stratigraphy of the Oriente Basin is organized into three tectonosedimentary cycles that reflect eustatic level changes and their influence on depositional sequences33. These cycles include basal erosional surfaces resulting from fluvial valley incision during sea-level falls, followed by sedimentary infills associated with fluvial, estuarine, and transgressive systems.

The generalized stratigraphic column of the Oriente Basin, illustrated in Fig. 6, synthesizes these units and their key characteristics, highlighting the evolution of depositional environments under varying geodynamic contexts and their relationship to eustatic changes.

Generalized stratigraphic column of the Oriente Basin.

For this study, the T and U sandstones of the Napo Formation were selected due to their significance as reservoir units in the Oriente Basin, Ecuador’s primary oil-producing region36. These sandstones exhibit key petrophysical properties, such as high porosity and permeability, making them strategic targets for the characterization and optimization of hydrocarbon exploitation.

Data colletion and analyses

To acquire the petrophysical data required for this study, well logs from the Tapi–TTT Oilfield were utilized, focusing on the ”U” and ”T” sandstones, which are productive zones within the Napo Formation. Data were provided during the second half of 2020 to the Escuela Superior Politécnica del Litoral (ESPOL) by Petroecuador EP.

For the model to function effectively, the selected sand must be sufficiently clean and the interval must have an adequate thickness. Additionally, the availability of at least gamma-ray and dipole sonic logs are essentials as well as density, neutron, and electrical logs. Based on these criteria, a database of 29 wells was filtered, eliminating 6 wells that did not meet the conditions necessary to be used in the model. The well TAPI-001I, was selected as the study well for developing the proposed model, as it meets all the minimum requirements for model development, including sufficient sand cleanliness, adequate interval thickness, and the availability of the necessary well log data.

For well data processing and analysis, the Lassio library in Python was employed37. This library enables the reading of LAS files and their conversion into a tabular data format, streamlining the manipulation and analysis of well logs. Initially, exploratory data analysis was performed to identify the thicknesses of interest within the stratigraphic column of the well. This process included determining the top and base of the sand intervals, which were identified through the correlation of multiple logs, including gamma ray, density (), neutron porosity (), and sonic transit time ().

Effective pressure estimation

An important parameter considered for developing the model was determining the effective pressure on the sample under reservoir conditions. This is essential because the computational model requires input values to be a function of effective pressure, whereas the available well log data depend on depth. Effective pressure was calculated as the difference between pore pressure and overburden stress46,47, as expressed in Eq. (20).

The overburden stress was estimated by multiplying the rock density by gravity and height. Pore pressure was determined using a relationship that accounts for depth and the average specific gravity of the fluid from the surface to the desired depth.

The sonic logs for the study intervals exhibit a clear compaction trend, characterized by a progressive and continuous increase in both P and S wave velocities. This behavior indicates that no noticeable or potential zones of overpressure or undercompaction are present within the analyzed strata.

Results and discussion

The sands comprising this formation are relatively thin, with thicknesses ranging from 20 to 200 feet. Due to these characteristics, a single sand layer does not provide an adequate pressure range for reliable modeling. However, given the similarity in their sedimentological properties, data from all the target sands were combined, resulting in a more robust dataset. This approach enables the generation of a representative model of the average conditions of the sands in the region, albeit not specific to any single layer.

The use of synthetic well logs in the Ecuador case introduces potential sources of uncertainty; however, their reliability was thoroughly assessed through direct comparison with intervals containing real measurements. The validation showed strong consistency between real and synthetic responses, with an average relative error of only 3.7% (96.3% similarity) and correlation coefficients exceeding 0.92 for key logs such as RHOB, DT, and neutron–density porosity. These metrics confirm that the synthetically reconstructed curves faithfully reproduce the behavior of the original logs and are suitable for evaluating the proposed mathematical framework.

These results reinforce the utility of the approach based on linear regressions and synthetic analysis for characterizing petrophysical properties and validating well log data.

To characterize the porous structure of the selected rock, approximately 0.03% of the pores were modeled using Euclidean geometries, whereas fractal geometries represented the remaining 99.97%. This contrast highlights the dominant role of fractal structures in capturing the rock’s porous and elastic behavior. The pore population was thus modeled as a combination of Euclidean and fractal pores. The relative proportion was determined based on two considerations: first, thin-section and SEM observations provided an approximate ratio of thin pores to larger ones; second, this ratio served as a starting point for a trial-and-error procedure in which the number and types of pores were manually adjusted to fit the elastic data.

The results of the elastic constant and porosity modeling, shown in Fig. 8, demonstrate a good acceptable correspondence between the model and the experimental data, including minimal error bars due to precise pressure control during measurements.

To evaluate the predictive performance of the methodology, the synthetic capillary pressure curves obtained from the elastic–fractal model were directly compared with the available laboratory MICP measurements. The synthetic curves successfully reproduce the overall shape, drainage trajectory, and characteristic transition from bound to free-water regimes. Minor discrepancies at low capillary pressures fall within the expected experimental variability reported for sandstone MICP data. These results demonstrate that the proposed pore-topology framework, when constrained by dipole sonic logs and fractal–Euclidean pore distributions, reliably captures the capillary behavior of the analyzed samples.

For completeness, the simulations were also extended to D values around 2.87. Furthermore, because the capillary pressure curves are not fitted to experimental capillary data but are instead computed directly from the pore-size distribution that best reproduces the experimentally measured elastic moduli, the resulting synthetic curves closely resemble those obtained from laboratory measurements. This agreement supports the selection of as a physically consistent value for the analyzed samples. A comparison with previous approaches further clarifies the positioning of the present method within the broader landscape of capillary pressure prediction. The empirical workflow of7, although effective for low-permeability () sandstones, depends on region-specific calibration and core measurements, which limits its transferability. Similarly, the machine-learning strategy of15 provides practical predictive capability but its performance is inherently tied to the characteristics and representativeness of the training dataset. In contrast, the synthetic capillary curves generated in this study arise from a fully mechanistic formulation that links dipole sonic velocities to pore-scale deformation through the boundary element method and a hybrid fractal–Euclidean pore topology. The successful application of the model to two geologically distinct, consolidated sandstone reservoirs–yielding low reconstruction errors and consistent agreement with laboratory capillary data–demonstrates its broader applicability and its advantage in contexts where empirical calibration or extensive core datasets are not available.

Conclusion

This work presents a methodology for calculating a topological model of the porous space in sedimentary rocks, emphasizing the integration of fractal and Euclidean pore distributions and their influence on the material’s elastic constants. The approach combines computational tools and field data to develop accurate representations of the porous space’s behavior under varying conditions. The effect of obtaining a unique pore distribution has a direct implication for the uniqueness of the capillary curve predicted by the model. Because the pore volume as a function of pore perimeter, which is directly related to fluid saturation, is uniquely determined by the pore distribution, the resulting capillary curve is therefore uniquely defined.

In the first case study, corresponding to the Tapi–TTT Oilfield in Ecuador, the synthetic capillary pressure curve generated by the model showed a deviation of approximately 10 psi compared to the laboratory measured curve. This discrepancy is likely due to the incorporation of synthetic data to compensate for incomplete well log sections and the limited availability of hard field data. The use of synthetic inputs, although necessary, increases the potential for error in the modeling process. To validate the methodology under more robust data conditions, the model was subsequently applied to the Las Piedras formation in western Venezuela, where complete and high-quality logs were available. The results obtained in this second case closely matched the experimental capillary pressure data, confirming the model’s reliability and accuracy when sufficient and consistent input data are available.

Two examples demonstrate the application of the methodology to reservoirs in different sedimentary basins, one is located in the eastern sedimentary basin of Ecuador (Tapi–TTT Oilfield), and the other in the western sedimentary basin of Venezuela (Las Piedras formation). Using distinct data acquisition methods, including well log profiles and laboratory measurements. These examples confirm the model’s capability to accurately simulate changes in porous space and elastic constants over a broad pressure range, aligning closely with experimental observations.

The model offers an innovative and cost-effective approach to calculating capillary pressures directly from well logs, such as dipolar sonic logs, bypassing the need for traditional laboratory core measurements or more expensive techniques like Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) logging. In addition to capillary curves, the methodology delivers valuable information on elastic constants and, in some cases, it could lead to formation permeability. The model’s most significant contribution lies in characterizing the pore space topology, which fundamentally dictates the physical behavior of reservoir rocks.

However, certain criteria must be met for successful application. The reservoir must have access to a minimum suite of well logs, including dipolar sonic, density, and gamma-ray logs, with neutron and resistivity logs (both shallow and deep) being highly desirable. Additionally, the rock thickness should be sufficient to create a pressure gradient from the top to the base, enabling measurable changes in elastic constants. For thinner formations, discontinuous layers with similar facies and pressure ranges can be combined if available to achieve the necessary elastic variations as long as the rocks under consideration are quite similar in terms of geological configuration.

In this study, model reliability was ensured by restricting the analysis to clean, well-consolidated sandstone intervals, where the NPHI–Wyllie porosity estimation and the underlying petrophysical assumptions remain valid. It must be noted, however, that the approach has not yet been tested in mixed lithologies, carbonates, shales, or clay-rich units; thus, its sensitivity to these rock types cannot be evaluated with the current dataset. Future work should focus on extending, calibrating, and validating the methodology in more heterogeneous lithologies to assess its broader applicability and potential limitations.

The methodology presented in this article has been applied exclusively to sandstones, primarily for two reasons. First, elastic measurement data from various sandstone types were available, which are essential for calibrating the elastic moduli response to seismic velocities. Second, the primary porosity of these rocks is intercrystalline, making them comparable to the types of rocks on which the original fractal measurements were performed (Katz & Thompson, 1985). Applying this methodology to carbonate rocks would be feasible if the analysis is restricted to those exhibiting porosity that can be described using fractal geometry. Gang et al. (2025) demonstrated that carbonates with secondary, fracture-type porosity can be characterized using fractal methods49. This requirement limits the types of carbonates to which the methodology can be applied; however, naturally fractured carbonate reservoirs are well-established hydrocarbon producers.

Regarding shales, previous studies have reported that their very low permeability has limited research on fractal characteristics related to pore types and their contributions to permeability. Their capillary curves are expected to exhibit high irreducible water saturation. Nevertheless, Zhang et al. (2023), using high-resolution imaging, showed that some pores in shales display fractal structures. This observation suggests that the methodology could potentially be applied to both carbonates and shales. However, specific measurements of fractal dimension and elastic constants are required to properly assess its feasibility50.

The synthetic capillary pressure curves derived in this study contribute to the efficient characterization of subsurface reservoirs, promoting better decision-making in resource extraction. These advancements support the goals of Sustainable Development Goal 12 (Responsible consumption and production) and SDG 9 (Industry, innovation, and infrastructure), by optimizing subsurface analysis workflows and reducing dependence on labor-intensive core analysis.
