Optimization of the Needle Tip Position for the Proper Contrast Spread Pattern and Clinical Outcomes During Cervical Transforaminal Epidural Injections
Authors: Jung Hwan Lee, Jun Ho Lee, Sang Gyu Kwak, Min Cheol Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo assess the relationship between needle tip position and contrast spread pattern as well as the presence of vascular spread during cervical transforaminal epidural steroid injection (CTFESI). Additionally, to assess how different needle tip positions influence pain reduction effects.MethodsA total of 185 consecutive patients who received CTFESIs to treat neck and upper extremity pain due to cervical disc herniation or stenosis were included in the study. Data were collected from 287 CTFESIs, including demographic data, numeric rating scale (NRS), and fluoroscopic findings at injection. Needle tip position was categorized into the lateral, middle, and medial third areas of the articular pillar. The contrast spread pattern was classified into epidural, intraforaminal, and extraforaminal. Vascular contrast spread was divided into present and absent. The relationship between needle tip position and contrast spread pattern, as well as the presence of vascular spread, was assessed. The subsequent reductive change in NRS according to needle tip position, contrast spread pattern, and vascular spread was also investigated.ResultsPositioning the Needle in the Medial Third of the Articular Pillar Markedly Produced the Proper Epidural Spread of the Contrast as Well as the Absence of the Vascular Spread Compared to the Lateral Third Position. Moreover, a Significant Improvement in Pain Reduction and the Subsequent Epidural Contrast Flow Was Observed and Reproduced Following Needle Placement in the Medial Third Area Compared to the Other Locations.ConclusionPositioning the needle at the medial one‐third of the articular pillar during CTFESI promoted improved access to the optimal target area with more spread into the epidural space, as well as providing improved pain reduction.Keywords: cervical epidural injection, contrast spread, intravasation, needle position, transforaminal injection

Introduction

Cervical transforaminal epidural steroid injection (CTFESI), is performed to deliver the medication into the epidural space as well as around the cervical root area. These areas are regarded as the main sources of neck and upper limb pain in patients with various cervical pathologies [1, 2, 3, 4]; thus, CTFESI is commonly performed to control these pains by removing the chemical mediators that stimulate the main pain sources [2, 3, 4].

Therefore, the appropriate needle positioning to enable accurate drug delivery into the main pain source areas, such as the epidural space and nerve root, is crucial to maximize the clinical effects and avoid adverse events. It is generally granted that successful CTFESIs are accomplished by delivering medication into the epidural space—not limited to the foraminal or extraforaminal areas [5, 6]—while, to avoid intrathecal injection and its related serious complications, the needle should not be advanced beyond the midpoint of the articular pillars or into the medial area to the uncinate [7, 8].

Since the targeted area, such as the neural foramina or articular pillars, which are situated at the needle trajectory site during the routine CTFESI, is usually concentrated with superfluous vascular structures, avoiding inadvertent intravascular drug administration has been considered. Indeed, inadvertent intravascular drug administration not only causes adverse events but also reduces the diagnostic or therapeutic value of the injection. Meanwhile, the incidence of vascular injection during CTFESI was reported as 32.8%, remarkably higher than the incidence rate of 10%–13% following the lumbosacral technique [9].

Subsequently, numerous studies have previously evaluated the appropriate need placement for obtaining optimal contrast flow [5, 6, 10]. However, these studies only focused on the contrast flow pattern as the determining factor in optimal injections instead of analyzing the importance of the needle location and its implications on inadvertent vascular spread and clinical outcomes.

Therefore, additional studies that evaluate the relationship between needle position and contrast flow, as well as clinical outcomes, along with unintended vascular spread, would be meaningful. Thus, this study aimed to determine whether the proper location of the needle at injection during CTFESI might produce an optimal contrast spread as well as less vascular leak and might subsequently produce more favorable clinical outcomes.

Methods

This was a retrospective study that reviewed 185 consecutive patients who received a CTFESI to treat neck and upper extremity pain due to cervical spine diseases, including intervertebral disc herniation and spinal stenosis. These patients had previously been diagnosed using simple radiography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) alongside clinical findings. CTFESIs were performed by a single interventional physiatrist with over 15 years of experience performing the procedure.

CTFESI Technique

Each patient was placed on a table in the supine position with a bolster placed beneath the shoulder to extend the head and neck slightly. An anteroposterior (AP) fluoroscopic view was obtained to identify the bony anatomy relevant to the injection target, which had been established based on the clinical and radiological evaluations. Then, the fluoroscope was rotated obliquely from the ipsilateral side to the affected side by approximately 30–40 degrees; meanwhile, the patient's head and neck were rotated towards the contralateral oblique side as maximally tolerated (target side up) to present the optimum visualization of the selected neural foramen. The skin directly over the posterior half of the target neuroforamen was draped using a sterile technique, and a local anesthetic was applied. A 25‐gage spinal needle was advanced to the division between the middle and lower thirds of the superior articular process that formed the posterior border of the neural foramen using a tunnel vision technique; this method entailed inserting the needle parallel to the fluoroscopic beam, which appeared as a single radiopaque dot superimposed over the target. After contacting the target, the needle was slightly redirected anteriorly and advanced into the neural foramen. The needle was moved forward to the articular pillars in an AP view, and 0.5 mL of contrast dye was injected under real‐time fluoroscopic guidance to demonstrate the contrast spread along the articular pillars and/or epidural space. If an arterial flow pattern was detected in the real‐time fluoroscopic view, or the contrast flow was dominantly observed in the venous system rather than around the nerve root or the epidural area, the needle was repositioned until a sufficient amount of contrast was spread around the target area, and the arterial flow was not observed by real‐time fluoroscopy. The intended needle placement was in the medial third or at least the middle third of the articular pillars; however, lateral placement occurred alongside failure of optimal needle placement, mainly due to technical difficulties or because the patients experienced severe pain during needle advancement. A 2 mL of a mixed solution of local anesthetic and dexamethasone was slowly injected for treatment [11].

Demographic and Clinical Data Acquisition

A total of 287 CTFESIs were performed on 185 consecutive patients. Patient data were collected, including age, gender, diagnosis, level and side of injection, and pain score (numeric rating scale (NRS)). The NRS reduction rate (%) was obtained to assess the degree of pain reduction achieved by CTFESI by dividing the change in NRS before and 2 weeks after CTFESI by the NRS before CTFESI. “NRS success” was additionally calculated to determine successful pain reduction. “NRS success” was defined as a reduction in NRS after CTFESI of at least 50% compared with before CTFESI [12, 13].

Needle Tip and Contrast Pattern Analysis

In addition to the needle tip position, the contrast spread pattern and presence of vascular contrast spread at injection were obtained by reviewing the AP fluoroscopic view recorded during contrast injection after final needle positioning, as determined by the independent observer.

Needle tip position was categorized into the lateral, middle, and medial third of the articular pillar (Figure 1A(a–c)) [11, 14]. The contrast spread pattern was classified into epidural, intraforaminal, and extraforaminal. Furthermore, the contrast spread pattern was defined as epidural when the contrast was filled medial to the uncinate process; intraforaminal was when the contrast was observed within the margins of the articular pillars; extraforaminal was when the contrast pattern was filled lateral to the articular pillars or observed as an ill‐defined myofascial pattern (Figure 1B(a–c)). The inner margin of the articular pillar corresponds to the contour where the lateral edge of the vertebral body is, while the outer margin is defined by the most lateral projection of the articular pillar [15]. If the contrast pattern was included in two or more categories, the classification was determined as the medial extent of the contrast flow. For example, a contrast spread pattern that was limited to the articular pillar was regarded as intraforaminal. Comparatively, an epidural spread pattern was identified when the contrast spread into the medial via the uncinate process, epidural space, and along the articular pillar. When the contrast was found lateral to and within the articular pillar, this was defined as intraforaminal [5]. A vascular spread was defined as present when venous contrast spread was found with the main target contrast pattern after needle repositioning (Figures 2A,B, 3A–C, 4A,B).

Statistical Analysis

A chi‐square test was performed to identify the relationship between needle tip position and contrast spread pattern and vascular spread, and to assess the association between needle tip position and minor side effects, such as a transient headache or dizziness. Upon a significant association being found, multinomial or binary logistic regression analyses were conducted for clearer results. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with the Tukey honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test or independent t‐test was used to evaluate the differences in pretreatment NRS, NRS reduction rate (%), and NRS success according to needle tip position, contrast spread pattern, and vascular spread. Furthermore, the difference in the NRS success rate following fluoroscopic findings, including needle tip position, contrast spread pattern, and vascular spread, was evaluated using a chi‐square test and binary logistic regression analysis. In addition, multivariable analysis was conducted to identify the relationship between the NRS success and fluoroscopic findings, including needle position, contrast spread pattern, and vascular spread. A simple linear regression (univariate analysis) and multiple linear regression (multivariate analysis) were employed to determine the relationship between the fluoroscopic findings and NRS reduction rate (%). The needle tip position was classified into three categories (lateral, middle, and medial). Therefore, two indicator variables were created in the linear regression analysis for use as independent variables: one was coded as 1 if the needle tip position was lateral; otherwise, 0 was recorded, and the other was coded as 1 if the needle tip position was middle and 0 otherwise. Similarly, the contrast spread pattern had three categories (epidural, intraforaminal, and extraforaminal), and two indicator variables were created: one was coded as 1 if the contrast spread pattern was intraforaminal; 0 otherwise, while the other was coded as 1 if the pattern was extraforaminal; similarly, it was otherwise coded as 0. These indicator variables were also included as independent variables in the regression model.

Results

Demographic data, diagnosis, and level and side of injection are presented in Table 1. The C5–6 level was found to be the most frequently treated area (58.7%), followed by C6–7 (30.3%) and C4–5 (11.1%). In 53.7% of the total injections, the needle tips were placed in the middle third, 25.8% were placed in the lateral third, and 20.6% were located in the medial third of the articular pillar. In total, the contrast spread pattern in 53.7% of the total injections was classified as epidural, 34.1% was intraforaminal, and 12.2% was extraforaminal. Inadvertent vascular spreads were observed in 28.9% of the total injections (Table 1). No intrathecal contrast spread was found in any injection.

Depending on needle tip position, differences were observed in contrast spread pattern and vascular spread (chi‐square test, contrast spread pattern: p < 0.001; vascular spread: p = 0.002) (Table 2). The epidural contrast spread pattern was shown more frequently when the needle tip was located in the medial third of the articular pillar in the multinomial or binary logistic regression analyses, whereas the vascular spread was revealed less often (Table 3). There were no serious complications after CTFESI. However, a transient headache or dizziness was observed in 12 cases, five of which were manifested when the needle tip position was at the lateral third of the articular pillar, three were in the middle third of the articular pillar, and four were in the medial third of the articular pillar. No significant differences were observed in the occurrence rate of minor side effects according to needle tip position (chi‐square test, p = 0.526). Regarding the pain reducing effect according to needle tip position, contrast spread pattern, and vascular spread, the pain reduction rate was the largest when the needle tip is located at the medial third of the articular pillar, followed by the middle third and lateral third (ANOVA with HSD, p < 0.001) (Table 4). Moreover, the pain reduction rate was the largest when the epidural contrast spread pattern was observed, followed by the intraforaminal and extraforaminal contrast spread patterns (ANOVA with HSD, p < 0.001) (Table 4). Additionally, according to needle tip position and contrast spread pattern, the rate of NRS success was different (chi‐square test, p < 0.001) (Table 4). In addition, the pain reduction rate was larger when vascular spread was absent than when vascular spread was observed (chi‐square test, p < 0.001); meanwhile, a higher rate of NRS success was shown without vascular spread (chi‐square test, p = 0.012) (Table 4). In the univariate binary logistic regression analysis, when the needle tip was located in the medial third of the articular pillar, a higher NRS success rate was shown compared to when the needle was located in the lateral third of the articular pillar. Furthermore, fewer patients showed NRS success when intraforaminal and extraforaminal contrast spread patterns or vascular spread were revealed (Table 5). Multivariate binary logistic regression demonstrated that when contrast spread occurred in the epidural space, a higher rate of NRS success was observed compared to those when intraforaminal or extraforaminal contrast spread patterns were shown (Table 5). However, the rate of NRS success was not significantly different in the multivariate binary logistic regression analysis, regardless of needle position or vascular spread.

Discussion

This study indicates that placing the needle tip position in the medial portion, and especially in the medial third, of the articular pillar during CTFESI is advantageous for obtaining a contrast spread into the epidural space along with the intraforaminal area. Moreover, these contrast spreads can eventually yield greater pain reduction with less probability of inadvertent intravascular drug leakage. Because axial or upper extremity pains originate from the epidural space along the nerve root, optimal delivery of medication into the epidural target area is supposed to be the crucial factor to accomplish improved clinical outcomes [6, 16].

A study by Hoang et al. compared contrast flow patterns according to different needle positions and found that foraminal or junctional needle placement between the foramen and extraforaminal zone obtained higher rates of intraforaminal contrast spread than extraforaminal needle placement [10]. However, Hoang et al. did not further classify the needle tip placements according to the location within the foramen to analyze the contrast pattern.

Meanwhile, the Levin et al. study subdivided needle positions into three areas within the neural foramen in addition to the extraforaminal area. Medially placed needles, such as the middle and lateral third of the articular pillar, obtained higher incidences of epidural and/or intraforaminal contrast flow than needles placed lateral to the articular pillars [5]. This result suggests that a needle position in the middle and lateral third of the articular pillar might contribute to a more favorable contrast spread than outside of the articular pillar. However, this study did not include the cases of more medially placed needles, as in the medial third of the articular pillars.

Shipley et al. analyzed contrast flow patterns concerning needle positions, including the more medial needle placements. Additionally, Shipley et al. divided these positions into lateral to, lateral half, medial half, and even beyond the medial cortex of the articular pillars [6]. The study by Shipley et al. revealed that over 80% and even 100% of epidural spread was observed when the needle was placed in the medial half and beyond the medial cortex of the articular pillars, respectively. This study strengthened the assumption that a more medially placed needle during CTFESI could obtain epidural contrast flow.

As anticipated, a more medial placement of the needle tip near the epidural space may allow for more effective delivery of medication into the epidural space, thereby increasing the likelihood of favorable clinical outcomes. As long as the needle tip does not advance medial to the uncinate process, it may be acceptable or preferable for the needle tip to be positioned medial to the midpoint of the articular pillar [8]. However, medial placement of the needle was frequently abandoned by the practitioner due to concerns regarding dural penetration or vascular invasion. This skepticism has contributed to reluctance in adopting medial needle placements in clinical practice, as well as a paucity of publications on the topic. Subsequently, the limited evidence may reinforce skepticism and reluctance to implement this technique among clinicians.

Advancing the needle along the posterior aspect of the neural foramen while maintaining contact with the ventral bony surface of the articular pillar might help avoid vascular penetration, even if the needle was advanced beyond the medial half of the articular pillar. Similarly, locating the needle tip lateral to the uncinate process in AP view might avoid the actual thecal sac penetration because the medial border of the articular pillar was usually supposed to be positioned in the same plane as the uncinate process and lateral to the thecal sac [6]. In the current study, designed to place the needle tip bounded between the medial one‐third of the articular pillar and the uncinate process, intrathecal contrast flow was never seen, nor any of its associated serious or fatal adverse effects. Furthermore, as a refutation of the common misconception, medial one‐third needle placement produced less vascular flow and a comparable incidence of transient headaches or dizziness compared to middle and lateral third needle placement.

There has been a paucity of studies demonstrating the influence of needle position on contrast flow patterns or clinical outcomes. To our knowledge, there are no studies examining CTFESIs with the final needle position in the medial portion of the articular pillar. Park et al. found no significant reduction in pain between the intraforaminal and extraforaminal spread groups during computed tomography‐guided CTFESI. However, Park et al. further categorized the two groups according to contrast spread pattern and did not assess the relationship of needle tip position with contrast flow or clinical outcome [17]. Our study revealed that medial needle placement outperformed lateral needle placement in terms of pain reduction by optimizing the target‐specific delivery of injectate to the epidural and/or intraforaminal area.

The incidence of vascular spread in this study was estimated as 28.9% of the total injections, slightly less than the 32.8% found in research conducted by Smuck et al. [9] Nonetheless, both studies defined vascular flow observed during injection using real‐time fluoroscopy as positive vascular spread. However, Smuck et al. included an intravascular‐only infusion as well as simultaneous epidural and intravascular spread at the initial needle placement. In contrast, our study included only the associated intravascular (mainly venous) spread after obtaining the target‐specific contrast flow and excluding arterial flow through needle readjustments. A possible explanation is that the needle readjustment for appropriate contrast spread after initial vascular spread did not change the category of present vascular spread into an absent one because the remnant vascular contrast pattern was left in the fluoroscopic view.

Notably, the present vascular spreading was more related to the lateral needle position than medial (p < 0.001) and reduced clinical improvement (p = 0.009). During CTFESI in this study, if the vascular contrast pattern was prominently visible, the needle was repositioned to ensure sufficient spread of contrast around the nerve root or epidural area without arterial flow under real‐time fluoroscopy. Thus, even though we could not completely exclude that a small amount of the drug was accidentally simultaneously injected into an artery, most cases where the drug was seen to spread through vessels (vascular spread) likely followed veins rather than arteries. The paravertebral plexus, a venous network that exists in the lateral portion of the articular pillar, was more extensive than the venous system in the medial portion [18]. This can explain the results of our study, in which a more frequent vascular spread was observed following the lateral needle placement.

A transient headache and dizziness were noted in about 12 cases (4.2%), which might be assumed to result from the intravascular drug injection. However, of the 12 cases, vascular flow was only observed in 4 cases (33.3%), which was not significantly greater than the overall incidence of vascular flow observed among all injections (28.9%). As mentioned previously, most of the vascular spread cases in this study were supposed to be venous flow. Thus, an intravenous injection was not usually considered to be the cause of adverse events, including headache or dizziness, because the venous plexus, a network of interconnecting vessels, bypassed the major veins [19, 20]. Similarly, the incidence of these adverse events was not related to needle tip position. Thus, these side effects might be attributed to another cause other than an adverse event from systemic drug administration, such as a vasovagal reaction.

This study had some limitations. First, this study was conducted using a retrospective design. The cases that were not followed up were not included and analyzed in this study. Second, the follow‐up period was short. However, our purpose was not to investigate the duration of the steroid effect. Instead, this study analyzed the contrast spread pattern and clinical result according to the different needle tip positions before the steroid effect deteriorated. Furthermore, dexamethasone, which was used in the current study, tends to have a shorter duration than methylprednisolone or triamcinolone; the treatment effects deteriorate within a short‐term period. In addition, several reports have stated that clinical data at long‐term follow‐ups are difficult to regard as clinical effects from previously performed epidural steroid injections because the effects substantially deteriorate over the follow‐up duration [21, 22, 23]. Thus, this study established a short follow‐up period, such as 2 weeks.

Positioning the needle into the medial third of the articular pillar during CTFESI helped inject the drug to a more optimal target area, along with fewer intravascular injections than required for the middle or lateral articular pillars. Moreover, positioning the needle at the medial one‐third of the articular pillar promoted an increased spread into the epidural space, leading to improved pain reduction effects. Furthermore, this effect could be accomplished without intrathecal injection or increasing the risk of minor side effects.
