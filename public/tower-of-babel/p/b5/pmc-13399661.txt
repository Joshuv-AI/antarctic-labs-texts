Measurement of Plasma Free Hemoglobin Using the Hemolysis Index of the Mindray BS‐800
Authors: Punchisa Viruttanachai, Nuttiruetai Chanpo, Anchalee Thedsawad, Janejira Kittivorapart, Theera Ruchutrakool, Chattree Hantaweepant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe Mindray BS‐800 chemistry analyzer (Mindray Bio‐Medical Electronics) has a hemolysis index (HI) function and is a candidate method for detecting free hemoglobin (fHb). This study aimed to validate the HI on the Mindray BS‐800 for measuring plasma fHb.MethodsUsing EDTA plasma diluted with normal saline, the analyzer measured hemoglobin absorbance at 570 and 605 nm. Results were compared with those of the Harboe method (reference standard), which measures absorbance at 380, 415, and 450 nm. Precision, linearity, limit of detection, interference, reference range, and carryover were also assessed.ResultsReplication experiments showed within‐run and between‐run precision of 0.41%–1.89% and 0.71%–2.10%, respectively. Linearity of fHb measurements was established over 2.3–507.9 mg/dL. The limits of detection and quantitation were 2 and 5 mg/dL, with coefficient of variation values of 16.28% and 6.37%, respectively. No notable interference was observed at triglyceride concentrations up to 520.9 mg/dL; however, bilirubin levels of 14.2–34.7 mg/dL produced a negative bias of up to 15%. The reference range was established at ≤ 23.9 mg/dL. Within the 0–130 mg/dL range, fHb correlated strongly with the Harboe method, yielding the regression equation: HI (mg/dL) = 1.0886 × Harboe (mg/dL) − 0.3071 (r = 0.9967).ConclusionsMeasurement of fHb using the HI on the Mindray BS‐800 analyzer provides accurate and reliable results. This approach offers a practical alternative to the Harboe method for routine clinical laboratory use, with improved safety and turnaround time.Keywords: assay validation, harboe method, hemolysis index, intravascular hemolysis, Mindray BS‐800, plasma free hemoglobin

Introduction

Intravascular hemolysis (IVH) occurs when red blood cells are destroyed within blood vessels, releasing substantial amounts of free hemoglobin (fHb) into the bloodstream. In contrast, extravascular hemolysis involves destruction of red blood cells by the reticuloendothelial system, primarily in the spleen or liver, where hemoglobin (Hb) is metabolized intracellularly. As a result, little or no fHb is released into the plasma, and quantification of serum or plasma fHb thus serves as a potential biomarker for IVH [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6].

Distinguishing IVH from extravascular hemolysis relies on the patient's history, physical examination, and laboratory findings. Extravascular hemolysis is typically characterized by splenomegaly or microspherocytes on peripheral blood smears, whereas IVH is suggested by hemoglobinuria or the presence of schistocytes, though accurate microscopic interpretation requires experienced personnel. Although standard markers such as indirect hyperbilirubinemia and low haptoglobin confirm hemolytic anemia, they are insufficient to localize the site of destruction. Furthermore, differentiating IVH from occult blood loss can be difficult because both may present with anemia and reticulocytosis without visible bleeding. These complexities complicate the differentiation of IVH from other conditions, potentially increasing diagnostic costs and delaying treatment.

The Harboe method is currently considered the reference method for measuring fHb [3, 5, 6, 7, 8]. However, it is less suitable for routine clinical laboratory use because of several limitations, including safety concerns during handling and disposal of chemical waste such as sodium carbonate. Moreover, it is a manual technique that requires technical expertise and is time‐consuming, increasing the risk of variability and analytical error. Modern automated clinical chemistry analyzers can quantify serum or plasma indices, such as lipemia, icterus, and hemolysis, which are often used to identify sample interference. In many countries, these analyzers have been adapted to calculate the hemolysis index (HI), which can serve as a surrogate marker for detecting IVH [3, 5, 7]. Compared with traditional methods, the automated approach is rapid, reproducible, safe, cost‐effective, and capable of high‐throughput analysis.

Currently, the HI is not used for IVH screening in Thailand, representing a gap in clinical practice. This gap is particularly relevant for the early diagnosis of diseases associated with IVH and for monitoring after treatment. Therefore, this study aimed to validate the HI on the Mindray BS‐800 (Mindray Bio‐Medical Electronics, Shenzhen, China) against the Harboe reference method to assess its accuracy and reliability [3, 5, 7, 9, 10]. The Mindray BS‐800 was selected because it is already available in our institution, serves as a routine analyzer, and is widely used in many hospitals in Thailand. These factors enhance the generalizability and potential applicability of the findings to other clinical laboratories.

Materials and Methods

The study protocols were approved by the Siriraj Hospital Institutional Review Board (Si‐355/2024). The study was conducted from June 1 to December 31, 2024, in accordance with Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Methods for Measuring fHb Levels

Several publications have recommended EDTA as the anticoagulant for fHb testing because it is commonly used in hematology laboratories [11, 12]. To measure fHb using the HI on the Mindray BS‐800, 10 μL of EDTA plasma was diluted with 200 μL of normal saline solution to measure absorbance at 570 and 605 nm [13]. Although both plasma and serum can be used, EDTA plasma was selected because it could be obtained from residual specimens after complete blood count testing. The test takes approximately 3 min. fHb levels were calculated using the equation: fHb (mg/dL) = (HI + 2.4219) / 1.2228. The formula was derived from a standard curve constructed using fHb solutions at concentrations of 0, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, and 600 mg/dL. Measurements were performed in 5 replicates for each concentration.

For the reference method, fHb concentration was determined using the Harboe method with a Shimadzu PharmaSpec UV‐1700 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan), measuring absorbance at 380, 415, and 450 nm. The test takes approximately 10 min. fHb levels were calculated using the Harboe equation: fHb (mg/dL) = 83.6 (2 × A415 − A380 − A450) [5, 8].

Preparation of Quality Controls

EDTA blood was frozen overnight at −20°C and thawed at room temperature. Cell debris was removed by centrifugation at 12000 g for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected as an Hb stock solution [3]. The Hb concentration was determined using a Sysmex XN‐350 analyzer (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). Two levels of internal quality control were prepared by diluting the Hb stock solution with pooled plasma to 20 and 200 mg/dL. Samples were aliquoted into 200 μL volumes and stored at −20°C until analysis; Hb is stable at this temperature for at least 6 months [14]. Internal quality controls were performed twice daily. Before each use, frozen aliquots were thawed at room temperature until completely defrosted and could not be left for more than 30 min and then thoroughly mixed using a vortex mixer.

Precision Analysis

Precision was evaluated according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute document EP05‐A3 [7, 10]. Two levels of fHb were prepared for repeatability (within‐run) and reproducibility (between‐run) assessment. For the repeatability test, 2 samples with low and high fHb concentrations were measured 20 times on the same day. For the reproducibility test, 2 levels of fHb were measured over 20 testing days with 2 runs per day and duplicates per run. The mean (SD) and percent coefficient of variation (%CV) were calculated. The Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments acceptance criteria were as follows: within‐run %CV ≤ 0.25% allowable total error (%TEa), between‐run %CV ≤ 0.33% TEa, and total %CV ≤ 0.33% TEa [10, 15].

Detection Limit Analysis

Three concentrations of fHb (2, 5, and 8 mg/dL) were prepared and repeated 60 times at each level to determine the limit of detection (LOD) and the limit of quantitation (LOQ). The LOD is defined as the lowest concentration detectable above background noise, and the LOQ as the lowest concentration measurable with acceptable accuracy and precision. The %CV was calculated at each level; acceptance criteria were %CV < 20% for the LOD and < 10% for the LOQ [3, 10, 16].

Linearity Analysis

Two levels of fHb were prepared: low (2.3 mg/dL) and high (507.9 mg/dL). Six dilution levels were prepared as follows: 1 low; 0.8 low + 0.2 high; 0.6 low + 0.4 high; 0.4 low + 0.6 high; 0.2 low + 0.8 high; and 1 high. Each level was measured in triplicate. Assigned and observed values were compared using linear regression analysis. Percent recovery (%recovery) and percent bias (%bias) were calculated for each sample [10, 15].

Accuracy Analysis

Method comparison was performed using 100 anonymous leftover samples that covered the linearity range of fHb. EDTA blood was centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min to separate plasma [14, 17]. fHb was then measured in duplicate on the same day using both the Harboe reference method and the HI. The Harboe method required sample fHb concentrations within 0–140 mg/dL [8]. Samples exceeding this limit were diluted with distilled water, and final values were calculated using the appropriate dilution factor. In contrast, samples measured using the HI were analyzed directly without dilution because of its broader linearity range. fHb levels from the 2 methods were compared using Bland–Altman plots and linear regression analysis. The correlation coefficient (r), %bias, percent total error (%total error), and error index were calculated [3, 7, 10].

Interference Analysis

Icteric and lipemic plasma are known interferents for this test. The bilirubin stock solution (500 mg/dL) was prepared by dissolving 50 mg of bilirubin powder (Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA) in 6 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide (Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA) and 4 mL of 0.1 M sodium carbonate (Sigma‐Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA). This stock was diluted with pooled plasma to obtain 3 concentrations of bilirubin. Twelve samples were prepared by combining 3 bilirubin concentrations with 4 fHb concentrations. Final bilirubin levels were 14.2, 24.9, and 34.7 mg/dL, and final fHb levels were 37.4, 69.3, 118.6, and 204.8 mg/dL.

For the lipemia interference test, Intralipid 20% (Sino‐Swed Pharmaceutical, Beijing, China) was diluted with pooled plasma to obtain 3 triglyceride concentrations, and 4 fHb concentrations were added. The final triglyceride concentrations were 192.8, 353.3, and 520.9 mg/dL. The 12 bilirubin interference preparations and the 12 triglyceride interference preparations were measured in duplicate. Percent difference (%difference) of fHb was calculated [3, 7].

Carryover Analysis

Low‐concentration (5 mg/dL) and high‐concentration (200 mg/dL) fHb samples were aliquoted into 11 low‐concentration cups and 10 high‐concentration cups. The testing sequence on the analyzer was as follows: 3 low, 2 high, 1 low, 2 high, 4 low, 2 high, 1 low, 2 high, 1 low, 2 high, and 1 low. Carryover was calculated as the difference between the mean of the high–low results and the mean of the low–low results. A high–low result is a low result that immediately follows a high result, and a low–low result is a low result that immediately follows another low result. The error limit was set at 3 times the SD of the low–low results [3].

Reference Range Analysis

A total of 120 visually clear plasma samples from healthy donors were analyzed for fHb levels using the HI to establish the reference range [10, 18]. Samples were obtained from the Blood Bank Division, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University. All donors met the institution's donation criteria: age 18–65 years, Hb levels ≥ 12.5 g/dL (regardless of sex), and a history of regular donation (at least twice annually). Because the study used anonymous leftover samples, it was granted an Institutional Review Board exemption and a waiver of informed consent. Baseline donor characteristics were not recorded because the samples were fully anonymized, precluding donor identification.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics, including mean (SD) and %CV, were calculated to evaluate the precision, detection limit, and carryover of the measurements. Percent recovery and %bias were calculated to assess linearity and interference. All calculations were performed using Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation). Linear regression parameters, including slope, intercept, and r, were calculated to evaluate assay linearity and to determine the relationship between fHb concentrations measured by the HI and the Harboe methods. Bland–Altman plots were used to evaluate the mean difference and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) between the 2 methods. The %TEa for fHb was set at 30% [3]. IBM SPSS Statistics, version 31 (IBM Corp) was used to perform all statistical analyses.

Detection Limit Analysis

The %CV values for 3 fHb levels (2, 5, and 8 mg/dL) were 16.28%, 6.37%, and 4.25%, respectively. The LOD was established at 2 mg/dL (%CV < 20%), and the LOQ at 5 mg/dL (%CV < 10%).

Linearity Analysis

Linear regression analysis of assigned and observed values for 6 fHb levels (2.3–507.9 mg/dL) demonstrated an excellent linear relationship. The regression equation was Y (observed) = 0.9977 × X (assigned) + 1.7948 (r = 0.9999). The %bias values for the 6 levels were – 1.67%, 3.08%, 1.19%, 0.11%, 0.40%, and – 0.06%, respectively. Accuracy Analysis

Comparison of 100 samples (fHb range: 5.46–481.41 mg/dL) between the Harboe method and the HI showed good correlation. The regression equation was HI (mg/dL) = 1.0886 × Harboe (mg/dL) − 0.3071 (r = 0.9967). The %bias, error index, and %total error were 11.38%, 99%, and 16.23%, respectively (Figures 2 and 3). However, Bland–Altman analysis with the Harboe method as reference revealed strong agreement for samples with fHb ≤ 140 mg/dL (78 samples). Carryover Analysis

The mean (SD) of the low–low results was 5.42 (0.21) mg/dL, and the mean (SD) of the high–low results was 5.66 (0.36) mg/dL. The carryover value was 0.24 mg/dL, which was well below the acceptable limit of ±0.63 mg/dL (3 SD), confirming that the method was not prone to sample‐to‐sample contamination.

Reference Range Analysis

fHb levels in the 120 healthy donor samples followed a normal distribution, with a mean (SD) of 16.5 (3.7) mg/dL. The reference range was 9.0–23.9 mg/dL (upper limit: ≤ 23.9 mg/dL).

Discussion

Although the HI has been validated for measuring fHb on several automated analyzers, its performance on the Mindray BS‐800 has not been previously evaluated. A precision study was conducted to evaluate random error. All values were well within the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments acceptance criteria (≤ 7.5% for within‐run, ≤ 9.9% for between‐run, and ≤ 9.9% for %total CV), demonstrating excellent reproducibility. Similar findings have been reported with different analyzers. Chung et al. reported within‐run and between‐run %CV of 0.9%–1.0% and 2.7%–2.8%, respectively, on the Toshiba TBA200FRneo [5]. Bürki et al. observed %CV of 0.9%–3.4% (within‐run) and 0.3%–0.4% (between‐run) using the Abbott Alinity c [7]. Whereas Barbhuiya et al. reported %CV of 1.7%–4.2% and 2.1%–7.0% on the Roche Cobas 8000 [3]. These findings collectively support the high precision of fHb measurement across various automated chemistry analyzers, including the Mindray BS‐800.

Assessment of analytical sensitivity determined an LOD of 2 mg/dL and an LOQ of 5 mg/dL, meeting Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute criteria (%CV < 20% for LOD and < 10% for LOQ). These results confirm that the HI can detect low fHb concentrations with acceptable precision. The linearity experiment established a validated analytical measurement range of 2.3–507.9 mg/dL with excellent linear correlation. The %recovery values (98.33%–103.08%) and %bias (−1.67% to 3.08%) were within acceptable criteria (%recovery ≥ 90% and %bias < 30%), supporting the method's accuracy across a wide concentration range. The established linear range with respect to the LOQ was 5.0–507.9 mg/dL.

Similar findings have been reported with different analyzers. Chung et al. demonstrated a linear range of 0.3–250 mg/dL using the Toshiba TBA200FRneo [5]. Bürki et al. reported a range of 3.0–725 mg/dL with an LOQ of 8 mg/dL on the Abbott Alinity c [7]. Barbhuiya et al. observed linearity of 5–506 mg/dL with an LOQ of 11 mg/dL using the Roche Cobas 8000 [3].

A method comparison was performed to evaluate systematic error by comparing the HI with the Harboe method. The results showed excellent agreement, with a regression equation of HI (mg/dL) = 1.0886 × Harboe (mg/dL) − 0.3071 (r = 0.9967; n = 100). The %bias (11.38%) and %total error (16.23%) were both below the %TEa of 30%, and the error index of 99% exceeded the minimum acceptance criterion of 95%. However, Bland–Altman analysis revealed a relatively large mean difference (HI − Harboe) of 6.86 mg/dL, with 95% LoA ranging from −22.14 to 35.87. Stratifying the data explained this variance: for samples with fHb > 140 mg/dL, the Harboe method required dilution, whereas the HI measured the samples directly. This procedural difference produced the wider mean difference of 24.92 mg/dL (95% LoA: −20.56 to 70.40) in this subgroup. In contrast, for samples with fHb ≤ 140 mg/dL, where both methods measured concentration directly, the mean difference narrowed considerably to 1.77 mg/dL (95% LoA: −5.88 to 9.42). Consequently, using an HI upper limit of 130 mg/dL ensures that fHb values measured by Harboe remain below 140 mg/dL, establishing the HI as a reliable clinical substitute for monitoring IVH within the 0–130 mg/dL range.

Spectrophotometric measurement of fHb is susceptible to interference from bilirubin and lipids because of overlapping absorption spectra [19]. In the present study, fHb concentrations showed a negative bias of up to 15% at bilirubin levels of 14.2–34.7 mg/dL, particularly in samples with mildly elevated fHb. Similarly, previous research using the Roche Cobas 8000 reported recoveries of −17.9% to 0.3% at bilirubin concentrations of 43.4 mg/dL [3]. Although these deviations remain within the %TEa of 30%, such bias may lead to clinically relevant underestimation of IVH in high‐risk patients, including those on extracorporeal membrane oxygenation or with hepatic impairment.

Regarding lipemia, triglyceride levels up to 520.9 mg/dL did not notably affect measurements, with %difference values ranging from −4.27% to 0.31% (all < 30%). In contrast, Sharaiha et al. found that at fHb > 50 mg/dL, a lipemic index exceeding 2.09 (Abbott Architect c16000) or 2.83 (Roche Cobas c502) led to underestimation and overestimation, respectively [20]. These discrepancies likely arise from platform‐specific algorithms and wavelengths. Furthermore, the lipemic index may not correlate perfectly with triglyceride levels because triglyceride content varies across lipoprotein classes. The lipemic index can also be artificially elevated by factors such as high immunoglobulin levels [19].

Both EDTA and lithium heparin are suitable anticoagulants for measuring plasma fHb, although EDTA underfilling can artificially elevate results [11]. In this study, the reference range for EDTA plasma using the Mindray BS‐800 was ≤ 23.9 mg/dL, based on routine collection from 120 healthy donors (aged 18–65 years). This value is slightly higher than previously reported heparin plasma intervals. Wan Azman et al. reported a reference range of ≤ 16 mg/dL on the Roche Cobas c702 using samples from 120 healthy individuals (aged 19–65 years) [21]. Notably, their samples were collected by an experienced phlebotomist. Barbhuiya et al. established a heparin plasma reference range of < 22 mg/dL on the Roche Cobas 8000, although individual characteristics and venipuncture techniques were not reported [3].

Lippi et al. reported values of ≤ 13 mg/dL for heparin plasma using the Roche Cobas c501 and ≤ 10 mg/dL on the Siemens Dimension Vista 1500 [22]. Their reference ranges were based on 135 outpatients (aged 22–78 years) under strictly controlled collection and processing conditions. These discrepancies likely arise from differences in analyzer technology, specimen type, and preanalytical variability inherent in routine clinical collection. Such variables must be carefully considered before clinical implementation.

A limitation of measuring fHb is that it cannot distinguish between In Vivo and In Vitro hemolysis, as it measures only the final products of erythrocyte destruction. If fHb exceeds the upper reference limit, clinicians should first evaluate potential preanalytical errors (e.g., suboptimal phlebotomy technique or inappropriate sample processing). If spurious hemolysis is confirmed or suspected, sample recollection is recommended. When preanalytical interference is absent or uncertain, additional hemolytic markers (e.g., serum haptoglobin or indirect bilirubin levels) should be assessed to differentiate pathological hemolysis from spurious results [5].

For laboratories using the Mindray BS‐800 analyzer, the HI can be applied for quantification of fHb. However, method verification is required before clinical implementation to ensure reliable performance within each specific setting. Additional studies are also needed for clinical validation, including establishment of HI cutoff values to distinguish IVH from other etiologies of anemia, which would reduce unnecessary investigations. Laboratories using analyzers from other manufacturers must perform a full method validation to establish performance characteristics and appropriate reference values.

Conclusions

The method validation for measuring fHb using the HI on the Mindray BS‐800 analyzer met all required criteria. This method provides accurate and reliable fHb measurements within the range of 0–130 mg/dL, offering a practical alternative to the Harboe method with improved safety and turnaround time. In practice, elevated fHb should first prompt investigation of In Vitro hemolysis, and clinicians should be aware that marked hyperbilirubinemia may lead to underestimation of fHb.
