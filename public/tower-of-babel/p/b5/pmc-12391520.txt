The influence of hydraulic hose length on dynamic pressure waveforms including wave phenomena
Authors: Michał Stosiak, Paweł Bury, Mykola Karpenko
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates dynamic pressure waveforms in hydraulic hoses. Steel hoses of various lengths were tested, with the frequency of pressure waves varied for a fixed hose length, and pulsating flow was generated by a positive displacement pump. At certain hose lengths, outlet pressure pulsations were amplified while inlet pulsations were attenuated, demonstrating hydraulic resonance. The analysis was conducted using a four-piece hydraulic model, and experimental verification showed excellent agreement with the model predictions. Hose vibrations induced by the pulsating flow were also recorded. The results highlight critical hose lengths and excitation frequencies to avoid resonance, reducing vibrations and environmental impact. These findings provide guidance for designing precise, reliable hydraulic systems and serve as a basis for modeling more complex configurations.

Introduction

Hydraulic systems are still widely used in many industries. Their widespread use is determined by their numerous advantages, including a favorable weight-to-power ratio, high density of the transmitted energy stream, ease of control automation, smooth movements, and the possibility of free spatial arrangement of hydraulic elements1,2. Many mining, construction3, and agricultural machines, airplanes4,5, and marine ships6,7 have hydraulic systems that perform vital functions. A working hydraulic system is a source of mechanical vibrations in a wide spectrum of frequencies. In a working hydraulic system, there are sudden changes in the pressure of the working fluid. These changes may be caused by pulsation of the displacement pump’s performance, sudden changes in external load, or mechanical vibrations acting on hydraulic valves. Additionally, the amplitudes of pressure changes may be magnified by the sometimes occurring hydraulic resonance8. This is related to wave phenomena in hydraulic pipes in which, under certain conditions, there may be an overlap of traveling and reflected waves that are in phase9, leading to local amplification of the pressure pulsation amplitudes in the hydraulic line. The length at which this occurs is called the resonance length of the hydraulic hose10. Indeed, the working hydraulic system, powered by a displacement pump, is constantly in a transient or quasi-steady state due to the pulsating nature of the displacement pump operation11,12, which is an indispensable element of almost every hydraulic system.

When testing transient states, special attention should be paid when dealing with a long hydraulic transmission line, i.e., when the length of the hydraulic line is equal to or longer than the length of the pressure wave propagated in it13. There are even recommendations in the literature to treat a hydraulic line as a hydraulic transmission line when the condition λf ≤ c0/(10∙fmax) is met (λf – wave length; c0 – pressure wave propagation speed; fmax – maximum value of the frequency of the propagated wave)14. In the analysis of systems that meet this condition, a hydraulic hose should be treated as an element with distributed parameters, so it must be taken into account that changes in pressure and flow rate propagate along the axis of the hose with a finite speed in the form of current and reflected waves, which superimpose on each other15,16. Two different methods analytically describe dynamic phenomena in such cases17. The first one treats the hydraulic hose as a hydraulic four-way device, i.e., a two-way element with two inputs and two outputs in the form of pressures and flow rates at the beginning and end of the hose18. Figure 1 graphically illustrates the model of a hydraulic four-piece with the inputs and outputs marked. This is a frequency method in which harmonic excitations are assumed (e.g., from pulsations of the displacement pump capacity)19.Fig. 1Hydraulic cross-over.

The second approach to modeling wave phenomena in hydraulic lines involves describing transient processes as a function of time20. Frequency methods were used for the theoretical considerations in this work, which were verified experimentally. Therefore, at the system design stage, attention should be paid to the length of the hydraulic c hoses and the expected excitation frequencies to prevent amplification of the pressure pulsation amplitudes inside the hose. If the pressure pulsation amplitude increases in the pipe, it may result in mechanical vibrations21 and noise22 that harm the environment, including humans23. The vibrations of the pipes induced in this way and the noise they produce occur over a wide frequency spectrum—from several dozen Hz to several hundred Hz, and sometimes even over 1 kHz24. Low-frequency vibrations and noise are particularly dangerous to the human body25. Low-frequency mechanical vibrations cause internal organs to vibrate and, with longer exposure, lower the position of internal organs26. However, low-frequency noise can cause numerous negative changes to the circulatory system, nervous system, and human psyche27. From this point of view, minimizing the impact of hydraulic systems on the environment is crucial. Vibration and noise reduction can be achieved in two ways. One method is passive28, i.e., one that does not interfere with the source of noise or vibration (e.g., sound-insulating housings or passive vibration isolation), and the other method is active, i.e., one that interferes with the structure of the source of vibration or noise29.

The aim of this study was to analyze the influence of hydraulic hose length on the amplitude of pressure pulsations and the resulting mechanical vibrations induced in the hose by the pulsating flow of the working fluid. The work included both theoretical considerations and experimental verification, in which the hydraulic hose was modeled as a hydraulic four-piece with two inputs and two outputs. The mathematical model incorporated a quasi-steady-state friction loss description to determine the transmittance characteristics of the hose. This approach allowed for the evaluation of the relationship between excitation frequency, hose length, and the dynamic response of the hydraulic line.

Understanding the influence of hydraulic hose length on pressure pulsations and vibrations is crucial for modeling and designing high-precision, reliable hydraulic systems. The conducted study and its validation may serve as an excellent starting point for analyzing more complex systems, where interactions between hoses, valves, pumps, and accumulators lead to unique dynamic responses that require precise modeling.

Experimental research on wave phenomena in a hydraulic pipe

To confirm and supplement the conclusions drawn from the numerical solutions, experimental studies were carried out on wave phenomena and induced mechanical vibrations in a hydraulic hose with periodic excitation. The test object was a straight-axial steel hydraulic hose with an internal diameter of 19 mm and a wall thickness of 3 mm. Testing used seven pipe lengths, including 1.1 m, 1.25 m, 1.4 m, 1.55 m, 1.7 m, 1.85 m, and 2 m. The pipe was powered by an external gear pump with 12 teeth, and the pump was driven by an electric motor whose control system allowed for a rotation speed of 500 rpm to 2100 rpm. The tested pipe was connected directly to the feed pump. The temperature of the working fluid, HL68 mineral oil, was stabilized using oil cooler. The average pressure in the tested pipe was determined using an adjustable throttle valve located at the end of the pipe. Conclusions

The current paper presents the results of theoretical considerations and experimental verification work. The hydraulic hose was treated as a hydraulic transmission line and described with a mathematical model of a hydraulic four-piece with two inputs and two outputs. The form of the model equations depends on the adopted friction loss model and uses a quasi-steady-state friction loss model to describe the transmittance of a hydraulic hose. This led to the results of numerical calculations being close to those of experimental tests. The results of numerical considerations and experimental tests indicate that for a given frequency of pressure pulsations (excitations), there is a length of the hydraulic hose (the so-called resonant length of the hose) that results in the amplification of pressure pulsations in the hose (at its end). Figures 6 and 7, created by intersecting Fig. 3 for a fixed frequency (Fig. 6) and a fixed pipe length (Fig. 7), allow the maximum value of transmittance Gp2/p1 to be determined, which corresponds to hydraulic resonance in the pipe. Based on these figures, it is possible to determine the pipe length for a given excitation frequency that should not be present in the system in order to avoid amplification of pressure pulsation amplitudes, Fig. 6. On the other hand, based on Fig. 7, it is possible to indicate which excitation frequencies will cause amplification of pressure pulsation amplitudes for a given length of hydraulic pipe.

The results of experimental tests presented in Fig. 12 indicate that for a pipe with a length of 1.4 m, the ratio of the pressure pulsation amplitude at the end of the pipe (p2) to the pressure pulsation amplitude at the beginning of the pipe (p1) strongly depends on the pulsation frequency (on the speed of the pump shaft). A p2/p1 ratio above 1 indicates an increase in the pressure pulsation amplitude at the end of the pipe relative to the pressure pulsation amplitude at the beginning of the pipe for a given pipe length and selected pump shaft speed (excitation frequency). The maximum values of the p2/p1 ratio for the first and third components of the spectrum occur at similar values of the pump shaft speed. On the other hand, the values of the p2/p1 ratio for the second component of the pressure pulsation spectrum are in antiphase, i.e. where the maximum values of the p2/p1 ratio for the first and third components occur, the minimum value of this ratio for the second component of the pressure pulsation spectrum occurs.

For the steady-state operation of the hydraulic system, pipe lengths that cause hydraulic resonance should be avoided, though this does not mean that hydraulic resonance will be eliminated. In a transient state (e.g., during system start-up), in which the rotational speed of the pump shaft feeding the hydraulic hose changes, the frequency of pressure pulsations changes, and frequency sweeping occurs. The lower limit of this range is the rotational speed of the pump drive shaft corresponding to the minimum pump capacity (zero in the extreme case), and the upper limit is the rotational speed of the pump drive shaft corresponding to the pump’s steady-state capacity. It may turn out that the frequency at which hydraulic resonance occurs (for a given pipe length) is within this range. Then, the pressure pulsation amplitude will temporarily increase (Fig. 11). Lowering the pressure pulsation amplitudes will result in lower pipe vibration amplitudes and reduce the impact of the pipe on the environment (including humans). The presented considerations also show that as the length of the hydraulic hose decreases, the transmittance of the hose increases at the resonance frequency. This can be explained by the influence of capacitance (Figs. 7, 11), which means that as the length of the pipe decreases while its diameter remains unchanged, the capacitance decreases, increasing in dynamic pressure amplitudes. Conversely, as the length of the hydraulic hose increases, the transmittance at the resonance frequency decreases due to greater damping, which reduces the amplitude of pressure pulsations. Longer hydraulic hoses also result in lower natural frequencies of the system, making it more susceptible to resonance at lower excitation frequencies.
