Systematic surveillance of Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales reveals persistent spread of IMP-4 IncM2 plasmids in New Caledonia
Authors: Alexandre Bourles, Léo Cousin, Gauthier Delvallez, Antoine Biron, Tiffany Ruge, Rémy A. Bonnin, Laurent Dortet, Sylvain Brisse, Federica Palma, Philippe Saliou, Malia Kainiu, Thibaut Objois, Elena Natiello-Menkes, Matthieu Pot, Frederic Veyrier, Cyrille Goarant, Julien Colot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) have been designated by the World Health Organization as critical global priority pathogens. Their widespread dissemination and nosocomial outbreaks pose a major public health threat. This study examined the transmission patterns and genetic characteristics of CPE isolated in New Caledonia between 2013 and 2022.Methods:A total of 214 CPE isolates were collected, comprising 199 non-duplicate clinical isolates from 164 patients and 15 environmental isolates from hospital surfaces. Isolates were characterized using phenotypic methods and whole-genome sequencing.Results:Among clinical isolates, the most prevalent genera was Enterobacter (n = 68; 34%) and Klebsiella (n = 50; 25%), with 194 isolates (98%) harbouring IMP-type carbapenemases. WGS of 89 selected CPE revealed the predominance of the blaIMP-4 gene (n = 82; 92%). This gene was primarily associated with IncM2-type plasmids carrying a class 1 integron, identified in 65 sequenced isolates. Long-read sequencing resolved these plasmids into seven distinct variants, differentiated by integron structures and a 9305-bp insertion. Genomic, phenotypic, and epidemiological triangulation have identified 12 hospital spread events, with 10 linked to IMP-4 IncM2 plasmids, whose transferability is confirmed by conjugation assays.Conclusion:These results highlighted the pivotal role of plasmid-mediated dissemination in CPE spread and emphasize the urgency of enhanced surveillance to curb silent transmission. The study provided critical insights for targeted infection control strategies in New Caledonia healthcare system, with broader implications for regional antimicrobial resistance containment.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) has designated antimicrobial resistance (AMR) as one of the top 10 global public health threats1. Among resistance mechanisms, carbapenem resistance is of particular concern, as Carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) are classified in the highest priority (“critical”) category of the 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, guiding research, development, and public health responses2. In Enterobacterales, carbapenem resistance is primarily driven by the acquisition of carbapenemase-encoding genes, including Ambler class A serine β-lactamases (blaKPC, blaGES), metallo β-lactamases (blaNDM, blaVIM, blaIMP) and carbapenem-hydrolysing class D serine β-lactamases (blaOXA-48-like)3. These genes are frequently plasmid-borne, facilitating horizontal gene transfer (HGT) among Enterobacterales and accelerating resistance dissemination4. Moreover, resistance plasmids often co-harbour additional antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs), conferring extensive drug resistance and severely limiting therapeutic options.

New Caledonia (NC), a French overseas territory in the southwestern Pacific, has not been spared by the global dissemination of CPE. Since the implementation of systematic CPE surveillance in 2004, the first CPE was isolated in 2013, followed by two major nosocomial outbreaks at the Centre Hospitalier Territorial (CHT), NC’s primary referral hospital, one in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) in 2017 and another one in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) in 20185. In NC, IMP-type CPE have predominated, with the number of IMP-positive isolates rising sharply from 4 in 2013 to 33 in 2018, an 8-fold increase over five years5,6. This carbapenemase, encoded here by the blaIMP-4 gene, is prevalent in the Asia-Pacific region7–9 and has become dominant in Australia10–14. Although the NC population is relatively isolated, there are links with Australia, the main site of many medical evacuations (Med Evac), and mainland France, where many population transfers occur.

Recent advances in genomic technologies have significantly enhanced our understanding of ARG dissemination15,16. Standardized, ISO-certified genomic workflows have been developed to support clinical and public health microbiology, enabling precise identification and surveillance of AMR17. Unlike traditional phenotypic methods, genomic data provide comprehensive insights into ARG profiles and plasmid-mediated resistance mechanisms, thereby complementing laboratory diagnostics. In this study, we integrated genomic, phenotypic, and clinical analyses of all CPE isolated in NC from the first documented case (July 2013) through December 2022, aiming to characterize the local epidemiology of CPE dissemination. For each patient, we collected clinical and epidemiological data. Potential spread events were initially identified via epidemiological investigations, including hygiene control assessments, and routine microbiological analyses. To refine these findings, we employed: whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to determine clonal relationships and ARG profiles, followed by long-read plasmid sequencing to characterize mobile genetic elements (e.g., plasmids, integrons), and conjugative transfer frequencies. This study aims to characterize the transmission patterns and genetic features of CPE isolated in NC over a ten-year period. By integrating genomic, phenotypic, and epidemiological data, we seek to identify the drivers of CPE dissemination and inform targeted infection control measures. The findings reveal the pivotal role of plasmid-mediated spread in driving CPE transmission, with the blaIMP-4 gene and IncM2-type plasmids emerging as key contributors to nosocomial outbreaks. These results underscore the need for enhanced surveillance and proactive interventions to curb the silent spread of AMR.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective study to analyse all CPE isolates collected in NC from July 2013 (first isolate detected) to December 2022. NC’s healthcare infrastructure includes three public hospitals and one private clinic for patients’ hospitalization. These facilities operate under the authority of the Department of Health and Social Affairs (DASS) of local Government, which has decision-making powers in health-related matters, a feature that is consistent with the administrative structure in France. The study was conducted in the CHT, the main and reference hospital of the archipelago. It comprises 645 beds, 12 operating rooms, medical units, surgery units and intensive care units. The number of hospitalizations approximates 40,000 a year. CHT laboratory is also the reference laboratory for CPE in NC, where all suspected CPE isolates are sent for expertise. In order to prevent spread of CPE, patients with CPE at the CHT are transferred to a dedicated isolation unit (cohorting unit) with a dedicated medical team5.

Workflow for CPE isolation at CHT

All patients admitted to the ICU, and those returning from a foreign hospital, or known previous carriers of CPE have been systematically screened for CPE carriage. When a CPE is isolated in a patient, all contacts within the department were also screened. CPE screening involves using a faecal swab culture on ChromID® ESBL (bioMérieux, Marcy-l'Étoile, France), with results available 24 to 48 h after incubation at 37 °C. All colony morphologies are identified using MALDI-ToF mass spectrometry (Microflex LT-MS, Bruker Daltonics). According to major circulation of IMP-type carbapenemase in NC5, carbapenem resistance was assessed on Mueller-Hinton agar with different antibiotic discs: ertapenem (10 µg), temocillin (30 µg) and ceftolozane/tazobactam (30/10 µg) inspired by the 2022 European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing18. Ceftolozane/tazobactam disc was added for guidance on the presence of IMP-type carbapenemase as suggested by Pfennigwerth et al. (2020)19. Confirmation was performed using NG-CARBA-5® immunochromatographic assays (NG Biotech Laboratories, Guipry, France), which detect the major carbapenemase types (VIM, IMP, NDM, KPC, and OXA-48-like)20,21.

For infectious samples, antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was systematically performed (unlike screening and environmental samples) using the VITEK-2® system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) with AST-N234 cards for all strains isolated before 2024, except for urine-derived strains isolated from 2016 onward, for which AST-N372 cards were used. Strains tested retrospectively after 2024 were assessed using AST-N441 cards. Finally, carbapenem minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) could be confirmed using the Etest® method (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France). This late approach was finally systematically applied to all samples collected during the study, as well as retrospectively to all conserved CPE in our collection (n = 69). The analysis finally included CPE strains from both colonized and infected patients, regardless of specimen type, along with environmental isolates (e.g., from table surfaces, sink areas in patient rooms, or healthcare trolleys) recovered during suspected spread events for comparative purposes. Furthermore, to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the results, all ASTs were re-interpreted using the EUCAST guidelines as updated in 202218.

Collection of clinical data

We systematically recorded the origin of each sample, specifying the hospital setting or name of the external laboratory, and categorized the sample types into five main groups: rectal swab, blood culture, urine, respiratory, and other. For all patients hospitalized at CHT, we collected clinical data relevant to the study and recognized as risk factors for CPE acquisition in other studies22. Finally, we recorded whether the isolate was likely associated with a spread event. Before the acquisition of genomics data, suspected spread event were characterised by the incidental discovery of the same CPE based on (i) specie identification (using MALDI-ToF), AST (when available, following random errors of VITEK-2® system, we considered identical ASTs if they differed by no more than one result), (ii) type of carbapenemase (using NG-CARBA-5® immunochromatographic assays) leading to the detection of a secondary case, or the detection of same CPE in the same department at short period (less than one month). As reported in Colot et al. (2022)5, when a CPE is detected in a healthcare department, all contact patients, i.e. those who have been hospitalized in the same department or who have shared the same healthcare team, are systematically screened (two screenings are performed 72 hours apart: culture then PCR). Contact positive cases are considered secondary cases and linked to the same episode. In this study, a spread event was considered as an outbreak if there were at least five secondary cases; otherwise, if there were fewer than 5 secondary cases, we considered this event as a transmission cluster. Following the analysis of the sequencing data, a subsequent hygiene investigation was conducted to attempt retrospective linkage of the strains or plasmids to spread event. Certain strains or plasmids were confirmed to be associated with a transmission event when patients had overlapping or sequential stays in the same department within a one-month interval. Other strains or plasmids were considered presumed transmission links if patients had stayed in different departments but within the same one-month interval, or in the same department but with stays more than one month apart.

Whole-genome sequencing

A batch of 64 CPE (corresponding to first isolates in NC or suspected of belonging to spread events) isolated from 2013 to 2019 was submitted to WGS by the French National Reference Center of Antimicrobial Resistance using Pasteur P2M core facility. Sequencing was conducted using a NextSeq500 instrument (Illumina, San Diego, CA; 2 × 150-nucleotide paired-end protocol). Additionally, WGS was performed on 25 CPE (in order to complete the study period until 2022, again with a focus on isolates suspected of belonging to spread events) collected between 2020 and 2022 by the Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI) Tech Solutions Hong Kong Co., Limited. Genomic DNA was extracted from 1.5 ml of Luria–Broth culture using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA purity was determined using Nanodrop (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel deposition. Paired-end libraries were prepared with an insert size of approximately 300 bp and sequenced on DNB-SEQ PE150 according to the supplier’s protocol.

Genome assembly and quality assessment

Paired-end reads were de novo assembled using the fq2dna workflow V21.06 (https://gitlab.pasteur.fr/GIPhy/fq2dna), which also includes various steps such as trimming, error correction, contaminant removal, and polishing. For completeness and contamination analysis, CheckM (V1.1.3, taxonomy_wf at genus level; https://github.com/Ecogenomics/CheckM) and QUAST version 5.2.023 (https://github.com/ablab/quast) were employed.

In-silico species identification and genomic screening

Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST) was conducted using MLST software V2.19.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst) and associated profiles provided by PubMLST24 or BIGSdb-Pasteur (Klebsiella pneumoniae species complex). To evaluate ARGs, Abricate software V1.0.1 was employed (https://github.com/tseemann/abricate; --minid 70 --mincov 90 --db resfinder)25. The prediction of genomic location of ARGs, plasmid identification and typing were performed using MOB-suite V3.0.126 (https://github.com/phac-nml/mob-suite). Furthermore, the identification of integrons carrying ARGs was conducted using IntegronFinder V2.0.227.

Phylogeny and relationship between isolates

The initial phylogenetic relationships between isolates from the different compartments and sample sites were examined using the k-mer-based tool JolyTree V2.128. The associated tree was midpoint-rooted and annotated with iTOL V629. Species identification was achieved for each assembly through the utilisation of a digital DNA:DNA hybridization approach (dDDH cut-off ≥ 70.0% formula d4) as facilitated by the Type Strain Genome Server (TYGS)30. Regarding Enterobacter genus, an additional analysis was performed using the approach and taxonomy proposed by Wu et al. (2020) with FastANI V1.33 (ANI cut-off ≥ 96.0%)31. Due to the significant genetic diversity of the sample and the need for precise comparison within clinical isolates, we conducted core genome-based phylogenetic analyses on isolates of the same sequence type (ST) with more than two assemblies. For each ST group, a good-quality reference genome was identified by using the online tool SimilarGenomeFinder against all public genomes in the BV-BRC resource center (V3.30.19a;)32,33. Mapping was performed using Snippy V4.6.0 against these selected references. The resulting core-SNP alignment was used to infer maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees for each ST group using IQ-tree V2.1.434, and recombination elements were removed with ClonalFrameML V1.1235. Then, specific SNP differences matrixes were generated with snp-dists V0.8.2. As proposed by David et al. (2019)36, we used 21 core genome SNPs as a threshold for discrimination of clonal dissemination.

Long reads plasmid sequencing

Plasmid DNA extraction

Mapping of short reads of the closest plasmid found in PLSDB (JX101693) has enabled the identification of different variants of the IncM2 plasmid. One representative strain of each variant (based on its species identification or involvement in spread events) was selected. A total of 11 strains was selected for plasmid sequencing. For the extraction of plasmid DNA the ZymoPURE II Plasmid Midiprep Kit was used. DNA extraction was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasmid DNA purity was assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and the quantity was determined using a Qubit 4.0 Fluorometer (Invitrogen Life Technologies, Inc., CA).

IncM identification

In order to check if plasmid of interest has been successfully extracted, plasmid Replicon Typing IncM were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using M-FW (5’- GGA TGA AAA CTA TCA GCA TCT GAA G −3’) and L/M- RV (5’- GAA CTC CGG CGA AAG ACC TTC-3’) primers37. The PCR conditions were as follows: 94 °C denaturation for 10 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 1 min, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s and elongation at 72 °C for 1 min, followed by 1 cycle at 72 °C for 5 min. Fragment size was controlled using melting curve analyses and agarose gel deposition. The expected amplicons were 738 bp. The same PCR was used in order to verify success of conjugation experiments.

Sequencing

Purified DNA was then prepared for nanopore sequencing using the Rapid Barcoding kit 24 V14 (Oxford Nanopore, UK) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on MinION flow cells (R10.4.1 FLO-MIN124, Oxford Nanopore, UK) and data collected using the MinKNOW software V3.6.5 (https://nanoporetech.com/nanopore-sequencing-data-analysis). Basecalling was done online through the EPI2ME service (https://github.com/epi2me-labs).

Long reads assembly and mapping of short reads

We used Autocycler V0.1.238 (https://github.com/rrwick/Autocycler) for de novo assembly of plasmids. Subsequently, short reads were utilized to polish the long-read assembly using Polypolish V0.6.039 (https://github.com/rrwick/Polypolish). We then performed plasmid and ARGs searches as described previously. For genomes where only short-read data were available, we used the circularized plasmids created as a reference database. We performed the mapping of short reads to these plasmids using Minimap2 V2.28; https://github.com/lh3/minimap2) in order to obtain consensus sequences Then the Mash distance between local sequences, IncM2 sequences with complete metadata, available on PLSDB (version 2021_06_23_v2) and recently sequenced IncM2 plasmid isolated in Australia40 was calculated using Mash V2.333 (https://github.com/marbl/Mash). The sequences of the different variant of IMP-4 IncM2 plasmids isolated in NC were then annotated using Prokka V1.14.541 (https://github.com/tseemann/prokka) and aligned with Clinker V0.0.2342 (https://github.com/gamcil/clinker).

Conjugation experiments

Conjugation efficiencies were assessed using the filter mating method previously reported43, with minor modifications. Donor and recipient cells were cultured in Luria–broth at 37 °C with agitation for 8 hours. Donor and recipient cultures were mixed at a 1:5 ratio, immobilized on a 0.45 µm nylon membrane (Merck Millipore, USA) placed on Chromogenic Coliform Agar (CCA; CHROMagar, Paris, France), and incubated for 20 hours at 37 °C. Following incubation, bacteria were resuspended in 1X phosphate-buffered saline, and transconjugants were selected on CCA supplemented with ertapenem (ETP, 0.025 µg/ml). Two recipient strains were used: a laboratory-adapted E. coli CIP 104049 (standard for conjugation assays) and a wild-type E. coli AE strain isolated from the environment in NC. Only E. coli was used as recipients, as CCA agar exploits chromogenic differentiation: E. coli colonies (recipients) appear blue, while other Enterobacterales (donors) appear pink. Putative transconjugants were confirmed via IncM-specific PCR, AST, and MALDI-ToF mass spectrometry described above. Conjugation frequency is given as the number of CFU of transconjugants/number of CFU of recipient.

Statistics and reproducibility

Concerning conjugation experiments, three independent experiments were performed, and statistical analyses were conducted using non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis tests, followed by Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) post-hoc tests (P < 0.05), in R software (V4.5.0).

Ethical approval

This work was approved by the CHT ethics committee (Avis N°3 2024). All patients were informed that their samples and/or data could be reused for research purposes, and no objections were received at the time of consent.

Results

Patients’ characteristics and corresponding isolates

As shown in Table 1, 57 (34%) isolates were derived from clinical samples (symptomatic patients), while 107 (66%) were identified through screening (asymptomatic contacts). Figure 1a illustrates the annual distribution proportion over the years. Among clinical samples, the sources were as follows: 22 (13%) from urine samples, 14 (9%) from blood cultures, 5 (3%) from respiratory samples, and 16 (10%) from other sources, mainly abscesses. The cohort included 99 males (60%) and 65 females (40%). Most patients (125/164, 76%) were managed at the CHT, although 30 (18%) isolates were referred from external laboratories. Among the 125 CHT patients, the departmental distribution was as follows (Fig. 1b): 51 were admitted in ICU (31%), 18 (11%) in the dedicated isolation unit in CHT (cohorting unit), 7 (4%) in paediatrics unit, 14 (9%) in NICU, 11 (7%) in nephrology and 24 (15%) in other departments (Fig. 1b). Phenotypic and genomic profiles of CPE isolates

Genus identification

A total of 199 CPE isolates were collected from the 164 patients between July 2013 and December 2022. The predominant genera identified using MALDI-ToF were Enterobacter (n = 68; 34%), Klebsiella (n = 50; 25%), Citrobacter (n = 35; 18%), Escherichia (n = 25; 13%) and Serratia (n = 15; 8%; Fig. 1c). Among these isolates, 194 (98%) were identified as possessing an IMP-type carbapenemase (Fig. 1d). Additionally, we included 15 CPE from hospital environmental surfaces, belonging to the genera Enterobacter (n = 7), Serratia (n = 7) and Klebsiella (n = 1).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

AST was conducted on 130 CPE isolates (119 human isolates and 11 environmental isolates), and human isolate results are detailed in Fig. 2. All human isolates were resistant to the tested third-generation cephalosporins (3GCs; cefotaxime, ceftazidime) except for one Citrobacter (CLIN139, isolated from a blood culture) and one Klebsiella producing an OXA-48 carbapenemase (CLIN189, isolated from a cutaneous wound). Notably, 24/119 isolates (20%) showed susceptibility to at least one carbapenem (ertapenem or imipenem). Regarding the ceftolozan/tazobactam combination, all IMP-type producers confirmed by immunochromatographic assays were resistant. In addition, 57 out of 119 isolates (48%) were susceptible to ciprofloxacin and 71/119 (60%) were susceptible to trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Susceptibility to the piperacillin-tazobactam combination was evaluated in 112 human CPE isolates, with 27 isolates (25%) showing susceptibility. This finding was confirmed for all 14 isolates tested using the Etest®. Phylogenetic relationships between isolates according to each genus

WGS performed on a selection of 89 representative CPE isolates throughout the study period allowed us to precisely identify 19 species using TYGS. The most dominant species were Enterobacter steigerwaltiii with 13 isolates, the second one was the recently described Serratia sarumanii44 with 11 isolates, and the third ones were Citrobacter freundii with 9 isolates and Enterobacter hoffmanii, according to the latest nomenclature31 with 9 isolates. Interestingly, one Serratia lineage with 6 isolates was identified as a potential new species according to TYGS approach (dDDH4 < 70%). The relationships of the sequenced isolates according to each genus are presented in Fig. 3 and core genome SNP analysis is reported in Supplementary Data 1. In Serratia sarumanii strains, the mean cgSNPs was equal to 16.75, (max: 53, min: 1) and all these strains were suspected to be part of an outbreak in NICU in 2018. Regarding lineage of Serratia sp. mean cgSNPs was equal to 26.20, (max: 36, min: 10) and 4 of 6 strains were suspected to be part of outbreak in Paediatric unit in 2018. The nine strains of E. hoffmannii belonged to ST104, and mean cgSNPs was equal to 23.86 (max: 33; min: 11) and were suspected to be implicated in two outbreaks in ICU and NICU in 2018. The Citrobacter ST98 cluster (mean cgSNPs: 19.16; max: 32; min: 1), identified as Citrobacter freundii. Interestingly, unlike previous strains, this strain appeared to be particularly persistent, as it had already been found in 2014 and continued to be found in 2021. ARGs and plasmids

The resistance gene annotation revealed the dominance of the IMP-4 producers (82/89). IncM2 plasmid

Long-read sequencing of plasmids enabled the identification of seven variants within IncM2 plasmids harboured by several species (Table 3). Variants differ mainly by a 9305 bp insertion and a 5721 bp deletion at the class 1 resistance integron corresponding to a loss of region harbouring ISCR1, qnrB2 and sul1 (Fig. 5). At the time, IncM2 plasmids with the 9305 bp insertion (n = 38) have not been reported anywhere else in the world (Fig. 6). Conjugation experiments

To assess the transferability of IncM2 plasmids between different bacterial species via conjugation, we conducted filter mating experiments. Using various combinations of donor and recipient strains (Escherichia coli CIP 104049 and E. coli AE), we performed 18 mating assays, resulting in the acquisition of 15 independent transconjugants (Fig. 7, with transfer frequencies detailed in Supplementary Data 2. While the conjugation frequency was lower in the environmentally isolated naïve strain compared to the laboratory-adapted E. coli recipient, successful plasmid transfer was nonetheless achieved. Notably, for isolates CLIN73, CLINX, and CLIN08, plasmid transfer was exclusively observed with the environmentally derived recipient strain, failing to conjugate into the laboratory-adapted E. coli strain. For isolates CLIN73 and CLINX, no growth of E. coli CIP 104049 was observed on CCA or CCA supplemented with ETP, suggesting competition between donor and recipient strains. Conversely, for CLIN08, growth of E. coli CIP 104049 was observed on CCA but not on CCA + ETP, indicating the conjugation assay was not positive in the conditions tested. Plus value of genomic analysis in detection of spread events

Based on hygiene investigations, species identification using MALDI-ToF and AST, 4 outbreaks (n ≥ 6) and 7 transmission clusters (n < 6) were initially (without genomic data) suspected over the study period (Fig. 3). The outbreaks occurred in 2017 in the ICU and in 2018 in the ICU, paediatric unit, and NICU. The 7 transmission clusters between 2018 and 2022 involved 2 to 5 CPE isolates each. Three of these transmission clusters occurred in the ICU, and two in the Nephrology settings. CPE were also isolated from the hospital environmental surface during several spread events (Fig. 3).

Based on the phylogeny of bacterial strains and new hygiene investigations, we confirmed the 4 outbreaks identified and 5 of the 7 transmission clusters (Fig. 4). Two suspected transmission clusters (2022-ICU and 2018-Digestive surgery) were disproved. In addition, 1 isolate initially labelled as an isolated case (CLIN104) was assigned to a transmission cluster in the cohorting unit in 2021.

Genomic analysis uncovered 2 transmission clusters that had not been previously suspected (2018-Oncology implicated 3 Enterobacter kobei ST125 and 2018-Other implicating 2 Enterobacter xiangfangensis ST88; Fig. 4). Interestingly, E. hoffmanii ST104 was found in the two outbreaks in ICU in 2017 and 2018 (Figs. 3 and 4) with one strain isolated from the ICU environmental surface in 2017 suggesting a relationship between these two outbreaks. Nine potential transmissions could not be conclusively confirmed through targeted hygiene reinvestigations (Figs. 4 and 6). Such cases were classified as ‘association not confirmed’ due to insufficient epidemiological evidence for direct transmission (please see Methods). These included: temporally separated cases: from patients in the same department but at non-overlapping time periods, and spatiotemporally discordant cases: from patients hospitalized concurrently but in different departments.

With regard to IncM2 plasmids, four potential new associations were hypothesized but could not be corroborated by epidemiological hygiene data (Fig. 6). Furthermore, several spread events appear to be epidemiologically linked. For instance, Variant 1 demonstrated extensive circulation in 2018, being identified in outbreaks across the paediatric unit, ICU, and oncology settings (Table 3). Notably, this plasmid variant was also detected years later in patients previously hospitalized in the ICU (Fig. 6), suggesting persistent colonization. Variant 2 was identified in the ICU during both 2017 and 2018, indicating temporal persistence of this plasmid within the department (Table 3). Notably, one strain (CLIN58) was isolated from an environmental surface in 2018, reinforcing the role of fomites in transmission. Finally, Variant 4 was detected in 2018 in both the NICU and paediatric unit, suggesting these cases may represent a single interconnected outbreak.

Discussion

Although CPE is classified as a critical priority on the WHO’s list of Bacterial Priority Pathogen2, research in the Pacific region remains scarce45–47. Since the first detection of CPE in July 2013 (Klebsiella pneumoniae IMP on a catheter in a patient admitted to the CHT’s pneumology unit), the number of CPE-positive patients has increased in NC, peaking in 2018, following outbreaks observed in the ICU in 2017 and the NICU in 20185. Our study highlights the importance of this period, which saw two outbreaks occur in the ICU and paediatric unit that went unidentified at the time.

Our study reported a 15% fatality rate among infected patients, which is lower than the rates reported in the literature48,49. This low fatality rate may be due to the limited number of associated resistances, with susceptibility retained to trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (60%) and fluoroquinolones (48%). These data highlight the importance of using screening agar to effectively detect CPE strains carrying the blaIMP-4 gene. Goire et al. (2016) demonstrated in Australia that screening media such as Brilliance™ CRE Agar are insufficiently sensitive for detecting blaIMP-4, in contrast to other CPE types13. In the case of a blaIMP-4, the authors recommended using a combination of ESBL screening media, the CarbaNP test, and an in-house blaIMP-4 real-time PCR test. Here, we also confirmed the low level of resistance to carbapenems in strains carrying the blaIMP-4 gene7,50, which are, however, well captured when using an ESBL agar plate. Additionally, the low level of resistance to carbapenems conferred by blaIMP-4, allows certain carbapenems to be used in combination with cotrimoxazole or fluoroquinolones51,52.

While traditional microbiology approaches primarily targeted bacterial isolates, modern genomic tools now enable in-depth analysis of plasmids, integrons, and resistance genes53. By integrating these multiple levels, our study traced the emergence and transmission of the blaIMP-4 gene within the region, mediated by an IncM2-type plasmid. To distinguish clonal lineages, we applied a threshold of 53 core genome single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), aligning with published ranges (21–80 SNPs) used to define hospital outbreaks, depending on the bacterial genus36,54. Our findings implicate Enterobacter hoffmannii ST104 in two ICU outbreaks (2017–2018), with the detection of an isolate (CLIN37) on a hospital environmental surface suggesting a likely connection between these events. Plasmid-level investigations further revealed epidemiological links between transmission clusters and finally highlighted the spread and persistence of IMP-4 IncM2 plasmids. For example, variant 4 of the IMP-4 IncM2 plasmid, identified in both the 2018 NICU and paediatric unit outbreaks, supports the hypothesis that these cases may represent a single, interconnected epidemic. Since 2018, the epidemiological situation has stabilized, with a noticeable decline in case numbers. This trend may be partially attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic, during which NC implemented a strict “zero-COVID” strategy, including rapid border closures, enhanced hygiene protocols, and reduced hospital patient volumes55. Additionally, proactive hospital measures, such as the establishment of a dedicated isolation unit in CHT (cohorting unit), systematic screening of patients before and after medical evacuations (Med Evac) from Australia (since 2015), and automated email alerts for admitted CPE carriers (since 2017) may have further mitigated potential CPE transmission in NC5. While the incidence of CPE has remained stable over the past four years, the persistent risk of dissemination underscores the critical need for sustained surveillance to prevent future outbreaks.

Our study confirmed the predominance of IMP-type carbapenemases in NC, with the blaIMP-4 gene serving as the primary genetic determinant. This carbapenemase, which is widely distributed across the Asia-Pacific region7–9,56 has emerged as the dominant carbapenem resistance gene in Australia7,10–14,56. Notably, genomic analysis revealed that blaIMP-4 was predicted to be carried on an IncM2 plasmid in over 68% of sequenced isolates, linking this plasmid as a key driver of hospital-associated transmission in NC. The IncM2 plasmid has circulated in Australia since 2004 and underwent widespread dissemination until at least 201912,40,57, further emphasizing its epidemiological significance in regional carbapenemase spread. Variants 1, 5 and of IMP-4 IncM2 isolated in NC are strictly identical to IncM2 found in Australia between in 2009 and 201212. Notwithstanding these similarities, our analysis revealed distinct genetic differences between the IncM2 plasmids identified in NC and those reported in Australia. The most notable divergence is a 9,305-base-pair insertion encoding colicin V production found in Variants 2, 3, 4 and 7. This region, which is also chromosomally located in these isolates, is flanked by an integrase on the plasmid, facilitating its integration into the plasmid backbone. At the time of this study, this specific plasmid variant had not been identified in any other regions, suggesting a potentially unique event in NC.

Spread potential was confirmed using conjugation assays; we obtained 15 transconjugants. Our conjugation experiments demonstrated that the IMP-4 IncM2 plasmid exhibited broad transferability, successfully conjugating not only into a laboratory-adapted E. coli CIP 104049 but also into an environmentally isolated (E. coli AE), plasmid-naïve recipient. This cross-context transfer efficiency spanning both controlled laboratory conditions and environmentally derived bacteria underscores the plasmid’s versatility in horizontal gene dissemination. This suggests its potential to persist and spread across diverse ecological niches, including clinical, environmental, and commensal bacterial populations. Strengthened surveillance and control of this plasmid are critical, as curbing its dissemination is key to preventing future CPE outbreaks. Our findings demonstrate that the blaIMP-4 gene was consistently embedded within a class 1 resistance integron, exhibiting two distinct structural configurations. These integron arrangements are well-documented in Australia as common vehicles for the blaIMP-4 dissemination40. Our results underscore the importance of plasmid-focused investigations, particularly in dissemination events involving genetically diverse strains but a shared plasmid, as observed in the 2018 paediatric outbreak.

Masecic et al. (2023) documented the widespread circulation of the blaIMP-4 gene in Australia, attributing its rapid dissemination to two key factors: (1) diversification of genetic contexts for the resistance determinant and (2) the emergence of high-risk strain-genetic setting pairs, termed “propagators”40. Our findings in NC align closely with this model. While we confirm the dominance of blaIMP-4 -bearing IncM2 plasmids, we also observe the emergence of an IncC-type plasmid harboring the same class 1 integron (structure 1). This suggests that NC may mirror Australia’s epidemiological trajectory, albeit with a temporal delay of a few years. Continued surveillance of CPEs, particularly the potential spread of IncC plasmids carrying this integron, is also critical. Regarding high-risk strains, we identified the persistent circulation of K. pneumoniae ST16, a globally recognized CPE sublineage with significant public health implications58, that has also been reported in Fiji59. Additionally, we isolated C. freundii ST98 (2014–2021), a known carbapenemase-producing lineage with worldwide distribution60. E. hoffmannii ST104 and Serratia sp. strains have also undergone extensive dissemination in NC, particularly in paediatric settings. Notably, these bacteria exhibit remarkable environmental persistence, with some strains detected since 2015 and demonstrating sustained transmission in hospital environments.

Beyond this temporal parallel, our study reveals direct epidemiological links between NC and Australia. For instance, E. hoffmannii ST254, a lineage previously implicated in the widespread transmission of blaIMP-4 in Australia61, was also identified in NC. Genomic analysis further demonstrates that the closest IncM2-type plasmids (based on Mash distance) were isolated from strains in two hospitals in Sydney12 and one in Melbourne6. Notably, Sydney serves as the primary destination for medical evacuations from NC, and in our study, one of three patients testing positive upon return from Sydney carried an IncM2 plasmid. Additionally, a repatriated patient from Australia was found to harbor E. xiangfangensis (CLIN71; formerly E. hormaechei subsp. xiangfangensis) with an IncHI2A-type plasmid (MOB_typer primary cluster AA739), a plasmid type previously reported in Australia31,50. Several other lineages detected in NC, including: C. freundii ST98 (IncC-type, MOB_typer clusters AA860/AA627), K. pneumoniae ST20 (IncM2-type, MOB_typer cluster AA02; or IncHI2A-type, MOB_typer cluster AA739; or IncC-type, MOB_typer clusters AA860/AA627) have also been documented in Australia12,17,62. These findings reinforce previously reported epidemiological connections between Australia and NC, such as those observed for vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus5 and Corynebacterium diphtheriae63. Further genomic collaboration is essential to elucidate microbial and AMR linkages within the Pacific region. Given these suspected cross-border spread events, enhanced screening of patients involved in medical evacuations, both departing from and returning to NC, is critically important.

While our findings reveal parallels with Australia’s epidemiological landscape, this study emphasizes the pivotal role of environmental reservoirs in both sustaining and disseminating AMR. Notably, several clones identified in clinical samples concurrently contained environmental isolates, including some isolates recovered from hospital environmental surfaces during all four outbreaks, strongly suggesting that the hospital environment serves as a critical reservoir for their persistence and transmission. To support this hypothesis, CLIN08, identified as Serratia, was isolated from a bucket containing disinfectant. Resistance genes to quaternary ammonium compounds (QACs), such as qacE∆1 located on class 1 resistance integron were detected in CPE (data not shown). The persistence in the hospital environment of antibiotic- and disinfectant-resistant bacteria with the ability to transfer plasmids poses a serious threat64. These results are concerning because Serratia isolates have already spread widely in various hospitals in Germany65. A particularly alarming observation is the rapid colonization of the new hospital facility (inaugurated in 2016) by these antibiotic-resistant bacteria. Our data demonstrate that the majority of Serratia spp. isolates originated from environmental sources, aligning with prior studies that highlight the role of environmental reservoirs in ICU66 or NICU67 settings for the endemicity of opportunistic pathogens, such as Serratia marcescens. In Australia, Masecic et al. (2023)40 have described Serratia spp. strains acting as “propagators” of resistance, while the E28 strain described by Kizny Gordon et al. (2020)12 exhibited significant homology with the Serratia isolates in our study. This genomic similarity supports the hypothesis of regional circulation of these resistant clones. The implementation of a dedicated isolation unit in CHT to isolate patients appears beneficial in NC, as evidenced by the stabilization of CPE case numbers and the absence of further outbreaks in the CHT since its establishment. However, our findings raise concerns, as we detected multiple cases of C. freundii ST98 associated with this unit, questioning its long-term efficacy in preventing transmission68. Given these results, we propose that a comprehensive analysis of hospital environmental surfaces, particularly within the cohorting unit, is urgently needed to elucidate transmission dynamics and enhance infection control strategies against these resistant pathogens.

Despite comprehensive surveillance in NC, our cohort exhibited a notable underrepresentation of community-acquired strains. This sampling bias suggests that hospital-centred surveillance alone may significantly underestimate the true burden and diversity of CPE circulation in NC. To address this limitation, future studies should expand surveillance efforts beyond healthcare settings to include community-based sampling (e.g., outpatient clinics, long-term care facilities) and environmental samples. Supporting this need, Bourles et al. (2023)69 detected KPC-type carbapenemases, alongside IMP producers, in hospital wastewater, neither of which was identified in our clinical cohort. Although our screening method (ESBL agar) may have underestimated pure OXA-48-producing strains, without ESBL associated, their low reported prevalence in our region70 suggests a limited impact on our results. This limitation was addressed through the systematic use of specific OXA-48 media since January 2025. Since its implementation, no OXA-48 strains have been detected. While conjugation experiments in this study were conducted directly using clinical CPE isolates as donor strains, a key refinement for future investigations would involve standardizing the donor background. By comparing transfer efficiencies using the same donor strains across different recipients, potential species-specific biases, such as variations in mating pair formation, plasmid stability, or host restriction systems, could be minimized or controlled. Such an approach would strengthen the reproducibility and comparability of horizontal gene transfer rates, providing a more robust assessment of the IncM2 plasmid’s intrinsic transferability and its role in CPE dissemination.

Long-read sequencing is currently being implemented at the CHT, a development that holds critical potential for infection control in NC. Given our genomic findings, particularly the dominance of IMP-4 IncM2 plasmids, and the rapid turnaround time demonstrated by Madueño et al. (2024) (results under 24 h)71 or less than one hour for Bloomfield et al. (2026)72, this technology represents an essential tool for limiting CPE transmission, especially during suspected outbreaks. The implementation of pathogen genomic surveillance has already been established as best practice in the Western Pacific region73. Considering NC epidemiological landscape and the documented links with Australia, we propose expanding surveillance efforts to include neighbouring Pacific islands Countries and Territories (PICTs) with strong epidemiological connections to NC, such as Wallis and Futuna, French Polynesia, and Vanuatu. Such regional genomic monitoring would provide critical insights into CPE dissemination dynamics across the PICTs, enabling more effective, coordinated infection control strategies.

Conclusion

Through the integration of phenotypic, genomic, and epidemiological analyses, this study explained the complex transmission dynamics of CPE in NC. Our findings reveal three critical insights: polyclonal outbreaks in high-risk units, plasmid-mediated dissemination as a dominant mechanism and an urgent call for multifaceted interventions. The persistent plasmid-mediated spread underscores the pivotal role of mobile genetic elements in CPE transmission, a hallmark of AMR as a silent pandemic. The endemic circulation of IncM2- blaIMP-4 demands immediate action, including enhanced genomic surveillance to track plasmid dissemination in real-time, targeted infection control measures to disrupt spread in healthcare settings, and One Health approach to monitor environmental and community reservoirs. Addressing these challenges is essential to curb the silent spread of CPE and preserve the efficacy of last-resort antibiotics, safeguarding global health security in the face of escalating AMR.
