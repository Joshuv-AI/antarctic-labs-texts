Cool routes: Assessing how green space and paved paths modify heat’s effects on urban bicycling
Authors: Michael D. Garber, David Rojas-Rueda, Paquito Bernard, Tarik Benmarhnia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Understanding how heat’s effect on bicycling varies spatially across the urban environment can inform who may be bicycling in the heat and how urban-planning measures can improve thermal comfort for bicyclists.Objective:We estimated effects of heat on recreational and commute bicycling in Denver, Colorado, USA using city-wide, finely resolved bicycling data and assessed heterogeneity in effects by area-level social vulnerability and proximity to the city center and whether off-street paths and green-space measures, if intervened upon, modify these effects.Methods:From Strava Metro, we obtained daily data on recreational and commute bicycling in Denver on each street segment (N = 218,641) during the summer months of 2019–2023. We estimated effects of hot (95–99 °F [35–37 °C]) and very hot (100–104 °F [38–40 °C]) temperature on daily bicycling using model-based standardization, controlling for temporally varying confounders. We assessed effect heterogeneity by area-level social vulnerability and proximity to the city center. To assess effect modification by intervention (defined in text) of paved paths and green-space measures, we additionally controlled for social vulnerability and proximity to the urban core.Results:Heat’s attenuating effects on recreational and commute bicycling were stronger in central Denver but did not differ across area-level social vulnerability. Heat’s attenuating effects were somewhat weaker on off-street paths and considerably weaker in greener areas and near blue space.Conclusions:Results support the provision of paved paths and access to green and blue space to support bicycling during hot weather.

Introduction

Overview of heat’s effects on urban bicycling

Cities worldwide including Denver, Colorado (Denver, 2019) are encouraging active transportation such as bicycling for its health and environmental benefits (Roaf et al., 2024; World Bank, 2023). Concurrently, climate change has made extreme heat events more frequent in urban areas (Tuholske et al., 2021), which may make bicycling less desirable and place bicyclists at risk of heat-related illness (Wallace et al., 2019). Understanding how hot air temperature affects urban bicycling is important for promoting this activity while addressing heat-related health concerns in the context of climate change.

Extensive research has explored how weather, including heat, affects urban bicycling (Gössling et al., 2023; Böcker et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2017; Bernard et al., 2021), with at least two consistent conclusions emerging (Bean et al., 2021). First, outdoor physical activity, including bicycling, rises with rising air temperature until a threshold, above which it falls (Gössling et al., 2023; Bernard et al., 2021). Bicycling’s threshold temperature varies by setting, ranging from about 18 °C to 28 °C (Gössling et al., 2023; Bean et al., 2021). Second, commute (or utilitarian) bicycling is less affected by extreme weather than recreational trips (Helbich et al., 2014; Heinen et al., 2010; Sabir, 2011). As stated in a review on commute bicycling, “if they are dependent on travelling by bicycle, they cycle regardless of the weather conditions.” (Heinen et al., 2010)

These consistent findings notwithstanding, gaps in understanding remain. In particular, limited research has assessed how effects may differ across spatially varying social and environmental characteristics within a city (Helbich et al., 2014; Hong et al., 2020; Breyer et al., 2025). A better understanding of how the heat-bicycling relationship may vary by patterns in the social or built environment could guide interventions aiming to increase the thermal comfort of bicyclists who may be riding despite heat, perhaps out of necessity (Liu et al., 2017).

Spatial variation in weather’s effects on bicycling

Some studies have examined within-city spatial variation in weather’s effects on bicycling. Using a travel-diary survey, researchers found that temperature had a weaker influence on bicycling in more central areas of Rotterdam, The Netherlands (Helbich et al., 2014). In Glasgow, Scotland, Hong et al. found that rain had a stronger attenuating effect on more separated bicycling infrastructure, a finding that they suggested might be explained by more casual, weather-sensitive bicyclists using these facilities (Hong et al., 2020). They did not assess variation in effects of temperature, given Glasgow’s mild summer temperatures. In 2025, Breyer et al. examined variation in heat’s effects on bicycling and pedestrian activity in Houston, Texas, finding that parks improved heat resilience more for pedestrians than for bicyclists (Breyer et al., 2025). In the latter two studies, app-generated bicycling data with high spatial resolution and broad coverage facilitated assessment of effect modification by spatial characteristics throughout the respective cities (Hong et al., 2020; Breyer et al., 2025). In contrast, other studies have compared weather’s impact on bicycling between types of bicycling facilities using stationary counters at a handful of locations (Zhao et al., 2018; Tin et al., 2012; Miranda-Moreno and Nosal, 2011; Lanza et al.; Ermagun et al., 2018), precluding citywide comparison.

Opportunities to advance understanding of spatial variation in heat-bicycling effects within urban areas

In researching exploring spatial variation in heat’s effect on bicycling (Helbich et al., 2014; Hong et al., 2020; Breyer et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2018; Tin et al., 2012; Miranda-Moreno and Nosal, 2011), improved understanding in three areas would help inform policy as cities seek to promote bicycling while remaining attentive to bicyclists’ health and thermal comfort in hot weather. First, limited research has assessed whether heat-bicycling effects vary by social vulnerability, an important question as vulnerable individuals may be at greater risk of exposure to transport-related heat and its health consequences (Karner et al., 2015). In the U.S., bicycle commuting is more common among individuals of lower socioeconomic status (Whitfield et al., 2015; Pucher et al., 2011), a pattern that may be explained by worse access to alternative modes. A socioeconomic gradient in the heat-bicycling effect might thus be expected whereby temperature effects are weaker in lower-resourced areas, as they are for commute bicycling in general. Research from San Francisco suggests that socially disadvantaged residents rely more on non-motorized transportation during extreme heat episodes (Karner et al., 2015), but it did not examine socioeconomic variation in heat’s effect on bicycling, specifically.

Second, the literature would benefit from a deeper understanding of how modifiable urban planning strategies, like paved off-street paths and urban greening, interact with heat’s effect on bicycling in hot weather. Research has compared effects of rain and temperature on and away from separated paths (Hong et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2018; Tin et al., 2012), but temperature distributions were mild, limiting conclusions regarding hot air temperature, specifically. Considering Hong et al.’s conclusions pertaining to rainfall (Hong et al., 2020), ridership on off-street paths may be more strongly attenuated during hot weather if these facilities are disproportionately used for weather-sensitive recreational rides. On the other hand, surface streets can intensify heat due to the micro-urban heat-island effect (Hoehne et al., 2022), so bicyclists might prefer off-street paths as a cooler alternative, mitigating heat’s effects on ridership on these facilities.

Like off-street paths, bicyclists may seek urban green space during hot conditions, as urban greening can provide localized cooling (Aram et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2020; Ge et al., 2024). Accordingly, the attenuating effect of extreme heat on bicycling may be less pronounced in areas with more nearby vegetation (Breyer et al., 2025). One study on outdoor physical activity, heat, and tree canopy found that tree canopy did not meaningfully alter heat’s effect on physical activity (Lanza et al., 2020). An acknowledged limitation was that the measures were confined to a short radius around participants’ homes (Lanza et al., 2020). The aforementioned study by Breyer et al. overcame this limitation by using city-wide activity patterns, finding that urban parks reduced heat avoidance by both pedestrians and bicyclists (Breyer et al., 2025), with heterogeneity by time of day attributed to trip purpose (leisure vs. commute). Additional research using high-resolution data in settings with hot summers would further inform the extent to which greenspace may modify heat’s effect on bicycling.

Third and finally, missing from this literature assessing how the provision of paths and greenspace may mitigate heat’s effect on bicycling is the consideration of spatial confounding. Parks, greenspace, and bicycling facilities are distributed unequally across socioeconomic groups in the United States (Klompmaker et al., 2023; Ermagun et al., 2023), including in Denver (Rigolon, 2017). Given that socioeconomic vulnerability may affect active-transportation behavior during extreme heat (Karner et al., 2015), heterogeneity in heat’s effects could be due to the urban-planning intervention itself (e.g., green space) or a third variable associated with it such as socioeconomic conditions. Proximity to the city center could also act as a confounder. For example, the city center—downtown in U.S. parlance—may have less green space (Garber et al., 2024) and proportionately more commute ridership (Helbich et al., 2014). If commute bicyclists are less heat-sensitive (Helbich et al., 2014; Heinen et al., 2010; Sabir, 2011), heat’s effect on bicycling may be weaker in these areas, though not necessarily due to their lower levels of green space. To clearly guide interventions, it would be useful to build upon studies that have assessed effect heterogeneity—a description of variation in an effect of a primary exposure (heat) on an outcome (bicycling) by values of a third variable (e.g., green space)—towards the study of causal interaction or “effect modification by intervention” (VanderWeele, 2009), using VanderWeele’s phrase, controlling confounding of the relationship between both factors and the outcome (VanderWeele, 2009, 2015).

Study aims

In the present study, we compare heat’s effect on recreational and commute bicycling across spatially varying measures of the socioeconomic and built environment in Denver, Colorado, USA. Specifically, we first assess heterogeneity of heat’s effects on recreational and commute bicycling by area-level social vulnerability and by proximity to the city center. Examining effect heterogeneity is important for understanding where bicyclists may be riding despite hot weather, even if the characteristic over which the effect varies does not necessarily cause the variation. Secondly, we estimate modification of the ride-purpose-specific heat-bicycling effects by off-street paths and measures of green space, adjusting for the relation between these modifiable urban planning interventions and the heat-bicycling effects. By estimating how the heat-bicycling would change depending on values of these third variables, in this aim, we assess “effect modification by intervention.” (VanderWeele, 2009)

To achieve these aims, following others (Hong et al., 2020; Breyer et al., 2025), we use smartphone-generated bicycling data from Strava with high spatial resolution and citywide coverage. Denver presents a good setting for this research given its semi-arid climate with hot summers, expansive network of off-street bicycle trails (Denver Bike Map, 2023), and considerable socioeconomic variation between neighborhoods (Denver, 2020). Understanding how heat’s effect on bicycling interacts with modifiable urban-planning strategies in Denver can inform climate-resilient active transport planning in similar cities.

Methods

Setting

The study is set in the City and County of Denver, Colorado (henceforth, Denver). Denver has a semi-arid climate with low humidity (Geography of Denver, 2024). The hottest month is July with an average historical daytime high of 89 °F (32 °C) (Geography of Denver, 2024). The historical average relative humidity in the summer months is 42 % (June: 43 %, July: 41 %; August: 42 % (NCEI, 2024). The historical average wind speed in the summer months is 4.3 mph (1.9 m/s). As measured by the 2022 5-year American Community Survey (United States Census Bureau, 2024), 1.9 % of commuters commuted by bike in Denver, a figure that is about four times the U.S. national average.

Data sources

Bicycling

We obtained daily bicycling data in Denver at the street-segment level during the summer months (June, July, August) of 2019–2023 from Strava Metro (n, days = 460). Strava is an app used by some bicyclists to record rides that captures an estimated 5 %–15 % of urban bicycling (Lee and Sener, 2020; Garber et al., 2023; Fischer et al., 2022). We obtained the number of total rides, the number of recreational rides, and the number of commute rides observed on every segment of roadway or trail in the study area on each day. Of the 218,641 segments in the study area, 94,371 were ridden upon in any day.

Strava infers the ride purpose (commute or recreational) using a proprietary model that uses rides marked as commutes by users as the ground truth (Strava, 2020). We assume the following distinction between commute and recreational cycling: Commuter cycling, considered here to be synonymous with utilitarian or utility cycling, is defined as “journeying for a purpose upon completion of the journey” (Aldred, 2015; Stewart et al., 2015), whereas leisure or recreational cycling is “defined as a trip that is undertaken for the purpose of the journey itself and in this sense is not a form of transport.” (Aldred, 2015) For each ride purpose, we calculated daily bicycle-distance ridden on a segment by multiplying the segment’s number of rides on that day by its length (Garber et al., 2023).

Heat and precipitation

For the same days, we downloaded spatially invariant daily data on maximum temperature and precipitation as measured at the Denver Water Department (station ID: GHCND:USC00052223) (NCEI, 2024), a station located in Central Denver just east of Interstate 25 and south of Colfax Avenue.

Spatially varying attributes of the urban environment

Social vulnerability.

We measured social vulnerability at the census-tract level using CDC’s social vulnerability index (SVI) (CDC and ATSDR, 2020). We used the composite index that considers all four themes: socioeconomic status, household characteristics, racial & ethnic minority status, and housing type & transportation. We categorized SVI into quintiles, with the fifth being the most vulnerable (Supplementary Materials, eFig. 2.1). We spatially joined segments with tracts using R’s sf package (Pebesma, 2018).

Downtown.

We gathered spatial data of Denver’s neighborhoods from the city’s open data portal. (Denver Open Data Catalog) We created a three-category variable, classifying neighborhoods as downtown (Union Station, Central Business District, and Civic Center) (Downtown Denver, 2025), bordering downtown, or elsewhere (eFig. 2.2). We spatially joined this neighborhood-level classification with segments.

Off-street paved paths.

We classified segments as comprising an off-street paved path or not using OpenStreetMap (OSM) data gathered with the osmdata R package (Padgham et al.). These facilities, equivalently referred to as multi-use trails or shared-use paths, do not generally follow the roadway network and “are designed for use by pedestrians, bicyclists, and people using other mobility devices ….” (NACTO, 2025) Denver has some bike-specific paved paths, which we combined with the multi-use variety for this analysis. Paved paths differ from protected bike lanes (also called cycle tracks), as the latter are bike-specific lanes within the roadway directly adjacent to the vehicular traffic’s right of way (NACTO, 2025). We focus on paved paths because Denver has an extensive network of them (Denver Bike Map, 2023) and because our research question concerns whether bicyclists prefer facilities away from streets, as surface streets can exacerbate urban heat (Hoehne et al., 2022).

Informed by guidance for classifying bicycle infrastructure using OSM (Ferster et al., 2023), we classified off-street paved paths as segments meeting specific combinations of highway keys (eSection 3.1). Through manual checks, we found that these criteria did not capture all paved-path segments in Denver, so we additionally gathered selected prominent paved paths by their names (details in R code: https://github.com/michaeldgarber/bike-denver). A map of resulting paved paths appears in eFig. 2.3.

Greenness and blue space.

We used two measures of green space: greenness, generally, and parks, specifically. These measures capture related but distinct constructs (Taylor and Hochuli, 2017), and each may affect the heat-bicycling relationship. Greenness refers to the continuous level of vegetation in green spaces, which can include parks, landscaped streets, and private lawns or gardens. Even if green spaces are not publicly accessible, bicyclists may prefer streets or paths with higher adjacent greenness, as the vegetation may provide shade or evaporative cooling.

We measured greenness using the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) at a spatial resolution of 30 m as measured by Landsat-8 as downloaded from US Geological Survey Earth Explorer. For each 30 m pixel, we calculated NDVI for June 12, 2021, a cloud-free day (image identifiers in eSection 3.2). (U.S. Geological Survey, 2024) We then drew a 30-m buffer around each segment and calculated the segment’s area-weighted average NDVI therein, where the areal average includes the segment of roadway or trail. NDVI is strongly influenced by the presence of water, so we gathered data on bodies of water including rivers, creeks, ponds, lakes, and reservoirs from OpenStreetMap again using osmdata (Padgham et al.). We then classified each segment as near (within 30 m of) water or not. Finally, each segment was categorized into one of four groups: (1) near water; (2) low greenness (below the 25th NDVI percentile of segments, <0.0872); (3) medium greenness (between the 25th and 75th NDVI percentiles, 0.0872–0.1996), or (4) high greenness (above the 75th NDVI percentile, ≥0.1996; map in eFig. 2.4).

Parks.

We also considered parks, specifically. As publicly accessible green spaces, parks may impact the heat-bicycling relationship differently from general greenness, as they allow bicyclists to freely pass through. Moreover, parks are well-defined areas with clear boundaries away from the roadway network, so bicyclists may route through parks more deliberately than the more continuous construct of general greenness.

We used osmdata to gather data on parks using the following criteria: [key = leisure & value = park] and [key = leisure & value = nature reserve] (map in eFig. 2.5). Following Breyer et al. (2025), we drew a 10-m buffer around the park polygons to capture nearby paths and streets. We classified segments as in a park or not using a spatial join.

Analysis

Heat classification

We classified days as either hot (95–99 °F [35–37 °C]) or very hot (100–104 °F [38–40 °C]). We categorize temperature rather than leave it continuous because our goal is not to model an overall temperature-bicycling relationship, as that aim has been achieved elsewhere (Gössling et al., 2023; Böcker et al., 2013; Liu et al., 2017; Bernard et al., 2021; Bean et al., 2021). Rather, we aim to assess heterogeneity of effects of hot temperature, specifically. We chose these cutoff values because they are locally interpretable multiples of 5 °F, they are on the upper end of the study period’s temperature distribution (67th and 96th percentile, respectively; eFig. 1.1), because the very-hot threshold has been used in other research on heat and outdoor physical activity in the Western U.S. (Breyer et al., 2025; Lanza et al.), and because they correspond to zones of caution or heat stress in common heat indices such as the Universal Thermal Climate Index (UTCI) (NOAA; Błażejczyk et al., 2013), assuming typical humidity and wind conditions in Denver’s summer (eSection 3.3).

We use temperatures of 90 °F (32 °C) and below (minimum value: 57 °F [14 °C]) as the referent category for descriptive comparisons and effect estimation. Although 90 °F would be considered hot in an absolute sense (on the UTCI scale, it falls between “moderate” and “strong” heat stress (Błażejczyk et al., 2013)), the temperature is the 45th percentile over the study period. As such, temperatures 90 °F and below are sufficiently common as to be plausibly exchangeable with the hot or very hot days but are nevertheless on the cooler side of the temperature distribution in this context.

Descriptive analyses

In descriptive analyses, we compared mean daily distance ridden on hot and very hot days with the referent category. We calculated the ratio of mean daily distance ridden on very hot and hot days with corresponding ridership on referent-temperature days for each ride purpose and within strata of the spatially varying covariates. As a preliminary measure of effect heterogeneity, we divided each stratum-specific ratio measure by the ratio in the covariate’s corresponding referent condition.

Estimation of heat-bicycling effects

Estimating summary effects over the study area.

We estimated the average treatment effect of both hot (95–99 °F [35–37 °C]) and very hot (100–104 °F [38–40 °C]) temperature on daily bicycle-distance among the treated days (i.e., the average treatment effect on the treated, ATT), where treatment is defined as days within the temperature range. For each ride purpose, we fit a linear model for daily bicycle-distance over all segments among days with the referent temperature (57–90 °F [14–32 °C]). All models included the following time-varying covariates: year (treated categorically as dummy variables), day of week (categorical), month (categorical), precipitation (no rain, less than 0.1 inch [0.25 cm] of rain, 0.1 inch of rain or more), and an interaction term between year and month. We included these time-varying covariates because we anticipated each may confound the heat-bicycling effect. For example, rain may suppress bicycling (Hong et al., 2020), and rainy days may be cooler than those without precipitation.

For each ride purpose, we used these models to estimate counterfactual ridership on treatment days had they not been treated. This approach can be considered model-based standardization or a variation of the parametric g formula (Hernán and Robins, 2020). The counterfactual clause—“had they not been treated”—is implied because each model is fit only in untreated observations (i.e., days in the referent temperature category) (Garber et al., 2023). For each temperature threshold and ride purpose, we estimated ratio effects by dividing the total observed bicycle-distance (BD) ridden over the treatment days by the corresponding predicted counterfactual BD, i.e., ratio=BDobservedBDestimatedcounterfactual.

Estimating effects within strata of spatially varying covariates.

When estimating effects within strata of spatially varying covariates, our modeling approach differed depending on whether we aimed to assess effect heterogeneity (by SVI and by downtown category) or effect modification by intervention (paved paths and greenness measures). To estimate effects within strata of SVI (model group 2, Table 1), we summarized bicycling data by day and SVI quintile. For each ride purpose, among the referent-temperature days, we fit linear mixed models for bicycling distance including the time-varying covariates previously described, along with SVI quintile, and, because they improved model fit, interaction terms between SVI quintile and year and between SVI quintile and day of the week (Table 1). We included a random intercept for day because observations repeated within day with this data structure. We fit analogous models using downtown category as the spatially varying covariate and the analogous interaction terms (model group 3, Table 1). We used these models to predict counterfactual bicycle-distance during the hot and very hot days within strata of SVI and downtown category from which we calculated corresponding ratio effects.

When our objective was to estimate effect modification (model groups 4–6, Table 1), we summarized bicycling data by day, the effect modifier of interest, and by each potential confounder between the effect modifier and the outcome (SVI and downtown category). In the reference-temperature days, we fit ride-purpose-specific models with the same time-varying terms along with a term for the effect modifier of interest, SVI, downtown category, and, for model fit, interaction terms between the effect modifier and downtown category, the effect modifier and SVI quintile, and SVI quintile and year (model groups 4–6, Table 1). We again included a random intercept for day to account for within-day repeated observations, and we used these models to estimate stratum-specific ratio effects.

Measures of effect heterogeneity or effect modification.

To measure effect heterogeneity, we calculated a ratio of ratios by dividing each stratum’s ratio effect estimate by the ratio in the corresponding referent condition. For example, we divided the estimated heat-bicycling ratio effect on paved paths by the corresponding value away from paved paths. We interpret the ratio of ratios for SVI and downtown category as measures of effect heterogeneity and those for paved paths and each greenness measure as measures of effect modification by intervention because the latter’s stratum-specific effects were estimated using models that condition on the potential confounders between the effect modifiers and the bicycling outcomes (VanderWeele, 2009).

To assess bias on the relative scale between the descriptive ratio-of-ratio measures described earlier and those that adjust for confounding, we described the unadjusted measure as either biased downward or upward and towards or away from the null, as compared with the adjusted measure, following terminology from epidemiology (Lash et al., 2009). We classified those for which the ratio comparing the two ratio-of-ratio measures was between 0.99 and 1.01 as unbiased.

Uncertainty.

We used bootstrapping to estimate uncertainty. We re-sampled each model-predicted value from a normal distribution, where the mean was the predicted estimate, and the standard deviation was the standard deviation of the model’s month-specific residuals. For example, if the predicted value corresponded to a day in July, we used residuals (true value minus predicted value) from the model’s fit in July. We propagated the randomly varying model-predicted value through the analysis to calculate summary measures in each replicate. Reported confidence intervals (CIs) are 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the corresponding measure over 3000 bootstrap replicates.

To further facilitate interpretation of both the magnitude and precision of the ratio-of-ratio measures and to avoid using CIs as a surrogate significance test (Rothman, 2014), we calculated the proportion of bootstrap replicates in which the ratio-of-ratios measure was above the null value of 1, where 1 indicates no relative difference in the estimated ratio effects between the groups. For the null value to be most compatible with the data (Amrhein and Greenland, 2022), we would expect about half of replicates to fall on either side of 1.

Results

Description of bicycling data

Of the 459 days under study, the median daytime high was 86 °F (30 °C) in June, 95 °F (35 °C) in July, and 94 °F (34 °C) in August (Table 2). Overall, 29 % of days were classified as hot (95–99 °F [35–37 °C]), and 10 % were very hot (100–104 °F [38–40 °C]; eFig. 1.1).

Between 7154 and 10,541 individuals per year recorded a ride appearing in the Strava bicycling dataset during the study period (eTable 1.1), an annual median of 47 % of which were aged 18–34 years old (eTable 1.2). About a third of their trips were commutes (eTable 1.1), and commutes comprised about 14 % of distance ridden (Table 2). Fig. 1 presents the spatial distribution of average daily ridership throughout the study area, and ridership stratified by spatially varying covariates is presented in Table 3. Downtown segments had relatively high ridership (7.4 times ridden upon per day) and commute share of distance ridden (23 %). Paved paths were ridden upon 46 times (47.4/1.03) more frequently than other segments, and this relative level was slightly lower (43 times) in the commute subset (Table 3).

Associations between spatially varying covariates

Associations between spatially varying covariates are presented graphically in the supplement (eSection 4) to inform the extent to which they may serve as a proxy for one another in estimates of effect modification if not adjusted for. Paved paths were distributed similarly across SVI quintiles, while greenness was weakly negatively associated with SVI, such that more vulnerable areas had less average greenness. The least and middle SVI quintiles had the highest share of segment length in a park (about 13 %). Paved paths were most common downtown (2.8 % of segment length) and least common adjacent to downtown (1.5 %). Greenness exhibited a strong gradient across downtown categories: most of segment length (74 %) downtown had low greenness, as compared with 39 % adjacent to downtown and 18 % elsewhere. The high-greenness share was similar on (29 %) and away from (26 %) paved paths, but paths had much lower prevalence of low greenness (2 % vs 22 %). Paved paths were far more likely to be near water, but most segments near water were not paved paths.

Descriptive associations between heat and bicycling

Compared with referent days, on very hot days, there was 0.94 times as much recreational ridership and 1.14 as much commute ridership (eTable 5). On hot days, there was 0.98 as much recreational ridership, and 1.02 times commute ridership. Within ride purpose, these descriptive associations, which do not adjust for either temporal or spatial confounding, were fairly consistent across strata of the spatially varying measures of the urban environment, though some variation was evident. For example, on very hot days in the most socially vulnerable areas, there was 1.17 times as much commute ridership as occurred on referent days, and this ratio was 1.04 times higher than the corresponding ratio in the least vulnerable areas (1.17/1.13 = 1.04).

Estimated effects of heat on recreational and commute bicycling

Overall

Over the study area, the estimated ratio effect of very hot (100–104 °F [38–40 °C]) temperature on recreational bicycling was 0.89 (95 % confidence interval (CI): 0.84, 0.96), and the corresponding estimate for commute bicycling was 0.98 (0.91, 1.06; Fig. 2), controlling for temporally varying covariates (Table 1). Adjustment for confounding by temporally varying covariates suggests that very hot temperatures had a stronger attenuating effect on both commute and recreational bicycling than the descriptive associations imply. For commute ridership, the point estimate moved across the null (from 1.14 [eTable 5] to 0.98). Considering the effect of hot (95–99 °F [35–37 °C]) temperatures on bicycling, the attenuating effect was slightly weaker for recreational ridership (0.94 [0.91, 0.98]) and about the same as the very-hot threshold for commute ridership (0.97 [0.93, 1.02]).

Effect heterogeneity by SVI and downtown category

Considering effect heterogeneity by SVI (Fig. 3), confidence intervals overlapped in all comparisons within ride type, suggesting differences in point estimates could be explained by modeling error or random variability. No discernible gradient between SVI and the heat-bicycling effects is apparent. Considering the complete distribution of bootstrap replicates, the weakest effects were observed in the middle SVI quintile. For example, in 82 % of bootstrap replicates, the effect of very hot temperatures on recreational bicycling was nearer to null in this quintile than in the least vulnerable quintile (Table 4).

By contrast, for both ride purposes and temperature thresholds, the estimated attenuating effect of heat on bicycling was strongest downtown, next strongest adjacent to downtown, and weakest elsewhere (Fig. 3). Although CIs overlap in all but one comparison, the distribution of replicates of the ratio-of-ratios measures favors non-null differences between the groups, particularly at the very-hot temperature threshold. For example, the estimated attenuating effect of very hot temperatures on commute ridership (ratio: 0.87 [0.79, 0.97]) was stronger—a ratio lower and farther from one—downtown than the corresponding ratio effect away from downtown (1.01 [0.92, 1.12) in 98 % of replicates (Table 4).

Effect modification by paved paths and green-space measures

Estimated effects within strata of off-street paved paths and each green-space measure, controlling for both temporally and spatially varying covariates are presented in Fig. 4. In between 67 % and 91 % of bootstrap replicates, ratio-of-ratio measures indicated that both recreational and commute ridership was less affected by heat on paths than elsewhere (Table 4). The modification of the heat-bicycling effect by paths was strongest for commute ridership, among which the ratio effect of the hottest temperatures on paved paths (1.00 [0.96,1.05]) was 1.06 (0.97, 1.14) times higher—closer to 1 and thus weaker—than the ratio effect (0.95 [0.89, 1.01]) on non-path segments. The ratio-of-ratios measure comparing the two effect estimates was above 1 in 91 % of replicates. Confounding adjustment suggests corresponding unadjusted ratio-of-ratios measures in eTable 5 are biased towards the null, implying that paved paths reduced heat’s attenuating effect on commute bicycling more strongly than the unadjusted associations indicate. By contrast, the modifying influence of paths on the heat-bicycling effect was weaker among recreational rides at the cooler threshold (ratio of ratios: 1.01 [0.96, 1.06], above 1 in 2 of 3 replicates), and confounding adjustment suggests ratio-of-ratio measures were unbiased.

Results suggest greenness and blue space strongly modify the heat-bicycling effect among both ride purposes at both temperature thresholds (Fig. 4). On very hot days, estimated ratio effects on recreational (0.91 [0.83, 1.01]) and commute bicycling (0.96 [0.86, 1.09]) in the greenest areas were, respectively, 1.16 (1.03, 1.33) and 1.24 (1.04, 1.49), times weaker than corresponding effects (0.78 [0.72, 0.85] and 0.77 [0.69, 0.88]) in the least green areas, and ratio-of-ratio measures were above 1 in 99 % of replicates. The modifying effect of blue space on the heat-bicycling pathway was similar to or stronger than that of the high-greenness category. Compared with their adjusted counterparts, all unadjusted ratio-of-ratio measures were biased downward and either towards or across the null (Table 4), suggesting that adjustment for confounding revealed stronger effect modification than indicated by unadjusted associations.

Finally, results show that at the hottest temperature threshold, parks strengthened heat’s attenuating effect on commute bicycling such that bicyclists avoided parks more than other areas (ratio of ratios: 0.90 [0.84, 0.96]; below 1 in 100 % of replicates). This modifying influence was in the same direction but weaker for recreational rides at both thresholds, e.g., ratio of ratios among very hot days: 0.98 (0.92, 1.06), with less precision (below 1 in 69% of replicates).

Discussion

Contributions

Using daily bicycling data with high spatial resolution and broad spatial coverage across Denver, Colorado over five summers, we estimated that hot (95–99 °F [35–37 °C]) and very hot (100–104 °F [38–40 °C]) attenuated recreational bicycling more so than commute bicycling. This result was expected, as commute bicycling has previously demonstrated less sensitivity to weather conditions than recreational bicycling (Helbich et al., 2014; Heinen et al., 2010; Sabir, 2011).

Our study’s major empirical contributions are its assessment of i) within-city variation in heat-bicycling effects by social vulnerability and proximity to downtown, and ii) effect modification by paths and green-space measures. We additionally make a conceptual contribution by accounting for confounding in estimates of effect modification by intervention (VanderWeele, 2009), adjusting for the possibility that heterogeneity in effects by paths and greenspace could be explained by variation in other characteristics with similar spatial patterns, such as socioeconomic conditions or proximity to downtown. In this way, our study explicitly estimates how heat-bicycling effects would change were these urban-planning measures intervened upon.

We expected a weaker effect of heat on commute bicycling in more vulnerable areas, as these areas may have more individuals without car access and who thus may continue bicycling out of necessity despite the heat (Karner et al., 2015). In contrast, no clear pattern emerged in the heat-bicycling effects across social vulnerability quintiles, suggesting that heat’s impact on bicycling behavior did not vary strongly by social vulnerability. This surprising finding could reflect the true pattern in this setting. It could also be partly explained by the aggregated and selected nature of the bicycling data, as we elaborate in Section 4.2.

The stronger attenuating effect of heat on both commute and recreational bicycling in more central areas was also surprising, since research from The Netherlands found that bicycling in more central areas was less sensitive to weather (Helbich et al., 2014). Our finding has at least two plausible explanations. First, downtown Denver has comparatively high density of public transit, (RTD System Map) so individuals may opt for the bus or train over bicycling on hot days. Second, as eFig. 4.6 shows, downtown Denver is much less green than other areas, and our effect-modification results suggest that bicyclists may be avoiding downtown on very hot days due in part to this low greenness.

Of the three modifiable urban-planning strategies we assessed—off-street paths, general greenness, and parks—both paved paths and general greenness reduced heat’s attenuating effects on commute and recreational bicycling, while parks did not. Off-street paths have shown to increase bicycling in general in U.S. settings (Garber et al., 2022a), as they remove stressful interactions with cars except at street-level intersections. Results here suggest paths may also positively affect bicycling on hot summer days by providing a more thermally comfortable place to ride away from surface streets, which can exacerbate heat (Hoehne et al., 2022). Notably, the share of commute ridership was about the same on and away from paths (Table 3), which contrasts with other research suggesting off-street paths primarily serve recreational bicyclists (Hong et al., 2020).

Our finding that general greenness and proximity to blue space reduced heat’s attenuated effects on bicycling for both purposes aligns with our expectation that the cooling effect of blue or green infrastructure may partly mitigate heat’s negative influence on bicycling (Gunawardena et al., 2017). Results do not necessarily imply that commute bicyclists detour to ride near greenery on hot days but rather that those bicyclists whose typical routes pass near greenness or water are less deterred from riding.

Our results align with some aspects of Breyer et al.’s Houston study but differ in others (Breyer et al., 2025). They found that ridership was less attenuated by heat in parks than elsewhere but that this pattern did not hold during commuting times of the day (Breyer et al., 2025). By contrast, we found that greenness similarly attenuated the heat-bicycling effect for both recreational and commute bicycling. Our results also differ from Breyer et al. regarding parks. Although greenness in general attenuated heat-bicycling effects in our study, parks, specifically, did not follow this pattern and may have even reversed it. On the hottest days, commute bicyclists avoided parks more than would have been expected were these days otherwise the same but not hot. This result is surprising considering both blue space and greenness attenuated heat’s negative effect on bicycling in our study, and parks had a higher share of both high greenness and blue space (eFig. 4.10). Differences in how parks mitigate heat’s effects on bicycling between the two cities could be partly due to differences in tree canopy. Although both Denver and Houston have relatively low tree canopy for their climate and population density (Kommenda, 2025), Houston’s tree canopy coverage of 22 % exceeds Denver’s value of 17 % (Kommenda, 2025). Future research could investigate park attributes, such as tree canopy, that may influence variation in heat-bicycling effects.

Limitations and caveats

This study has limitations and caveats that should be considered when interpreting its results. First, the data are aggregated at the area level; individuals are not followed. This caveat is particularly pertinent when interpreting results stratified by the area-level SVI measure. Effects on ridership in socially vulnerable areas could be partly due to the ridership of individuals who reside in less vulnerable areas but travel through vulnerable areas. In aggregate, however, because most rides would begin or end at home, a considerable share of the ridership occurring in socially vulnerable areas is likely contributed by residents of those areas, and thus we expect area-level results to approximate the individual-level analogue.

Second, the data are restricted to Strava-using bicyclists. Although research indicates that Strava data, its commute ridership in particular (Fischer et al., 2020), have become more demographically representative of the broader bicycling population since 2020 (Fischer et al., 2022), Strava-using bicyclists are unlikely to fully represent the general bicycling population (Garber et al., 2019). A lack of representativeness does not necessarily imply selection bias in effect estimates, however. To bias effect estimates, conditional on ride purpose, Strava-using bicyclists would have to respond differently to heat than those who do not use the app (Garber et al., 2019). The potential magnitude and direction of such a bias is difficult to predict. If Strava-using bicyclists are more enthusiastic bicyclists (Garber et al., 2019), they may be willing to ride despite the heat. Conversely, if they skew wealthier (Garber et al., 2019), they may have more resources to switch modes in response to heat.

Measures of effect heterogeneity may be even more resistant to bias than overall effects. Bias in measures of effect heterogeneity would require not only that Strava-using bicyclists respond differently to heat than the general bicycling population but also that the heterogeneity in this response differs from the general population on the relative scale (Garber et al., 2022b). For example, for the paved-paths ratio-of-ratios measure to be biased, Strava-using bicyclists would have to more strongly prefer paved paths during hot days than the general bicycling population.

Policy implications

Our results provide empirical evidence that off-street paved paths and access to vegetation and blue space can attenuate heat’s negative effects on bicycling in a semi-arid city in the Western U.S. Developing paths near vegetation and bodies of water aligns with recommendations to create cooling corridors to improve bicyclist comfort. This intervention may particularly benefit those who have little choice but to continue bicycling despite the heat (Lanza et al.). These corridors could be supported by other approaches for reducing health effects of hot weather, such as providing shading infrastructure, misting fans, and water fountains along high-commute routes (Jay et al., 2021). These approaches align with climate justice goals while promoting active transportation.
