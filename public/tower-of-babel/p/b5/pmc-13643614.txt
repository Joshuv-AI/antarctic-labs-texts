Maternal glycaemia and child educational attainment at age 5 in the Born in Bradford cohort
Authors: Claire A Wilson, Gillian Santorelli, Emily Simonoff, Louise M Howard, Khalida Ismail, Rebecca M Reynolds
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThere is emerging evidence that maternal hyperglycaemia may be associated with adverse offspring neuro‐behavioural outcomes, which are foundational to educational success. We hypothesised that higher levels of maternal glucose would be associated with poorer educational attainment in early childhood.MethodsThe sample included 13,627 children from the UK's Born in Bradford cohort. Exposures included maternal fasting glucose, 2‐h post‐load glucose and a clinical diagnosis of gestational diabetes. The primary outcome was failure to achieve a ‘good level of development’ on the Early Years Foundation Stage Profile at age five. The association was tested using multivariable Poisson regression, accounting for sibling clusters and using multiple imputation for missing data.ResultsHigher maternal fasting glucose (at 26–28 weeks gestation) was associated with an increased risk of failing to achieve a good level of development (adjusted RR 1.04; 95% CI 1.00, 1.08; p = 0.034). This association appeared stronger in children of Pakistani ethnicity compared to White British ethnicity. No association was found for 2‐h post‐load glucose or gestational diabetes diagnosis.ConclusionsThese findings offer insights into the developmental origins of inequalities in child educational outcomes and highlight potential opportunities to optimise future health and learning through interventions during pregnancy.Keywords: diabetes in pregnancy, educational attainment, gestational diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Gestational diabetes (GDM) is defined as ‘glucose intolerance with onset during pregnancy’. Its global prevalence may be up to 30% depending on the diagnostic criteria employed and population studied. 1 GDM is associated with adverse outcomes for the children, including an increased risk for a range of metabolic disorders, such as Type 2 diabetes and obesity. 1 There has been substantially less focus on neuro‐behavioural offspring outcomes but emerging evidence suggests negative sequalae, which may in turn impact a child's readiness for school and later educational success.

Early studies of small samples of primary school‐age children exposed to GDM in utero have reported lower IQ scores and reduced gross and fine motor achievements than unexposed children. 2 , 3 However, these were most prominent in younger children (around 9 years of age) and differences between children born to mothers with or without GDM tended to diminish with age. There are fewer studies of older school‐aged children, but some find evidence for an association between GDM exposure and Autism Spectrum Disorders (ASD), Attention‐Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) and other neurodevelopmental disorders. 4

There is a well‐documented linear relationship between maternal glucose and adverse pregnancy outcomes; this was demonstrated in the Hyperglycaemia and Adverse Outcome (HAPO) study of around 25,000 pregnant individuals across nine countries, for outcomes such as macrosomia and neonatal hypoglyaemia. 5 Indeed other cohorts of school‐age children have observed an inverse correlation between maternal glycaemia and both IQ scores 6 and the degree of fine and gross motor deficiency. 7

Few studies have investigated the extent to which these early developmental delays may translate into educational outcomes. We hypothesised that increased levels of maternal glycaemia would be associated with poorer offspring educational attainment at age 5 years. We tested this hypothesis in Born in Bradford (BiB): a large prospective longitudinal cohort in the UK with a high prevalence of GDM.

METHODS

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is provided in supplementary material (Figure S1).

Sample

BiB is a prospective longitudinal cohort of 12,450 individuals with 13,758 pregnancies in Bradford, UK. Bradford has high levels of deprivation; 60% of babies born in Bradford are born into the poorest 20% of the English population according to the Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD 2010). Its ethnic makeup is predominantly bi‐ethnic: Pakistani and White British. 8

Pregnant individuals were recruited to BiB during a routine appointment for a 2 h 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), as currently recommended by the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) as the gold standard for diagnosing GDM. The test was offered to all participants between 26 and 28 weeks gestation. The sample used for analysis was all live births in the BiB cohort (Figure 1), excluding those with pre‐gestational (Type 1 and Type 2) diabetes.

How the sample was obtained.

Exposure: Measures of maternal glycaemia

The primary exposure was the continuous variable of fasting glucose. Two‐hour post‐load glucose on the OGTT was used as a secondary variable. A diagnosis of GDM and its management was included as an additional secondary exposure. In BiB, GDM was diagnosed according to modified World Health Organisation (WHO) criteria (either fasting glucose ≥6.1 mmol/L or 2‐h post‐load glucose ≥7.8 mmol/L). A three‐category variable was used of: (1) no GDM, (2) GDM treated without insulin and (3) insulin‐treated GDM.

Outcome: Failure to meet a good level of development on the early years foundation stage profile

There is linkage within the BiB cohort to the child's education records, providing the results of national examinations. The earliest national collection of data on educational progress from school‐aged children in England is the Early Years Foundation Stage Profile (EYFSP), completed at the end of reception year (the first year of primary school) when children are aged 5 years. Teachers are asked to rate children across a number of different aspects of learning, based on their knowledge and accumulating observations of the child throughout the school year.

The most widely used indicator of child development at this age is a ‘good level of development’ (GLD) on the EYFSP, which is being rated by the teacher as ‘expected’ or ‘exceeding’ expectations across five of the areas covered by the EYFSP: (1) communication and language, (2) literacy, (3) mathematics, (4) physical development, (5) personal, social and emotional development. 9 This binary variable of failure to attain GLD (yes or no) was used as the primary outcome.

Confounders

There were a number of variables identified as potential confounders due to their association with both GDM and child educational attainment but not on the causal pathway from exposure to outcome. 10 These were maternal age at the time of the OGTT, tobacco smoking during pregnancy, singleton versus multiple pregnancy and other obstetric complications (as a binary variable of pre‐eclampsia and/or gestational hypertension). Maternal pre‐pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was also included, which was a continuous variable of BMI at pregnancy booking. Moreover, rather than using an index of deprivation to assign socioeconomic status, a five‐category variable of maternal education was used as a proxy because education is a more stable indicator of socioeconomic position than income, employment or area‐based deprivation and because the high levels of deprivation in BiB result in a highly skewed index of multiple deprivation distribution with limited discriminatory power.

There is also some evidence that the common mental disorders of depression and anxiety (CMD) may be associated with GDM 11 and child neuro‐behavioural outcomes so CMD occurring prior to pregnancy (‘preconception’ CMD) was also included as a potential confounder. Consent was obtained for linkage to primary care (GP) records, which are held on SystmOne: a clinical system used by all practices in Bradford. These records provided information on medication prescriptions and diagnoses from that individual's birth to the date of conception of that individual's first ever pregnancy; these methods have been published elsewhere. 12

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using Stata version 18. Multivariable robust Poisson regression was employed to examine the association between the measures of maternal glycaemia and failure to attain GLD, within a generalised estimating equation framework using the robust sandwich estimator for standard errors to account for clustering from those individuals with more than one child in the sample (either due to twin or triplet pregnancies or multiple pregnancies occurring during the data collection period). Two analyses were performed: unadjusted and adjusted for the confounders described above to produce risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Multiple imputation by chained equations was implemented to handle missing data. 13 46 imputations were used to reflect the proportion of participants with any missing data across exposure, outcome and confounder variables. 14 All analysis variables were included in the imputation model and an additional variable of being in receipt of child benefit payments or not was used as an auxiliary variable, as it was associated with being an incomplete case. Estimates were obtained by pooling results using Rubin's rules. 15 Complete case analyses were also conducted.

Sensitivity analyses

The results of the analyses for each of the five domains of the EYFSP are provided in Tables S3–S7 (supplementary material). For those with EYFSP results, 25% of children failed to meet GLD in communication and language, 37.4% in literacy, 32.9% in mathematics, 18.6% in physical development and 22.4% in personal, social and emotional development. While the results of these analyses of the sub‐domains of the EYFSP were similar to that of the global measure used for the primary analyses, there was somewhat more evidence of an association between fasting glucose and communication and language (unadjusted RR 1.06; 95% CI 1.00, 1.12; p = 0.038; adjusted RR 1.06; 95% CI 0.99, 1.12; p = 0.078) and physical development (unadjusted RR 1.08; 95% CI 1.00, 1.16; p = 0.036; adjusted RR 1.06; 95% CI 0.98, 1.14; p = 0.160) than literacy (unadjusted RR 1.04; 95% CI 1.00, 1.09; p = 0.062; adjusted RR 1.03; 95% CI 0.99, 1.08; p = 0.146), mathematics (unadjusted RR 1.04; 95% CI 0.99, 1.09; p = 0.119; adjusted RR 1.04; 95% CI 0.99, 1.09; p = 0.135) or personal, social and emotional development (unadjusted RR 1.05; 95% CI 0.99, 1.12; p = 0.117; adjusted RR 1.05; 95% CI 0.98, 1.12; p = 0.179).

DISCUSSION

Summary of findings in context

In this study, higher maternal fasting glucose was associated with an increased risk of offspring failure to achieve a good level of development (GLD) at age five (adjusted RR 1.04; 95% CI 1.00, 1.08; p = 0.034). To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate such a linear relationship between maternal glucose and child educational attainment at age five. Reduced educational attainment at age 16 has been observed in another UK birth cohort (the Avon Longitudinal Study of Parents and Children (ALSPAC) cohort) for those children exposed to pre‐gestational diabetes (but not to GDM or maternal glycosuria); maternal glycosuria was associated with lower IQ at age eight. 16 The ethnic composition of the ALSPAC cohort, which is predominantly White British, differs from that of BiB, which is bi‐ethnic (White British and Pakistani). In our subgroup analysis, the association between fasting glucose and poorer educational outcomes appeared stronger in children of Pakistani than White British ethnicity. We have shown, in previous analyses of BiB, that the educational attainment of children of Pakistani ethnicity may be more vulnerable to adversities such as GDM exposure in utero. 17 However, subgroup analyses stratified by maternal ethnicity and child sex showed less evidence of associations than the primary analysis and the wider confidence intervals in the stratified models suggest reduced precision due to smaller sample sizes. It is also plausible that adjustment for socioeconomic factors, strongly patterned by ethnicity in BiB, contributed to the attenuation. Differences between child sex would be expected given the differential developmental programming often observed between sexes, 18 including findings from a recent systematic review that offspring of female sex were more at risk of neurodevelopmental disorders following exposure to maternal Type 2 diabetes. 19

Furthermore, there was no evidence of an association between the secondary exposures of 2 h post‐load glucose and GDM diagnosis and failure to attain GLD. However, it is important to note that the fasting diagnostic threshold for GDM in the BiB cohort was relatively high and the 2 h threshold relatively low compared to other diagnostic criteria for GDM, using the modified WHO criteria at the time. 20 The (non‐significant) differing directions of effect seen for insulin treated (reduced risk of failure to attain GLD) versus non‐insulin treated GDM (increased risk) may reflect the positive effect of treatment or may merely reflect a population more engaged with and able to access effective GDM treatment; the somewhat greater reduction in risk with insulin treated GDM in the White British subgroup may support this hypothesis.

Potential mechanisms

Hyperglycaemia is associated with increased levels of oxidative stress, which is known to affect the developing brain 21 ; epigenetic changes 22 and neuroinflammation are putative underlying mechanisms. 23 One of the brain areas most impacted appears to be the hippocampus, involved in learning and memory, explaining the increased risk for poorer educational attainment with higher levels of maternal glucose, although it is less clear what may explain the differences, albeit small, between EYFSP subdomains. In BiB, poorer EYFSP attainment is associated with ASD; ASD is also associated with exposure to GDM 24 so this may explain the slightly stronger evidence of an association between fasting glucose and communication and language, which is a core area of impairment in ASD. Hyperglycaemia during pregnancy may also be associated with adverse outcomes in the postnatal period, such as perinatal mental disorders, 25 which may serve to mediate the relationship with subsequent adverse neuro‐behavioural outcomes. The reasons why basal glucose state may be more detrimental to fetal neurodevelopment than post‐prandial spikes are unknown and require exploration in other datasets.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of this study include its large, bi‐ethnic cohort, which provides a novel opportunity to explore ethnic differences and the use of the EYFSP: a widely‐used and validated national outcome measure. 26

However, the study also has important limitations. The cohort has high rates of consanguinity and congenital anomalies, 8 which could independently impact educational attainment. Data on consanguinity are classified as highly sensitive information so were not available for use in this analysis. The findings may also have limited generalisability beyond this specific population, which is characterised by a predominantly bi‐ethnic (Pakistani and White British) composition and high levels of socioeconomic deprivation.

Moreover, loss of outcome data is minimised by using routinely collected data, which minimises some forms of attrition bias, although selection bias remains possible. School absence rates in state‐funded primary schools in Bradford (4.4%) are slightly higher than the national average (4%), suggesting that outcomes for the most vulnerable children may have been disproportionately missed. 27

Implications and conclusions

The observed association between higher levels of maternal fasting glucose and poorer educational attainment in offspring has implications for future research, clinical practice and health policy. Our findings related to educational attainment add to the now well‐documented linear relationship between maternal glucose and adverse pregnancy outcomes. 5 , 28 There has been considerable debate about the diagnostic criteria for GDM and some have even argued that GDM may be more usefully conceptualised as a spectrum of maternal hyperglycaemia than a distinct disease. 29 Intervention studies that focus on optimising maternal glucose should include educational outcome measures, in order to ascertain the extent to which this is associated with improvements in child outcomes. Further examination of the underlying mechanisms, including potential mediators for the relationship between maternal glycaemia and child educational outcomes, could illuminate future targets for intervention. Information about GDM treatment other than insulin was limited in BiB; for example, only 50 individuals are recorded as using metformin. This is an important consideration for future research studies given the evidence that metformin has a neuroprotective effect in the developing infant. 30 Our findings also highlight potential ethnic inequalities in the early origins of child educational attainment, underpinning the importance of disaggregating data when examining developmental pathways 31 in order to fully understand and optimise child development within diverse populations.
