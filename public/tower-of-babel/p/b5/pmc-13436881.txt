Erector spinae plane versus transversus abdominis plane block for hysterectomy: Beyond statistical significance
Authors: Alessandro De Cassai, Giulia Aviani Fulvio, Burhan Dost, Annalisa Boscolo
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Hysterectomy remains one of the most frequently performed gynecological procedures worldwide. It is estimated that up to 30% of women will undergo a hysterectomy by the age of 60 years, with approximately 590,000 procedures performed annually in the United States alone.[1] Despite its prevalence, hysterectomy is not without important consequences, both physical and psychological. Among these, chronic postsurgical pain develops in approximately 10%–50% of patients, with up to 7% experiencing severe and persistent pain, particularly following abdominal approaches.[2] Given this burden, optimizing perioperative analgesia is of paramount importance, and as expected that considerable research efforts continue to focus on improving pain management strategies in this population.

In this issue of the Saudi Journal of Anesthesia, Ahmed et al.[3] present a meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block and the erector spinae plane (ESP) block in patients undergoing hysterectomy. Their findings suggest a favorable profile for the ESP block, particularly in the early postoperative period, with reductions in opioid consumption and postoperative nausea and vomiting.

Both TAP and ESP blocks are widely used regional anesthesia techniques in gynecological surgery. While the ESP block has demonstrated promising results in other contexts, such as cesarean delivery,[4] robust evidence specifically addressing hysterectomy has been limited. The work by Ahmed et al.[3] contributes meaningfully to this gap by synthesizing the currently available data.

However, an important question arises: do these statistically significant differences translate into clinically meaningful benefits?

The authors should be commended for moving beyond conventional reporting of P values and incorporating the proportion of confidence intervals (CI) exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID). This approach represents a more informative and clinically oriented framework, particularly in a research landscape where P values are often overemphasized.

Indeed, the P value is frequently misunderstood. By definition, it represents the probability of observing results as extreme as those obtained assuming the null hypothesis is true. It does not indicate the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor does it convey the magnitude or clinical importance of an effect. Nevertheless, it is commonly misused as a binary marker of “significance,” fostering the misleading notion that P < 0.05 implies importance, whereas P ≥ 0.05 implies irrelevance. This dichotomization obscures the continuum of evidence and neglects key factors such as sample size, variability, and study design. Consequently, trivial effects may appear “significant” in large samples, while clinically meaningful differences may go undetected in underpowered studies. This misconception has become pervasive, extending even to inappropriate hypothesis testing of baseline characteristics in randomized trials.[5]

In contrast, CIs provide essential information about both the magnitude and precision of an effect. When interpreted alongside the MCID, they allow clinicians to determine whether observed differences are likely to be meaningful for patients. In this study, the authors adopted an MCID threshold of 1 point on the visual analog scale,[6] finding that at 2 hours postoperatively, the proportion of the CI exceeding the MCID (Pδ) was 0.45, indicating uncertainty regarding clinical relevance. At later time points (4, 6, 12, and 24 hours), all CIs fell below the MCID threshold, suggesting that although differences were statistically significant, they were unlikely to be clinically meaningful.[7]

It is worth noting, however, that the MCID threshold used was derived from general surgical populations and may not be directly applicable to hysterectomy. Other studies have proposed a higher MCID of 1.5 points.[8] When applying this more conservative threshold, all observed differences fall below the MCID (e.g. −0.95 cm at 2 hours; 95% CI [−1.50, −0.40]), further reinforcing the likelihood that the observed benefits do not translate into meaningful clinical improvement, particularly within the context of contemporary multimodal analgesia protocols.

In conclusion, while the ESP block may offer statistically measurable advantages over the TAP block in the early postoperative period, the clinical significance of these differences appears limited. This study highlights the critical importance of interpreting results beyond P values, emphasizing effect sizes, CIs, and clinically relevant thresholds. Future research should aim to establish procedure-specific MCID values and focus on patient-centered outcomes to better guide clinical decision-making. Ultimately, the goal should not be merely to demonstrate statistical superiority, but to ensure that observed differences translate into tangible benefits for patients.
