Efficacy of pirfenidone in fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Ahmed Uzair, Hamnah Sohail, Muhammad Sajawal, Nauman Ijaz Bhatti, Muhammad Waseem, Ayesha Saleem, Asher Ishaq, Zoha Toor, Osama Ahmad, Bibek Giri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), a progressive and irreversible interstitial lung disease (ILD), carries a significant risk of morbidity and mortality due to the narrow range of therapeutic options available. The role of anti-fibrotic drugs for this disease entity has been called into question, and the results of various studies have been unclear so far. This meta-analysis evaluates the efficacy of the drug pirfenidone, an anti-fibrotic agent in fibrotic HP, to document a clear consensus of the benefits in disease outcomes.Materials and methods:PubMed, Scopus, and MEDLINE were searched for the randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the efficacy of pirfenidone in fibrotic HP against a placebo. Data were extracted from the RCTs that met the inclusion criteria, and pooled analysis was performed to evaluate the significance of % Predicted FVC (forced vital capacity), % Predicted DLCO (diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide), and change in SGRQ (George’s respiratory questionnaire) at the completion of the trial.Results:No significant change was observed in the % predicted FVC [Mean difference (MD): 3.65; 95% Confidence interval (CI): −1.63, 8.94; P: 0.18] and the % predicted DLCO [MD: 7.64; 95% CI: −1.46, 16.74; P: 0.10]. However, there was a significant decrease in the SGRQ score at the end of the intervention [MD: −5; 95% CI: −6.88, −3.12; P < 0.00001].Conclusion:Pirfenidone was not associated with a significant change in the lung function metrics, but it has been shown to improve the quality of life, as evidenced by a significant decrease in the SGRQ score.

Introduction

Hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), also called extrinsic allergic alveolitis, is a complex syndrome of varying intensity, clinical presentation, and natural history rather than a single, uniform disease[1]. It is characterized by an immune-mediated response to inhaled organic and non-organic materials that leads to inflammation and fibrosis of the lung interstitium[2]. There exists a genetic predisposition to HP, as only a minority of the exposed patients develop this disease[3]. Historically, HP has been classified as acute, sub-acute, and chronic based on the duration, frequency, and intensity of exposure as well as the span of the illness. In 2020, HP was categorized into non-fibrotic (purely inflammatory) and fibrotic (mixed inflammatory and fibrotic or purely fibrotic) subtypes on the basis of the identification of fibrosis through imaging or histology[4]. It is a progressive disease leading to irreversible lung changes that may progress to end-stage lung disease[5].

Current management options for HP primarily focus on the identification and removal of the offending agent and the use of corticosteroids to manage acute inflammatory symptoms[6]. The standard treatment recommendations and practices show corticosteroids as the mainstay therapy for symptomatic relief and slowing disease progression. However, there is a dearth of high-quality supporting evidence, and the existing literature does not address the long-term management of chronic or progressive HP[7]. Recent research has unveiled the potential of anti-fibrotic agents, such as pirfenidone, in the resolution of the non-fibrotic and fibrotic components of HP[8,9]. Additionally, lung transplantation has also been considered as a prospective treatment, as it improves survival in select patients with progressive fibrotic HP[10].

Pirfenidone, originally developed for the treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)[11], has shown promising results due to its anti-fibrotic, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. Its efficacy in IPF treatment has been well established, evidenced by the results of multiple large-scale studies. However, its role in the treatment of HP is not yet clear. Considering the pathophysiological overlap between IPF and HP, there is an immense need to assess whether pirfenidone could also offer benefits to HP patients, particularly those with progressive forms of the disease.

Although some individual studies do support its use in HP, the overall evidence is not consistent[12]. So, there is a need for a comprehensive analysis of the available data to determine the role of pirfenidone in the treatment of HP. This meta-analysis aims to pool data from various studies to evaluate the efficacy of pirfenidone in the management of fibrotic HP. By systematically reviewing the existing evidence, this analysis aims to provide clear guidance for clinicians regarding the use of the anti-fibrotic drug, pirfenidone, in the treatment of this chronic and progressive lung illness. Additionally, this study is conducted and reported in accordance with the TITAN (Transparency In The reporting of Artificial INtelligence) guideline checklist[13].

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis have been reported under strict compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA GUIDELINES) and registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251021145).

Search strategy

The electronic search was carried out by two independent investigators from the PubMed, Scopus, and MEDLINE databases from their origin until 31 July 2024. The following search terms were applied to screen the RCTs documenting the use of pirfenidone in fibrotic HP: (pirfenidone) AND (fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis OR fibrotic HP OR chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis OR chronic HP OR hypersensitivity pneumonitis OR HP) AND (randomized controlled trial) OR (RCT).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria of the RCTs in this study were as follows: (1) Comparison of the efficacy of pirfenidone with a placebo. (2) The targeted population should meet the diagnostic criteria for fibrotic HP. (3) The studies must report at least one outcome of interest. (4) A follow up period of at least 6 months. The RCTs that failed to meet the above-mentioned characteristics were excluded from the study.

Data extraction and literature screening

The relevant literature was screened and cross-checked by two investigators to meet the demands of the inclusion criteria. Any disagreement, if present, was effectively managed by mutual consensus between the authors. Afterwards, two authors were to extract the data from the included RCTs, which mainly included the author and its publication year, sample size in the experimental and control group, the intervention done in the experimental and control group, the duration, and the documented outcome of the study.

Evaluation of quality

Quality assessment and the risk of bias of the included RCTs were carried out by the Cochrane Collaboration’s risk of bias tool. This tool screened the RCTs across five different domains and labeled the studies as low risk, high risk, or unclear risk of bias according to their criteria.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis of this study was carried out by Review Manager (Version 5.3, Copenhagen: The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2014). Analysis of the continuous outcomes was done by the inverse variance method using the random effects model. Mean change and the standard deviation (SD) of change were extracted from the included studies and represented as forest plots to evaluate the significance of intervention for each outcome. The cause of heterogeneity was ruled out by conducting subgroup and sensitivity analysis. Publication bias and thus the funnel plots were not presented due to the limited number of included studies. In this study, the difference was statistically significant with P < 0.05.

Outcomes of interest

The following outcomes were analyzed to evaluate the statistical significance of the mean change at the end of the intervention.

% Predicted FVC

The primary outcome of interest in all three studies was the mean change in % predicted forced vital capacity (FVC) on the completion of the trial. Pooled analysis of the mean change in % predicted FVC with the SD of change revealed a non-significant increase in the measured outcome [mean difference (MD): 3.65; 95% confidence interval (CI): −1.63, 8.94; P: 0.18]. The heterogeneity (I2) for this outcome was 25% (Fig. 4). Figure 4.Mean change in FVC (% Predicted).

% Predicted DLCO

The pooled analysis of the mean change in the % predicted diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide (DLCO) revealed a non-significant increase in the measured outcome [MD: 7.64; 95% CI: −1.46, 16.74; P: 0.10]. The I2 was 36% for the included studies that have documented this outcome (Fig. 5). Figure 5.Mean change in DLCO (% Predicted).

SGRQ score

Two of the studies documented the mean change in the George’s respiratory questionnaire (SGRQ) score and the accompanying SD of change. The pooled analysis revealed a significant decrease in the SGRQ score by five points [MD: −5; 95% CI: −6.88, −3.12; P < 0.00001]. The I2 for the included studies was 0% (Fig. 6). Discussion

Fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP) is a progressive interstitial lung disease (ILD), primarily marked by radiological or histopathological fibrosis[4]. Nearly a decade ago, anti-fibrotic agents (pirfenidone and nintedanib) were approved for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF)[13,14]. Recently, anti-fibrotic agents and their efficacy in the treatment of interstitial pneumonitis and non-IPF ILD have been an area of interest[15]. However, the individual outcomes of studies done on the role of anti-fibrotic agents in the treatment of fibrotic HP have been vague and uncertain so far. Hence, there was a need for a comprehensive meta-analysis to analyze their efficacy. In our meta-analysis, we aimed to study the efficacy of pirfenidone, an anti-fibrotic agent, in patients with fibrotic HP.

Although the described meta-analysis focused primarily on clinical outcomes, it is useful to keep in mind possible biological mechanisms by means pirfenidone might be able to act as a fibrotic HP therapy. Pirfenidone has been found to disrupt transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) mediated pathways, as well as to decrease fibroblasts proliferation, and to exert an anti-profibrotic and a proinflammatory cytokine inhibition such as, TNF-α and IL-1β. It is relevant to this research because it fibrotic HP is described as a chronic alveolar inflammation with abnormal remodeling of lung parenchyma occurring after repetitive antigen exposure. While the mechanistic studies on pirfenidone in HP are limited, demonstrated benefit of anti-fibrotic, anti-proinflammatory properties of pirfenidone in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and preclinical models raises the possibility that pirfenidone may be of benefit to fibrotic HP.

FVC and DLCO have been utilized in the past to assess the decrease in lung function and to study the effects of treatments in patients with fibrotic HP and ILD[16,17]. Decline in FVC has been utilized to predict survival and mortality in chronic HP patients[18]. In addition to FVC, DLCO has also been successfully used to assess treatment effects in HP[19]. SGRQ score has its application in the assessment of quality of life in patients suffering from ILD[20]. Hence, in this meta-analysis, we studied 3 main outcomes of interest following intervention with pirfenidone in patients with fibrotic HP, namely, % predicted FVC, % predicted DLCO, and SGRQ score. The outcomes were followed for a period of 6 months to 1 year in the included studies. From baseline to the completion of the included trials, a non-significant increase in % predicted FVC and % predicted DLCO was observed. A significant decrease in SGRQ score at the end of the trials was observed through pooled analysis. In short, our meta-analysis reveals that pirfenidone can improve the quality of life of patients with fibrotic HP but is not associated with improved lung function.

The efficacy of pirfenidone in the treatment of IPF has been studied extensively as compared to fibrotic HP. It was observed that the mean FVC and DLCO of patients with IPF either stabilized or improved upon entry into an open-label study[21]. In the CAPACITY study, pirfenidone significantly reduced the decline in FVC (P = 0.001)[11]. The results of the ASCEND study were also promising[13]. Since chronic HP and IPF share the same pathobiology, it is only to be expected that pirfenidone should also improve lung function metrics in HP[22]. Three studies were included in this meta-analysis that fulfilled the inclusion criteria[9,23,24]. Two of them showed no significant improvement in FVC and no significant difference in FVC between the pirfenidone and placebo groups[9,24]. However, one showed a significant increase in FVC with the use of pirfenidone in addition to conventional treatment as compared to the patients who were maintained only on conventional treatment[23]. All of this accounts for our result of a non-significant increase in % predicted FVC. A meta-analysis studied the efficacy of anti-fibrotic agents in rheumatoid arthritis associated-ILD and reported a significant reduction in FVC decline with the use of pirfenidone as compared to the placebo[25]. In a retrospective study done on 23 patients with chronic HP, the change in VC was significantly improved after 6 months of treatment with pirfenidone (P = 0.047)[8]. Additionally, another meta-analysis revealed that for all patients with PPF, the decline in % predicted FVC was significantly smaller for the pirfenidone group[26]. Presently, there is a need to study % predicted FVC exclusively in patients with fibrotic HP.

Two studies assessed % predicted DLCO and reported no significant improvement in it with the use of pirfenidone[9,24]. Similarly, a meta-analysis comparing pirfenidone with placebo in progressive pulmonary fibrosis revealed that there was no significant difference in the % predicted DLCO. However, in the same study, pirfenidone led to a significant improvement (MD: 0.40, 95% CI: 0.10–0.70) in DLCO[26]. In one of the included studies, although not significant, a small improvement in % predicted DLCO was seen in the group that was using pirfenidone with azathioprine and prednisone[9]. A past meta-analysis found that in the pirfenidone group, alteration in the % predicted DLCO from baseline to the completion of the trial was −2.32% as compared to −3.95% for nintedanib, vouching for the effectiveness of pirfenidone[27]. Maher et al. studied the efficacy of pirfenidone in unclassifiable progressive fibrosing ILD, and the results indicated that the pirfenidone group experienced a lesser decline in DLCO than the placebo group at week 24[28]. In a 2-year follow-up study, only 14.3% of IPF patients had greater than 15% decline in DLCO while on pirfenidone[29]. Yet another study involving 3-month treatment with pirfenidone in post-COVID pulmonary fibrosis patients showed that pulmonary function tests significantly improved by the end of the trial[30].

Both of the studies in this analysis that assessed the quality of life showed significantly lower SGRQ scores upon the completion of respective trials[9,23]. On the other hand, a past study observed that the mean change in SGRQ score from baseline to week 24 was similar in both the pirfenidone and placebo groups[28]. However, in that study, the patients were suffering from unclassifiable progressive fibrosing ILD. Our analysis showed a significant decrease in SGRQ score in favor of pirfenidone (MD-5; P < 0.00001), corroborating higher quality of life in pirfenidone users. Such observed difference is higher than the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the SGRQ in interstitial lung diseases, which is expected between 4 and 8 points on average, suggesting that this change may be clinically relevant to patients. SGRQ, but captures primarily respiratory symptom burden and functional limitation so it is possible that this “improvement” is mostly a measure of symptomatic relief rather than actual disease modification. Also, other commonly used tests to assess the quality of life, including the SF-36 or the King’s Brief Interstitial Lung Disease Questionnaire, were not homogenously reported in the selected studies. Future studies should examine additional patient-reported outcomes as part of a more complete assessment of pirfenidone’s effect on various aspects related to physical, emotional, and social quality of life.

Low to moderate statistical heterogeneity among the studies analyzed was observed, but significant clinical heterogeneity was found; the later could have impacted the main results of the present meta-analysis. The studies were heterogeneous in terms of design, protocol, and sample characteristics. While Toledo et al. (2020) studied a triple therapy with pirfenidone, prednisone, and azathioprine, Shebl et al. (2021) used pirfenidone as add-on therapy to standard treatment, without detailing what this treatment included among other immunosuppressors. Perez et al., 2023, instead tested pirfenidone alone versus placebo, adding yet more variability for a treatment comparison.

The cohort studies had variations in the measures of disease severity at baseline, as well as the outcomes measured. Shebl 2021 reported a high-value FVC and 6MWD improvement with pirfenidone, apparently indicating a less fibrotic, more reversible disease population. Inversely, Perez, 2023 did not observe significant differences in FVC or DLCO that could perhaps suggest a greater fibrotic stage, making anti-fibrotic effects more difficult to discern. In addition, the variable length of the follow-up period, between 6 months in Shebl to 1 year in Perez and Toledo, would influence the time-dependent efficacy of pirfenidone.

The use of differing measures makes results difficult to compare and may mask treatment effects that are relevant in a specific sub-population. The relatively low number of trials, but, did not allow for the performance of formal subgroups analyses according to baseline characteristics, stage of disease, or concomitant therapies. Future research ought to work toward more uniform inclusion criteria, stratified randomization and standardization of outcome measures, in order reduce heterogeneity and make more refined pooled analysis possible.

In light of the limited number of studies that are part of the current meta-analyses, it was not possible to conduct a formal test for publication bias (e.g., funnel plots, Egger’s regression) given that tests of publication bias have low power when less than 10 studies exist. On the other hand, we cannot completely discard the existence of publication bias. The overestimation of pirfenidone comes from the fact that negative or null studies are less likely to be published. Our results should be taken then as preliminary, and future studies should focus on ways to reduce selective publishing of results, such as by registering trials prospectively and publish all results, regardless of whether they do or do not turn out significant.

All the included studies offer a very favorable side effect profile. Most common side effects noted in the pirfenidone group were nausea, diarrhea, dyspepsia and mild elevation of the hepatic enzymes. No major side effect was reported that have warranted withdrawal from the study. There was no mortality in the pirfenidone group. This emphasizes a sense of safety for the long-term use of pirfenidone.

Limitations

The heterogeneity was low to moderate for all the included outcomes. Though our meta-analysis provides a cohesive conclusion regarding the studies done so far on the efficacy of pirfenidone in patients with fibrotic HP, this study had some limitations. All of the studies included in this meta-analysis had a limited number of participants and were single-center studies. This limits the generalizability of our findings. Multicentered studies with a high statistical power are thus required to make confident remarks on the internal validity as well as external validity of the results presented. There were variations in the treatment arm, two of the studies had compared pirfenidone with combination of other drugs, while only one study had a placebo group. This clinical and methodological heterogeneity might have restricted the generalizability of our results. All the studies had a follow up period ranging from 6 months up to 1 year, which is inadequate to assess the long-term effects of pirfenidone on the disease progression. Studies with extended period of follow up are required to better assess the efficacy and safety profile of pirfenidone in broader time frames. In addition, there is a lack of trials and high-quality studies on the efficacy of pirfenidone in fibrotic HP.

Conclusion

Following the results of this meta-analysis, pirfenidone may be prescribed as an addition to primary therapy to increase the quality of life of patients suffering from fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis. However, more research with larger sample sizes is needed to arrive at a more refined conclusion regarding the efficacy of pirfenidone in HP. This is an often-overlooked condition and is harder to manage than other subtypes. Hence, in this context, the efficacy and potency of the anti-fibrotic drug, pirfenidone (which also has some anti-inflammatory effects) must be investigated thoroughly.
