Persistent inspiratory muscle weakness among extubated patients after prolonged intubation is frequent and can be predicted early by maximal inspiratory pressure measured 12 days after its initial diagnosis: a prospective observational study
Authors: Valentin Oestreicher, Marius Hennemann, Roger Hilfiker, Thibault Faivre, Greg Zoni, Marianne Demont, Mélanie Teixeira, Roberto Ucchino, Didier Tassaux, Lise Piquilloud, Olivier Contal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged mechanical ventilation (MV) frequently results in inspiratory and peripheral muscle weakness, impairing recovery. These conditions can be identified at the bedside using respectively Maximal Inspiratory Pressure (MIP) measurement and Medical Research Council (MRC) score. This study investigated the evolution over the acute-care hospital stay of MIP and MRC score in patients with documented post-extubation inspiratory muscle weakness (IMW), defined as MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O, and looked for the factors associated with persistent IMW at the end of the acute care hospital stay.MethodThis exploratory prospective observational study was conducted across five Swiss hospitals. Patients in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) who were extubated after ≥ 7 days of MV, with IMW diagnosed within 48 h post planned extubation, were included. Patients’ characteristics and ICU-related factors were recorded throughout the acute care stay as were MIP and MRC score. ICU-acquired weakness (ICU-AW) recorded in the medical file, ICU readmission, reintubation, and hospital mortality were also documented. Descriptive statistics and linear interpolation for missing MIP data were applied, and associations with persistent IMW (MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O) at study completion were explored using univariable logistic regression. The optimal timepoint for predicting persistent IMW using MIP was identified using a random forest model.ResultsSixty-nine patients (48 men, 21 women) completed the study. At study completion, persistent IMW was observed in 30 patients (43%), with a median MIP of 22 [13–24] cmH2O. MIP was of 44 [36–64] in patients without persistent IMW. Persistent IMW was positively associated in univariable logistic regression with female sex, duration of catecholamine use until inclusion, MIP at day 12, MRC score at day 12 and changes in MIP from inclusion to day 12. The presence of respiratory comorbidities was negatively associated with persistent IMW.ConclusionPersistent IMW following prolonged MV is frequent throughout the acute care stay and until acute care hospital discharge. The MIP measured at day 12 after inclusion, as well as its change from inclusion to day 12, were strongly associated with persistent IMW.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05718-8.Keywords: Intensive care, Prolonged mechanical ventilation, Maximal inspiratory pressure, Inspiratory muscle weakness, Peripheral muscle weakness, Intensive care unit acquired weakness

Background

Inspiratory Muscle Weakness (IMW) is frequent in patients undergoing Mechanical ventilation (MV) in Intensive Care Units (ICUs) [1–4]. Risk factors for IMW include MV itself, deep sedation and muscle paralysis, among other contributing factors [1, 5–9]. At the bedside IMW can simply and non-invasively be evaluated with Maximal Inspiratory Pressure (MIP) measurement [3, 5, 10–12]. In ICU settings, a threshold of MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O is often used to define IMW, independently from individual patient characteristics [5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13]. Similarly to ICU acquired weakness (ICU-AW), IMW is associated with longer duration of MV, a higher risk of re-intubation, difficult weaning, impaired physical function after ICU discharge, reduced quality of life up to five years after ICU discharge and reduced survival [3, 5–7, 12–20]. In this context, early detection of patients at risk of persistent IMW after prolonged MV may provide guidance for therapies [5, 6]. However, the evolution of IMW during the acute care hospital stay is largely underexplored [4, 7, 21]. It was hypothesized that some patients extubated after prolonged MV will recover inspiratory muscle strength during their hospital stay in acute care, while others will not. Furthermore, it was hypothesized that the trajectory of IMW recovery was associated with clinical parameters identifiable early during acute care hospitalization.

The primary objective of this study was to describe the natural course of inspiratory muscle strength throughout acute care in adult patients invasively ventilated during at least 7 days and with documented IMW within the 48 h after planned extubation. The secondary objective was to explore associations between persistent IMW at study completion defined as MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O and clinical parameters available earlier during hospitalization. Additionally, investigations were undertaken to explore whether these clinical parameters were associated with the absolute value of MIP at study completion.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, multicentre, exploratory observational study was conducted across two tertiary academic hospitals in Lausanne (CHUV) and Geneva (HUG) and three non-academic hospitals (Morges, Neuchâtel and Rennaz) in Switzerland. Ethical approval was obtained, and written informed consent was provided by all participants or their representatives. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Clinical Practice Guidelines (ICH-GCP), and national regulations.

Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, hospitalization in an adult ICU, extubation after ≥ 7 days of MV (either consecutive days or non-consecutive days in case of extubation failure), and documented IMW, defined as a MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O within 48 h post-planned extubation, independently from individual predicted normal MIP value. Non-inclusion criteria were absence of consent, recent solid organ transplantation, primary neurological disorders, major burns, palliative care or planned therapeutic withdrawal, laryngeal oedema, continuous dependence on non-invasive ventilation or significant hemodynamic instability. In addition, for organisational reasons, recruitment was interrupted during the first and second waves of the COVID-19 pandemia.

Due to the exploratory nature of the study and the absence of available quantitative data, no formal sample size calculation was performed. A convenience sample of 90 patients was targeted.

Measurements procedures and data collection

Baseline and ICU-related clinical data leading to inclusion were recorded. According to the usual practice of the participating centres, assessment of MIP in accordance with the ERS statement [10] and MRC score [22, 23] were routinely performed by trained physiotherapists within 48 h post-planned extubation in patients who had undergone ≥ 7 days of MV.

In more detail, measurement of MIP was performed via handheld electronic manometers: the POWERbreathe KH2 (POWERbreathe, United Kingdom) in Lausanne and Geneva, and the micro-RPM® (MD Spiro, United States) in Morges, Neuchâtel, and Rennaz. Measurements were performed at residual volume. Patients were instructed to maintain a maximal inspiratory effort for at least two seconds, with pressure sustained over one second. Initially, two different interfaces were used in each patient (see below), a naso-oral anaesthesia mask and a mouthpiece with a nose clip in place. The highest MIP value obtained with each interface was recorded. If patients were unable to generate a numeric value (reported as “measurement error” on the device), due to insufficient effort or poor coordination during the measurement, the result was documented as IMW.

For the 24 first included patients, five measurements using the mouthpiece and five measurements using the naso-oral mask were made. Assessment of MIP and MRC score were conducted daily during the ICU stay, every three days following ICU discharge, and weekly beyond day 28 after inclusion. An analysis of the data of the first 21 patients for whom analysis was possible (i.e. after the exclusion of the data from three patients who did not sign post-hoc consent) showed a strong correlation between the MIP measurements performed with the two different interfaces (see additional file 1). Based on this correlation, the protocol was amended starting with patient 25: assessments were then performed using the preferred interface, with three to five MIP measurements at each session. From patient 25, MIP and MRC evaluations were conducted on days 0 (inclusion), 4, and 7, and subsequently every seven days. For all patients MIP and MRC score were also recorded at ICU discharge and at study completion. Study completion corresponded to the end of the follow-up, that was defined as: discharge from the acute care hospitalization, transfer to a non-participating acute care facility, death, or day 90 post-inclusion, whichever occurred first.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was persistent IMW, defined as MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O at study completion. Secondary outcomes included absolute value of MIP and MRC score at study completion, changes in MIP and MRC score from inclusion to study completion, clinical diagnosis of ICU-AW, reintubation, ICU and hospital length of stay, ICU readmission, and hospital mortality.

Statistical analysis

Results are presented as median with first and third quartiles for continuous variables, and as numbers and percentages for categorical variables. Descriptive statistics are reported for the population and separately for the subgroups with and without persistent IMW, namely with and without MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O at study completion. Between-groups comparisons were conducted using chi-squared for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney tests for continuous variables. To evaluate associations between clinical factors and persistent IMW at study completion, univariable logistic regression was used, reporting Odds-Ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). As a sensitivity analysis, univariable linear regression was used to identify factors associated with the absolute MIP value measured at study completion. Those results are presented as raw coefficients and standardized coefficients (standardized by 2 standard deviations of the given variable) with 95% CI and p-values.

Inference of missing data and identification of the best timepoint to predict persistent IMW

As daily MIP measurements were not performed in all patients, missing MIP data for days 7 to 12 post-inclusion were imputed using linear interpolation with the Stata ipolate command. The optimal early timepoint for predicting persistent IMW at study completion was then identified using a random forest model in R (see additional file 2) [24]. The same strategy was used to interpolate MRC scores values between days 7 and 12.

Results

Study population

From 28th February 2021 to 31 st January 2023, a total of 136 patients invasively ventilated for a minimum of 7 days were routinely assessed for IMW within 48 h post-extubation. Of these, 22 had MIP > 30 cmH2O, and 41 either declined participation or were not recruited due to lack of resources.

To note, no patients were excluded due to laryngeal oedema, continuous dependence on non-invasive ventilation or significant hemodynamic instability. Overall, 73 patients were thus included across participating centres. Most of them (N = 63) were recruited from the two tertiary academic centres. As four patients did not sign post-hoc consent, 69 patients completed the study. The study flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Most patients (90%) were included after one continuous episode of at least 7 days of MV. The remaining 10% experienced extubation failure but had at least 7 days of MV in total. No adverse effects were reported for either peripheral or inspiratory muscle testing. Patients underwent a median of 6 [4–9] MIP measurements during the study.

Factors associated with persistent IMW at study completion

Female sex, duration of catecholamine use prior to inclusion, MIP at day 12, MRC score at day 12, changes in MIP from inclusion to day 12, and ICU-acquired weakness diagnostic documented in the medical file showed a significant positive association with persistent with persistent IMW. The presence of a respiratory comorbidity (e.g. known chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, interstitial lung disease or asthma) was negatively associated with persistent IMW.

To further explore the robustness of the findings, a post-hoc sensitivity analysis using univariable linear regression was conducted to assess the relationship between the MIP value at study completion and the variables used for the logistic regression (see Additional file 3).

Discussion

The natural progression of inspiratory muscle strength was investigated among patients presenting with IMW defined as MIP ≤ 30 cmH2O after prolonged MV (≥ 7 days). At study completion, 43% of patients had persistent IMW, and 57% had an MRC score < 48/60, corresponding to the threshold of ICU-AW. In univariable logistic regression, persistent IMW at study completion was positively associated with female sex, duration of catecholamine use until inclusion, MIP and MRC scores at day 12, and changes in MIP from inclusion to day 12. Among these, MIP at day 12 showed the highest predictive value, with its change from inclusion also significantly associated with the outcome.

To the authors’ knowledge, this study is the first to longitudinally document the spontaneous evolution of inspiratory muscle strength during acute care in patients invasively ventilated for ≥ 7 days. It shows that persistent IMW is frequent and reveals its heterogeneous recovery trajectories, distinguishing patients with spontaneous recovery from those with persistent IMW.

In prior research the occurrence of IMW is associated with several risk factors [2, 5, 12, 14, 15, 18, 21, 25]. Among these, this study found only catecholamine use to be associated with persistent IMW. No significant association was found between total MV duration and persistent IMW, suggesting that recovery depends on other factors than MV duration when it exceeds 7 days [16]. Interestingly, patients with documented respiratory comorbidity were more likely to recover inspiratory muscle strength, possibly due to tailored management. This however must be confirmed by further dedicated studies.

This study highlights the difficulty of predicting persistent IMW based on patient characteristics, ICU admission reasons, or treatments received, and underscores the importance of repeated MIP assessments to better identify those at risk. According to the present data, measurement of MIP at day 12 appears to be a relevant clinical opportunity to identify the patients who are at high risk of having persistent IMW. Patients identified at this stage may benefit from targeted interventions, such as Inspiratory Muscle Training (IMT), which is both feasible and safe in the ICU setting [11, 26–30]. Early targeted and personalized rehabilitation strategies may be especially valuable as IMW, similarly to ICU-AW, is related to poor outcomes beyond acute care, including the Post-Intensive Care Syndrome, affecting up to 56% of ICU survivors one year after discharge and being associated with reduced quality of life [31]. Both respiratory and peripheral weakness worsen prognosis, increasing mortality and reducing independence. In the present, consistent with previous findings [5], IMW was significantly associated with hospital mortality. Early identification and tailored interventions may thus improve both short- and long-term outcomes, including survival [25, 31–34].

This study has several strengths, namely its prospective design and the use of simple bedside evaluations of muscle strength. Unlike previous studies that assessed IMW exclusively at the time of weaning or sedation withdrawal [1, 5, 15, 17, 18], the present study documents muscle recovery with repeated measurements throughout acute care. This longitudinal data collection highlights the existence of patients with persistent IMW and provides a timeframe to prospectively identify those at-risk.

Several limitations require consideration. First, poor patient cooperation and coordination may affect measurement reliability—particularly in case of delirium or reduced consciousness shortly after extubation. In this cohort, MIP was not feasible in 9% of evaluations, mostly (75%) within the first five days after inclusion, underscoring the need for repeated assessments. Second, the study included only patients with a MIP ≤ 30 cmH₂O within 48 h post-extubation, excluding those with higher initial values and thus evaluated only a subpopulation of ICU patients with documented inspiratory muscle weakness. Third, follow-up was limited to the acute care hospital stay in the participating hospitals, and early discharges may have led to overestimating the prevalence of persistent IMW at study completion. Fourth, variability in ICU discharge criteria across centres may have influenced assessment timings. Fifth, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power and generalizability. Given the sample size, multivariable regression and statistical analysis that specifically account for time were deemed inappropriate. Finally, pre-admission nutritional status—a known determinant of muscle recovery—was not analysed and may have influenced the results.

Conclusion

This study highlights the high prevalence of persistent IMW throughout the acute care stay among patients with critical illness who experienced prolonged intubation. Factors positively associated in univariable logistic regression with persistent inspiratory muscle weakness were female sex, duration of catecholamine use until inclusion, MIP at day 12, MRC score at day 12 and changes in MIP from inclusion to day 12. This suggests that early identification of patients at risk of persistent muscle weakness is feasible using MIP measurement and could be used to tailor and individualize rehabilitation strategies for these patients who experience poor long-term outcomes. Future research is essential to confirm the results of the present study and to assess the potential impact on long-term outcomes of a systematic early detection of patients at risk of persistent IMW coupled with individualized rehabilitation programs.
