Patterns of nutrients intake in relation to depression, anxiety, and psychological distress among Iranian university employees: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sanaz Benisi-Kohansal, Shervin Kazeminejad, Keyhan Lotfi, Mohammad Reza Monazzam, Hossein Imani, Ahmad Esmaillzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are few studies examining the relationship between nutrient intake patterns and mental disorders.ObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the association between major nutrient patterns (NPs) and depression, anxiety, and distress among Iranian adults.MethodsA total of 2921 adults from the Tehran University of Medical Sciences Employee’s Cohort study were included in this cross-sectional study. A validated dish-based food frequency questionnaire was applied to assess dietary intakes. Mental health was examined by the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale. The association between NPs and anxiety, depression, and distress were established through logistic regression.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 41.4 (y), and 1800 (61.6%) were females. Two NPs were derived by principal components analysis. NP1 had high fatty acids, zinc, sodium, protein, phosphorus, riboflavin, thiamine, biotin, niacin, cholesterol, copper, iron, folate, magnesium, calcium, and manganese. NP2 had high beta-carotene, vitamin A, maltose, sucrose, fructose, glucose, fiber, B6, potassium, and vitamin C. After considering confounders, no significant association was seen between NPs and depression in both genders. However, an inverse relation was detected between NP1 and distress among women (ORQ4 vs. Q1: 0.60, 95%CI: 0.39, 0.93). Also, NP1 was negatively related to 48% lower odds of anxiety in women (ORQ4 vs. Q1: 0.52, 95%CI: 0.32, 0.85). Such associations were not significant for NP2.ConclusionsWe observed that NP high in fats, protein, and minerals are inversely related to anxiety and distress in women. However, this pattern was not related to depression in men and women. Further prospective studies are required to replicate the findings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01135-3.Keywords: Factor analysis, Nutrients, Mental health, Mental disorders, Cross-sectional studies

Introduction

Mental health disorders have become one of the most significant health-related challenges worldwide [1]. They are now listed among the top ten primary causes of disease burden [2]. Depression, anxiety, and psychological distress were considered as common psychological disorders which their prevalence is drastically increasing [3]. Global prevalence of depression and anxiety were estimated 10.8 and 21.7%, respectively [4]. In Iran, 14–43% of adults suffer from depression, while 15.6% experience anxiety [5, 6]. Individuals with mental disorders face a high risk of developing comorbidities, including cancers, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), which can lead to reduced quality of life and increased mortality [7]. Therefore, understanding the potential contributing factors to psychological disease is important.

Several biological and environmental factors are related to psychological disorders [8]. For instance, dietary habits and dietary intakes have been suggested to have a significant role in these disorders [9, 10]. Several studies have evaluated the association between some food groups and nutrients with mental disorders [11–13]. Moreover, the relation between different dietary patterns including the Mediterranean, MIND and plant-based dietary patterns and psychological disorders have been studied [14–16]. Despite the potential ability of dietary patterns in predicting chronic diseases, comprehending the associated mechanisms are difficult [17]. In contrast, patterns of nutrient intake could present more accurate and reliable data on the underlying pathways for diet-disease relations [18]. In addition, dietary patterns could be affected by a wide range of cultural and geographical factors. However, nutrient patterns (NPs) are similar among different populations, and therefore, NPs would be more comparable [19].

Previous investigations have assessed NPs in relation to various chronic diseases [20–22]; however, studies focusing on the association between NPs and mental disorders in adults are limited [23, 24]. For instance, a study by Shakya et al. examined the relationship between three NPs and depression among Australian adults, finding a protective association between a plant-based nutrient pattern and depressive syndromes [24]. However, no substantial association was observed between “animal-sourced” and “mixed-sourced” NPs with depression [24]. Given the growing prevalence of common mental disorders such as depression, anxiety and distress as well as the limited studies investigating the possible role of NPs in their development, we aimed to examine the association between major intake patterns of nutrients with depression, anxiety and psychological distress among a sample of Iranian adults.

Materials and methods

Participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted using a subsample from the Tehran University of Medical Sciences (TUMS) Employee’s Cohort study (TEC), which took place between 2018 and 2019. Detailed study methodology has been published earlier [25]. For the current study, we first included a total of 4,625 participants from the TEC study. Participants were TUMS employees (both men and women) who were at least 18 years old and voluntarily agreed to take part in the study. Participants with incomplete data were excluded from the analysis. Additionally, individuals who met any of the following criteria were also excluded: (1) those on a special diet; (2) those who were pregnant or lactating; (3) those who reported implausible energy intakes (less than 800 or greater than 4,200 kcal/day); and (4) those with a history of kidney diseases, cancers, or hypo-/hyperthyroidism. Individuals with the mentioned conditions were excluded from the analyses as their routine dietary intakes and nutrients metabolism might be altered as a result of their condition, and would substantially bias nutrient pattern analysis. We decided to remain participants who used antipsychotic medications as their usage is relatively common in the general population, and excluding these individuals would risk introducing selection bias and reducing generalizability of the findings. Instead, we considered antipsychotic medications usage in the multivariable analyses to minimize its confounding effect while remaining the representativeness of our findings. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran (IR.TUMS.MEDICINE.REC.1400.217).

Dietary intakes assessment

A 142-item dish-based food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) was applied to gather data on individuals’ dietary intakes in the preceding year [26]. A study on 230 adults examined the validity of this FFQ using 24-h recalls and biomarkers as gold standard and showed that the FFQ is valid and reliable [27]. This questionnaire examines dietary intakes through 6 major food groups: (1) breads (different types of bread; 9 items), (2) dairy products (dairies, cream and butter; 9 items), (3) mixed dishes (cooked or canned; 58 items), (4) nuts, seeds and dried fruits (9 items), (5) vegetables and fruits (also fruit and vegetable juices; 25 items), (6) miscellaneous foods and beverages (such as ice cream, sweets, carbonated beverages and synthetic juices; 32 items). Frequency responses were based on a 9-item option varying from “never or less than once a month” to “more than 6 times per day”. The consumption of each food item was questioned according to their common portion sizes. Gram/day values for all food items were computed by the guidelines of household measurements [28]. The US Department of Agriculture’s (USDA) databank was applied to export daily intakes of energy, micro- and macronutrients for participants [29].

Assessment of psychological health

Mental disorders were assessed by the Iranian validated version of the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale-42 (DASS-42) [30]. This 42-item self-administered questionnaire has three subscales of anxiety, depression, and psychological distress. Each subscale is assessed through fourteen questions. A four-point Likert scale including 0 (never), 1 (little), 2 (sometimes) and 3 (always) was considered to answer each question. Thus, the overall score for each mental disorder ranged from 0 to 42. Higher scores correspond to higher levels of anxiety, depression, or psychological distress. In the current study, we considered depression, anxiety and psychological distress cases as having scores ≥ 14, ≥10 and ≥ 19, respectively.

Assessment of other variables

Weight was measured by a digital scale (to the nearest 0.1 kg) while participants had light clothes and no shoes. Standing height was assessed by a tape measure (to the nearest 0.1 cm) in a normal position without shoes. Waist circumference (WC) was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm (at anatomical landmarks), at the middle of lower rib margin and iliac crest, using a soft tape meter, without any pressure to the body. BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). We examined physical activity using the short form of International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ). The validity of IPAQ has been previously examined [31]. Metabolic equivalent-minute per week (MET-min/week) was calculated from the IPAQ. MET scores for moderate- and vigorous-intensity activities as well as walking (for at least 10 min) were multiplied by the time spent (minutes) in the preceding week. Then, different activity scores were summed up to obtain total MET-min/week. Further information on participants’ age, sex (male/female), education (≤ diploma/> diploma), marital status (single/married/divorced), antipsychotic medications (yes/no), menopausal status (yes/no), and multivitamins and minerals supplements use (yes/no) was taken using self-administered questionnaires.

Statistical analysis

Factor analysis with orthogonal transformation (varimax method) was performed to determine the nutrient patterns according to 39 nutrients and bioactive compounds. Factors were retained for further analysis based on eigenvalues on the scree plot test (Supplemental Figure 1). In this study, we retained factors with eigenvalues greater than 3, as this threshold yielded interpretable dietary patterns. Factors with eigenvalues of 3 or less did not account for a sufficient proportion of the overall variance. This cut-point has been used by some previous studies [32, 33]. Factor score for each pattern was computed by summing up the nutrients’ intake weighted by their factor loadings. For each nutrient pattern, a factor score was assigned to the participants. Participants were categorized according to quartiles of nutrient pattern scores. Differences in continuous and categorical variables across nutrient pattern quartiles were assessed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and the Chi-square test, respectively. Binary logistic regression was used to obtain multivariable-adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for depression, anxiety, and psychological distress across quartiles of nutrient patterns. In the adjusted model, age and energy intake, physical activity, marital status, supplement use, antipsychotic medication, education level, economic status and BMI were considered. Confounders were selected based on a literature review identifying variables associated with both the exposure and the outcome [34–36]. Individuals in the first quartile of the nutrient patterns were considered as the reference group in crude and adjusted models. The trends of odds ratios across quartiles of nutrient patterns were estimated by considering the nutrient pattern quartiles as an ordinal variable. We used the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS Inc., Chicago IL, USA; version 26) for statistical analysis. P-values < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Figure 1 shows the study selection process. Out of 4,625 individuals recruited for the study, 1,084 were excluded due to incomplete data on any questionnaire, 247 had a history of kidney diseases, cancers, or hypo-/hyperthyroidism, and 373 reported energy intakes outside the range of 800-4,200 kcal/day. Consequently, the present analysis included a total of 2,921 participants. The mean age of the individuals was 41.4 ± 8.9 years, with 1800 (61.6%) being women. The overall prevalence of depression, anxiety, and psychological distress was 626 (21.4%), 623 (21.3%), and 853 (29.2%), respectively. Detailed information about total population characteristics is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

Two major nutrient patterns were extracted from this study population, which together explained 58.2% of total nutrient intakes variance (Supplemental Table 1). KMO value was 0.83, which indicated good sampling adequacy. The first nutrient pattern (NP1) is defined by high consumption of individual fatty acids, protein, sodium, zinc, cholesterol, riboflavin, niacin, biotin, pantothenic acid, phosphorus, iron, magnesium, calcium, and molybdenum. The second nutrient pattern (NP2) was high in fructose, glucose, maltose, folate, vitamin E, magnesium, potassium, pyridoxine, total fiber, vitamin C, beta-carotene, and vitamin A.

The general characteristics (age, physical activity, BMI, education level, marital status, supplement use, economic status, antipsychotic medication usage, menopausal status, and history of diabetes) for both men and women did not significantly differ across NP1 quartiles. Also, no significant difference in general characteristics of men was found, comparing NP2 quartiles. However, females who were in the top quartile of NP2 had higher economic status. No other significant differences were found across NP2 quartiles (Table 1).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study, we found a significant inverse association between a NP characterized mainly by high levels of fats, proteins, sodium, and minerals and the likelihood of distress and anxiety in women considering potential confounders. However, no significant relationship was identified between this NP and mental disorders in men. Additionally, we did not observe a significant relationship between a high-carbohydrate NP and the odds of psychological disorders, except for depression in men, which disappeared after adjusting for confounders.

Mental disorders affect a considerable percentage of the global population [2]. Anxiety and depression can severely impair quality of life and impose a considerable economic burden on public health systems [37]. Therefore, exploring potential risk factors for mental disorders is crucial for reducing their negative impacts on individuals and governments. In our study, we identified two major nutrient patterns. The first, referred to as NP1, was high in MUFA, SFA, PUFA, cholesterol, protein, sodium, zinc, phosphorus, iron, B-vitamins, copper, magnesium, and calcium. This pattern is close to an animal-based pattern as its highly loaded nutrients mainly come from animal foods. Furthermore, NP2 was a carbohydrate rich pattern due to high levels of glucose, maltose, sucrose, lactose, galactose, and fructose. This pattern also included significant amounts of dietary fiber, vitamin C, beta-carotene, and vitamin A, suggesting that NP2 could be associated with a diet rich in fruits and vegetables.

Few studies have investigated nutrient patterns in relation to these mental disorders. A cross-sectional study conducted on Iranian adults found that an omnivorous nutrient pattern was negatively associated with the odds of depression in men and psychological stress in women [23]. This NP was characterized by high intakes of SFA, cholesterol, amino acids, phosphorus, zinc, cobalamin, pyridoxine and pantothenic acid which are predominantly found in animal foods. On the other hand, two other identified NPs (fruit-vegetable and grain-dairy) were not significantly related to odds of psychological disorders [23]. Another cross-sectional study among Australian adults failed to find a significant association between an animal-sourced (high in PUFA, MUFA, vitamin E, cholesterol, SFA and relatively protein) NP and depression in two different time points [24]. Similarly, this study failed to observe a significant association between a plant-based diet (high in beta-carotene, vitamin C, fiber, potassium) and depression [24]. The discrepancies in these findings may be attributed to differences in nutrient selection and methodologies used for factor analysis. Additionally, variations in dietary assessment methods, mental health evaluation, control of confounding variables, and the characteristics of different study populations could contribute to the inconsistencies. Due to the lack of data on NPs and major mental disorders, more prospective studies are required.

In this study, the derived NPs contain both health-promoting and health-threatening nutrients. NP1 contained SFA that has been thought to have negative effects on mental health [38]. On the other hand, protective association between dietary calcium, phosphorus, potassium, iron, magnesium, copper and zinc and depression has been suggested [39, 40], that all are highly loaded in NP1 in our study. Furthermore, NP2 had high amounts of sucrose that might be related to increased odds of common mental disorders [41]. Similarly, a positive relation has been suggested between dietary glycemic index and risk of depression [42]. However, NP2 also contained high amounts of fiber and beta-carotene that could be related to decreased risk of depression [43, 44]. This pattern is also close to a fruit-vegetable pattern. Previously, the protective role of fruit and vegetables in mental health has been reported [45]. Although existing a wide range of nutrients in these patterns made it difficult for us to interpret the results, exploring NPs is a valuable approach for understanding diet-disease relations and the potential interactions between nutrients. Therefore, more studies with prospective designs are needed to shed light on the association between the patterns of nutrient intake and mental health.

Different relations of NPs with mental disorders among men and women could be due to differences in the accuracy of dietary intake assessment, effects of gonadal steroids, and different body compositions. Another important point in observational studies is the possibility of reverse causation hypothesis. This considers an opposite direction in the relation for which the study is conducted [46]. In our study, it might be possible that individuals who suffered from mental disorders altered their dietary intakes. Therefore, reverse causation might be a reason for existing both health-promoting and health-threatening nutrients in the identified NPs and should be taken into account while interpreting the findings.

Some mechanisms might explain the inverse relation between the first identified NP, anxiety and stress. It has been widely suggested that inflammation could have a substantial role in the pathogenesis of psychological disorders [47]. Inflammation can disrupt neurotransmitter balance, impair neuroplasticity, and promote oxidative stress in the brain, contributing to mood dysregulation [48]. For instance, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines like CRP, IL-6, TNF-α have been linked to increased neuronal apoptosis, altered hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function, and heightened vulnerability to mental health issues [49]. Although NP1 had high SFA content that might negatively relate to mental disorders, MUFA and PUFA are highly loaded in this pattern as well. It has been suggested that olive oil, as a major source of MUFA, could increase adiponectin secretion as an anti-inflammatory adipokine [50]. MUFAs and PUFAs might also lower the concentrations of proinflammatory cytokines such as CRP, IL-6 and TNF-α [51, 52]. NP1’s high mineral content also plays a critical role. Zinc, for example, is essential for over 300 enzymatic reactions and modulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), a protein that supports neuronal survival, differentiation, and synaptic plasticity [53]. Low zinc levels are associated with increased oxidative stress and inflammation, which can impair BDNF signaling and heighten depressive symptoms; conversely, adequate zinc intake reduces inflammation and enhances BDNF activity, fostering better mental resilience [54, 55]. Also, magnesium, zinc, and iron could be involved in the activation of N-methyl-D-aspartate that might be an important factor in depression [39]. Calcium, meanwhile, facilitates serotonin synthesis and release by regulating enzymatic pathways like tryptophan hydroxylase, thereby promoting mood stabilization [39]. Therefore, the interactions between the nutrients might be responsible for the negative observed associations with mental disorders and it seems that the effects of the health-promoting nutrients loaded in NP1 could negate the detrimental effects of other nutrients. More studies on the NPs and mental disorders are warranted to explore the mechanistic pathways.

This study has some strengths. First, we examined the relation between two major identified NPs and odds of common mental disorders among a large number of Iranian adults. Second, the relations were assessed among both genders, separately. Third, dietary intakes, mental health status, and physical activity were assessed through validated questionnaires. Nevertheless, some limitations are also important and should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of our study limits us to confer a causal relation and prospective studies are needed in this field. Second, although a validated FFQ was applied for examining dietary intakes, measurement error and participants misclassification cannot be fully excluded. Third, some other confounders might still remain that could affect our findings and should be considered in future studies. Finally, participants were Iranian university employees with potentially higher health literacy, which may limit generalizability to the broader Iranian population. Future studies should explore longitudinal associations among other populations with a wide range of socioeconomic and demographic status to replicate our findings and to establish causal relations between dietary nutrient pattern and mental disorders. It is recommended for future studies to consider other important characteristics that could confound such relations including genetic predisposition, types of nutritional supplements, sleep health, and cognitive function.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study found a significant inverse association between the nutrient pattern high in fats, protein, and minerals and anxiety among women. This nutrient pattern was also related to stress in women, while no substantial relationship was observed in men. Additionally, a high-carbohydrate nutrient pattern (rich in beta-carotene, fiber, and vitamin C) showed no association with mental disorders in either gender. More prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
