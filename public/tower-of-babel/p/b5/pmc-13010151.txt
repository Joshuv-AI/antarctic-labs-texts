Elucidating the kinetics of CRISPR–SaCas9 action to obtain effective HIV DNA excision with two gRNAs
Authors: Zhenghao Yu, Yuanling Bao, Ana Alcalá-Lalinde, Zaria Andrade dos Ramos, Merve Koroglu, Ben Berkhout, Elena Herrera-Carrillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe persistence of integrated human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) proviral DNA poses a major barrier to viral eradication, as the viral reservoir enables rapid rebound upon treatment interruption, despite effective virus inhibition. CRISPR–Cas-based editing strategies, especially those using double-site cleavage, show promise in excising proviral DNA, yet the rate and determinants of excision efficiency remain poorly understood. In this study, we systematically evaluated both single- and dual-SaCas9/gRNA approaches for HIV-1 inactivation. Sequence analysis revealed that SaCas9 can eliminate all wild-type HIV-1 genomes with a single gRNA, unlike other CRISPR–Cas systems. Dual-gRNA strategies improved antiviral efficacy, with the Gag3 + Pol5 combination achieving 97% excision efficiency. Kinetic analysis showed that excision efficiency correlates with the kinetic compatibility of paired gRNAs. Pairs of gRNAs with fast and similar kinetics achieved the highest excision efficiency. In contrast, the Gag3 + Env4 pair exhibited discordant kinetic characteristics (fast and slow), resulting in the failure to induce excision as the cut DNA will be repaired before the second cut is realized. Consequently, no excision but regular editing occurred at the two target sites. These findings provide a mechanistic framework for optimizing CRISPR–Cas-mediated excision, highlighting the critical role of both antiviral activity and kinetic synergy in guiding gRNA selection.

Introduction

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major global health challenge, with antiretroviral therapy (ART) effectively suppressing viral replication but failing to eradicate latent viral reservoirs integrated into the host genome [1, 2]. The persistence of reservoirs necessitates the development of curative strategies that enable the inactivation of the HIV provirus. CRISPR–Cas-based gene editing has emerged as a promising tool for HIV elimination, offering cleavage of viral DNA to disrupt viral replication [3–7]. Previous studies demonstrated that CRISPR nucleases such as Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9), Cas12a, and Cas12b can inactivate HIV-1 in T cells [7–12]. However, these systems face challenges in overcoming viral escape that is spurred by rapid mutation of the target sequence when it is repaired by an error-prone DNA repair mechanism [13–15]. Dual-gRNA strategies have been shown to improve the level of HIV inactivation, with excision of HIV sequences between the two target sites representing the most effective outcome, as it generates a product that is beyond repair and thus ideal for a viral cure. Yet these approaches suffer from a low excision efficiency, with regular editing occurring separately at the two target sites [9, 10, 12]. Achieving efficient and consistent excision remains a critical challenge. No specific gRNA combination has been identified that consistently achieves high-frequency excision. The exact determinants that govern the excision frequency remain poorly understood.

Although several reports suggested that excision is a product of such a double gRNA attack [14, 16, 17], close inspection of the reaction products reveals that double editing events often produce regular indel mutations at both target sites [9]. In fact, we argued that the often-used polymerase chain reaction (PCR) method to detect excision is rather dangerous as it dramatically favors amplification of the shorter excision product over double-edited HIV genomes and other products [16]. An explanation for the apparent inefficiency of excision is that the cellular process of DNA repair is extremely fast as it has to protect our genome, such that the cleaved HIV DNA is likely repaired before the second cut is made. In other words, the kinetics of CRISPR–Cas action may be key to the design of an effective HIV excision regimen and different gRNAs may differ in their kinetic properties. We therefore set out to measure the kinetics of selected anti-HIV CRISPR–SaCas9 (CRISPR–Staphylococcus aureus Cas9) gRNAs, chosen for their efficiency and for targeting highly conserved viral sequences, making viral escape by mutation difficult or potentially impossible. In vitro assays were set up and we measured remarkable differences among these gRNAs in the kinetics of DNA cleavage. We reasoned that combination of a “fast” and “slow” gRNAs would be the ideal scenario for a double editing event. For excision, it would seem beneficial if the two gRNAs act with fast and—perhaps even more important—similar kinetics. This idea was validated in HIV inactivation studies, providing direct experimental evidence that matching cleavage kinetics can enhance the proviral excision efficiency.

Materials and methods

gRNA design

gRNA spacers were designed using Benchling CRISPR guide design software (https://www.benchling.com/), targeting the HIV-1 DNA genome of the primary LAI virus isolate. Each gRNA is 21 nucleotides (nt) in length and was designed with the SaCas9 protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) sequence NNGRRT, where “N” represents any nucleotide and “R” represents either adenine (A) or guanine (G). This PAM sequence was chosen to maximize on-target DNA cleavage by SaCas9 [26]. To ensure optimal transcription of the transgene construct with a Pol III promoter, which has a strong preference for A or G at the transcription start site (+1), an additional A was added to the spacer sequence if the gRNA began with thymine (T) or cytosine (C) [18]. In the initial design phase, multiple gRNAs were generated in Benchling and subsequently filtered based on conservation of the viral target sequence among different virus isolates. A total of 5 381 complete HIV-1 sequences from the Los Alamos HIV sequence database (www.hiv.lanl.gov) were aligned in 2022. Shannon entropy analysis was conducted using BioEdit 7.7 to quantify sequence variability, enabling the identification of highly conserved regions suitable for gRNA design. This process prioritized gRNAs that target highly conserved regions of the HIV-1 genome, thus favoring broad-spectrum efficacy, while minimizing the risk of selection of escape mutations because these conserved targets are more likely to execute an important viral function [19–21]. Additionally, gRNAs targeting less-conserved regions, such as the long terminal repeats (LTRs) that flank the proviral genome, were included because of their potential to enable nearly full-length provirus excision with a single gRNA. To minimize unintended cleavage in the cellular chromosome and reduce off-target effects, gRNA sequences with significant complementarity to the human genome (fewer than four mismatches) were excluded from the selection process. This stringent criterion was meant to ensure high target specificity while reducing potential off-target activity. Additionally, sequences present in the lentiviral backbone were avoided to prevent self-targeting [22]. gRNAs encoding sequences with stretches of four or more consecutive Ts were excluded to prevent premature transcription termination [18]. These adjustments should ensure optimal transcription efficiency and functionality of the gRNAs.

Plasmid construction

The lentiviral plasmid (pXPR_206, Addgene #96920) containing a SaCas9 gene and gRNA expression cassette was obtained from Root and Doench [23]. The green fluorescent protein (GFP) gene was cloned downstream of the SaCas9 gene, separated by a P2A self-cleaving peptide, creating the construct Lenti-SaCas9-eGFP. This design allows for simultaneous expression of SaCas9 and GFP from a single transcript, enabling easy monitoring of the transduction efficiency. The selected anti-HIV gRNA spacers were inserted into the vector Esp3I restriction sites. The negative control gRNA (CTRL, GCTATTCTGATTACACCCGAG) does not target HIV DNA or the human genome. The plasmid pLAI contains the full-length genome of the HIV-1 subtype B isolate LAI, including all viral genes and regulatory elements required for viral replication [24]. The plasmid pRL-CMV (Promega) drives the constitutive expression of Renilla luciferase.

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Life Technologies, Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FCS), 1% minimal essential medium nonessential amino acids, penicillin (100 U/ml) and streptomycin (100 μg/ml) at 37°C and 5% CO2. SupT1 T cells (ATCC CRL-1942) were maintained in Advanced RPMI medium (GIBCO BRL) supplemented with 1% L-glutamine, 1% FCS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 100 μg/ml streptomycin, at 37°C and 5% CO₂.

Transient DNA transfection

HEK293T cells (1.4 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded in 24-well plates. After 24 h, when cells reached 70%–80% confluence, they were transfected with 350 ng of SaCas9/gRNA plasmid, 150 ng of pLAI plasmid, and 1 ng of pRL-CMV using Lipofectamine 2000 (Life Technologies, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell culture supernatants were collected and analyzed using an in-house developed CA-p24 ELISA to quantify viral production [25]. Renilla luciferase activity was measured to account for experimental variability, including differences in transfection efficiency. A plasmid encoding SaCas9 without a gRNA (SaCas9) was used as a negative control, with CA-p24 production normalized to 100%. In addition, a non-targeting gRNA (CTRL), which does not recognize HIV DNA or the human genome, served as an additional negative control. Three independent transfections were performed in duplicate, and values were adjusted for inter-session variation as previously described [26].

Lentivirus production and transduction

Lentiviral vector production in HEK293T cells and subsequent transduction of SupT1 T cells were performed as previously described [27, 28]. Briefly, HEK293T cells were transfected with lentiviral plasmids encoding the transgene along with packaging plasmids (pSYNGP, pRSV-rev, and pVSV-G) using Lipofectamine 2000, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Two days post-transfection, lentiviral vector-containing supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane. Viral stocks were concentrated 20-fold by ultracentrifugation (SW 32.1 Ti Swinging-Bucket Rotor at 32 000 × rpm, 90 min) over a 5% sucrose cushion. Aliquots were prepared and stored at −80°C for future use. Viral titers were determined based on GFP expression in transduced SupT1 cells. Three days after transduction, lentiviral titers were quantified via flow cytometry. Transduced cells were washed with FACS buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% FCS), and GFP-positive cells were measured using a BD LSR Fortessa equipped with FACS-Diva software. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of GFP was analyzed with FlowJo version 10.8.1. The transduction titer was calculated using the following formula: transducing units per ml (TU/ml) = cell number × [(percentage of GFP-positive cells/100)/dilution factor of the vector].

SupT1 cells were transduced at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.15 by seeding 1 × 106 cells in 5 ml of medium in a T25 flask, followed by the addition of the lentiviral vector. Three days post-transduction, viable cells were sorted via FACS, and GFP-positive cells were selected and expanded for subsequent virus infection experiments. For dual gRNA delivery, the additional gRNA was cloned into a lentiviral vector expressing mCherry, which was then used to transduce the sorted GFP⁺ cells. Dual-positive cells (GFP⁺/mCherry⁺) were subsequently sorted and expanded, generating a stable SupT1 population expressing both gRNAs for downstream experiments.

HIV production and infection

To produce a stock of HIV-1 LAI virus, HEK293T cells were transfected with 5 µg of the pLAI molecular clone in a T25 culture flask. Three days post-transfection, the cell supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 1 500 × rpm for 3 min, filtered through a 0.45 µm membrane, aliquoted, and stored at −80°C. The MOI was quantified using SupT1 cells through a CA-p24 enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), following a previously described in-house protocol [21, 25, 29]. CRISPR–Cas-transduced SupT1 cells (2 × 10⁵ in 1 ml of culture medium) were sorted by flow cytometry, expanded, and infected with HIV-1 LAI at MOIs of 0.1, 1, and 10. Six parallel cultures were maintained for each condition. Infected non-transduced WT SupT1 cells served as infection controls. Additionally, a control group was transduced with a non-targeting gRNA (CTRL). Cultures were maintained for at least 83 days post-infection (dpi) and passaged twice weekly. Virus replication was monitored every 3–4 days by assessing syncytia formation.

Proviral sequencing

In cultures where breakthrough virus replication occurred, supernatant was collected at the peak of infection and passaged on fresh CRISPR–Cas-transduced cells. When replication was observed upon passaging, the appearance of escape mutations in the HIV target sequence was analyzed through Sanger sequencing to assess potential resistance mechanisms. Cultures exhibiting sustained suppression of HIV replication were harvested at the end of the experiment for sequencing to evaluate the “cure status”.

Total cellular DNA, including integrated HIV proviruses, was extracted using the DNeasy kit (Qiagen). The gRNA target regions were amplified by PCR using primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. PCR amplification was performed with different primer pairs and subsequent sequencing to detect genome modifications induced by SaCas9. The resulting PCR amplicons were purified from agarose gels, cloned into a TA vector, and multiple individual clones were sequenced by Sanger sequencing. The obtained sequences were aligned with the HIV-1 LAI reference sequence to identify acquired mutations. Note that PCR amplification of proviral DNA targeted by dual gRNAs tends to favor the detection of excision events due to preferential amplification of shorter over longer fragments [16]. This size-dependent bias can lead to an overrepresentation of excised sequences, while non-excision events, such as double editing events with point mutations and indels (insertions or deletions), are underrepresented [30, 31]. To address this limitation, we performed single-genome sequencing for gRNA target sequences spaced more than 600 nucleotides apart. This approach enables the amplification and sequencing of individual viral genomes while minimizing PCR length artifacts. By amplifying each genome as an independent template, it eliminates length-dependent bias and ensures balanced detection of both excision and non-excision events. HIV DNA copy number was quantified using quantitative PCR (qPCR), as described previously [32]. The primers and probe used are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Following Poisson distribution principles, the DNA was serially diluted to ensure that only a single copy of the HIV-1 provirus was present in the template and amplified using nested PCR (primer sequences in Supplementary Table S3), and PCR products were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis. The presence of PCR bands in <25% of wells indicated an 80% probability that each amplified product originated from a single cDNA molecule, ensuring minimal amplification bias [33].

Production of SaCas9 protein and gRNAs

The SaCas9 expression plasmid p6XHis_NLS-SaCas9 (Addgene #101086) was transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) competent cells (Novagen). A bacterial preculture was grown overnight at 37°C with shaking and used to inoculate 200 mL of LB medium at a 1:100 ratio. The culture was incubated at 37°C with shaking until reaching an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was induced by adding 500 μM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG), followed by incubation at 18°C with shaking for 14–16 h. Bacterial cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in 20 ml lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 600 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 5 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP) and lyzed by sonication. The His6-tagged SaCas9 protein was purified using HisPur™ Ni-NTA Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher) and eluted with elution buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 600 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 100–300 mM imidazole, 1 mM TCEP).

In vitro transcription of gRNA was performed using T7 RNA polymerase in run-off reactions, following the manufacturer’s instructions for the MEGAshortscript T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen). Transcription templates were prepared by PCR amplification from the Lenti-SaCas9-eGFP plasmid using primers listed in Supplementary Table S4.

Kinetic profiling of SaCas9/gRNA binding and cleavage in vitro

Two DNA constructs, referred to as beacons, were designed based on previous studies [34, 35]. Unmodified and chromophore-labeled DNA oligonucleotides were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). The target and non-target strands were individually labeled at their PAM-distal ends with a fluorescent marker and a quencher. According to the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) mechanism, fluorescence emission is quenched in the intact beacon, resulting in low baseline fluorescence intensity [36]. Two or three oligonucleotides were designed to generate ∼40 bp DNA fragments, incorporating a 21 bp protospacer, a 6 bp PAM, and a 12 bp PAM-distal sequence based on the HIV LAI genome. Equal amounts of complementary oligonucleotide strands were mixed in a buffer containing 40 mM Tris (pH 7.5) and 100 mM NaCl, heated at 90°C for 2 min, and then gradually cooled to 20°C to facilitate annealing, resulting in the formation of DNA beacons.

Beacon 1 was designed to assess the binding kinetics of the SaCas9 RNP complex. It was constructed using three oligonucleotides, with its non-target strand disrupted between the PAM and the protospacer sequence. Upon binding of the SaCas9 RNP complex, a DNA-RNA hybrid forms, leading to the immediate dissociation of oligo 3. This separation results in fluorescence increase due to the release of the quencher. Beacon 2 was designed to evaluate the cleavage kinetics of SaCas9 RNP. It consisted of two fully complementary oligonucleotides, with the protospacer region extended by three nucleotides relative to Beacon 1. When the SaCas9 RNP complex binds to Beacon 2, cleavage at the 5′ end of the non-target strand results in detachment of the quencher-labeled fragment, leading to a progressive increase in fluorescence intensity. Fluorescence-based assays were performed at 37°C using a FLUOstar OPTIMA microplate reader. Reactions were conducted in a binding buffer containing 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.1 mM DTT, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 0.02% Tween 20. This MgCl₂ concentration was selected to mimic the intracellular free Mg²⁺ level, ensuring optimal SaCas9/gRNA complex binding and cleavage activity [37]. Each reaction mixture contained 25 pmol SaCas9 protein, 25 pmol gRNA, and 0.25 nmol fluorescently labeled beacon. Fluorescence emission from labeled target DNA was monitored using a fluorometric assay, with intensity measurements recorded every 2 s. Excitation and emission wavelengths were set at 485 and 520 nm, respectively, enabling real-time fluorescence tracking. Data were collected from three independent experiments and analyzed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10.2.0). Results are presented as the mean values from three independent replicates.

Competitive cell growth assay

Lentivirally transduced SupT1 cells were generated at an MOI of 0.15 and screened for potential growth impairment due to lentiviral integration and/or CRISPR–SaCas9–gRNA expression using a sensitive competitive cell growth assay [38–40]. Briefly, a mixed population of transduced (GFP+/CRISPR+) and untransduced (GFP−/CRISPR−) cells was cultured for 42 days and longitudinally monitored by FACS, simply tracking changes in the GFP+/− ratio. The impact on cell proliferation was quantified as a percentage reduction in cell growth.

T7 endonuclease 1 cleavage assay

To predict potential off-target sites of SaCas9/gRNA, the Cas-OFFinder algorithm was used [41]. Total cellular DNA was extracted to evaluate SaCas9-mediated editing at the predicted sites. Purified genomic DNA was used as a PCR template to amplify target regions with primers listed in Supplementary Table S5. The PCR products were denatured and reannealed to form heteroduplex structures, indicative of mismatches caused by CRISPR-mediated editing. These heteroduplexes were then incubated with T7 endonuclease I (T7E1) using the EnGen Mutation Detection Kit (BioLabs) to cleave mismatched DNA. The resulting fragments were analyzed by agarose gel electrophoresis to detect and quantify editing events.

Results

Single CRISPR–SaCas9 gRNAs target HIV DNA and inhibit viral expression in transient transfection

A set of 31 gRNAs was designed to target highly conserved regions of the HIV-1 genome (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Table S6). We first assessed the anti-HIV effect in transient assays where HIV LAI DNA was co-transfected into HEK293T cells along with plasmids encoding the SaCas9 endonuclease and an individual gRNA. To account for variation in transfection efficiency, a Renilla plasmid was included. Virus production was measured 2 days post-transfection by quantifying the HIV CA-p24 protein levels in the culture supernatant (Fig. 1B). The SaCas9 plasmid was used as a negative control, with CA-p24 production normalized to 100%. Most of the designed gRNAs exhibited robust anti-HIV activity, reducing virus production to below 10% of the reference control. However, there were exceptions; gRNAs LTR2, Pol6, Pol7, Pol13, and Env1 demonstrated suboptimal performance and were subsequently excluded from further testing. The remaining gRNAs advanced to further testing against the replicating virus in infected T cell cultures.

The gRNA targets for SaCas9 attack on the HIV DNA genome. (A) Positions of the antiviral gRNAs on the HIV proviral DNA are indicated, targeting both the positive and negative strands. (B) Antiviral activity of single SaCas9/gRNAs. The cleavage activity of each gRNA was evaluated by transiently co-transfecting HEK293T cells with plasmids encoding HIV-1 LAI and SaCas9/gRNA. Two days post-transfection, supernatants were collected, and viral gene expression was quantified by CA-p24 ELISA. Values were normalized to the SaCas9 reference (set as 100%) and are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments.

SaCas9/gRNA-mediated HIV-1 inhibition in infected T cell cultures

Stable cell lines expressing CRISPR–SaCas9 and a single gRNA were established by transducing the SupT1 T cell line with the respective lentiviral vector. For each transduced and sorted cell line, six parallel infections were performed with the HIV LAI isolate at an MOI of 0.1 (Fig. 2A). Syncytia formation was monitored as an indicator of active viral replication until the end of the experiment at 83 dpi. Unhindered HIV replication was observed in control SupT1 cells and CTRL transduced control cells at 7 dpi, as virus-induced cytopathic effects were evident in all six cell cultures. Cells transduced with LTR1, LTR3, LTR4, and LTR5 showed a modest inhibition of virus replication, with syncytia formation and cell death occurring between 7 and 20 dpi. Cell cultures transduced with Pol3, Pol4, Env3, and Env6 showed a moderate delay in viral replication compared to the LTR-targeting gRNAs, but some eventually showed breakthrough HIV replication. Nevertheless, the majority of gRNAs inhibited viral replication for up to 83 days. To assess subtle differences among the more effective gRNAs, we increased the viral infection dose to an MOI of 1 (Fig. 2B). WT SupT1 and CTRL-transduced cells rapidly showed viral replication accompanied by massive cell death. Notably, no syncytia were observed in any of the six cultures transduced with vectors encoding Gag2, Gag4, Gag5, Pol5, or Env4, and in five of six Gag3 or Pol8 cultures. In contrast, cell cultures transduced with LTR1-LTR5 rapidly developed syncytia, consistent with the modest inhibition observed in the plasmid transfection experiment (Fig. 1B). The most effective anti-HIV candidates (Gag2-5, Pol5, Pol8, and Env4) were selected for further experiments. These cell lines expressing one of the 7 gRNA candidates were infected with a higher viral dose (MOI of 10 in Fig. 2C). Both control groups exhibited syncytia and cell death by 5 dpi. Although Gag5, Pol8, and Env4 delayed viral replication, syncytia appeared in all six independent cultures between 15 and 43 dpi. Gag3 inhibited HIV in five cultures, Pol5 in four, Gag4 in three, and Gag2 in one, meaning that viral replication was suppressed for up to 90 days. Consequently, these four gRNAs were identified as the most potent and durable HIV inhibitors.

Phenotype and genotype analysis of HIV escape candidates

We previously reported that virus escape from CRISPR–Cas attack is facilitated by the error-prone repair of the cleaved HIV DNA, which introduces point mutations (edits) at the target site and can allow the virus to escape from the sequence-specific attack [10, 11]. This phenomenon is attributed to the nonhomologous end joining (NHEJ) mechanism of cellular DNA repair [19]. To test for gRNA-resistant HIV variants, cell-free virus collected at the peak of infection was passaged onto a fresh batch of transduced cells expressing the corresponding gRNA inhibitor. Viral replication within 21 days was considered as evidence of the viral escape phenotype. In some cultures, viral replication was indeed observed. To analyze the viral genome, total cellular DNA was extracted and used as a template for PCR-amplification of the target sites. Sanger sequencing was performed on 4 × Gag2, 1 × Gag3, 2 × Gag4, 5 × Gag5, 1 × Pol5, 4 × Pol8 and 5 × Env4 cultures at a high viral dose (MOI = 10). While LTR-targeting gRNAs exhibited lower inhibitory efficiency compared to other gRNAs, we selected LTR4, the most promising LTR-targeting gRNA, and proceeded with it under the assumption that targeting the LTR with a single gRNA could result in excision of the full HIV genome. However, due to the rapid and unhindered breakthrough replication we observed, we did not observe any editing outcomes in cells infected at an MOI of 10. Consequently, we analyzed samples from the MOI 1 experiment to assess the editing profile of LTR4 and observed small indels and delins, a special class of editing products with a deletion and insertion occurring in the same sequence (Fig. 3). A few point mutations were identified within the Gag, Pol, and Env targets. Because the selected targets are highly conserved among different virus isolates, they are likely critical for the virus, such that no gross changes will be allowed in the viral genome. Consistently, all observed mutations preserved the HIV open reading frames, indicating maintenance of the encoded protein functions to support virus replication.

Phenotype and genotype analysis of cure candidates

To further demonstrate complete inactivation of HIV in cultures showing sustained viral suppression, two additional experiments were conducted. First, potentially cured cells were co-cultured with WT SupT1 cells to verify the absence of infectious virus. Second, the target sites of the integrated HIV provirus were sequenced. Mutations resulting from SaCas9/gRNA-mediated editing were identified as the likely cause of HIV inactivation. In similar experiments with other Cas systems, a minority of WT HIV sequences were always detected, thus explaining the inability to fully eliminate infectious virus [42]. Remarkably, no untouched WT HIV was detected in the genomes edited by SaCas9/gRNA, consistent with a complete HIV cure in this cell culture model. While previous cure results required a combinatorial attack with multiple gRNAs, SaCas9 appears capable of achieving this result with a single gRNA. A diversity of genetic changes was observed, as expected, with the majority of edits resulting in frameshifts in the respective open reading frame (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S1A).

Why is SaCas9 able to achieve a cure with a single gRNA? Is it simply a more efficient editor, or does it create more substantial lesions that inactivate HIV? To address this, we analyzed the frequency of different types of genomic changes induced by single-SaCas9/gRNA attack on the target sequence Fig. 4B. The sizes of deletions and insertions generated by single-gRNA attacks are shown in Fig. 4C, with an average deletion size of 11.7 bp and an average insertion size of 4.2 bp. Mutations in the so-called “delin” group, characterized by a combined deletion plus insertion, were also analyzed (Fig. 4D), revealing an average deletion of 17.6 bp and an average insertion of 4.3 bp. Figure 4E shows the frequency distribution of insertion sizes within the delin group, ranging from 1 to 25 bp, with the majority falling between 1 and 9 bp. Compared to SpCas9, the editing profile of SaCas9 was not markedly different. However, the key distinction with SaCas9 is the complete absence of any remaining wild-type (WT) sequences following editing [10, 11]. Together, these results strongly support the notion that a single SaCas9/gRNA can fully inactivate HIV in T cell lines, even under conditions with a high viral challenge (MOI:10).

HIV inhibition by dual SaCas9/gRNAs

To investigate whether combining gRNAs could enhance the antiviral activity, we evaluated the efficacy of dual-gRNA strategies. The two lentiviral vectors for expressing gRNA1 and gRNA2 are shown in Fig. 5A. The position of the five selected gRNAs and the expected HIV excision products are illustrated in Fig. 5B. Cells were challenged with a high viral input (MOI = 10) (Fig. 5C). Two control cell lines, WT SupT1 and SupT1 cells expressing CTRL gRNA, exhibited robust HIV replication. In cultures treated with a single SaCas9/gRNA, rapid viral rebound occurred in most cases, with only a few cultures showing sustained HIV suppression. In contrast, all cultures transduced with any of the five dual-gRNA combinations exhibited durable viral inhibition throughout the experiment. These results confirm that dual-gRNA approaches provide significantly improved suppression of HIV replication compared to single-gRNA treatments [9, 11, 12].

We next systematically investigated editing outcomes driven by different dual SaCas9/gRNA combinations. As shown in Figs 6 and7, the outcomes can be classified in two categories: excision or double editing of both target sites (including point mutations, insertions, deletions, and delins). Remarkably, no WT sequences were recovered for any of the tested combinations.

For gRNA pairs targeting shorter genomic intervals (e.g. Gag2 + Gag3 and Gag2 + Gag4), regular PCR amplification followed by TA cloning and sequencing was performed. Excision was observed in 7 of 15 sequences with Gag2 + Gag3, while the remaining 8 sequences exhibited double editing (Fig. 6B). A similar pattern was found for Gag2 + Gag4, with 8 of 17 sequences showing excision and 9 showing double editing (Fig. 6C). For gRNA pairs targeting larger genomic intervals (e.g. Gag2 + Pol5 and Gag3 + Pol5), regular PCR is prone to amplification bias favoring shorter fragments. To address this, we used a limiting dilution PCR approach to ensure amplification from a single HIV DNA copy, minimizing such bias. Using this method, Gag2 + Pol5 yielded 17 excisions among 20 sequences, with the remaining 3 showing double editing (Fig. 6D). Notably, the Gag3 + Pol5 combination resulted in 31 excision events among 32 analyzed sequences, with only a single sequence exhibiting double editing (Fig. 6E). All 16 sequences amplified by regular PCR and subsequently amplified by TA-cloning exhibit excision (Supplementary Fig. S1B). This is the first identified gRNA pair in our massive studies over the last 9 years that achieves such a high excision frequency. In contrast, for the Gag3 + Env4 combination, no shortened PCR fragments were detected, and subsequent sequencing revealed that all 24 clones displayed double editing, with no evidence of excision events (Fig. 6F).

In summary, the balance between excision and double editing varied among gRNA pairs. As summarized in Fig. 7A and compared in Fig. 7B, excision frequencies ranged from 0% for Gag3 + Env4, to 47% for Gag2 + Gag3 and Gag2 + Gag4, 85% for Gag2 + Pol5, and 97% for Gag3 + Pol5. We further characterized the spectrum of insertions and deletions generated during editing. As shown in Fig. 7C, the average deletion length was 14 bp, and the average insertion length was 3.3 bp. Within the delin group, sequence variants exhibited average deletion and insertion sizes of 15.3 and 3.1 bp, respectively (Fig. 7D). Insertions were strongly skewed toward sizes ranging from 1 to 3 bp (Fig. 7E). Notably, SaCas9 achieved a substantially higher excision efficiency than other systems, including SpCas9, Cas12a, and Cas12b, in which dual-gRNA approaches rarely produced detectable excision events [9, 11, 12]. These findings highlight the critical importance of the chosen endonuclease system and gRNA pair selection for efficient excision.

Proliferation of SaCas9/gRNAs transduced cells

As an initial assessment of potential negative effects of the LV vector, SaCas9, or the gRNA on host cell metabolism, we evaluated the proliferative capacity of transduced SupT1 cells. We co-cultured transduced GFP-positive cells with untransduced GFP-negative cells and monitored their proportions by flow cytometry. The results indicate that the fraction of GFP-positive transduced cells decreased only minimally (Fig. 8A), suggesting that the different gRNA constructs do not have a major impact on the growth of the transduced SupT1 cells.

Absence of off-target DNA cleavage for SaCas9/gRNA

The Gag3 + Pol5 combination exhibits the strongest antiviral activity and the highest cure efficiency due to prevalent excisions that prevent viral escape. To further assess safety, potential off-target sites for Gag3 and Pol5 in the Homo sapiens genome (GRCh38/hg38) were predicted using Cas-OFFinder [41] (Supplementary Table S5). Total cellular DNA was extracted from HIV-infected cells transduced with Gag3 and Pol5. PCR amplification of the on-target sites generated 727 bp and 687 bp fragments for Gag3 and Pol5, respectively, serving as positive controls. DNA from CTRL-transduced cells was amplified under identical conditions to serve as negative controls. All amplicons, including those from on-target and off-target sites, were subjected to T7E1 digestion followed by agarose gel electrophoresis. On-target cleavage produced the expected fragment sizes: Gag3 yielded 201 bp and 526 bp fragments, while Pol5 generated 335 bp and 352 bp fragments (Fig. 8B). Importantly, no cleavage was detected at any of the predicted off-target sites, and the negative control (CTRL) also showed no cleavage activity. These results demonstrate that Gag3 and Pol5 efficiently cleave the intended targets without detectable off-target effects, highlighting the precision and specificity of this approach for HIV.

Kinetics of different SaCas9/gRNAs

Differential kinetics may underlie the distinct editing outcomes observed with dual-gRNA combinations (excision versus double editing). To explore this, we designed two DNA constructs, referred to as beacons (Fig. 9A; sequences in Supplementary Fig. S2) and assessed both the binding and cleavage kinetics of SaCas9 RNP complexes (Fig. 9B and C). Upon addition of SaCas9 RNP to beacon 1, fluorescence intensity increased as a result of complex formation. Among the RNP complexes tested, Gag3 and Pol5 displayed the fastest binding kinetics, reaching peak fluorescence levels more rapidly than other complexes. Complexes containing Gag4 and Gag2 exhibited intermediate rates of fluorescence increase, whereas Env4 demonstrated slower kinetics relative to several other gRNAs. Notably, we observed that gLTR4 did not exhibit a rapid increase in fluorescence intensity, but instead showed a slow and gradual increase, not reaching a fluorescence plateau within the detected time frame (Fig. 9B). In parallel, the cleavage kinetics of SaCas9 RNP complexes were monitored using beacon 2. As shown in Fig. 9C, addition of SaCas9 RNP led to specific cleavage of target beacon 2, resulting in a marked increase in fluorescence intensity post-cleavage. Consistent with the binding kinetic profiles, Gag3 and Pol5 complexes reached the plateau phase of fluorescence intensity most quickly, while Gag4 and Gag2 exhibited a more gradual increase. Env4 again demonstrated substantially slower kinetics, reaching the plateau phase later than most gRNAs, whereas LTR4 exhibited the slowest kinetics overall, and it did not reach maximal fluorescence within the observation period. To confirm that the observed fluorescence increase was specifically due to the interaction between the SaCas9 RNP and the target DNA, CTRL was included as a negative control. The CTRL does not bind to the target DNA or the human genome, and no fluorescence increase was observed (data not shown). The observed fluorescence enhancement in the experimental group reflects the specific binding and cleavage of the target by SaCas9 RNP, which separates the quencher from the fluorophore and generates the signal.

The dissociation constants (Kd) derived from kinetic analyses, along with the 95% confidence intervals (CIs), are summarized in Table 1. The ranking of Kd values for both binding and cleavage followed a consistent trend: Gag3 (7.5 nM, 8.8 nM) < Pol5 (7.6 nM, 12.1 nM) < Gag4 (8.3 nM, 19.3 nM) < Gag2 (16.2 nM, 66.0 nM) < Env4 (63.3 nM, 507.6 nM) < LTR4 (161.3 nM, 994.2 nM). This variation in kinetic parameters closely aligns with the efficiency of each SaCas9/gRNA in inhibiting HIV-1 in long-term challenge studies. In the long-term infection assay at an MOI of 10, the number of cured cell cultures for each gRNA was as follows: Gag3 (5 of 6 replicates), Pol5 (4/6), Gag4 (3/6), Gag2 (1/6), and Env4 (0/6). These results demonstrate how gRNAs with faster cleavage kinetics (lower nM values) tend to show higher efficacy in reducing HIV-1 replication, suggesting a potential link between cleavage efficiency and long-term antiviral effectiveness. Notably, as shown in Fig. 9D, Gag3 and Pol5 exhibited comparable binding and cleavage kinetics, which likely explains why their combination achieved the highest excision efficiency (97%). In contrast, the pair with the greatest kinetic disparity, Gag3 and Env4, failed to generate detectable excision events. Intermediate outcomes were observed for gRNA combinations with a moderate kinetic alignment, such as Gag2 + Gag3 (47%), Gag2 + Gag4 (47%), and Gag2 + Pol5 (85%). These findings highlight that kinetic compatibility between gRNAs is a critical determinant of the excision efficiency, with gRNAs showing fast and closely matched kinetics being most effective at driving HIV-1 genome excision.

Discussion

HIV remains a major global health challenge. While ART can effectively and durably suppress viral replication, it fails to eliminate the virus. The persistence of latently infected cells harboring integrated proviral DNA is the primary barrier to a cure, as it enables rapid viral rebound upon treatment interruption [43]. Given that proviral integration is the principal barrier to HIV eradication, genome editing technologies—particularly the CRISPR–Cas9 system—offer a promising avenue for removing the source of viral persistence. The first attempts with a single gRNA demonstrated potent HIV inhibition, but viral escape was apparent [19]. To prevent viral escape, a gRNA targeting a site such as the LTR (with two copies in the HIV genome) or multiple gRNAs can be employed. Inducing two cleavage events may facilitate excision of the (parts of the) provirus, providing a more definitive disruption that is beyond repair than small indels. While the use of additional gRNAs remains a possibility for obtaining more robust HIV-inactivation, this approach will also create more problems (vector efficiency, off-target effects, etc.). As such, the two-gRNA system seems ideal as it demonstrates sufficient efficacy for proviral excision, while minimizing the delivery challenges and complexities associated with incorporating additional gRNAs [44]. While previous studies have demonstrated CRISPR–Cas-mediated HIV-1 excision, the reported excision efficiency is low and likely overestimated because of PCR-bias for amplification of shortened excision fragments [17, 45–47]. Accurate data on the excision efficiency and whether there are differences between gRNA pairs are rare. In this study, we utilized SaCas9 to inactivate HIV in SupT1 cells through both single- and dual-gRNA strategies, supported by sequence analysis of the edited HIV products. We demonstrate that there is a strong correlation between the actual excision efficiency and the kinetic compatibility of paired gRNAs as measured in in vitro assays on the kinetics of RNP binding to DNA and subsequent cleavage. In case of kinetic incompatibility of the two gRNAs, DNA repair of site 1 has already taken place before site 2 is cleaved. Our findings offer key insights into the molecular basis of CRISPR-mediated excision and highlight its potential as a curative strategy for HIV-1.

CRISPR–Cas-mediated cleavage of HIV-1 proviral DNA triggers immediate DNA repair, but this error-prone process will generate indel-type of mutations. Actually, we showed that HIV can use this sequence variability, introduced precisely at the cleavage site, to escape from this CRISPR–Cas attack [19]. While previous studies using the SpCas9, Cas12a, and Cas12b endonucleases reported partial viral inactivation, residual WT sequences persisted [9–11]. In contrast, our findings demonstrate that SaCas9 efficiently eliminates all infectious HIV-1 DNA, even under high viral input (MOI = 10), highlighting its superior editing capability. SaCas9 operates as a multiple-turnover enzyme, allowing repeated rounds of DNA cleavage and enhancing the likelihood of synchronized cuts at both target sites. In contrast, SpCas9 is a single-turnover nuclease, which limits its capacity for generating multiple cleavage events [48]. This difference likely underlies the higher excision efficiency observed with specific gRNA pairs when using SaCas9. Notably, single-gRNA attack successfully inhibited viral replication in most replicates: Gag3 (5/6), Pol5 (4/6), Gag4 (3/6), and Gag2 (1/6). Consistent with prior studies, dual-gRNA attack showed enhanced efficacy over single-gRNA attack [9, 11]. All gRNA combinations successfully blocked HIV-1 replication without viral escape in six independent cell cultures, surpassing the inhibitory capacity of single gRNAs. This supports the model that cleavage at two loci increases the inhibition efficiency and reduces the likelihood of viral escape, making it a far more sustained strategy for HIV-1 inactivation toward a cure.

Excision of the HIV provirus is a key strategy in efforts toward development of a functional cure, with numerous studies exploring CRISPR–Cas-based approaches to remove integrated viral DNA [49, 50]. Our data identify the Gag3 + Pol5 combination as the most effective gRNA pair, reaching excision efficiencies up to 97%. Although complete eradication of the virus was not achieved, such highly effective pairs could still contribute to a functional cure by durably suppressing viral rebound, as they prevent the reactivation of integrated HIV genomes. Notably, different SaCas9/gRNA combinations exhibited strikingly different excision efficiencies. For example, while Gag3 + Pol5 yielded high excision, the Gag3 + Env4 pair failed to induce excision events, despite the potent antiviral activity of Gag3 alone. A possible explanation for the distinct excision frequencies is that cleavage at the Gag3 site was repaired before Env4-mediated cleavage could occur, preventing excision and instead leading to double editing at both target sites. Successful excision requires the near-simultaneous and fast cleavage at both gRNA targets, allowing excision to occur before DNA repair intervenes. Consistently, binding and cleavage kinetic analyses identified Gag3 + Pol5 as the most compatible pair, correlating with its highest excision rate. In contrast, gRNA combinations with mismatched kinetic properties, such as Gag3 + Env4, failed to induce excision despite strong single-target inhibition capacity. This failure is likely due to the misalignment of cleavage kinetics between the two gRNAs. Specifically, if the timing of the cleavage events is not synchronized, such as one gRNA inducing cleavage and repair before the second gRNA cleaves its target. Analysis of breakthrough cultures with the LTR4 gRNA revealed no excision events, which aligns with the slower binding (Kd = 161.3 nM) and cleavage (Kd = 994.2 nM) kinetics, when compared to the other tested gRNAs. These slower kinetics likely hinder simultaneous cleavage at both LTR targets. Given the rapid nature of DNA repair, this mismatch in timing prevents efficient excision. Therefore, when selecting gRNAs for HIV-1 excision, it is crucial to prioritize efficient, synchronized cleavage. We think that other variables that were mentioned in literature [16], such as the distance between target sites, are unlikely to play a major role. However, we acknowledge that the number of gRNA pairs analyzed in this study is limited. Therefore, we cannot fully exclude the contribution of additional variables. Future studies should systematically compare larger sets of gRNA pairs with well-defined and matched binding and cleavage kinetics, as well as generate excision fragments of comparable sizes, to more rigorously assess the relative contribution of these parameters.

Our study further supports the feasibility of the SaCas9 endonuclease as a highly efficient system for HIV-1 inactivation, demonstrating that a single-SaCas9/gRNA complex can eliminate all infectious HIV DNA in cell culture. Dual-gRNA attack enhances the antiviral potency, with certain combinations achieving robust excision of part of the integrated proviral DNA. Notably, this is the first study to systematically analyze how gRNA binding and cleavage kinetics impact excision, providing a theoretical foundation for optimizing CRISPR strategies. Additionally, we conducted an off-target analysis and detected no unintended cleavage events, although further in-depth studies are needed to fully evaluate the safety of this approach. By integrating the kinetic profiling into the process of gRNA selection, future research can refine excision-based strategies, bringing us closer to the development of a functional HIV cure. The current findings may also catalyze other genome excision strategies, e.g. for the removal of parts of human chromosomes [51, 52].
