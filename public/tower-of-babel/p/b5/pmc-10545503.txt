Pharmacokinetics and optimized dosing of dispersible and non-dispersible levofloxacin formulations in young children
Authors: Louvina E van der Laan, Anneke C Hesseling, H Simon Schaaf, Megan Palmer, Heather R Draper, Lubbe Wiesner, Paolo Denti, Anthony J Garcia-Prats
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLevofloxacin is used for treatment and prevention of rifampicin-resistant (RR)-TB in children. Recent data showed higher exposures with 100 mg dispersible compared with non-dispersible tablet formulations with potentially important dosing implications in children. We aimed to verify and better characterize this finding.MethodsWe conducted a crossover pharmacokinetic trial in children aged ≤5 years receiving levofloxacin RR-TB preventive therapy. Pharmacokinetic sampling was done after 15–20 mg/kg doses of levofloxacin with 100 mg dispersible and crushed 250 mg non-dispersible levofloxacin formulations. A population pharmacokinetic model was developed.ResultsTwenty-five children were included, median (IQR) weight and age 12.2 (10.7–15.0) kg and 2.56 (1.58–4.03) years, respectively. A two-compartment model with first-order elimination and transit compartment absorption best described levofloxacin pharmacokinetics. Allometric scaling adjusted for body size, and maturation of clearance with age was characterized. Typical clearance in a 12 kg child was estimated at 4.17 L/h. Non-dispersible tablets had 21.5% reduced bioavailability compared with the dispersible formulation, with no significant differences in other absorption parameters.Dosing simulations showed that current recommended dosing for both formulations result in median exposures below adult-equivalent exposures at a 750 mg daily dose, mainly in children >6 months. Higher levofloxacin doses of 16–30 mg/kg for dispersible and 20–38 mg/kg for crushed non-dispersible tablets may be required in children >6 months.ConclusionsThe dispersible paediatric levofloxacin formulation has improved bioavailability compared with the crushed non-dispersible adult formulation, but exposures remain below those in adults. We propose optimized age- and weight-based dosing for levofloxacin, which require further evaluation.

Introduction

The burden of rifampicin-resistant (RR)-TB, i.e. TB caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis resistant to at least rifampicin, in children is substantial, with modelled estimates of 25 000–32 000 cases annually.1,2 There are also an estimated 2 million children infected with RR-TB globally, many of whom would benefit from effective and safe preventive therapy.1 Optimizing RR-TB prevention and treatment in children is critically important.

The fluoroquinolones are key components of current and future RR-TB treatment regimens (WHO group A priority drug) and are also used for the prevention of RR-TB.3,4 Levofloxacin has been widely used to treat paediatric RR-TB, has known pharmacokinetic parameters, does not have significant interactions with antiretroviral drugs, and has limited cardiac, liver and overall safety concerns at currently recommended doses for the treatment and prevention of RR-TB.5 A major barrier to its use in young children to date has been the lack of a widely available child-friendly formulation. A 100 mg levofloxacin scored dispersible taste-masked tablet has been developed (Macleods Pharmaceutical, Ltd, Mumbai, India), is WHO prequalified and available for procurement through the Global Drug Facility. However, the optimal dose for this dispersible levofloxacin formulation in children is uncertain. This is largely because of a marked observed difference in bioavailability (+41%) between the dispersible and crushed non-dispersible formulations in young children.6,7 Because other confounding factors (e.g. other TB drugs, HIV status, HIV treatment, formulation manipulation) could have contributed to the large observed difference previously reported,6 a rigorous comparative study is needed to establish the optimal and safe dose for this formulation. High-quality data on the bioavailability of this new dispersible levofloxacin formulation in children, and how this compares with the crushed non-dispersible tablet, are critically important to guide levofloxacin’s optimal routine clinical use in children.

The key pharmacokinetic features of levofloxacin include rapid absorption with a high bioavailability (>90%), limited metabolism and primary renal elimination.8

We compared the pharmacokinetics of the levofloxacin 100 mg dispersible tablets with the levofloxacin crushed 250 mg non-dispersible tablets in young HIV-negative children routinely receiving levofloxacin for RR-TB preventive therapy.

Methods

This was an open-label, randomized, crossover pharmacokinetic study to evaluate the bioavailability of dispersible and non-dispersible levofloxacin formulations, given according to a weight-banded dosing schedule, in child contacts of adults with RR-TB.

HIV-negative children under 6 years of age receiving RR-TB preventive therapy including levofloxacin for at least 2 weeks were eligible. Children were enrolled in representative age strata (groups 0 to <1, 1 to <2 and 2 to <6 years of age) and randomized 1:1 to receive one dose of either the dispersible or non-dispersible levofloxacin formulation on pharmacokinetic sampling Day 1, followed by the other formulation on pharmacokinetic sampling Day 2. On days other than pharmacokinetic sampling, children received their routine formulation and dose of levofloxacin as prescribed by their usual provider.

Weight was measured for dosing at the entry visit (1 day before the pharmacokinetic sampling Day 1) and was used to determine the dosing per body weight as close to 15–20 mg/kg for both formulations. Both study drug formulations, levofloxacin 100 mg dispersible tablets and levofloxacin 250 mg non-dispersible tablets, were from Macleods Pharmaceuticals (Mumbai, India).

On pharmacokinetic sampling days, children were fasted before receiving the levofloxacin dose administered by the study team. All doses were administered orally, and preparation methods were standardized between both formulations on the pharmacokinetic days for all participants, i.e. mixed in 2.5–5 mL of water and followed with the same amount of rinse, the non-dispersible tablet being crushed beforehand. A standard breakfast was offered to each child 1 h post dose, and concomitant medications were dosed at least 3 h post the levofloxacin dose.

On both pharmacokinetic sampling days, children were sampled at pre-dose (time 0), and then at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h after the observed levofloxacin dose. Levofloxacin assays were performed at the Division of Clinical Pharmacology, University of Cape Town, Cape Town, South Africa according to previously described methods.9 The lower limit of quantification was 0.0781 mg/L.

Non-linear mixed-effects modelling was conducted using NONMEM version 7.5 (Icon Development Solutions, Ellicott City, MD, USA). All statistical analyses, including summary statistics and visual displays, were generated in R (http://www.R-project.org/). Furthermore, Perl-speaks-NONMEM (PsN), the R package Xpose4 (http://xpose.sourceforge.net) and Pirana were used in the model-building process for data exploration, visualization and creation of diagnostics.10

An existing levofloxacin population pharmacokinetic model generated from two previous studies conducted in South Africa6,9 was used as a starting point for this analysis. The model structure was revisited and the model parameters re-estimated when fitting the new data. Several structural models were tested for levofloxacin: one- and two-compartment disposition with first-order elimination, and first-order absorption, with and without absorption lag time or transit compartments.11 The relative bioavailability was fixed to 1 for the dispersible formulation, and the non-dispersible formulation’s bioavailability was estimated with a fractional change from the former.

The pharmacokinetic samples collected pre-dose were treated as a separate occasion in the model, to allow estimation of both between-subject variability and between-occasion variability. A log-normal distribution was assumed for these random effects, and correlation between them was investigated both at between-subject and between-occasion variability level. The residual unexplained variability was described using a combined additive and proportional model. Concentrations reported as below the lower limit of quantification were handled by the method M3 by Beal.12

Covariate model building was based on scientific and biological plausibility, statistical significance and clinical relevance. The inclusion of covariate effects explaining the variability in the model was evaluated by considering several aspects, including statistically significant changes in the objective function value (OFV) (drops of 6.64 for one extra parameter were considered significant at P < 0.01 assuming a chi-squared distribution), improvement in goodness-of-fit plots and visual predictive checks, decrease in between-subject and/or between-occasion variability, improvement in the precision of parameter estimates, and model stability.

Allometric scaling was applied to all apparent clearance (clearance and intercompartmental clearance) and volume of distribution (central and peripheral) parameters to account for differences in body size,13 and a maturation model was used to describe the effect of age.14,15

Renal function was tested as a continuous covariate, given levofloxacin’s primary renal elimination.14,16,17 The creatinine clearance from the screening visit, and closest to the pharmacokinetic sampling days, if re-screened, was used.

The effect of levofloxacin formulation (dispersible versus non-dispersible) on absorption-related parameters describing extent (bioavailability) and time course of the absorption (mean transit time or absorption lag time and absorption rate constant) was evaluated. The potential difference in variability between the dispersible and non-dispersible tablets was also investigated.

Other covariates evaluated were sex, race, other TB preventive treatment (high-dose isoniazid and ethambutol) and other concomitant medications.

Simulations

Monte Carlo simulations were performed using the final model. We compared the exposure in different weight bands with the exposure in adults receiving currently recommended dosing.

The simulations were performed using an in silico population with values of weight/height/age consistent with the target population of children needing TB treatment or prevention:18 86 271 children (weight 3–24 kg) from 0 to 14 years of age. We explored the simulated exposure across different weight bands as suggested in the WHO guidelines and suggested necessary adjustments.

We aimed to achieve similar exposures compared to adults on RR-TB treatment receiving the recommended levofloxacin lower dose of 750 mg,19 i.e. an observed AUC of 101 mg h/L. In addition, the simulated maximum concentrations (Cmax) were compared with the observed Cmax levels from the same study for adults receiving the recommended upper dose of 1000 mg.19

Finally, doses for each formulation were identified separately, considering any difference observed in the absorption or bioavailability parameters in the crushed non-dispersible and dispersible levofloxacin formulations, while considering practical implications (splitting in half, quarters, functional scoring).

We used current 2022 WHO weight bands for all TB drugs for ease of implementation considering current dosing guidelines. Formerly optimized dose suggestions for the dispersible9 and non-dispersible6 formulations were used as a starting point for selected doses.

Ethics

The study was approved by Stellenbosch University (M20/02/004), the South African Health Product Regulatory Authority, and the WHO Ethical Review Committee. Written informed consent was obtained from parents/legal guardians.

Results

Twenty-five children were included with a median (IQR) weight and age of 12.2 (10.7–15.0) kg and 2.56 (1.58–4.03) years, respectively. All children were HIV negative and had normal renal function. A total of 286 samples were available for analysis. Only one pre-dose sample was below the limit of quantification of the assay. Seven samples from two children were excluded: the first due to an abnormal pharmacokinetic profile inconsistent with the dose time, and the other due to vomiting of the study drug.

A two-compartment model with first-order elimination and transit compartment absorption described the pharmacokinetics of levofloxacin. Using bi-compartmental disposition was significantly better than mono-compartmental [change in the objective function value (ΔOFV) was 60.7; 2 degrees of freedom (df); P = 0.0001]. The inclusion of transit compartments to describe absorption improved the model fit significantly (ΔOFV = 103; 3 df; P = 0.0001). However, because the estimation of the number of transit compartments was not stable it was fixed. The inclusion of allometric scaling using weight best adjusted for the effect of body size (ΔOFV = 39.2). Using fat-free mass20 instead of weight did not provide any meaningful further benefit. The typical clearance in a 12 kg child was estimated at 4.17 L/h. Maturation parameters could be estimated, and further improved model fit (ΔOFV = 37.2; 2 df; P = 0.0001); clearance was predicted to reach half its mature value at ∼4 months of age and full maturation at ∼2 years of age.

A significant difference between the bioavailability of the dispersible and non-dispersible formulations was detected. After including an effect of formulation on bioavailability the model improved significantly (ΔOFV = 11.7; 1 df; P = 0.0001), with the crushed non-dispersible levofloxacin formulation having a decreased bioavailability of −21.5% (95% CI: −31.4% to −11.7%) compared with the dispersible formulation. No significant differences were detected for the absorption parameters or variability. To allow for a robust assessment of bioavailability and absorption between the study formulations, the routine formulation was initially modelled separately, and parameters were fixed to values previously reported.9

No effect of renal function, sex or race could be detected. Even though the data were not fully powered to evaluate the effect of other TB preventive therapy (high-dose isoniazid, ethambutol) and concomitant medications (pyridoxine), these effects were investigated.

After adjustment and testing of all covariates mentioned above, the model still identified a large between-occasion variability in mean transit time, and a moderate between-occasion variability in the absorption rate constant and bioavailability. Graphical analysis of between-occasion variability in mean transit time revealed that a log-normal distribution may not be optimal to describe the variability and so a Box–Cox transformation was attempted. The Box–Cox transformation improved the model non-significantly with no improvement in diagnostic plots, and therefore the former log-normal distribution for mean transit time was retained. The model significantly improved (ΔOFV = 23.6; 1 df; P = 0.0001) when the estimate of the greater variability (∼3.24-fold) in bioavailability of the routine levofloxacin doses was allowed, which comprises 27 pre-dose concentrations (two patients did not have consecutive-day sampling of study-drug pharmacokinetics).

Simulated exposure

The final model was used to simulate the exposures achieved in children with different weights and ages receiving either the dispersible or non-dispersible levofloxacin tablets, and these were compared with the exposure (AUC0-24 of 101 mg h/L) observed in adults.

Firstly, exposures achieved with the current 2022 WHO-recommended weight-banded dosing were compared with the adult exposures at a 750 mg dose. The resulting exposures across WHO-specific weight bands are shown in Figure 2. These simulations show that the exposures achieved for the dispersible and non-dispersible tablets are suboptimal across all weights except in some children (<6 months), in which maturation of clearance is still incomplete.

Discussion

We found a 21.5% reduction in bioavailability when levofloxacin is administered with the crushed non-dispersible tablets as opposed to Macleod’s levofloxacin dispersible tablets. This supports the use of dispersible tablets in children at the current recommended dose, thus obtaining bioavailability and exposures more in line with adults.

A recent study showed considerably higher levofloxacin exposures in children receiving a new dispersible levofloxacin formulation compared with the non-dispersible tablets. Given the importance of levofloxacin in current WHO-recommended RR-TB treatment and preventive regimens, further exploration of the impact of formulations on levofloxacin exposure in children was needed. In this study, we attempted to keep the population of RR-TB-exposed children homogeneous, minimizing known and possible other confounding factors on levofloxacin pharmacokinetics, i.e. HIV-negative status, using the same drug brand, no nasogastric tube use, and by using similar preparation and administration methods. The difference observed in bioavailability was smaller (−21.5%) than the value of −41.3% previously observed in children receiving the adult levofloxacin tablets compared with 24 children receiving the dispersible Macleod’s levofloxacin formulation.6 The smaller difference observed in the current study is reassuring, especially given that most children affected by RR-TB live in resource-limited settings, where access to the dispersible paediatric formulation may be limited and where the use of crushed non-dispersible adult levofloxacin tablets is still common.

Exposures achieved using the Macleod’s dispersible tablets in the current study were lower compared with those observed in the 24 children receiving the same formulation in the previous study (60.0 versus 89.3 mg h/L).6 The demographics of the study participants in both studies were comparable: all were HIV-negative and on RR-TB preventive treatment, with a median (IQR) age and weight of 2.1 (1.2–2.7) years and 12.3 (8.6–13.5) kg, respectively. All received the Macleod’s dispersible formulation and four children received their levofloxacin dose via a nasogastric tube on the pharmacokinetic day. One difference compared with this study was that children received levofloxacin as a single drug for RR-TB prevention and not in combination with other routine RR-TB preventive drugs as in the previous pharmacokinetic study,6,7 including high-dose isoniazid and ethambutol. Our study was not powered to evaluate the effect of a possible drug–drug interaction between the other RR-TB preventive drugs and levofloxacin. Also, no such interactions were previously described. This should be formally evaluated in future studies. However, it may be advantageous to dose levofloxacin as stand-alone treatment for RR-TB prevention, as opposed to giving it in combination with other antituberculosis drugs.

The model best fitting the data in our study was a two-compartment model with first-order elimination and a transit compartment absorption. Previous population models in adults have used a one-compartment21–23 or two-compartment24 model to describe the pharmacokinetics of levofloxacin. For children, previous models have included both one-compartment25 and two-compartment6,9 models with first-order elimination and absorption, and inclusion of an absorption-lag time.9 In our model, we included the effect of allometry and maturation, which were found to be important descriptors of body size and function, similar to previous results.6,9 Our study included four children aged ≤1 year, one of whom was only 8 weeks old, which enabled the description of maturation despite the limited sample size. Maturation parameters were in line with previous reports,6,9 with clearance reaching half its mature value at ∼4 months (∼13 months post-menstrual age), as opposed to ∼2 months of age (∼11 months post-menstrual age). Our parameter values and model structure were comparable to previous reports in children (clearance of 4.70 L/h,9 versus 4.17 L/h), except for using a transit compartment instead of a lag time, which provided a better model fit when describing absorption.

Dosing simulations show that current WHO-recommended dosing for the dispersible and non-dispersible tablets results in median exposures below adult-equivalent exposures at a 750 mg daily dose, in all children except in some <6 months of age (i.e. those with incomplete maturation function). To achieve adult-equivalent exposures at a 750 mg daily dose, higher levofloxacin doses of 16–30 mg/kg for dispersible and 20–38 mg/kg for crushed non-dispersible tablets may be required in children >6 months. Routine clinical practice is to keep the dose range for levofloxacin between 15 and 20 mg/kg, which is associated with an acceptable safety profile and good clinical outcome.6,26 However, optimizing treatment exposure further may result in improved treatment outcomes, including potential shorter treatment duration, while maintaining an acceptable safety profile. We recommend an optimized age- and weight-banded dosing regimen within the current WHO international weight-banded framework, specific to the crushed 250 mg non-dispersible and 100 mg dispersible tablet formulations, which are likely to achieve adult exposure concentrations.

A limitation of the study was that children received routine levofloxacin until 1 day before the first intensive pharmacokinetic sampling day. This means that the first pre-dose sample was based on the routine and not on any of the study formulations. This has been addressed and tested when modelling the data. In the final model additional variability in the pre-dose samples associated with the routine levofloxacin was implemented. A second limitation is that due to the limited amount of blood that can ethically be drawn from small children, the sampling schedule may not have allowed robust characterization of the fast absorption of levofloxacin in some of the participants, because the first sample after the dose was the Cmax already. However, although this would represent a significant issue if the data were analysed with non-compartmental analysis, non-linear mixed-effects modelling is known to be robust with sparse pharmacokinetic sampling and we tested for potential differences between formulations in speed of absorption.

In summary, we observed a decrease of 21.5% in bioavailability and exposure when using the crushed adult non-dispersible levofloxacin tablet compared with the new child-friendly 100 mg dispersible tablet, when dosed as part of routine RR-TB preventive treatment in young children. This finding favours the use of the child-friendly dispersible compound with consideration of dosing, given the higher exposure. The exposures we observed for the dispersible levofloxacin were not as high as previously reported where levofloxacin was given as stand-alone treatment. Further studies are needed to exclude possible drug–drug interactions with other RR-TB preventive treatment, which includes high-dose isoniazid and ethambutol. We propose new optimized dosing, based on different levofloxacin formulations.
