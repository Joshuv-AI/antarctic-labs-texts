A computational model of radial scaffolds in the human fetal brain based on MRI
Authors: Fumitaka Homae, Ernst Schwartz, Daisuke Tsuzuki, Hirotaka Gima, Hama Watanabe, Daniela Prayer, Gregor Kasprian, Georg Langs, Gentaro Taga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe organization of the human cerebral cortex during fetal development is regulated by multiple processes, including neurogenesis and neuronal migration, which are essential for cortical expansion and folding patterns. According to the radial unit hypothesis, neurons originate in the germinal zone and migrate radially along the radial glial scaffolds to reach the cortical plate. However, the spatial distributions of these scaffolds and their roles in cortical folding remain unclear. Consequently, a computational model was developed to simulate virtual scaffolds extending from the cerebral ventricular surface to the white matter, incorporating region-specific neurogenic potential. The results demonstrate dense scaffold distribution in the perisylvian region, where complex cortical folding emerges, suggesting that differences in scaffold distribution contribute to region-specific cortical expansion. Notably, increased neuron influx along the scaffolds in the perisylvian region may contribute to early volumetric growth, potentially influencing Sylvian fissure formation. These findings align with previous reports that demonstrate distinct developmental patterns in this region. Being an accurate representation of radial migration pathways, this model provides a framework for integrating tangential migration of inhibitory neurons, refining scaffold distribution estimates, and quantifying early cortical development, offering insights into neurogenetic regional variations, scaffold architecture, and cortical folding in the human fetal brain.

Introduction

The human brain undergoes significant development in volume and cortical surface area during the fetal period (Prayer et al. 2006; Kostovic et al. 2019). This cortical expansion alters brain morphology by driving gyrification and sulcation (Ronan and Fletcher 2015). According to the radial unit hypothesis (Rakic 2007), two primary factors contribute to this transformation: the proliferation of neuroepithelial stem cells (NSCs) and the generation of neurons. The former increases the number of radial columns, expanding the cortical surface area, whereas the latter enhances cortical thickness by adding more cells within each column. The extent of neurogenesis varies across brain regions; in ferrets, more neurons are generated in the lateral than the medial cortical surface and in the anterior than the posterior regions (McSherry and Smart 1986). A similar pattern was observed in neural stem cell density in the adult mouse brain (for a review, see Fiorelli et al. 2015). These regional variations in neurogenesis possibly play a crucial role in shaping both the structural and functional organization of the brain.

Neurons do not originate in the regions where they ultimately function, but arise specific proliferative regions, including the germinal zone near the lateral ventricles (LV) and ganglionic eminence (GE). Excitatory neurons are primarily generated in the germinal zone, whereas inhibitory interneurons originate from both the germinal zone and GE. After generation, excitatory neurons and tangentially migrating interneurons from the GE to the ventricular zone (VZ) of the isocortex must undergo radial migration from deeper regions such as the VZ and subventricular zone (SVZ), moving outward from the periventricular regions to the cortical plate (CP) beneath the pial surface, covering a distance of more than 3,500 μm (Rakic 2003). This extensive migration is facilitated by the radial glial (RG) cells, which serve as scaffolds for neuronal movement.

Two distinct types of RG cells have been identified in the human fetal brain, both of which function as radial glial scaffolds for neuronal migration (Rakic 1972; Bystron et al. 2008). The ventricular radial glia (vRG), located in the VZ, extend processes that maintain continuous connections between the ventricular and pial surfaces, whereas the outer radial glia (oRG), situated in the SVZ, project only to the pial surface (Hansen et al. 2010; Nowakowski et al. 2016; Ostrem et al. 2017). Neurons elongate their leading processes, attach to the RG fibers, and regulate their nuclear translocation to migrate upward. During this process, they can occasionally alter their migration trajectory and switch between the RG fibers horizontally (Tabata and Nakajima 2003). Moreover, their movement can be obstructed by structural barriers, such as lenticular nuclei of the basal ganglia (BG), which lie between the germinal zone and the CP of the insular cortex (Mallela et al. 2023). This dynamic radial migration occurs predominantly during the mid-fetal period, peaking around gestational week (GW) 20 (Kostovic 2020).

Adjacent RG scaffolds in the germinal zone also maintain close connections on the cortical surface. However, similar to the ribs of a folding fan converging at a pivot in the germinal zone, the RG fibers can extend outward over a certain angular range. The variation in RG cell density across the germinal zone suggests that different regions may support distinct scaffold arrangements, potentially influencing neuronal migration. Indeed, the density of RG cells within the germinal zone varies depending on location. Despite spatial constraints in the VZ limiting the potential for further increases in vRG cells, the number of oRG cells in the SVZ can be increased, enabling more scaffolds to extend toward the cortical surface. Consequently, these oRG-derived scaffolds further enhance neuronal migration, promoting increased neuron influx into the developing CP and contributing to cortical surface expansion. These RG-rich regions are located beneath the prospective gyri (Reillo et al. 2011; Nowakowski et al. 2016).

With advancements in diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), fibers extending radially and coherently from the germinal zone to the cortical surface have been successfully traced in human fetuses between 13 and 31 GW (Huang et al. 2009; Takahashi et al. 2012; Mitter et al. 2015; Khan et al. 2019; Charvet et al. 2020). When Fractional anisotropy (FA), a measure of water diffusion along coherently aligned neural fibers, was analyzed and overlaid onto fetal cortical images, the values within the CP reveal an overall decrease from 13 to 21 GW (Huang et al. 2013), likely reflecting processes, such as synapse formation, dendritic arborization, and neuronal differentiation. However, the perisylvian region, which includes the frontal, temporal, and parietal regions near the Sylvian fissure, retained relatively high FA values even at 21 GW. Similarly, high FA values were reported in the medial frontal region at 20 GW (Yu et al. 2016). These findings, supported by DTI and histological studies, suggest that the prospective perisylvian cortical surface receives a relatively large number of RG scaffolds and that the spatial distribution of scaffolds is not homogeneous across the developing brain.

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying fetal cortical development and the spatial distribution of scaffolds in the human brain, this study aimed to construct a model of virtual scaffolds (VSs; Fig. 1) that connect the LV to the inner surface of the CP (hereafter referred to as the white matter [WM] surface). In this model, the spatial distribution of the VSs was designed to simulate the pattern of neurogenesis, characterized by lateral > medial regions: anterior > posterior regions. This approach enabled the assessment of whether such a distribution reflects an increased number of scaffolds in the perisylvian region, as reported in fetal FA maps from DTI studies. This model will provide a framework for predicting the spatial distribution of NSCs and RG cells generated in the germinal zones of the human fetal brain, representing the protomap of the human brain.

Four assumptions are made to construct and evaluate the proposed model. First, approximately 20 GW is an appropriate period for investigating the distribution of the scaffolds. Accordingly, structural MRIs of four fetuses aged 19 GWs and 2 d (19 + 2 GW) to 22 + 3 GW were used. Second, VSs are not necessarily perpendicular to the WM surface. Third, the number of NSCs and RG cells, as well as their capacity for cell generation, varies across brain regions. To account for these regional differences, two functions were implemented: (i) a spatially uniform distribution on the LV surface (Model 1), and (ii) a distribution dependent on the mediolateral and anteroposterior coordinates of the LV surface (Model 2). Finally, in the fetal brain, the BG prevents the VSs from passing through. The resulting surface map, representing the spatial distribution of VSs on the WM surface, was then compared with previously reported features of the fetal brain.

Materials and methods

MRI data

Four in vivo fetal MRI scans were employed (gestational ages: 19 + 2, 20 + 0, 21 + 1, and 22 + 3 wk). MRI scans were performed using a 1.5 T (Intera, Philips Medical Systems, Best, the Netherlands) without maternal or fetal sedation. T2-weighted, single-shot, fast spin-echo images, with 3.0 to 4.0 mm slice thickness and 0.78 to 1.17 mm in-plane resolution, were obtained using repetition time (TR) of 6,729 to 15,987 ms, echo time (TE) of 100 to 140 ms in the axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, using the brainstem as a reference. The scans were reconstructed into a single 0.5 mm isotropic volume for each fetus. All data were segmented into seven regions: gray matter, WM, BG/thalamus, lower brainstem, cerebellum, LV, and outside the brain (cerebrospinal fluid, skull, and soft matter). MRI data processing and segmentation methods have been described previously (Schwartz et al. 2021). The study was approved by the local institutional review boards of the Medical University of Vienna and the University of Tokyo. Consent forms from the legal guardians were waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Procedures

Surface meshes of the WM, BG/thalamus, and LV were generated from the segmented MRI (Fig. 2) and spatially smoothed using SurfIce software (Rorden 2025; https://www.nitrc.org/projects/surfice/) and SurfaceSmooth (Lalancette 2023; https://www.mathworks.com/matlabcentral/fileexchange/45416-surfacesmooth). The mean number of WM faces is 112,792.5 (80,624, 103,226, 105,270, and 162,050), and that of LV faces is 31,602 (23,166, 29,590, 28,762, and 44,890). First, for each LV face, an outward normal vector was determined and drawn at the face’s center of gravity (Fig. 1A and Fig. 2). Lines were drawn from the center of each face of the LV mesh to the centers of all WM faces within the same hemisphere (Fig. 1B). The angles (θs) between the normal vector of each LV face and that of each WM face were calculated (Fig. 1C). We hypothesized that the strength of the VS (hereafter, VS values) is determined by the product of three types of weights. The angle θ defines the first weight (Wθ), modeled as a normal distribution with mean 0° and standard deviation 25°. Accordingly, Wθ = 1 at θ = 0°, ie when the LV and WM faces are parallel, Wθ = 0.5 at θ = 29.4°, and Wθ ≈ 0 at θ = 90°. The second weight (Wa) is the area of a given LV face divided by the sum of the areas of all LV faces. The third weight (Wc) is defined as a function of the coordinates of an LV face in the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space. Results

When the VSs with the highest 0.01% values in Model 1 were selected and drawn, the occipital and ventral temporal regions received VSs in all fetuses. At a threshold of 0.025%, VSs appeared in the anterior frontal and medial regions (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Figs. 1–3). At a threshold of 0.02%, VSs appeared in the anterior frontal and medial regions. As the threshold decreased, the regions receiving VSs continuously expanded from the above regions. The medial regions exhibited high total VS values (Fig. 4). In these total VS maps, high values were also observed in the posterior temporal and parietal regions, which did not receive the highest VSs (Fig. 3) but received moderate or relatively lower VSs from many LV faces, so that summations of the values were high. These patterns were consistently reproduced in all fetuses (Supplementary Figs. 4–6). However, total VS values in the ventral and insular regions were relatively low.

In Model 2, high-value VSs appeared in the anterior frontal and medial frontal regions (Fig. 5). Almost all lateral temporal regions showed high values, and these regions extended to the temporoparietal junction in the three older fetuses (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figs. 7–9). These spatial configurations were reproduced in the VS map (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Figs. 10–12). The values in the frontal, temporal, and parietal regions surrounding the Sylvian fissure, namely the perisylvian and medial frontal regions, were high. Although the total VS values in the medial and parietal regions were high in both models, the high values in the perisylvian region were specific to Model 2.

When the BG/thalamus was not assumed to interfere with VS formation, the total VS values were high not only in the perisylvian region, but also in the posterior insula (Supplementary Fig. 13). Moreover, we calculated the shape index (Koenderink and Van Doorn 1992) and curvedness (Lefèvre et al. 2016) of the WM surface of the fetuses (Fig. 7). In Models 1 and 2, we calculated the Spearman’s ρ between these shape metrics and the total VS values (Supplementary Table 1). The acquired ρ values were relatively low, although the P values were nearly 0, likely due to the high degrees of freedom. We also evaluated the bivariate Moran’s I (Czaplewski and Reich 1993; Lee 2001) between total VS values and the above shape metrics. Moran’s I was positive for the shape index and negative for curvedness. Importantly, the absolute values of Moran’s I were lower than or close to 0.2, indicating a weak spatial relationship between total VS values and the shape metrics; the results were comparable between the two models. These findings demonstrate that convex regions exhibited higher total VS values than concave regions, irrespective of the model, and that the total VS values were determined not by the local unevenness of the WM surface, but by the spatial relationship between the WM and LV surfaces.

Discussion

In this study, VSs were projected from the LV surface to the WM surface. When assuming a uniform generative ability of the VSs across the LV surface (Model 1), the total VS values exhibited regional dependency, with higher values observed in the medial and parietal regions. This is because the medial surface and atrium of the LV are nearly parallel to the WM surfaces of the medial and parietal regions, respectively, minimizing the angles between the normal vectors of the LV faces and the VS projection directions. This results in higher weights and, consequently, higher total VS values. In Model 2, the perisylvian regions, in addition to the medial regions, exhibited high total VS values. The results of both models were largely symmetrical, with minimal hemispheric differences. Furthermore, surface maps generated from four fetal brains at approximately 20 GW showed consistent patterns, reinforcing the robustness of these findings.

The results for the perisylvian region in Model 2 align with those of previous studies of FA maps in fetuses (Huang et al. 2013; Yu et al. 2016). The FA values in these studies primarily represented fibers in the CP that were coherently aligned in the radial direction. Although regions near the subplate (SP) and intermediate zone (IZ) border exhibited low FA values (Huang et al. 2013; Khan et al. 2019), the IZ itself showed a subsequent increase in FA values (Huang et al. 2013). Given that RG scaffolds extend through the IZ to reach the SP and CP, FA maps of the CP likely reflect not only radial neuronal fibers within the CP but also RG scaffolds spanning from the VZ/SVZ to the pial surface. This overlap may explain the consistency between this current model and the FA map findings, suggesting that this model effectively represents RG scaffolds in the fetal brain.

The SP in the perisylvian region remains thick in fetuses between 20 and 32 GW (Corbett-Detig et al. 2011; Vasung et al. 2016). This study’s results suggested that the perisylvian region receives a substantial number of scaffolds from the germinal zone, facilitating the migration of numerous neurons into the SP. These neurons may consequently attract thalamocortical and corticocortical fibers, contributing to the formation of a thick layer in this region. The Sylvian fissure initially appeared as a simple depression at the onset of cortical development (Kostovic and Vasung 2009; Kostovic et al. 2019). As the posterior operculum, comprising the temporal and parietal lobes develops to cover the insula, it forms a posterior Sylvian fissure before the anterior operculum undergoes further growth (Chen et al. 1995).

Mallela et al. (2020) reported relative volumetric changes primarily characterized by volume expansion in the gyri surrounding the Sylvian fissure. Closure of the Sylvian fissure begins with a volume increase in the supramarginal gyrus at approximately 23 GW. Between 23 and 25 GW, the posterior part of the fissure gradually closes. This volume expansion is likely to drive changes in the surface morphology, as indicated by surface curvature measurements. Notably, the concavity of these regions increased significantly, reflecting the folding changes that the Sylvian fissure experienced up to 26 GW (Habas et al. 2012). Even beyond 30 GW, the relative rate of cortical expansion remained pronounced at the posterior end of the Sylvian fissure (Garcia et al. 2018). The regional specificity of cortical folding, as reported in previous studies, may be explained by the scaffold distribution predicted by our model.

The mechanisms underlying the emergence of left–right asymmetry in the Sylvian fissure during early development remain unclear. In the adult brain, the posterior ascending ramus of the Sylvian fissure extends further backward in the left hemisphere, possibly because of the larger supramarginal gyrus, Heschl’s gyrus, and planum temporale compared to the right hemisphere (Lyttelton et al. 2009). This asymmetry has also been observed in preterm infants, in whom opercularization is more pronounced in the left hemisphere (De Vareilles et al. 2023). Such hemispheric differences may arise from asymmetries in the generative abilities of NSCs and RG cells within the VZ/SVZ beneath the perisylvian region. This hypothesis can be tested in future studies by modifying the current model parameters to account for potential left–right differences in scaffold distribution. The modified model might contribute to deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying the earlier emergence of the right superior temporal sulcus (Kasprian et al. 2011), and the neurobiological foundation of language acquisition, which is differentially organized in the left and right perisylvian language networks (Homae et al. 2006; Mahmoudzadeh et al. 2013; Homae et al. 2014; Friederici et al. 2017).

Model 2 assumed that the weight values reflecting the generative abilities of NSCs and RG cells were region-dependent, with higher values concentrated in the anterolateral corners of the ventricles. To our knowledge, the spatial distribution of generative abilities in human fetuses has not been fully characterized. Mapping the FA values of the radial components in the VZ/SVZ onto the LV surface might provide insights into some aspects of their spatial distribution. Using such FA map of the ventricles would allow for evaluating assumptions and refining the weight functions in future studies, potentially reflecting a protomap of the human brain.

The heterogeneity of the developing LV surface parallels the modular gene expression patterns observed in gyrencephalic species. In ferrets, many differentially expressed genes (DEGs) exhibit modular patterns, characterized by alternating blocks of high and low expression levels (de Juan Romero et al. 2015; Llinares-Benadero and Borrell 2019) located beneath the prospective primary gyri and sulci. While periodic folding across the cortex is at least partially driven by these DEG-based patterns, regionalization of the entire cortex is likely influenced by more graded morphogen activity.

The morphogen concentration depends on its diffusion from the signaling source, and cells exposed to different morphogen levels exhibit differential behaviors (Rogers and Schier 2011). Three classical morphogen signaling centers have been identified: the cortical haem, antihaem, and anterior neural ridge (Tiberi et al. 2012). Signals from the cortical haem and anterior neural ridge establish concentration gradients along the medial-to-lateral and anterior-to-posterior axes, respectively. Moreover, the levels of retinoic acid exhibit a gradient along the anterior-to-posterior axis of the developing cortex (Shibata et al. 2021). The findings from Model 2 suggest that some molecular signals exhibiting these gradient patterns may exist on the LV surface, serving as biological correlates of the VS weight distribution.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations; based on the findings of Mallela et al. (2023), BG was assumed to obstruct RG scaffold elongation in these current models. When this assumption was removed from the analysis, the insula received VSs and exhibited a high total VS value (Supplementary Fig. 13). This result is consistent with the observations of Mallela et al. (2023), who reported that neurons in the insula originate from the lateral GE rather than the LV. However, the current model simplified the scaffold structures, representing VSs as multiple lines with a normal distribution for their weights (Wθ) rather than incorporating curvilinear fibers or actual neuronal migration pathways observed in human and ferret fetuses (Reillo et al. 2011; Nowakowski et al. 2016). This simplification may have influenced the assignment of the scaffolds to different brain regions. If the sigma value of the normal distribution in the present study were increased to (eg sigma = 30), the insula could receive some VSs from the LV faces, even when BG/thalamus obstruction was applied. The effect of this obstruction and the most appropriate sigma value should be investigated further in future studies.

This model primarily focuses on radial scaffolds that facilitate neuronal migration in the radial direction. However, while more than 65% of GABAergic neurons originate from the SVZ (Letinic et al. 2002; Rakic 2007) and migrate radially along scaffolds to the CP, a considerable proportion of inhibitory neurons arise in the GE, migrate tangentially to the SVZ or VZ (Kolasinski et al. 2013; Horton and Paredes 2025), and subsequently undergo radial migration toward the CP (Kriegstein and Noctor 2004). Because the volume of GE reaches its maximum at 21 GW (Stuempflen et al. 2023) and these GE-derived neurons contribute to CP thickness, the precise locations at which they switch from tangential to radial migration remain crucial for accurately modeling CP development. Although the current model was not rigidly restricted to the radial migration of excitatory neurons and incorporated scaffold use by inhibitory neurons migrating both radially and tangentially (Fig. 2C and D), it did not account for the pathways of tangential migration and transition points in the SVZ/VZ or MZ, where GE-derived inhibitory neurons shifted from tangential to radial migration. Given that the number of inhibitory neurons influences the cortical volume, the cortical surface morphology may also be affected by this factor. A curvilinear model of VS will enable the prediction of both radial and tangential migration pathways of excitatory and inhibitory neurons originating from the SVZ/VZ and GE.

Furthermore, normalization was not applied to the fetal brain to closely examine each fetus individually. While this approach is appropriate for capturing individual variations, which differ greatly depending on gestational age, it also presents challenges. Specifically, it limits the ability to represent common features across fetuses in a single image and complicates statistical analyses. Constructing a common reference frame for the human fetal brain (Schwartz et al. 2023) is a valuable step in refining our model and investigating developmental changes.

Ultimately, this current model provides an accurate representation of radial migration pathways and a framework that can be expanded to incorporate the tangential migration of inhibitory neurons and refine scaffold distribution estimates. It offers a quantitative framework for early cortical development and invaluable insights into regional variations in neurogenesis, scaffold architecture, and cortical folding in the human fetal brain.
