Divergent immunometabolic landscapes of chicken and swan goose identify SERPINF2 as a novel restriction factor for influenza A virus
Authors: Fangbing Li, Chenyang Ren, Wanlin Liu, Xin Yu, Jindan Yu, Yunfeng Ma, Yali Feng, Ying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As the natural hosts of avian influenza virus (AIV), wild birds generally exhibit a more tolerant immune response to AIV infection compared to domestic poultry. However, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, partly due to limited access to wildlife biological samples imposed by animal protection principles. To investigate these differential immune responses, we established a peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) infection platform. PBMCs from domestic chickens and swan geese were infected with H9N2 AIV, and label-free quantitative comparative proteomics was performed to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between the two species, aiming to uncover potential host antiviral factors. Proteomic analysis revealed that chicken PBMCs showed marked activation of antiviral defense and inflammatory pathways, accompanied by upregulation of oxidative stress-related pathways. In contrast, swan goose PBMCs predominantly activated pathways associated with cellular integrity maintenance, metabolic homeostasis, and RNA surveillance. Subsequently, 12 DEP candidates with high expression fold-changes in swan goose PBMCs were selected and expressed in DF-1 cells to evaluate their antiviral activities. Among these, only SERPINF2, a member of the serine protease inhibitor family, significantly inhibited H9N2 AIV replication in DF-1 cells. Mechanistically, SERPINF2 suppressed viral replication by specifically inhibiting the cleavage of the viral hemagglutinin (HA) protein, thereby reducing the production of infectious progeny virions. This study reveals distinct immune response patterns to H9N2 AIV infection in chickens and swan geese, and identifies SERPINF2 as a novel restriction factor against H9N2 AIV, providing new insights into the antiviral mechanisms in natural AIV hosts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-026-03208-z.

Introduction

Wild birds, serve as the primary natural reservoir of avian influenza viruses (AIVs) [1]. Notably, migratory wild birds spread influenza viruses globally through ecological behaviors such as migration. In contrast to most hosts that display severe clinical manifestations, wild birds exhibit minimal or no symptoms during a AIV infections, with the exception of certain highly pathogenic H5N1 strains—an outcome of their prolonged co-evolution with these viruses [2]. Similarly, a parallel phenomenon is observed between chicken and ducks. While infection with HPAI H5N1 (A/Vietnam/3046/04) induces fulminant systemic disease in chickens, resulting in 100% mortality within 72 h and widespread high-titer viral dissemination, ducks exhibit subclinical infection with restricted viral replication limited to the trachea and cloaca [3–5]. Studies have found that the differential clinical outcomes are related to species-specific innate immune responses, particularly the expression and functionality of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as retinoic acid-inducible gene-I (RIG-I) and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5) [3, 6]. Innate immune cells employ PRRs to detect viral pathogens by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns [7]. Ducks mount rapid and potent early antiviral responses through RIG-I, which is key to their tolerance to HPAI infection. In contrast, the absence of RIG-I in chickens forces them to rely on the comparatively less efficient MDA5 pathway, resulting in both delayed interferon responses and uncontrolled inflammation, ultimately leading to high mortality rates [6]. This difference in immune response may reflect the different evolutionary paths formed by waterfowl and land fowl under long-term viral pressure. Understanding why wild birds often remain asymptomatic during AIV infection is crucial for elucidating broader host–virus coevolution dynamics. This study aims to address this gap by investigating the shared and distinct immune response mechanisms to AIV in wild and domesticated birds.

The hemagglutinin (HA) protein of influenza viruses plays an essential role in their life cycle. Influenza virus infection is initiated by HA binding to cellular receptors, followed by fusion of the viral membrane with the endosomal membrane [8]. The influenza virus HA protein is synthesized as an inactive precursor, HA0, in infected cells. Only after cleavage by host cell proteases into the fusion-competent HA1/HA2 complex can it induce membrane fusion. The virus becomes infectious only upon budding and cleavage of HA0 [9]. Host organisms counteract this process through serine protease inhibitors, such as SERPINE1, which inhibit the cleavage of HA0 into HA1/HA2 by host proteases, thereby constituting an important innate immune defense against influenza virus proliferation in mammalian host [9, 10]. The efficiency and specificity of this regulatory mechanism may largely determine the level of viral replication and the outcome of infection in different hosts.

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), comprising lymphocytes and monocytes, play critical roles in immune defense, surveillance, and regulation against pathogens and malignancies, thereby serving as a representative model for systemic host–pathogen interaction [11]. Due to their capacity to reflect systemic immunological changes while offering experimental feasibility, PBMCs have been widely employed as a responsive model for studying host immune reactions to various viral infections, including COVID-19 and human cytomegalovirus [12, 13].

In this study, we used PBMCs isolated from chickens and swan geese (Anser cygnoides) to investigate the innate immune response to avian influenza virus (AIV) infection in wild versus domestic birds. By analyzing differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) during the early stages of infection, we aimed to identify the molecular signatures that underlie the divergent immune strategies between these two species.

Materials and methods

Cells, virus, and plasmids

Madin-Darby canine kidney (MDCK) cells and chicken embryo fibroblasts (UMNSAH/DF-1, DF-1) were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM). MDCK cells were supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Cellmax, Beijing, China), and DF-1 cells were supplemented with 10% FBS. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from chickens and swan geese were isolated using species-specific lymphocyte separation kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 39 °C with 5% CO₂. The H9N2 avian influenza virus strain A/chicken/Liaoning/07/2016 (CKLN/07, GenBank: MW255942-MW255949) [14], isolated and preserved by our laboratory, was used in this study. The plasmid pCAGGS-flag-C was purchased from Ouke Gene.

Viral replication analysis

PBMCs (1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 96-well plates) were infected with CKLN/07 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1 for 1 h at 37 °C. After infection, cells were washed twice with PBS and cultured in DMEM medium containing 0.5 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin. Cell pellets and cell culture supernatantswere harvested for viral RNA quantification, and cell culture supernatants were collected for TCID₅₀ assay at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 h post-infection (hpi). Viral M gene copy numbers were determined by one-step qRT-PCR (Vazyme Biotech, China).

Virus adsorption and entry

All pre-infection procedures were performed on ice to prevent premature viral internalization. Overnight-cultured PBMCs (1 × 10⁶ cells per well in 96-well plates) were used. Culture medium was aspirated from each well, and cells were washed twice with ice-cold sterile PBS to remove residual medium and debris. Cells were infected with CKLN/07 at an MOI of 1, while the control group received an equal volume of DMEM basal medium. Plates were incubated on ice for 1 h to allow virus adsorption, with gentle rocking every 15 min to ensure uniform virus distribution.

After adsorption, all liquid was removed from the wells. Half of the wells were harvested immediately to quantify cell-bound virus. The remaining wells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS to remove unbound virus, then 100 µL of ice-cold virus maintenance medium containing 0.5 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin was added to each well. Plates were transferred to a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator to initiate viral entry. Cells were harvested at 90 min and 160 min post-incubation (three biological replicates per time point), transferred to 1.5 mL RNase-free centrifuge tubes, and stored at -80 °C until use. Viral RNA was extracted and AIV M gene levels were quantified by one-step qRT-PCR (Vazyme Biotech, China) under strict RNase-free conditions. Viral copy numbers were calculated by interpolating Ct values into a pre-generated standard curve, and viral internalization kinetics were analyzed by comparing viral copy numbers across different time points.

Viral gene replication

PBMCs were infected with CKLN/07 at an MOI of 1 as described above. Triplicate samples were harvested at 2, 12, and 24 hpi. Viral RNA was extracted under strict RNase-free conditions, reverse-transcribed using the influenza-specific U12 primer, and AIV M gene vRNA levels were quantified by qPCR (Vazyme Biotech, China). Viral copy numbers were calculated by interpolating Ct values into a pre-generated standard curve, and viral gene replication kinetics were analyzed by comparing viral copy numbers across different time points.

Viral protein expression and release assays

PBMCs were infected with CKLN/07 at an MOI of 1 as described above. Triplicate samples were harvested at 0, 2, 24, and 48 hpi. Uninfected control cells were processed in parallel. Viral NP protein expression was detected by Western blotting using anti-NP antibodies (SinoBiological, China) and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibodies (Beyotime, China). For viral release assays, both cell culture supernatants and corresponding cell pellets were collected separately at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 hpi. Viral RNA was extracted from each fraction, and AIV M gene RNA copy numbers were quantified by qRT-PCR as described above. Viral release rate was defined as the ratio of viral M gene RNA copy number in the supernatant to the total viral M gene RNA copy number (supernatant + cell pellet) at each individual time point.

Comparative proteomics

Proteins from infected and control PBMCs were digested and analyzed by tandem mass spectrometry (Thermo Scientific Q Exactive HF) in data-dependent acquisition mode. Raw data were processed using MaxQuant (v2.4.9.0) with label-free quantification (LFQ) and iBAQ. Protein identification was performed using the chicken (UniProt: Gallus gallus, taxonomy ID: 9031) and swan goose (UniProt: Anser cygnoides, taxonomy ID: 8845) databases, filtering out contaminants and reverse sequences. LFQ intensities were normalized and compared using the limma package in R (version 4.2.0). Homologous proteins between species were identified using NCBI BLASTp (lowest p-value matches). We identified differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in the 24 h and 48 h post-infection groups of chickens and swan geese using the criteria of |Log₂ (Fold Change) | > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05. Subsequently, GO (Gene Ontology) enrichment analysis and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway analysis were performed on the identified DEPs, respectively.

Screening of swan goose-specific differential proteins

Venn diagrams (https://cloud.oebiotech.com/) were used to identify differential proteins unique to swan geese. A total of 251 and 318 specific differential proteins were identified in swan geese at 24 and 48 hpi with CKLN/07, respectively. Cross-referencing with proteomics data, we screened for swan goose-specific differential proteins with |logFC| > 4 at both time points. After excluding proteins with no sequence, uncertain sequence, or incomplete sequence, 12 differential proteins were selected for subsequent experiments.

Eukaryotic expression vector construction and transfection

The eukaryotic expression vectors were constructed based on the nucleotide sequences of 24 selected differentially expressed protein genes from chickens and swan geese, which were retrieved and downloaded from NCBI. The vector backbone used was pCAGGS-flag-c (HonorGene, China). Each target gene fragment (for nucleotide sequences, see Supplementary Materials) was chemically synthesized and subsequently cloned into the vector between the Sac I and Mlu I restriction sites. All recombinant plasmids were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.

DF-1 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and incubated overnight at 39 °C with 5% CO₂. When cell confluence reached 70–90%, transfection was performed using Lipofectamine® LTX reagent (Invitrogen, China). After 6 h, the medium was replaced with 1 mL of 10% DMEM complete medium. Cells were harvested at 48 hpi, lysed, and subjected to Western blot to verify protein expression using anti-flag antibody (Abmart, China) as the primary antibody and HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beyotime, China) as the secondary antibody.

Overexpression assay and viral TCID50 determination

DF-1 cells were transfected with 22 protein plasmids and the pCAGGS-flag-C control vector. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with 1 mL of 10% DMEM complete medium. At 48 hpi, cells were infected with CKLN/07 at an MOI of 0.01. After virus adsorption for 1 h at 37 °C, the inoculum was removed, and cells were washed three times with pre-warmed PBS. Cells were then cultured in serum-free DMEM supplemented with 0.1 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin for the duration of the infection. Supernatants and cell pellets were collected at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60 and 72 hpi for TCID50 assay to determine viral titer and 24hpi for Western blot to detect NP protein expression.

HA cleavage assay

DF-1 cells were transfected with sgSERPINF2 and pCAGGS-flag-C. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with 1 mL of 10% DMEM complete medium. At 48 hpi, cells were infected with H9N2 A/chicken/Liaoning/07/2016 at an MOI of 0.01. After 1 h incubation at 37 °C, the supernatant was removed, and cells were washed three times with pre-warmed PBS. Cells were then cultured in serum-free DMEM supplemented with 0.1 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin for the duration of the infection. Cell pellets were collected at 12, 24, and 36 hpi for Western blot to detect HA protein expression and cleavage.

Infectivity restoration assay

DF-1 cells were transfected with SERPINF2-flag and pCAGGS-flag-C plasmids. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with 1 mL of 10% DMEM complete medium. At 24 h post-transfection, cells were infected with CKLN/07 at an MOI of 0.01. After 1 h of incubation at 37 °C, the medium was replaced with serum-free DMEM medium supplemented with 0.1 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin. Cell culture supernatants were collected at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 hpi. For the infectivity restoration assay, half of the duplicate samples from all collected supernatants were incubated with an additional 2 µg/mL TPCK-trypsin at 37 °C for 30 min to cleave the HA0 precursor protein and restore viral infectivity, followed by TCID₅₀ titration on MDCK cells. Viral titers were then determined for the SERPINF2-flag group, SERPINF2-flag trypsin rescue group, and pCAGGS-flag-C group.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the means ± standard deviations (SDs). Statistical analyses were performed using t-tests in GraphPad Prism software. P values indicate significant differences (*p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001); ns indicates no significant difference.

Results

Establishment of PBMCs AIV infection platform

We first confirmed that if H9N2 virus can replicate in the PBMCs of chicken and swan geese. PBMCs from the two species were infected with H9N2 AIV, CKLN/07, with a MOI of 1. The infected cells were harvested at different time points. The viral adsorption and invasion, viral gene replication, viral protein expression in chicken and swan geese PBMCs were compared sequentially.

H9N2 AIV can absorb and invade chicken and goose PBMCs (S1 Fig), and the virus copy number and TCID50 are decreasing with time (S2A,2B Fig), indicating that AIV cannot normally proliferate in these two PBMCs. Viral nucleoprotein (NP) was detected at 24 h and 48 h (HPI) after infection rather than within 2 h (Fig. 1), which confirmed that the NP protein of H9N2 avian influenza virus was expressed in chicken and goose PBMCs rather than simply ingested by viral protein. There was no difference in the adsorption and invasion efficiency of AIV infection between chicken and goose PBMCs (S1 Fig). The combination of TCID50 (S2B Fig), viral M gene replication (S2C Fig) and WB results (Fig. 1) showed that the replication efficiency of H9N2 avian influenza virus on PBMCs of the two hosts was similar and viral release was also comparable (S3 Fig). In conclusion, the H9N2 avian influenza virus abortion infection model can be used for subsequent comparative studies.

Proteomics reveals fundamentally divergent host response landscapes

Mass spectrometry-based proteomic analysis, conducted using thresholds of |Log₂(Fold Change)| > 1 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05, revealed significant differences in the quantity and dynamics of DEPs between infected chicken and swan goose PBMCs at 24 and 48 h post-infection (hpi). Following normalization and correction, the proteomic analysis revealed dynamic host responses. In chicken PBMCs, 65 DEPs were downregulated and 121 were upregulated at 24 hpi (Fig. 2A); these numbers increased to 147 downregulated and 224 upregulated DEPs by 48 hpi (Fig. 2B). In contrast, swan goose PBMCs exhibited 43 downregulated and 232 upregulated DEPs at 24 hpi (Fig. 2C), which further changed to 80 downregulated and 299 upregulated DEPs at 48 hpi (Fig. 2D).

Functional enrichment analysis defines high-cost inflammatory versus precision homeostatic defense strategies in chicken and swan geese

To elucidate the functional implications of the DEPs, we conducted GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses. These analyses revealed evolutionarily divergent immune strategies between the two species (S4 Fig).

In chickens, the response was marked by acute inflammatory activation, complement pathway engagement, and oxidative stress, indicative of a “high-cost” viral clearance strategy that may entail immunopathological consequences (Figs. 3A and B and 4A). Specifically, GO analysis showed that chicken PBMCs upregulated defense responses against viruses and Toll-like receptor 4 signaling at 24 hpi, followed by complement activation and interleukin-1β secretion at 48 hpi (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, strong NF-κB mediated responses coincided with downregulation of antioxidant enzymes, further underscoring the metabolically demanding and potentially self-damaging nature of this inflammatory strategy [15].

In stark contrast, swan geese deployed a “low-damage” strategy centered on maintaining cellular integrity and metabolic homeostasis. Their response emphasized cellular structure preservation, lipid metabolic balance, RNA surveillance, and immunoproteasome assembly (Figs. 3C and D and 4B). GO analysis indicated early enhancement of lipid transporter activity and autophagosome-lysosome fusion, supporting precise vesicular trafficking via SNARE complex recycling. Furthermore, swan geese reinforced cell–matrix adhesion through integrin-mediated signaling and suppressed actin cytoskeleton reorganization, which may physically impede viral dissemination. This structural stabilization occurred alongside PPAR-driven fatty acid degradation, alleviating lipotoxicity, and was coupled with sustained RIG-I helicase activity and mitochondrial membrane potential regulation, as a signature of metabolically integrated antiviral defense (Fig. 3C and D).

KEGG pathway analysis further delineated these adaptive contrasts. In chickens, activation of PPAR signaling and glycolysis was paradoxically accompanied by suppression of glutathione metabolism, fostering a pro-oxidative state. Concurrent upregulation of the proteasome and RNA degradation pathways reflected an energy-intensive effort to clear viral proteins. Nevertheless, persistent lipid peroxidation markers and ferroptosis pathway enrichment indicated unresolved oxidative stress, which could exacerbate tissue damage despite augmenting neutrophil extracellular trap formation (Fig. 4).

Conversely, swan geese optimized ribosome biogenesis, with preferential 28 S rRNA modification, and activated RNA surveillance pathways, while attenuating actin cytoskeleton reorganization. This coordinated adjustment supported precise antigen presentation through immunoproteasome assembly and selective protein translation, thereby minimizing off-target immune activation and collateral damage (Fig. 4B).

Together, these findings illustrate fundamentally distinct host adaptation strategies: chickens employ a high-energy, inflammation-driven clearance mechanism at the risk of self-injury, whereas swan geese adopt a finely tuned, homeostasis-preserving defense that limits viral spread without excessive inflammatory cost.

Screening and identification of the swan goose-specific antiviral factor SERPINF2

Based on the observed disparities in immune response strategies, we hypothesized that swan geese may express unique antiviral effector proteins. To identify such candidates, we performed Venn diagram analysis on the DEPs specific to swan geese following CKLN/07 infection, which identified 251 and 318 unique proteins at 24 and 48 hpi, respectively (S5 Fig). From this pool, we applied a stringent filter (|logFC| > 4 at both time points) and cross-referenced the results with proteomic data to prioritize high-confidence candidates (S6 Fig). After excluding proteins with missing or incomplete sequences, 12 proteins were selected for further functional validation (S1 Table). Corresponding expression plasmids for both the swan goose and chicken orthologs of these candidates were constructed, each carrying an N-terminal flag tag. Western blot analysis confirmed successful expression of 22 out of the 24 proteins, with C19orf12 being the sole exception (S7 Fig).

SERPINF2 effectively inhibits influenza virus replication

To screen for antiviral activity among candidate proteins, DF-1 cells were transfected with expression plasmids for 22 proteins. Viral replication assays performed 6 h post‑transfection revealed that overexpression of either chicken (chSERPINF2) or swan goose (sgSERPINF2) SERPINF2 significantly suppressed viral replication by 24 hpi (Fig. 5A), whereas none of the other proteins showed a notable effect (S8 Fig). Western blot analysis further confirmed the antiviral activity of SERPINF2, demonstrating a marked reduction in viral nucleoprotein (NP) levels at 24 hpi compared with the control (Fig. 5B). The results showed that overexpression of SERPINF2 reduced the specificity of AIV CKLN/07 in the presence of trypsin.

SERPINF2 exerts its antiviral function by inhibiting hemagglutinin cleavage

As a member of the serine protease inhibitor (SERPIN) family, SERPINF2 was hypothesized to inhibit influenza virus replication by interfering with hemagglutinin (HA) cleavage, which was a mechanism employed by other SERPINs [9]. To test this, DF‑1 cells overexpressing SERPINF2 were infected with virus (MOI = 0.01). We examined the effect of SERPINF2 overexpression on HA cleavage during CKLN/07 infection in the presence of trypsin. At 24 h post-infection, SERPINF2 overexpression significantly reduced total HA protein levels and blocked trypsin-mediated HA0 cleavage into HA1 and HA2(Fig. 6). These results indicate that SERPINF2 restricts the replication of influenza virus by specifically blocking HA cleavage, thereby impairing the production of infectious viral particles.

Discussion

Our comparative proteomic analysis of chicken and swan goose PBMCs in response to H9N2 AIV infection reveals fundamental differences in their immunometabolic strategies. Chickens mounted a robust yet metabolically costly inflammatory response, marked by oxidative stress and activation of tissue damage pathways. In contrast, swan geese employed a tempered strategy centered on maintaining cellular integrity and metabolic homeostasis, consistent with their ecological role as natural viral reservoirs. Through targeted screening, we identified and functionally validated SERPINF2 as a novel host restriction factor that inhibits influenza virus replication by specifically blocking viral HA cleavage.

This study confirmed that H9N2 avian influenza virus undergoes abortive infection in chicken and goose peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Specifically, although the virus could normally adsorb, invade and express nucleoprotein (NP), its viral RNA levels and infectious titers declined progressively over time, and no infectious progeny virus was produced. This phenotype is fully consistent with the core characteristic of abortive infection—“virus enters cells but fails to complete its full replication cycle”—as reviewed by Embry and Gammon [16]. This abortive outcome is not unique. Londrigan et al. [17] have reported that seasonal influenza virus replication in mouse macrophages can be restricted at either the entry stage or the late stages of the viral life cycle. In our study, NP protein persisted throughout the infection period in the absence of progeny virus production, suggesting that restriction of H9N2 in avian PBMCs occurs primarily at a late post-entry stage of replication. The essence of abortive infection is the multilayered host response that is rapidly activated following virus invasion. This limited infection process provides an ideal window for analyzing innate immune responses without the confounding effects of high-level progeny virus replication. Liu et al. [18] have revealed extensive dynamic proteomic changes in human alveolar macrophages following influenza virus infection, and Adams et al. [19] have also identified species-specific differences in the immune responses of chicken and duck PBMCs to H11N9 virus using transcriptomic analysis. Based on these findings, the chicken and goose PBMC abortive infection models established in this study exhibit similar final phenotypes of restricted virus replication, but the underlying molecular strategies are likely to be species-specific. This provides a stable comparative platform for subsequent time-resolved comparative transcriptomic, proteomic and other multi-omic analyses. Just as Haas et al. [20] have successfully identified host targets of pandemic viruses through integrated proteomic and genetic analyses, this model system is expected to enable panoramic mapping of the responses of these two avian cell types to the same virus strain, identify conserved antiviral core modules and species-specific regulatory nodes, and ultimately provide key evidence for elucidating the molecular basis of waterfowl natural resistance to influenza virus and the host barrier mechanisms of avian influenza virus cross-species transmission.

Proteomic analysis of early host responses underscored an evolutionary divergence in antiviral defense. In chickens, PBMCs exhibited strong and sustained activation of inflammatory and innate immune pathways at both 24 and 48 hpi, which may be consistent with a strategy prioritizing rapid viral containment, which was a critical trait in susceptible poultry. However, this heightened response carried immunopathological costs, as seen in the persistent upregulation of PPAR signaling and glycolysis, which may support inflammatory mediator synthesis and energy demands but could also alter host metabolic equilibrium in ways that inadvertently benefit the virus [21, 22]. Furthermore, the concurrent suppression of spliceosome activity, glutathione metabolism, and proteasome function in chickens may reflect viral evasion mechanisms that impair host defense and facilitate immune escape [23–26].

Conversely, swan goose PBMCs displayed minimal inflammatory activation, which may be consistent with an evolved state of immune tolerance that avoids excessive and energetically expensive inflammation. For migratory birds energy conservation is a critical adaptation strategy [21, 27]. This subdued response involved downregulation of cytoskeleton reorganization, focal adhesion, and extracellular matrix receptor interaction pathways, potentially limiting immune cell mobility and activation, alongside enhanced RNA degradation that may further temper immune signaling [28, 29]. Such “damage-limiting” immunity aligns with observations in other reservoir hosts, such as ducks and bats, where fine-tuned metabolic and immune regulation permits viral persistence without clinical disease [4, 30].

A central finding of this study is the identification of SERPINF2 as a potent, cross-species inhibitor of influenza virus replication. Both chicken and swan goose orthologs significantly suppressed viral propagation. Mechanistically, we demonstrate that SERPINF2 interferes with the proteolytic cleavage of viral HA, a step essential for viral infectivity. This places SERPINF2 within the growing class of host protease inhibitors, such as SERPINE1/PAI-1 and SPINK6, known to restrict influenza virus by blocking HA0 processing into fusion-competent HA1/HA2 [9, 10].

A key remaining question is which specific host protease(s), such as TMPRSS2, furin, or other trypsin-like enzymes, SERPINF2 directly inhibits. Future studies employing protease activity assays, protein interaction mapping (e.g., co-immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry), and structural analyses will be essential to define its precise molecular target(s) and to evaluate its broad-spectrum potential against influenza subtypes that rely on different cleavage enzymes. Furthermore, elucidating why SERPINF2 is strongly upregulated in swan geese but not in chickens will require exploration of species-specific differences in transcriptional and epigenetic regulation.

From a translational perspective, the discovery of SERPINF2 as a novel host-derived restriction factor opens promising avenues for intervention. Its mechanism targets a conserved step in the viral life cycle and is less likely to drive rapid viral resistance, making it an attractive candidate for the development of novel antiviral agents, such as small-molecule mimics or peptide therapeutics. In poultry health, strategies to enhance the expression or activity of such restriction factors through breeding or biotechnological approaches could yield strains with greater inherent resistance to avian influenza, mitigating economic losses and reducing zoonotic risk.

Despite these findings, several limitations warrant cautious interpretation. First, we used an in vitro PBMC model, which is the most experimentally tractable and ethically acceptable system for wild birds, but PBMC heterogeneity and baseline subset differences may confound results, and this model lacks in vivo tissue context, as PBMCs are not the primary targets for productive AIV replication; thus, our findings should be extrapolated to whole-animal outcomes with caution. Second, although SERPINF2 showed antiviral activity in vitro, its expression in natural target tissues is unknown, which means our data only establish its function in immune cells and definitive explanations for asymptomatic natural infection cannot yet be drawn. Third, all mechanistic results are restricted to a single H9N2 isolate.

Conclusion

Our comparative proteomics analysis of abortive H9N2 AIV infection in chicken and swan goose PBMCs demonstrated that chicken cells activated antiviral, inflammatory, and oxidative stress pathways, whereas swan goose cells prioritized cellular integrity, metabolic homeostasis, and RNA surveillance. We identified SERPINF2 as a restriction factor that blocks HA0 proteolytic cleavage; its overexpression reduced viral infectivity, which was fully rescued by exogenous trypsin. These findings uncover distinct species-specific immune strategies against AIV and define a novel antiviral mechanism of SERPINF2, providing new insights into host–AIV interactions.
