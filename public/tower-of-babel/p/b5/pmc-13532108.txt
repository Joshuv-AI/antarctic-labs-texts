Synthesis and Biological Evaluation of Well‐Defined M6P(n)‐Modified Glycopeptides for Targeted Protein Degradation
Authors: Patrycja Lenartowicz, Jan‐Willem H. Langenbach, Nataliia Voloshyna, David Aidukas, Arwin J. Brouwer, John A. W. Kruijtzer, Geert‐Jan Boons
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLysosome‐targeting chimeras (LYTACs) is an emerging therapeutic strategy to deplete circulating and cell‐surface proteins. It harnesses endocytic receptors, such as the cation‐independent mannose‐6‐phosphate receptor (CI‐M6PR), to direct a protein of interest for lysosomal degradation. We have developed a synthetic methodology to prepare multifunctional M6PR‐targeting glycopeptides of varying valency using common building blocks. Peptides were prepared having one to six 4‐methyltrityl (Mtt)‐protected lysine moieties that could selectively be removed to give amines that were coupled with mannose‐6‐phosphate (M6P), mannose‐6‐phosphonate (M6Pn) and mannosyl building blocks having an α‐configured anomeric 2‐(2‐(2‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)ethoxy)ethoxy)acetic acid. The hydroxyls, phosphate and phosphonate of the building blocks were protected as p‐methylbenzyl, which exhibited excellent stability during synthesis but could be removed under mild conditions without affecting sensitive functionalities. The glycopeptides have an azido lysine moiety for functionalization by strain‐promoted azide‐alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC). Cetuximab (Ctx) was functionalized with bicyclo[6.1.0]non‐4‐yne for conjugation to M6P and M6Pn containing peptides via SPAAC. The resulting mAbs were examined for cellular uptake of soluble Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR). Only mABs modified by glycopeptides carrying three or six M6P or M6Pn residues significantly increased uptake. Phosphonates were more efficient in removing cell surface EGFR by Ctx modified by tri‐ or hexavalent M6P or M6Pn ligand.

Introduction

Targeted protein degradation is an attractive strategy to target undruggable proteins [1]. Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) are bifunctional molecules composed of a ligand for binding to a protein of interest and a small molecule binder of an E3 ubiquitin ligase. These compounds make use of an E3 ligase to selectively ubiquitinate a protein of interest, which is subsequently targeted for degradation by the proteasome [2]. A limitation of PROTACs is the inability to target extracellular and membrane‐bound proteins lacking a suitable cytosolic domain. Lysosome‐targeting chimeras (LYTACs) are addressing this limitations [3, 4], by harnessing endocytic receptors, such as the cation‐independent mannose‐6‐phosphate receptor (CI‐M6PR) or the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR), to direct a cell surface or soluble protein to the lysosome for degradation [3]. In particular, LYTACs composed of a monoclonal antibody targeting a protein of interest and a polymeric ligand for CI‐M6PR have received considerable attention [5].

It is well‐established that multivalency is important for M6PR binding, however, the optimal valency required for binding and internalization remains understudied [6, 7]. It has been shown that CI‐M6PR binds monovalent ligand with low affinity (K D = 7 µM) and that an increase in valency can substantially improve the avidity [8]. It has been postulated that the increase in avidity is due to interactions between two binding domains of a single CI‐M6PR which leads to a conformational change in the receptor (the hook model) resulting in internalization. An alternative model proposes that a multivalent M6P ligand induces receptor dimerization by binding to two distinct high‐affinity binding sites (the ladder model) [9, 10]. In a proof of concept study, Bertozzi and coworkers demonstrated targeting of M6PR using polymers containing multiple serine‐O‐mannose‐6‐phosphate residues (20–90 M6Pn per peptide) [5]. Other studies have indicated that peptides with two, three, five, and six M6PR ligands are sufficient for binding and internalization via the M6PR pathway [11, 12, 13, 14, 15].

In an early study, copper‐mediated 1,3‐dipolar cycloaddition was utilized to incorporate alkyne containing M6P into a peptide scaffold composed of azido‐lysine residues [15, 16]. This approach relies, however, on a toxic copper catalyst and lacks orthogonality for attachment to a variety of other entities such as monoclonal antibodies. To address these limitations, an alternative strategy was developed that employs a C‐glycoside of mannose‐6‐phosphonate (M6Pn) building block for solid phase peptide assembly [12]. The phosphonate was protected as a t‐butyl ester which could be removed during cleavage from the solid support by trifluoracetic acid (TFA)/triisopropylsilane (TIS)/H2O (95/2.5/2.5). However, tert‐butyl protected phosphate/phosphonate derivatives are unpredictably unstable due to autocatalytic degradation during storage, especially when a carboxylic acid is present [17]. A series of multivalent M6Pn derivatives was prepared by thiol‐ene reactions of cysteine containing peptides with M6Pn modified by an anomeric allyl moiety to explore the importance of valence for cellular uptake and targeted protein degradation [14]. Peptides having multiple cysteines are difficult to handl and combined with the need for radical initiator or UV irradiation makes the preparation of these compounds challenging.

We aimed to develop an efficient synthetic strategy for the preparation of a range of well‐defined M6P and mannose‐6‐phosphonate (M6Pn) containing compounds to investigate the valency required for M6PR‐mediated internalization and subsequent lysosomal degradation. M6Pn derivatives were included because these analogs have a much better metabolic stability [18]. This synthetic strategyie is based on the synthesis of resin bound multi‐functional peptides 1a‐ d (Figure 1A). These peptides have an azido lysine moiety for functionalization by strain‐promoted azide‐alkyne cycloaddition (SPAAC). Furthermore, it has one, two, three or six 4‐methyltrityl (Mtt)‐protected lysine moieties and another lysine residue masked by tert‐butyl carbamate (Boc). The Mtt protecting groups can selectively be removed without affecting Boc by treatment under mild acidic conditions. The resulting amines can then be coupled with M6P (2), M6Pn (3) and mannosyl (4) building blocks (Figure 1B), having an α‐configured anomeric 2‐(2‐(2‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)ethoxy)ethoxy)acetic acid [19] by conventional amide bond chemistry. The hydroxyls of the building blocks 2–4 are protected as p‐methylbenzyl (MBn) ethers [20, 21, 22]. which have excellent stability under reaction conditions commonly employed for the synthesis of complex carbohydrates. This protecting group is, however, also attractive for glycopeptide synthesis and can be cleaved during the final deprotection and release from the resin using moderately strong acids. We developed novel reagents for phosphorylation and phosphonylation also based on MBn protection using an established synthetic protocol [23]. The resulting protected phosphates and phosphonates have excellent chemical stabilities but can conveniently be deprotected using reaction conditions commonly employed in glycopeptide synthesis. The final deprotection step also removes the Boc protecting group to give a lysine residue that greatly improves sensitivity for ESI‐based mass spectrometry. Also, a tyrosine moiety was included in the glycopeptides to facilitate detection by UV and a GGGS sequence which is commonly employed as a flexible spacer for the development of antibody drug conjugates [24, 25]. Various assays were employed to examine receptor‐mediated uptake of the resulting multivalent glycopeptides 5a,c,d, 6a–d, and 7a–d (Figure 1C). For this purpose, cetuximab (Ctx), which is an FDA‐approved EGFR‐blocking monoclonal antibody (mAb), was functionalized with bicyclo[6.1.0]non‐4‐yne (BCN) for subsequently conjugation to glycopeptides 5a,c,d, 6a–d, and 7a–d via SPAAC. The resulting mAbs were examined for cellular uptake of soluble Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor vIII (EGFRvIII) modified AF647. It was found that only mABs modified by glycopeptides carrying three or six M6P or M6Pn residues significantly increased uptake compared to controls. The phosphonates were more efficient in facilitating removal of cell surface EGFR by Ctx modified by tri‐ or hexavalent M6P or M6Pn ligand.

Results and Discussion

Chemical Synthesis of M6P (2), M6Pn (3, 22, 24), and Man (4) Building Blocks

The preparation of compound 9 started with alkylation of 8 [26] using 4‐methylbenzyl bromide (MBnBr) in the presence of sodium hydride (NaH) in DMF to give the glycosyl donor 9 (Scheme 1A). A glycosylation of 9 with ethyl 2‐(2‐(2‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)ethoxy)ethoxy)acetate 10 [27], using N‐iodosuccinimide (NIS) and trimethylsilyl triflate (TMSOTf) as the promoter [28] required extensive optimization to achieve high α‐anomeric selectivity because the MBn protecting group at C‐2 cannot perform neighboring group participation (See Supporting Information 1.2). Initially, the glycosylation was performed in a mixture of 1,4‐dioxane and diethyl ether, as these solvents are known to promote α‐anomeric selectivity [29]. Both diethyl ether and 1,4‐dioxane can participate in the reaction through the formation of a diethyloxonium ion intermediate, which preferentially adopt the more stable equatorial configuration. This intermediate is proposed to direct nucleophilic attack from the axial face, thereby favoring formation of an α‐glycoside. However, under these conditions, substantial formation of the β‐mannoside was observed, and the α‐anomer was obtained in a yield of 16%. The use of 1,4‐dioxane/toluene solvent mixture led to an improvement of anomeric selectivity (α/β = 10/1) [30]. Only the α‐anomer was formed by performing the glycosylation at an elevated temperature (50°C) in toluene [31, 32]. providing 11 in a yield of 63%. The glycosylation was scalable and was performed using 19 g of donor 9. Compound 11 was subjected to desilylation using tetrabutylammonium fluoride (TBAF) in THF to yield key intermediate 12. The C‐6 hydroxyl of 12 could readily be phosphorylated employing novel phosphoramidite 13 that was prepared in two steps starting from PCl3 and diisopropylamine (DIPA) to afford diisopropylphosphoamidous dichloride, which was immediately reacted with 4‐methylbenzylalcohol (MBnOH) (Supporting Information). The resulting phosphoramidite 13 was purified by silica gel column chromatography and could be stored for a prolong period at −20°C. Treatment of 12 with 13 in the presence of 5‐phenyl‐1H‐tetrazole, followed by oxidation of the intermediate phosphite with H2O2, afforded 14 in an overall yield of 69%. Finally, saponification of the ethyl ester of 14 with aqueous sodium hydroxide gave carboxylic acid 2.

Synthesis of (A) M6P (2), M6Pn (3), and mannosyl (4) building blocks and (B) phosphonylating reagents 18 and 20.

Next, attention was focused on the preparation of M6Pn building blocks 3, 22, and 24 (Scheme 1A). A reported approach for the preparation of phosphonates is based on triflation of a primary alcohol of a monosaccharide that is displaced by the carbanion of dimethyl or di‐tert‐butyl methylphosphonate [16]. A drawback of this approach is that the 2,3‐diol of the mannoside needs to be protected as an isopropylidene acetal to avoid 3,6‐anhydro‐formation. In an alternative approach, a C‐6 hydroxyl of a mannoside is oxidized to an aldehyde, which is subjected to a Horner‐Wadsworth‐Emmons reaction using the carbanion of tetraethyl methylenediphosphonate [5]. Reduction of the resulting double bond by hydrogenation (Pd/C, H2) and cleavage of the ethyl ester of the phosphonate using trimethylsilyl bromide (TMSBr) gives then a phosphonate. We choose an approach based on a Horner‐Wadsworth‐Emmons reaction because of the expected compatibility with MBn ethers at the C‐2, C‐3, and C‐4 hydroxyls of a mannoside. We explored the use of an ethyl (22), benzyl (24) and 4‐methylbenzyl (3) ester for the protection of methylenediphosphonate. As will be detailed in the glycopeptide synthesis section, the selection of the phosphonate ester protecting group is critical for the successful synthesis of the targeted phosphonates. Thus, the primary alcohol of the common building block 12 was oxidized using Dess‐Martin periodinane (DMP) and the resulting aldehyde was subjected to a Horner‐Wadsworth‐Emmons reaction using the anion of commercially available tetraethyl methylenediphosphonate 15 to afford alkene 21 in a 54% yield over two steps (see Supporting Information 1.3 for optimization of the reaction conditions). Subsequent reduction of the double bond by nickel boride (Ni2B), which was generated in situ from NiCl2·6H2O and NaBH4 [33], followed by saponification of the ethyl ester by aqueous sodium hydroxide yielded mannose‐6‐diethyl‐phosphonate 22.

Phosphonylating reagent 18 was prepared according to a described procedure [23]. The synthesis of the novel phosphonylating reagent 20 started from a reaction of triethyl orthoformate with 4‐methylbenzyl alcohol (MBnOH) in toluene in the presence of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) to provide, after removal of excess MBnOH by in vacuo distillation, 4‐methylbenzyl orthoformate 19, which was then reacted with commercially available medronic acid under solvent free conditions at 150°C to yield tetramethylbenzyl methylenediphosphonate 20 (Scheme 1B). Compound 12 was then subjected to phosphonylation using 18 and 20 using the optimized reaction conditions to give phosphonates 23 and 25. Reduction of the double bond of 23 and 25 followed by ester hydrolysis provided 24 and 3 in high yield (Scheme 1A).

The synthesis of the mannosyl building block 4 started with the alkylation of 26 [34] using MBnBr in the presence of NaH in DMF to give 27. The glycosyl donor 27 was glycosylated with 10 using NIS/TMSOTf as the promoter under optimized conditions to provide 28 as only the α‐anomer in a 54% yield. Saponification of the ethyl ester of 28, using aqueous sodium hydroxide, provided the mannosyl building block 4 in a quantitative yield (Scheme 1A).

Synthesis of Well‐Defined Glycopeptides

Having the monosaccharide building blocks in hand, we focused on the preparation of the desired glycopeptides. The precursor peptides 1a–d were assembled using a CEM Liberty Blue microwave synthesizer, employing CarboMAX as the coupling method and Fmoc‐t‐Bu strategy on Tentagel S Ram as the solid support (Scheme 2) [35]. Fmoc deprotection was performed using 20% piperidine in DMF (2 min, 90°C) [36]. Coupling reactions were carried out at 90°C for 4 min using five equivalents of the appropriate Fmoc amino acid, diisopropylcarbodiimide (DIC) as an activator, and oxyma as a base (1:2:1) [35]. The N‐terminus of the glycopeptides was manually acetylated using a Ac2O (0.5 M), 1‐hydroxybenzotriazole hydrate (HOBt) (0.015 M), N,N‐diisopropylethyl amine (DIPEA) (0.125 M) in N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP). Next, the Mtt protecting groups were selectively removed in the presence of Boc by treating the resin with a cocktail of TFA/TIS/CH2Cl2 (1:5:94, v/v/v) (10 times, 2 min each), followed by the addition of NEt3 in CH2Cl2 (5:95, v/v) (2 times, 2 min each) to afford intermediates 29a–d. The free amines were then coupled with compounds 2, 3, 4, 22, and 24 using benzotriazol‐1‐yloxytris(dimethylamino)phosphonium hexafluorophosphate (BOP)/DIPEA using three equivalents for each carboxylic acid per lysine residue to ensure complete coupling. Completion of the reactions was monitored using a Kaiser test.

Synthesis of well‐defined multivalent glycopeptides 5a,c,d, 6a–d, and 7a–d.

The use of building blocks 2 and 4 resulted, after purification by HPLC using a HILIC column (ReproSil Star ZIK HILIC), in the isolated glycopeptides 5a,c,d and 6a–d. The assembly of the phosphonylated glycopeptides 7a–d required extensive optimization and the selection of the phosphonate ester protecting group proved critical for the successful preparation of the targeted compounds. To establish optimal reactions conditions, we first evaluated the coupling of the free carboxylic acid 22 to the lysine residue of the monovalent peptide 29a to give after global deprotection glycopeptide 7a. Deprotection of the diethyl phosphonate esters on a peptide scaffold proved, however, difficult (See Supporting Information 2.4). Standard McKenna conditions (TMSBr/pyridine in MeCN [14, 37], gave no product, and only the starting material was detected by MALDI‐TOF MS, which is likely due to the low solubility of the peptides, as indicated by the presence of a precipitate. Next, we attempted deprotection using 1 M TMSBr in TFA, which led to degradation of the peptide as indicated by the multiple peptide fragments detected by MALDI‐TOF MS and multiple peaks in the HPLC chromatogram. The use of thioanisole and m‐cresol as nucleophilic scavengers, as previously reported for phosphonate deprotection, also failed to produce the desired product 7a [38].

Next, we explored the use of benzyl protected phosphoesters because of the expectation that these can be deprotected using a TFA cocktail [39]. Thus building block 24 (Schemes 1A and 2) was condensed with monovalent peptide 29a followed by final deprotection and cleavage of the resin‐bound glycoconjugate using a TFA cocktail. It yielded the desired glycopeptide 7a, however, a substantial quantity of mono‐OBn‐protected peptide was isolated as a by‐product (See Supporting Information 2.5). Fortunately, the use of compound 3 followed by subsequent deprotection with a cocktail of TFA/TIS/H2O (95:2.5:2.5, v/v/v) resulted in complete deprotection and clean conversion to yield the desired glycopeptide 7a and partially deprotected phosphonate was not detected by LC–MS. The crude material was purified by HPLC using a HILIC column (ReproSil Star ZIK HILIC) to give 7a. The phosphonylated glycopeptides 7b–d were prepared in a similar manner starting from 3 and 29b–d. The resulting glycopeptides were purified by HPLC using a HILIC column, and their identity confirmed by high‐resolution‐mass spectrometry (HR‐MS). Glycopeptides were isolated in quantities of 0.4–6.0 mg based on 0.025 to 0.10 mmol of resin loading corresponding to overall yields of 5–15%. It is important to note that at intermediate stages, compounds were released from part of the resin and analyzed by LC‐MS and Kaiser tests compromising some compound recovery.

Cell Uptake Studies

We evaluated the ability of glycopeptides 5a,c,d, 6a–d, and 7a–d to facilitate internalization of a target protein of interest. For this purpose, Ctx, which is an FDA‐approved blocking mAB targeting the EGFR, was modified by the glycopeptides. The ability of the resulting Ctx conjugates (Ctx‐5a,c,d, Ctx‐6a‐d, and Ctx‐7a‐d) to internalize a fluorescently labeled soluble domain from EGFR (EGFRvIII) [5, 40, 41] by HELA cells was examined. Thus, Ctx was functionalized via amines of lysine residues with BCN‐PEG3‐N‐hydroxysuccinimide (BCN‐PEG3‐NHS) resulting in approximately eight BCN moieties per antibody (31, Figure 2B) [42]. The latter was determined by SPAAC of the BCN moieties of the mAB with AF647‐N3 followed by purification by P6 size exclusion column chromatography and fluorescent measurement. Next, we performed SPAAC of the BCN moieties of the mAB with the azide moiety of glycopeptides 5a,c,d, 6a‐ d, and 7a‐ d to give, after purification by size exclusion column chromatography, glycan modified mAbs Ctx‐5a,c,d, Ctx‐6a–d, and Ctx‐7a–d (Figure 2B).

Next, the modified mABs Ctx‐5a,c,d, Ctx‐6a–d, and Ctx‐7a – d were examined for their ability to internalize soluble EGFRvIII conjugated to AF647 fluorophore (∼1.5 fluorophores per EGFRvIII). For this purpose, HeLa cells were co‐incubated with 40 nM of soluble EGFRvIII‐AF647 and 20 nM of the Ctx conjugates for 24 h. Next, the cells were washed with FACS buffer (HBSS, 0.5% BSA, 5 mM EDTA) and the uptake of EGFRvIII‐AF647 was analyzed by flow cytometry (Figure 2C). As expected, the mAbs modified by mannosides of different valency (5a,c,d) did not exhibit a significant improvement of uptake of EGFRvIII‐AF647 compared to control (Ctx alone). Furthermore, mABs derivatized by glycopeptides 6a,b and 7a,b, carrying one or two M6P or M6Pn residues (Ctx‐6a,b and Ctx‐7a,b), respectively also did not show an increase in uptake. However, a significant increase of EGFRvIII‐AF647 uptake was observed when the mAb was modified with glycopeptides 6c,d or 7c,d having three or six M6P of M6Pn moieties, respectively (mABs Ctx‐6c,d and Ctx‐7c,d). The mAb carrying the hexavalent glycopeptide (Ctx‐6d and Ctx‐7d) showed the greatest uptake potential. The results agree with an earlier study that demonstrated that trivalent Man6Pn is substantially better taken up by cells compared to a divalent derivative [14].

We also examined mABs modified by tri‐ and hexavalent glycopeptides Ctx‐6c,d and Ctx‐7c,d for their ability to remove cell surface EGFR. Thus, HELA cells were exposed to 10 nM of the mAbs or control for 24 h. Next, the cells were washed using FACS buffer and then incubated with primary antibody (Rabbit‐anti‐EGFR, D38B1 Cell Signaling Technology) for 30 min. (0°C), followed by secondary antibody (Goat‐anti‐rabbit AF647, Invitrogen) for 30 min (0°C) for detection. The cells were incubated with infrared live‐dead marker for 5 min on ice and then analyzed by flow cytometry. It was found that Ctx modified with M6P and M6Pn removed cell surface EGFR efficiently (Figure 2D). It appears that the phosphonate modified mAbs Ctx‐7c and Ctx‐7d are the most efficient to remove cell surface EGFR.

Conclusions

A synthetic methodology has been developed to prepare well‐defined and multifunctional M6P and M6Pn ligands of different valencies using common building blocks. The strategy entails the preparation of M6P and M6Pn building blocks in which the phosphate and phosphonate are protected as a 4‐methylbenzyl esters. The building blocks carry also an anomeric 2‐(2‐(2‐(2‐hydroxyethoxy)ethoxy)ethoxy)acetic acid‐linker [19] which facilitated controlled coupling to the amine of lysine residues of peptides prepared by solid phase synthesis. The peptides were assembled using different numbers of lysine residues protected by Mtt that could by selectively removed by mild acid treatment for subsequent modification with the Man, M6P, and M6Pn building blocks. The peptides also harbor an azido lysine moiety for conjugation purposes by SPAAC. Cleavage of the glycopeptides from the resin resulted in concomitant cleavage of the 4‐methylbenzyl esters of the phosphates and phosphonates. The use of other phosphate protecting groups led to the formation of by‐products. Glycopeptides modified by one, two, three or six Man, M6P or M6Pn residues were conjugated to the mAB, Ctx, and the ability of the resulting conjugates to facilitate cellular uptake of fluorescently labeled EGFRvIII was examined. Only mABs modified by glycopeptides carrying 3 or 6 M6P or M6Pn residues demonstrated significantly greater uptake compared to controls. It is important to note that the mABs are modified by ∼8 glycopeptides and thus the valence of the mABs is high, and it is likely that a combination of the Hook and ladder binding modes is important for efficient cellular uptake. Surprisingly, it was found that phosphonates were more efficient in removing cell surface EGFR by Ctx modified by tri‐ or hexavalent M6P or M6Pn ligands.
