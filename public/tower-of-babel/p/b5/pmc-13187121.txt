Spatial analysis of physical activity and ambient air pollution in Kuwait: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tesfaye R Feyissa, Victor M Oguoma, Saad Alsharrah, Ralf-Dieter Schroers, Fahd Al-Mulla, Mark Daniel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAir pollution may be associated with lesser physical activity (PA), a major risk factor for cardiometabolic disease. Such an analysis has not been conducted for Kuwait, a major petroleum-producing nation with some of the highest rates of diabetes in the world. This study aimed to assess the associations between spatiotemporally expressed ambient air pollutant concentrations and PA in Kuwait. A cross-sectional study was conducted from 2011 to 2014, involving 2529 adults ≥ 18 years. Participants were classified as physically active if they exceeded 600 MET minutes per week. Monthly mean air pollutant concentrations (hydrogen sulphide, nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide, nitric oxide, nitrogen oxides, ozone, and PM10) were spatially interpolated to local residential areas using data from local monitoring stations. Random effects logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the associations between PA and air pollutants (an alpha level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests). Participants were predominantly male (63%) with mean body mass index 29.3 kg/m². 52% were physically active. The overall air pollutant score was inversely associated with PA (adjusted odds ratio: 0.87, 95% CI 0.79–0.95). A 10 mean increase (ppb) in air pollutant concentration was associated with proportionately lower odds of being physically active for hydrogen sulphide (10%), nitric oxide (14%), nitrogen dioxide (16%), and nitrogen oxides (14%). In contrast, a 10 increase (ppb) in ozone concentration was associated with 15% greater odds of being physically active. Our findings highlight for Kuwait a high level of insufficient PA and a previously undocumented negative association between PA and air pollution, particularly nitrogen-based pollutants and hydrogen sulphide. Research is required to evaluate how air pollution may inhibit PA and whether reductions in emissions could increase PA in Kuwait.Keywords: Air pollution, Physical activity, GEO-spatial location, KuwaitSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Elevated airborne pollutant concentrations and physical inactivity are directly linked to an elevated risk of premature death from non-communicable diseases1,2 including diabetes3 and cardiovascular disease4. Physical inactivity is the fourth leading risk factor for mortality, annually responsible for 4–5 million premature deaths worldwide1. Less active people are more likely to develop cardiometabolic diseases5 amongst other chronic diseases. Air pollution in 2020 accounted for 7 million deaths worldwide2. Exercise and air pollution exposure are together associated with adverse blood pressure and systemic conduit artery and microvascular function changes6; respiratory and systemic inflammation7; cardiac autonomic control defects2; cardiovascular disease3,8 and type 2 diabetes4,9. Evidence assessing relationships between air pollution and PA is limited for arid, high-exposure settings such as Kuwait where pollutant exposure patterns may differ from other countries, given frequent dust events and elevated emissions from petroleum extraction.

High air pollution concentrations exceeding World Health Organisation (WHO) guideline thresholds (e.g., nitrogen dioxide: 200 µg/m³ 1-hour or 40 µg/m³ annually; ozone: 100 µg/m³ 8-hour; PM₁₀: 50 µg/m³ 24-hour or 20 µg/m³ annually) are known to discourage physical activity (PA)10. A review of the association between air pollutants and PA highlighted that people commonly exercise less in areas with high levels of air pollution, but also that some may sustain PA despite elevated air pollution11.

It is important to better understand how PA is shaped by exposure to airborne pollutants. Limited data availability is a barrier to understanding the relationship between air pollution and PA, particularly in areas with industries known to emit high levels of environmental pollution. In 2019, an estimated 90% of the Global South’s population lived in areas where air quality exceeded WHO guidelines12. Air pollution and related adverse health outcomes are a major public health issue in North Africa and the Middle East (MENA) countries experiencing a rapidly growing chronic disease burden2,13,14.

In MENA countries, oil production and refining are major causes of air pollution, releasing pollutants including sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, and particulate matter15. Flaring, refining processes, and vehicular emissions further contribute to poor air quality. More research is required to understand the effect of these air pollutants on PA. Systematic reviews of non-MENA regions have highlighted particulate matter (PM), ozone, and nitrogen oxides as the most relevant air pollutants related to insufficient PA11,16. These reviews indicate that associations between PA and air pollution vary according to geographical setting17.

Having the highest age-standardised prevalence of physical inactivity in the world18, research from the oil-producing State of Kuwait could provide important insights about the impact of air pollution on PA. Physical inactivity in Kuwait is evident across all socioeconomic strata19,20, and systematic reviews of air pollutant exposure and PA for other nations suggest the likelihood of inverse, inhibitory relationships for Kuwait11,21.

Most studies showing adverse effects of air pollution on PA have been conducted for countries largely complying with stringent national air quality standards16. Kuwait does not meet its national air quality standards, given a combination of industrial emissions, traffic congestion and vehicular emissions, and naturally occurring dust storms22,23. Kuwait air quality standards are periodically updated in alignment with national and international air quality guidelines for countries of the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)24. Many countries, including those in the MENA region, similarly struggle to meet air quality standards, making air quality a major public health issue across GCC and MENA regions25.

Despite evidence from other locales of associations between air pollutants and PA, studies remain scarce for desert-based, petroleum-producing environments such as Kuwait. Kuwait’s context presents a unique combination of ambient air pollution, rapid urban development, and lack of greening, all of which exacerbate the health risks associated with PA. A lack of spatially demarcated, context-specific information on air pollution and PA in Kuwait further limits knowledge on the specificity of associations relevant to the design of public health initiatives intended to counter both ambient air pollution and low levels of PA.

Although PA can confer substantial health benefits, these may be devalued for even motivated individuals when PA is considered in settings characterised by high levels of air pollutants26. Spatiotemporal research enables the estimation of contemporaneous exposure-outcome relationships across locations within a given geographic setting, better assessing how PA covaries with local ambient pollutant concentrations and supporting time-sensitive, geographically targeted public health interventions. This study examined the relationship between ambient air pollution and PA in Kuwait’s adult population, utilising a spatiotemporal framework in the distinctive context of a rapidly urbanising arid environment.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and participants

Study details have been previously described27. Briefly, data from a national sample of 2529 participants aged 18 years and older were retrieved from the Kuwait Adult Diabetes Epidemiology Program, a cross-sectional diabetes screening survey conducted from 2011 to 2014. The study used a random sample of participants stratified by ethnicity and governorate (akin to municipality, in which are nested suburbs) from the computerised Public Authority for Civil Information register. Diabetes was defined as self-reported history with prescribed glucose-lowering medication, otherwise fasting plasma glucose ≥7mmol/L or HbA1c ≥6.5%.

Measures

Physical activity

PA was assessed using the WHO Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ)28. As previously reported29, self-reported data were collected for five domains of physical activity: vigorous-intensity work, moderate-intensity work, travel from place to place, vigorous-intensity recreation, and moderate-intensity recreation. The reported frequency (days/week) and duration (min/day) of PA per domain were multiplied by the corresponding metabolic equivalents of tasks (MET) values and summed to derive total METs expended per week30, representing overall PA status. Participants were classified as physically active if they achieved a minimum of 600 MET min/week, per WHO recommendations28. The GPAQ tool does not identify whether participants primarily exercise indoors or outdoors.

Anthropometric measures

Measures used in this study have been previously described27. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared31 with obesity defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2.

Air pollution

Ambient air pollutant measures were recorded at local monitoring stations for 16 sites across Kuwait for the period 2011–2014 (Fig. 1), for which data were supplied by the Kuwait Environment Public Authority. Air pollutants monitored included hydrogen sulphide, methane, nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide, nitrogen oxides, non-methane hydrocarbons, ortho-xylene, ozone, PM10 (particulate matter < 10 micrometres), sulphur dioxide, toluene, total hydrocarbons, and total sulphur. PM2.5 data were available for just one monitoring station for the study observation period, and on this basis, PM2.5 was omitted from consideration. PM10 was measured in µg/m³; methane, non-methane hydrocarbon, and total hydrocarbon were reported in parts per million (ppm); all other pollutants were reported in parts per billion (ppb). Air pollutant measures at each station were provided as monthly means for the 4-year study period.

A smoothing function using a piecewise cubic interpolation was applied to monthly pollutant concentrations recorded at each monitoring station to generate continuous spatial surfaces across the Kuwait study area for each pollutant. Using the Geocube Python package32 the interpolated surfaces were rasterised into 500-by-500 m gridded arrays, constructing a spatiotemporal dataset for the 2011–2014 study period. These data arrays enabled the assignment of pollutant estimates for specific dates (month and year) corresponding to the timing of survey participation for each respondent; it also enabled constructing mean-value aggregate values at the suburb level where PA occurred. This approach enabled temporally aligned, location-specific exposure-outcome assessment.

Piecewise cubic spline-based interpolation was used to generate smooth spatial surfaces from the monthly pollutant data, preserving continuity in concentration gradients between monitoring stations while remaining faithful to observed values. Cubic spline-based interpolation offered a practical alternative to restrictive assumptions that would have been questionable for other methods (e.g., Kriging) and has been shown in other spatial epidemiological research to perform well in representing pollution gradients for exposure assessment33,34 where the third-order polynomial function balances smoothness and adherence to data values.

Climatic variable

Air temperature data for the survey period (2011–2014) were compiled from Kuwait’s weather and climate stations as monthly aggregates (provided by the Kuwait Meteorological Centre with stations from the Capital, Farwaniya, Hawally, and Ahmadi Governorates). Interpolated surfaces with a 500 m-by-500 m grid spatial resolution were created for each parameter for each month over the four-year study period. This process enabled the extraction of climatic data for the dates and locations corresponding to participants’ survey responses. Spatiotemporal data were aggregated to the corresponding suburb for the specific month and year for which PA was reported. Monthly heat index values were calculated from air temperature and relative humidity for these multi-dimensional spatiotemporal data.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were described as mean and standard deviation, or median and interquartile range, as appropriate; categorical data were presented as counts and percentages according to sex and ethnic group.

Spatial interpolation of pollutant monitoring station observations using a polynomial function generated a small proportion of negative values for nine of 15 pollutants evaluated where the original values were close to zero (< 2% of values for 8 of 9 such pollutants, and < 5% of values for the ninth). These values were retained in regression analyses consistent with standard practice for environmental monitoring datasets to preserve the exposure distribution of interpolated pollutants and prevent the introduction of bias through distortion of the exposure distribution at the lower end. Such observations were truncated to zero for descriptive reporting (Table 1). Sensitivity analyses in which all negative values were truncated to zero prior to inferential modelling yielded unchanged results and interpretations.

The association between PA and pollution variables was assessed using random effects logistic regression analysis. The regression model accounted for clustering at the suburb level, considering participants living in the same suburb (or served by the same/nearby station) were likely to share very similar ambient exposure profiles and broader suburb-level characteristics that influence PA. First, we assessed the univariable relationship between PA and specific air pollutants. Three adjusted models were fitted for each pollutant with covariates including age, gender, ethnicity, presence of obesity, diabetes, and average monthly air temperature38. Model 1 adjusted for age, gender, presence of diabetes, and obesity. Model 2 adjusted for age, gender, ethnicity, presence of diabetes, and obesity; and Model 3 adjusted for age, gender, ethnicity, presence of diabetes, obesity, and monthly average air temperature. Model estimates are presented as odds ratio (95% confidence interval). An alpha level of 0.05 was used for all statistical tests. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata 18 (StataCorp, Texas, USA).

Ethics

Study review was conducted by the Dasman Diabetes Institute (DDI) and approved by DDI’s Institutional Review Committee (RA2010–004). The review was consistent with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to their enrolment in the study.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 2,529 participants, including 1597 men (63%), were enrolled in the study (Table 1). Mean age was 44.0 years, with the mean BMI 29.3 kg/m². 52% of participants (1311) were classified as physically active.

Mean levels of ambient air pollutants (averaged across month of observation) were: hydrogen sulphide (7.25 ppb, SD = 5.82), nitric oxide (23.70 ppb, SD = 14.50), nitrogen dioxide (41.54 ppb, SD = 15.32), nitrogen oxides (64.99 ppb, SD = 22.72), ozone (19.77 ppb, SD = 8.55), PM10 (238.40 µg/m³, SD = 120.28), and pollutant score (128.27, SD = 28.94).

Association between physical activity and ambient air pollutants

Table 2 presents univariable associations between PA and ambient air pollutant concentrations. Statistically significant univariable negative associations were observed between PA and several air pollutants: hydrogen sulphide (crude odds ratio (cOR)): 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.96), nitric oxide (cOR: 0.78, 95% CI 0.72–0.85), nitrogen dioxide (cOR: 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83), nitrogen oxides (cOR: 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83), sulphur dioxide (cOR: 0.86, 95% CI 0.79–0.94), total hydrocarbons (cOR: 0.88, 95% CI 0.81–0.97), total sulphur (cOR: 0.81, 95% CI 0.73–0.90), and pollutant score (PM10, NO, NO2) (cOR: 0.79, 95% CI 0.73–0.86). A statistically significant positive association was observed between ozone and PA (cOR: 1.20, 95% CI 1.08–1.34). Air pollutants and climatic variables exhibiting no statistically significant association with PA included meta- and para-xylene (p = 0.14), orthoxylene (p = 0.47), PM10 (p = 0.63), toluene (p = 0.18), and wind index (p = 0.42).

Discussion

This study examined spatial and temporal associations between PA and ambient air pollutants in Kuwait, a major petroleum-producing country that does not meet its national air quality standards, for which such relationships have not previously been reported. Despite the presence of environmental regulations, there has been a lack of institutional urgency in addressing air pollution as a health risk in Kuwait. A general lack of policy enforcement and accountability has also contributed to persistent air pollution exposure, with air quality concerns often marginalised in the national discourse. In this context, just 52% of our survey participants were minimally physically active. Airborne pollutants including hydrogen sulphide, nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide, and nitrogen oxides, were statistically significantly associated with lesser odds of being physically active. These pollutants may discourage PA due to adverse effects such as respiratory irritation and physical discomfort2,6,7.

Our findings documenting associations between air pollutants and PA build on prior research in non-MENA regions indicating that particulate matter, ozone and nitrogen oxides are linked to insufficient PA11,16. Our findings expand on previous approaches in documenting spatiotemporal covariation between air pollutant concentrations and PA. These observations are of clear public health significance insofar as Kuwait has highest age-standardised prevalence of PA in the world18 with a need for evidence to underpin environmental interventions, including policy to reduce pollutant emissions, to facilitate greater PA. There is a pressing need for spatiotemporal and environmental epidemiology studies in Kuwait, where air pollution remains under addressed, and PA levels are among the lowest globally. The findings suggest a hesitancy among government authorities to openly recognise the connection between public health issues and emissions from oil processing operations. Additionally, enforcement of existing emission regulations on local industries appears insufficient. Generating such evidence is essential to inform public health interventions and guide urban and environmental policies.

Monthly mean ambient air pollutant concentrations in our study corresponded to lesser odds with proportionate reductions of engaging in PA. The reductions were as follows: per unit increase in pollutant score, 13%; per 10 ppb increase, hydrogen sulphide, 10%; nitric oxide, 14%; nitrogen dioxide, 16%; and nitrogen oxides, 14%. Similar negative associations have been reported elsewhere. A study in China reported an association between air pollutant concentration and lesser PA39, and a systematic review of studies in Western countries reported a negative association between air pollution and PA16. Furthermore, two Danish cohort studies reported that high air pollution concentrations diminish the health benefits of PA, also exacerbating existing respiratory and cardiovascular problems40,41.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of seven studies (six from the United States, one from the United Kingdom) reported that, beyond the negative physiological effects of air pollution (e.g., pulmonary inflammation), potential cognitive disinclination towards PA due to visible smog could further compromise health by preventing benefits that would otherwise accrue if air quality were conducive to engaging in PA16. Dust storms from the Arabian Desert also cause seasonal incremental synergistic effects, wherein dust combined with air pollutants further deteriorates air quality in Kuwait42. Poor air quality (e.g., visible smog) may act as a deterrent to outdoor activity, not only due to physical discomfort but also the cognitive perception of health risk. In Kuwait, given high levels of airborne nitrogen-based pollutants including nitric oxide and nitrogen dioxide22 a negative association between ambient air pollutants and PA may reflect both mechanisms16. That is, (1) individuals may consciously avoid outdoor PA due to perceived health risks, while (2) physiological irritation from ambient air pollutants to which individuals are exposed in day-to-day activities, indoors, outdoors, or both, inhibits desire for PA41. Our study unfortunately did not include information on whether participants exercised indoors or outdoors, a core limitation addressed below. Further research is needed to assess the mechanisms by which air pollution impacts PA according to the locational patterning of PA over time.

For our sample, a proportionate 15% greater odds of being physically active per 10 ppb increase in ozone concentration was a counterintuitive finding given ozone is considered a measure of poor air quality. Similar counterintuitive results for ozone have, however, been reported elsewhere. A Brazilian cross-sectional study reported a statistically significant positive association between ozone and PA43. Ozone levels are often relatively higher in coastal areas44, which in Kuwait may also provide more opportunities for outdoor activity (e.g., waterfront spaces). The apparent positive association may reflect spatial co-location or residual confounding. It has been speculated that people may also be less aware of the risks of ozone vis-à-vis other air pollutants, and the physical discomfort caused by ozone may not be as immediate as that from nitrogen oxides45. People living in areas where ozone levels are regularly elevated may find that early adverse biological symptoms related to ozone exposure subside over time46. High ozone concentrations often coincide with favourable weather conditions, such as clear skies, which may encourage outdoor exercise. During periods of high ozone concentrations and high temperatures, people may also prefer to move their physical activity indoors, thus accruing corresponding PA. How awareness of pollutant-specific cognitions regarding health risk shapes PA and its location requires further investigation. These findings underscore the need for longitudinal analyses with specific attention to where people engage in PA (indoors or outdoors – including whether coastal or inland) to better separate pollutant-specific impacts from contextual influences.

Findings from our study are relevant to public health strategies in major oil-producing nations with high levels of ambient air pollution including Kuwait. As a desert nation undergoing rapid urbanisation, i.e., expression of modernity concomitant with socioeconomic advancement, Kuwait faces a unique set of environmental and public health challenges. Urban expansion, car-dependent planning, and oil- and industrial-related emissions come at the price of worsening air quality and a built environment inconducive to PA. Requirements to reduce air pollution could contribute importantly to improving PA levels, and consequently overall public health22. For example, initiatives that improve urban design, such as the provision of natural open spaces (greenery), can help reduce the adverse effects of ambient airborne pollutants. Sustainable, climate-adaptive design42, such as shaded paths, corridors for air flow, and greened outdoor building surfaces, can reduce radiant heat and provide improved opportunities for PA. These interventions are crucial in settings where environmental and health inequalities limit opportunities for PA. Implementing these and other viable strategies is important, especially for individuals engaging in outdoor PA. Given just half of participants in our study were physically active, well below the global average according to the WHO’s moderate-to-vigorous PA guideline47,48, it is essential to not only reduce ambient air pollution but to promote supportive conditions enabling PA. Individuals can reduce exposure to airborne pollutants by monitoring daily air quality reports and adjusting outdoor activities accordingly (e.g., moving PA indoors, avoiding coastal areas, and choosing times of day when pollution is lower). Individuals at higher risk—children, older adults, pregnant women, and those with asthma or cardiovascular/metabolic disease—may benefit from additional precautions and by following clinical advice.

Public awareness of air pollution health risks may shape behaviours (e.g., changing outdoor activity), but our study did not assess awareness or risk perception. Population-level data on perceptions of pollution in Kuwait are not available. Strengthening risk communication—routine air-quality index reporting, timely alerts during high air pollution, and tailored guidance for vulnerable groups—may facilitate protective actions while maintaining safe opportunities for PA. Future research on air pollution and PA must distinguish the location of physical activity such that associations can be compared for individuals engaged in PA indoors or outdoors and, if the latter, for coastal or inland areas.

This study has worthy strengths yet key limitations. Amongst strengths are the large, population-based sample, strong regional coverage of airborne pollutants with 16 monitoring stations enabling demonstration at scale of spatiotemporal variations in pollutant-PA relationships, and use of a standard measure for PA (GPAQ). Limitations are as follows. First, PA was self-reported with the inherent potential recall biases of such GPAQ data. Second, associations derived from cross-sectional studies do not infer causality. Third, despite using spatiotemporal pollutant estimates for local areas, this approach might not reflect actual exposure (inhaled ambient air pollutants molecules) which could vary with daily activities and time spent in different environments. Fourth, the number of pollutants evaluated raises the possibility of Type I error49(although the near-consistent patterning of associations with PA supports our main conclusions). Fifth, information was not available on whether participants engaged in PA indoors or outdoors. Hence, we are unable to assess if pollutant exposure influences PA more for those who exercise outdoors relative to those who exercise indoors, or if air pollutant exposure is not associated with location-based differentials in PA. People who exercise outdoors might be directly exposed to ambient air pollutants and be discouraged from exercising during periods of poor air quality. For indoor PA, exposure to ambient air pollutants depends on quality of ventilation and air filtration (as well as exposures during any related transit to indoor venues). But there could be a bias against PA irrespective of the venue, indoors or outdoors, based on olfactory or other sensation (e.g., alveolar shortness of breath) related to airborne pollutants. If an individual acknowledges that it is hard to breathe and that it smells badly outside, that person may altogether downgrade their commitment to exercise, whether indoors or outdoors. Sixth, given the exposure and outcomes data for our cross-sectional study span 2011-14, we are unable to comment on the representativeness of airborne pollutant concentrations during this period relative to those in the years prior to or following our study period. Whilst our results are internally valid, the generalisability of our results is necessarily limited to the range of pollutant levels observed during our study period, and recent studies from Kuwait indicate that air pollution concentrations remain high, above WHO thresholds13,14. Despite limitations inherent in this study, our findings support a need for public health guidelines and policies to increase PA engagement concomitant with ensuring air pollutants at levels below recommended limits.

Conclusion

This study observed that just 52% of participants in a population-based survey in Kuwait were physically active. With one exception, we documented an almost exclusively negative set of associations between ambient air pollutant concentrations and PA. Air pollutant score, hydrogen sulphide, nitric oxide, nitrogen dioxide, and nitrogen oxides, were negatively associated with PA, while ozone was positive associated with physically activity. Public health planning should continue to pursue strategies to minimise ambient airborne pollutant exposure and to create supportive outdoor and indoor environments for PA. Further research is needed, notably longitudinal designs with more refined measurement of PA including not just whether undertaken indoors or outdoors, but also detailed information as can be provided by wearable devices (exact geolocation, heart rate, respiration rate, oxygen saturation, body temperature, exact duration and nature of activity). Such refinements are essential to elucidate the causal behavioural and biological mechanisms underlying how air pollution may inhibit PA, as well as to evaluate whether reducing pollutant exposure can assist improving PA in Kuwait.
