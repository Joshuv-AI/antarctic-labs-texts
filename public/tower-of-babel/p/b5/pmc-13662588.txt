Prefrontal cortex to dorsal medial striatum neural circuitry mediates social interaction
Authors: Meng-Wei Zhang, Shao-Hui Chen, Hai-Li Zhu, Li-Jun Zhang, Shuang Wang, Qing Yao, Shu-Wei Hua, Ning-Hua Wu, Wei Meng, Ling Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prefrontal cortex and striatum have been implicated in social interaction while the underpinning neuronal and circuit mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we report that frontostriatal neurons are strongly activated when naive male C57BL/6J mice interact with a conspecific. Furthermore, cell-type-specific inhibition or activation of these neurons substantially impairs social interaction. Moreover, optogenetic inhibition of the prefrontal cortex (PFC)–dorsomedial striatum (DMS) pathway significantly disrupts social interaction. These findings reveal a key function of frontostriatal neurons in social interaction and suggest that selectively targeting the PFC–DMS circuit may help alleviate social dysfunction in related disorders.

INTRODUCTION

Social interaction is an innate behavior that is fundamental to survival and reproduction of sexually and asexually reproducing species (Liebig & Amsalem, 2025; Lischinsky et al., 2023; Wei et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2023). Unfortunately, social deficits are common among individuals with mental illnesses, including autism, depression, schizophrenia, and anxiety disorders (Pan et al., 2023; Salloum-Asfar et al., 2024; Sato et al., 2023). These impairments impose a significant societal burden and severely affect the daily lives and occupational functioning of patients (König et al., 2020; Xiang et al., 2020). Hence, continued investigation into the neural mechanisms underlying social interaction is critical for advancing our understanding of the pathogenesis of social dysfunction. Studies have shown that the prefrontal cortex (PFC) is crucial for social interaction in humans and other animals (Adolphs, 2009; Bicks et al., 2015; Lee & Green, 2016; Murugan et al., 2017; Watson & Platt, 2012). The medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC) is known to integrate and distribute sensory and state information in robust ways through several interconnected networks. However, the neural circuit mechanism by which the PFC regulates social interaction remain incompletely understood.

A major glutamatergic efferent of the PFC is the dorsomedial striatum (DMS) (Gabbott et al., 2005; Wall et al., 2013), a key brain region involved in regulating movement (Lopez-Huerta et al., 2021), drug addiction (Volkow & Morales, 2015), cognition (Mestres-Missé et al., 2012), working memory (Wilhelm et al., 2023), and reward (Kang et al., 2020). Evidence from both human and animal studies suggests that the DMS is essential for the control of social interaction (Fan et al., 2024; Kang et al., 2020; Mielacher et al., 2024). In particular, projection neurons from the mPFC directly regulate the activity of DMS neurons (Alexander & Brown, 2011; Aron, 2011; Narayanan & Laubach, 2006; Terra et al., 2020). Furthermore, it was found that the mPFC to DMS projection is responsible for cognitive functions (Hupalo et al., 2019; Terra et al., 2020) and action initiation (Brockett et al., 2022). However, whether frontostriatal neurons projecting from the mPFC to the DMS are involved in social interaction remains an open question.

In this study, we investigated frontostriatal circuits using the three-chamber social interaction assay in freely moving mice, as described previously (Liu et al., 2020). Our findings demonstrate a correlation between social interaction behavior and the activation of frontostriatal projection neurons. Notably, selective inhibition of the frontostriatal pathway impaired social interaction. Our findings demonstrate that frontostriatal projection neurons exert top-down control during social interaction and offer insights into the fundamental principles governing frontostriatal circuits, which may be relevant for understanding circuit dysfunction in human disease.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

We employed C57BL/6J male wild-type mice (2–4 months old) in this study. All mice were provided with unrestricted access to food and water, at 22°C±1°C and humidity of 55%±5%, under a 12 h light/dark cycle. Except for mice implanted with microelectrodes, all other mice were kept in shared housing with their group (3–5 mice in each cage). All experiments were carried out during the daylight period, specifically between 0900h and 1700h, using male mice aged 8–10 weeks. Every step was taken to lessen potential pain or distress experienced by the animals. Prior to the behavioral tests, the animals were acclimated to the experimenter through handling sessions lasting at least 15 min each day for a minimum of 5 consecutive days. In the same behavioral experiment, all mice were tested on the same day. Animal care and use were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the animal research ethics committee of Hubei University of Science and Technology (approval number: 2021-01-009).

Behavioral tests

Three-chamber social interaction test

Three-chamber social interaction test was performed as described previously (Liu et al., 2020). Mice were given a minimum of 1 h to adapt to the behavioral testing environment. The light intensity in the behavioral testing room was maintained at 10 lux. The social testing setup consisted of a three-compartment box with each compartment measuring 20 cm long×40 cm wide×20 cm high. A test mouse was subsequently positioned in the central compartment of the apparatus, where it was given the opportunity to explore its surroundings for a duration of 10 min. Following this acclimatization, a stimulus mouse, which was unfamiliar to the test mouse yet matched in age, sex, and strain, was introduced into the wire cup situated within one of the side compartments, identified as the social chamber. In contrast, the compartment on the opposite side, which contained an empty wire cup, was designated as the neutral chamber. Social chamber was randomly selected and counterbalanced for each group. The test mouse was allowed to freely explore all three compartments of the apparatus for another 10 min. The behavior was automatically captured using a video camera and analyzed using the EthoVision XT video tracking system (Noldus, Netherlands). The amount of time that test mouse spent in each chamber (term as “time in chamber”) and in the immediate vicinity (5 cm) of the cages regardless of the animal’s orientations (term as “time in zone”) were measured. The social interaction index calculated as the difference in the time spent in the social and neutral chambers (or zones), divided by the sum of the time spent in both chambers (or zones). To prevent olfactory bias and maintain experimental integrity, we thoroughly cleaned all components of the apparatus, including the wire cups, with a 75% ethanol solution between testing sessions.

Novel object recognition test

Novel object recognition test was performed based on previous protocols with appropriate modifications (Liu et al., 2020). A test mouse was placed in the middle compartment of the three chamber and was allowed to freely explore the apparatus for 10 min. After this habituation period, an object was placed inside the cylinder cage in one of the side compartments designated as the object chamber and the opposite compartment designated as the empty chamber. The test mouse was allowed to freely explore all three compartments of the apparatus for another 10 min. The overall activity of the test mouse in the apparatus was automatically recorded with the EthoVision XT video tracking system (Noldus, Netherlands). The amount of time that test mice spent in each chamber or in the immediate vicinity (5 cm) of the cages (termed as “zone”) was measured. After each session, the apparatus and cylinder cages were thoroughly cleaned with 75% ethanol to prevent olfactory cue bias.

Open field test

The open field test was employed to assess anxiety-like behaviors and locomotor activity within a controlled environment with a light intensity of 10 lux, and the apparatus measured 45 cm in length, width, and height. Over a 10-min period, several important variables were documented, including the total distance traveled and the duration spent in the center zone. Data was precisely collected using EthoVision XT 11.5 software (Noldus, Netherlands). To maintain the integrity of the test environment and minimize potential confounding variables, we thoroughly cleaned the arena with 75% ethanol between each trial. The behavioral assays were performed in the order of three-chamber social interaction test, novel object recognition test and the open field test.

Virus injection

Stereotaxic surgery was conducted according to our previous protocol (Liu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2019). The following viral vectors were used: ScAAV-hSyn-Cre-WPREs (AAV2/9, 2.50×1012 genomic copies/mL), AAV-EF1α-DIO-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry (AAV2/9, 2.54×1012 genomic copies/mL), AAV-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry (AAV2/9, 2.66×1012 genomic copies/mL), AAV-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry (AAV2/9, 2.67×1012 genomic copies/mL), AAV-EF1α-DIO-mCherry (AAV2/9, 2.56×1012 genomic copies/mL), and AAV-EF1α-DIO-eNpHR3.0-mCherry (1.35×1012 genomic copies/mL). All viral constructs were obtained from Taitool (Shanghai) or BrainVTA (Wuhan). The caudal region of the DMS was targeted using coordinates relative to bregma as follows: anteroposterior: +1.10 mm, mediolateral: ±1.00 mm, dorsoventral: −2.80 mm. For the PFC, the coordinates were AP: +1.90 mm, ML: ±0.50 mm, and DV: −2.00 mm. 100−200 nL of virus was administered on both sides to manipulate behavior, and one side for optogenetic identification of neurons. The chemogenetic manipulation experiments commenced 4 weeks after the viral injection, whereas the optogenetic manipulation experiments started 6 weeks following the viral injection.

Optical fiber implantation

An optical fiber (internal diameter: 200 μm; numerical aperture: 0.37; Inper Inc., Hangzhou) was housed within a ceramic ferrule (external diameter: 1.25 mm) and positioned through a craniotomy. For the purpose of optogenetic intervention, a pair of optical fibers was implanted bilaterally to target the DMS (AP: +1.10 mm, ML: ±1.00 mm, and DV: −2.80 mm). The ceramic ferrule was affixed to the skull using 3M Vetbond tissue adhesive, followed by dental cement.

Pharmacogenetic manipulation

During pharmacogenetic inhibition and activation studies, clozapine-N-oxide (CNO; Sigma, USA) was prepared by dissolving it in saline (0.9% NaCl solution) to achieve a final concentration of 0.75 mg/mL. CNO was administered to the hM3D- and hM4D-expressing mice via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection at a dosage of 5 mg/kg body weight, 50 min prior to the behavioral tests. To avoid potential confounding effect of CNO metabolite, the same amount of CNO was administered to mCherry transfected control animals as comparison.

Optogenetic manipulation

In experiments involving optogenetic manipulation, optical fibers were connected to a 589 nm or 470 nm laser generator (Inper Inc., China). Using a commutator, we split the laser beam into two separate paths and subsequently coupled them to the implanted optical fibers. During optogenetic manipulation, the photostimulation power was maintained at 1–5 mW at the tip of the optical fiber, which is a light intensity that does not interfere with neuronal activity (Fenno et al., 2011; Owen et al., 2019). Photostimulation was delivered continuously for NpHR-expressing subjects. Control mice injected with the mCherry virus were subjected to the same procedures and received equivalent photostimulation.

In vivo electrophysiological recording

Surgical implantation of an optrode: To achieve optogenetic tagging of frontostriatal projection neurons, we implanted a specially designed optrode—comprising a single optical fiber surrounded by eight tetrodes—in the mPFC. Each tetrode consisted of four twisted fine platinum/iridium wires (12.5 μm inner diameter; USA). The tetrode was fixed to the nut through a polyimide electrode guide tube, allowing it to advance synchronously with the nut. Each complete rotation of the screw enables the tetrode to move downward precisely by 280 μm (Xu et al., 2019). The implant was secured to the skull using four small screws, cyanoacrylate adhesive, and dental cement. Mice were subsequently placed on a heated pad to facilitate recovery and then housed individually.

Chronic electrophysiological recording during social interaction

Following the surgical procedure, the mice were allowed a 7-day recovery period to ensure their well-being before the experiments commenced. Subsequently, the mice underwent a gradual acclimatisation process to the experimenter over 5 d, with each session lasting 15 min. This preparation was essential prior to initiating electrical recordings aimed at investigating mPFC activity during social interactions. Recordings were conducted while the subject mice explored a specially designed three-chamber apparatus that clearly delineated social and neutral zones, enabling a comprehensive correlation of mPFC neuronal activity with behavioral responses. The spiking activity generated was digitized at a rate of 40 kHz and bandpass filtered between 250 Hz and 8 000 Hz. To maximize the animals’ freedom of movement during the experiment, we suspended the connecting cable above the apparatus using a nitrogen balloon. The recorded video footage was registered and synchronized with the electrical recordings, enabling detailed offline analysis via the Plexon system (Plexon Inc, USA).

Spike sorting

In the analysis of extracellular waveforms, signals with amplitudes falling below three standard deviations from the mean were systematically excluded to preserve data integrity and relevance. Single-unit sorting was subsequently carried out using the Offline Sorter 4.0 software developed by Plexon (Plexon Inc, USA), which facilitated an organized approach to data management. To enhance visualization, principal component scores were automatically computed, enabling representation of the data in a three-dimensional space. Following this computational step, the experimenter verified the accuracy of the waveforms. Waveforms exhibiting similar principal component scores were manually grouped into distinct clusters. Clusters were further characterized based on the characteristics of their waveforms. Specifically, if waveforms within a given cluster displayed a distinct refractory period exceeding 1 ms, this was taken as evidence that they originated from a single neuron. To ensure data quality, we only included units that were well-isolated (L ratio<0.2, isolation distance>15).

Spike rate analysis

To analyze the firing rate heat map for each isolated neuron, we constructed two distinct matrices. The first matrix, termed the spike map, recorded the quantity of spikes occurring within each pixel of the three-chamber apparatus. The second matrix, termed the behavior map, documented the duration spent in each pixel. The firing rate map for each neuron was then generated by dividing the spike map by the behavior map. To assess differences in firing rates between the social zone and the neutral zone, we calculated spike rates for each neuron within both zones, defined as a 5 cm area surrounding each cage. The firing rate was calculated by counting spikes per zone and dividing by the time spent in each zone. The average firing rates for the social and neutral zones were computed by averaging the spike rates across multiple visits (more than 5). For each neuron, firing rates from individual visits to both zones were further analyzed to evaluate the significance of differences using a one-way ANOVA. To ensure accuracy, we only considered zone visits (center-of-body tracking in Ethovision) that lasted longer than 1 s and were separated by at least 2 s from the preceding visit for spike rate computation.

Optogenetic identification of frontostriatal neurons

To achieve electrophysiological identification of frontostriatal neurons, we delivered blue light pulses with a wavelength of 470 nm and a duration of 5 ms. These light pulses were administered at 20 Hz. Neurons were classified as light-responsive if they demonstrated time-locked spiking with high reliability, defined as greater than 90%. Additionally, responsive neurons exhibited a short first-spike latency of less than 5 ms following illumination by the light pulses.

Electrophysiological recording of brain slices

Brain slice preparation

ScAAV-hSyn-Cre-WPREs was administered to the DMS of C57BL/6J mice. Three days later, either AAV-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry or AAV-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry was introduced into the PFC and allowed to express for 6 weeks. Mice were deeply anesthetized and then perfused with 20 mL of ice-cold, oxygenated modified artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF), containing 87 mmol/L NaCl, 2.5 mmol/L KCl, 1.25 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 26 mmol/L NaHCO3, 1 mmol/L CaCl2·2H2O, 2 mmol/L MgSO4·7H2O, 75 mmol/L sucrose, and 10 mmol/L glucose. Coronal sections of the PFC were prepared using a vibrating microtome (Leica VT1200S, Germany). Brain slices were allowed to recover, followed by incubation in oxygenated standard ACSF. The standard ACSF consisted of 119 mmol/L NaCl, 2.5 mmol/L KCl, 1.25 mmol/L NaH2PO4, 24 mmol/L NaHCO3, 2 mmol/L CaCl2·2H2O, 2 mmol/L MgSO4·7H2O, and 12.5 mmol/L glucose (osmolarity: 300–305 mOsm/kg).

Whole-cell patch clamp recordings

Whole-cell recordings were conducted on visually identified neurons using a 40× water-immersion objective on an upright microscope (Olympus, Japan). Patch electrodes (3–6 MΩ) were made using a P-97 puller (Sutter Instrument, Novato, CA) and filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): 130 K-gluconate, 5 KCl, 2 MgCl2·6H2O, 0.6 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 4 Mg-ATP, and 0.3 Na-GTP (pH 7.2–7.3; osmolarity 285–290 mOsm/kg), suitable for current-clamp recordings. Data was acquired using a Multiclamp 700B amplifier (Molecular Devices, USA) and analysis was performed offline using pClamp9 software (Molecular Device, USA). To examine the effect of CNO on neurons expressing hM4D(Gi) or hM3D(Gq), we compared the number of action potentials elicited by current injections before and after administration of CNO (10 μmol/L).

Immunohistochemistry

Mice underwent transcardial perfusion with 20mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and 30 mL 4% paraformaldehyde; brains were post-fixed 2 h then immersed in 30% sucrose-immersed at 4°C. Utilizing a cryostat (Leica Biosystems Nussloch GmbH, Germany), we obtained coronal sections with a thickness of 40 µm in PBS. These sections underwent two washes with PBS (15 min each) and then incubated in blocking solution (10% normal goat serum, 1% BSA, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with rabbit anti-CaMKII (1:300; Abcam, ab52476) in diluted (1:10) blocking solution (10% normal goat serum, 1% BSA, 0.2% cold fish gelatin, and 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS) overnight at 4°C. After three 15 min washes in diluted blocking solution, the sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1 000; Cat. No. A-11012 Invitrogen， USA) for 2 h at room temperature. After three final PBS washes, we mounted the sections and coverslipped them. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus， Japan) to assess viral expression.

Statistical analyses

We conducted data analysis with GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). A one-way ANOVA was employed for comparison between neuronal firing rates and the social and neutral zones. Differences in behavioral outcomes resulting from pharmacogenetic or optogenetic manipulations were assessed using two-way ANOVA, Bonferroni multiple comparison post hoc tests, or an unpaired t-test. A P-value of <0.05 was regarded as the threshold for identifying a statistically significant difference. Levels of significance are denoted as follows: *: P<0.05; **: P<0.01; ***: P<0.001; ****: P<0.0001. Data are presented as the mean±SEM, unless otherwise stated.

RESULTS

Identification and recording of frontostriatal projection neurons in freely moving mice

Among the various downstream projections of PFC neurons, the DMS plays a pivotal role in regulating social behavior (Van Kerkhof et al., 2013; Zayed et al., 2022). To validate the structural connectivity between PFC neurons and the DMS, we injected a scAAV-Cre in the DMS and a Cre-dependent virus carrying the light-sensitive opsin Channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2) fused to mCherry into the mPFC (Figure 1A, B). Consequently, viral expression was observed in the dorsal mPFC and corresponding axonal labeling in the DMS. Six weeks following ChR2-mCherry injection into the PFC, mice were euthanized. Subsequently, we obtained 40-μm-thick frozen brain sections including the PFC and DMS. We detected mCherry expression in the dorsal region of the mPFC, along with associated axonal labeling in the DMS (Figure 1C, D). Concurrently, we implanted an optrode composed of an optical fiber surrounded by eight tetrodes, and this optrode assembly was affixed to a moveable drive (Figure 1B). This made it possible to record single neurons in the mPFC while enabling optogenetic identification of ChR2-expressing frontostriatal neurons (Figure 1E–G). Spiking activity from well-isolated individual neurons was recorded during social interaction. Frontostriatal projection neurons were recognized by their activation in response to short bursts of blue light (470 nm, 5 ms, 0.5–1.35 mW). To determine the identity of frontostriatal neurons, double immunofluorescent staining was performed with antibody against calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase α (CaMKIIα), a marker of excitatory neurons. We found 23.4%±3.2% of CaMKIIα positive neurons expressed ChR2-mCherry, and 89.2%±3.6% of ChR2-mCherry-expressing neurons were CaMKIIα positive (Supplementary Figure S1). Together, these results therefore revealed that frontostriatal neurons are labeled with CaMKIIα.

Firing activity of frontostriatal neurons closely correlates with social interaction

To investigate whether frontostriatal neurons are involved in real-time social interaction, we recorded the spiking activity of these neurons in mice subjected to a three-chamber social interaction paradigm, a widely used assay for assessing social behavior in rodents (Moy et al., 2004). As shown in Figure 2A, mice implanted with an optrode were given the freedom to explore two adjacent chambers: one containing an empty cage (neutral stimulus) and the other containing a cage with a conspecific mouse (social stimulus). The animals’ movement within the three-chamber was recorded using a video monitoring system, enabling exact temporal alignment between neuronal spiking and periods of social interaction (Supplementary Movie 1; Supplementary Movie 2).

Our analysis began with an examination of the frontostriatal neuronal population’s responses, comparing activity in the social and neutral chambers. We first plotted the mean firing rates of specific neurons relative to the position of the test mouse within the three-chamber apparatus (Figure 2B). A total of 182 well-isolated neurons were analyzed, with 15.38% (28) identified as light-activated frontostriatal neurons. To precisely characterize neuronal activity during social interactions—following established protocols (Liu et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2019)—we quantified the discharge rates of neurons during periods of social behavior, analyzing data from both the social and neutral zones (Figure 2C). Notably, the social zone elicited higher activity compared to the neutral zone in all recorded frontostriatal neurons, demonstrating that social stimuli tend to increase firing rates of frontostriatal neurons. Quantitative analysis of the mean discharge rates in the social and neutral zones (28 units from 11 mice, Figure 2D) supported this finding: in all frontostriatal neurons, the firing rate increased significantly when the test subjects were actively engaged with the stimulus mouse (social zone: 8.58±0.73 Hz; neutral zone: 4.81±0.52 Hz; P<0.0001). When analyzing the activity of optically untagged PFC neurons during social interaction, we observed that these neurons exhibit increases, decreases, or no changes in their activity (Supplementary Figure S2). These results demonstrate a strong association between elevated frontostriatal neuronal activity and social interaction.

Pharmacogenetic inhibition and activation of frontostriatal neurons impair social interaction

For investigating a potential direct link between the enhancement activity of frontostriatal neurons and social interaction, the neural activity of frontostriatal neurons was manipulated using chemogenetic methods. Specifically, we administered scAAV-Cre into the bilateral DMS via injection, and after 3 d, we injected AAV2/9-DIO-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry into the bilateral PFC of naive C57BL/6J mice (Figure 3A, B). For validating the effectiveness of chemogenetic inhibition, the effect of CNO on frontostriatal neurons was examined in vitro. As expected, CNO administration significantly decreased the number of action potentials generated by frontostriatal neurons (baseline: 6.50±0.34; CNO: 2.50±0.85 Hz; n=6 neurons from 3 mice; P=0.0015; Figure 3C).

We initiated the three-chamber social test 1 h after administering the same dose of CNO to all the test mice. (Figure 3D). The results indicated that hM4D-expressing mice spent substantially less amount of time in the social chamber (hM4D: 283.17±8.36 s; mCherry: 370.68±6.60 s; P<0.0001) and displayed a significantly decreased social index compared to the controls (hM4D: 0.29±0.03; mCherry: 0.56±0.03; P<0.0001; Figure 3E, F). This finding highlights a notable impairment in social engagement in the hM4D-expressing mice. A detailed analysis of behavior in both the social and neutral zones further supported this reduction in sociability, as evidenced by decreased interaction time (hM4D: 216.62±8.37 s; mCherry: 273.33±6.75 s; P<0.0001) and lower social index scores in the hM4D-expressing mice (hM4D: 0.29±0.04; mCherry: 0.57±0.03; P<0.0001) (Figure 3G, H). Importantly, this deficit in social behavior is not due to altered locomotor activity, as CNO treatment did not influence the total distance recorded in the open field test (Supplementary Figure S3A–C). To evaluate the specificity of chemogenetic inhibition for social interaction, an object interaction test was subsequently conducted. After administering CNO, the time spent interacting with a non-living object (a plastic Lego) was not significantly different between mice expressing hM4D and controls expressing mCherry (Supplementary Figure S3D–F). Overall, these findings suggest that silencing frontostriatal neurons leads to a disruption of typical social behavior in mice.

The aforementioned loss-of-function studies highlight the crucial function of frontostriatal neurons in the modulation of social interaction. To further elucidate the role of these neurons in social interaction, we pharmacogenetically activated frontostriatal neurons. Specifically, scAAV-Cre was administered bilaterally into the DMS, and after 3 d, we injected AAV2/9-DIO-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry bilaterally into the PFC of naive C57BL/6J mice (Figure 4A, B). To confirm the efficacy of pharmacogenetic activation, the impact of CNO on frontostriatal neurons was assessed in vitro. As anticipated, we noted a significant increase in the number of frontostriatal neurons’ action potentials following administration of CNO (baseline: 1.50±0.22; CNO: 6.83±0.48; n=6 neurons from 3 mice; P=0.003; Figure 4C). Notably, similar to frontostriatal neuron inactivation, pharmacogenetic activation of frontostriatal neurons significantly decreased in both the time spent in the social chamber (hM3D: 320.11±7.37 s; mCherry: 363.07±9.87 s; P=0.0018; Figure 4D) and the social index (hM3D: 0.35±0.03; mCherry: 0.48±0.04; P=0.0071; Figure 4E, F). A closer examination of social and neutral zones confirmed the reduced sociability, reflected in decreases in both interaction time (hM3D: 225.27±5.58 s; mCherry: 263.45±9.76 s; P=0.0048) and social index (hM3D: 0.37±0.04; mCherry: 0.53±0.04; P=0.0056; Figure 4G, H). This effect was not accompanied by changes in mice locomotion or anxiety and was exclusively related to social interaction (Supplementary Figure S4). Together, these results reveal that both inhibition and activation of frontostriatal neurons impair social interaction.

Inhibition of the PFC–DMS pathway disrupts social interaction

To explore whether the PFC-DMS projection is essential for social interaction, this pathway was inhibited by expressing Cre in the DMS and Cre-dependent halorhodopsin (eNpHR3.0) in the PFC, followed by implanting optical fibers bilaterally or an optrode unilaterally in the DMS (Figure 5A). We first characterized functional connectivity between mPFC neurons and the DMS with in vivo electrophysiological recording. Briefly, we stimulated axon terminals expressing eNpHR3.0 of frontostriatal neurons with yellow light, and simultaneously recorded from DMS neurons. The increased firing rate of certain neurons in the DMS suggests that stimulation with yellow light effectively inhibits NpHR-expressing frontostriatal neurons (baseline: 1.48±0.22 Hz; Light: 4.94±0.45 Hz; n=5 neurons from 3 mice; P<0.0001; Figure 5C). A continuous yellow light (~5 mW) was applied throughout the full duration of the three-chamber social interaction test for both NpHR- and mCherry-expressing mice. Example heatmaps depicting movement patterns from an mCherry-expressing mouse and an NpHR-expressing mouse are presented in Figure 5D. Relative to the control group expressing mCherry, mice expressing NpHR showed significant decreases in both the time spent in the social chamber (NpHR: 260.25±10.20 s; mCherry: 344.45±5.10 s; P<0.0001) and the social interaction index (NpHR: 0.12±0.03; mCherry: 0.42±0.02; P<0.0001, Figure 5E, F). Analysis of the social zone similarly revealed reductions in both social interaction time (NpHR: 155.27±11.36 s; mCherry: 210.95±9.43 s; P<0.0001) and social interaction index (NpHR: 0.15±0.04; mCherry: 0.48±0.02; P<0.0001) in the NpHR-expressing group (Figure 5G, H). Moreover, we conducted an open field test to examine locomotion and anxiety-like behavior. The total movement distance and time spent in the center did not differ significantly between mice expressing NpHR and mice expressing mCherry (Supplementary Figure S5A–C). Furthermore, the impact was limited to social behavior, as exposure to the same stimuli did not alter the amount of time NpHR-expressing mice interacted with a non-living object (Supplementary Figure 5D–F). These findings suggest that the PFC-DMS connection is required for social interaction.

DISCUSSION

Here, we investigated whether PFC projection neurons targeting the DMS encode social interaction, employing chemogenetics, optogenetics, and chronic single-unit recordings of frontostriatal neurons. This study was built on a previous research and serves to extend it by focusing on social interaction-related mechanisms (Liu et al., 2020). Specifically, our results highlight the involvement of frontostriatal neurons in regulating social interaction, broaden the current understanding of PFC function, and complement existing neural networks underlying social interaction.

The PFC is a key locus for social behavior, playing a key role in social interaction, social motivation, empathy, and the processing of social hierarchies (Cao et al., 2024; Choi et al., 2024; Mohapatra & Wagner, 2023). The PFC forms intricate neural circuits with subcortical brain regions, working synergistically to regulate social interaction. Specifically, the dorsolateral subregion of the PFC modulates dopaminergic neuronal activity in the striatum via glutamatergic projections, thereby influencing reward evaluation during social decision-making (Alsharif et al., 2024). In contrast, the ventromedial PFC forms reciprocal connections with the amygdala, contributing to emotional recognition and response modulation within social contexts (Cai et al., 2024). Studies in both humans and rodents demonstrate activation of the PFC and DMS during social interactions (Armstrong-Carter et al., 2025; Hu et al., 2025; Landsiedel et al., 2022). These findings suggest potential functional connectivity between these regions. More specifically, anatomical studies reveal dense innervation of the DMS by the PFC, providing strong evidence for functional coupling between these regions (Gabbott et al., 2005; Marquand et al., 2017). Studies in primates and rodents have further elucidated how the PFC and DMS regulate social interaction (Armstrong-Carter et al., 2025; Lee et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020; Van Kerkhof et al., 2013). On top of that, we further discovered the circuit mechanisms that underlie frontostriatal neuron’s function in social interaction as discussed below.

Research has demonstrated that specific subpopulations of neurons within the PFC exhibit distinct dynamic activity patterns during social interactions. For example, PFC-NAc neurons are activated during social investigation (Murugan et al., 2017). Our previous work identified putative pyramidal neurons showing mixed modulation (Liu et al., 2020). Employing in vivo multichannel recordings, we found that all frontostriatal projection neurons are activated during social interaction. Considering the involvement of the PFC in social interaction, frontostriatal projection neurons predominantly consist of those neurons that display an increased response. Further, these neurons are excitatory pyramidal neurons, which has been confirmed by a previous research (Morgenstern et al., 2022). Interestingly, activation or inhibition of frontostriatal neurons have been found to reduce social interaction behavior, potentially due to an excitation/inhibition imbalance (E/I imbalance) within the PFC. In mice with a heterozygous Eukaryotic elongation factor 2 condition, there is a documented decrease in dendritic spine density, diminished excitability, and impaired α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionic acid receptor-mediated transmission in mPFC pyramidal neurons, collectively contributing to E/I imbalance and notable social deficits (Ma et al., 2022). In an autism model characterized by Melanoma Antigen Family Member L2 deficiency, hyperexcitation of somatostatin (SOM) interneurons leads to decreased activity of pyramidal neurons in the PFC. Optogenetic stimulation of SOM neurons has been shown to mitigate social deficits (Wang et al., 2025), thereby supporting the notion that excessive inhibitory control can also disrupt E/I equilibrium. Additionally, overactivation of PFC pyramidal neurons in mice similarly induces social deficits (Yizhar et al., 2011). Moreover, neuromodulators such as dopamine, serotonin, and acetylcholine have been linked to social interaction and likely play an important role in regulating activity of frontostriatal neurons (Christoffel et al., 2021; Mei et al., 2022). Therefore, future researches should focus on elucidating the observed behavioral abnormalities from the perspective of overall regulation. Specifically, this can be achieved by combining in vivo local field potential recordings with synaptic physiology to dissect how neuromodulators fine-tune the E/I balance in frontostriatal circuits during social interaction.

The mPFC mediates social behaviors via its output connections to subcortical brain regions (Gunaydin et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2025). Di et al. (2022) demonstrated that morphological and functional alterations of dorsal striatal neurons contribute to social deficits. Similarly, Shank3 mutant mice exhibit altered striatal dendritic spine density and morphology (Wang et al., 2024). Moreover, projection-specific activity is particularly important for the causal elicitation of complex behaviors, as observed both here and elsewhere (Deisseroth, 2014). Notably, we identified a monosynaptic projection from the mPFC to the DMS. Optogenetic inhibition of PFC-DMS circuit impairs social interaction in mice, which is consistent with previous research results (Salimi et al., 2024).

A significant body of researches has demonstrated that there is a marked hypoconnectivity between the PFC and the DMS in individuals suffering from various mental disorders that exhibit social dysfunctions (Geffen et al., 2024; Kim et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021). This finding serves as a fundamental reference for understanding the neural mechanisms underlying abnormal social interaction, aligns with previous research focusing on the neural circuit basis of social dysfunction, and possesses certain scientific significance (Liu et al., 2020).

This study has several limitations, which are outlined as follows. First, the experiment employed the classic three-chamber test paradigm, primarily assessing social tendencies through the spatial preferences of animals. However, this relatively simplified scenario makes it difficult to accurately simulate the complex social interactions found in natural environments, such as the establishment of hierarchy, cooperation, or conflict behaviors. As a result, it may not fully capture the multidimensional characteristics of social behavior. Second, the experiment exclusively utilized male mice, omitting female individuals. Given the significant gender differences in social behaviors and the neural regulatory mechanisms of rodents, conclusions drawn from this research may not be generalizable to female populations, thereby limiting the overall applicability of the results. Finally, the absence of a sucrose preference test or other reward-related control experiments hinders the ability to effectively distinguish whether the observed social behaviors arise from intrinsic motivation for social interaction or from preferences for novel stimuli or potential non-social rewards. Consequently, this complicates the clarification of the specific motivational mechanisms underlying social interaction.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that frontostriatal neurons are essential for social interaction and that inhibition of the PFC-DMS pathway impairs social interaction behavior in mice (Figure 6). Our research advances the understanding of the PFC and contributes to existing knowledge of the neural networks underlying social interactions.
