Estimating time-varying cholera transmission and oral cholera vaccine effectiveness in Haiti and Cameroon, 2021−2023
Authors: Erin N Hulland, Marie-Laure Charpignon, Ghinwa Y Hayek, Lihong Zhao, Rosemary Martoma, Angel N Desai, Maimuna S Majumder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundIn 2023, cholera caused over 95 000 deaths globally, predominantly in low-income and conflict settings, and contributed to the wasting, stunting and malnutrition of millions in countries where the disease is endemic. Moreover, the frequency and magnitude of cholera outbreaks are rising. As a result, the demand for outbreak control interventions is quickly outpacing existing resources. Oral cholera vaccination (OCV) is one intervention among many used to limit further transmission. Since 2022, one-dose OCV (OCV1) has replaced the standard two-dose OCV due to limited global supply. However, only a handful of on-the-ground surveys of OCV1 effectiveness presently exist.ObjectiveThis study aims to assess the transmission of cholera in outbreak settings using digital public health approaches and quantify OCV1 effectiveness in complement to on-the-ground surveys.MethodsUsing Haiti and Cameroon as natural case studies representing two disparate geographical and sociodemographic contexts, we employed computational digitisation techniques to extract weekly case counts from non–machine-readable images of outbreak epidemic curves. We then leveraged the R package EpiEstim to derive estimates of the effective reproduction number (Rt). To assess OCV1 effectiveness in the two considered countries, we applied VaxEstim, an extension of EpiEstim requiring three inputs: the basic reproduction number (R0), Rt, and vaccine coverage. Notably, our work provides the first known real-world application of VaxEstim in low-resource settings.ResultsEarly in either outbreak, weekly estimates of Rt were elevated (Haiti, 2.60 (95% credible interval (CrI) 2.42 to 2.79); Cameroon, 1.90 (95% CrI 1.14 to 2.95)). Thereafter, Rt estimates oscillated around the critical threshold of 1 in both settings. Haiti exhibited marginally higher OCV1 effectiveness than Cameroon (75.3% (95% CrI 54.0 to 86.4%) versus 54.9% (95% CrI 18.9 to 84.9%)).ConclusionsThis study underscores the value of combining digitised case data with computational techniques and the utility of VaxEstim for rapid, inexpensive estimation of vaccine effectiveness in data-poor outbreak settings.Keywords: Public health, Climate Change, Digital Technology, Disease Outbreaks, Vaccination

Introduction

Cholera, a severe diarrhoeal disease caused by toxigenic serogroups O1 or O139 of the bacteria Vibrio cholerae, has ravaged humanity through seven deadly pandemics since its first documented international spread in 1817.1 Presently, cholera affects about one-quarter of the world’s countries, causing approximately 1.2 to 4 million cases and 95 000 deaths annually.1,3 Alarmingly, the number of cholera outbreaks in novel locations is rising, with over 20 countries currently or recently affected: from Haiti in the Caribbean, to the Democratic Republic of Congo, Malawi and Cameroon in Africa, to Lebanon and Afghanistan in the Middle East, and to India and China in Asia.4 Moreover, due to limited resources, poor infrastructure, worsening political instability, and human displacement, many of these outbreaks are ongoing.5

Cholera spreads through contaminated water—either via naturally occurring bacteria or faecal transmission in water by infected individuals—and is of particular concern in locations lacking access to improved water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) systems.6 While the burden of cholera is currently concentrated in low-income and conflict settings, middle-income and high-income countries remain susceptible to its resurgence due to outdated infrastructure and extreme weather events posed by climate change.7,10 Specifically, flooding and heavy rainfall can increase cholera transmission through increased algal blooms, population displacement, and the spread of contaminated water due to overflowing sanitation systems11,13; landslides and earthquakes can damage existing healthcare and WASH infrastructure,14 15 and droughts can both increase the concentration of V. cholerae in groundwater and can further drive populations to unsafe water sources.13 16 Although long-term prevention of cholera transmission hinges on strengthening WASH systems, infrastructure development is costly, time-intensive, and thus unsuitable as a primary intervention during ongoing outbreaks.3 17 Thus, in such contexts, vaccination with killed whole-cell oral cholera vaccines (OCVs) is considered a safe and effective intervention when cases are surging, and often accompanied by educational campaigns about the benefits of handwashing to reduce the risk of further transmission.18 Typically, vaccination with OCVs requires administration of two doses (OCV2).19 OCV2 field studies in Haiti, Guinea, Zambia, Bangladesh, and India have demonstrated 2-year effectiveness ranging from 37% to 97.5%, averaging around 65%.20,25 This variability in vaccine effectiveness, rather than efficacy, reflects in part country-level differences in the strength of health systems and challenges associated with vaccine distribution in urban versus rural areas. The Gavi-supported global OCV stockpile has recently faced shortages, with demand consistently outpacing supply despite annual increases in procurement; in fact, in October 2024, the stockpile was altogether depleted.1926,28 As of October 2022, the International Coordinating Group for vaccine delivery has thus restricted outbreak vaccination campaigns to one-dose vaccines (OCV1).29 Studies recently conducted in Bangladesh, South Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo suggest that short-term OCV1 effectiveness ranges from 40% to 80%, with subsequent waning.30,32 Most notably, long-term OCV1 effectiveness was higher in people older than 5, suggesting that previous exposure to cholera may help with immunological priming under a one-dose strategy.30,32 However, in emergency outbreak settings where the goal is to reduce the potential for transmission, short-term effectiveness is of higher priority. In most countries where both OCV2 and OCV1 campaigns were conducted, such short-term estimates of one-dose effectiveness largely align with their two-dose counterparts, adding to the evidence supporting the use of OCV1 when facing shortages.

While Cameroon has had recurrent outbreaks since reporting its first case in 1971,33 34 Haiti had no confirmed cholera case until 2010, in the aftermath of the deadly earthquake in Port-au-Prince. In Cameroon, the current outbreak began in 2021, whereas the first case of Haiti’s most recent outbreak was not reported until 2022; both countries deployed subsequent OCV1 campaigns as part of the global response.35,39 While the basic and effective reproduction numbers (R0 and Rt)—two related but distinct measures of transmissibility—have been used as the key metrics to understand cholera outbreaks, most research has employed mechanistic compartmental models requiring the availability of machine-readable surveillance data, computational resources, mathematical modelling expertise, and assumptions about the underlying disease process and corresponding parameterisation. Because such requirements are poorly suited to outbreak conditions like those in Haiti and Cameroon, we explored an alternative approach employing more parsimonious phenomenological models with non–machine-readable (NMR) surveillance data—that is, data not readily usable by computational programmes in their current format (eg, images in PDFs). Using Haiti and Cameroon as compelling natural case studies of the 20+ countries experiencing new or worsening outbreaks since 2022,4 we sought to investigate cholera transmission dynamics and evaluate OCV1 effectiveness in these two settings.

Methods

Basic and effective reproduction numbers

In epidemiology, the basic reproduction number (R0) measures the average number of secondary infections caused by a primary infection over the course of the infectious period, assuming the entire population is susceptible.40 When the population is not fully susceptible, due to either vaccination or natural immunity, the time-varying effective reproduction number (Rt) is used instead. Using available epidemic curves, Rt can be readily estimated at each time point t via the Bayesian framework by Cori et al and companion R package, EpiEstim.41,43 When vaccine coverage data are available, estimates of vaccine effectiveness can be derived from these estimates of Rt, along with R0 values obtained from the literature.

Data sourcing and cleaning

Lacking readily-accessible, machine-readable cholera surveillance data, we capitalised on published epidemic curves from situation reports and outbreak news reports. Although estimates of OCV1 coverage were reported infrequently, we extracted every mention for subsequent use in our vaccine effectiveness models. For Haiti, we leveraged situation reports released by the Pan-American Health Organisation (PAHO) and the Ministère de la Santé Publique et de la Population (MSPP).36 39 For Cameroon, we used WHO Disease Outbreak News (DON) reports.35

In Cameroon, the most recent outbreak of cholera was declared on 29 October 2021. By May 2022—the last published DON report at the time of analysis—over 6000 confirmed or suspected cases and 370 deaths had been registered.44 In Haiti, the most recent outbreak was confirmed on 1 October 2022, following a 3-year period without any cholera cases. By August 2023—the last report published by MSPP and PAHO at the time of analysis—over 58 000 confirmed or suspected cases and 800 deaths had been registered.36 38 39

Information about the serial interval (SI)—the time elapsed between the onset of symptoms in two consecutive cases—was obtained from previous modelling studies on cholera,45 46 as well as from prospective and retrospective epidemic assessments.47 48 Accordingly, we assumed that the SI followed a gamma distribution with a mean of 4 days and standard deviation of 3.45 46 Data on the historic R0 for cholera were gleaned from national and regional reports of prior outbreaks and other modelling studies. Given the importance of using country-specific or regional data to estimate R0, we used different priors for Haiti versus Cameroon. In our main analysis, we considered a mean R0 of 2.27 for Haiti, estimated using country-specific publications,49,53 and a mean R0 of 1.95 for Cameroon, estimated using one country-specific study54 and two regional analyses.45 46 While eligible priors for R0 were available by country in the literature, we did not find country-specific priors for Rt. Thus, in our main analysis, we used a gamma distribution with a mean of 2 and SD of 0.7 for the prior distribution of Rt, consistent with published research.45 46

In response to rising case and death counts, OCV1 campaigns were conducted in both countries. Cameroon launched an initial campaign in February 2022, followed by a second campaign in April 2022. In Haiti, the vaccination campaign started in mid-December 2022 and continued through January 2023. In Cameroon, the first campaign targeted the South-West region and reached a vaccination coverage of 85.5%, while the second campaign targeted the Littoral, South, and South-West regions and reached a vaccination coverage of 89.0%.35 In Haiti, the campaign reached a vaccination coverage of 76.0% on average, with marked geographical differences (ie, 69.9% in the Ouest department versus 100.0% in the Centre department).55

Digitising epidemic curves

In low-income settings, timely, open-access, and machine-readable surveillance data of infectious disease case counts are frequently inaccessible for modelling; thus, using alternative data sources is paramount. Distinctly, our approach started with the digitisation of case count data from outbreak and situation reports, often provided in a NMR format. In this study, we used the metaDigitise package in R for digitisation of these NMR images to obtain cholera case count trajectories in each country (online appendix figure S1).56

Epidemic data for Cameroon were reported weekly. Since the reporting time period (here, 7 days) was longer than the mean SI (here, 5 days), we transformed weekly incidence data into daily incidence data to align with EpiEstim conventions.57 Specifically, we used a uniform distribution of cases over the entire 7-day week (eg, 14 cases in week X=2 cases per day in week X). For Haiti, case counts were already reported daily and did not require any additional transformation. Of note, these epidemic curve data were the only NMR images converted to a digital format in this study.

Leveraging EpiEstim and VaxEstim in low-resource settings

We used the EpiEstim42 43 framework and corresponding R package to estimate weekly values of Rt, independently for Haiti and Cameroon. The procedure outputs a country-specific posterior distribution of Rt at each time point. Using the mean, 2.5th percentile, and 97.5th percentile, as well as the corresponding reported OCV1 coverage (VCt) and estimated R0 from the literature, we then employed the following canonical equation,58 previously referred to as VaxEstim,59 60 to estimate OCV1 effectiveness (VEt) in Haiti and Cameroon over time: VEt = [1-(Rt /R0)] / VCt.

Since vaccine effectiveness ranges from 0% to 100%, we removed estimates of VEt outside this range (online appendix table S1). Sensitivity analyses considered the rolling window length for EpiEstim estimation (online appendix figure S3), the impact of alternate parameterisations of the serial interval (online appendix figure S4), alternate estimates of OCV1 coverage on VEt (online appendix figure S5), changing our assumptions of R0on VEt (online appendix figure S6), using localised estimates of cholera cases and OCV1 coverage in Haiti (online appendix figure S7), and counterfactual scenarios of low to severe under-reporting (online appendix figure S8, S9, and S10). All analyses were performed using R V.4.3.1, and visualisations were created using the ggplot2 package.61 62

Results

In Cameroon, 6652 confirmed and suspected cases were officially reported between 10 October 2021 and 24 April 2022; in Haiti, 58 230 confirmed and suspected cases were reported between 1 October 2022 and 5 August 2023. In parallel, our digitisation of epidemic curves yielded a total of 6616 cases in Cameroon and 54 927 cases in Haiti, resulting in overall relative error rates of 0.5% and 5.7%, respectively (figure 1).

The y-axis shows all daily confirmed and suspected cases. For each country, colour-coded segments correspond to specific events: OCV1 campaigns (light grey); major rainfall, flooding events and related landslides (blue)86; droughts and low rainfall events (light beige)87; and earthquakes (dark green).88 These extreme weather events were sourced using an AI-assisted tool developed by our team.89 OCV1, one-dose vaccine.

The median Rt at the start of the second outbreak week was estimated at 1.90 (95% credible interval (CrI) 1.14 to 2.95) in Cameroon and 2.60 (95% CrI 2.42 to 2.79) in Haiti (figure 2). Interestingly, while Rt generally remained between 0.8 and 1.2 in Haiti, it was much more variable in Cameroon. Specifically, its trajectory showed a distinct oscillating pattern: Rt rose rapidly to 2.50 (1.88 to 3.25) in the third outbreak week, then fell steeply to 0.30 (0.15 to 0.52) and rose once more to 1.79 (1.32 to 2.37) before settling around 1, the threshold above which an outbreak propagates. In both countries, Rt values in subsequent periods hovered around this critical threshold. In Cameroon, the overall median Rt was 1.07 (0.52 to 1.94), and nearly two-thirds (126 out of 195, or 64.6%) of all median Rt estimates were above 1. In contrast, in Haiti, the overall median Rt was 0.97 (0.80 to 1.38); only about one-third (105 out of 302 or 34.8%) of median estimates were above 1, indicating less potential for an explosive outbreak. Despite the proportion of days with a median Rt over 1 being higher in Cameroon than in Haiti (64.6% versus 34.8%), Haiti reported a larger number of outbreak-period cases than Cameroon (58 230 versus 6652). This is especially striking as Haiti’s population is about half that of Cameroon,63 resulting in a cholera infection rate of 50.6 per 10 000 people in the former versus 2.4 per 10 000 people in the latter.

Using published estimates of R0 and the aforementioned administrative statistics for vaccine coverage, we then estimated time-varying OCV1 effectiveness for Cameroon and Haiti separately, following the initiation of vaccination campaigns in each country (figure 3). In Cameroon, the median OCV1 effectiveness over 18 February to 30 April 2022 was estimated at 54.9% (18.9 to 84.9%); it ranged from a minimum of 17.4% (23 March) to a maximum of 90.4% (17 April). In Haiti, the median OCV1 effectiveness over 19 December 2022 to 5 August 2023 was estimated at 75.3% (54.0% to 86.4%); it ranged from a minimum of 51.3% (1 May) to a maximum of 96.9% (5 August).

The start date for our estimates corresponds to the first day following the beginning of the vaccination campaign (ie, 18 February 2022 in Cameroon and mid-December 2022 in Haiti). Estimates were obtained using the EpiEstim package in R with the VaxExtim extension to calculate vaccine effectiveness over time.

Median OCV1 effectiveness is best understood when contrasted with the herd immunity threshold—the level at which the population is no longer susceptible to disease transmission, thanks to immunity acquired through either immunisation or previous infection. To calculate such country-specific thresholds, we used the standard approximation of 1-(1 /R0) for herd immunity64 and sourced country-specific R0 values from the literature. We found that the median OCV1 effectiveness estimates in Cameroon and Haiti exceeded the calculated herd immunity thresholds of 48.7% and 55.9%, respectively. However, the estimated median OCV1 effectiveness in each country dropped below these thresholds at certain times, suggesting periods when sustained transmission occurred.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to contrast cholera outbreaks in two countries and assess the impact of OCV1 campaigns on local transmission using open-access, NMR outbreak case data and the R package EpiEstim. Additionally, our work provides the first application of the VaxEstim extension to low-resource settings.59 60 Digitisation methods used to extract epidemic curve data from NMR images yielded an overall relative discrepancy between actual (published) and inferred cumulative case counts of ≤6%. For Haiti, where the overall error rate was notably higher (ie, 5.7% versuss 0.5% in Cameroon) and the magnitude of the outbreak was much larger, we additionally performed a visual inspection by overlaying our digitised epidemic curve with its published counterpart; the published and digitised case count trajectories were largely similar throughout the study period (online appendix figure S2). By using data from situation reports and DON reports, along with parameters extracted from the literature, we demonstrate that EpiEstim and VaxEstim can be easily and quickly employed during outbreaks occurring in data-scarce settings to aid in decision-making and monitoring. Going forward, we encourage other infectious disease researchers and mathematical modellers to consider such tools when data are not immediately accessible in a machine-readable format, thereby reducing the burden of time-consuming data access requests on local public health officers.

In both Haiti and Cameroon, outbreaks initially caused large surges in Rt. Although point estimates for Rt in Haiti (2.60 (2.42–2.79)) were higher than those in Cameroon (1.90 (1.14–2.95)), the corresponding credible intervals partially overlapped, owing in part to greater uncertainty in Rt estimates in Cameroon. Notably, our results corroborate several patterns of cholera transmission reported in prior studies. First, the order of magnitude of our early-outbreak estimates largely aligns with published values of R0, whether specifically in Haiti and Cameroon or in other sub-Saharan countries.4546 49,54 While R0 and Rt are markedly different metrics, studies have relied on Rt estimates obtained during the first week of an outbreak to generate an ‘early outbreak reproduction number’.46 Indeed, such a value can be more closely compared with R0 than later Rt estimates because interventions to curb transmission are rarely implemented in the week following the index case.46 Second, in both countries, these initial periods, marked by high values of Rt, were followed by longer periods during which Rt oscillated around the critical threshold of 1 but was never sufficiently low for transmission to cease completely. This temporal pattern is consistent with prior analyses of Rt dynamics for cholera,4565,67 a finding that is particularly reassuring since most previous analyses capitalised on ‘gold standard’ machine-readable data sources or mechanistic models, as opposed to the NMR data and parsimonious phenomenological modelling approach we use here.

Most importantly, our results suggest that OCV1 campaigns in Haiti and Cameroon were generally effective, corroborating field surveys of OCV1 effectiveness conducted in other countries, including Bangladesh, South Sudan, and the Democratic Republic of the Congo.30,32 The absence of a statistically significant difference in OCV1 effectiveness between Haiti and Cameroon suggests that the effectiveness of the vaccine was similar across both contexts. This key finding indicates that the success of OCV1 campaigns depends less on external factors than do other vaccination campaigns (eg, cold-chain maintenance for routine immunisations like the oral polio vaccine,68 higher dropout rates for diphtheria–tetanus–pertussis three-dose vaccine and several other routine immunisations requiring multiple doses.)69 70 From a global health equity standpoint, such robustness in vaccine effectiveness across differing country settings is encouraging: it signals that the implementation of OCV1 campaigns in response to new outbreaks should work similarly in other locations. Of note, estimates of OCV1 coverage were highly variable over the study period, particularly in Cameroon; thus, they should be interpreted in light of all available evidence. Moreover, vaccine effectiveness results derived from VaxEstim are subject to the same limitations as with EpiEstim: namely, the effects of vaccine-conferred immunity cannot be fully disentangled from infection-conferred immunity nor from non-pharmaceutical intervention (NPI)-conferred immunity (such as handwashing campaigns or water treatment). In addition, vaccine effectiveness estimates can be affected by environmental factors, under-reporting, and misdiagnosis. However, in many outbreak settings, data to guide decision-making is exceptionally sparse; under these conditions, the estimates generated by EpiEstim and VaxEstim can provide valuable guidance and inform response efforts.

Since one-dose campaigns are relatively new, the literature on OCV1 field studies is still sparse. Field-based assessments of ongoing or upcoming OCV1 campaigns will provide confirmatory evidence regarding vaccine effectiveness. Meanwhile, our proposed method complements on-the-ground surveys but requires much less time and funding to conduct. As demonstrated through our case studies in Haiti and Cameroon, our approach would be especially relevant in locations lacking resources to implement field studies of vaccine effectiveness. Moreover, using epidemic curve digitisation and the EpiEstim package early in an outbreak could provide near real-time information about ongoing transmission, while repeated applications of VaxEstim could provide dynamic insights into the local effectiveness of vaccine allocation programmes. Nevertheless, the local drivers of disease transmission and barriers to efficient vaccine delivery should also be considered when interpreting results derived from this approach, as noted variations in vaccine effectiveness were observed.

Given that Haiti has much higher population density than Cameroon (415 versus 58 people per km2),71 we hypothesise that this heightened opportunity for contact influenced disease transmission dynamics, resulting in explosive early transmission in the outbreak when the population was highly susceptible.72 73 In addition, lower access to improved water sources in Haiti (68.3% versus 73.9%) and the high frequency and magnitude of extreme weather events and heavy rainfall in 2022–2023 (figure 1) likely contributed to heightened early-cholera transmission in Haiti.74,77 In contrast, given that cholera has been circulating in Cameroon for over 50 years, we hypothesise that local transmission is affected more by health behaviours and practices than by environmental factors alone, with local knowledge gleaned through frequent outbreaks reinforcing behaviours that reduce onward transmission, such as handwashing and water treatment.

Thus, in allocating scarce OCV1 stores, the magnitude and impact of extreme weather events on health infrastructure and further spread of V. cholerae should be considered in tandem with local knowledge about existing endemicity and effective behavioural NPIs. One consideration may be to preemptively allocate vaccines to locations affected by extremely volatile weather. Another consideration is to plan routine vaccination campaigns in locations where cholera reoccurs endemically, such as Cameroon. We believe such prioritisation will be especially beneficial when paired with behavioural interventions, such as handwashing, water filtration, and chlorination.78

Limitations

While the digitisation of NMR epidemic curves allows modellers to expand outbreak analyses by leveraging static data sources, their use brings some challenges. First, as illustrated by our case study, the digitisation of epidemic curves can result in marginally discrepant case counts. Although the discrepancy between digitised and reported case counts did not substantially impact our analysis, outbreak trajectories are not always well-captured by the digitisation process (eg, a low number of incident cases may be indistinguishable from zero when daily case counts are otherwise high). Nonetheless, for many outbreaks, the digitisation of NMR images of epidemic curves enables computational analyses that were previously infeasible.

Second, our results rely on the case definition of cholera, which frequently encompasses all incidents of acute watery diarrhoea during an outbreak.79,82 As such, disentangling cholera infections from other drivers of diarrhoea (eg, norovirus, Salmonella, or Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli) remains a challenge both in the field and during analysis.83 Although OCV is not efficacious against non-cholera watery diarrhoea, routine, non-vaccination treatment, including oral rehydration therapy, is similar regardless of causative pathogen.84 Thus, understanding incidence patterns of all acute watery diarrhoea is important in its own right. Relatedly, because many episodes of acute watery diarrhoea are mild to moderate and go under-reported, a broader case definition is advised during outbreak settings to maximise sensitivity.79 80 Although Haiti and Cameroon established suspected case definitions in line with WHO guidance for outbreak settings, different definitions of confirmed cases were applied.81 82 For our analysis, this difference was not meaningful, as we evaluated all suspected and confirmed cases; however, an analysis focused exclusively on confirmed cases could generate biased and incomparable results. Given the likelihood of under-reporting of cholera—due to weakened health systems, mild symptomology or the aforementioned case definition ambiguity—we conducted a sensitivity analysis to examine the impact of under-reporting at various stages of the outbreak.85 With minor (20%) to moderate (50%) under-reporting, differences in estimated Rt were limited, affecting only short time periods; however, under conditions of severe under-reporting (80%), more pervasive differences in Rt were observed (online appendix figure S8-S10). In light of this sensitivity analysis, additional caution should be taken when severe under-reporting is suspected.

Third, since phenomenological models like those underlying EpiEstim do not rely on assumptions about the disease process and generally require few parameters, they are often less precise than mechanistic compartmental models. One notable example is that it can be difficult to disentangle the impact of interventions versus transmission-mediated depletion of susceptible populations, among other influential factors, resulting in high variability in one location over time. This is additionally true for our estimates of vaccine effectiveness where improved observed effectiveness may be driven by reduced susceptibility over time, due to other interventions aimed at reducing transmission, or the effect of the vaccine itself. However, a trade-off exists between the precision of epidemiological models and their practicality and relevance for decision makers. Here, we demonstrated that EpiEstim allows estimations of Rt— even early in an outbreak—without requiring any additional information beyond the SI of a disease and an up-to-date epidemic curve. This built-in R package is highly accessible, enabling decision makers at all levels to make informed choices that are locally applicable and timely.

Fourth, we relied on published reports of vaccine coverage, which were relatively sparse. Therefore, we did not have access to the exact location or timing of OCV1 delivery; instead, we used the overall coverage as reported. Specifically, we assumed a simplified coverage timeline, with instantaneous coverage on the first reported date. However, through this approximation, we were able to extend the application of VaxEstim to places where vaccine coverage surveys were not conducted or where the resulting data are not openly available. Moreover, to estimate vaccine effectiveness, we combined information at two different geographical resolutions: our vaccine coverage data represent only specific regions of each country, corresponding to locations where campaigns were conducted, whereas outbreak case data are generally available and analysed at the national level. However, in Haiti and Cameroon, OCV1 campaigns targeted the most affected regions, likely accounting for a substantial portion of all cases. Notably, in our sensitivity analysis focused on the Ouest department of Haiti (online appendix figure S7), we found slightly higher vaccine effectiveness than in our main, country-level analysis, yet the difference was not statistically significant. Lastly, our estimates reflect the effectiveness of OCV1 interventions only in the short term, and such effectiveness likely wanes over time. However, given that rapid response is critical amidst outbreaks, understanding short-term effectiveness remains the top priority. In summary, the robustness of our findings underscores the need for conducting epidemiological analyses early in an outbreak to enable targeted decision making and emphasises the utility of our data digitisation, transmission modelling, and vaccine effectiveness estimation methods for modelling epidemic cholera.
