A pH/ATP Dual-Sensitive Nanoprobe-Based Bioluminescence Imaging for Intraoperative Human Multicancer Detection
Authors: Xiaofeng He, Jiahui Wang, Junwei Li, Yangsi Zheng, Wenqi Shi, Jiatian Song, Jiayu Li, Zhiyong Wu, Menglong Zhao, Tiantian Zhai, Siqi Qiu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current intraoperative cancer diagnosis predominantly relies on frozen section histopathology, which is time-consuming and prone to sampling bias. Development of techniques for intraoperative cancer diagnosis is urgently needed. Notably, malignant tumors manifest distinctive microenvironmental signatures characterized by acidic pH gradients and elevated ATP concentrations, which are important biomarkers for tumor identification. Based on these signatures, a pH/ATP dual-sensitive bioluminescent nanoprobe, d-luciferase-liposome-PEOz (DLPNPs), was engineered using thin-film hydration. The nanoprobe displayed a spherical morphology (∼140 nm in diameter), low pH-dependent activation (pH <6.5), and good biocompatibility in vitro. Ex vivo bioluminescence imaging of freshly resected specimens from five murine tumor models (breast, liver, colon, lung, and tongue) and five human cancer types (breast, colorectal, endometrial, lung, and gastric) demonstrated significantly elevated tumor signals compared to normal tissues. The median tumor-to-background ratios (TBRs) were 21.49 (IQR, 8.86–105.7) in mice and 47.89 (IQR, 5.27–238.9) in humans. The nanoprobe achieved 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity in cancer diagnosis, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 1.00 across all cancer types evaluated in both mouse and human tissues. Microscopic analysis further confirmed precise colocalization between bioluminescent signals and malignant regions. Notably, the entire workflow, from nanoprobe application to image acquisition, requires less than 10 min. These findings indicate that DLPNPs enable rapid and accurate intraoperative tumor diagnosis by leveraging tumor microenvironmental signatures. The multicancer applicability, high TBR, and streamlined workflow indicate that DLPNPs are worthy of further evaluation for clinical translation in future studies with larger sample sizes and more diverse cancer cohorts.

Introduction

Cancer remains a major global health challenge, with 20 million new cases and 9.7 million deaths annually as reported in GLOBOCAN 2022. Surgery remains one of the cornerstones for cancer treatment. Achieving a negative surgical margin is essential for cancer surgery as positive margins increase the risk of tumor recurrence. , However, positive margin rates are high in current practice, such as 20–40% in patients with breast cancer who receive breast-conserving surgery. − Methods, such as frozen sectioning, have been explored for intraoperative tumor diagnosis to reduce positive surgical margin rates. However, these techniques have limitations, such as time-consuming or sampling errors. − Therefore, there is an urgent need for development of novel methods that provide more efficient and more precise intraoperative feedback to surgeons for assisting intraoperative decision-making and lowering positive margin rates.

Fluorescent probes have emerged as a powerful diagnostic tool for surgical oncologists to visualize tumor cells. , Although the antibody-targeted probes can offer high specificity in tumor detection, their clinical application has been hampered by the heterogeneous expression of targeted markers among tumors and patients. To address this limitation, researchers have been increasingly focusing on the tumor microenvironment as a generic target for the development of novel fluorescent probes. , One of the key differences between tumor and normal tissues in the microenvironment is the pH value, which is generally acidic in the tumor microenvironment, primarily due to the glycolytic metabolism in cancer cells. , The pH value in tumor tissue usually lies between 6.7 and 7.1, and in extreme cases, it goes down to 6.0, compared with about 7.4 in normal tissues. , Consequently, this pH disparity between tumor and normal tissue makes pH a promising biomarker to differentiate malignant from normal tissues. In a Phase I clinical trial, a pH-sensitive fluorescent probe was tested in 30 patients with different cancer types, including breast, head, and neck cancers, providing preliminary evidence to support the use of a pH-sensitive probe for tumor detection. However, the 33% false-positive tumor detection rate as demonstrated in this Phase I study also highlights the need to improve the diagnostic accuracy of pH-sensitive probes. The false-positive tumor detection by pH-sensitive probes is mainly due to their nonspecific interactions with acidic environments outside the tumor, like inflamed tissues or paratumoral areas.

Another distinct feature of the tumor microenvironment compared to normal tissue is the high ATP level due to the high metabolic activity of tumor cells. In the high-ATP environment, luciferase catalyzes its substrate luciferin, resulting in bioluminescence emission at 560 nm. , This feature could be applied to reduce the false-positive rate of pH-sensitive probes by designing a probe that can only be activated in the low pH and high ATP environment.

In this study, we aim to develop a dual-sensitive nanoprobe, d-Luciferase-Liposome-PEOz (DLPNPs), that is activated by a low pH and high ATP condition (Figure ). We first validated the activation condition of this nanoprobe in in vitro cell experiments. After that, the performance of the nanoprobe for ex vivo tumor detection was evaluated using tumor samples from different tumor-bearing mouse models. Finally, we assessed its accuracy in ex vivo human cancer detection in a multicancer setting, demonstrating the potential of using the dual-sensitive nanoprobe to assist quick intraoperative decision-making in surgical oncology.

Schematic diagram of the mechanism of action of the nanoprobe in the tumor microenvironment. Under acidic conditions, protonation of the DSPE-PEOz component in d-Luciferase-Liposome-PEOz (DLPNPs) leads to destabilization and disruption of its lipid bilayer structure. The tumor microenvironment is characterized by the low pH and high ATP conditions. Upon exposure to the tumor microenvironment, the DLPNPs is activated by the acidic condition, leading to the pH-sensitive release of luciferase. Simultaneously, bioluminescence is generated by the catalysis of luciferin by luciferase in an ATP-dependent way in the tumor microenvironment. In contrast, normal tissues do not exhibit bioluminescence due to the absence of the conditions that are required for the activation of the nanoprobe.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of DLPNPs

The pH- and ATP-sensitive nanoprobe, DLPNPs, was synthesized using the thin-film hydration method. Lecithin, cholesterol, and 1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-Poly­(2-ethyl-2-oxazoline) (DSPE-PEOz) were dissolved in chloroform at a molar ratio of 2:1:0.6 in a round-bottom flask. DSPE-PEOz is an amphiphilic block copolymer composed of hydrophobic distearoylphosphatidylethanolamine and hydrophilic poly. DSPE-PEOz segment contains tertiary amines in its side chains, which protonate in acidic environments, conferring pH-responsive membrane properties to the lipid vesicles. The chloroform was then evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator, followed by a 12-h vacuum treatment to ensure complete removal of residual solvent. To hydrate the lipid film, 1 mg of d-luciferase was dissolved in 3 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) devoid of Ca2+ and Mg2+. This solution was added to the lipid film, and the mixture was incubated at 42 °C for 30 min. The resulting suspension was then sonicated to form a transparent Lu-liposome-PEOz solution. The solution was subsequently purified via ultracentrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C. The purified liposome was then passed through a liposome extruder fitted with filters of varying pore sizes to obtain the final DLPNPs product with a size of around 140 nm in diameter.

Characterization

The surface morphology and size of the DLPNPs nanoprobe were analyzed using the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM-F200, Japan). The TEM images provide detailed information on the shape and structural features of the nanoprobe. The average hydrodynamic diameter was measured using a Malvern Zetasizer Ultra (Malvern, UK), allowing for the assessment of particle size distribution in suspension.

The pH/ATP Dual-Sensitive Performance of DLPNPs

To evaluate the pH sensitivity of DLPNPs, solutions (0.5 mg/mL) with varying pH values (from 5.6 to 7.4 with an interval of 0.3) were first prepared. Thereafter, 0.5 mg/mL DLPNPs and 4 mM ATP were added into each solution. A positive control group containing free luciferase and ATP was also prepared for comparison. In addition, solutions with varying pH values (from 5.6 to 7.4 with an interval of 0.3) and containing 0.5 mg/mL DLPNPs but without ATP were used to evaluate the ATP responsiveness of the nanoprobe. Bioluminescence imaging (BI) was performed using the AniView100 Imaging System (BLT, China) at the 560 nm channel. The mean bioluminescence radiance (MBR) of each solution was calculated.

Cell Culture

The ex vivo dual-sensitive property and cytotoxicity of the nanoprobe were evaluated using the 4T1 murine breast tumor cells (ATCC, USA). The 4T1 cell line, mouse liver cancer cell line HEPA1–6 (ATCC, USA), mouse colon cancer cell line CT26WT (ATCC, USA), human tongue squamous cell carcinoma cell line CAL-27 (ATCC, USA), and human lung cancer cell line A549 (ATCC, USA) were cultured to establish the tumor-bearing mouse models. The 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Bioss, C7149, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, 10099141C, Australia) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Innochem, B46667, Korea). HEPA1–6, CT26WT, CAL-27, and A549 were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Gibco, 11965092, USA) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

Dual-Sensitive Performance of DLPNPs in In Vitro Cell Experiments

In a 35 mm cell culture dish, 1 × 106 4T1 cells were cultured and subjected to different experimental conditions: DLPNPs + pH 6.5, luciferin + pH 6.5, DLPNPs + luciferin + pH 7.4, and DLPNPs + luciferin + pH 6.5. The concentration of DLPNPs was set at 0.5 mg/mL, while luciferin was administered at 10 mM. BI was performed using the AniView100 Imaging System at the 560 nm channel to evaluate the nanoprobe’s responsiveness under various conditions at the cellular level.

Cytotoxicity Assay with DLPNPs

4T1 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated with varying concentrations of DLPNPs for 24 h. Following the incubation period, the cytotoxicity of DLPNPs was assessed using the Cell Counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The assay provided a quantitative measure of cell viability, indicating the potential cytotoxic effects of the nanoprobe on the 4T1 cells.

Animal Model Establishment and Ex Vivo BI of Fresh Tumor Specimens Incubated with DLPNPs

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of Shantou University Medical College (approval number: SUMC2020–273). Female BALB/c-nude mice, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing 17–20 g, were used for tumor model establishment. Each mouse was inoculated in the right axillary region with 1 × 106 tumor cells suspended in 0.1 mL of PBS. The cell lines used for inoculation included breast tumor 4T1, hepatocellular carcinoma HEPA1–6, colon cancer CT26WT, tongue squamous cell carcinoma CAL-27, and lung cancer A549, with five mice assigned to each tumor type. Mice were maintained on an alfalfa-free diet throughout the study. When the tumors reached an approximate size of 300–500 mm3, the mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation under isoflurane anesthesia. Tumors and corresponding normal tissues (e.g., breast for breast tumor, liver for liver tumor, and so on) were then surgically excised. Immediately after excision, the specimens were rinsed with PBS to remove residual blood. Each sample was incubated with 1 mL of DLPNPs at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in PBS for 5 min. Excess nanoprobes were washed away three times for 10 s each with PBS. And then 0.1 mL of 10 mM luciferin was added for BI. BI of the tumors and corresponding normal tissues was performed using the AniView100 Imaging System at the 560 nm channel. Following imaging, the tumor and normal tissue specimens were fixed with 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for pathological examination through hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining.

Patient Population and Ex Vivo BI of Human Fresh Surgical Specimens Incubated with DLPNPs

To further validate the nanoprobe’s performance in cancer detection, we conducted BI experiments on human fresh surgical specimens. The Medical Ethics Committee of the Shantou Central Hospital approved this study (approval number: [2024] Keyan-060). Specimens were obtained from patients who underwent tumor resection surgeries at Shantou Central Hospital between November 2023 and January 2024. The experiment included specimens from patients with breast cancer, colorectal cancer, endometrial cancer, lung cancer, and gastric cancer, with three cases for each cancer type. Clinicopathological data, such as age at diagnosis, TNM staging, histology, vascular invasion, and tumor size, were extracted from electronic medical records. Immediately after surgical excision, both tumor and corresponding normal tissue specimens were collected and preserved in PBS at 37 °C. To maintain tissue viability, transportation from the operating room to the laboratory was completed within 5 min. Each sample was incubated with 1 mL of DLPNPs at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in PBS for 5 min. Specimens were then washed three times for 10 s each with PBS. And then 0.1 mL of 10 mM luciferin was added for BI. BI was then conducted using the AniView100 Imaging System at the 560 nm channel. Following imaging, the tumor and normal tissue specimens were fixed with 10% formalin, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned for pathological examination through HE staining.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis of bioluminescence images was performed by using the software of AniView100 Imaging System (BLT, China) and ImageJ (RRID: SCR_003070). Normality testing (Shapiro-Wilk test, α = 0.05) confirmed that MBR data for both tumor and matched normal tissue samples violated assumptions of Gaussian distribution (p < 0.05 for all cohorts). Since the probe was designed to generate a stronger signal in the tumor region, we prespecified a directional hypothesis. Consequently, nonparametric statistical analyses were performed using one-tailed Mann–Whitney U tests to compare the differences in the bioluminescence signal between tumor tissues and their normal counterparts. Data were presented as bar graphs, where central tendencies were represented by medians and dispersion by interquartile ranges (IQR). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn, and the areas under the curves (AUCs) were used to assess the diagnostic performance of DLPNPs. The cutoff value was determined using Youden’s index, and sensitivity and specificity were then calculated based on it to assess the diagnostic accuracy of DLPNPs. All statistical computations and visualizations were executed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (RRID: SCR_016479) and GraphPad Prism 9.5 (RRID: SCR_002798), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Characterization of DLPNPs

Following the synthesis of DLPNPs, preliminary characterization was performed using TEM (Figure a). The TEM images revealed spherical nanoparticles with uniform morphology, confirming the successful assembly of the components. To further characterize the nanoprobe, dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements were conducted, indicating an average hydrodynamic diameter of 140.7 ± 2.1 nm (Figure a). The zeta potential of the DLPNPs was measured to be −17.68 ± 0.47 mV, indicating a moderately negative surface charge that contributes to colloidal stability (Figure b). This size range is optimal for efficient cellular uptake and transport, suggesting the potential of this nanoprobe for further applications. Moreover, the DLPNPs exhibited good storage stability, with no significant changes in hydrodynamic size observed over 7 days when stored in PBS at 4 °C (Figure c).

In Vitro Responsiveness of DLPNPs

The pH responsiveness of the nanoprobe was initially observed under TEM. At pH 7.4, the nanoprobe maintained an intact spherical structure with a smooth surface. However, as the pH decreased to 6.5 and 5.6, the surface of the nanoprobe became disrupted, adopting a half-moon shape under the microscope (Figure d). This morphological change indicates the nanoprobe’s sensitivity to acidic conditions.

BI further confirmed the nanoprobe’s pH responsiveness at a macroscopic level. In solutions with ATP, the bioluminescent signal began to appear when the pH was at 6.5, with further increase in signal intensity at lower pH levels. However, in the solutions without ATP, no apparent bioluminescent signal was observed even when the pH decreased to 5.6. These resulted in a significant difference in the bioluminescence intensity between the two groups when the pH level was at 6.5 or lower (Figure e). These results demonstrate the nanoprobe’s dual responsiveness to pH and ATP.

Preliminary assessment of the nanoprobe’s biocompatibility was conducted using the CCK-8 assay. No apparent cytotoxicity was found in 4T1 cells after 24-h exposure to different concentrations of DLPNPs. The cell viability was higher than 95% even when the concentration of DLPNPs was as high as 1 mg/mL (Figure f). Additionally, in vitro cell experiments showed that the bioluminescent signal was generated only when both low pH and high ATP conditions were present (Figure g), indicating its potential for precise tumor detection.

The Performance of DLPNPs in Ex Vivo Tumor Detection in Mice Freshly Resected Tissues

As shown in Figure a and Figure S1, ex vivo BI demonstrated significantly brighter bioluminescent signal in tumor tissues compared to corresponding normal tissues across different tumor types from mice. In the multicancer setting, the median MBR of all tumor tissues was 3.51 × 107 (IQR, 3.29 × 107-3.63 × 107), markedly higher than that of normal tissues, which had a median MBR of 0.16 × 107 (IQR, 0.03 × 107-0.38 × 107, p < 0.0001) (Figure b). The median tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was 21.49 (IQR, 8.86–105.7) as shown in Figure S2. The subgroup analysis confirmed a significant difference in MBR between tumors and corresponding normal tissues across various cancer types, including breast, lung, liver, tongue, and colorectal cancers (p < 0.01). DLPNPs exhibited outstanding performance in discriminating tumor tissues from normal tissues, with AUCs of 1.000 (95% confidence interval [CI], 1.000–1.000) in both the multicancer setting and subgroup analysis (Figure c). These findings underscore the high specificity and broad applicability of DLPNPs for ex vivo tumor detection in mice experiments.

Microanalysis of Bioluminescence Distribution within Mice Freshly Resected Tumors

To further investigate the correlation between the distribution of bioluminescence and tumor regions, we performed spatial registration analysis at the microscopic level by aligning histopathological information with ex vivo BI. As shown in the representative images in Figure a, the regions with bioluminescence in BI almost perfectly overlapped with tumor areas identified in HE-stained sections, while areas lacking bioluminescence were confirmed to be normal or stromal tissues. Further analysis of the BI demonstrated distinct high-bioluminescence peaks within tumors and low-bioluminescence baselines in surrounding nontumor areas, effectively delineating tumor margins (Figure b). These findings underscore the precision of the nanoprobe in distinguishing tumor boundaries and its potential for intraoperative margin assessment.

Across the entire patient cohort, the median MBR of tumor tissues was 5.18 × 107 (IQR, 4.44 × 107-5.27 × 107), significantly higher than that of normal tissues, being 0.11 × 107 (IQR, 0.02 × 107-0.84 × 107) (p < 0.0001) (Figure b). Subgroup analysis demonstrated a consistent trend of a higher median MBR of cancer tissues as compared to that of normal tissues across five different cancer types, including breast cancer, colon cancer, lung cancer, esophageal cancer, and endometrial cancer (Figure b). This emphasizes the generalizability of the nanoprobe for ex vivo cancer diagnosis. Furthermore, both overall and subgroup analyses demonstrated that DLPNPs achieved an AUC of 1.000 (95% CI, 1.000–1.000) for ex vivo cancer differentiation, with a median TBR of 47.89 (IQR, 5.271–238.9), indicating exceptional capability in tumor discrimination (Figure c and Figure S2).

Microanalysis of Bioluminescence Distribution within Human Freshly Resected Cancers

Accurate intraoperative assessment of tumor margins is critical for surgical oncology. Beyond evaluation of the diagnostic capability of DLPNPs at the macroscopic level, we further performed microscopic analyses to investigate DLPNPs’ potential in tumor boundary delineation. Correlative mapping of ex vivo BI with histopathological sections revealed enhanced bioluminescent signals in histologically confirmed tumor regions compared to adjacent normal tissues­(Figure a). Notably, the bioluminescence detected a microscopic tumor focus that was missed by visual inspection (<3 mm) in a specimen from a patient with gastric cancer, suggesting DLPNPs’ capability for detecting millimeter-scale tumor lesions (Figure a). Quantitative analysis of bioluminescence intensity along the linear lines further showed a sharp bioluminescence intensity gradient across tumor-normal interfaces, reflecting superior spatial resolution for margin delineation (Figure b). These findings highlight the excellent performance of DLPNPs-based BI for intraoperative multicancer diagnosis and tumor margin delineation.

Discussion

In this study, we developed a pH/ATP dual-sensitive nanoprobe, DLPNPs, to address the limitations of current intraoperative diagnostic methods. By leveraging the tumor microenvironment’s acidic pH and elevated ATP levels, DLPNPs achieved rapid and precise cancer detection in both murine and human ex vivo specimens, offering a novel solution for intraoperative cancer diagnosis.

TBR is a critical metric to evaluate the potential of an intraoperative imaging modality as a diagnostic tool for cancer detection. Commonly, a TBR of higher than three is considered acceptable to distinguish tumor from normal tissues. Molecular-targeted probes, such as folate receptor- or VEGF-targeted probes, achieved TBRs ranging from 3.1 to 18.7. − However, their performance in cancer detection is affected by the heterogeneity in the expression of targets among different cancer types as well as different patients with the same cancer type. − In this study, DLPNPs achieved exceptionally high TBRs (>20) in both murine models and human specimens. This high TBR directly contributed to its outstanding diagnostic performance in cancer detection.

Current techniques for intraoperative cancer diagnosis are constrained by several limitations. Traditional pathological methods, such as frozen section analysis, have decreased the re-excision rate from 27% to 6%. However, the whole process of frozen sectioning takes more than 30 min, significantly prolonging the operation time. Another pathological technique, imprint cytology, achieves rapid evaluation of surgical margins. Nevertheless, its diagnostic sensitivity is heavily influenced by tumor size and pathologists’ expertise, resulting in unstable outcomes. Other imaging techniques have also been explored for intraoperative cancer diagnosis with moderate accuracy. For example, the sensitivity and specificity of intraoperative ultrasound in breast cancer diagnosis were reported to be 72% and 78%, respectively, while these were only 52% and 77% for intraoperative mammography and 68% and 69% for intraoperative micro-CT. Dedicated devices like the ClearEdge system demonstrated a false-positive rate of as high as 20% and a false-negative rate of 15%, and the MarginProbe system exhibited false-positive rates ranging from 13% to 54% for surgical margin assessment in breast-conserving surgery. − These error-prone approaches frequently cause over-resection of healthy tissues or under-resection of residual tumor. The sensitivity and specificity of cancer diagnosis by the click-to-sense technology were reported to be 93.3% and 98.3%, respectively, and these were 91–97% and 86–93% by confocal microscopy. , Nevertheless, their prohibitive costs and technical barriers persistently hinder widespread implementation.

In our experiments, DLPNPs demonstrated 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity in ex vivo cancer diagnosis across diverse cancer types, which were superior to the above-mentioned conventional and novel intraoperative diagnostic techniques. The high diagnostic accuracy of DLPNPs is mainly due to its dual responsiveness to pH and ATP. This could also explain its high diagnostic performance in gastric cancer specimens, as the residual gastric acid in normal gastric tissue theoretically could have an impact on probes that are sensitive only to pH changes, while it has minimal influence on our pH and ATP dual-sensitive probe. These data support the DLPNPs-based bioluminescence as an effective novel technique for multicancer intraoperative diagnosis.

In the present study, we found minor inconsistency in the tumor margin delineation between bioluminescence and HE staining. This may arise from the tissue processing for pathological analysis, including fixation, dehydration, clearing and paraffin embedding. In the contrary, the bioluminescence imaging was performed on fresh tissues, which status were intact during imaging procedure. We also found heterogeneous signal distribution in the bioluminecent images, which is likely to reflect the heterogeneity of pH and ATP levels within the tumor tissues, a feature that cannot be captured by HE staining. These signal variations may provide functional information regarding the spatial heterogeneity of tumor metabolism. We will further explore the potential of DLPNPs in revealing the tumor biological heterogeneity in subsequent studies.

The potential for clinical translation of DLPNPs lies in its seamless integration with the intraoperative workflow. Traditional frozen section histopathology relies on multidepartmental collaboration (such as tissue handling, sectioning, and analysis by the department of pathology), and usually takes up to 30 min to 1 h. This significantly prolongs the operation time and increases patient risks. In contrast, the DLPNPs-based BI significantly simplifies the diagnostic process. It takes only 10 min from probe incubation to result interpretation, and can be independently done by surgeons in the operating room without the need for input from other departments. This provides quick feedback to surgeons and significantly improves the efficiency of intraoperative decision-making, especially for complex tumor resection surgeries that require immediate assessment of the margin status. In this study, we established a rapid standardized intraoperative diagnostic workflow that ensures immediate processing of tissues following resection, thereby preventing the potential limitation of probe activation failure due to ATP degradation over time. This standardized workflow would promote the clinical translation of this technique.

Another advantage of DLPNPs-based BI is its versatility for diagnosis of multiple cancer types. Leveraging the tumor microenvironment as a biomarker renders its application not restricted to any single specific cancer. The consistent results obtained in this study across five murine cancer models and five human cancer types provide preliminary support for their broad applicability. However, this study has several limitations. First, as a proof-of-concept study, we established the performance benchmark of DLPNPs using normal tissues as controls; however, its specificity has not yet been validated in more clinically challenging scenarios such as inflamed or reactive paratumoral tissues, which represent a critical step toward clinical translation. Second, the activation of DLPNPs relies on an acidic and high-ATP tumor microenvironment. Its applicability to tumors that are lack of these typical features, such as certain hypometabolic or neurogenic tumors, remains to be further explored. Third, the high AUC may due to the small sample size in the present study. Together, these limitations highlight the importance for future validation and refinement of this technique in larger and more diverse cancer cohorts with various benign control tissues.

Conclusions

This study developed a pH/ATP dual-sensitive nanoprobe, DLPNPs, leveraging the acidic pH and elevated ATP levels of tumor microenvironment. The DLPNPs-based BI demonstrated excellent performance in intraoperative rapid tumor diagnosis across diverse murine and human cancer types. Further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to confirm the multicancer diagnostic utility of this novel technique.
