Hearing Aid Use Attenuates the Association Between Hearing Loss and Frailty: A 2003–2018 NHANES Cross Sectional Analysis
Authors: Cora Leigh White, Suryaa Gupta, Sarah B. Andrea, Deborah Karasek, Lisa N. Miura, Kelly M. Reavis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo examine the association between objectively measured hearing ability and frailty using the accumulation of deficits model in adults aged ≥ 65 years and assess if the strength of this association differs by sex and hearing aid use.MethodsNHANES data pooled from six cycles (2003–2018) formed the analytic sample (n = 2644). Hearing ability was examined continuously using an audiometrically determined hearing threshold. Frailty was defined using a 52‐item frailty index (Rockwood's model) and was treated continuously and categorically. Analysis consisted of adjusted linear regression and multinomial logistic regression models. We tested for effect measure modification by sex and hearing aid use, with the latter analysis limited to those with at least mild hearing loss.ResultsThe weighted prevalence of frailty was 22.5%. A modest association between hearing ability and frailty was observed in covariate adjusted models. Each 10 dB (dB) increase in hearing threshold (poorer hearing) was associated with 24% higher odds of being frail compared to being robust (odds ratio 1.24, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.14, 1.34). A 10 dB increase in hearing threshold was associated with a 6.9% increase in frailty index score (95% CI: 1.8, 12.2). Sex did not modify the observed association (p = 0.436), but hearing aid use did (p = 0.031). For non‐hearing aid users, each 10 dB increase in hearing threshold corresponded to a 17.9% (95% CI: 1.9, 36.5%) higher frailty index score. For hearing aid users, the corollary result was null at −4.2% (95% CI: −10.1, 2.2%).ConclusionsThis study demonstrated a positive association between hearing threshold and frailty, such that poorer hearing (higher thresholds) was associated with greater frailty. Hearing aid use attenuated this association, highlighting the need for better access to hearing care and informing the development of frailty screening tools.

Introduction

Hearing loss affects two thirds of adults 70 years of age and older in the United States [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. Importantly, hearing loss is a potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia, social isolation, falls, and inability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) [1, 2, 3, 4, 7]. Collectively, these associations highlight a possible connection between hearing loss and frailty in older adults. A comprehensive examination of the association between hearing loss and frailty is of public health importance to identify modifiable risk factors for frailty and reduce the growing burden of age‐related disability.

Frailty is the accumulation of age‐associated physiological deficits which increase vulnerability to external stressors and risk for adverse health outcomes including falls and fractures, cognitive decline, dementia, hospitalization, and death [1, 2, 3, 8, 9, 10]. The path to frailty is nonlinear, with an intermediate pre‐frail state distinguishing robust from frail states [8, 11]. The decline of physiological function puts a compounding strain on the frail individual, their family, care team, and the health care system [12]. Extant work has described a moderate, positive association between hearing ability and frailty, likely mechanistically occurring through social isolation, cognitive decline, and reduced situational awareness [1, 2, 3, 7]. However, research gaps in this area remain. A 2021 meta‐analysis outlined that of the 14 qualifying studies, 12 relied on self‐reported measures of hearing loss [2]. Self‐report can differ across sociodemographic groups, including age [13], suggesting that studies relying on self‐reported measures of hearing difficulty as a proxy for audiometric hearing loss may lead to under‐ or overestimation of the association with frailty. While self‐report data improves the accessibility and cost‐effectiveness for large‐scale studies, audiometry is the most objective and reproducible measure of hearing ability [5, 6].

Furthermore, all prior examinations of the association between hearing ability and frailty have been measured by the frailty phenotype [2]. The frailty phenotype is defined by unintentional weight loss, self‐reported exhaustion, weakness, slow walking speed, and low physical activity [7, 12, 14, 15]. A second measure, the frailty index, quantifies frailty using the accumulation of 30 or more cognitive, physical, and psychological deficits across a range of systems [8, 9, 10, 14, 16]. Frailty indices developed using electronic health record (EHR) data are an area of active research interest [17, 18, 19, 20]. These EHR derived scales are designed to serve as efficient frailty screening tools across clinical settings and may help hospitals meet recently established Age‐Friendly Requirements from the Center for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS). Both the frailty index and phenotype are validated approaches that capture distinct but complementary aspects of frailty [21]. Because both approaches are widely used, it is important to assess whether observed associations are robust to both frailty constructs.

Limited evidence exists regarding how certain factors may modify the association between hearing and frailty. Understanding how associations differ across groups is important for identifying heterogeneity that may inform about high‐risk populations and targeted interventions. Sex was selected based on established differences in both hearing loss and frailty. Postmenopausal estrogen decline in females increases the risk of osteoporosis and the likelihood that a fall will lead to sustained or increased frailty [22, 23]. Because hearing loss can elevate fall risk through vestibular dysfunction and reduced spatial awareness, females may be more susceptible to frailty precipitated by hearing loss compared to males [22, 23, 24]. Hearing aid use was additionally considered given its potential to reduce downstream effects of hearing loss, including cognitive decline and depression, which contribute to frailty [1, 3, 6]. These factors were prioritized given their strong biological and clinical relevance to both hearing loss and frailty.

Study Objective and Hypothesis

This study examined the association between objective hearing ability, defined by pure tone hearing thresholds from audiometric testing, and a frailty index. We tested how this association differed based on sex and hearing aid use in a nationally representative sample of older adults (aged ≥ 65 years) in the United States to better understand heterogeneity across these groups. We hypothesized that poorer hearing ability (higher thresholds) would be associated with greater frailty, and that this association would be stronger among females than males and attenuated by hearing aid use.

Methods

Study Design & Setting

This study utilizes publicly available data from adults over 65 years of age participating in select 2003–2018 waves of the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) [25, 26]. The 2007–2008, 2013–2014, and cycles after 2018 were excluded due to absence of audiometry data collection [25, 26]. The data were publicly available and de‐identified, thus not requiring Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval.

Exposure

Hearing was measured using pure tone audiometry by trained examiners. Participants underwent hearing threshold testing in both ears across seven frequencies. In this study, hearing ability was defined continuously using the better‐ear pure tone average (PTA) of thresholds at 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz; these frequencies are critical for understanding speech [27]. Participants who were unable to respond to the pure tone at any level for one or more frequencies were assigned scores of 5 dB above the maximum output level, reflecting the minimum intensity at which the pure tone could theoretically be detected. Retesting was performed when there was a significant discrepancy between left and right ear results; the associated data were used when available [27]. We operationalized hearing ability continuously with main results expressed per 10 dB increase in hearing threshold. For descriptive purposes only, hearing loss was categorized in decibels hearing level (dB HL), as none (BPTA ≤ 19 dB HL), mild (BPTA 20–34 dB HL), or moderate or greater (BPTA ≥ 35 dB HL) [28].

Outcome

Frailty was operationalized using a frailty index which quantified 52 health deficits across multiple health domains including cognition, ADLs, diagnosed health conditions and laboratory values quantified through self‐report and objective assessment (Table S1). Frailty index construction was adapted from methodology published by Hakeem et al. and Blodget et al. [8, 29] Scoring procedures are shown in Table S1 and were implemented through the adaptation of publicly available source code [30]. The index was calculated as a proportion of deficits experienced to total measured deficits, with the denominator reflecting the number of non‐missing items, which was required to be ≥ 30 for study inclusion [9, 10]. The frailty index was modeled continuously and using robust (< 0.10), pre‐frail (0.10–0.21), and frail (> 0.21) categories [11]. When treated continuously, log transformation was applied to the frailty index to normalize its distribution and a small coefficient (1*10−8) was added to all values to allow for transformation of zero values.

Effect Measure Modification

Potential effect measure modifiers of the association between hearing ability and frailty include sex (male, female) and hearing aid use (yes, no). Hearing aid use was defined as hearing aid use at the time of the survey during the 2017–2018 cycle, but as ever use of hearing aids in all other study cycles. To harmonize responses, hearing aid use was defined as any reported use for the purposes of this study.

Confounders

Possible confounders were selected a priori using lived experiences, clinical knowledge, and review of literature in the construction of directed acyclic graphs (DAG) [31]. The minimal adjustment set sufficient to address potential confounding (Figure 1) included the following self‐reported covariates: continuous age, race/ethnicity (Mexican American, other Hispanic, Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black, other Hispanic, other or Multi‐Racial), education (< 9th grade, 9–11th grade, high school graduate, some college, college graduate, or above), and occupational noise exposure (yes, no) [6, 7, 32]. These covariates were included in the adjusted regression models to control for confounding.

Analytic Sample

Of the 7817 adults aged ≥ 65 years who participated in NHANES, 3779 participated in the audiometric examination and were eligible for study inclusion (Figure 2). Analyses were further restricted to those with PTA data for both ears (n = 414 excluded) and responses to at least 30 of the frailty measures (n = 1 excluded) according to published guidelines for frailty index construction [9, 10]. After further restricting to participants with complete data for all measures defined above (n = 720 excluded), the final analytic sample consisted of 2644 participants.

Statistical Methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0. Complex survey design methods accounting for sampling weights, primary sampling units, and strata were applied according to NHANES analytic guidelines for combining survey cycles [33, 34, 35], using the R survey package. We examined the distribution of key study variables by hearing loss (none, mild, moderate, or greater). For categorical variables, summary statistics were reported by number of observations (unweighted) and proportions expressed as weighted percentages. Central tendency was expressed as weighted mean with standard error for continuously normally distributed variables, and as median with interquartile range otherwise. Linearity of the association between hearing loss and frailty was assessed visually using scatter plots.

The association between hearing threshold and frailty index was examined using linear regression (continuous, log transformed index) and multinomial logistic regression for categories of frail, pre‐frail, and robust (referent). Estimates and 95% confidence intervals were obtained from unadjusted and adjusted models. When frailty index was examined continuously, beta coefficients were multiplied by 10 and exponentiated representing the percent change in frailty score per 10 dB increase in hearing threshold. When frailty index was modeled categorically, beta coefficients were exponentiated to odds ratios, corresponding to the change in relative odds of pre‐frailty or frailty per 10 dB increase in hearing threshold in comparison to the robust reference group.

Effect measure modification by sex and hearing aid use were modeled using the following interaction terms in separate models: (hearing threshold*sex) and (hearing threshold*hearing aid use). Effect modification by hearing aid use was assessed in a sub population of individuals with at least mild hearing loss (BPTA ≥ 20 dB HL; n = 1460). An F‐test was used to compare models with and without interaction terms and terms were retained if p < 0.10. Coefficients and their 95% confidence intervals were calculated using linear combinations of the main effect and interaction terms before being exponentiated.

Robustness Checks

We conducted three robustness checks. First, descriptive statistics were examined for participants with missing data on key study variables to assess patterns of missingness that may contribute to selection bias.

Second, as frailty is commonly operationalized as the frailty phenotype in extant hearing literature, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing using the categorical frailty phenotype as an alternate outcome definition. The frailty phenotype was constructed using methodology adapted from Kurnat‐Thoma et al. [36], covering the criteria of weakness, low physical activity, exhaustion, slow walking speed, and unintentional weight loss. Individuals who demonstrated 3 or more criteria were categorized as frail, 1–2 as pre‐frail, and 0 as robust (referent) [36]. Scoring procedures are shown in Table S2. Multinomial logistic regression models were used. Covariate adjustment and measure of association interpretations were identical to the main model.

Third, individuals with a suspected conductive component to their hearing loss were excluded based on tympanometry data to see if our results differed when those with only a presumed sensorineural hearing loss were included. Abnormal tympanometry was defined, in the better hearing ear, as an ear canal volume < 0.8 or > 3.0, peak pressure < −105 or > 50, or a compliance < 0.3 or > 2.5 [37, 38]. Conductive hearing loss is typically transient and medically treated. By limiting the analysis to participants with presumed sensorineural hearing loss, which is generally permanent and often progressive, we aimed to isolate associations most relevant to frailty.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Overall, the average age of our study population was 73 years, and participants were more commonly female (54.7%) and non‐Hispanic White (82.7%; Table 1). The sociodemographic composition varied by degree of hearing loss: those with the least hearing loss were, on average, more likely to be female, younger, more racially and ethnically diverse, and had higher levels of educational attainment. In contrast, those with the most hearing loss were, on average, more likely to be male, older, most likely to be non‐Hispanic White, and had comparatively lower levels of educational attainment. Most participants reported not using hearing aids; however, usage increased with hearing loss severity, with 53.7% of individuals with moderate or greater hearing loss reporting hearing aid use.

Hearing Ability & Frailty

Among older adults (aged ≥ 65 years), the frailty index was 0.12 on average with 39.7% classified as pre‐frail and 22.5% classified as frail (Table 2). We observed a robust positive association between hearing ability and frailty that persisted with covariate adjustment. In the adjusted model, each 10 dB increase in hearing threshold was associated with 7% higher odds of being pre‐frail (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 0.98, 1.16) and 24% higher odds of being frail (OR: 1.24, 95% CI 1.14, 1.34), compared to being robust. When the frailty index was modeled as a continuous outcome, each 10 dB increase in hearing threshold was associated with a 6.9% (95% CI: 1.8, 12.2%) increase in frailty index score.

Robustness Checks

Compared to our final study sample which only included participants who had complete responses to our exposure, outcome, effect measure modifiers, participants excluded from the analysis were more racially diverse, more likely to be women, and less likely to report occupational noise (Table S3).

Results were robust to alternative specification of frailty using the frailty phenotype (Table S4). In the adjusted model, each 10 dB increase in hearing threshold was associated with 14% higher odds of being pre‐frail versus robust as defined by the frailty phenotype (OR: 1.14, 95% CI: 1.04, 1.26) and 20% higher odds of being frail versus robust as defined by the frailty phenotype (OR: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.03, 1.39). Additionally, restricting the sample to participants with normal tympanometry data (N = 596 excluded) did not meaningfully alter the results (data not shown).

Discussion

We examined the relationship between objectively measured hearing ability and frailty, operationalized using a frailty index, in a nationally representative sample of community‐dwelling adults aged 65 and older in the United States. We observed a modest positive association between hearing ability and frailty that did not differ by sex and was robust to alternative specifications of the frailty phenotype. When the frailty index was analyzed categorically, the positive association was strongest for frail versus robust individuals, while associations for pre‐frail versus robust individuals were generally weak and not statistically significant. We found evidence of effect measure modification by hearing aid use, such that a strong positive association between hearing loss and frailty was observed among nonusers, but the association was not present among hearing aid users.

Overall, our findings support the hypothesis that hearing ability is positively associated with frailty and suggest this relationship may be attenuated by hearing aid use. Our results are consistent with findings defining the existing association between hearing loss and frailty [2]. Prior meta‐analysis found a 56%–87% increased risk of frailty among individuals with hearing loss; however, nearly all studies used subjective or crude hearing loss measures, undifferentiated for type of hearing loss, and the frailty phenotype as the measure of outcome [2]. Additionally, there was limited assessment of how the relationship may differ by hearing aid use or sex, with the few existing analyses yielding conflicting results [2]. Our study addresses these gaps by assessing the relationship between objective hearing measures and the frailty index, excluding those with potential conductive hearing loss and evaluating effect measure modification by sex and hearing aid use.

Extant work has described that postmenopausal estrogen declines in females impact bone density in the inner ear bones as well as fall risk vulnerabilities, revealing a statistically significant association between hearing loss and low bone density [6, 23, 24, 39]. However, men experience a higher risk and earlier onset of hearing loss [24]. Although women generally have higher frailty scores compared to men [8, 12, 14, 16], morbidity and mortality due to frailty are higher among men, often described as the sex frailty paradox [14, 16, 40, 41]. While these past reports point to a complex interaction between physiological differences in hearing, causes of chronic illness, and psychosocial factors, in our study we did not observe evidence of sex differences in the association between hearing ability and frailty. Future work may require a more nuanced approach to understanding sex‐related differences regarding the physiological manifestation of hearing loss and potential differential protectiveness of hearing aids.

Hearing aid use is a well‐established mitigator against downstream effects of hearing loss [1, 3, 6, 7], including cognitive decline and depression, which are components of the frailty index. This is further supported by the understanding that hearing aid use enhances auditory and cognitive processing, which are often impaired with hearing loss. By improving the clarity of the auditory signal, hearing aids may reduce the cognitive load required for listening and understanding degraded speech thereby preserving cognitive resources and improving ability to function, cognitively and physically [3]. Thus, hearing aid use was hypothesized to have a protective effect on the relationship between hearing loss and frailty. We found that hearing aid use attenuates the association between hearing loss and frailty. Our findings are consistent with Assi et al.'s [1, 3] work which demonstrated stronger positive associations between hearing loss and frailty among non‐hearing aid users when compared to hearing aid users. Our findings add to this scarce body of literature, establishing that hearing aid use modifies the association between hearing loss and frailty across different study populations. It should be noted that the NHANES survey screens for hearing aid use by asking participants whether they “ever” used a hearing aid device; current or active use the time of the survey was unknown in all cycles except 2017–2018. Future work should incorporate more explicit measures of current hearing aid use.

This study capitalized on preexisting data in the NHANES database, thus as a cross‐sectional study, temporality is lacking, and it is not possible to draw conclusions about causality. While we attempted to control for all identified confounders, we were limited by the data available in the database and as such, it is likely that some confounders remained unmeasured [4, 34]. While there are multiple causes of sensorineural hearing loss, the most common is presbycusis, an age‐related hearing loss [6]; thus, for the older adult population, it would be important to consider other causes of sensorineural hearing loss and that may also be associated with frailty as potential confounders. Additionally, while we appraised sex as an effect measure modifier, other demographic and clinical factors may modify or mediate the association between hearing loss and frailty and should be a feature of future research. There may have also been misclassification of frailty as many of the variables utilized to construct the index were self‐reported and resulted from slightly different questions across survey cycles. Self‐reported measures of overall health historically have been overestimated; people tend to report better perceived health compared to their actual health [42]. As such, scores on the frailty index may be underestimated and the relationship between hearing ability and frailty may be greater than reported in this study.

Implications for Policy and Practice

Our results contribute to the growing literature suggesting interventions targeting hearing loss may help modify the risk of downstream effects [1, 3]. Importantly, Medicare defines hearing aids as assistive rather than medical devices; thus, it is currently not covered under this health insurance infrastructure [6]. Improved access to hearing aids could help prevent subsequent effects of hearing loss and frailty, both of which increase the overall burden of disease and have been established as major public health issues [1, 3, 4, 6, 28, 29, 43].

Additionally, the accumulation of deficits model operationalized through the frailty index is increasingly relevant to clinical practice through the development and implementation of electronic frailty indices (eFIs) in the setting of the recently passed CMS age‐friendly hospital measure. Beginning in 2025, hospitals are required to report compliance with the new measure, which includes a Frailty Screening and Intervention component [44, 45]. eFI tools are particularly relevant as they utilize readily available clinical data to screen for frailty without necessitating additional examinations or patient interviews [17, 19, 20]. As objective hearing measures are not always known, the impact of hearing on downstream outcomes like falls and hospitalization underscores the need to expand access to hearing tests and integrate these data into patients' EHRs. The utility of any eFI tool depends on the quality and completeness of data available in the EHR; thus, this work may help inform the construction of more robust eFI tools utilized by healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This study is among the first to demonstrate a meaningful association between objectively measured hearing ability and frailty using the accumulation of deficits model, expanding upon previous work that relied on self‐reported hearing and alternate frailty definitions. Given that hearing loss generally precedes and contributes to many components of frailty, it should be recognized as a modifiable risk factor. Our findings suggest that hearing aid use may attenuate this relationship, underscoring the importance of improving access to hearing healthcare and Medicare coverage while also informing the development of frailty screening tools. These results have implications for both clinical practice and public health policy and support the importance of future prospective longitudinal research to evaluate whether timely hearing interventions can reduce frailty progression and improve health outcomes in older adults.
