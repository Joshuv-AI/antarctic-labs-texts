FMCL: a transformer-based feature-map classifier learning approach for enhanced brain tumor detection in MRI
Authors: Turki M Alanazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn order to achieve a higher level of precision in the diagnosis of brain tumors using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data, both traditional image processing and advanced machine learning techniques are utilized. This enables adaptive identification of regions based on relationships between extracted features. In this article, we propose a Feature-Map Contrast Learning (FMCL) framework for improved brain tumor identification. Unlike conventional CNN- or transformer-based methods, FMCL explicitly enforces an inverse proportional relationship between contrast and intensity during feature extraction, preventing feature domination in heterogeneous or low-contrast tumor regions. A proportionate correlation between contrast and intensity is maintained across a large number of classification cases. FMCL performs a multi-class classification of brain MRI images, distinguishing multiple tumor types and healthy tissue by integrating transformer-based self-attention over regulated feature maps, which enhances spatial coherence and tumor boundary localization. During classification, transformer learning differentiates high, low, and zero classification possibilities, and adjacent-region verification with SoftMax normalization further improves segmentation reliability. By utilizing maximal alterations across feature maps, classed regions are accurately recognized and segmented as tumor-infected. To achieve optimal detection precision, FMCL is trained using multiple classification outputs while traversing pixels. The proposed FMCL approach demonstrates improved performance, enhancing accuracy by 8.85% and 8.3%, while reducing mean error by 16.19%, compared to existing methods. The proposed FMCL framework was evaluated using the Kaggle Brain Tumor MRI Dataset, which contains 3264 MRI images of 512 × 512 pixels, including classes for No Tumor, Glioma, Meningioma, and Pituitary tumors.Keywords: Brain tumor, Feature map, Magnetic resonance imaging, SoftMax function, Transformer learningSubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Background

The primary objectives of the analysis of medical images are to identify examples of brain tumors and to enhance the precision of diagnostic procedures. In order to accomplish these objectives, the processing of digital images is utilized. Taken from scanned images, this is accomplished by extracting the relevant information. In contrast, conventional diagnostic techniques frequently encounter issues such as noise, artifacts, and variations in the appearance of the tumor, all of which have the potential to result in incorrect perception and a reduction in accuracy1. As a result of these issues, radiologists require more sophisticated machines that can assist them in producing results that are dependable and consistent from the very beginning. Techniques for digital image processing such as filtration, segmenting, and morphological processes have the potential to improve the quality of photographs2. The use of these technologies can make it simpler to locate the locations of tumors. The process of distinguishing tumors from the tissues that surround them is facilitated by edge identification, which facilitates boundary recognition. The use of limitations and region-based segmentation, on the other hand, makes it possible to precisely pinpoint and define issue areas3,4. For the aim of diagnosis, enhancement of images and removal of noise make it simpler to perceive, which in turn reduces the amount of errors that occur when interpretations are performed manually. Furthermore, image fusion algorithms incorporate data from a variety of imaging techniques, which enables us to gain a deeper understanding of the characteristics of tumors5,6. It is possible to obtain anatomical information that is essential for the diagnosis of brain illnesses through the use of magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI, which is capable of producing images with a high resolution7. In order to provide a more accurate description of tumors, techniques that extract properties, such as texture, its sharpness, and also form-based analysis, can be utilized. Methods such as the wavelet transformation, the gray-level correlation matrix, and the histogram analysis are examples of techniques that can be utilized to obtain an accurate classification of items8–10. These kind of approaches are able to capture a variety of visual characteristics. Through the utilization of techniques for lowering dimensions, such as principal component analysis, it is possible to preserve significant data while also simplifying computations. There are a few instances of spatially feature markers that can assist in locating and distinguishing between different types of cancers11,12.

Two examples of these markers are Harrington functions and local binary patterns. By automated classification and creating predictions that are more accurate, Machine Learning (ML) techniques are able to circumvent a significant number of the challenges that are associated with manual analysis13. As a result of the involvement of individuals, the conventional method of diagnosis is dependent on subjectively evaluation, which increases the likelihood of errors occurring14. In order to discover hidden patterns that point to the presence of tumors, methods of machine learning can be utilized to search through massive datasets of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). It is common practice to employ models such as convolutional neural networks (CNN), support vector machines (SVMs), as well as random forests in order to simplify the process of locating and correctly categorizing tumors15,16. In spite of the fact that there is not a great deal of data, the system is made far more dependable and precise through the utilization of deep neural network frameworks, learning through transfer, and data augmentation17–19. Previous studies have shown that MRI-based tumor recognition is getting better, but many more studies still have problems, such as not being able to handle noise well, not being able to segment well, and overfitting because they only have small datasets. The goal of the current study is to make a way to diagnose brain tumors that is both more effective and easier to use computers for. This method will mix good ways to prepare pictures, pull out features, and classify them using algorithms. The main goals are to (1) make the picture clearer and the segmentation more accurate, (2) make feature-based tumor segmentation more accurate for diagnosis, and (3) cut down on diagnostic mistakes by making machine learning models better.

Novelty

The novelty of this work lies in the proposed Feature-Map Contrast Learning (FMCL) framework, which introduces a focused and systematic approach to MRI-based brain tumor detection. Unlike existing methods that treat contrast and intensity as independent or additive features, FMCL explicitly enforces an inverse proportional relationship between contrast and intensity during feature extraction, preventing feature dominance in heterogeneous and low-contrast tumor regions. Furthermore, the framework integrates temporal derivatives of contrast and intensity to stabilize feature responses across pixel traversals, improving robustness to noise and illumination variations. Instead of applying transformer learning directly to raw image patches, FMCL employs self-attention over regulated and noise-suppressed feature maps, enabling improved spatial coherence and accurate tumor boundary localization. Additionally, the proposed adjacency-aware TL-SoftMax verification mechanism refines regional classification by incorporating spatial continuity, significantly reducing false positives caused by minor intensity fluctuations. These combined contributions distinguish FMCL from conventional CNN- and transformer-based approaches and result in improved detection accuracy and reliability.

The existing brain tumor detection methods that treat contrast and intensity as independent or additive features and directly apply CNN or transformer models, the proposed Feature-Map Contrast Learning (FMCL) framework introduces a fundamentally different learning strategy by explicitly enforcing an inverse proportional relationship between contrast and intensity during feature extraction and mapping. This prevents feature domination in heterogeneous and low-contrast tumor regions, a common limitation in conventional approaches. In addition, FMCL incorporates temporal derivatives of contrast and intensity to stabilize feature responses across pixel traversals, improving robustness to noise and illumination variations. Rather than applying transformer learning directly on raw image patches, FMCL integrates self-attention over regulated and noise-suppressed feature maps, enabling improved spatial coherence and accurate tumor boundary localization. Furthermore, the proposed adjacency-aware TL-SoftMax mechanism refines regional classification by considering spatial continuity, significantly reducing false positives caused by minor intensity fluctuations. These combined innovations distinguish FMCL from existing methods and result in improved detection accuracy, lower false rates, and more reliable tumor segmentation in MRI scans. The proposed FMCL-based framework enhances real-world clinical workflows by acting as a decision-support tool rather than a standalone classifier. The model produces region-aware outputs that highlight salient areas contributing to classification, enabling radiologists to visually validate high-risk patterns instead of relying on black-box predictions. Its high recall and low mean error reduce the likelihood of missed lesions and allow automated triage of suspicious cases in busy screening pipelines. Moreover, by exposing uncertainty and inconsistent features across regions, the system flags ambiguous scans for additional review, thereby reducing cognitive load and improving diagnostic confidence. Through these mechanisms, the method integrates seamlessly into radiologist workflows, improving efficiency, prioritization, and diagnostic reliability without replacing clinical expertise. The proposed FMCL-based framework achieves superior performance overall, it exhibits notable limitations in challenging imaging scenarios.

In particular, low-contrast tumors with poorly defined boundaries tend to reduce feature discriminability, causing the model to miss subtle textural cues and occasionally misclassify borderline cases. Likewise, very small tumors occupying only a few pixels may not generate sufficiently representative regional features, leading to reduced recall despite strong performance on larger lesions. These observations suggest that model accuracy remains partially dependent on tumor size, contrast quality, and visual separability. Future research will explore adaptive contrast enhancement, multi-scale aggregation, and targeted data augmentation to mitigate these limitations and improve reliability in early-stage or visually subtle tumor presentations. Yang et al.49 proposed an explainable ensemble learning framework for OCT detection that integrates transfer learning with interpretability mechanisms. Their method demonstrates how AI models can be both accurate and transparent, allowing clinicians to understand and validate predictions, which is critical for real-world adoption in medical imaging workflows. Tan50 introduced a causally-informed instance-wise feature selection method for visual classifiers. By focusing on causally relevant features, this approach improves explainability and trustworthiness of AI models in clinical applications, addressing concerns over spurious correlations that can arise in black-box systems. Luo et al.51 developed StegMamba, a multi-image steganography approach using a state-space model for distortion-free immune-cover. While primarily applied in secure image transmission, the principles of robust feature preservation and noise resilience can inform techniques in medical imaging pipelines, particularly for maintaining feature integrity across preprocessing or data augmentation steps.

Conclusion

This piece talks about a new feature map-based learning model that can help with the proportional feature classification problem in finding brain tumors correctly. The suggested method for finding brain tumors used contrast and intensity features in MRI pictures, taking advantage of the fact that they work against each other to make feature extraction better. Using transformer learning to sort MRI regions and the TL SoftMax function to check nearby segments makes segmentation more accurate and makes sure that the tumor is correctly identified. Using feature maps, classification, and neighboring segment validation helps tell the difference between brain tissue that has a tumor and brain tissue that doesn’t have a tumor. This makes it easier to find tumors, which would help with early identification. With the suggested FMCL method, accuracy went up by 9.01% and 8.03%, recall went up by 10.55%, and the mean error went down by 11.41% for the highest feature count.

Future work

The limited proportional feature relationship between the features can only be used for fewer area detections. Due to different pixel ranges, the change in false rates across limited features goes against the proportional relationship. In order to deal with this particular change, a more complicated SoftMax function that uses the feature map is going to be created and used in the future. With accurate region information, this would keep the drop in specific region segmentation from happening.
