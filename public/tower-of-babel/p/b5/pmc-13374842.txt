Neurodegeneration in Glaucoma: Microstructural Magnetic Resonance Imaging Evidence Within and Beyond the Visual Pathway
Authors: Daniel Zhang, Eryn Kwon, Helen V. Danesh-Meyer, William Schierding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate structural and microstructural brain changes in glaucoma using multimodal magnetic resonance imaging across primary, secondary, and higher-order visual brain regions, and their associations with glaucoma diagnosis, optical coherence tomography–derived retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness, ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness, and/or IOP.MethodsFrom the UK Biobank, we identified glaucoma cases (n = 1465) and 10-fold age- and sex-matched controls (n = 14,650). Magnetic resonance imaging modalities comprised T1-weighted structural, diffusion tensor imaging, and neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging. Associations with glaucoma status and ophthalmic measures (RNFL, GCL, and IOP) were assessed using regression models adjusted for age, sex, polygenic risk score, and Townsend Deprivation Index, with false discovery rate correction.ResultsGlaucoma was associated with reduced gray matter volume in primary visual regions (lateral geniculate nucleus, optic chiasm, intracalcarine cortex, and occipital pole) and diffusion tensor imaging/neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging abnormalities in the posterior thalamic radiation (all P < 0.001). Glaucoma was also associated with secondary regions (lateral occipital cortex, lingual gyrus, and occipital fusiform gyrus) and microstructural changes in the inferior fronto-occipital and inferior longitudinal fasciculus (all P < 0.001). Higher-order and supporting regions were also associated with glaucoma, including the right putamen and paracingulate gyrus (P < 0.05). The RNFL (P < 0.05) and GCL (P < 0.01) correlated linearly with most primary visual regions, whereas the IOP showed no significant associations.ConclusionsGlaucoma is associated with structural and microstructural brain changes beyond the eye to primary, secondary, and higher-order brain regions. These changes correlate with RNFL and GCL thinning but not IOP. Involvement of the occipital pole is consistent with the plausibility of trans-synaptic degeneration in glaucoma.

Glaucoma is a group of progressive optic neuropathies characterized by degeneration of the retinal ganglion cells and corresponding visual field loss.1 Evidence now suggests that glaucoma is associated with changes along the central visual pathway. Postmortem studies have demonstrated atrophy in the optic nerve, lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN), and visual cortex, and voxel-based morphometry magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies have shown reduced gray matter volume across multiple cortical regions beyond the retina and optic nerve.2–6 These findings imply that glaucoma may involve more widespread central nervous system involvement than previously recognized.

More recent advanced MRI techniques have enabled in vivo interrogation of microstructural brain changes.7,8 Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has revealed altered fractional anisotropy (FA) and diffusivity metrics along the optic radiations and associated tracts, suggesting microstructural compromise.9–14 However, DTI lacks specificity, as changes may reflect diverse processes such as axonal loss, myelin damage, or altered fiber orientation. Newer techniques such as neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) provide greater biological specificity by differentiating axonal density from fiber disorganization.15 These modalities offer the potential to identify subtle and early microstructural changes associated with glaucoma, before overt clinical manifestations.16,17

Despite these insights, prior studies have been limited by small sample sizes, heterogeneous patient populations, a narrow focus on isolated brain structures, and single imaging modalities. Few studies have comprehensively evaluated both structural (T1-weighted MRI) and microstructural (DTI and NODDI) alterations across the full spectrum of visual processing—from primary relay nuclei to secondary and higher-order cortical regions—and linked these changes to established ophthalmic markers of disease severity such as retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) and ganglion cell layer (GCL) thickness, or IOP.

Here we leveraged the UK Biobank (UKB), the largest population-based imaging cohort worldwide,18,19 to investigate structural and microstructural brain changes in glaucoma across primary, secondary, and higher-order visual brain regions. By integrating multimodal MRI with detailed ophthalmic characterisation, including optical coherence tomography (OCT)-derived macular RNFL and GCL, we aimed to clarify the extent of brain structural and microstructural changes associated with glaucoma and its relationship to retinal biomarkers and IOP. This work provides a population-scale assessment of brain structural and microstructural changes associated with glaucoma, offering new insights into central nervous system involvement beyond the eye.

Methods

Participants

The UKB is a prospective cohort study which has recruited 502,166 participants ages 40 to 69 across the UK. From the full UKB cohort, participants were excluded who had withdrawn consent, were lost to follow-up, were pregnant at baseline, had sex mismatches between genetic and phenotypic data, or had missing information on age or sex, leaving 499,172 eligible participants. Furthermore, we only included patients in the MRI imaging subcohort with structural (T1-weighted) or diffusion MRI data available, resulting in 43,201 participants.20

We did not Systematically exclude all central nervous system disorders (e.g., prior stroke) because complete neurological phenotyping was not available across all participants in the analysis.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants to participate in the UKB, and ethical approval for the UKB was received from the research ethics committee (North West Multi-Centre Ethics Committee). This research has been conducted using the UKB Resource under project number 76765.

Glaucoma Definition

The UKB glaucoma cohort and controls were defined from three sources: self-report, hospital inpatient, and primary care (complete for the whole UKB cohort, except primary care: where data were available for <50% of participants). Glaucoma cases were defined by a positive glaucoma record in any of the three sources. For self-report, glaucoma diagnosis was positive if the participant responded “yes” to a touch screen questionnaire stating, “Has a doctor told you that you have any of the following problems with your eyes?”, then “glaucoma” from a list of medical conditions (UKB field 6148) or if the participant responded “glaucoma” to the question “In the touch screen you selected that you have been told by a doctor that you have other serious illnesses or disabilities, could you now tell me what they are?” (UKB field 20002). For hospital inpatient records, glaucoma diagnosis was positive if the participant had a record of International Classification of Diseases (ninth edition [ICD-9], UKB field 41271 – responses 365.1, 365.8, 365.9; or tenth edition [ICD-10], UKB field 41270 – responses H40.1, H40.8, or H40.9) codes for POAG, other glaucoma, or glaucoma, unspecified, responses. For the primary care data, glaucoma diagnosis was positive if any codes used for Read2 and Read3 (UKB field 42040) corresponded with the ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes described according to the National Health Service Digital Technology Reference Data Update Distribution. Consistent with UKB Eye and Vision Consortium publications, positive glaucoma records were considered beyond the initial assessment (2006–2010) to include positive results in the first repeated assessment visit (2012–2013), imaging visit (2014 or after), or first repeat imaging visit (2019 or after).

All controls were defined as participants without any evidence of a glaucoma diagnosis, medication, or glaucoma-related surgery across all available data sources. This excluded participants with a history of glaucoma suspect (ICD-9: 365.0 and ICD-10: H40.0) or other glaucoma-related procedures not linked to a definitive diagnosis (from hospital [ICD-9/10] or primary care records [Read2/Read3]). Previous UKB studies have shown that age and sex have significant effects on brain MRI metrics in the UKB cohort, including advanced diffusion MRI parameters.21–23 Therefore, controls in this study were age and sex matched (MatchIt package in R; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to cases in a ratio of 10:1.

Brain Scans: T1 Weighted and Diffusion MRI

Brain MRI data were acquired on Siemens Skyra 3T scanners, as described in detail in UKB resource 1977: Primary brain imaging documentation. To measure structural differences, T1‐weighted images were acquired using three-dimensional magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MPRAGE; 1 mm3 isotropic resolution) and analyzed with the Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain Software Library (http://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl).

To measure differences in brain connectivity, diffusion MRI data (2 × 2 × 2 mm³, 50 directions at b = 1000 and 2000 s/mm², plus 5 b = 0 images, 3 blip reversed) were used to derive DTI measures (FA, mean diffusivity [MD], and axial [L1] and radial diffusivity [L2, L3]) and NODDI measures (intracellular volume fraction [ICVF], isotropic compartment volume fraction, and OD). All diffusion MRI measurements are reported as weighted means for each region, consistent with similar studies, rather than as mean skeleton values.24

All brain structures were selected and categorized based on their established roles in visual processing pathways (Table 1). Structures involved in processing direct visual input were classified as part of the primary visual pathway. Structures involved in intermediate-level visual analysis (e.g., motion, color, depth, and spatial orientation) were categorized as the secondary visual pathway. Structures responsible for higher-level integration (including cognitive interpretation, attention, memory, and emotional modulation) were classified as high-order visual processing. Finally, structures not classically defined as part of the visual pathway but that indirectly influence visual processing were defined as supporting visual structures.

Glaucoma Risk Factors and Structural Biomarkers

Along with glaucoma status, three ophthalmological measures commonly used in monitoring and diagnosis of glaucoma were analyzed for associations with the MRI measures: corneal compensated IOP (IOPcc), the only established modifiable risk factor, and two structural biomarkers of disease severity from the macular OCT (thickness of the RNFL and GCL). IOPcc was measured cross-sectionally. For individuals who were reported to be on an IOP-lowering medication, IOPcc values were adjusted by a multiplier of 1.3, consistent with prior studies using UKB data as a pragmatic adjustment; however, these values do not represent true untreated baseline, and medication burden was not consistently available to model directly.25 For eye-level OCT biomarkers, we selected the eye with the thinner RNFL/GCL thickness and highest adjusted IOPcc to capture maximal structural disease burden. We acknowledge that averaging both eyes is an alternative approach and may yield different effect estimates.

OCT measures were derived from the UKB eye imaging protocol (Topcon 3D OCT-1000 Mark II spectral-domain OCT system, Topcon Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), which acquires macula-centered scans rather than disc-centered peripapillary scans. Automated segmentation of retinal layers, including the macular RNFL and GCL, was generated using the manufacturer's software and provided by the UKB following centralized quality control procedures (UKB Resource 100237).26 We therefore analyzed macular RNFL thickness and macular GCL thickness as retinal structural biomarkers. Disc-centered circumpapillary RNFL thickness was not acquired in the UKB and cannot be reliably derived retrospectively from the available macular-centered scans.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analysis was performed on GraphPad Prism Software (Version 8, La Jolla, CA) or R version 4.2.1. Baseline characteristics of all glaucoma and healthy control groups were compared using a combination of Pearson's χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables.

Linear regression was carried out to select for MRI measures with a significant association with glaucoma, controlling for known confounders and genetic influences (indicated MRI measure ∼ Age + Sex + Social deprivation score + Polygenic risk score + Glaucoma status). A similar linear regression was carried out to select for MRI measures with a significant association with ophthalmological markers, again controlling for known confounders and genetic influences (indicated MRI measure ∼ Age + Sex + Social deprivation score + Polygenic risk score + [RNFL or GCL or IOPcc]). P values were corrected for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure across all 1145 diffusion MRI traits (q < 0.05; the largest raw P value among FDR‑significant results was .0124.

Results

Baseline Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

After exclusions, a total of 16,173 participants were identified as having a recorded diagnosis of glaucoma from at least one of three data sources: self-report (n = 8695), hospital inpatient records (n = 9727), or primary care records (n = 5290). However, only a small subset of the UKB cohort have MRI data available. After all exclusions, the final cohort consisted of diagnosed glaucoma cases (n = 1465) and a control cohort (n = 41,736). Because glaucoma patients had a statistically significant higher age overall as compared with controls (60 vs. 55), an age- and sex-matched control cohort was selected.

Case-Control Matching Density and Bar Plots

The glaucoma cohort was sex and age matched 10:1 with controls (n = 14,650). Case-control matching was effective in balancing sex and age (Supplementary Fig. S1). This resulted in both the glaucoma and control cohorts having 55% males and a median age of 60 years.

Ophthalmic Biomarkers and Ocular Comorbidities in Glaucoma

Individuals with glaucoma demonstrated significant differences across established glaucoma-related ophthalmic markers (Wilcoxon rank-sum FDR P < 0.05) when compared with the control cohort (Table 2). As expected, both genetic indicators (MYOC mutation carrier status and polygenic risk score [PRS]) were strongly associated with glaucoma, as was treatment-adjusted IOP (IOPcc). Structural retinal biomarkers also showed robust differences, with macular RNFL and GCL thicknesses significantly reduced in glaucoma cases, reinforcing their central roles as biomarkers for the disease.27,28 In addition, there are significant increases in the proportion of glaucoma cases versus controls for incidence of AMD, diabetic retinopathy, and cataracts. However, glaucoma was not associated with any lifestyle factors (body mass index, exercise, smoking, or blood pressure). Also, despite being a neurodegenerative disease of the retina, glaucoma status is not associated with the prevalence rates of any of the common neurodegenerative diseases (Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, or multiple sclerosis).

Discussion

Glaucoma has traditionally been viewed as an optic neuropathy confined to the eye and optic nerve. However, retinal ganglion cells form the first-order neurons of the visual pathway, projecting centrally through the optic nerve, optic chiasm, and optic radiations to the LGN and primary visual cortex. Degeneration of retinal ganglion cells, therefore, has the potential to propagate along the visual pathway via trans-synaptic mechanisms leading to structural and microstructural changes beyond the eye.1,4,6

Whereas prior MRI studies of glaucoma, both using and without using the UKB, have largely focused on the primary visual pathway, our population-scale analysis shows that glaucoma is associated with widespread structural and microstructural alterations in the brain, extending beyond the primary visual pathway. By leveraging high-resolution multimodal MRI and comprehensive phenotypic data from the UKB cohort, including OCT-derived retinal biomarkers, we demonstrated that glaucoma is associated with measurable alterations not only in the primary visual pathway, but also in secondary and higher-order regions involved in visual processing. These brain changes also show systematic association with macular RNFL and GCL thinning, consistent with a relationship between retinal structural measures and central nervous system changes.

Primary Visual Pathway Involvement

As expected, we observed significant gray matter atrophy in core components of the primary visual pathways, including the LGN, intracalcarine cortex, and occipital pole. We also found decreased white matter volume in the optic chiasm. These results are consistent with previous MRI studies, including smaller population studies10 and a meta-analysis on LGN volume.29 The presence of these well-established changes provides internal biological validation of our analytic approach.

Importantly, volumetric reductions within these primary visual structures were strongly associated with macular RNFL and GCL thinning, but not IOP measured at a single time point. Other small-scale studies also found LGN volume to correlate with the RNFL.30,31 An Alzheimer's study also found a correlation between visual cortex volume with the RNFL.32 These findings reinforce the association between retinal structural loss and imaging changes in downstream visual structures and are more consistent with trans-synaptic mechanisms than with single-timepoint pressure metrics alone.33–36

Consistent with these gray matter findings, diffusion MRI revealed microstructural abnormalities within the posterior thalamic radiation (optic radiations), across both DTI and NODDI metrics. These findings align closely with prior literature demonstrating compromised white matter integrity along the geniculostriate pathway in glaucoma.37 In one of the earlier quantitative MRI studies, Hernowo et al.38 used region-of-interest volumetric analysis and demonstrated reduced white matter volume along the visual pathway in glaucoma. Subsequent DTI studies confirmed these findings, showing decreased FA and increased MD in the optic tracts, chiasm, and optic radiations, consistent with compromised axonal integrity.39 Together these studies provide strong anatomical precedent for our findings.

Extension Beyond the Primary Visual Structures

Having established expected involvement of the structures in the primary visual pathway, we next examined whether glaucomatous changes extend into secondary visual and associative structures. We identified gray matter atrophy in the lingual gyrus, paracingulate gyrus, and supracalcarine cortex, which play key roles in visual processing.

In parallel, diffusion MRI analyses revealed microstructural abnormalities in associative tracts such as the IFOF and ILF. Extending beyond the geniculostriate pathway, Frezzotti et al.40 reported reduced FA and elevated radial diffusivity, not only in the optic tract, chiasm, and radiations, but also in associative tracts such as the IFOF and ILF, indicative of compromised fiber integrity. Our findings are consistent with these reports: we observed significantly lower FA, higher radial diffusivity (L2 and L3), and reduced MD in the posterior thalamic radiation (optic radiation), IFOF, and ILF. To our knowledge, prior reports of associative tract involvement in glaucoma have primarily described these abnormalities at the case-control level, whereas our population-scale analysis demonstrates that extrageniculostriate white matter alterations are also systematically associated with OCT-derived retinal structural loss.

These tracts connect occipital visual regions with temporal and frontal cortices and are integral in integrating visual, motor, and semantic information.41,42 These findings were consistent across both DTI and NODDI, indicating that these changes likely reflect underlying alterations in axonal density and fiber organization rather than nonspecific diffusion effects alone.

Higher-Order Regions

Beyond secondary visual processing pathways, we explored whether retinal structural loss was associated with changes in regions involved attentional and integrative aspects of vision. We did find decreased gray matter volume in the paracingulate gyrus, along with associations between retinal structural thinning, most prominently GCL thickness, in multiple higher-order regions. These structures are involved in visuospatial integration and attention. These associations were fewer in number and smaller in magnitude than those observed in the primary and secondary visual pathways.

A plausible explanation is that the chronic disruption of visual afferent input may lead to downstream changes in networks supporting visuospatial attention and sensorimotor integration, reflecting network reorganization rather than direct degeneration. Alternatively shared factors such as aging, vascular risks, and genetics may contribute to the retinal and cerebral changes. Importantly, these findings do not imply direct anatomical correlation and should be interpreted with caution; they require validation in further longitudinal studies.

Microstructural Specificity From NODDI

The inclusion of NODDI analysis provides additional biological specificity beyond tensor-derived metrics. Reduced ICVF is consistent with axonal loss while modestly increased OD suggests altered fiber architecture and disorganization. These results are concordant with those of Haykal et al.,43 who reported a lower neurite density index (equivalent to ICVF) and a higher OD index in the optic tracts and radiations of glaucoma patients. This finding supports the interpretation that glaucoma is associated with axonal compromise rather than nonspecific diffusion abnormalities alone.

Specificity of Findings

The persistence of these findings after adjustment for AMD reduces the likelihood that macular disease alone explains the observed central changes. However, residual confounding by other ocular or unmeasured neurological comorbidities remains possible and cannot be excluded.

Limitations and Future Directions

The strengths of this work include the large UKB cohort and the integration of advanced multimodal imaging. However, several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes inference regarding causality or temporal sequencing of retinal and brain changes. The case definition did not allow reliable stratification by glaucoma subtype, although prior work suggests that both POAG and angle-closure glaucoma show similar pathway involvement. Unmeasured neurological comorbidity may influence MRI metrics independent of glaucoma and could contribute to residual confounding.

Macular-only RNFL measures may underestimate some retinal changes; thus, the structure–function association (anatomical correspondence) would be limited given there may be significant changes in the nerve fiber that simply are not captured within the region scanned at the macula.44 Single-timepoint IOP values (affected by topical treatment) limit interpretation of pressure–brain relationships.

Longitudinal studies with serial ophthalmic and MRI measures are needed to define the temporal sequence of these changes. As glaucomatous laterality and eye-specific involvement (including unilateral vs. bilateral disease and hemifield distribution) were not systematically captured in the UKB dataset, eye-specific or eye–hemisphere mapping was not incorporated into the analysis. Diffusion MRI measures were analyzed separately for left and right brain regions, consistent with the native outputs of the standard processing pipeline, with hemispheres treated as homologous but distinct measurements rather than averaged post hoc into global values. This approach reflected a methodological consideration rather than a hypothesis of lateralized glaucomatous pathology. We acknowledge that retaining hemisphere-specific measures increases the number of comparisons; accordingly, any apparent left–right differences should be interpreted cautiously and not as evidence of hemisphere-specific effects of glaucoma.

Conclusions

Glaucoma is associated with measurable structural and microstructural alterations within the visual pathway and in higher-order brain regions. These brain changes are significantly associated with thinning of the RNFL and GCL, whereas cross-sectional IOP shows no significant association in this dataset. These findings advance understanding of glaucoma by identifying associations between retinal structural measures and postretinal visual pathway structure.
