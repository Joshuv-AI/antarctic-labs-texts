Predicting Prolonged Hospital Length of Stay in Trauma Patients Using Machine Learning Techniques: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Maasoumeh Maghsoudi, Azadeh Bashiri, Vahid Rahmanian, Somayyeh Zakerabasali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimHospitalization due to trauma places a significant financial burden on healthcare systems, patients, and insurers. Predicting the length of stay can support resource management and workflow, ultimately enhancing healthcare interventions. This study uses machine learning techniques to predict trauma patients' hospital length of stay (LOS).MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 795 trauma patients registered at Jahrom University of Medical Sciences between March 21, 2021, and December 14, 2022. Data preprocessing, modeling, and evaluation were performed using Python. Seven machine learning algorithms—Support Vector Machine, K‐Nearest Neighbors, Random Forest, Adaptive Boosting, Decision Tree, Artificial Neural Network, and Extreme Gradient Boosting—were applied for prediction. The models were compared using evaluation metrics: accuracy, precision, recall (sensitivity), F‐measure, and the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve.ResultsThe Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting algorithms demonstrated the best performance, with accuracy, precision, and recall of 93%. The Decision Tree algorithm achieved the highest area under the ROC curve (0.74). Key predictive features included oxygen saturation level, Glasgow Coma Scale, ICU stay duration, injury severity score, Abbreviated Injury Scale, and comorbid conditions.ConclusionAmong the tested algorithms, Decision Tree, Extreme Gradient Boosting, and Random Forest exhibited superior predictive performance. These models can support better resource allocation, policy‐making, and healthcare planning, ultimately improving hospital efficiency and patient care.Keywords: length of stay, machine learning, prediction, trauma

Introduction

Trauma is a significant global public health concern [1]. It is the leading cause of death and disability, particularly among individuals under 40 years of age [2, 3]. Annually, between 20 and 50 million people suffer from injuries worldwide, resulting in approximately 5.8 million deaths [4, 5]. Beyond mortality, trauma‐related injuries often lead to long‐term complications, including chronic pain, reduced quality of life, emotional distress, and permanent disability, imposing a heavy socioeconomic burden on societies [6, 7].

The increasing demand for health services presents substantial challenges in hospital resource management. Length of stay (LOS), defined as the duration between patient admission and discharge, is a critical indicator of healthcare efficiency, resource utilization, and care quality [7, 8]. Prolonged hospital length of stay (PLOS) is associated with reduced efficiency in care delivery, increased consumption of resources, higher risk of hospital‐acquired infections, and greater financial burdens for both healthcare systems and patients [8, 9, 10, 11]. Accurate prediction of LOS is therefore essential for optimizing clinical preparedness, anticipating operational costs, and enabling more efficient allocation of financial and human resources [9, 12].

In the context of trauma care, accurately predicting which patients are at risk of PLOS is crucial for improving clinical outcomes and operational logistics. However, the development of robust predictive models faces challenges, including the frequent issue of imbalanced datasets where the number of patients with prolonged stays is significantly lower than those with standard stays. This class imbalance can severely bias model training and compromise predictive accuracy, necessitating the use of specialized techniques to address it.

In computer science, machine learning (ML) refers to algorithms designed to learn from data and generate predictions [13]. Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a powerful tool for clinical predictive modeling, often outperforming traditional methods [11]. Technological advancements have further accelerated the adoption of ML in trauma care, providing innovative solutions for data‐driven predictions [14].

This study applies machine learning techniques to classify trauma patients' LOS into short‐term and long‐term categories. Acknowledging the potential challenge of class imbalance, we aim to develop and compare the performance of various algorithms to identify the most effective model for this prediction task. By accurately identifying patients at risk of PLOS, hospitals can enhance patient care planning, allocate resources more efficiently, and ultimately improve overall healthcare delivery.

The definition of “prolonged LOS” varies significantly across studies, influencing both the model's task and its outcomes. For instance, Stonko et al. and Weng et al. used a 9‐day cutoff [20, 24], while Abujaber et al. defined prolonged LOS as 23 days for moderate‐to‐severe traumatic brain injury patients [20], and Staziaki et al. used a mere 2‐day cutoff for torso trauma [23]. This heterogeneity underscores the context‐dependent nature of PLOS.

The types of features utilized also differ. While most studies rely on demographic and clinical parameters collected at admission, some have integrated more specialized data to boost performance. For example, Staziaki et al. found that incorporating CT imaging findings with clinical parameters yielded superior predictive accuracy compared to using clinical data alone. [2]

In terms of model performance, high AUCs and accuracy have been reported across diverse trauma subpopulations. Fang et al. achieved an exceptional AUC of 0.94 for traumatic brain injury patients using a combined CNN‐SVM model [18]. Similarly, studies on specific injury types, such as spinal cord injuries [17] and hand injuries [19], have shown that ensemble methods like Random Forest and Linear Discriminant Analysis often rank among the top performers.

Data Understanding

The research population for this study comprises a dataset of 795 patients recorded in the trauma registry of Jahrom University of Medical Sciences between March 21, 2021, and December 14, 2022. The inclusion criteria included trauma patients hospitalized for more than 6 h and the availability of a signed consent form permitting anonymous access to patient information. No sampling was performed to maximize data utilization.

Based on a thorough review of related studies, the selection of features can vary depending on the study's scope. The initial set of features, identified through a literature review of relevant texts and articles, was refined in consultation with field experts. As a result, 28 features were selected for this study, as listed in Table 2. The values of these features were extracted electronically from the trauma registry and hospital information system and compiled into an Excel file.

Additionally, the occupation and employment features were combined to create a single job feature.

Features such as occupation, type of insurance, mechanism of injury, and discharge disposition were reviewed to balance the sample sizes within each group. The age feature was analyzed in both discrete and grouped formats, with the discrete format selected for modeling.

The dataset was checked for duplicate records using the national code as the unique identifier, confirming no duplicates. Outliers and missing values were identified and addressed. Missing values were initially cross‐referenced with the hospital information system and patient medical records. Various imputation methods, including Mean, Median, Mode, k‐nearest neighbors (KNN), and Random Forest, were tested, with the Random Forest method providing the best results.

Min‐max scaling, Standard Scaling, and Robust Scaling were evaluated to reduce data dispersion, with Standard Scaling ultimately applied. Class imbalance was mitigated using Weighting, Over‐Sampling (ADASYN and SMOTE), and Under‐Sampling techniques. The SMOTE method was selected for its effectiveness in balancing the dataset. To prevent data leakage, the SMOTE oversampling technique was applied only to the training folds during the fivefold cross‐validation process, and never to the entire dataset before the train‐test split or to the final test set itself.

This study did not employ sampling; instead, it aimed to utilize the entire dataset for maximum data representation.

Modeling

A random state was applied to divide the dataset. Various configurations were tested, and the one yielding the best performance was selected. The data were randomly split into 90% for training (715 patients) and 10% for testing (80 patients) to maximize learning opportunities for the algorithms and achieve better results. The pre‐processing pipeline (including scaling and SMOTE) was fitted solely on the training set. The entire model development and hyperparameter tuning process was conducted using a fivefold cross‐validation strategy on the training set. The final evaluation was performed on the untouched test set.

Because the study focuses on prediction and classification, supervised learning methods were employed. Seven classic machine learning algorithms were selected, and their optimal parameters were determined through systematic investigation. Random Forest (RF)

Random Forest is an advanced ensemble learning method that constructs multiple decision trees using different subsets of features from the dataset. Each tree independently predicts an outcome; majority voting among the trees determines the model's final prediction. By leveraging the diversity of multiple decision trees, this method enhances prediction accuracy, reduces variance, and minimizes overfitting [26].

Support Vector Machine (SVM)

Support Vector Machines (SVM) classify data by finding the optimal hyperplane that best separates the classes. The hyperplane is positioned to maximize the margin between the nearest data points of each class, known as support vectors. This margin optimization enhances the model's generalization of new, unseen data. For non‐linearly separable data, SVM employs the kernel trick technique to project the data into a higher‐dimensional space where it becomes linearly separable [27].

Adaptive Boosting (Ada Boost)

Adaptive Boosting is a meta‐algorithm designed to improve prediction performance by adjusting the distribution of sample weights during training. Initially, all samples are assigned equal weights. In subsequent rounds, the algorithm increases the weights of incorrectly classified samples, encouraging the weak learners to focus on these challenging cases. This iterative process minimizes error and maximizes the margin between classes [28].

K ‐ Nearest Neighbor (KNN)

K‐Nearest Neighbors is a simple yet effective algorithm that classifies data points based on the majority class of their k nearest neighbors. This algorithm does not build a general model but retains the entire training dataset. Predictions are made by calculating the distance between the query point and its neighbors, typically using metrics like Euclidean distance [29].

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)

Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) is a robust, tree‐based machine learning algorithm widely used in bioinformatics and other fields. It applies gradient‐boosting techniques to improve performance and minimize overfitting. XGBoost includes features like regularization, parallel processing, and efficient memory usage, significantly accelerating computations and enhancing predictive accuracy [19].

Decision Tree (DT)

A decision tree is a supervised learning technique primarily used for classification tasks but can also be applied to regression problems. The tree starts with a root node, representing the initial decision point for dividing the dataset based on a single feature that best separates the data into distinct classes. Each split is connected by edges to either new decision nodes—containing additional features for further segmentation—or terminal nodes that predict the outcome. This hierarchical process of data partitioning is known as recursive partitioning [26].

Artificial Neural Network (ANN)

An artificial neural network (ANN) is a machine learning algorithm modeled after biological neural networks. It consists of layers of nodes, analogous to neurons, interconnected by weighted connections similar to synapses. Each node receives input, processes it using an activation function, and passes the result to subsequent nodes. The network adjusts these weights through learning algorithms to optimize prediction accuracy and provide reliable outcomes [26].

Evaluation

Given the uneven distribution of data in this dataset, where 90% of cases represent short stays, and 10% represent extended stays, specific evaluation metrics were chosen to assess model performance. These metrics include accuracy, precision, sensitivity (recall), F‐measure, the area under the ROC curve (ROC‐AUC), and the area under the Precision‐Recall curve (PR‐AUC).

The K‐Fold Cross‐Validation method was used to address the class imbalance. This method splits the dataset into k parts, with one part used for testing and the remaining parts for training in each iteration. This process ensures that a new model is trained independently in each fold, enhancing the evaluation's robustness. For this study, K was set to 5. The results reported in Section Conclusion, 4 represent the mean ± standard deviation of the performance metrics across these fivefolds. Table 5 provides a brief explanation of these metrics.

Identifying Influential Features

Identifying the most influential features involves techniques that assign scores to input features based on their predictive importance for the target variable. Although all features were used in the predictive model, ranking these features helps identify those contributing most significantly to the model's performance [20].

This study employed the correlation method and the Random Forest algorithm. The correlation method measures the relationship between input features and the target label [25]. This method identified five key numerical features from the dataset. Two features, oxygen saturation level and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) showed an inverse relationship with the length of stay, making them particularly significant. Additionally, two features—Injury Severity Score (ISS), and Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS)—demonstrated a direct relationship with the length of stay. Most other features displayed a weak but direct relationship with the length of stay, except for systolic blood pressure, which appeared nearly irrelevant. The heat map in Figure 3 illustrates these relationships.

Correlation heatmap based on length of stay.

The Random Forest algorithm ranked features by importance scores, highlighting oxygen saturation level and comorbidities (patient history) as the most critical predictors [19]. Like the correlation method, the AIS, ISS, and GCS were ranked among the top ten features by the Random Forest algorithm. This algorithm is advantageous due to its simplicity in parameter tuning, high generalization capability, and relatively short training time, regardless of dataset size [30]. The results of this feature importance analysis are shown in Figure 4.

An interesting observation was the apparent discrepancy where GCS showed a moderate correlation with LOS in the heatmap but a lower relative importance in the tree‐based model. This can occur because correlation measures a linear relationship, whereas ensemble models like Random Forest capture complex, non‐linear interactions and conditional dependencies, potentially reducing the standalone importance of a correlated feature.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates the effective application of several machine learning algorithms to predict prolonged length of stay in trauma patients. The findings indicate that tree‐based ensemble methods, particularly Random Forest and Extreme Gradient Boosting, achieved the highest balanced accuracy on the test set (93%), while the Decision Tree model yielded the highest ROC‐AUC (0.74).

The superior performance of RF and XGBoost can be attributed to their inherent design as ensemble methods. These algorithms combine multiple weak learners (decision trees) to create a strong, robust predictor. They are particularly adept at handling complex, non‐linear relationships between clinical features and the target outcome, which are common in medical data. Furthermore, their built‐in mechanisms like bagging (in RF) and boosting with regularization (in XGBoost) help reduce overfitting, a crucial advantage given our relatively small and imbalanced dataset. In contrast, simpler models like KNN, which rely on distance measures in feature space, and SVM, which can be sensitive to the choice of kernel and parameters, struggled to capture the underlying data complexity as effectively. The Artificial Neural Network's moderate performance may be due to the dataset size, as ANNs typically require larger volumes of data to reach their full potential without overfitting.

The preprocessing steps played a critical role in model development. The application of the SMOTE method exclusively to the training folds was essential in mitigating the class imbalance, allowing the models to learn meaningful patterns from the minority class (extended stay) without introducing data leakage. Without this step, models would have been heavily biased towards predicting the majority class. Similarly, feature scaling ensured that algorithms sensitive to feature magnitudes (like SVM and KNN) were not unduly influenced by variables on different scales.

Our feature importance analysis revealed that oxygen saturation level was the most consistent and powerful predictor across methods. This finding is clinically intuitive, as low oxygen saturation often indicates respiratory compromise or systemic instability, which can complicate recovery and prolong hospitalization. The importance of the GCS, AIS and ISS further underscores the significance of neurological status and overall injury burden in determining patient trajectory. Comorbidities are a well‐established risk factor in clinical practice; they were also among the top‐ranked features in our model.

When compared to previous studies, our results are encouraging. Weng et al. [19] also identified Random Forest as the best model for predicting LOS in hand surgery patients, achieving an AUC of 0.88, which is higher than our ROC‐AUC of 0.70. However, their accuracy (80%) was lower than ours (93%). Similarly, Ayyoubzadeh et al. [25] found that the Random Forest algorithm delivered the best performance in predicting LOS, with an AUC of 0.89 and an accuracy of 82%, a difference that may stem from variations in the dataset characteristics, the definition of prolonged LOS, or the patient population. Additionally, Gibbs et al. [24] confirmed the high predictive power of gradient boosting in pediatric trauma patients, aligning with our positive findings for XGBoost.

This study faced several limitations. A key limitation, as noted in the results, is the modest PR‐AUC values, which reflect the ongoing challenge of achieving high precision and recall for the minority class despite using SMOTE. This suggests that while our models are good at overall classification, there is room for improvement in reliably identifying all patients at risk of a prolonged stay. Although the dataset was obtained from a trauma registry and missing cases were completed as much as possible using the hospital information system and patient records, potential human errors during data entry could affect accuracy and precision. This issue is a common limitation across databases and may impact the reliability of the results. The retrospective nature and single‐center source of our data also limit generalizability. Future work should involve external validation on multi‐center datasets and exploration of more advanced techniques for handling imbalance.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study confirms that machine learning models, particularly Decision Tree, Extreme Gradient Boosting, and Random Forest, can effectively predict prolonged length of stay in trauma patients using readily available clinical and demographic data. The implementation of a rigorous preprocessing, including targeted handling of class imbalance, was crucial to this outcome. These models can serve as valuable decision‐support tools for hospital administrators and clinicians, enabling proactive resource allocation, streamlined staff scheduling, and improved bed management. For future deployment, we recommend developing a user‐friendly clinical decision support system that integrates these models into the electronic health record, providing real‐time predictions to aid in patient care planning and ultimately enhancing the efficiency and quality of trauma care.
