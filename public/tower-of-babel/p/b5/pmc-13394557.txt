Tumor‐Derived Extracellular Vesicles in Plasma for Predicting Anti‐PD‐1 Antibody Efficacy in Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Tomonari Kinoshita, Shigeki Ohta, Seiki Wakui, Chihaya Maeda, Yuichiro Hayashi, Aya Misawa, Ryosuke Satomi, Shinnosuke Ikemura, Kenzo Soejima, Tomonori Yaguchi, Shotaro Maruyama, Hiroshi Kagamu, Yutaka Kawakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLiquid biopsy is a promising biomarker for cancer detection and prediction of therapy responses. In this study, we developed new antibody‐based sandwich assays to measure three extracellular vesicle (EV) subsets that express cell surface vimentin (CSV) and co‐express epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) or PD‐L1 in plasma. Fifty‐seven non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients who underwent complete resection and 89 stage IV NSCLC patients who received anti‐PD‐1 antibody were enrolled. Correlations between the plasma EV subsets at baseline and various clinicopathological factors were evaluated. Plasma EpCAM/CSV‐EV levels were significantly higher in stage I NSCLC patients compared to healthy donors, suggesting this may be a valuable biomarker for detecting early‐stage NSCLC. High plasma CSV/CSV‐EV levels at baseline were significantly correlated with a poor prognosis after treatment, both in the surgery cohort and the anti‐PD‐1 antibody cohort, which suggests that this may be useful for predicting the post‐treatment prognosis in NSCLC patients. High plasma CSV/PD‐L1‐EV levels at baseline were significantly correlated with poor postoperative prognosis. However, high plasma CSV/PD‐L1‐EV levels at baseline were significantly correlated with a favorable clinical response and prognosis following anti‐PD‐1 antibody treatment in stage IV NSCLC. Therefore, plasma CSV‐related EV subsets at baseline may be attractive biomarkers for early cancer detection (EpCAM/CSV), prediction of postoperative prognosis (CSV/CSV), and prediction of clinical responses and prognosis after anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy (CSV/PD‐L1). These EV subsets can be easily measured repetitively at appropriate timing. These novel liquid biopsies may be additional and complementary diagnostic biomarkers for NSCLC patients.Keywords: biomarker, CSV, EpCAM, extracellular vesicle, non‐small cell lung cancer, PD‐L1

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer deaths worldwide. A variety of new therapeutic modalities have recently become available, including molecular target therapies and combination immunotherapies based on immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as anti‐programmed cell death protein 1 (PD‐1)/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD‐L1) antibodies, and cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte‐associated protein 4 (CTLA‐4) antibodies [1, 2, 3]. Precision medicine is crucial for developing successful therapeutic approaches to manage heterogeneous populations of patients with different characteristics of cancer cells. Diagnostic biomarkers are required for individualized medicine to predict prognosis and treatment responses. Particularly in lung cancer, genome profiling methods have been developed for targeted therapy and immunotherapy [4, 5, 6]. In addition to tumor tissue‐based diagnostic tools such as immunohistochemical analyses, peripheral blood‐based technologies are essential because they are less invasive, and frequent collection of samples is feasible at appropriate times. Peripheral blood samples not only provide systemic information but also offer insights into the local tumor microenvironment, which contains cancer cells and other cells, serving as a form of liquid biopsy. Recently, various new liquid biopsy technologies, including soluble factors, cell‐free tumor DNA/RNA, circulating tumor cells (CTCs), and extracellular vesicles (EVs), have been exploited for non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [7, 8].

In this study, we characterized peripheral blood EVs from NSCLC patients as a liquid biopsy for early cancer detection, for predicting postoperative prognosis, and responses to treatment with an immune checkpoint inhibitor, anti‐PD‐1 antibody. We developed cell surface vimentin (CSV) positive EV subsets that also express either epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) or PD‐L1. EVs are small nanovesicles with a size range from 30 to 150 nm [9] that are released from various types of cells. Cancer cells have been reported to release more EVs than normal cells into various body fluids such as blood, semen, and ascites [10]. Vimentin is a major component of the intermediate filament protein family, expressed in normal mesenchymal cells, and is crucial for maintaining cell integrity and providing resistance to stress. Vimentin is also expressed by a variety of cancer cells undergoing epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) that exhibit invasive and metastatic features and cancer associated fibroblasts (CAF) [11, 12]. We have previously reported that EMT cancer cells exhibit immunosuppressive activity, which leads to immune evasion and metastasis [13]. Interestingly, vimentin is expressed as a cell surface membrane protein and is frequently detected in circulating tumor cells (CTCs) in the peripheral blood of patients with various types of cancers [14]. CTCs captured by CSVs have been reported to express transcription factors regulating the EMT, such as FOXC2 [15].

Here, we established an antibody‐based sandwich ELISA‐like assay to measure CSV‐positive EV subsets that co‐express EpCAM (CD326), a marker of epithelial cells, including NSCLC cells, or immunosuppressive PD‐L1, a ligand for the immune checkpoint molecule PD‐1 on activated T cells. Three EV subsets, CSV/CSV, EpCAM/CSV, and CSV/PD‐L1, were evaluated for their potential roles as biomarkers in early cancer detection, postoperative prognosis, and responses to anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy. We found that EpCAM/CSV for early cancer detection, CSV/CSV for postoperative prognosis, and CSV/PD‐L1 for responses to anti‐PD‐1 may be useful diagnostic biomarkers for patients with NSCLC.

Material and Methods

Patient Characteristics

For surgical cases, 57 patients with NSCLC were enrolled who underwent lobectomy and systematic lymph node dissection at Keio University Hospital. For ICI cases, 49 patients who received anti‐PD‐1 antibody treatment for stage IV NSCLC were enrolled at Saitama Medical University Hospital (cohort A). A total of 40 patients were enrolled at the National Hospital Organization and at Keio University Hospital (cohort B). Detailed data of these enrolled patients and healthy donors are listed in Table S1. Informed consent was obtained from all patients. Data collection and analyses were approved by the Institutional Review Boards at Keio University (approval number: 20110159, 20,130,122), International University of Health and Welfare (approval number 24‐lm‐15), and Saitama Medical University (approval number: 5–221).

Analysis of EV Samples

For the detection of each EV subset in plasma, serum, or cell culture supernatants, magnetic beads were coated with either an anti‐CSV antibody, an anti‐EpCAM antibody, or an anti‐PD‐L1 antibody (capture beads). After mixing the capture beads with the samples, the anti‐CSV or anti‐PD‐L1 antibody labeled with alkaline phosphatase, serving as the detector antibody, was added. The luminescence intensity was measured using a chemiluminescence detector, and the signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR) was calculated. MISEV criteria were referred to define the exosome characteristics in this study, and we named our observed particles EVs. EVs were analyzed using a NanoSight LM10 and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to evaluate particle size, and a flow cytometer with magnetic beads‐conjugated antibodies specific for EV markers CD9 or CD63 and antibodies specific to cell surface molecules CSV, EpCAM, or PD‐L1 to evaluate antigen expression.

Statistical Analysis

Overall survival (OS) is defined as the interval between the date of surgical resection or the date of initial ICI administration and the date of death or the last follow‐up. Recurrence‐free survival (RFS) is defined as the interval between the date of surgical resection and the date of initial metastasis, death, or last follow‐up. Progression‐free survival (PFS) is defined as the interval between the initial date of ICI administration and the date of disease progression (PD), as defined by the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST, version 1.1) [16]. The cut‐off values that provided the best separation between the patient groups (high versus low) were determined from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method, while the log‐rank test was used to compare groups. After conducting a normality test using a quantile‐quantile plot, nonparametric tests were performed: the Mann–Whitney test for two groups. Statistical significance is defined as p < 0.05. The detailed material and methods are shown in Doc. S1.

Results

Development of Assays to Measure Tumor Tissue‐Derived EV Subsets in Plasma

We attempted to develop methods to quantitatively measure EV subsets in the plasma of NSCLC patients using two different antibodies: one conjugated to magnetic beads for EV capture and the other conjugated to alkaline phosphatase for the detection of EV subsets (Figure 1). In this study, to evaluate tumor tissue‐derived EV subsets in plasma, we tested combinations of three antibodies specific for cancer cell surface antigens: CSV which is expressed by some cancer cells, particularly those with EMT and circulating tumor cells [15, 17, 18, 19], and CAFs (Figure S1), EpCAM which is expressed by epithelial cells including lung cancer cells and tumor‐derived EVs [20], and PD‐L1 which is expressed in some of the cancer cells, immune cells, and CAFs [21, 22]. The EVs measured with combinations of these capture and detection antibodies are named CSV/CSV, EpCAM/CSV, and CSV/PD‐L1.

Magnetic detection of EV subsets expressing two cell surface antigens Schematic images showing the newly developed antibody‐based sandwich assays utilizing two capture and detection antibodies with magnetic beads to measure three extracellular vesicle (EV) subsets that express cell surface vimentin (CSV) (CSV/CSV) and co‐express epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) (EpCAM/CSV) or PD‐L1 (CSV/PD‐L1).

We first evaluated whether CSV is expressed on EVs in the plasma of NSCLC patients, as CSV expression on EVs has not been previously reported. The particle sizes of EVs captured with the CSV antibody on beads are compared with those of EVs captured with an antibody specific for the EV marker CD9 using NanoSight. It was found that the particle sizes of both EVs are similar, indicating that CSV is expressed on EVs (Figure 2A). The CSV antibody‐captured EVs in culture supernatants of EGFR wild type lung adenocarcinoma cell line A549 were visualized as approximately 100 nm nanoparticles by transmission electron microscope (Figure 2B). We then measured EVs expressing both CSV and CD9 using different combinations of capture and detection antibodies (CSV/CD9 or CD9/CSV) in the culture supernatants of A549, which express CSV, EpCAM, and PD‐L1. The quantity of the EV subsets was measured as the signal‐to‐noise ratio (SNR), which is the total value divided by the value obtained without EV samples. Almost identical amounts of EVs were detected in either sequential use of those antibodies (Figure 2C). A moderate correlation (R 2 = 0.57) was observed between plasma CSV/CSV‐EV (CSV/CSV) levels and the expression levels of vimentin mRNA in tumor tissues, using paired samples from NSCLC patients (Figure 2D). We also evaluated EV subsets expressing either CSV, EpCAM, or PD‐L1 using their specific detection antibody on the beads with captured antibody specific for two representative EV markers, either CD9 (Figure 2E top) or CD63 (Figure 2E bottom) in culture supernatants of A549 using a flow cytometer, and confirmed the presence of the EV subsets, expressing CD9 or CD63 and either CSV, EpCAM, or PD‐L1. Similarly, CD63 antibody‐captured EVs expressing either CSV, EpCAM, or PD‐L1 were detected in culture supernatants of EGFR wild type non‐small cell lung cancer cell lines H358 and H522 (data not shown). These results demonstrated the presence of CD9‐ or CD63‐positive EVs expressing either CSV, EpCAM, or PD‐L1 in the culture supernatants of NSCLC cell lines.

Plasma EpCAM/CSV Is a Potential Biomarker to Detect Early‐Stage NSCLC

We then evaluated the combination of the epithelial cell marker EpCAM for EV capture and CSV for EV detection to measure lung cancer cell‐derived EVs in the plasma of NSCLC patients (Table S1). Significantly higher levels of EpCAM/CSV‐EVs (EpCAM/CSV) were detected in the plasma of 33 stage I resectable NSCLC patients, including 19 stage IA patients with less than 3 cm diameter tumors (red dots, Figure 3A), than those of 20 healthy donors. The plasma levels appeared to increase in patients with stage II‐III disease (p = 0.09) (Figure 3A). EpCAM/CSV showed the highest accuracy (100% sensitivity and specificity) for the detection of cancer patients in all stages compared with CSV/CSV or CSV/PD‐L1 (Figures 3B, 4B and 5B), indicating that EpCAM/CSV is a potential biomarker for the detection of relatively early‐stage NSCLCs, including stage IA (red dots). EpCAM/CSV levels were drastically decreased following surgery when paired pre‐ and post‐surgery samples were evaluated (Figure 3C). A trend of correlation was observed between high EpCAM levels detected by immunohistochemistry in tumor tissues and plasma EpCAM/CSV (p = 0.08, Figure S2A,E). There was no significant difference in plasma levels of EpCAM/CSV between squamous and non‐squamous lung tumor patients (Figure 3D). There was a trend that higher levels of plasma EpCAM/CSV were correlated with worse RFS (Hazard Ratio [HR]: 2.078, 95% confidential interval [CI]: 0.908–8.551, p = 0.066) and OS (HR: 2.258, 95% CI: 0.881–6.581, p = 0.106) after surgery in 57 NSCLC patients (Figure 3E), which may be explained by a larger tumor burden and a more invasive and metastatic EMT feature of patients with high plasma EpCAM/CSV. However, no significant difference in EpCAM/CSV was observed in RECIST responses or the prognosis (OS and PFS) in 49 stage IV NSCLC patients treated with the anti‐PD‐1 antibody (cohort A) (data not shown). These findings suggest that EpCAM/CSV is derived from EpCAM‐positive cancer cells in tumors and may serve as a potential blood marker for monitoring relapses after surgery.

Plasma CSV/CSV May Serve as a Prognostic Biomarker for Patients With NSCLC Following Surgery

We next evaluated CSV/CSV‐EVs (CSV/CSV) in plasma obtained from healthy volunteers and NSCLC patients following surgery or anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy (Table S1). Similar to EpCAM/CSV, significantly higher levels of CSV/CSV were detected in 33 stage I NSCLC patients, including those with 19 stage IA (red dots), as well as in 24 stage II‐III NSCLC patients (Figure 4A and Table S1), compared to 20 healthy donors. However, CSV/CSV was less accurate for detecting cancer patients compared with EpCAM/CSV, although it still showed high accuracy (AUC = 0.956) (Figure 4B). This may be explained by the fact that CSV/CSV may also be derived from non‐cancer cells, including CAFs. CSV/CSV were also drastically reduced following surgery (Figure 4C), indicating that CSV/CSV were also derived from tumor tissues. No difference in plasma CSV/CSV levels was observed between squamous and non‐squamous cell lung tumor patients (Figure 4D). A trend of correlation was observed between the plasma CSV/CSV levels and expression of vimentin in tumor tissues, as detected by immunohistochemistry (p = 0.06, Figure S2B,E).

Higher levels of preoperative plasma CSV/CSV were significantly correlated with a worse RFS (HR: 4.876, 95% CI: 1.332–17.83, p = 0.027) and OS (HR: 3.941, 95% CI: 1.022–15.20, p = 0.047) in 57 stage I‐III NSCLC patients who received surgery (Figure 4E). Similar trends were observed in stage I NSCLC patients (no lymph node metastasis) (RFS; HR: 10.59, 95% CI: 1.558–91.43, p = 0.008, OS; HR: 14.74, 95% CI: 0.933–232.9, p = 0.056), but not in stage II‐III patients (Figure S3A,B), indicating that preoperative plasma CSV/CSV may predict RFS and OS only in stage I patients. Plasma CSV/CSV may not accurately predict the prognosis of patients with stage II‐III disease, who may have non‐resected lymph node metastasis. In 49 stage IV NSCLC patients treated with the anti‐PD‐1 antibody (cohort A), high levels of plasma CSV/CSV at baseline were also correlated with poor PFS (p = 0.028) and OS (p = 0.024) following anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy (Figure 4G). However, clinical responses were not correlated with plasma CSV/CSV at baseline in NSCLC patients (Figure 4F). Significant correlations between high plasma CSV/CSV at baseline and poor post‐therapy prognoses following surgery or anti‐PD‐1 antibody treatment, which may be partly explained by immunosuppressive CSV‐expressing EMT cancer cells and CAFs, suggest that CSV/CSV may be a poor prognostic biomarker for post‐surgery early‐stage NSCLC patients and stage IV NSCLC patients treated with anti‐PD‐1 antibody.

Plasma CSV/PD‐L1 as a Potential Predictive Biomarker in NSCLC Patients Treated With Anti‐PD‐1 Antibody

We finally evaluated CSV/PD‐L1‐expressing EVs (CSV/PD‐L1). NSCLC patients with PD‐L1‐positive cancer cells have been reported to have a poor prognosis, likely due to their immunosuppressive features resulting from PD‐L1/PD‐1 interactions and the more malignant characteristics of PD‐L1‐expressing cancer cells [23]. However, high PD‐L1 positivity of cancer cells in tumor tissues (Tumor Proportion Score; TPS), particularly when more than 50% of the TPS, was correlated with good clinical responses to anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibody therapy because PD‐L1 expression indicates preexisting anti‐tumor CD8+ T cell responses necessary for the efficacy of PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibody therapy [24]. Plasma CSV/PD‐L1 showed similar trends to EpCAM/CSV and CSV/CSV, with high levels in stage I to III NSCLC patients (Figure 5A,B) and a drastic decrease following surgery (Figure 5C), indicating that this is derived from tumor tissues. No significantly different levels of CSV/PD‐L1 were observed between squamous and non‐squamous cell lung cancers (Figure 5D).

High levels of plasma CSV/PD‐L1 at baseline were correlated with poor RFS (HR: 3.505, 95% CI: 1.346–6.717, p = 0.014) but not with OS (HR: 2.185, 95% CI: 0.6152–7.760, p = 0.227) (Figure 5E) in 57 stage I‐III NSCLC patients following surgery, particularly in stage II‐III patients (RFS; HR: 5.737, 95% CI: 2.773–15.69, p = 0.001), whereas less amounts of CSV/PD‐L1 than CSV/CSV might not be sufficient to show prognostic differences in stage I patients (Figure S4A,B). The immunosuppressive nature of patients with high CSV/PD‐L1 derived from the remaining tumors may result in poor RFS compared with patients with stage I. High plasma CSV/PD‐L1 levels were correlated with nodal metastasis, possibly due to the high metastatic activity of PD‐L1‐positive cancer cells, including those undergoing EMT (Table S2). Plasma CSV/PD‐L1 has a tendency (p = 0.07) to correlate with high vimentin immunohistochemically detected in tumor tissues, which may suggest expression of PD‐L1 on vimentin‐positive EMT cancer cells or CAFs that may cause poor prognosis after surgery through their immunosuppressive nature (Figure S2C,E).

However, interestingly, NSCLC patients with high plasma CSV/PD‐L1 at baseline showed a favorable PFS (HR: 0.3484, 95% CI: 0.1672–0.7258, p = 0.009) and OS (HR: 0.3499, 95% CI: 0.1925–0.8947, p = 0.030) following anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy in 49 stage IV NSCLC patients (cohort A) (Figure 5F). Patients with high plasma CSV/PD‐L1 at baseline had favorable PFS in patients with either squamous carcinoma (PFS; HR: 0.2934, 95% CI: 0.2095–0.8875, p = 0.003) or non‐squamous cell carcinoma (PFS; HR: 0.2249, 95% CI: 0.1127–0.5647, p = 0.001) in the PD‐1 antibody therapy (Figure S5A,B). In terms of response rates defined by RECIST1.1, higher levels of CSV/PD‐L1 at baseline were also significantly correlated with favorable responses (CR and PR) to the anti‐PD‐1 antibody compared with intermediate response (SD and PD) in non‐squamous cell carcinoma (most are adenocarcinoma) and EGFR wild‐type adenocarcinoma which is known to include immunogenic cancers. All patients with EGFR mutations, except one, were PD, as previously shown to be tumor mutation burden (TMB)‐low, non‐immunogenic cancers (Figure 5G,H, cohort A). The different prognostic roles of CSV/PD‐L1 and CSV/CSV in anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy indicate the involvement of an anti‐tumor T cell response in NSCLC patients with high levels of plasma CSV/PD‐L1, similar to those with high TPS.

We further attempted to validate the role of EVs expressing both CSV and PD‐L1 using PD‐L1 antibody capture and CSV antibody detection in serum from another cohort of 40 stage IV NSCLC patients treated with anti‐PD‐1 antibody (Table S1, cohort B). Similar to the plasma CSV/PD‐L1, high serum PD‐L1/CSV was correlated with favorable PFS (HR: 0.401, 95% CI: 1.164–5.335, p = 0.036) and OS (HR: 0.390, 95% CI: 0.945–10.63, p = 0.090) following anti‐PD‐1 antibody treatment (Figure 6A), and high serum PD‐L1/CSV was correlated with clinical responses in all the NSCLC patients (p = 0.008) and adenocarcinoma patients (p = 0.012) (Figure 6B). Although the number of samples analyzed was limited, a positive correlation was observed between serum PD‐L1/CSV at baseline and the immunohistochemically analyzed PD‐L1 expression in cancer cells in tumors (Figure S2F). These results suggest that peripheral blood EVs expressing both CSV and PD‐L1 may serve as predictive biomarkers for NSCLC patients treated with anti‐PD‐L1 antibodies, similar to the already approved PD‐L1 expression in tumor cells (TPS). Therefore, peripheral blood CSV/PD‐L1 may be an additional and complementary biomarker in combination with the available tumor‐based biomarkers, including TPS and TMB, because plasma EVs can be easily measured repetitively at appropriate timing before anti‐PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibody therapy, different from TPS, which may need tumor biopsies from advanced and inoperable stage IV NSCLC patients. Further validation of their diagnostic roles in a large number of patients is warranted in comparison with TPS.

The Relation Between PD‐L1‐Related Molecules in Plasma and Tumor Tissues

We also measured soluble PD‐L1 (sPD‐L1) in the plasma of 49 NSCLC patients following anti‐PD‐1 antibody treatment using ELISA. As previously reported, sPD‐L1 was a potential negative predictive biomarker for anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy [25, 26], high plasma sPD‐L1 at baseline was also significantly correlated with worse PFS (HR: 3.429, 95% CI: 1.640–7.533, p = 0.019) and OS (HR: 4.592, 95% CI: 1.765–10.35, p = 0.003) in our study with NSCLC patients following anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy (cohort A) (Figure 6C). A trend was observed (p = 0.095) that high baseline plasma sPD‐L1 levels were associated with PD (Figure 6D). These results indicate that plasma CSV/PD‐L1, different from sPD‐L1, is a positive predictive biomarker similar to the PD‐L1 expression in tumor cells (TPS). The differential roles of plasma CSV/PD‐L1 and sPD‐L1 in relation to post‐therapy prognosis in anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy may be explained by the fact that CSV/PD‐L1 are mainly derived from PD‐L1‐positive EMT cancer cells and CAFs, induced by cytokines such as IFN‐γ released from preexisting anti‐tumor T cells, but sources of sPD‐L1 may not be limited to anti‐tumor T cell responses, and its immunosuppressive activity on T cells was reported to be more potent than that of PD‐L1 positive EVs.

We then evaluated the relationship between plasma levels of CSV/PD‐L1 and sPD‐L1, as well as the mRNA expression of PD‐L1 in tumor tissues, as detected by quantitative real‐time PCR. There was a high correlation between CSV/PD‐L1 and sPD‐L1 (R 2 = 0.589), and there were high correlations of tumor tissue PD‐L1 mRNA with plasma sPD‐L1 (R 2 = 0.529) and relatively less correlation with plasma CSV/PD‐L1 (R 2 = 0.397) (Figure S6A). In addition, there were high correlations between IFN‐γ mRNA in tumor tissues and either PD‐L1 mRNA in tumor tissues (R 2 = 0.684), plasma CSV/PD‐L1(R 2 = 0.504), or plasma sPD‐L1 (R 2 = 0.471) (Figure S6B). There was also a tendency (p = 0.11) of correlation between high plasma PD‐L1/CSV and high immunohistochemically measured CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues (Figure S2D,E). These results indicated that PD‐L1 in tumors and PD‐L1‐related molecules in plasma, such as CSV/PD‐L1 and sPD‐L1, are partly induced by IFN‐γ from preexisting anti‐tumor T cells infiltrating tumors. Similar to PD‐L1 expression in tumor cells (TPS), CSV/PD‐L1 may serve as additional predictive biomarkers for pre‐existing IFN‐γ‐producing CD8+ T cell responses in tumors and clinical response to anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy.

Discussion

In this study, we developed new methods to measure unique subsets of peripheral blood EVs using a newly developed sandwich ELISA‐like technique with two different capture and detection antibodies specific for cell surface molecules, CSV, EpCAM, or PD‐L1. These EVs appear to be derived from tumor tissues, as evidenced by the drastic reductions in their levels following surgery, suggesting that they may serve as liquid biopsies that reflect the conditions of the tumor tissues. The association among EpCAM/CSV, CSV/CSV, and PD‐L1/CSV‐EVs was not strongly significant (EpCAM/CSV vs. CSV/CSV, R 2 = 0.36; EpCAM/CSV vs. PD‐L1/CSV, R 2 = 0.21, ICI Cohort A), indicating that their origins may be different with some overlap: EpCAM/CSV from CSV‐positive EMT cancer cells, CSV/CSV from CSV‐positive cancer cells and CAFs, and CSV/PD‐L1 from PD‐L1‐positive cancer cells and CAFs in tumor tissues.

Detection of early‐stage cancer using various liquid biopsy techniques, including CTCs, cfDNA, or miRNA [27], has been proposed. However, the roles of those biomarkers, including their purpose of use, diagnostic accuracy, and detection reliability, have not yet been well established. In this study, we evaluated unique subsets of plasma EVs. Among them, EpCAM/CSV, which may be derived from CSV‐positive EMT cancer cells, showed the best AUC score for cancer detection compared to other CSV‐expressing EVs such as CSV/CSV and CSV/PD‐L1 even in the surgical cohort that included 12 cases (21%) with tumor diameters less than 3.0 cm evaluated by high‐resolution CT for cancer detection and 10 cases (17.5%) with ground‐glass opacity lung adenocarcinomas that did not show prominent abnormal shadows on chest X‐ray, suggesting that its use for early cancer detection. Validation of the use of EpCAM/CSV‐EVs as an early detection marker in a larger cohort is warranted. Furthermore, plasma EpCAM/CSV showed a tendency to correlate with the high expression of immunohistochemically detected EpCAM in tumor tissues, and it decreased drastically after surgery, suggesting that plasma EpCAM/CSV may be mainly derived from tumor tissues. This finding also supports its potential use for monitoring disease recurrence. (p = 0.08, Figure S2A,E).

We then evaluated the roles of CSV‐positive EVs in the postoperative prognosis of NSCLC patients. High plasma levels of CSV/CSV at baseline were significantly correlated with a worse prognosis following surgery in 57 NSCLC patients, particularly in 33 stage I NSCLC patients. The mechanism may be explained by the malignant features of CSV‐expressing cells in tumors [28], as CSV can be expressed in immunosuppressive CAFs and EMT cancer cells, which we have previously reported to cause immunosuppression and promote metastasis [13, 29]. However, plasma CSV/CSV may not accurately predict the prognosis of patients with stage II–III disease, who may have non‐resected lymph node metastasis. Therefore, CSV‐positive EVs, particularly CSV/CSV, may serve as useful biomarkers to predict the prognosis of postoperative stage I NSCLC patients and to identify patients who may benefit from adjuvant therapy.

Predictive biomarkers for ICIs have been explored in NSCLC patients, among whom only about 20% respond to treatment with an anti‐PD‐1 antibody alone [30]. Biomarkers to identify immunogenic cancers that may respond to ICI, including tumor cells' DNA mutation burden that generates immunogenic neo‐antigens (e.g., TMBhigh: more than 10 mutations per Mb, DNA repair dysfunction such as dMMR and POLE mutations), pre‐existing immune responses (e.g., PD‐L1 expression, T cell/IFN‐γ responses, anti‐tumor T cell subsets such as Tscm/Tpex) at baseline, and the induction of anti‐tumor immune responses (increased CD8+ T cell responses) are being evaluated. TMBhigh, MSIhigh, and PD‐L1 expression in tumor cells (immunohistochemically evaluated TPS) have already been approved as predictive diagnostic biomarkers for anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy for NSCLC patients [31, 32]. However, the prediction accuracy of those markers is still limited, therefore, further searches for better and additional biomarkers have been conducted using various new technologies, including single‐cell and multi‐omics analysis, such as spatial transcriptome and imaging mass spectrometry, to analyze the heterogeneous tumor microenvironment [33].

Among the approved predictive biomarkers, PD‐L1 expression in tumor cells, particularly TPS more than 50%, appears to correlate with responses to PD‐1/PD‐L1 antibody treatment in NSCLC patients, as it is induced by cytokines such as IFN‐γ, which are produced by preexisting anti‐tumor infiltrating T cells in patients with immunogenic cancer cells that harbor a relatively high DNA mutation burden. However, the availability of tumor tissue samples of primary and metastatic NSCLC at appropriate timing before anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy, accurate immunohistochemical evaluation of PD‐L1 expression using only a part of the tumor tissues, and prediction accuracy are the major issues for TPS as a biomarker. Therefore, liquid biopsy reflecting TPS and preexisting T cell responses has been exploited, including PD‐L1 expression in circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and exosomes. The use of PD‐L1 expression in CTCs has been reported, but it requires a larger volume of blood (e.g., 7.5 mL) [34]. PD‐L1 proteins in serum EVs isolated by centrifugation, and PD‐L1 mRNA in plasma EVs, have previously been reported to negatively and positively correlate with responses to anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy in relatively small numbers of patients [35, 36]. Their potential clinical uses still need to be confirmed in a large number of patients.

This study demonstrated that the peripheral blood CSV/PD‐L1 or PD‐L1/CSV‐EV subset may be a predictive biomarker for anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy in stage IV advanced NSCLC patients. High plasma CSV/PD‐L1 at baseline was significantly correlated with clinical responses in NSCLC patients, particularly in those with adenocarcinoma and wild‐type EGFR, which includes immunogenic cancers. These EVs were also positively correlated with prognosis (PFS, OS) following anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy. Similar to TPS, CSV/PD‐L1 may reflect preexisting anti‐tumor T cells, as high CSV/PD‐L1 expression tended to correlate with CD8+ T cells and IFN‐γ expression in tumors in this study. Plasma CSV/PD‐L1 levels decreased in responders after anti‐PD‐1 antibody treatment, likely due to a reduction in tumor burden, similar to a previous report showing a decrease in exosomal PD‐L1 in anti‐PD‐1 antibody responders [37]. Although some reports suggested that high exosomal PD‐L1 proteins at baseline might correlate with non‐response to checkpoint inhibitors in NSCLC [35, 38], further analysis of the possible mechanism is needed. Compared to the difficulties in immunohistochemical evaluation of PD‐L1 in tumor tissues (TPS), our liquid biopsy EV methods may be superior to evaluating PD‐L1 expression status of primary and metastatic tumors, quickly and repetitively at appropriate time points before anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy, thus this EV‐based liquid biopsy may serve as an additional and complementary biomarker for ICI therapy with the previously approved biomarkers.

Interestingly, plasma CSV/PD‐L1 was found to be a negative biomarker following surgery in this study, possibly due to immunosuppressive and malignant features of PD‐L1‐expressing cancer cells or CAFs without PD‐1/PD‐L1 blockade therapy [23, 39]. CSV/PD‐L1 tended to correlate with high vimentin protein expression in tumors (p = 0.07; Figure S2C,E). It was previously reported that exosomal PD‐L1 can decrease cytokine production by T cells, induce apoptosis of CD8+ T cells, and promote tumor growth, possibly through immune evasion, in NSCLC patients [40]. However, anti‐PD‐1 blocking antibodies may overcome this immunosuppressive condition and lead to a favorable prognosis for patients with high plasma CSV/PD‐L1 levels by activating preexisting anti‐tumor T cells.

CSV/PD‐L1 positively and sPD‐L1 negatively correlated with response to anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy in this study, although both appeared to be induced by IFN‐γ produced by T cells. The CSV/PD‐L1 may be primarily derived from PD‐L1‐positive EMT cancer cells and CAFs, which IFN‐γ can induce from preexisting anti‐tumor T cells in tumor tissues. Sources of sPD‐L1 may not be limited to those cells reflecting the anti‐tumor T cell responses in tumor tissues. Its immunosuppressive activity may be more substantial than CSV/PD‐L1 [25, 26]. Further functional analysis of CSV/PD‐L1 is needed for comparison with total PD‐L1‐expressing EVs.

This study has several limitations and biases. The number of patients enrolled was relatively small. Patients with postoperative radiation therapy were not included, and the effect of postoperative chemotherapy was not considered. In the treatment of NSCLC patients with anti‐PD‐1 antibodies, PD‐L1 protein expression in cancer cells (TPS) is one of the standard approved biomarkers for the use of anti‐PD‐1 antibodies. We could evaluate the relationship between CSV/PD‐L1‐EVs and TPS in a semi‐quantitative way. It may be essential to compare TPS and CSV/PD‐L1 values more quantitatively in anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy across a larger patient population, as the correlation between PD‐L1 expression in tumor tissues and blood exosomes has not been well studied [41].

This newly developed liquid biopsy based on the measurement of peripheral blood CSV‐expressing EV subsets may be additional and complementary diagnostic tools along with the approved tumor‐based biomarkers for early cancer detection (EpCAM/CSV), prediction of postoperative prognosis and identification of patients for adjuvant therapy (CSV/CSV), and prediction of clinical responses and post‐therapy prognosis in anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy (CSV/PD‐L1) (Figure S7).

Figure S2: The relationship between plasma CSV‐related EVs and the expression of the associated molecules in tumors.

(A) Tendency of positive correlation was observed between plasma EpCAM/CSV and EpCAM expression in tumors, between plasma CSV/CSV and CSV/PD‐L1 and vimentin expression in tumors, and between plasma CSV/PD‐L1 and CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumors. Tumor tissues were stained with either anti‐vimentin, anti‐EpCAM, or anti‐CD8 antibody. Patients were divided into high (hi) and low (lo) groups based on the percentage of expression of EpCAM (High/Low 7/7), vimentin (CSV/CSV, High/Low 7/6; CSV/PD‐L1, High/Low 7/7), or cell density of CD8+ cells (High/Low 7/7) (defined by median value), and the EV values of the two groups were statistically analyzed using the Mann–Whitney test. Box plots were drawn based on the interquartile range (IQR). The values that exceeded 1.5 times the interquartile range were excluded. (B) Vimentin, EpCAM, and CD8 immunostaining images in a surgically excised tumor tissue (scale bar 50 μm). The representative image of high and low expression was shown. (C) Positive correlation between serum PD‐L1/CSV ‐EV levels and PD‐L1 expression in cancer cells in the biopsied tumor tissues evaluated by immunohistochemistry using paired samples(n = 27).

Figure S3: Clinical characteristics of plasma CSV/CSV‐EV measurement in NSCLC patients with stage I or stage II‐III disease following surgery.

(A, B) RFS and OS according to plasma CSV/CSV‐EV signal levels and TNM stages (A; 33 stage I, High/Low 8/25, B; 24 stage II‐III, High/Low 12/12). Poor postoperative prognosis of patients with high plasma CSV/CSV in NSCLC patients with stage I, but not in patients with stage II‐III disease.

Figure S4: Clinical characteristics of plasma CSV/PD‐L1‐EV measurement in NSCLC patients with stage I or stage II‐III disease following surgery and anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy.

(A, B) RFS and OS according to CSV/PD‐L1‐EV signal levels and TNM stages (A; 33 stage I, High/Low 13/20, B; 24 stage II‐III, High/Low 14/10). Poor RFS of patients with high plasma CSV/PD‐L1in NSCLC patients with stage II‐III.

Figure S5: Clinical characteristics of plasma CSV/PD‐L1‐EV measurement in squamous and non‐squamous NSCLC patients following anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy.

(A, B) PFS and OS following anti‐PD‐1 antibody therapy according to CSV/PD‐L1 EV signal levels and histological subtypes (squamous n = 17, non‐squamous n = 32). Favorable post‐treatment prognosis (RFS) in both squamous and non‐squamous NSCLC patients.

Figure S6: The relationship among plasma CSV/PD‐L1‐EVs, serum sPD‐L1, and gene expressions of PD‐L1 or IFN‐γ in paired tumor samples.

(A) The relationship between the levels of serum sPD‐L1 and plasma CSV/PD‐L1, between levels of serum sPD‐L1 and PD‐L1 mRNA expression in tumors, and between levels of plasma CSV/PD‐L1‐EVs and PD‐L1 mRNA expression in tumors in a surgery cohort (n = 37). (B) The relationship between mRNA expression of IFN‐γ and PD‐L1in tumors, and between levels of plasma CSV/PD‐L1 or serum sPD‐L1, and IFN‐γ mRNA expression in tumors in a surgery cohort (n = 37).

Figure S7: Graphical Abstract.

New antibody‐based sandwich assays to measure plasma extracellular vesicle (EV) subsets derived from tumor tissues were developed. These EVs can be easily measured repeatedly at appropriate times. They may be useful as liquid biopsies for early cancer detection and for predicting efficacy following surgery and anti‐PD‐1 antibody in NSCLC patients.
