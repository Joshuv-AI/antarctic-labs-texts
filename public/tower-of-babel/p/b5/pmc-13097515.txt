Community Medicine Competencies for Postgraduate Year Residents: A Qualitative Study Based on the Six ACGME Competencies in Taiwan
Authors: Shih‐Ming Li, Jin‐Shang Wu, Hsin‐Yun Chang, Pei‐Ling Sung, Zhi‐Cheng Fang, Zih‐Jie Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCommunity medicine is a key topic of training for postgraduate year (PGY) doctors. When physicians practice in community settings, the traditional six core competencies defined by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) are adapted for community contexts. In this study, we performed a qualitative analysis to identify core competencies and relevant training needs for community medicine practice in PGY residents.MethodsSeven clinical teachers involved in community medicine education and seven PGY doctors who had received community medicine training were invited to participate in in‐depth interviews and a focus group, respectively. The interviews and group discussion were transcribed and analyzed thematically. A six‐step thematic analysis was performed to identify the core competencies for community medicine and related training issues.ResultsThe six‐step thematic analysis revealed six core competencies for community medicine: community‐based patient care, community‐based medical knowledge, integration into community systems, the humanistic spirit in the community, self‐improvement and a sustainable community, and interpersonal and communication skills in the community. Training goals and training settings were also identified.ConclusionWe modified the six core competencies for resident physicians to improve alignment with community medicine. The modified competencies may inform relevant PGY training programs.

Introduction

Educational reforms and advances in medical technology and health care have led to the emergence of competency‐based medical education (CBME), which aims to improve the effectiveness of medical training. In CBME, learners' courses or programs are designed in accordance with their core competencies. Training sessions are no longer used to determine whether learners have completed their training. Instead, CBME emphasizes learner‐centered flexibility and training outcomes, enabling learners to demonstrate their acquired skills in community medical settings [1]. The US Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME) proposed the Outcome Project in 1999, which shifted the assessment of education outcomes from a process‐oriented approach to an outcome‐oriented one and outlined six core competencies for resident physicians: practice‐based learning and improvement (PBLI), patient care and procedural skills (PC), system‐based practice (SBP), medical knowledge (MK), interpersonal and communication skills (ICSs), and professionalism (PRO) [2]. In 2013, the Association of American Medical Colleges added two competencies to the ACGME framework—interprofessional collaboration and personal and professional development, expanding the core competencies required in medical education [2]. Using analytic hierarchy process analysis, a study determined the relative importance of the six ACGME competencies for emergency physicians in Taiwan: PC, 27.83%; MK, 20.77%; PRO, 17.93%; ICS, 17.41%; PBLI, 15.52%; and SBP, 8.33% [3]. Furthermore, an importance–performance map analysis revealed that the overall performance value of the six core competencies in Taiwan family medicine was 92.6 when both the importance and fitness indices of 22 sub‐competencies were considered [4]. The six ACGME competencies have been used in Taiwanese medical education [5]. However, some modifications have been suggested to better accommodate specific settings or practice areas [6].

A study identified three knowledge domains by using the workplace learning model: canonical, situational, and personal occupational knowledge [7]. Core competencies may be conceptualized as a multilayered construct that includes canonical, contextual, and personalized competence [8]. Undergraduate medical training prioritizes canonical competence, whereas postgraduate training prioritizes contextual competence [8]. The postgraduate training was made in response to community needs and quality requirements in health care and emphasizes outcome‐based, developmental, competency‐oriented medical education, enabling doctors in training to acquire professional skills and meet community needs [9]. Such core competency frameworks have informed course design and evaluation, contributing to the wider adoption of CBME in health‐care training. Students entering community settings require specific competencies related to community practice, such as home‐based primary care and primary care delivered within the community [10]. Recently, health‐care delivery has shifted from hospital‐based settings to community‐based settings, highlighting differences between hospital and community practice [10, 11].

The severe acute respiratory syndrome outbreak in 2003 exposed several shortcomings in Taiwan's medical practice and medical education systems. In particular, general medical training was insufficient, and specialization was excessive and premature. After the epidemic subsided, the Ministry of Health and Welfare proposed a project to reform clinician training. In July 2003, the postgraduate year (PGY) training program was officially implemented. This program was initially designed as a 3‐month course to help clinicians acquire the six ACGME core competencies during residency. The goals of PGY training are to cultivate residents' ability to provide holistic patient care, reduce premature and excessive specialization in residency training, and improve physicians' competence in managing common medical problems [12]. In the 2‐year PGY program, the first year involves rotations among various medical specialties, with an emphasis on community medicine and elective courses. The first‐year training enables residents to gain exposure to both primary care and specialty practice. The second year allows residents to select one of the following four specialties: general internal medicine, general surgery, general pediatrics, or general obstetrics and gynecology. Residents can also opt for an unspecialized training track with a longer training period, which allows them to explore their clinical interests and develop their career goals as physicians [13].

In the 2‐year PGY training program, a 2‐month community medicine course is included. It seeks to inspire PGY residents to engage with the community, practice holistic care, understand community health factors, and acquire skills essential for assessing community health and developing and implementing health‐care plans. As part of competency‐based, outcome‐oriented medical training, PGY courses must be designed to enhance clinical skills and align with training evaluation. Numerous medical specialty associations have developed or are developing milestones to serve as auxiliary teaching aids for specialty residency training. Nevertheless, appropriate evaluation tools for PGY community medicine training are yet to be developed. CBME focuses on ensuring that learners develop specific competencies after completing the curriculum [11]. Although the six ACGME core competencies have been applied in Taiwanese medical education [3, 4, 5], some modifications may be required for specific fields. Recent studies have identified competencies required for urban general practitioners and family physicians by using a modified Delphi method [14] and have revisited these competencies in rural contexts [15]. In addition, research has outlined competencies for community health nurses [16]. However, few studies have focused on community medicine in PGY training [17]. Of late, health care in the United States has shifted from clinic‐based to community‐based settings [18]. The workplace‐based learning model [10, 11] and the concept of contextual competence in community settings [8] indicate that the six ACGME core competencies require modification for application in community settings. Thus, in the present study, we used a qualitative approach to refine the six ACGME core competencies for PGY residents for application in community settings.

Methods

This qualitative, exploratory study included doctors and teachers with experience in community medicine. In‐depth interviews and a focus group were conducted to identify the core competencies of interest. An expert group (Table 1) was consulted to verify the resulting six core competencies and associated training goals. Rigor for Qualitative Research

Rigor in qualitative research was ensured through congruent design, reflexivity, trustworthiness, data saturation, and mentorship [22, 23].

Design

Because this was a qualitative study, in‐depth interviews and a focus group were conducted to collect data, and thematic analysis was performed to ensure coherence with the study design.

Reflexivity

Reflexivity refers to a set of continual, collaborative, and multifaceted practices through which researchers critically appraise how subjectivity and context affect the research process [24]. Throughout the qualitative study, reflexivity was maintained by the first author (S.‐M.L.) through regular research meetings with physicians who had experience in community medicine training. The remaining research team members—a qualitative researcher, a community medicine physician, a medical educator, a family medicine physician, and two clinical psychologists—contributed multiple perspectives to support self, interpersonal, methodological, and contextual reflexivity.

Trustworthiness

Triangulation was used to improve trustworthiness [22, 23]. Data from each in‐depth interview and the focus group were analyzed by a multidisciplinary research team that included physicians, a qualitative researcher, and clinical psychologists with experience in community settings. In addition, an expert group comprising seven community medicine physicians from different regions was consulted to validate the refined competencies.

Data Saturation

Data were analyzed on a case‐by‐case basis. First, data from Interviewees A–E were used to establish the six core competencies for community medicine. Then, data from Interviewees F–G were used to verify these competencies. The final sample size was determined following the principle of data saturation: data collection and analysis were performed until a comprehensive, multiperspective understanding of the research questions was achieved and no new themes emerged [19].

Mentorship

This study included experts (Table 1) with experience in designing community medicine training programs. For mentorship, two expert group meetings were conducted to validate the six core competencies for community medicine.

Results

Our six‐step thematic analysis revealed three main themes:

Core Competencies in Community Medicine

PGY training in community medicine involves immersing residents in community settings to cultivate core competencies relevant to community medicine. Currently, medical education in Taiwan is competency‐based and focuses on the following six core competencies: PC, MK, SYS, PRO, PBLI, and ICS. On the basis of in‐depth interview and focus group data regarding the characteristics of community medicine and the results of the six‐step thematic analysis (Table 4), we refined the core competencies for community medicine.

Community‐based PC involves the provision of medical services, community diagnosis, health promotion, and preventive advocacy in the community. Physicians practicing in the community must integrate individuals living together into a healthy community and create environments that promote healthy living.Now that we have recognized this, integrating individuals living together into a community based on healthy living is our responsibility as physicians. We must build this community into one that promotes healthy living. Every resident undergoing community medicine training must follow this concept and work toward it (I‐D‐327–330).

Physicians practicing in the community must possess sufficient MK to answer questions that arise in community settings.Our goal is to enable students to understand the situations and problems that they may encounter when serving in the community. Otherwise, they may be unable to adequately care for their patients who often seek their advice first when questions arise. Family physicians encounter a wide range of problems. Patients come to them for guidance, and if referral or hospital admission is required, patients also rely on their recommendations because they are unfamiliar with hospital procedures. PGY Community Medicine Training Goals

According to the three course objectives proposed by the Joint Commission of Taiwan (i.e., community health programs, health‐care systems, and community special care) [25] and the interview data, the primary goals of community medicine are to identify community health problems and use available community resources to provide holistic care.As mentioned earlier, the learning goals include first identifying potential threats or risks to community health and then understanding target populations and intervention methods, including health education. Health education may be delivered through one‐to‐one or one‐to‐many approaches, various media and communication channels, or community activities such as public events to raise awareness and build consensus. Learners must become familiar with multiple intervention methods. Finally, the plan–do–check–act cycle should be introduced. After an intervention is completed, it is necessary to review whether the problem has been addressed and how strategies must be adjusted. Learners must also establish effective connections with community organizations, nongovernmental organizations, and public authorities and learn how to work with different groups, including administrative bodies. These elements constitute additional learning components alongside the six core competencies for resident physicians, which are integrated throughout the training process (I‐F‐43–53).

Training Settings for PGY Community Medicine

Community medicine training involves place‐based learning [25]—also referred to as community‐based medical training [11]—that requires medical students to enter community settings and learn across diverse fields to better understand localized medical practice and the principles of holistic care. In addition to training in hospitals, visits to community‐based facilities such as clinics, health centers, community centers, and long‐term care institutions are vital. Through visiting and serving in these settings, students can develop a clearer understanding of community‐based medical services.I mainly work with the community through a family medicine department, and I also go to local health clinics or visit small‐ and medium‐sized hospitals. I receive more comprehensive training in these settings. For example, in a nursing home or a long‐term care institution, I learn to provide health education, lead activities, and even participate in patients' daily lives (G‐K‐3–7). Discussion

The purpose of medical education is to train student physicians to become competent in providing health‐care services that meet social needs [26]. On the basis of the results of in‐depth interviews, a focus group, and expert group meetings, we modified the six core competencies defined by the ACGME for application in community medicine—also called community‐based medical education [25]. The modified core competencies for community medicine were as follows: community‐based PC, community‐based MK, integration into community systems as SBP, the humanistic spirit in the community as PRO, self‐improvement and a sustainable community as PBLI, and ICSs in the community. Thus, CBME can be effectively applied in community‐based medical training by enabling residents to develop the six core competencies and acquire fundamental skills in community health diagnosis, community resource integration, and provision of holistic care to community residents as specialists [27]. Among the original ACGME competencies, PC, MK, and ICSs have already been applied in community settings, whereas the remaining three competencies have been adapted as SBP, PRO, and PBLI.

Similar to the transition in health care observed in the United States, the primary workplace for medical practice has shifted from clinic‐based settings to community‐based settings [18]. In the context of community medicine, patient care follows a community‐oriented model that includes community health diagnosis [28], primary care practice, and community health promotion. MK in community medicine encompasses preventive medicine, behavioral science [29], and the social determinants of health [30]. Integration into community systems refers to awareness of and responsiveness to the broader health‐care context and system [31] as well as effective use of health‐care resources to provide optimal health care [32]. Self‐improvement and a sustainable community, conceptualized as PBLI [33], involve staying up to date with evidence‐based medicine, using medical technologies to integrate medical information, and adjusting health‐care models in response to community needs. PRO in community medicine extends from individual responsibility in hospital settings to kindness and resilience in the community, reflecting the humanistic spirit in community practice [15, 34]. ICSs in community medicine may be conceptualized across three domains: building relationships with patients and family members, leading teamwork, and connecting with the broader community [35].

As per the multilayered competency model [8], PGY training may place greater emphasis on contextual competence. PGY training is grounded in workplace learning, which includes canonical, situational, and personal occupational knowledge [7]. The six core competencies for community medicine have been aligned with the community context to reflect the needs of PGY residents. Conventional teaching strategies in community medicine include exposure visits to public health facilities, institutions, and community‐based organizations [36]. A study on a community‐based medical training program reported that health centers provide vital service experience; hospital administrators indicated that training in health centers located in remote areas is effective in fostering a sense of responsibility and strengthening ICSs in resident physicians within diverse health‐care systems [37]. Place‐based learning, also referred to as community‐based flipped learning, is an emerging learning model [38] in community medicine training [18]. A study conducted in Japan indicated that community medicine training involves arranging visits to multiple environments, such as primary care clinics and educational institutions [18]. Another study reported that integrating students into communities through exposure to multiple fields enables them to gain diverse learning experiences [36]. Community‐based flipped learning includes four levels of learning: knowledge, attitudes, skills, and societal contribution [38]. In addition to knowledge dissemination, socially immersive learning that integrates learners into community settings is crucial. Similar to service learning, CBME enables learners to connect with the community [39]. Family medicine rural track programs have adopted a prototypical “1 + 2” format in which residents complete PGY1 in an urban setting followed by PGY2 and PGY3 in a rural setting [40]. The present study indicates that community medicine training can be implemented in settings such as primary clinics, health centers, long‐term care institutions, and community organizations to support the development of community‐engaged health care [41]. Integrating the six core competencies and these training settings into PGY community medicine programs can support the cultivation of physicians with competencies in community health diagnosis, community resource integration, and holistic community care. Milestones may serve as a mechanism for implementing CBME and for continually defining and refining core competencies in response to changes in the health‐care delivery system [32]. In the future, milestones for community medicine based on the six core competencies identified in this study will be developed to further enhance the quality of PGY training.

Limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical study to systematically compile data to identify core competencies for community medicine practice among PGY residents in Taiwan. Because this study adopted a qualitative research approach, future studies can use quantitative methods, such as expert questionnaires, to verify the applicability of these findings in teaching practice.
