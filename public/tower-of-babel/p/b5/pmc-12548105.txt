Identifying the needs of parents admitted to the paediatric emergency department
Authors: Ahmet Butun, Ahmet Yeşil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe paediatric emergency department (PED) represents a vital entry point into the healthcare system, particularly for children experiencing acute health issues. However, despite playing a pivotal role in the paediatric care process, the experiences and needs of parents accompanying their children during these visits are often overlooked. Understanding these needs is essential for improving the quality of care provided in PEDs. This study aimed to identify the needs of parents of children admitted to the PED and examine how these needs vary according to demographic and experiential factors, including satisfaction with care.MethodsThis study is a descriptive cross-sectional study. This study was conducted in the PED of a 700-bed tertiary public hospital in southeastern Turkey. The study population consisted of 406 parents of children admitted to the PED. Data were collected between October 7, 2024, and November 15, 2024, using structured questionnaires, including: (1) a socio-demographic form and, (2) the Critical Care Family Needs Inventory - Emergency Department. Data analyses were conducted using SPSS 22.0 with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsParents rated “family member participation in care” significantly higher than other needs (mean difference = 0.11–0.21, all p < 0.05). Introduction

The Paediatric Emergency Department (PED) serves as a critical access point for healthcare, particularly for children experiencing acute health issues. However, despite their significant role in the care process, the experiences and needs of parents accompanying their children during these visits are often overlooked. Understanding these needs is essential for improving the quality of care provided in PEDs, as parents frequently face challenges in recognising when to seek emergency care for their children, leading to unnecessary visits or treatment delays [1, 2]. Research indicates that parents often struggle with the emotional and informational demands of navigating the emergency care system. Many parents exhibit heightened anxiety, particularly when dealing with younger children, which can lead to misinterpretations of symptoms and inappropriate care-seeking behaviours [3, 4]. Furthermore, the lack of clear communication and support from healthcare providers can exacerbate these challenges, leaving parents feeling unprepared and uncertain about their child’s condition [5]. This highlights the importance of developing strategies that not only address the clinical needs of paediatric patients but also consider the psychosocial aspects of parental involvement in emergency care.

Moreover, the existing literature suggested that enhancing the experience of parents in PEDs could lead to better health outcomes for children [5–7]. For instance, effective communication and emotional support have been identified as key components of a satisfactory care experience [5, 8]. Enhancing communication and understanding in the context of PED not only affects parental satisfaction but also correlates with improved adherence to treatment and better health outcomes for children. Parents who are satisfied with the care their children receive are more likely to follow medical recommendations [9]. In addition, personalised communication, transparent information sharing and family engagement have emerged as critical needs during acute paediatric illness [10]. By actively involving parents in decision-making and care processes, healthcare providers can build a collaborative environment that alleviates parental stress and improves overall satisfaction with the emergency care experience [11]. The exploration of parents’ needs in PED reveals a complex interplay of factors influencing their decision-making processes and experiences. Understanding these needs is crucial for improving care delivery and ensuring that parents feel supported during stressful situations.

Given the complex and multifaceted nature of parental needs in paediatric emergency care, a structured framework is essential for systematic evaluation. The Critical Care Family Needs Inventory – Emergency Department (CCFNI-ED) provides such a framework, capturing four core domains of parental needs: (1) family member participation in care, (2) communication with family members, (3) comfort, and (4) supporting family members. In addition, parental satisfaction with staff, communication, information, and care processes is closely linked to how these needs are met, making these factors central to understanding parents’ experiences in the PED. While parental needs in PED have been previously studied [12–15], few studies have examined these needs in low-acuity cases within the context of Turkish healthcare settings, where cultural expectations and service structures differ significantly from developed countries contexts. This study aimed to identify the needs of parents of children admitted to the PED and examine how these needs vary according to demographic and experiential factors, including satisfaction with care. By focusing on the parent perspective, this study could help to enhance the quality of care in paediatric emergency settings, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes for children and greater satisfaction for families navigating these critical healthcare experiences.

Research question

What are the primary needs and satisfaction levels of parents in PEDs, and how do these vary according to socio-demographic characteristics?

Methods

Design

This study is a descriptive cross-sectional study.

Settings, participants, and sampling

This study was conducted in the PED of a 700-bed tertiary public hospital in southeastern Turkey. The tertiary hospital site was chosen as it serves as the regional referral centre for southeastern Turkey, and its PED volume is an average of 400 paediatric cases a day. This site represents the typical resource availability for public hospitals in the region.

Participants were parents (mother or father) of children aged 0–18 years admitted to the PED and were classified into the green-zone category (least urgent code, non-urgent). The inclusion and exclusion criteria were presented in Table 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The minimum required sample size was calculated as n = 384 using the Sample Size Calculator based on a 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and an assumed response distribution of 50%, which represents maximum variability. A total of 406 parents of children admitted to the PED participated in this study which exceed the minimum number of participants and provided sufficient power to detect medium-sized effects.

Data collection

Data were collected between October 7, 2024, and November 15, 2024. Participants were recruited using a convenience sampling method. Only parents whose children were triaged according to the green-zone category (least urgent code, non-urgent) were approached. Parents were recruited after their child had been triaged and while they were waiting to be seen by a physician to avoid interference with clinical care. The researcher (A.B.) explained the purpose and procedures of the study verbally and through a written participant information form, and obtained written informed consent before data collection. Participation was entirely voluntary, and parents were informed to withdraw at any time without any impact on their child’s care. Data were collected using a paper-based, self-administered questionnaire, which parents completed in the PED waiting area. The researcher was only available to assist if clarification was requested and parents completed the questionnaires independently. No identifying information was collected on the questionnaires. Data were later entered into a password-protected electronic database. All electronic files were stored on a secure computer with restricted access. The time to fill out the questionnaire was approximately 10 min.

Data collection tools

Socio-demographic and related-questions form

This form was developed by researcher (A.B.) based on the current literature and his previous experiences [16] (Supplementary file). This form was piloted with 10 participants selected from the target population through convenience sampling before the actual data collection process. Pilot data were collected to assess the clarity and comprehension of the questions and identify any technical issues with data collection. Key modifications based on pilot feedback included adjusting the order of the demographic questions. Pilot participants’ data were excluded from the final analysis, as the purpose was solely to refine the instrument. The form collected the gender of the child (as reported by the parent), age of the child and parents, number of children, place of residence, and educational and employment status of the parents. Questions regarding parents’ satisfaction with the PED healthcare staff, communication, information provided, adequacy of time allocated for care, and satisfaction with overall care were measured using categorical response options (Yes / Partly / No). Questions on service use (e.g., number of PED visits in the past year, arrival time, waiting time, urgency perception) were collected using either multiple-choice or numeric response items. The form did not include free-text responses.

Critical care family needs Inventory - Emergency department (CCFNI-ED)

The CCFNI-ED is a validated instrument developed by Redley and Beanland in 1996 [17] to assess family needs in emergency settings (Supplementary file). The Turkish validity and reliability of the scale was conducted by Sucu [18]. The scale consists of 40 items rated using a 4-point Likert scale. The scale comprises four sub-dimensions: (1) family member participation in care, (2) communication with family members, (3) comfort, and (4) supporting family members. In the evaluation of the scale, the average of each item and the total item average of each sub-dimension were evaluated between 1: not important, and 4: very important. The scores for each item within a domain are averaged to obtain a mean domain score, and an overall mean score can also be calculated across all items. Higher scores indicate greater perceived importance of the need by parents. Conversely, lower scores reflect needs considered less important. In the original study, the Cronbach alpha coefficients of the sub-dimensions of the scale ranged from 0.68 to 0.87, and the total Cronbach alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.91. In this study, the total Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of the scale was 0.976.

Data analysis

The data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0. Frequency and percentage analyses were used to determine the descriptive characteristics of participants, and mean and standard deviation statistics were used to examine the scale. Kurtosis and Skewness values were examined to determine whether the research variables showed normal distribution. In the relevant literature, the results of the kurtosis skewness values of the variables between + 1.5 and − 1.5 [19], + 2.0 and − 2.0 [20] are accepted as normal distribution. It was determined that the variables showed a normal distribution. Parametric methods were used in the analysis of the data.

Independent-samples t-tests were employed to compare mean needs scores between two groups (e.g., mother vs. father, employed vs. non-employed parents). One-way ANOVA (Analysis of Variance) was used for comparisons across more than two groups (e.g., education levels, place of residence, urgency levels, number of visits). When significant differences were found, post hoc analyses were conducted using Tukey or LSD (Least Significant Difference) tests depending on homogeneity of variances.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to examine associations between continuous variables (e.g., child’s age, waiting time, number of children) and parental needs scores, as the assumptions of normality were sufficiently met. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from Mardin Artuklu University Non-Invasive Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Date: 10/09/2024, Reference number: 2024/9–30). In addition, institutional permission was obtained from the Mardin Provincial Directorate of Health (Date: 17/09/2024, Reference number: E-68051626-770-254151354). Also, written informed consent forms were obtained from all participants. This study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The results regarding the descriptive characteristics of the participants were provided in Table 2. When the descriptive characteristics of the parents and their children who visited the PED were examined, 52% (n = 211) of the participants were male and 48% (n = 195) were female. Of all children visits to the PED, 51.2% (n = 208) of the visits were made by mothers and 48.8% (n = 198) by fathers. Among the parents, mothers most frequently held a bachelor’s degree or higher (33.3%, n = 135), whereas primary school graduates represented the lowest proportion (22.9%, n = 93). Similar trends were observed for fathers, with the highest proportion holding bachelor’s or postgraduate degrees (40.6%, n = 165) and the lowest proportion completing only secondary school (20.9%, n = 69). Considering the employment status of the mothers, 64.5% (n = 262) of the mothers were not employed, while 35.5% (n = 144) were employed. In contrast, 88.2% (n = 358) of fathers were employed, and only 11.8% (n = 48) were not employed. Full demographic details were presented in Table 2.

Considering the mean values, the mean age of the children was 6.28 years (min = 0; max = 17), the mean age of the mothers was 33.84 years (min = 20; max = 57), and the mean age of the fathers was 37.30 years (min = 23; max = 69). The average number of children that families have was 2.81 (min = 1; max = 8).

Parents’ experiences with PED were provided in Table 3. When examining the participants’ arrival time to the PED, it was found that 67.7% (n = 275) of the visits were made between 08:00 and 17:00. Of participants, 35.2% (n = 143) reported visiting the PED 1–3 times in the past year, 29.3% (n = 119) reported visiting the PED 4–5 times, and the rate of those who visited the PED 10 or more times in the past year was 8.6% (n = 35). Parents reported a variety of health complaints for their children (Table 3). As participants were allowed to indicate more than one complaint, the cumulative percentages exceed 100%. The most common self-reported health complaints for admission by parents were fever (54.9%, n = 223), cough (42.4%, n = 172) and fatigue (36.0%, n = 146), while the least common health complaints were blackout (2.0%, n = 8) and chest pain (4.2%, n = 17). When evaluating parents’ self-perception of the urgency of their child’s condition, 54.2% (n = 220) of parents perceived their child’s condition as “semi-urgent”, 27.8% (n = 113) reported as “urgent” and 18.0% (n = 73) reported as “non-urgent”.

When the parents’ satisfaction levels with the healthcare staff were evaluated, it was found that 64% (n = 260) of the parents were satisfied with the healthcare staff, and 65.3% (n = 265) were satisfied with the communication of the healthcare staff. The rate of satisfaction with the information provided by the healthcare staff was 61.8% (n = 251). The proportion of parents who believed that the duration of treatment and care was sufficient was 59.4% (n = 241). Of the parents, 69.2% (n = 281) stated that they would revisit PED if their child had the same health problem in the future. The mean waiting time of parents in the PED was 35.22 min (SD = 38.336, range = 1–330).

Means of parents’ needs score were presented in Table 4. When the mean scores of the parents’ needs were evaluated, the mean of general needs was found to be 2.842 ± 0.713 (range = 1–4). When the sub-dimensions were examined, the highest mean score was observed in the sub-dimension of family members’ participation in care (2.895 ± 0.729, range = 1.-4). The mean score for the communication sub-dimension was 2.886 ± 0,749 (range = 1–4), indicating relatively high importance reported by parents. The mean score of the comfort sub-dimension was 2.789 ± 0.781 (range = 1–4), and the supporting family members sub-dimension was 2.798 ± 0.800 (range = 1–4). The scores of both sub-dimensions were close to each other, indicating that parents’ expectations for physical and emotional support were balanced. While ‘family participation in care’ showed the highest mean (2.895 ± 0.729) (range = 1–4), it did not differ significantly from ‘communication with family members’ (2.886 ± 0.749; p = 0.82, d = 0.01) but was marginally higher than ‘comfort’ (2.789 ± 0.781; p = 0.06, d = 0.14) and ‘supporting family members’ (2.798 ± 0.800; p = 0.08, d = 0.13).

The parents’ needs score means

The differentiation of parents’ needs scores according to descriptive characteristics was provided in Table 5. In the analysis of parents’ needs scores according to different demographic characteristics, significant differences were observed in some variables. In terms of the gender of the child, it was determined that parents with a female child scored higher in the sub-dimensions of comfort (p = 0.025) and supporting family members (p = 0.039). The fathers scored significantly higher than the mothers in the sub-dimension of supporting family members (p = 0.008). According to the mother’s employment status, it was determined that employed mothers had significantly higher scores than non-employed mothers in the sub-dimension of supporting family members (p = 0.004). Similarly, fathers who were not employed had higher scores than employed fathers in the sub-dimensions of comfort (p = 0.005) and supporting family members (p = 0.007).

According to the place of residence, families living in the village had significantly higher scores in all sub-dimensions than those living in the city centre and the district (p = 0.05). In addition, families who visited the PED between 24:00 and 08:00 at night had significantly higher scores in all sub-dimensions in terms of participants’ arrival time to the PED (p = 0.001). According to the urgency of the child’s condition, non-urgent visits scored higher in the sub-dimension of family member support processes than semi-urgent and urgent cases (p = 0.045).

In terms of the number of visits to the PED in the last year, families who visited 6–7 times received the highest scores, whereas those who visited 10 or more times received the lowest scores. This difference was significant in the sub-dimension of supporting family members (p < 0.001). In terms of factors such as satisfaction with healthcare staff, satisfaction with the communication of healthcare staff, and providing information by healthcare staff, the needs scores of satisfied families were higher, and this difference was significant in all sub-dimensions (p < 0.001).

Among the variables that did not differ significantly were the education level of the mother and father, the differences between those living in the district and the city centre in terms of the place of residence, the child’s care satisfaction. These results showed that some demographic variables and satisfaction status were important determinants in determining the needs of parents.

The correlation analysis between parents’ needs and some variables was presented in Table 6. Correlation analysis between parents’ needs and some variables showed significant relationships between the child’s age and waiting time in the PED and parents’ needs. A significant negative correlation was found between the age of the child and the general score of the parents’ needs (r=-0.141, p = 0.004). This relationship suggests that the needs of families decrease as the age of the child increases. Similarly, significant negative relationships were found between the age of the child and communication with family members (r=-0.111, p = 0.025), participation of family members in care (r=-0.173, p < 0.001), comfort (r=-0.107, p = 0.032) and supporting family members (r=-0.136, p = 0.006).

A significant negative correlation was found between waiting time in the PED and the overall score of the parents’ needs (r=-0.116, p = 0.020). Among the sub-dimensions, significant negative relationships were found between comfort (r=-0.132, p = 0.008), supporting family members (r=-0.141, p = 0.004) and waiting time. This suggests that as the waiting time increases, families’ negative perceptions of the level of meeting their needs increase.

No significant relationship was found between variables such as mother’s age, father’s age and number of children, and parents’ needs (p > 0.05). These results show that parents’ age and number of children did not affect their perception of needs.

Discussion

This study revealed the needs of the parents of children admitted to PED. This study indicated that “family member participation in care” was the most important need of parents of children admitted to PED. In addition, “communication with family members” was the second most important need of parents, followed by “comfort” and “supporting family members” respectively. Another study found that “communication with family members” was the most important need, followed by “family member participation in care”, “supporting family members”, and “comfort” respectively [13].

This study found that the highest mean score was in the sub-dimension of family members’ participation in care. This situation showed that the involvement of the patient’s relatives in the care process was more important than other needs. Family participation in care starts with clinicians soliciting information from the parent, listening to them, and then incorporating the family’s knowledge and preferences into the child’s treatment plan [21]. Family participation should be considered an integral part of paediatric emergency care, as supported by prior research highlighting its role in improving both child outcomes and parental satisfaction [7]. This was supported by the existing literature that found that family participation can lead to improved communication and reduced anxiety for patients [22]. Family participation in the treatment process fosters a collaborative environment that enhances the overall care experience [22]. This aligns with the reported mean score, indicating that families value their role in the care process. Moreover, involving families in care decisions leads to better treatment plan adherence and improved health outcomes [23].

Implementation of family involvement protocols is recommended. Such initiatives can improve the delivery of high-quality nursing care to hospitalised children, ultimately optimising patient outcomes and family satisfaction. The existing literature emphasises the importance of family-centred care and collaborating with parents [24]. This could improve the delivery of high-quality nursing care to children, ultimately optimising patient outcomes and family satisfaction [24]. This supports the notion that high family participation scores reflect a positive trend towards integrating family members into the care process.

The results revealed that the need for communication was also an important need in PED settings. This indicates that families recognise the importance of effective communication in their child’s care experience. Effective communication between healthcare providers and family members is essential to ensure that families are well-informed about their child’s conditions and treatment plans. In line with this result, the existing studies found that clear communication is important in the doctor-patient relationship [25]. Unlike studies that identified communication as the single most important parental need in emergency settings [26, 27], our results suggested that participation in care ranked slightly higher. This may reflect cultural expectations regarding family involvement in care decision-making in the local context. In addition, inadequate communication can lead to misunderstandings and increased anxiety for both patients and their families, ultimately decreasing satisfaction with the care received [25].

The mean scores of “comfort” and “supporting family members” sub-dimensions were close to each other, indicating that parents’ expectations for physical and emotional support were balanced. These results are consistent with those reported in the existing literature [26, 27]. Parmar et al. [28] suggested that enhancing the support provided to family caregivers can lead to improved comfort levels for both caregivers and patients. This underscores the necessity of balancing physical comfort with emotional and psychological support in healthcare settings.

The study provided important insights into understanding the parents’ overall satisfaction level and admission habits. This study revealed that 64% of parents were satisfied with the healthcare staff. In addition, 65.3% of parents were satisfied with the communication of the healthcare staff. In terms of factors such as satisfaction with healthcare staff, satisfaction with the communication of healthcare staff, and providing information by healthcare staff, the needs scores of satisfied families were higher, and this difference was significant in all sub-dimensions (p < 0.001). While the rate of satisfaction with the care the child received in the PED in this study (61.8%) appears higher than those reported in previous studies, 52.7% in [16] and 40.2% in [29], this discrepancy may be influenced by methodological factors such as sampling approach and data collection setting. Satisfaction with care was positively related to meeting the needs of the families [30]. Various factors influence parental satisfaction, including the quality of care provided, the communication skills of healthcare professionals, and the information shared during medical encounters. Research consistently shows that effective communication between healthcare providers and parents is fundamental to enhancing satisfaction levels. In line with these results, the existing literature found that clear communication regarding treatment plans and the emotional support provided by healthcare staff was associated with higher satisfaction rates among parents in PED [31, 32]. Providing healthcare staff with communication skills training could result in improved patient satisfaction [33].

Fever was the most frequently reported reason for admission. This showed that fever was an important cause of admission in children. Participants were allowed to report more than one complaint; therefore, these results reflect cumulative frequencies rather than mutually exclusive chief complaints. In line with this study, existing studies found that the rates of PED visits due to fever were 32.1% [16] and 19% [34]. Insufficient parents’ fever management process at home may lead to PED visits and inappropriate medication use [35]. Therefore, this study suggested that increasing parental health literacy regarding common childhood illnesses, such as fever, could help parent to better management of their child’s health problems at home rather than visiting the PED.

In addition, 54.2% of the parents reported that their child’s condition was “semi-urgent”, and 18% reported “non-urgent”. In line with the results of this study, Butun [16] found that 31.8% of PED visits were semi-urgent and 4.9% of PED visits were non-urgent cases, respectively. This indicates that PED was used for children with non-urgent conditions. This result is consistent with the existing literature [3, 36, 37]. Using the PED for non-urgent conditions contributes to overcrowding and many negative consequences, including poor patient outcomes and dissatisfaction of patients and healthcare staff.

Implications for practice and recommendations

This study highlights the specific needs of parents in PED, particularly the importance of communication and participation in care. These needs are closely associated with parental satisfaction and may inform targeted interventions to improve the patient and family experience. For example, structured family engagement strategies and communication training for PED staff could enhance both informational and emotional support, especially during non-urgent visits. Although generalizability is limited due to the single-site design, the results suggest that increasing family involvement through shared decision-making and consistent communication may improve satisfaction and outcomes. Furthermore, the results showed that 69.2% of parents would return to the PED for similar issues underscores the need for further research into parental health-seeking behaviours and reasons for choosing PED over alternative healthcare services. Future efforts should focus on refining communication strategies, promoting family participation, and exploring contextual factors, such as health literacy and access to primary care, that shape parental use of emergency care services.

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is the use of the CCFNI-ED, a robust and psychometrically validated tool (Cronbach’s α = 0.976 in this study), to systematically capture nuanced parental needs that are often overlooked in PED settings. However, this study is not without limitations. This study was conducted only in one PED of tertiary hospital; therefore, the results may not be generalisable to all PEDs. However, the results concurred with the existing literature. In addition, the needs scores were significantly higher among parents who reported satisfaction with healthcare staff, communication, and information provision. This relationship suggests a potential response bias, where parents who are more satisfied may perceive their needs as being better met. This factor should be considered when interpreting the results of the overall needs assessment, as it may influence the generalizability of the results. As this study was conducted in a single tertiary hospital in southeastern Türkiye, the results may not be generalisable to other settings or populations with different healthcare structures, cultural norms, or parental expectations.

Conclusion

This study identified the specific needs and satisfaction levels of parents presenting with non-urgent cases to the PED. Identifying the needs of the parents of children admitted to the PED could enhance the quality of care in PED settings and lead to improved health outcomes for children and greater parental satisfaction. Among the four domains assessed, parents reported the greatest needs in relation to participation in care and communication with healthcare staff. These results underscore the importance of family-centred practices and highlight the role of effective communication in shaping parental satisfaction.

While the results provide valuable insights into parental expectations, they should be interpreted with caution given the single-centre design and restriction to non-urgent visits. Future studies with multicentre and comparative designs are warranted to further explore how contextual and cultural factors influence parental needs and satisfaction in emergency settings.

In practice, the results suggest that healthcare providers can enhance parental satisfaction through strategies such as structured communication training, shared decision-making, and initiatives that promote active family involvement in care. Addressing these needs has the potential to improve the overall experience of families in the PED, support more efficient service delivery, and strengthen trust in emergency healthcare systems.
