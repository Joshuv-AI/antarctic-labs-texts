Effect of vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy and lactation on the development of infants born to Tanzanian women living with HIV: a secondary analysis of a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Temiwunmi Shobanke, Alfa Muhihi, Nandita Perumal, Nzovu Ulenga, Fadhlun M Alwy Al-Beity, Christopher P Duggan, Wafaie W Fawzi, Karim P Manji, Christopher R Sudfeld
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfants born to pregnant women living with HIV (WLHIV) are at greater risk for morbidity and mortality and may also have poorer developmental outcomes as compared with infants who are not exposed to HIV. Nutrition interventions in pregnancy may affect developmental outcomes.ObjectivesThis study evaluated the effect of maternal vitamin D supplementation on infant development outcomes.DesignWe conducted a secondary analysis of a randomised, triple-blind, placebo-controlled trial of maternal vitamin D supplementation from June 2015 to October 2019.SettingAntenatal care clinics in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania.ParticipantsPregnant WLHIV and their offspring.InterventionsDaily 3000 IU vitamin D3 or placebo supplements taken during pregnancy and lactation.Outcome measuresInfants were assessed for cognitive, language and motor development at 1 year of age with the Caregiver Reported Early Development Instruments (CREDI).ResultsA total of 2167 infants were eligible, and 1312 of them completed CREDI assessments at 1 year of age. Vitamin D supplementation had no effect on overall CREDI z-scores (standardised mean difference (SMD) 0.03, 95% CI −0.09, 0.15, p value 0.66). There was also no evidence of a difference between vitamin D and placebo groups in language (SMD 0.06, 95% CI −0.08, 0.21, p value 0.40), motor (SMD 0.02, 95% CI −0.09, 0.14, p value 0.69) or cognitive domain z-scores (SMD 0.05, 95% CI −0.08, 0.17, p 0.48).ConclusionsMaternal vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy and lactation did not affect infant development outcomes.Trial registration numberClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02305927.

Introduction

Globally, there are an estimated 14.8 million HIV-exposed uninfected (HEU) children, children who have a mother living with HIV but are not infected themselves, and the majority reside in sub-Saharan Africa.1 The number of HEU children is expected to grow in sub-Saharan Africa due to increased coverage of antiretroviral therapy (ART) among pregnant women living with HIV (WLHIV).1 2 HEU infants in sub-Saharan Africa have been well documented to have a greater risk of mortality as compared with infants born to women without HIV infection.3 However, studies have found that HEU children may also be at greater risk for suboptimal cognitive, motor and social–emotional outcomes as compared with their HIV-unexposed peers.46 While HIV exposure and selected ART medications may influence health and developmental outcomes, HIV-exposed infants may also experience social, economic and environmental factors related to living in an HIV-affected household, including caregiver illness, parental mental health issues and limited availability of learning resources.7 8 As a result, interventions are needed to improve the health and developmental outcomes of children born to pregnant WLHIV.

Maternal and child nutrition interventions may have positive effects on child developmental outcomes.9 Vitamin D is an essential micronutrient that is well-known to play an important role in calcium–phosphorus homeostasis and bone metabolism.10 Of note, observational studies have found that vitamin D deficiency is common among adults living with HIV and is associated with increased risk of HIV disease progression.11 In addition, vitamin D has been documented to have extraskeletal effects, and several studies have also shown that vitamin D may have neuroprotective functions.12 13 Vitamin D may be related to the aetiology of degenerative neurological diseases, including the development of multiple sclerosis as well as progression of Parkinsonism and dementia among older adults.14 Vitamin D may also play a role in fetal and infant neurodevelopment.15 16 Meta-analyses of randomised trials have found that vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy may improve birth weight, but the evidence on whether these benefits extend to early childhood growth and development remains unclear.17 18 Of note, due to the high risk of suboptimal developmental outcomes among HEU children, there may be greater potential to benefit from vitamin D supplementation or other interventions in this population.

We conducted a study to evaluate the effect of maternal vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy and lactation for WLHIV on infant developmental outcomes in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. We hypothesised that vitamin D supplementation would improve developmental outcomes.

Methods

We conducted a secondary analysis of a randomised, triple-blind, placebo-controlled trial of vitamin D3 supplementation that was conducted among 2300 pregnant WLWH on ART in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania, from June 2015 to October 2019 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02305927). The trial protocol and the primary trial results on maternal HIV progression or death, risk for small-for-gestational-age and risk for infant stunting at 12 months have been reported elsewhere.19 20 Briefly, pregnant WLHIV were eligible to be included in the study if they were adults aged ≥18 years, were 12–27 weeks gestation as determined by the reported last menstrual period at the time of randomisation, were receiving ART and had normal serum albumin-adjusted calcium levels (≤2.6 mmol/L) at the time of screening. The trial exclusion criteria were mothers who planned to relocate outside of Dar es Salaam during the study follow-up period, enrolment in any other clinical trial and not providing informed consent.

Trained research nurses identified pregnant WLHIV and assessed eligibility criteria. Pregnant participants who were eligible for randomisation and consented to enrolment were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to a vitamin D supplementation or placebo group, stratified by the study clinic. The randomisation list was computer-generated with sequence blocks of 10 that were stratified by the antenatal care clinic. The participants, study staff, investigators and study statisticians were masked to randomised group assignments, and therefore the trial was triple-blind. The vitamin D supplementation group received 3000 IU vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) oral supplements to be taken daily from randomisation to 1 year post partum. The placebo group received identical placebo supplements that were to be taken daily from randomisation to 1 year post partum. As defined by the US Institute of Medicine, the upper limit for vitamin D intake during pregnancy and lactation stands at 4000 IU/day.21 To fully conceal allocation, regimen bottles were prelabelled with sequentially numbered participant identification codes. Follow-up study visits occurred every 4 weeks during pregnancy, at delivery, and mothers and infant pairs were followed up at 6 weeks and every month thereafter until trial discharge at 1 year. All infants received an HIV-1 DNA PCR test at 6 weeks and 12 months of age.

Infants were assessed at trial endline at 10–16 months of age with the ‘Long Form’ of the Caregiver Reported Early Development Instruments (CREDI).22 The CREDI is a caregiver-reported measure of early childhood development for children from birth to age 3, which focuses on milestones and behaviours across motor, cognitive, language and social–emotional development domains.22 The CREDI included 44 items and was a culturally appropriate tool, given it was developed and initially validated in Tanzania.22 CREDI assessment was implemented during the conduct of the trial from 3 April 2017, with all infants being eligible for CREDI assessment on or after this date. A total of 855 infants completed their 1-year endline visit before CREDI was implemented as part of the trial protocol.

Statistical analysis

We conducted complete case analyses with the intention-to-treat approach as the primary analytic strategy to examine the effect of maternal vitamin D supplementation on child development outcomes in the trial. CREDI norm-referenced standardised z-scores were calculated with the CREDI scoring application (https://credi.shinyapps.io/Scoring_App/). The reference population for the CREDI long form scoring was based on a sample of children with ‘advantageous’ home environments. The CREDI norm-referenced z-scores have a mean of 0 and SD of 1. Our estimates can be interpreted as standardised mean differences or z-score differences; nutrition interventions may improve development outcomes by ~0.1 z-scores while parenting interventions have a larger effect of ~0.3 z-scores.23 24 In a post hoc power calculation, based on our observed sample size of 1312 infants, we had 80% power to detect a 0.16 z-score difference in developmental scores.

Linear mixed-effects regression models were used to determine the effect of vitamin D supplementation on infants’ continuous overall, motor, language and cognitive CREDI z-scores at 1 year of age. Clustering for twin infants was accounted for in analyses with compound symmetry matrices. The clinic site was included as a fixed effect to account for stratified randomisation. Standardised mean differences (SMDs) or differences in z-scores and their 95% CIs for vitamin D supplementation as compared with placebo are presented for overall and domain scores. The primary analyses also included covariates for child sex and age to account for extraneous variation in CREDI scores and increase statistical power.

We conducted two sensitivity analyses for each outcome. The first was a sensitivity analysis to address the potential for baseline imbalance between treatment arms that adjusted for baseline maternal age, education and wealth quintile. We also conducted sensitivity analyses using inverse probability of censoring weighting (IPCW) to account for potential biases from loss to follow-up and missing child development data.25 We constructed stabilised weights (ratio of the marginal probability of remaining uncensored to the conditional probability of remaining uncensored given baseline covariates), using available variables that were theoretically associated with loss to follow-up or developmental outcomes.25 This weighting approach creates a pseudo-population in which censoring is independent of measured covariates, allowing for unbiased effect estimates. Stabilised censoring weights were constructed using maternal age (continuous), wealth quintile (categorical: poorest, middle and richest quintiles), gestational age (continuous), WHO HIV stage (categorical: I–IV), maternal body mass index (categorical: <18.5 kg/m2, 18.5 kg/m2–24.9 kg/m2, 25.0kg/m2–29.9 kg/m2 and ≥30.0 kg/m2), CD4 count (categorical: <200, 200–349, 350–499 and ≥500), infant sex at birth (categorical: male, female), birth weight (continuous) and gestational age (continuous). In addition, we also a priori aimed to explore whether the effect of vitamin D supplementation on infant development was modified by maternal age, child sex, maternal education, socioeconomic status and parity. Effect modification was assessed by including interaction terms between each potential modifier and the intervention group in multivariable regression models. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted with R V.4.3.1.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The trial flow diagram is presented in figure 1. A total of 3153 pregnant WLHIV were screened for recruitment, of which a total of 2300 were enrolled and randomised. In the placebo group, one mother was administratively censored from the study after testing negative for HIV. A total of 2167 live births were recorded, and 1312 infants had CREDI data available at 1 year of age. Online supplemental table 1 summarises the baseline characteristics of mothers who had CREDI data (n=1295) as compared with mothers without CREDI data (n=1004). Pregnant WLHIV whose infants had CREDI data tended to be older, had higher parity, a greater proportion of missing CD4 T-cell counts and a higher proportion of those with more severe WHO HIV disease stage as compared to pregnant WLHIV whose infants did not have CREDI data. Table 1 summarises the characteristics of mothers and infants who had CREDI data stratified by randomised regimen. There was no difference between baseline characteristics by randomised group (online supplemental table 2). In the analytic sample of infants with CREDI data, the vitamin D3 and placebo groups appeared well-balanced.

Sensitivity analyses with multivariable adjustment for potential baseline imbalance are presented in online supplemental table 3. The multivariable model findings were consistent with the primary analyses for overall CREDI z-scores (SMD 0.02, 95% CI −0.11, 0.14, p value 0.81) as well as cognitive (SMD 0.03, 95% CI −0.09, 0.16, p value 0.62), language (SMD 0.04, 95% CI −0.10, 0.19, p value 0.56) and motor z-scores (SMD 0.01, 95% CI −0.11, 0.12, p value 0.89). Sensitivity analyses using IPCW to address missing data and loss-to-follow-up were also consistent with the primary analyses (online supplemental table 4).

We then explored effect modification for overall (table 3), cognitive (online supplemental table 5), language (online supplemental table 6) and motor z-scores (online supplemental table 7). There was no evidence of modification of the effect of vitamin D supplementation on overall and developmental domain z-scores by child sex, parity, household wealth and maternal education. There was some evidence of a potentially greater effect of vitamin D supplementation on overall CREDI z-scores among young pregnant women aged 18–24.9 years at randomisation (SMD 0.35; 95% CI −0.83, 0.12) compared with those aged 25–34.9 years (SMD 0.04; 95% CI −0.27, 0.20) and 35+ years (SMD −0.14; 95% CI −0.21 to –0.48) (p value for effect modification: 0.02). There was also a similar pattern of effect modification by maternal age for language and motor z-scores (p values for effect modification: 0.04 and 0.03, respectively). However, there was no statistically significant effect of vitamin D as compared with placebo on development outcomes within any subgroup of maternal age.

Discussion

We found no effect of maternal vitamin D3 supplementation during pregnancy and lactation overall, as well as cognitive, language and motor domain CREDI z-scores at 1 year of age. These findings were robust to sensitivity analyses. There was no clear evidence for potential modifiers of the effect of vitamin D supplementation on child development scores; however, there was some suggestion that younger mothers may have a more beneficial effect as compared with older mothers.

Our trial findings are consistent with randomised trials that have found that maternal vitamin D supplementation does not influence childhood development outcomes. A double-blinded, placebo-controlled trial in Denmark (COPSAC study) with 623 pregnant women receiving either high-dose (2800 IU/day) or standard-dose (400 IU/day) vitamin D3 during the third trimester showed no significant effect on neurodevelopmental outcomes up to 6 years of age.26 Similarly, a randomised controlled trial conducted in Finland compared daily higher-dose (1200 IU/day) versus standard-dose (400 IU/day) of vitamin D3 administered to full-term healthy infants over the first 2 years of life and found no significant differences in developmental and emotional/behavioural scores between the infant groups receiving either higher or standard doses.27 In combination with our trial findings, there is no evidence of an effect of maternal vitamin D supplementation on child development outcomes from randomised controlled trials.

Our study finding that there was no effect of maternal vitamin D supplementation on child development scores is not consistent with observational studies that have found lower 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) concentrations in pregnancy were associated with poorer developmental outcomes. A large observational cohort study in southwest England among 7065 mother–infant pairs found that 25(OH)D concentrations <50 nmol/L in pregnancy were associated with a greater risk for children to be in the lowest quartile for gross motor, fine motor and social development at 30 months of age; however, in this study, there was no association with developmental outcomes at 7, 8 and 9 years of age.26 Relatively large studies in Spain (n=1820), China (n=363) and Australia (n=250) also found that higher 25(OH)D levels in pregnancy or cord blood were positively associated with selected developmental outcomes among children under 2 years of age.2729 Nevertheless, multiple observational studies have also found null associations. A study conducted in China also found no association of 25(OH)D in pregnancy with communication, motor skills, problem-solving and personal social development of children at 2 years of age.30 Similarly, a study in the USA found no association between 25(OH)D in pregnancy and cognitive development, achievement or behaviour among children 8 months–7 years of age.31 As a result, observational evidence on the role of vitamin D in pregnancy with child development is mixed and may differ by child age and developmental domain. Nevertheless, it is important to note that observational studies of vitamin D are at risk of confounding by factors such as immune activation, health status, socioeconomic status and other factors that are associated with vitamin D status and child development.

Maternal vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy and lactation may affect child development through multiple proposed pathways, including improvements in birth outcomes associated with suboptimal development outcomes and improvements in infant vitamin D status through breastmilk.32 However, in our trial and meta-analyses of vitamin D supplementation trials, maternal vitamin D supplementation has not been shown to have a beneficial effect on preterm birth, small-for-gestational age births or low birth weight.18 In terms of improvements in infant vitamin D status, there have been multiple proposed mechanisms for vitamin D to affect brain development based on in vitro studies. Vitamin D metabolites can cross the blood-brain barrier and may play a role in the differentiation of neurons and act as an anti-inflammatory agent in the brain.33 34 In addition, vitamin D may affect neurotransmitter synthesis, neurotrophic factor expression, intracellular calcium signalling and antioxidant activity during fetal development.35 Nevertheless, despite these potential biological mechanisms, epidemiological evidence is growing and suggests there is no effect of maternal vitamin D supplementation on functional infant developmental outcomes. In our trial, we found maternal vitamin D supplementation increased infant 25(OH)D concentrations at 6 weeks and 6 months of age, but not at 12 months of age, as compared with placebo.19 As a result, vitamin D supplementation trials for infants and children may have a greater impact on their vitamin D status and developmental outcomes as compared with maternal vitamin D supplementation alone. Future randomised trials should evaluate the effect in populations where maternal and infant severe vitamin D deficiency is present, and studies that combine maternal and infant vitamin D supplementation.

The strengths of our study include the randomised design and relatively large sample size. In addition, the majority of previously reported evidence examining the association between vitamin D and child development has been reported in the context of high-income countries, whereas our study adds to the evidence from sub-Saharan Africa and the context of HIV. Nevertheless, there are several limitations to this study. First, we assessed child development using CREDI, a caregiver-reported measure, which introduces the possibility of misclassification in development status due to the reliance on caregiver perceptions rather than direct child-administered assessments. This misclassification may have biased the results towards the null. In addition, we assessed infant development at 1 year of age, and therefore, we were not able to capture effects that may emerge or are more easily measured at older ages. In addition, we had limited power to detect small effects on developmental outcomes. In addition, there was missing data on CREDI scores, but sensitivity analyses with IPCW were fully consistent with the primary analyses. Finally, the trial was conducted among pregnant WLHIV and their infants in urban Tanzania, which may limit the generalisability of the findings to other populations.

Conclusions

Overall, we found that maternal vitamin D supplementation during pregnancy and lactation did not affect developmental outcomes at 1 year of age for infants born to pregnant WLHIV. Our findings are consistent with the current WHO recommendations, which do not recommend routine vitamin D supplementation in pregnancy or lactation. Interventions designed to promote child development, such as packages of interventions that combine parenting, maternal mental health, infant health and nutrition interventions, should be pursued to support the development of children born to pregnant WLHIV.
