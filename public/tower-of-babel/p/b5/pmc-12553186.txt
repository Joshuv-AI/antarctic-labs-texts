Racial and ethnic disparities in childhood cardiovascular health: evidence of the role of social disadvantage from NHANES 2013–2018
Authors: Nicole D Fields, Holly C Gooding, Amanda M Perak, Miriam B Vos, Liliana Aguayo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe loss of cardiovascular health (CVH)—a strong predictor of future health—begins early in life and disproportionately affects racial/ethnic minorities. We sought to quantify the contribution of household disadvantage to racial/ethnic disparities in CVH among US youth.MethodsWe analyzed data from 6690 youth (60.3% non-Hispanic White [NHW], 14.9% non-Hispanic Black [NHB], and 24.8% Hispanic/Latinx) ages 2–18 years old from the 2013–2018 National Health and Nutrition Examination Surveys. CVH was measured using the American Heart Association’s Life’s Essential 8 guidelines to combine 8 factors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep, BMI, blood glucose, blood pressure, and blood lipids) into one score ranging from 0 to 100 (higher is better). The contributions of six household-level determinants of health: parental education, marital status, household income, household food insecurity, overcrowding, and home ownership, to differences in CVH by race/ethnicity and sex were quantified using Kitagawa-Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition tests.ResultsCVH scores were higher among NHW girls (mean [SD] 78.3 [10.6]) and boys (78.2 [10.8]) than their NHB and Hispanic/Latinx counterparts. No significant difference in CVH was observed between NHW and NHB boys. NHW girls had CVH scores 2.5-point higher than NHB girls (mean difference [MD] = 2.45; 95% CI: 0.91, 3.98). Cumulatively, all six household determinants explained 67% of girls’ CVH gap. Disparities in household income alone accounted for 41% of this difference (b = 1.00; 95% CI: 0.10, 1.90). NHW boys and girls had 1.6- and 1.5-point higher CVH scores than their Hispanic/Latinx same-sex counterparts. The combined six household determinants explained over 75% of these differences. For Hispanic/Latinx boys, differences in household food insecurity explained 36% of their mean CVH difference (b = 0.59; 95% CI: 0.12, 1.06), while differences in household income accounted for 67% of the CVH gap for Hispanic/Latinx girls (b = 1.00; 95% CI: 0.14, 1.85).ConclusionsNHB and Hispanic/Latinx youth had lower CVH scores than their NHW counterparts, with differences in household income and food security accounting for most of their CVH gap. Background

Cardiovascular health (CVH) is a construct introduced by the American Heart Association to measure, monitor, and modify cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk [1]. This construct, first introduced in 2010 as Life’s Simple 7 and redefined in 2022 as Life’s Essential 8 (LE8), combines 8 factors into a single metric ranging from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating more optimal CVH. In fact, research with the original CVH metric has shown that higher CVH scores are associated with delayed onset and duration of morbidity [2], lower healthcare costs [3], reduced risk for CVD [4], cancer [5], and dementia [6]. Unfortunately, despite the well-established benefits associated with higher levels of CVH, high CVH levels are rare. In the USA, only 0.45% of adults and 2.2% of children have CVH scores of 100 [7]. Between 2011 and 2020, non-Hispanic Black (NHB) adults consistently had the lowest LE8 scores (62 points) followed by Hispanic/Latinx adults (65.9 points) while non-Hispanic White (NHW) adults had an average LE8 score of 67.7 points [8]. These racial and ethnic disparities in CVH remained unchanged from 2011 to 2020 and likely stem from the lower CVH levels found among NHB and Hispanic/Latinx youth when compared with NHW youth [7, 9].

CVH is higher in childhood and gradually lost throughout the lifespan, with early differences in CVH often tracking from childhood into adulthood [10–12]. Living in single-parent households and households with lower parental education attainment, family income, and higher cumulative exposure to social disadvantage in early ages are associated with lower levels of adult CVH [9, 13, 14]. Less is known about how exposure to these household-level social determinants contributes to racial and ethnic disparities in CVH early in life. This study aims to quantify the specific contribution of parental education, parental marital status, household income, food insecurity, and housing characteristics to the sex-specific gap in CVH scores between NHW and NHB or Hispanic/Latinx children.

Methods

Study population

Publicly available National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data from 2013 to 2018 were used [15]. NHANES is a cross-sectional survey that utilizes a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to collect a nationally representative sample of the non-institutionalized, civilian US population. Participants (n = 6690) aged 2 to 18 years old, identified as male or female, and NHB, Hispanic/Latinx (including Mexican American and other Hispanic), or NHW with measurements of 3 or more CVH metrics were included in this study. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in Fig. 1. Briefly, we excluded participants identified as non-Hispanic Asian (n = 964) or other race (n = 770) due to insufficient sample size to estimate sex-specific differences. Youth who did not complete an examination (n = 8) and participants with missing data on the CVH metrics or social determinants (n = 1533) were also excluded. NHANES data collection obtained written informed consent and approval from the National Center for Health Statistics Institutional Review Board (IRB). Secondary analyses of deidentified data are exempt from IRB approval.

CVH health behaviors

All CVH health behaviors were self-reported. Diet quality was assessed via two 24-h diet recall interviews with either parents (children aged 2–11 years) or participants themselves (12–18 years old). Nutrient intake was extracted from the US Department of Agriculture’s Food Patterns Equivalent Database based on the reported food and beverage consumption and then scored using the 2015 Healthy Eating Index (HEI-2015) to ascertain overall diet quality [16]. HEI-2015 scores were categorized into quintiles and scored following LE8 criteria to assign more points to more ideal diets.

Nicotine exposure was assessed differently for children younger than 12 years old and older youth. For children younger than 12, NHANES only collects data on exposure to secondhand smoke in the home [17]. Participants aged 12 years and older were asked about their direct nicotine exposure (i.e., cigarette use within the past 30 days or use of e-cigarettes in the past 5 days) and indirect nicotine exposure (i.e., residing in a home with a smoker). No exposure to any source of nicotine (e.g., first or secondhand exposure) results in higher scores.

Physical activity was self-reported by children ages 12 and older. Information on physical activity of children younger than 12 years old was provided via proxies. For children younger than 16 years old, physical activity was defined by the number of days per week engaging in exercises that increased their heart rate and breathing. For older adolescents (aged 16–18 years), physical activity was defined as the number of minutes per week engaged in moderate or vigorous recreational activities (e.g., basketball). Scores ranging from 0 (no activity) to 100 points were assigned following the age-specific criteria outlined in Table 1.

Sleep health is evaluated by using average sleep duration per night. Since NHANES only measures sleep duration among youth aged 16–18 years old, higher scores were assigned based on adherence to an average of 8–10 h of sleep per night [18].

CVH clinical factors

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated via objective height and weight measurements collected during an examination at a mobile examination center. Higher scores are assigned to youth with healthy weight and lower scores to youth with overweight or obesity. Blood pressure scores are derived from the average of two of the three blood pressure measurements taken during the examination, after discarding the first measurement. If a participant only had one valid reading, this was used as their blood pressure measurement. For youth ages 8–12, blood pressure percentiles were calculated based on participants’ age, sex, and height [19]. Adult scoring was applied to participants aged ≥ 13 years. Following the LE8 criteria, blood pressure measurements over the 90th percentile or greater than 120/80 received lower scores.

Hemoglobin A1c and lipids were derived from venous blood draws. As with other CVH metrics, the presence of adverse clinical factors (i.e., high blood sugar or hypercholesterolemia) resulted in lower scores on that specific metric, which indicates worse CVH.

CVH scoring methods

Each metric is scored on a scale of 0 to 100 points following the scoring criteria introduced in Table 1 and then averaged to create a composite CVH score (also ranging from 0 to 100) [1]. Since NHANES does not collect measurements on all 8 CVH metrics in childhood, following the AHA recommendations, we calculated LE8 scores for younger children using available metrics divided by the number of metrics included [1, 20]. This method has been used in prior studies assessing CVH in youth [7, 20, 21]. We estimated the overall CVH scores using the following metrics available for each age group: [1] scores for ages 2–6 were derived from BMI, diet, nicotine exposure, and physical activity metrics; [2] for ages 6–7, scores combined BMI, diet, nicotine exposure, physical activity, and lipids measurements; [3] for ages 8–11, scores combined BMI, diet, nicotine exposure, physical activity, lipids, and blood pressure; [4] for ages 12–15, BMI, diet, physical activity, lipids, blood pressure, nicotine exposure, and glucose; [5] and for ages 16–18, all 8 metrics were collected, which included BMI, diet, physical activity, lipids, blood pressure, nicotine exposure, glucose, and sleep health.

The additional CVH health behaviors scores for ages 2–18 were estimated using 3 metrics (diet, nicotine exposure, and physical activity) for participants aged 2–15 years old, and 4 CVH behaviors (diet, nicotine exposure, physical activity, and sleep) for ages 16–18 years old. For all CVH metrics, higher scores indicate better or optimal CVH [1]. Conversely, greater adherence to a healthier lifestyle resulted in higher scores denoting better CVH.

Household-level social determinants of health (SDoH)

Six household-level SDoH were examined: parental education status, household income to poverty ratio (IPR), household food security status, parental marital status, overcrowding, and home ownership. Parental education was based on the level of education of the parent or guardian with the highest education attainment and was categorized as less than high school, high school diploma/GED degree/associate degree or some college, and college graduate or higher education. IPR is calculated by comparing annual household income to a federally defined poverty threshold determined by the number of individuals in a household. IPR was defined as low income (< 1.30), moderate income (1.30–3.50), and high income (> 3.50) [22]. Food security was assessed with 28 items in the US Food Security Survey Module, including 18 items for households with children. Participants were defined as “food secure” if they had one or fewer responses (i.e., full and marginal food security) and “food insecure” if they had 2 or more affirmative responses (i.e., low and very low food security) [23]. Parental marital status was categorized as single-parent household (divorced, separated, widowed or never married) or two-parent household (married or living with a partner). We included two housing determinants of CVD risk: overcrowding and home ownership status [24]. Overcrowding was defined as having more than two people per room. This measurement was derived from a continuous ratio capturing how many people occupied the total number of rooms in the home. Home ownership was assessed by a single question as to whether the home was rented or owned.

Statistical analysis

Three continuous NHANES survey cycles: 2013–2014, 2015–2016, and 2017–2018 were combined. Data were stratified and compared by sex and race or ethnicity using survey-adjusted chi-square and t-tests. We further stratified overall CVH and health behavior scores by age, given all CVH metrics are not measured across all ages.

Kitagawa-Blinder-Oaxaca decomposition methods (hereafter referred to as “decomposition”) were used to quantify the total explained contribution of the six household-level SDoH, and the individual contributions of each SDoH to the racial and ethnic gap in CVH scores by sex. This form of multiple linear regression and counterfactual approach can be used to quantify the contribution of factors to disparities between groups with respect to health outcomes [25–30]. In quantifying the total and individual contributions of household-level SDoH to mean differences in CVH, we can assess how much of the difference in CVH is explained and unexplained. The “explained difference” is the difference in CVH scores that would hypothetically decrease if racial and ethnic minority children had the same levels of SDoH of their NHW counterparts. The “unexplained difference” is the remaining difference in CVH scores due to unobserved factors (e.g., genetic variability [31], neighborhood conditions [32]) after the slopes calculated for each SDoH were presumed equivalent between NHW, NHB, and Hispanic/Latinx children. Supplemental Methods provide further detail of decomposition methods. For analytical purposes, NHW were selected as the “reference group” in sex-stratified comparisons based on their documented CVH and social advantage, when compared to NHB and Hispanic/Latinx children [4, 5, 9]. To assess potential bias due to missingness, data from the analytical and excluded sample were compared (Additional file 1: Table S1). Given the differences in CVH metrics available at younger ages and previously documented steep declines in adolescence [22, 33], we performed additional decomposition analyses to quantify racial and ethnic differences separately for children (ages 2–11; Additional file 1: Tables S2 and S3) and adolescents (ages 12–18; Additional file 1: Tables S4 and S5). Descriptive estimates and decomposition analyses were performed using sample weights to reflect the US population at the midpoint of the combined cycles [7]. All analyses were carried out with Stata v.17.1 (College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC).

Results

Descriptive characteristics by gender-race subgroups are presented in Table 2. Among boys, 61% were NHW, 14.7% were NHB, and 24.4% were Hispanic/Latinx. NHW boys had more favorable SDoH compared to NHB and Hispanic/Latinx boys. NHW boys were more likely to reside in homes that were food secure (84.8% vs. 67.4% and 62.2%, p < 0.0001), had high income (39.3% vs. 11.5% and 12.9%, p < 0.0001), had parents with at least a bachelor’s degree (35.2% vs. 16.9% and 11.4%), and had two parents in the household (80.4% vs. 50.1% and 75.8%, p < 0.0001). NHW boys were more likely to live in overcrowded homes but less likely to live in a rented home compared to their NHB and Hispanic/Latinx counterparts. Among girls, 59.6% were NHW, 15.2% were NHB, and 25.2% were Hispanic/Latinx. Similarly, NHW girls also had more favorable SDoH compared to their NHB and Hispanic/Latinx counterparts.

Differences in CVH scores

CVH scores, for all youth and age subsamples, are presented in Table 3. Among boys, NHW boys had a higher CVH score (M = 78.2; 95% CI: 77.4, 79.0) compared to NHB (M = 77.9; 95% CI: 76.7, 79.1) and Hispanic/Latinx boys (M = 76.6; 95% CI: 75.7, 77.5); however, these differences were only borderline statistically significant (p = 0.05). The CVH behavior scores of boys did not differ across race and ethnic groups (p = 0.36). Analyses of age-specific CVH scores showed racial and ethnic differences only among 2 to 7-year-old boys (p = 0.03). NHB boys had the highest CVH score (M = 81.9; 95% CI: 8.02, 83.5), followed by NHW boys (M = 81.2; 95% CI: 79.7, 82.8). Among 2 to 7-year-old children, Hispanic/Latinx boys had the lowest CVH score (M = 79.0; 95% CI: 77.4, 80.5).

Supplemental analyses

We further stratified decomposition analyses by age, comparing differences in overall CVH scores among children ages 2–11 years old and adolescents ages 12–18 years old. Among children ages 2–11 years old, there was no difference in CVH comparing NHB boys and girls with their NHW counterparts (Additional file 1: Table S2). When comparing NHW and Hispanic/Latinx boys ages 2–11 years old, the mean CVH difference was 2.42 points higher for NHW boys (MD = 2.42; 95% CI: 0.94, 3.89). Disparities in household SDoH explained 28% of this difference (b = 0.68; 95% CI: − 0.23, 1.60). Among girls ages 2–11 years old, NHW girls had a 1.82-point higher CVH score compared to Hispanic/Latinx girls (MD = 1.82; 95% CI: 0.30, 3.34). Disparities in household SDoH explained 90% of their CVH gap (b = 1.64; 95% CI: 0.72, 2.57), but none of the six SDoH alone made a significant contribution to the difference in CVH.

When comparing NHW and Hispanic/Latinx adolescents ages 12–18 years old, there was no difference in CVH (Additional file 1: Table S3). The mean difference in CVH between NHW and NHB boys was 1.78 (MD = 1.78; 95% CI: 0.29, 3.27) suggesting a higher CVH score for NHW boys. Disparities in SDoH accounted for the entire difference (b = 1.93; 95% CI: 0.58, 3.28). Though none of the individual SDoH significantly explained part of the observed difference in CVH, the largest contributor was household food security (b = 0.71; 95% CI: − 0.05, 1.32). NHW adolescent girls had a 4.6-point higher CVH score compared to NHB adolescent girls (MD = 4.59; 95% CI: 2.37, 6.82). Disparities in SDoH explained 44% of the observed difference (b = 2.00; 95% CI: 0.63, 3.38). Household food security explained the largest portion of the observed difference such that if NHB adolescent girls had the same level of food security as NHW adolescent girls, their CVH score would increase by 0.7 points (b = 0.67; 95% CI: 0.04, 1.30). The unexplained difference accounted for 56% of the observed difference in adolescent CVH comparing NHB and NHW girls (b = 2.59; 95% CI: 0.33, 4.85) but none of household-level SDoH examined made a significant contribution.

There were no observed racial and ethnic differences in the CVH behaviors score by sex among youth ages 2–11 years old (Additional file 1: Table S4). Among adolescents (Additional file 1: Table S5), NHW girls had a higher behavioral score (MD = 3.69; 95% CI: 0.56, 6.82) than their NHB adolescent peers. No individual SDoH significantly explained part of this difference.

Discussion

In this nationally representative study of US NHW, NHB, and Hispanic/Latinx youth aged 2–18 years old, NHB and Hispanic/Latinx youth had lower CVH scores than their NHW peers and were more likely to live in households with greater social disadvantages. Household disparities in income and food security largely accounted for the lower CVH scores of NHB and Hispanic/Latinx boys and girls when compared with their NHW counterparts. Given that CVH scores in childhood are strong predictors of CVH in adulthood [14, 34], future CVD and overall health risks [10], findings indicate racial and ethnic disparities in CVD and overall health outcomes are one more negative consequence of childhood poverty.

In a previous study among 30,239 adults from the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) cohort, the cumulative effect of three SDoH—education, income, and insurance status—explained 21% to 38% of the higher CVD mortality among NHB adults when compared with NHW adults [35]. Others also found that disparities in the SDoH, particularly in income and education, explain from 27 to 100% of the differences in CVD risk [30, 36, 37]. Among children, a previous study showed that childhood socioeconomic disadvantage was strongly associated with CVH in adulthood, and this association was independent of childhood CVH status [38]. Our decomposition analyses advance this literature by identifying two household-level SDoH with a significant contribution to racial and ethnic disparities in children’s CVH: household income and food insecurity. Disparities in income and food insecurity are detrimental to adult CVH and CVD outcomes. Our findings suggest disparities in both SDoH also have intergenerational harmful effects on the CVH of the offspring of NHB and Hispanic/Latinx parents.

CVH scores of NHB and Hispanic/Latinx were about 0.5 to 2.5 points lower than their NHW counterparts. A previous study found that a 10-point decrease in CVH score is associated with an approximate 2-year reduction in life expectancy [2]. Thus, our findings are suggestive of a 6-month decline in life expectancy with a 2-point lower CVH score. Though a 2-point difference on a scale of 100 appears small, similar differences in CVH attributable to social and psychosocial determinants have been documented among adults in NHANES [37]. These differences are expected to persist and widen with advancing age.

In childhood, the LE8 scores provide a summary of cardiometabolic and lifestyle factors that complement other measurements of physical health [39] allowing us to identify differences before clinically meaningful thresholds have been reached. Lower levels of CVH during adolescence and young adulthood are powerful predictors of premature incidence of hypertension, metabolic syndrome, hyperlipidemia, and sub-clinical measurements of CVD as well as clinical CVD and CVD mortality [7, 40]. For example, children with the lowest CVH levels by age 8 are more likely to follow a declining CVH trajectory and by age 55, have greater buildup of plaque in the carotid artery wall that precedes calcification and clinical CVH. This buildup is reflected in higher cIMT measurements among children with lower CVH when compared with their peers who preserved higher CVH levels [10]. Together, these findings demonstrate primordial prevention in childhood—before the effects of social disadvantages have fully manifested—is paramount in reducing racial and ethnic CVD disparities. Previous studies suggest that higher CVH can be preserved when interventions are implemented in early childhood [41] but improvements are modest in adolescence [42].

Our findings emphasize the critical role of disparities in food security and income in driving racial and ethnic disparities in CVH, from early childhood ages. There are several ways through which household income and food security can influence youth CVH behaviors. It has been shown that when financial resources are limited, competing basic needs lead individuals to forgo medications or preventive medical care in order to afford food. Financial constraints also trigger maladaptive eating behaviors, including diet quality declines due to increased intake of more affordable ultra-processed, high-calorie, and nutrient-poor foods [43]. At lower income levels, the limited safe and affordable opportunities for physical activity help explain the lower physical activity levels [44], worse sleep outcomes [45, 46], and higher frequency of sedentary behaviors [47, 48]. In contrast, policies that reduce income inequality and mitigate the adverse effects of food insecurity, such as participation Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), reduce the impact that food insecurity has in childhood CVH [49], limiting future racial and ethnic disparities in CVD. Beyond SNAP, opportunities for relocation from high-poverty or high racial residential segregation to low-poverty and low-segregation neighborhoods are associated with long-term improvements on economic and educational attainment, and improvements in blood pressure and reductions in the prevalence of CVD risk factors [50, 51].

Housing conditions, particularly home ownership status, were associated with racial and ethnic disparities in NHW and NHB boys CVH. Inadequate housing and instability have recently been identified as contributors to poor adult CVH [24, 52]. In a recent systematic review, greater housing instability was associated with higher prevalence of overweight or obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease among US adults [53]. Our study advances the literature by evidencing that NHB boys are more likely to reside in rented residences which contribute to a significant portion of their disparity in CVH relative to their NHW peers.

We observed the proportion of CVH differences explained by differences in income differed by race/ethnicity and sex. Studies of the diminishing returns hypothesis have demonstrated NHB and Hispanic/Latinx individuals achieve less health benefits with higher socioeconomic achievement than their NHW counterparts, likely due to societal and structural barriers [54, 55]. Modest reductions have been documented on the prevalence of obesity [55, 56], hypertension [57], and e-cigarette smoking [58] that are achieved with higher socioeconomic status and education attainment among historically marginalized groups when compared with NHW. Our study advances the literature on the diminishing returns hypothesis by introducing evidence of another dimension of disadvantage when sex interacts with race or ethnicity and documenting diminishing returns from childhood ages. Specifically, we noticed differences in income explained a large proportion of the difference in CVH among NHB and Hispanic/Latinx girls, but not among NHB or Hispanic/Latinx boys. Notably, both food insecurity and income are interconnected yet distinct dimensions of hardship, each with distinct pathways through which childhood adversity influences CVH. Food insecurity more directly constrains nutritional adequacy and diet quality [25, 43], whereas insufficient income can affect multiple behavioral and clinical risk factors simultaneously [22, 44, 47]. Previous studies suggest that when financial resources are scarce, minority parents often promote traditional gender roles and place a higher burden in the distribution of childcare and domestic responsibilities on their daughters, which leaves them with less freedom to practice physical activities than their sons [59]. However, additional research is needed to evaluate sex differences and sex-specific pathways through which food insecurity and income shape CVH trajectories from early childhood ages.

We focused on quantifying the contributions of SDoH to CVH differences between NHW children and their NHB and Hispanic/Latinx counterparts. We did not analyze differences between NHB and Hispanic/Latinx children. Decomposition analysis is a counterfactual approach used to analyze how much of an existing disparity would be reduced if individuals were exposed to the same favorable SDoH as the group with the most favorable conditions, in this case NHW children. Both NHB and Hispanic children have lower CVH scores and more adverse SDoH (e.g., lower parental education attainment, lower household income) than their NHW counterparts. Because neither group represents a reference category with consistently favorable conditions (as compared to NHW children), using KBO decomposition between these two groups would not yield interpretable or policy-relevant findings. In contrast, such comparisons could be misinterpreted and risk stigmatizing communities or reinforce deficit-based narratives, rather than advancing our understanding of the health consequences associated with structural inequities affecting NHB and Hispanic children.

Limitations

Findings should be interpreted in the context of study limitations. Findings from previous studies have shown that unmeasured factors like the acculturation gap [60], discrimination [61], systemic racism [61], and other household-level and individual SDoH are associated with differences in CVH factors among racially and ethnic diverse youth. We recognize that some of these unmeasured factors, such as systemic racism, also influence opportunities to receive equitable nutrition, education, and income. Unfortunately, these upstream determinants of health were not explicitly measured in NHANES which precluded us from examining the specific contribution of these determinants to the racial and ethnic differences in CVH among US youth.

Cross-sectional and observational data are subject to unmeasured confounding. While we focused on six established household-level SDoH, other SDoH at the individual (e.g., depression) and neighborhood levels (e.g., food deserts) have been associated with racial and ethnic disparities in CVH metrics. These data were collected for children or were outside of the scope of this study. Still, the fact that six household-level factors statistically explained such a large proportion of the CVH disparities in childhood and adolescence highlights the increasing importance that childhood home environments have as determinants of CVH and future CVD risks. It is important to recognize that our methods produce findings that apply to mean differences in CVH at the population level, whereas contributors to individual-level CVH and variability in CVH (distributions) may vary substantially within any racial and ethnic group. We did not have complete data on LE8 for all ages, as not all CVH clinical factors are measured in children younger than age 16. However, available data suggest that there is wider variety and earlier decline in behavioral CVH metrics (e.g., even at ages 12–19 few adolescents have lost ideal BP, but most do not have ideal diet) [62]. Worsening behavioral CVH metrics are key mechanisms through which clinical CVH metrics later decline. These observations make the behavioral CVH metrics particularly relevant in childhood samples.

We could not examine CVH differences for groups other than NHW, NHB, and Hispanic/Latinx children due to small sample sizes of other racial and ethnic groups. Our analyses combine data from 2013 to 2018. While the NHANES 2019–2020 data were available at the time of our study, this latest cycle was not included because it follows a different study design to address the data collection suspension during the COVID-19 pandemic. After newer NHANES data is released, future studies should compare the distribution of the SDoH pre- and post-pandemic to elucidate the COVID-19’s social and economic impact on the CVH disparities of NHB and Hispanic/Latinx youth [63]. We acknowledge that youth excluded due to missingness were more likely to be NHB and Hispanic and have more adverse household SDoH (e.g., low-income household, lower parental educational attainment, less likely to be homeowners) than children in our analytical sample. Nonetheless, given that household income was a significant contributor to observed differences in CVH, it is likely that we underestimated the explained contributions of household SDoH to the difference in CVH. Lastly, we did not have information on the specific heritage of all participants; consequently, our findings do not account for the diversity among Hispanic/Latinx subgroups.

Conclusions

In this national representative sample of US children and adolescents aged 2–18 years old, disparities in two household-level SDoH—household income and food insecurity—accounted for the largest proportion of the gap in CVH scores between NHW children and their NHB or Hispanic/Latinx same sex and age counterparts. These findings show that financial constraints are not only detrimental to adult CVH but also contribute to the disproportionally higher risk for CVD of families in disadvantaged households, from childhood ages.
