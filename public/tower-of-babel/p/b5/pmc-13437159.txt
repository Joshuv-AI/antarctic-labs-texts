Comparison of Clinical Outcomes According to Preoperative Skin Preparation in Hip Arthroplasty for Hip Fracture: A 3-Arm Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Chanhee Park, Seung-Cheol Choi, Seong Gyu Kim, Myung-Rae Cho, Suk-Kyoon Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with hip fractures undergoing arthroplasty are typically old and frail, and superficial surgical site infections (SSIs) can delay mobilization and prolong hospitalization. Preoperative skin antisepsis is a low-cost, universally used intervention. However, clinical practice varies, and evidence from elective arthroplasty may not fully generalize to urgent hip fracture surgery. Sequential dual-antiseptic protocols combining povidone-iodine (PVI) and chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) are increasingly adopted; however, the optimal order for “scrub” versus “paint” remains unclear. We compared the prophylactic efficacy of 3 skin preparation protocols using different sequences of PVI and CHG.MethodsIn this single-center, prospective, randomized controlled trial, 108 patients undergoing hip arthroplasty for hip fracture were randomly allocated to 3 groups based on the antiseptic agents used for the mechanical “scrub” and final “paint” steps: group 1 (PVI scrub/PVI paint), group 2 (PVI scrub/CHG paint), and group 3 (CHG scrub/PVI paint). The primary outcome was the rate of positive skin culture results immediately before draping and pathogen identification results. The secondary outcome was the incidence of superficial SSI.ResultsPost-preparation culture positivity was significantly lower in the CHG-containing groups (group 2 and group 3: 5.6% each) than in the PVI-only group (group 1: 27.8%; p = 0.005). The incidence of superficial SSI differed significantly among the 3 protocols (p = 0.015). The highest superficial SSI rate was observed in group 1 (30.6%), followed by group 2 (13.9%). Group 3 had the lowest superficial SSI rate (5.6%). In multivariable logistic regression adjusted for confounders, group 1 was associated with a significantly higher risk of SSI than group 3 (adjusted odds ratio, 3.89; 95% CI, 1.22–12.37; p = 0.021).ConclusionsThe sequence of antiseptic application significantly influenced microbiological outcomes, with CHG-containing protocols demonstrating lower post-preparation culture positivity. A protocol using a CHG scrub followed by PVI paint provided the most effective bacterial suppression. Differences in superficial SSI rates were also observed and may reflect the impact of improved microbiological control. Optimizing the order of antiseptic administration may contribute to improved infection prevention in this high-risk population.

Hip fractures are among the most consequential injuries in older adults, often leading to a sudden loss of independent ambulation and a cascade of medical complications. Recent large-scale studies have further demonstrated that postoperative complications and resulting burden remain highly prevalent in this population.1234) Hip fracture treatment is broadly categorized into open reduction, internal fixation, and hip arthroplasty. Recently, a growing trend toward hip arthroplasty has been observed owing to the high prevalence of patients with poor bone quality.2) Although the clinical outcomes of hip arthroplasty in these patients are generally excellent, the clinical impact of infection can be amplified, leading to a profound negative effect on prognosis. Specifically, postoperative site infections delay rehabilitation and discharge planning, increase antibiotic exposure, and contribute to higher readmission and reoperation rates. These adverse outcomes are particularly devastating for frail patients who possess limited physiologic reserve.3456)

Postoperative infection in orthopedic trauma is a multifactorial event influenced by host vulnerability (age, comorbidity burden, malnutrition, and immunosuppression), procedure-related factors (operative duration, implant use, and soft-tissue handling), and perioperative care processes (antibiotic prophylaxis, operating room environment, and skin antisepsis).7) Unlike elective arthroplasty populations, hip fracture cohorts often include patients with acute physiological stress, sarcopenia, anemia, and compromised skin integrity and may experience prolonged preoperative bed rest and catheter-related exposures.2) These features can increase susceptibility to both surgical and non-surgical site infections (SSIs), making infection prevention strategies highly relevant to outcomes and resource use in orthopedic trauma units.345)

Among modifiable perioperative measures, preoperative skin preparation is a foundational, universally applied step intended to reduce the microbial burden at the incision site.89) International guidelines for SSI prevention recognize alcohol-based preparations and commonly discuss chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG)- and iodophor-based solutions as major options.89) However, real-world implementation varies widely across institutions and is driven by tradition, availability, perceived safety, and discipline-specific preferences. Because the effectiveness of antiseptics depends not only on the active ingredient but also on factors such as formulation, contact time, drying, and application technique, the comparative effectiveness of skin preparation protocols remains an area of active investigation, particularly in high-risk populations. Recent network meta-analyses and contemporary orthopedic studies have further refined our understanding of these antiseptic strategies.101112)

CHG and povidone-iodine (PVI) have complementary microbiologic profiles, and recent evidence suggests that alcohol-based CHG preparations may offer advantages in reducing microbial burden and SSI.101113) CHG is known to bind to the stratum corneum and can provide residual antimicrobial activity that suppresses recolonization over time, whereas PVI provides rapid, broadspectrum microbicidal activity but typically exhibits a less persistent residual effect, as iodine is consumed during its reaction.13) This distinction becomes particularly important when skin preparation is conceived not as a single action but as a 2-step process that includes (1) a mechanical scrub step intended to remove debris and reduce resident flora (“remove + reduce”) and (2) a final paint step that leaves the chemical antiseptic film present at the time of draping and incision (“coat + keep killing”).

Sequential dual-antiseptic protocols are increasingly being used; however, the optimal sequence, specifically, which agent should be used for the scrub step versus the final paint, remains uncertain.14) Currently, no consensus exists regarding the optimal antiseptic administration method for infection prevention. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial aimed to compare the prophylactic efficacy of 3 preoperative skin preparation protocols in patients undergoing hip arthroplasty for hip fractures. We hypothesized that the sequence of antiseptic application would influence microbiological outcomes, and that protocols incorporating CHG would demonstrate lower post-preparation culture positivity. The primary outcome was the rate of post-preparation skin culture positivity, and the secondary clinical outcome was the incidence of superficial SSI.

METHODS

Study Design, Study Period, and Ethics

This was a single-center, prospective, parallel-group, randomized controlled trial. The trial was conducted between July 31, 2020, and December 31, 2022, at a tertiary academic hospital. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Daegu Catholic University Medical Center (IRB No. CR-20-148). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment.

Participants

We screened consecutive adult patients scheduled for primary hip arthroplasty for hip fractures. Patients were eligible if they were diagnosed with femoral neck or intertrochanteric fractures requiring arthroplasty. Only 3 patients with intertrochanteric fractures were included in the arthroplasty group. These cases were selectively treated with arthroplasty due to a high risk of internal fixation failure. Two cases demonstrated severe comminution involving both the posteromedial and anterior cortices, and 1 case involved a combined femoral neck and intertrochanteric fracture pattern. In these situations, internal fixation was considered to carry a high risk of failure. The exclusion criteria were open fractures or active infections at or near the surgical site, such as ulcers or pressure sores. Patients with a history of surgery on the ipsilateral hip, known hypersensitivity to iodophors or CHG, or systemic signs of acute or chronic infection were also excluded. Patients who were unable to provide informed consent were excluded from the study.

A total of 139 patients were assessed for eligibility, of whom 31 were excluded according to the predefined criteria. Finally, 108 patients (46 males and 62 females) were included and randomly allocated into 3 groups (36 patients per group). The patient selection and allocation process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Randomization and Blinding

The participants were randomly assigned in a 1 : 1 : 1 ratio to 1 of the 3 skin preparation protocols 91skin preparation procedure. Owing to the distinct visual properties of antiseptic solutions, such as color and texture, blinding of the operating surgeon (MRC) and scrub team was not feasible. However, to minimize bias, outcome assessors who evaluated postoperative SSIs and microbiologists analyzing culture samples were blinded to the group allocation.

Preoperative Standardization

To standardize baseline skin conditions, all patients were instructed to shower with soap and water either the night before or on the morning of surgery. Hair removal was performed only when necessary and was carried out using single-use electric clippers immediately prior to surgery to minimize skin trauma, in accordance with current guideline recommendations.8) Gross contamination and oils were removed from the skin surface using sterile sponges before antiseptic application. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered in accordance with institutional guidelines and international recommendations, and 2 g of cefazolin was administered intravenously 30–60 minutes before skin incision. Vancomycin (1 g) was used as an alternative in patients with a history of beta-lactam allergy.815) In our institution, prophylactic antibiotics are routinely continued for up to 48 hours following surgery. The duration of antibiotic administration may be further extended at the discretion of the treating surgeon in cases of local wound complications or clinical suspicion of infection. Antibiotic administration was not modified based on post-preparation skin culture results. In cases of superficial SSI, antibiotics were continued until clinical improvement of the wound was observed. No routine changes in antibiotic regimen were performed.

Intervention: Skin Preparation Protocols

The 3 study groups differed in the sequence and choice of antiseptic agents used during the mechanical “scrub” step and the final “paint” step. The agents used were a 10% PVI aqueous solution (Besetine Solution, Hyundai Pharm) and 2% CHG in 72% ethanol (Hexitanol ET 2% Solution, Firson). The preparation procedure consisted of 2 distinct stages. The first step was the scrub step, in which the assigned antiseptic agent was applied using sterile gauze with friction in a concentric circular motion, moving outward from the incision site for approximately 5 minutes. This step aimed to mechanically remove debris and reduce the resident flora, after which the area was allowed to air-dry completely. The second step was the paint step, which was performed after drying, during which the second assigned agent was applied as a final film using sterile gauze for 5 minutes without friction and allowed to dry before draping.

The participants were allocated to 1 of 3 specific protocol groups. Group 1 (PVI/PVI) received a scrub with 10% PVI, followed by a paint step using the same agent. Group 2 (PVI/CHG) was scrubbed with 10% PVI, and the paint step was performed using 2% CHG in 72% ethanol. Conversely, group 3 (CHG/PVI) received a scrub with 2% CHG in 72% ethanol, followed by a paint step with 10% PVI.

Surgical Procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by a single experienced surgical team using the modified Hardinge approach (direct lateral approach) on the hip.16) Standard aseptic techniques and operative protocols were maintained across all groups to prevent confounding variables related to the surgical technique.

Microbiological Sampling and Laboratory Procedures

To objectively evaluate the bactericidal efficacy of each protocol, quantitative skin cultures were obtained at 2 distinct time points: first, immediately after positioning the patient but before any antiseptic application (pre-preparation); and second, after the completion of the paint step and full drying, immediately before surgical draping (post-preparation). Samples were collected from the skin over the greater trochanter using sterile swabs.

Swabs were immediately placed in transport media and delivered to the microbiology laboratory within 30 min. Specimens were inoculated onto sheep blood agar plates for the isolation of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, yeast, and Candida, and onto MacConkey agar plates for the selective isolation of gram-negative bacilli such as Pseudomonas and Enterobacteriaceae. Plates were incubated at 37 ℃ for 5 days, and bacterial growth was identified to the species level using standard biochemical methods.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the rate of positive skin cultures after completion of skin preparation, along with the identification of isolated microorganisms. The secondary clinical outcome was the incidence of postoperative superficial SSI during the index hospital admission. In our institution, patients undergoing hip arthroplasty are managed under a standardized clinical pathway that includes hospitalization until suture removal at 2 weeks postoperatively. Accordingly, all patients were followed with daily wound assessments during this period.

Definition of Superficial SSI and Data Collection

Superficial SSI was defined according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention National Healthcare Safety Network (CDC/NHSN) criteria,8) as an infection involving only the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the incision that met at least 1 of the following conditions: purulent drainage from the superficial incision; organisms identified from an aseptically obtained culture of fluid or tissue from the superficial incision; or a superficial incision that was deliberately opened by a surgeon or spontaneously dehisced in the presence of localized signs of infection, including pain, tenderness, swelling, erythema, or heat; or diagnosis of a superficial SSI by a physician. Diagnoses made by the attending orthopedic surgeon or treating physician were reviewed and adjudicated using these criteria. Postoperative wound assessments were performed daily during the index hospitalization by the attending orthopedic team, consisting of 2 clinicians. Evaluation was primarily based on clinical examination, including inspection for erythema, warmth, swelling, wound dehiscence, and discharge. No formal scoring system was used; however, suspected cases of superficial SSI were reviewed according to CDC/NHSN criteria.

Baseline demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), history of smoking and alcohol consumption, and nutritional status, assessed using the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) score.

Statistical Analysis

Sample size calculation was performed a priori based on the primary outcome of post-preparation skin culture positivity. The estimation was guided by previous studies evaluating surgical skin antisepsis.1417) For the overall comparison across the 3 groups, a moderate effect size (Cohen’s w ≈ 0.29) was considered. With a 2-sided alpha level of 0.05 and 80% power, the required total sample size was estimated to be approximately 108 patients, corresponding to 36 patients per group.

Statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistics software version 19.0 (IBM Corp.). Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, depending on the data distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables and 1-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at a 2-sided p-value < 0.05.

RESULTS

Study Population and Baseline Characteristics

A total of 108 patients underwent randomization and were included in the final analysis, with 36 patients allocated to each of the 3 study groups. The mean age of the study population was 68.7 ± 16.0 years, and 42.6% were male. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including BMI, CCI, history of smoking and alcohol consumption, and nutritional status assessed using the NRS-2002 score, were well balanced across the 3 groups, with no statistically significant differences observed (Table 1). Major comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, rheumatoid arthritis, and corticosteroid use were also comparably distributed among the groups.

Microbiological Outcomes of Skin Preparation

Baseline culture positivity before skin preparation

Before the application of antiseptic agents, the overall skin culture positivity rate was 61.1% (66 / 108). No significant differences were observed in baseline culture positivity among the 3 groups (group 1, 61.1%; group 2, 66.7%; and group 3, 55.6%; p = 0.627), indicating a comparable baseline skin bioburden across the study arms (Table 2).

Post-preparation culture positivity by antiseptic protocol

Following the completion of the assigned skin preparation protocols, the overall culture positivity rate decreased markedly to 13.0% (14 / 108). Post-preparation culture positivity differed significantly among the groups (p = 0.005). Group 1 (PVI scrub and paint) showed the highest residual positivity rate at 27.8% (10 / 36). Conversely, both CHG-containing protocols showed substantially lower residual culture positivity, with rates of 5.6% (2 / 36) in group 2 (PVI scrub followed by CHG paint) and 5.6% (2 / 36) in group 3 (CHG scrub followed by PVI paint) (Table 2, Fig. 2).

Distribution of identified microorganisms

Before skin antisepsis, coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) were the most frequently isolated organisms, accounting for the majority of positive cultures (55 isolates), followed by Bacillus and Corynebacterium. After skin preparation, the total number of CoNS isolates decreased substantially, from 55 to 12. Notably, group 3 demonstrated the most pronounced reduction in resident flora, with only 1 CoNS isolate detected after preparation, compared to 9 isolates in group 1. These findings suggest a superior suppression of resident skin microorganisms when CHG was used during the scrub phase (Tables 3 and 4).

Clinical Outcomes

Superficial SSI

The incidence of postoperative superficial SSI significantly differed among the 3 skin preparation protocols (p = 0.015). Group 1 exhibited the highest superficial SSI rate at 30.6% (11 / 36), followed by group 2 at 13.9% (5 / 36). Group 3 had the lowest superficial SSI rate at 5.6% (2 / 36) (Table 5, Fig. 3). No cases of deep or organ/space SSI requiring surgical or interventional management were observed in any group during the index admission.

Other postoperative clinical outcomes

The other postoperative clinical outcomes did not differ significantly among the 3 groups. The mean length of hospital stay was 20.9 ± 14.2 days overall, with no significant intergroup difference (p = 0.237). Similarly, the duration of antibiotic use was comparable among the groups (overall mean, 5.2 ± 6.8 days; p = 0.325) (Table 5).

Multivariable Analysis of Risk Factors for Superficial SSI

In logistic regression analyses evaluating the risk factors for postoperative superficial SSI, group 3 served as the reference category. In the unadjusted analysis, patients in group 1 had a significantly higher risk of superficial SSI compared with those in group 3 (odds ratio [OR], 4.00; 95% CI, 1.34–11.96; p = 0.013). After adjustment for potential confounders, including age, sex, BMI, diabetes mellitus, and CCI, group 1 remained independently associated with an increased risk of superficial SSI (adjusted OR, 3.89; 95% CI, 1.22–12.37; p = 0.021). Group 2 showed a trend toward a higher infection risk than group 3 (adjusted OR, 2.37), although this association was not statistically significant (p = 0.152) (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

In this randomized controlled trial evaluating preoperative skin preparation protocols for hip arthroplasty in patients with hip fractures, we found that the sequence of antiseptic application significantly influenced both microbiological and clinical outcomes. The protocol using 2% CHG in alcohol for the mechanical scrub step, followed by 10% PVI for the final paint step (group 3) resulted in the lowest rate of SSI complications and the lowest rate of post-preparation culture positivity. These findings suggest that optimizing the distinct roles of “scrubbing” and “painting” with pharmacologically complementary agents may provide superior protection, consistent with recent systematic reviews and network meta-analyses evaluating antiseptic efficacy.101112)

These findings are clinically relevant, particularly in populations undergoing arthroplasty for hip fracture, which differs substantially from elective arthroplasty cohorts. Patients with hip fractures are typically older and frailer, often presenting with limited preinjury mobility, prolonged preoperative bed rest, urinary catheterization, incontinence, and compromised skin hygiene. Contemporary studies have demonstrated that postoperative complications remain highly prevalent and significantly affect outcomes in this population.34) These factors are known to increase baseline skin bioburden and vulnerability to periincisional contamination, thereby amplifying the clinical impact of even minor deficiencies in perioperative infection prevention measures.25) The relatively high superficial SSI rate observed in our study may reflect the frail characteristics of hip fracture patients and the active in-hospital surveillance performed during the index admission. Despite the relatively high incidence of superficial SSI, no cases of deep infection were observed in our cohort. This may be explained by several factors. First, patients were closely monitored during a standardized inpatient clinical pathway for approximately 2 weeks, allowing for early detection and timely management of superficial wound complications. Second, most superficial SSIs were mild and limited to localized soft-tissue involvement without evidence of deep extension. Third, antibiotic therapy was continued until clinical improvement was achieved in affected cases, which may have prevented progression. However, given that follow-up was limited to the index hospitalization, delayed deep infections occurring after discharge may not have been captured.

The duration of antibiotic use in this study exceeded current guideline recommendations, which typically suggest discontinuation within 24 hours after surgery. At the time of this study, our institutional protocol included routine administration of prophylactic antibiotics for up to 48 hours following hip arthroplasty. In addition, antibiotic use was occasionally extended based on clinical judgment in the presence of wound-related concerns. Notably, our current institutional practice has since been updated in accordance with guideline recommendations, and prophylactic antibiotics are now routinely limited to within 24 hours postoperatively. Given these considerations, refinement of universally applied, low-cost interventions such as skin antisepsis may have disproportionate benefits for postoperative outcomes.

The observed superiority of the group 3 protocol can be explained by fundamental differences in the mechanisms of action of CHG and iodophors. CHG is a cationic bisbiguanide that binds to the negatively charged stratum corneum and bacterial cell walls, providing a persistent antimicrobial effect known as substantivity.111819) When CHG is used during the mechanical scrub step, the agent is frictionally worked into skin irregularities and follicular openings, potentially suppressing the regrowth of resident flora during the operation. Conversely, PVI functions as a rapid, broad-spectrum microbicide that kills organisms via the oxidation of cellular components but lacks the same degree of residual activity because the active iodine is consumed upon contact with organic matter.1018) The application of PVI as the final paint layer ensures a potent, immediate bactericidal film at the exact moment of incision. Therefore, the sequence of “long-acting scrub” (CHG) followed by “immediate-kill paint” (PVI) theoretically maximizes the strengths of both agents.

Our microbiological analysis supports this mechanistic hypothesis. Although the sample size was relatively limited, there was a statistically significant difference in post-preparation culture positivity, suggesting a potential microbiological advantage of the intervention. Notably, we observed a substantial reduction in CoNS in the post-preparation cultures. CoNS are the most common commensal flora of the skin but are also a leading cause of periprosthetic joint infection and are notorious for their ability to form biofilms on implanted foreign materials.2021) The reduction of these specific organisms on the skin surface immediately before incision is clinically relevant because direct inoculation during surgery is the primary pathway for deep prosthetic infection.20) Although post-preparation culture positivity was identical between groups 2 and 3, the difference in superficial SSI rates may be explained by differences in residual antimicrobial activity related to the method of antiseptic application. CHG applied during the friction-based scrub phase may provide more sustained suppression of skin flora through penetration into follicles and skin crevices, whereas application during the paint phase without friction may result in less durable antimicrobial effects.

These results align with and expand upon existing high-impact literature, including recent network meta-analyses and contemporary orthopedic studies supporting the effectiveness of CHG-based antisepsis.11122223) The superiority of CHG alcohol-based preparations over PVI preparations has been demonstrated in landmark trials in general surgery and cesarean section.2223) However, our study adds a nuanced perspective specific to orthopedic trauma: it is not merely the presence of CHG that matters but its placement in the preparation sequence. While Darouiche et al.22) established CHG alcohol as a standard, our findings suggest that in dual-agent protocols, the order of application is a critical variable that warrants standardization.

Conceptually, preoperative skin preparation may be viewed as a 2-step process: (1) mechanical reduction of the resident flora and (2) maintenance of antimicrobial activity at the time of incision. Aligning antiseptic selection with these functional roles provides a practical and biologically plausible framework for optimizing perioperative infection prevention strategies, including evidence-based skin antisepsis protocols. This approach remains essential in contemporary hip fracture surgery.34810) In addition, perioperative strategies such as early mobilization may further contribute to improved postoperative outcomes in this vulnerable population.24)

This study has some limitations. First, it was a single-center trial with a relatively small sample size of 108 patients, which limited statistical power to detect differences in rare outcomes, such as periprosthetic joint infection. Consequently, we used a composite endpoint for SSI complications. Second, blinding the surgeons was not feasible because of the distinct visual differences between the antiseptic solutions, although the outcome assessors were blinded. Third, skin cultures serve as surrogate markers and may not perfectly correlate with deep-tissue inoculation. Fourth, although patients were closely monitored during a standardized inpatient clinical pathway for approximately 2 weeks, the follow-up period was limited to the index hospitalization. Therefore, delayed or deep infections, including periprosthetic joint infections, occurring after discharge may not have been captured. Future studies with extended follow-up are warranted to better evaluate long-term infection outcomes. Despite these limitations, demographic and clinical homogeneity across the groups strengthens the validity of the comparison.

In conclusion, this trial demonstrates that the sequence of antiseptic application significantly influences microbiological outcomes in hip-fracture arthroplasty. A sequential protocol using CHG for the mechanical scrub followed by PVI for the final paint achieved the lowest post-preparation culture positivity. Although differences in superficial SSI rates were observed, these findings should be interpreted as secondary clinical outcomes that may be influenced by microbiological efficacy. Optimizing antiseptic sequencing represents a rational and potentially impactful strategy for infection prevention in this high-risk population.
