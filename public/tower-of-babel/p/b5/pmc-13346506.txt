Sequential co-extraction of gut microbial DNA and fecal polyamines enables integrated microbiome–metabolite profiling in an Alzheimer’s disease mouse model
Authors: Shion Akagi, Yuma Sato, Taiichi Sakamoto, Mayumi Minamisawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early, non-invasive biomarkers for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) are urgently needed. Impaired polyamine metabolism, regulated by intracellular pathways and the gut microbiota, has been reported in postmortem brains of AD patients. Here, we developed a sequential co-extraction workflow (Method 10) that enables recovery of gut bacterial DNA and fecal free polyamines—putrescine, spermidine, and spermine—from the same small mouse fecal sample, allowing paired microbiome and metabolite profiling. Applying this workflow to an AD knock-in mouse model and age-matched controls at 8, 32, and 56 weeks revealed an early decrease in Lactobacillus abundance at 8 weeks accompanied by elevated spermidine levels (p < 0.05), while total fecal polyamine concentrations increased further in AD mice at 56 weeks. These findings suggest that integrated fecal microbiome–polyamine profiling may provide exploratory microbiota–polyamine signatures associated with AD progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54312-7.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) and tau pathology, leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss1. With more than 55 million affected individuals worldwide, AD represents a major global health challenge2,3. Although disease-modifying therapies targeting Aβ have recently been introduced for early-stage patients4, these interventions do not reverse established neuronal damage. Therefore, identifying early pathological changes and developing minimally invasive markers for disease progression remain critical objectives5.

Polyamines, including putrescine (Put), spermidine (Spd), and spermine (Spm), are small polycationic molecules derived from L-arginine metabolism (Fig. 1). They are essential for cell proliferation, protein synthesis, mitochondrial function, and cellular homeostasis6,7. Polyamine homeostasis is tightly regulated through intracellular synthesis, recycling, degradation, and uptake of exogenous sources. Several studies have reported abnormalities in polyamine metabolism in postmortem brain tissue from patients with AD8–10. In addition, previous work using AD model mice demonstrated dysregulation of peripheral urea cycle pathways and downstream polyamine metabolism11. However, the systemic dynamics of polyamine metabolism during disease progression remain incompletely understood. The gut microbiota constitutes an important exogenous source of polyamines. Intestinal bacteria synthesize polyamines from dietary substrates and host-derived metabolites, and these microbial products contribute to host metabolic homeostasis12,13. Age-related decline in endogenous polyamine synthesis may increase dependence on microbiota-derived polyamines. Recent studies suggest that alterations in gut microbiota composition are associated with AD pathology14,15, but the relationship between microbiota composition and fecal polyamine levels during disease progression has not been systematically evaluated. A schematic overview of microbiota–host polyamine metabolism is shown in Fig. 1. Polyamines produced by gut bacteria can be absorbed by the host and further metabolized, while excess polyamines are excreted in feces. This bidirectional interaction suggests that fecal polyamines may reflect both microbial and host metabolic states.

Analysis of gut microbiota typically relies on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing, which enables taxonomic profiling of bacterial communities in fecal samples16–18. In parallel, polyamine quantification in biological matrices requires appropriate extraction and derivatization procedures for reliable chromatographic analysis19–21. However, most studies assess microbiota composition and metabolite levels using separate samples, limiting the ability to directly integrate microbial and metabolic information from the same biological material.

In this study, we established a sequential co-extraction workflow that enables recovery of gut bacterial DNA and fecal polyamines from the same small mouse fecal sample. Using an AD knock-in mouse model carrying Swedish, Arctic, and Iberian mutations (AC)22 and age-matched control mice carrying only the Swedish mutation (WC), we combined 16 S rRNA sequencing with quantitative analysis of Put, Spd, and Spm to evaluate time-dependent changes at 8, 32, and 56 weeks of age. This integrated approach allowed us to examine coordinated alterations in gut microbiota composition and polyamine metabolism during AD progression.

Results

Optimization of gut bacterial DNA extraction from mouse feces

DNA extraction protocols suitable for integrated microbiome analyses were compared across seven extraction conditions (Methods A–G; Table S1) using fecal samples from AD model mice (AC). DNA yield per milligram of feces served as the primary evaluation criterion, complemented by taxonomic classification performance from 16 S rRNA gene sequencing.

Method G (QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit with PowerBead Pro Tube) produced the highest DNA yield (166.5 ± 1.7 ng mg−1; Fig. 2a). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of extraction method on DNA yield (p < 0.001). Post hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that Method G yielded significantly more DNA than Method C (p = 0.0003), Method D (p < 0.0001), Method E (p < 0.0001), and Method F (p < 0.001). No other method approached the yield obtained with Method G (exact p values in Supplementary Table S1).

Representative agarose gel electrophoresis of DNA extracted using Methods C–G showed a broad smear centered at approximately 6 kbp, consistent with partial DNA shearing during fecal bacterial DNA extraction as reported previously23 (Fig. 2b). To evaluate whether the extraction procedure influenced taxonomic recovery, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing targeting the V4 region was performed. Genus-level classification identified 14 dominant genera, including Barnesiella, Turicibacter, Bacteroides, Lactobacillus, Clostridium XIVa, and Alistipes (Fig. 2c). Relative abundance values were normalized to 100% per sample prior to averaging across replicates; the implications of this normalization are noted in the Methods. The proportion of reads not assigned at the genus level (“Reject hit”) differed significantly among extraction methods (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Table S1). Method G exhibited the lowest reject rate (3.9 ± 0.7%), which was significantly lower than Method C (8.8 ± 1.3%, P = 0.002), Method D (8.4 ± 0.8%, P = 0.001), and Method E (10.2 ± 0.3%, P < 0.0001), suggesting improved taxonomic assignment efficiency with Method G. Comparison of Gram-positive representation revealed method-dependent differences (Fig. 2e). Method G (42.3 ± 2.0%) yielded significantly higher Gram-positive proportions than Method C (12.5 ± 0.7%, P < 0.001) and Method D (17.6 ± 3.0%, P = 0.004). Method E (33.0 ± 0.6%) also showed greater Gram-positive recovery than Method C (P = 0.01). These observations are consistent with prior reports that bead-beating efficiency and inhibitor removal affect the recovery of Gram-positive taxa with thick peptidoglycan walls16–18.

Collectively, Method G provided superior DNA yield, reduced reject rate, and balanced representation of Gram-positive and Gram-negative taxa (Supplementary Table S1). Therefore, Method G was selected for all subsequent experiments. Exact p values for all statistical comparisons are summarized in Supplementary Table S1.

Evaluation of extraction methods based on recovery efficiency and polyamine balance

To evaluate polyamine extraction performance from mouse feces, we compared ten extraction procedures including conventional solvent-based extraction methods (Methods 1–9) and a DNA-extraction-based workflow using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Method 10). Method 1 corresponds to the non-volatile amine measurement procedure described in the Japanese Pharmaceutical Society’s Methods of Analysis in Health Science 202019, whereas Methods 2–9 employed commonly used extraction solvents including hydrochloric acid, trichloroacetic acid, methanol, and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Method 10 utilizes the residual fraction obtained after bacterial DNA extraction from feces (Fig. 3a).

Polyamine recovery efficiency

The extraction yields of Put, Spd, and Spm were quantified for each extraction method (Fig. 3b–d). Among Methods 1–9, Methods 4 and 5 showed the highest extraction efficiencies for Put and Spd. Method 4 yielded Put = 0.28 ± 0.08 nmol mg−1 and Spd = 0.24 ± 0.04 nmol mg−1, while Method 5 yielded Put = 0.28 ± 0.1 nmol mg−1, Spd = 0.24 ± 0.03 nmol mg−1. Compared with Method 10 (Put = 0.26 ± 0.03 nmol mg−1), several methods yielded significantly different Put levels. Method 1 (P = 2.62 × 10−4), Method 6 (P = 1.18 × 10−4), Method 7 (P = 7.18 × 10−4), Method 8 (P = 5.24 × 10−5), and Method 9 (P = 8.47 × 10−5) showed significantly different Put levels compared with Method 10. In contrast, Spd extraction was significantly higher in Method 10 compared with all conventional extraction methods (Methods 1–9), with extremely small p values ranging from P = 2.11 × 10−15 to P = 2.22 × 10−6. Similarly, Spm extraction was significantly higher in Method 10 compared with Methods 1–9 (all P < 1 × 10−8). These results indicate that Method 10 preferentially improves recovery of Spd and Spm relative to conventional solvent-based extraction methods.

Measurement reproducibility

The analytical reproducibility of the measurements was assessed by calculating the coefficient of variation (CV) for each polyamine (Table 1). Method 10 showed CV values of Put = 9.8%, Spd = 11.8%, and Spm = 5.7%, indicating relatively stable measurements across replicates.

Polyamine extraction balance

Extraction balance across polyamine species was examined by calculating the intra-sample coefficient of variation among Put, Spd, and Spm (Fig. 3e). This metric reflects how uniformly the three polyamines are extracted from each sample. Method 1 showed the lowest CV values, indicating highly balanced extraction across polyamine species, although this method exhibited markedly reduced total polyamine recovery. In contrast, Methods 6 and 7 showed substantially higher CV values, indicating pronounced imbalance in the extraction of individual polyamines.

Integrated evaluation of extraction methods

A composite performance score was calculated by integrating normalized recovery efficiency and extraction balance metrics (Fig. 3f). While Method 1 demonstrated excellent extraction balance, its low extraction yield resulted in a reduced overall score. In contrast, Method 10 achieved both high recovery efficiency, particularly for Spd and Spm, and moderate CV values, resulting in the highest composite performance score among the tested methods.

These results show that Method 10 is the most suitable extraction strategy, enabling efficient polyamine recovery while maintaining acceptable extraction balance and allowing simultaneous extraction of bacterial DNA and polyamines from the same fecal sample. Exact p values for all statistical comparisons are summarized in Supplementary Table S2.

Alterations in gut microbiota composition and fecal polyamine metabolism in AC mice

Gut microbial composition and fecal polyamine levels were analyzed using the same fecal samples collected from AC and WC mice at 8, 32, and 56 weeks of age. At the genus level, Lactobacillus was the dominant bacterial taxon in WC mice at all time points (Fig. 4a). In WC mice, the relative abundance of Lactobacillus increased with age, accounting for 33.7%, 45.7%, and 55.7% of the microbiota at 8, 32, and 56 weeks, respectively. In contrast, AC mice showed markedly lower proportions of Lactobacillus, representing 14.0%, 10.8%, and 13.4% at 8, 32, and 56 weeks, respectively. Comparison of the overall microbial community structure revealed differences in several bacterial genera between AC and WC mice (Fig. 4b). Genera belonging to Lachnospiraceae-related taxa, including Clostridium XIVa, Faecalitalea, and Lachnospiraceae incertae sedis, displayed altered relative abundance between the two groups. Differences were also observed in Bacteroidetes-associated genera, such as Bacteroides and Alloprevotella, as well as in Alistipes and Prevotella.

These observations suggest differences in gut microbial community composition between AC and WC mice. Alpha diversity analysis demonstrated group- and age-associated differences in Shannon diversity indices (Supplementary Fig. S3a). Beta diversity analysis based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity showed partial separation between AC and WC samples in principal coordinate analysis (Supplementary Fig. S3b). PERMANOVA analysis further supported differences in microbial community structure between groups (P = 0.001).

Fecal polyamine concentrations were subsequently quantified from the same samples (Fig. 4c, d). At 8 weeks of age, AC mice exhibited significantly higher concentrations of Spd (1.2 ± 0.23 nmol mg−1) compared with WC mice (P < 0.01). At 32 weeks of age, no significant differences in individual fecal polyamine concentrations were detected between the two groups. However, at 56 weeks of age, AC mice showed markedly elevated levels of Put, Spd, and Spm, which were 2.0-fold (P < 0.001), 2.2-fold (P < 0.0001), and 1.4-fold (P < 0.05) higher, respectively, than those observed in WC mice. Two-way ANOVA revealed significant effects of group, age, and their interaction on total fecal polyamine levels (group: P = 3.12 × 10−5; age: P = 7.21 × 10−5; interaction: P = 0.00071). Post hoc comparisons indicated that total polyamine concentrations were significantly higher in AC mice than in WC mice at 8 and 56 weeks of age. Consistent with these findings, the total fecal polyamine concentration (Put + Spd + Spm) in AC mice increased approximately twofold between 8 and 56 weeks of age (P < 0.001), whereas total fecal polyamine levels in WC mice peaked at 8 weeks of age and remained relatively stable thereafter (Fig. 4d).

Taken together, these observations suggest that alterations in gut microbiota composition in AC mice are accompanied by changes in fecal polyamine metabolism during disease progression.

Discussion

This study suggests that early alterations in the microbiota–polyamine axis occur in an AD mouse model and can be captured through integrated analysis of gut microbiota and fecal polyamines from the same biological sample.

Using a sequential co-extraction workflow, we identified a consistent early decrease in Lactobacillus accompanied by increased Spd levels at 8 weeks, followed by a marked increase in total fecal polyamines at later stages. These findings suggest that alterations in microbial composition and polyamine metabolism may be linked and may emerge during an early phase of disease progression, consistent with previous reports of microbiota alterations in AD12,24. Our findings extend our previous work demonstrating systemic metabolic dysregulation involving the gut–pancreas–liver axis in AD model mice11. While the previous study focused on peripheral metabolic alterations and urea cycle disruption, the present study provides direct evidence that these systemic changes are accompanied by early alterations in gut microbiota composition and fecal polyamine metabolism. This supports the concept that microbiota-derived metabolites are closely linked to host metabolic dysfunction during AD progression.

Previous studies have systematically compared DNA extraction kits in terms of DNA yield, purity, and taxonomic bias16,17. These studies have demonstrated that extraction protocols can significantly influence microbiome profiling outcomes. However, they primarily treat DNA extraction as an independent analytical step. In contrast, the present study does not aim to establish a novel DNA extraction method or to benchmark kit performance alone. Instead, our approach demonstrates that the residual fraction generated during DNA extraction can be repurposed for metabolite recovery, enabling integrated analysis of microbiota composition and polyamine metabolism from the same biological sample. This sequential co-extraction strategy provides a distinct analytical advantage by linking microbial and metabolic information while reducing sample consumption, which has not been addressed in previous studies.

Several biological mechanisms may explain these observations. Changes in gut microbiota composition, particularly the reduction of Lactobacillus, may alter microbial polyamine biosynthesis and metabolic cross-feeding pathways12,13,25,26. In parallel, host-related processes such as inflammation, epithelial dysfunction, or altered metabolite transport may contribute to increased extracellular polyamine levels27. The early increase in Spd prior to global polyamine elevation suggests an initial functional imbalance that precedes overt metabolic disruption. Importantly, this study highlights the advantage of paired microbiome–metabolite analysis from the same sample. Unlike conventional approaches that require separate samples, the present workflow enables direct integration of microbial and metabolic information, reducing variability and improving interpretability in studies with limited sample availability28. From an analytical perspective, the use of matrix-matched calibration and the absence of detectable polyamines in extraction reagent controls support the reliability of the quantification and exclude significant contributions from kit-derived contamination.

This study has several limitations. First, the number of biological replicates was limited, and therefore the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of AD-associated microbiota dysregulation. Second, although all mice were maintained under identical environmental conditions within the same animal facility and cages were placed in close proximity, mice were group-housed according to genotype and treatment group. Therefore, cage-associated microbiota effects cannot be completely excluded. Third, microbiome datasets are inherently compositional, and relative abundance differences should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In addition, both fecal polyamine levels and microbiota composition may be influenced by environmental variables and host physiology. Future studies incorporating larger cohorts, cohousing strategies, littermate-controlled designs, and deeper metagenomic or metabolomic analyses will be important for validating the biological significance of the present findings. Overall, the present study demonstrates the feasibility of integrated microbiome–polyamine profiling from the same fecal sample and suggests that coordinated alterations in gut microbiota composition and polyamine metabolism may occur during AD progression.

Conclusion

We developed a sequential co-extraction workflow that enables the recovery of gut microbial DNA and fecal polyamines from small stool samples and demonstrated its feasibility in an AD mouse model. The present findings suggest that integrated fecal microbiome–polyamine profiling may be useful as an exploratory approach for studying AD-associated metabolic dysregulation.

Methods

Reagents

All reagents were of analytical grade. Acetonitrile (HPLC grade, 99.9%), acetone (99.5%), and toluene (99.5%) were purchased from Kanto Chemical Co., Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). Proline (99.0%), putrescine dihydrochloride (99.0%), spermine (95.0%), trichloroacetic acid (TCA), hydrochloric acid, sodium carbonate (99.8%), PBS, and methanol (99.8%) were obtained from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan). Spermidine (98.0%) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and dansyl chloride (10% in acetone) from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Ultrapure water was prepared using a PURIC-Z II system (Organo Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Reagents for electrophoresis were obtained from Gellex International, MaestroGen, and Nippon Gene.

AD model mice

Animal experiments were conducted at the Chiba Institute of Technology in accordance with institutional guidelines and approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee (approval no. 18001). The study followed ARRIVE guidelines. All experiments involving genetically modified organisms were performed in compliance with the Act on the Conservation and Sustainable Use of Biological Diversity through Regulations on the Use of Living Modified Organisms (Cartagena Act, Act No. 97 of 2003) and the regulations of the Chiba Institute of Technology Recombinant DNA Safety Committee (approval no. 2018-01-01).

Knock-in mice carrying mutations in the amyloid-β region of the App gene were generated as previously described22. C57BL/6-App^tm3(NL-GF)Tcs^ mice were used as the AD model (AC), and C57BL/6-App^tm1(NL)Tcs^ mice served as controls (WC). Three mice per group were used. Mice were maintained under identical environmental conditions within the same animal facility and were provided the same standard diet and husbandry conditions throughout the study. Animals were group-housed according to genotype and treatment group, and cages were maintained in close proximity within the same housing rack to minimize environmental variation between groups. Animal resources were provided in accordance with the material transfer and usage conditions of the RIKEN BioResource Research Center.

Evaluation of DNA extraction methods

Seven extraction conditions (Methods A–G; Table 1) were evaluated using two commercial kits (QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit and QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit) with different bead types. DNA yield per mg feces was used as the primary evaluation metric. Taxonomic classification performance was assessed using 16 S rRNA sequencing.

Polyamine extraction

Ten extraction procedures (Methods 1–10; Table 1) were evaluated. Method 1 followed previously described protocols for non-volatile amines19. Methods 2–9 compared extraction solvents and bead types, whereas Method 10 was developed to recover polyamines from waste liquid generated during DNA extraction. In brief, fecal samples were homogenized with extraction solvents using bead-based disruption, followed by centrifugation to obtain supernatants for analysis. For Method 10, waste liquid from DNA extraction was combined with TCA-treated fecal residues prior to centrifugation.

Detailed step-by-step protocols, including solvent compositions, bead types, homogenization conditions, centrifugation parameters, and sample handling procedures for each method, are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Fluorescent derivatization and HPLC analysis

Polyamines were derivatized with dansyl chloride under alkaline conditions and analyzed by fluorescence HPLC. Chromatographic separation was performed using a C18 column with an acetonitrile–water gradient. Detection was carried out at excitation 340 nm and emission 515 nm.

Calibration and quantification of polyamines

Polyamine concentrations were determined using external calibration curves (0.1–1.0 µg mL−1). Calibration curves showed high linearity (R2 ≥ 0.996). Limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were calculated as LOD = 3.3σ/S and LOQ = 10σ/S, where S represents the slope of the calibration curve and σ is the standard deviation of the intercept. Analytical parameters are summarized in Table 1.

Evaluation of extraction performance

Extraction performance was evaluated using recovery efficiency and extraction balance. Recovery efficiency was defined as measured polyamine concentrations. Extraction balance was assessed using the coefficient of variation (CV): CV = SD / mean × 100. A composite performance score integrating recovery efficiency and extraction balance was calculated as: Composite score = 0.6 × Recovery_norm + 0.4 × Balance_norm.

16 S rRNA sequencing and sequence processing

The V4 region of the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). Libraries were prepared according to standard Illumina protocols29 and sequenced on an Illumina MiniSeq platform. Sequence data were processed using the BaseSpace pipeline with the DADA2 classifier.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2). DNA yield and polyamine extraction efficiencies across extraction methods were compared using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Comparisons between AC and WC mice across ages were performed using two-way ANOVA with post hoc multiple comparison tests where appropriate. Alpha diversity was evaluated using the Shannon diversity index. Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity followed by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Statistical significance of community structure differences was evaluated using PERMANOVA with 999 permutations.

For variability analysis (coefficient of variation, CV), each method was compared with Method 10 as a reference using Student’s t-test, with p values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. To assess robustness, non-parametric comparisons were additionally performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

All statistical tests were two-sided. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Exact p values are provided in Supplementary Tables S1–S3. Detailed experimental procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods.
