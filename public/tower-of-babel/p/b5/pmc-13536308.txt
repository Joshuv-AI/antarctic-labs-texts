Higher serum gastric parietal cell antibody titers are associated with increased frequencies of macrocytosis, vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia in patients with oral lichen planus
Authors: Yu-Hsueh Wu, Yi-Pang Lee, Julia Yu-Fong Chang, Yi-Ping Wang, Andy Sun, Chun-Pin Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposeOur previous study found 139 gastric parietal cell antibody (GPCA)-positive oral lichen planus (OLP) patients (GPCA+OLP patients) in a group of 588 OLP patients. This study evaluated whether higher-titer (serum GPCA titer ≥160) GPCA+OLP patients had greater frequencies of macrocytosis, anemia, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than lower-titer (GPCA titer ≥80) GPCA+OLP patients or 588 healthy control subjects.Materials and methodsComplete blood count, serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, homocysteine, and GPCA levels in 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients, 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, and 588 healthy control subjects were measured and compared.ResultsWe found that 32.1%, 37.7%, 24.5%, 43.4%, and 86.8% of higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients and 14.0%, 24.4%, 23.3%, 19.8%, and 34.9% of 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients were diagnosed as having macrocytosis, anemia, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia, respectively. Moreover, both 53 higher-titer and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients had significantly greater frequencies of macrocytosis, anemia, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than 588 healthy control subjects (all P-values <0.001). In addition, 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients also had significantly greater frequencies of macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia than 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients (all P-values <0.05).ConclusionThe higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients have significantly greater frequencies of macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia than lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients. Thus, we conclude that higher serum GPCA titers are associated with increased frequencies of macrocytosis, vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia in patients with oral lichen planus.

Introduction

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory disease that primarily affects the oral mucosa. It is characterized by T cell–driven cytotoxicity against basal keratinocytes, leading to epithelial damage and a band-like lymphocytic infiltrate in the superficial lamina propria. Clinically, OLP presents in several forms, including reticular, papular, plaque-like, erosive/atrophic, ulcerative, and bullous types. The prevalence of OLP in the general population ranges from approximately 0.2%–3%, with a predilection for middle-aged and elderly women.1,2 Although the exact etiology remains unclear, OLP has been associated with systemic conditions, psychological stress, and autoimmune disorders, suggesting that immune dysregulation plays a central role in its pathogenesis.2

Gastric parietal cell antibodies (GPCAs) are autoantibodies directed against the H+/K+-ATPase proton pump expressed on gastric parietal cells. These cells secrete intrinsic factor and hydrochloric acid (HCl). Intrinsic factor is indispensable for vitamin B12 absorption in the terminal ileum. The presence of GPCAs indicates autoimmune-mediated destruction of parietal cells, as observed in autoimmune gastritis, resulting in intrinsic factor deficiency and hypochlorhydria.3–6 Consequently, impaired vitamin B12 absorption may ensue, leading to vitamin B12 deficiency, macrocytosis, and, in more advanced stages, pernicious anemia (PA). Vitamin B12 deficiency also disrupts homocysteine metabolism, causing elevated serum homocysteine levels (hyperhomocysteinemia), which has been associated with endothelial dysfunction and an increased risk of cardiovascular disease.5–11 In addition, hypochlorhydria may impair iron absorption, giving rise to iron deficiency, microcytosis, and iron deficiency anemia (IDA).12–17

Accumulating evidence suggests a close association between oral lichen planus (OLP) and autoimmune phenomena, including the presence of various circulating autoantibodies.2 Several studies have reported a higher frequency of GPCAs in patients with OLP than in healthy controls, implying a shared autoimmune background.1–4 Because GPCA positivity can compromise vitamin B12 and iron absorption and lead to hematinic deficiencies, OLP patients with higher serum GPCA titers may be at increased risk of developing microcytosis, macrocytosis, anemia, vitamin B12 and iron deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia. 1–17 However, the relationship between serum GPCA titer levels and hematologic or metabolic abnormalities in OLP patients remains incompletely understood. Therefore, the present study aimed to determine whether higher serum GPCA titers were associated with significantly increased frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, anemia, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in patients with OLP.

In our previous investigations, 139 of 588 OLP patients tested positive for GPCAs.1,3,4 Among these 139 GPCA-positive OLP patients (designated as GPCA+OLP patients in the present study), 16 (11.5%), 29 (20.9%), 41 (29.5%), 33 (23.7%), 40 (28.8%), and 76 (54.7%) GPCA+OLP patients exhibited microcytosis, macrocytosis, anemia, serum iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia, respectively. 4 In the current study, when sera from GPCA+OLP patients were positive at dilutions of 1:80 or 1:160, they were assigned GPCA titers of 80 or 160, respectively. Based on this criterion, 53 of the 139 GPCA+OLP patients had serum GPCA titers ≥160 (designated as higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients), whereas 86 had titers ≥80 (designated as lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients).1,3,4 We compared the hematologic profiles of the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients with those of the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients and 588 healthy control subjects to determine whether higher serum GPCA titers were associated with increased frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, anemia (particularly PA), vitamin B12 and iron deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia in OLP patients.

Materials and methods

Subjects

In this study, 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients (5 men and 48 women; age range, 34–87 years; mean age, 60.0 ± 11.0 years) and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients (15 men and 71 women; age range, 29–83 years; mean age, 58.0 ± 12.2 years) were selected from the 588 OLP patients reported in our previous study.1 For each of the 588 OLP patients, one age- (±2 years) and sex-matched healthy control subject was recruited. Accordingly, 588 age- (±2 years) and sex-matched healthy control subjects (110 men and 478 women; age range, 20–89 years; mean age, 56.0 ± 14.1 years) were included in the present study.1 All 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients, 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, and 588 healthy control subjects were consecutively examined, diagnosed, and treated at National Taiwan University Hospital (NTUH) between July 2007 and June 2023. The 53 higher-titer and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients were enrolled in accordance with the inclusion and exclusion criteria for OLP described in our previous studies. 1–4,11,14,17–21 Healthy control subjects had no oral mucosal or systemic diseases, apart from dental caries or mild periodontal disease. None of the OLP patients had received treatment for OLP within three months prior to enrollment.

Blood samples were obtained from the 139 GPCA+OLP patients and 588 healthy control subjects for measurement of complete blood counts, as well as serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, homocysteine concentrations, and serum GPCA levels. All 139 GPCA+OLP patients and 588 healthy control subjects provided written informed consent before participation. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of NTUH (202402086RINC).

Determination of blood hemoglobin (Hb), serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine concentrations

Complete blood counts and measurements of serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine levels were performed using routine laboratory procedures in the Department of Laboratory Medicine, NTUH, as described previously.1–4,9–60

Determination of serum gastric parietal cell antibody levels

Serum GPCA levels were determined by indirect immunofluorescence using rat stomach tissue as the substrate, as previously described.1–4,9–60 Sera were considered positive if fluorescence was observed at a dilution of 1:10 or higher. When sera from GPCA+OLP patients were positive at dilutions of 1:80 or 1:160, they were assigned GPCA titers of 80 or 160, respectively. In the present study, higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients were defined as those with GPCA titers ≥160, whereas lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients were defined as those with GPCA titers ≤80.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of mean corpuscular volume (MCV), blood Hb levels, and serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine concentrations between the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients or 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients and the 588 healthy control subjects, as well as between the 53 higher-titer and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, were performed using Student’s t-test. Differences in the frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb deficiency, serum iron, vitamin B12, and folic acid deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia between the two GPCA+OLP patient groups and healthy controls, as well as between the two GPCA+OLP subgroups, were analyzed using the chi-square test. In addition, the frequencies of macrocytic, normocytic, or microcytic anemia between the 53 highertiter and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients were compared using the chi-square test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The MCV and mean blood Hb, serum iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, and homocysteine levels in the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients, 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, and 588 healthy control subjects are presented in Table 1. Because men generally have higher blood Hb and serum iron levels than women, these two parameters were analyzed separately by sex. The 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients exhibited significantly lower mean blood Hb (women only), serum iron (women only), and vitamin B12 levels, as well as a significantly higher mean serum homocysteine level, compared with the 588 healthy control subjects (all P< 0.001; Table 1). In addition, compared with the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients had a significantly lower mean serum vitamin B12 level and a significantly higher mean serum homocysteine level (all P< 0.05; Table 1). Furthermore, the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients showed significantly lower mean blood Hb (both men and women), serum iron (both men and women), vitamin B12, and folic acid levels, together with a significantly higher mean serum homocysteine level, than the 588 healthy control subjects (all P< 0.05; Table 1).

According to the World Health Organization criteria, microcytosis was defined as an MCV <80 fL,12–14 and macrocytosis as an MCV ≥100 fL.55–57 Men with Hb < 13 g/dL and women with Hb < 12 g/dL were classified as having Hb deficiency (anemia).61 Patients with serum iron levels <60 μg/dL,62 serum vitamin B12 levels <200 pg/mL,63 or serum folic acid levels <4 ng/mL64 were defined as having iron, vitamin B12, or folic acid deficiency, respectively. Hyperhomocysteinemia was defined as a blood homocysteine level >12.0 μM, corresponding to the mean value for healthy controls plus two standard deviations. Based on these definitions, 15.1%, 32.1%, 37.7%, 24.5%, 43.4%, and 86.8% of the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients, and 9.3%, 14.0%, 24.4%, 23.3%, 19.8%, and 34.9% of the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, were diagnosed as having microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb deficiency, serum iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia, respectively. Both higher-titer and lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb deficiency, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the 588 healthy control subjects (all P< 0.001; Table 2). Moreover, the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients exhibited significantly higher frequencies of macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients (all P< 0.05; Table 2).

Anemia (defined as Hb < 13 g/dL in men and <12 g/dL in women)61 was identified in 20 higher-titer and 21 lowertiter GPCA+OLP patients. Among the 20 anemic higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients, 9 (45.0%) had PA (defined as anemia with MCV ≥100 fL, serum vitamin B12 < 200 pg/mL, and positive serum GPCA),5,6 one (5.0%) had macrocytic anemia other than PA,55–57 4 (20.0%) had normocytic anemia (anemia with MCV 80.0–99.9 fL),58–60,65–68 5 (25.0%) had IDA (anemia with MCV <80 fL and serum iron <60 μg/dL),15–17,61,62 and one (5.0%) had thalassemia trait–induced anemia (defined as anemia with RBC count >5.0 M/μL, MCV <74 fL, and Mentzer index <13) (Table 3).69 Among the 21 anemic lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients, 8 (38.1%) had PA, one (4.8%) had macrocytic anemia other than PA, 8 (38.1%) had NA, and 4 (19.1%) had IDA (Table 3). We further compared the frequencies of overall anemia and of macrocytic, normocytic, or microcytic anemia between the 53 higher-titer and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients. Although the higher-titer group exhibited numerically greater frequencies of overall anemia, macrocytic anemia, and microcytic anemia than the lower-titer group, these differences did not reach statistical significance (all P> 0.05; Table 4).

Discussion

The principal finding of this study was that the 53 highertiter GPCA+OLP patients exhibited significantly greater frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb deficiency, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the 588 healthy control subjects. In addition, the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients had significantly higher frequencies of macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients. Although the frequencies of anemia and serum iron deficiency were higher in the higher-titer group (37.7% and 24.5%, respectively) than in the lower-titer group (24.4% and 23.3%, respectively), these differences did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). Higher GPCA titers are presumed to reflect more extensive destruction of gastric parietal cells, resulting in more severe deficiencies of intrinsic factor and hydrochloric acid. Consequently, patients with higher GPCA titers are more likely to develop more pronounced vitamin B12 and iron deficiencies than those with lower titers. Vitamin B12 is essential for DNA synthesis and erythrocyte proliferation,5,6 whereas iron is a key component of Hb, along with protoporphyrin IX, α-globin, and β-globin.15–17,61,62,69 Therefore, more severe deficiencies of vitamin B12 and iron may account for the higher frequency of blood Hb deficiency (anemia) observed in the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients (37.7%) compared with the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients (24.4%), even though the difference was not statistically significant (Table 2).

Among the 17 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients with macrocytosis, 16 had vitamin B12 deficiency (vitamin B12 < 200 pg/mL), and none had folic acid deficiency. Similarly, of the 12 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients with macrocytosis, 11 had vitamin B12 deficiency, and none had folic acid deficiency. These findings indicate that vitamin B12 deficiency is the principal factor contributing to macrocytosis in both higher-titer and lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients.55–57 The frequency of vitamin B12 deficiency was significantly higher in the 53 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients (43.4%) than in the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients (19.8%) (P = 0.005, Table 2), which may explain the correspondingly higher frequency of macrocytosis in the higher-titer group (32.1% vs. 14.0%; P = 0.019, Table 2).55–57

Homocysteine is generated during methionine metabolism, and both vitamin B12 and folic acid serve as essential coenzymes in the remethylation of homocysteine to methionine.7–11 Accordingly, deficiencies of vitamin B12 and/or folic acid may lead to hyperhomocysteinemia. Supplementation with folic acid, vitamin B12, and vitamin B6 has been shown to reduce serum homocysteine levels.8 Our previous studies further demonstrated that supplementation with vitamin BC capsules combined with the appropriate deficient vitamin B12 and/or folic acid significantly decreased elevated serum homocysteine levels in patients with atrophic glossitis or burning mouth syndrome. 70,71 In the present study, the frequency of hyperhomocysteinemia was significantly higher in the 53 highertiter GPCA+OLP patients (86.8%) than in the 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients (34.9%). Among the 46 higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients with hyperhomocysteinemia, 23 (50.0%) had vitamin B12 deficiency and none had folic acid deficiency. Similarly, among the 30 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients with hyperhomocysteinemia, 15 (50.0%) had vitamin B12 deficiency and none had folic acid deficiency. These observations suggest that GPCA-associated vitamin B12 deficiency is the primary contributor to hyperhomocysteinemia in both higher-titer and lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients.9–11 Thus, the significantly higher frequency of vitamin B12 deficiency in the higher-titer group (43.4% vs. 19.8%) likely accounts for the markedly greater frequency of hyperhomocysteinemia observed in these patients (86.8% vs. 34.9%; P< 0.001, Table 2).

The findings of this study have significant clinical implications for the management of OLP patients, particularly those with higher serum GPCA titers. The results of this study indicate that higher serum GPCA titers are associated with more severe hematologic abnormalities, such as macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia, in OLP patients. Clinicians should be aware of the increased risk for vitamin B12 and iron deficiencies, as well as the potential for anemia, in OLP patients with higher serum GPCA titers. Given the critical role of vitamin B12 in erythropoiesis and the cardiovascular risks associated with hyperhomocysteinemia, early detection and supplementation of vitamin B12 in these patients could help mitigate the risk of PA and cardiovascular disease.5–8 Furthermore, the higher frequency of deficiencies in these patients highlights the need for routine hematologic screening and careful monitoring of serum vitamin B12 and homocysteine levels in OLP patients, particularly those with higher serum GPCA titers.

Despite the valuable insights provided, this study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between higher serum GPCA titers and the observed hematologic abnormalities. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether higher serum GPCA titers lead to the development of anemia, vitamin deficiencies, or other metabolic complications over time. Additionally, while the study compares OLP patients with healthy control subjects, it does not investigate the potential impact of other factors, such as medication use or dietary habits, which may also influence hematologic parameters. The sample size of the higher-titer GPCA+OLP group (53 patients) is relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Finally, the study only included patients from a single institution, which could introduce bias due to regional or institutional differences in patient populations.

In summary, both the 53 higher-titer and 86 lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients exhibited significantly higher frequencies of microcytosis, macrocytosis, blood Hb deficiency, serum iron and vitamin B12 deficiencies, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the 588 healthy control subjects. Moreover, the higher-titer GPCA+OLP patients demonstrated significantly greater frequencies of macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia than the lower-titer GPCA+OLP patients. These findings indicate that higher serum GPCA titers are associated with more pronounced macrocytosis, serum vitamin B12 deficiency, and hyperhomocysteinemia in patients with OLP.
