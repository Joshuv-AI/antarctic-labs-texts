Topographic Steric Maps and Noncovalent Interactions of Dinuclear Silicon-Bridged Bis(indenyl)zirconocene Cations: Insights from DFT
Authors: Wijitra Jitonnom, Mikko Linnolahti, Jitrayut Jitonnom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dinuclear zirconocene cations such as [(Cp2ZrMe)2(μ-Me)]+ are adducts of a cationic monomethyl zirconocene and a neutral dimethyl zirconocene joined by a bridging methyl (μ-Me) group and act as dormant species in equilibrium with the active mononuclear catalyst. Using density functional theory at the M06–2X/def2-TZVP level with a polarizable continuum (PCM) toluene solvent model, we examined four configurational isomers of the silicon-bridged bis­(indenyl) complex [(Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+ (Ind = indenyl): cis-RS, cis-RR, trans-RS, and trans-RR. The relative stability follows the order cis-RS > trans-RS > trans-RR > cis-RR, with the heterochiral (RS) isomers more stable than the homochiral (RR) isomers in both the cis and trans series. The stability ordering tracks with a larger buried volume around the μ-Me bridge, a more negative charge on the bridging methyl, and a larger HOMO–LUMO gap, although these are reported as exploratory trends given the small set of isomers. The most stable isomers adopt near-perpendicular torsion angles between the metal centers and Zr–Zr distances of about 4.8 Å. Topographic steric maps show pronounced hindrance around the methyl bridge, and a noncovalent interaction analysis identifies attractive, repulsive, and van der Waals regions whose balance accompanies the stability ordering. Because olefin insertion requires dissociation of the adduct, these results bear on catalyst behavior through the stability of the dormant species and the ease of its dissociation, clarifying how steric and electronic factors shape the energy landscape of these μ-Me-bridged bimetallic complexes.

Introduction

Zirconium-based systems have attracted considerable interest for their applications in catalysis and materials science. Among them, dinuclear zirconocene cations such as [(Cp2ZrMe)2(μ-Me)]+ have been identified as intermediates in olefin polymerization. These bimetallic complexes form as adducts between a cationic monomethyl zirconocene and a neutral dimethyl zirconocene, with the two metal centers joined by a bridging methyl (μ-Me) group, and their structural and electronic features govern how readily they form and dissociate. ,

The presence of μ-Me bridges in such dinuclear species has been established experimentally: X-ray crystallography and NMR spectroscopy show that the bridging methyl groups act as electron-donating linkers between the two zirconium centers, and DFT calculations reproduce the corresponding structural and electronic features. , In polymerization catalysis, μ-Me-bridged dinuclear zirconocene species have been associated with catalyst resting states and observed as intermediates during the activation of B­(C6F5)3-based systems. , Because olefin insertion requires an open coordination site at a single metal center, these complexes are best regarded as dormant species in equilibrium with the active mononuclear catalyst, whose formation modulates the concentration of active species rather than participating directly in chain propagation. , In related cyclopentadienyl-amidinate zirconium systems, reversible methyl-group transfer between active and dormant centers, proceeding through a methyl-bridged bimetallic intermediate, has been shown to influence stereoregularity, which illustrates how the dynamics of such dinuclear species can carry stereochemical consequences. The ansa-bridging ligand also influences their formation: introducing a bridge generally stabilizes the dinuclear adduct, and silicon-based bridges in particular favor it.

Despite these advances, the specific contributions of steric effects and noncovalent interactions (NCIs) to the relative stability of these bimetallic complexes remain insufficiently characterized, as does the influence of isomerism in chiral silicon-bridged systems that can adopt distinct diastereomeric forms. , Given the growing recognition of NCIs in selective polymerization catalysis, a clearer picture of these contributions is useful for catalyst design.

In this study, we examine the silicon-bridged bis­(indenyl) system [(Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+ (Ind = indenyl). Analogous dinuclear species have been detected by NMR spectroscopy, but their steric environments and noncovalent interaction networks have not been characterized. The rac form of this chiral indenyl-based zirconocene is widely used in the production of isotactic polypropylene, which makes the relative stabilities of its possible bimetallic adducts relevant to catalyst behavior. We consider four configurational isomers, cis-RS, cis-RR, trans-RS, and trans-RR (Figure ), using density functional theory (DFT). Our objectives are to (1) determine the relative stabilities of these isomers and identify the structural factors that distinguish them, (2) quantify the steric environment around the metal centers using topographic steric maps, (3) characterize the noncovalent interactions present, and (4) relate steric parameters, electronic properties, and relative stability. The aim is a clearer account of how steric and electronic factors together shape the energy landscape of these μ-Me-bridged bimetallic complexes.

Theoretical Methods

DFT Calculations

All calculations were conducted using the Gaussian 16 software, employing the M06–2X density functional, in combination with the def2-TZVP basis set for all atoms. A 28-electron relativistic effective core potential was applied to the zirconium atom. The basis sets were taken from the EMSL basis set exchange. The DFT/mixed basis set methods have previously been applied successfully in our previous studies on polymerization process. − We employed the M06–2X functional, a highly parametrized hybrid meta-GGA functional with 54% exact exchange that performs well for noncovalent interactions and main-group thermochemistry within a single-reference framework. This M06–2X/def2-TZVP approach has been validated for related dinuclear zirconocene cations against electrospray ionization mass spectrometry data and shown to give results in good agreement with single-point CCSD­(T) calculations, supporting its reliability for the bimetallic complexes studied here. All geometries were fully optimized without any constraints using the integral equation formalism variant of the polarizable continuum model (IEFPCM) to simulate the toluene solvent, with a dielectric constant of 2.3741. Frequency calculations were performed to ensure all stationary points were minima and to derive Gibbs free energies (T = 298.15 K, p = 1.013 bar). Natural population analysis (NPA) was used to determine the charge distribution of atoms in molecules.

Topographic Steric Maps Analysis

The steric map and percent buried volume analysis reported in this work were generated using the SambVca 2.1 web application. This SambVca tool is widely used for visualizing and quantifying the spatial occupation of molecular fragments or substituents around a central point, such as metallocenes. , We applied this resource to explore its ligand steric maps and percent buried volume (% V bur). The percent buried volume quantifies how much of the sphere’s surface is occupied by substituents. It is given by the equation %Vbur=(occupiedvolumewithinradiusR/totalvolumeofsphereofradiusR)×100

A common radius R = 3.5 Å is used for metal complexes, ensuring the steric effects of ligands are captured effectively.

Analysis of Noncovalent Interactions

Noncovalent interactions (NCIs) analysis was performed to visualize and characterize weak interactions within the structures of the bimetallic complex [(Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+. To address this purpose, the NCIweb server was used and calculated, which are based on electron density (ρ) and reduced density gradient (RDG or s), according to the equation RDG=12(3π)1/3|∇ρ|ρ4/3

The analysis employed RDG isosurfaces to identify regions of significant noncovalent interactions, which were further analyzed using color-coded mapping to distinguish their nature: red regions for repulsive steric interactions, blue regions for strong attractive interactions (e.g., electrostatics and hydrogen bonds), and green regions for weak van der Waals (vdW) interactions or dispersion forces.

Statistical Analysis on Steric and Electronic Descriptors

Given the structure–property connection’s importance for zirconocene catalyst design, steric and electronic descriptors were evaluated for the four configurational isomers (cis-RS, cis-RR, trans-RS, and trans-RR) in order to establish any stability–property relationship. The descriptors included percent buried volume (% V bur), Frontier orbital (HOMO and LUMO) energies and their energy gap (E gap), NPA atomic charges, as well as dipole moment. The % V bur descriptor quantitatively reflects the steric hindrance surrounding the catalytic center by measuring the spatial occupation of ligands around the metal. This parameter directly relates to substrate accessibility and catalyst stability by influencing molecular geometry and interligand interactions. The Frontier orbital energies serve as a descriptor of the electronic reactivity of the zirconocene complexes, while atomic charge distributions provide insight into electron localization and bonding character. Linear regression analyses were performed using the least-squares method, and the goodness of fit was assessed by the coefficient of determination (R 2). Parameter pairs with significant values (R 2 > 0.6) were visualized as correlation plots (Figures S1 and S2 of Supporting Information).

Results and Discussion

Stability-Electronic Relationship

We performed DFT calculations to analyze the structural stability of [(Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+ in its cis and trans isomeric forms. Throughout, ΔΔG denotes the Gibbs energy of each isomer relative to the most stable isomer (cis-RS) at the same composition. These values therefore compare the relative stabilities of the four configurational forms of the bimetallic complex and do not represent adduct formation energies. We used ΔΔG values to reveal important insights into how steric and electronic factors influence their geometries.

Among the four isomers, cis-RS emerged as the most stable configuration (ΔΔG = 0.00 kcal/mol). This enhanced stability can be attributed to its favorable spatial arrangement that effectively minimizes steric repulsion while maintaining electronic stability (Figure A). The more negative charge on the methyl bridge (Q μMe = −0.325e) and high LUMO and E gap energies (−2.423 and 5.591 eV) further contribute to this stability. In contrast, cis-RR is the least stable isomer (ΔΔG = 9.0 kcal/mol). This reduced stability correlates with its lowest LUMO and E gap values (−2.650 and 5.389 eV) and a less negative charge on μ-Me (Q μMe = −0.318e). This is also consistent with the LUMO orbital distribution showing significant electron density around the μ-Me atoms (Figure S3). These electronic characteristics suggest destabilization arising from two factors: reduced efficiency in metal–ligand bonding and unfavorable electronic distribution across the bimetallic complex structure. The more and fewer positive charges on Zr1 and Zr2, respectively, and the negative μ-Me charge indicates a charge imbalance between the two Zr centers, which is likely a key to the cooperative stabilization across the bimetallic complex structure (Figure S2). These electronic features, combined with a more compressed molecular geometry, render cis-RR particularly unfavorable (see μ-Me···Zr2 shortening from 2.48 Å in cis-RS to 2.46 Å in cis-RR, Figure B).

For the trans isomers, we found that trans-RS (ΔΔG = 1.3 kcal/mol) is more stable than trans-RR (ΔΔG = 6.9 kcal/mol). This stability difference aligns with the electronic properties of trans-RS, which exhibits higher LUMO and E gap energies (−2.454 and 5.537 eV) and a larger negative charge on the μ-Me group (−0.324e) compared to trans-RR (Figure C,D). Among the electronic descriptors examined (Figure S1), the LUMO energy, E gap, and the μ-Me charge (Q μMe) tracked most closely with the stability ordering (Figure S2). With only four isomers, we treat these as exploratory trends rather than statistically robust correlations; together with the steric analysis below, they are consistent with stability arising from a combination of steric and electronic factors rather than any single descriptor.

To support the structural basis for these energetic differences, we analyzed key geometric parameters: Zr–Zr distances and two critical angles (ϕ and θ) for each isomer. The θ angle reflects the torsion across the μ-Me bridge (Me–Zr···Zr–Me), while ϕ describes the equatorial coordination geometry (Figure ). These parameters provide valuable insights into the spatial arrangements that influence stability. The most stable isomer, cis-RS, exhibits a θ angle of 84.7° and a Zr–Zr distance of 4.81 Å. This near-perpendicular arrangement minimizes steric interference between the bulky indene ligands, allowing for optimal spacing between the two zirconocene units. This configuration appears to strike an ideal balance between metal–metal interaction and ligand repulsion. In contrast, cis-RR adopts a 2-fold smaller θ angle of 43.4° despite maintaining a similar Zr–Zr distance (4.81 Å). This compressed angular geometry forces the ligands into closer proximity, creating severe steric congestion. This unfavorable spatial arrangement, combined with the charge distribution note earlier, explains its reduced stability (ΔΔG = 9.0 kcal/mol) and narrowed HOMO–LUMO energy (E gap = 5.389 eV).

For trans isomers, geometric differences also correlate with their stability variations. Trans-RS displays a θ angle of 80.9° and a Zr–Zr distance of 4.80 Å, values remarkably similar to those of cis-RS. This relaxed geometry contributes to its position as the second most stable isomer. Interestingly, in trans-RR, the θ angle expands markedly to 159.9°, and the Zr–Zr distance extends to 4.86 Åthe longest among all isomersdue to van der Waals strain between the indene ligands of the two zirconocenes (Figure S4).

We also observed distinct differences in the electronic structure between isomers that correspond to these geometric variations. In particular, the cis isomers exhibit more efficient electron delocalization from Zr1 to Zr2 in their LUMO orbitals, suggesting enhanced electron transfer compared to the trans isomers (Figure S3). For trans-RR, the extended geometry appears to reduce orbital overlap between metal centers, which is consistent with its lower E gap and less negative μ-Me charge compared to the more stable isomers.

These structural analyses provide further evidence that steric distortion and electronic destabilization operate cooperatively rather than independently. The isomers with balanced charge distribution also tend to adopt more favorable spatial arrangements, while those with electronic imbalances display distorted geometries. Overall, these results highlight the interplay between steric geometry and electronic structure, particularly the roles of metal–metal spacing, torsional strain, frontier orbital energies, and charge distribution in dictating the stability of dinuclear zirconocene cations.

In both the cis and trans series, the heterochiral (RS) isomers are more stable than the homochiral (RR) isomers (ΔΔG = 0.0 and 1.3 kcal/mol for cis-RS and trans-RS, versus 9.0 and 6.9 kcal/mol for cis-RR and trans-RR). Because the rac form of silicon-bridged bis­(indenyl)­zirconocenes is the catalyst used to produce isotactic polypropylene, the relative stabilities of the bimetallic adducts that form from it are relevant to its behavior. The greater stability of the heterochiral adduct implies that a larger fraction of metal would be sequestered as this dormant species, particularly at low cocatalyst-to-catalyst ratios, thereby reducing the concentration of active mononuclear catalysts available for polymerization rather than altering stereoselectivity directly.

Steric Environment Around the Bridging μ-Me Groups

The steric maps of the [(Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+ bimetallic complexes were generated using the SambVca tool based on the DFT-optimized geometry. Figure presents these steric maps along with their percent buried volume (% V bur) values, providing a 3D visualization of the steric environment surrounding each zirconium center. These maps complement our structural analysis by revealing the spatial distribution of steric bulk around the catalytic centers. The steric maps align remarkably well with the optimized geometries discussed earlier. In all four isomers, the methyl bridging groups predominantly occupy the equatorial positions (XY plane), creating significant steric hindrance in these regions (visualized as red zones). In contrast, the axial sites (+Z direction) remain relatively unobstructed, suggesting potential access points for incoming substrate or ligand interactions during catalysis. The extensive red zones in these maps indicate high steric congestion, which would likely restrict the approach of bulky cocatalysts or monomers during the polymerization process.

Steric maps of the four studied isomers of [(Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+ in different cis/trans isomers: (A) cis-RS, (B) cis-RR, (C) trans-RS, and (D) trans-RR. Values of buried volumes (% V bur) for each isomer were obtained, using a sphere radius of 3.5 Å centered on the μ-Me group.

Quantitative analysis revealed a notable correlation between steric parameters and stability. Across the four isomers, higher % V bur is associated with lower ΔΔG, that is, with greater stability (Figure S2C and Table ). We refrain from interpreting the associated R 2 value as a measure of explained variance, given that the fit involves only four points; the relationship is best regarded as an exploratory trend for these specific isomers. This suggests that % V bur accounts for approximately 88% of the variance in stability differences among these isomers. The regression line exhibits a negative slope, indicating that as % V bur increases, the ΔΔG values tend to decrease, corresponding to enhanced stability. This correlation is clearly illustrated when comparing specific isomers. Those with higher % V bur valuescis-RS (88.2%) and trans-RS (88.5%)exhibit lower ΔΔG values (0.0 vs 1.3 kcal/mol, respectively), indicating greater stability. Conversely, isomers with lower %V bur,cis-RR (86.5%) and trans-RR (86.3%)show higher ΔΔG values (9.0 vs 6.9 kcal/mol), reflecting greater steric strain and destabilization. This pattern suggests that increased steric shielding around the metal centers actually enhances bimetallic complex stability, likely by creating a protective steric environment that minimizes destabilizing interactions.

While these findings support the importance of steric effects in modulating bimetallic complex stability, we acknowledge that our analysis is based on a limited sample size of four isomers. No statistical significance testing or error analysis was performed; therefore, the observed correlation should be interpreted as a compelling trend rather than definitive evidence. Nevertheless, these results suggest that steric effects, as quantified by %V bur, play an important role in determining the relative stabilities of dinuclear zirconocene cationsan insight that could inform future catalyst design strategies.

Noncovalent Interactions

Noncovalent interactions play a crucial role in organometallic chemistry, often determining structural integrity and reactivity patterns. To complement our steric and electronic analyses, we investigated the weak interactions influencing the stability and reactivity of the four configurational isomers (cis-RS, cis-RR, trans-RS, and trans-RR) using NCI analysis. We employed the NCIweb computational tool with the optimized geometries of these isomers as inputs. This approach excels at distinguishing weak vdW forces, π–π stacking, and steric clashesinteractions recently shown to be critical in zirconium-based systems. ,

Figure presents both 2D and 3D visualizations of these interactions for all four isomers. Our detailed 2D reduced density gradient (RDG) analysis uncovered five specific interaction patterns (labeled as ①–⑤) that help explain the stability trends observed earlier. Two distinct blue peaks in the plots correspond to localized attractive interactions: ① a sharp, low-density peak at sign­(λ2)­ρ = −0.05 au reveals attractive interactions between silicon atoms and both methyl and indenyl groups, visualized as blue donut-shaped isosurfaces in the 3D representations. ② A wider peak at sign­(λ2)­ρ = −0.04 to −0.05 au indicates strong attractive interactions between Zr atoms and various ligands (indenyl and methyl groups, including the μ-Me bridge), appearing as extensive blue isosurfaces in the 3D models. ③ Most significant for bimetallic complex stability, we observed a broad green region at sign­(λ2)­ρ = −0.01 to −0.01 a.u, indicating delocalized vdW interactions between the two zirconocene units. These appear as green isosurfaces at the contact points between the zirconocene species and provide critical stabilization for the bimetallic complex structure. The analysis also identified two types of repulsive interactions: ④ A sharp peak at sign­(λ2)­ρ = 0.03 au corresponds to localized repulsive interactions within the 5-membered rings of the indenyl ligands. ⑤ A broader peak at sign­(λ2)­ρ = 0.04 to 0.05 au reflects stronger repulsive forces within the 6-membered rings of the indenyl ligands, visualized as compact red isosurfaces.

The NCI index is a qualitative, density-based descriptor that visualizes noncovalent interactions through the reduced density gradient and does not partition interaction energies. To compare the relative prominence of the different interaction regions across isomers, we integrated the electron density within each NCI region (Figure S5 and Table S1). The same interaction types are present in all four isomers, with the attractive and repulsive regions of comparable magnitude and a smaller van der Waals region; these values reflect density integrals and not energetic contributions. Within this qualitative comparison, the most stable isomers (cis-RS and trans-RS) show the most favorable arrangement: in cis-RS, the attractive region between the Zr centers and ligands (type ②) is somewhat more prominent, while the repulsive regions (types ④ and ⑤) remain limited. These density-based differences are not apparent from the steric analysis alone and complement it in rationalizing the stability ordering.

These findings align with our earlier structural and steric map analyses, demonstrating that the bimetallic complex stability results from a delicate balance of attractive and repulsive forces. The most stable isomers (cis-RS and trans-RS) likely benefit from optimal distribution of these noncovalent interactions, where attractive forces are maximized and repulsive interactions are minimized. This noncovalent interaction network appears to work in concert with the steric and electronic factors discussed earlier to determine the overall stability of these catalytically important dinuclear species.

Conclusion

Using DFT, we mapped the relative stabilities of four configurational isomers of the silicon-bridged bis­(indenyl) bimetallic complex [Ind2ZrMeSiMe2)2(μ-Me)]+ and identified the structural features that distinguish them. The cis-RS isomer is the most stable, followed by trans-RS, trans-RR, and cis-RR. In both the cis and trans series, the heterochiral (RS) combination of the two zirconocene units is more stable than the homochiral (RR) combination. The stability ordering tracks with two concrete features: a larger buried volume (% V bur) around the bridging μ-Me group, which reduces unfavorable contacts between the two halves of the complex, and, separately, a more negative charge on the bridging methyl together with a larger HOMO–LUMO gap. Steric protection of the μ-Me bridge appears to be the dominant factor across the four isomers examined, with the electronic features contributing in addition to, rather than as part of, the steric effect. The NCI analysis is consistent with this picture, showing a comparable balance of attractive and repulsive regions and a smaller dispersion contribution across the isomers.

Because olefin insertion requires dissociation of the bimetallic complex into its mononuclear components, these results bear on catalyst behavior through the stability of the dormant adduct and the ease of its dissociation, not through monomer coordination at the intact complex. Within that scope, the trends suggest that ligand modifications increasing steric protection around the μ-Me bridge, without lengthening the Zr–Zr separation, would stabilize the adduct, and that a more negative charge on the bridging atom would act in the same direction. Because the heterochiral adducts are more stable, a racemic catalyst would form homochiral and heterochiral dormant species in differing amounts, with corresponding effects on the concentration of active mononuclear catalysts. More broadly, the present work provides a sober account of the energy landscape of these μ-Me-bridged bimetallic complexes, clarifying how steric and electronic factors jointly determine their relative stability. Quantifying the energetics of adduct formation from the separated cationic and neutral fragments would be a natural next step, allowing the factors that promote or suppress these dormant species to be addressed directly.
