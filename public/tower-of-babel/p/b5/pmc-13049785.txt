Mental health issues and associated factors amongst healthcare workers in US forensic-correctional settings: a systematic review of literature since the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Eric Yu, Michael Wang, Jayden Berdugo, Shahnawaz Towheed, Jaehyeong Yang, Insiya Moosavi, Sarah Lalji-Mawji, Christopher Scott Czapla, Britta Klara Ostermeyer, Andrew Toyin Olagunju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare professionals provide essential services to populations in the criminal justice system, often at the expense of their own well-being. This review synthesized literature findings on mental health challenges faced by healthcare professionals working in the US forensic-correctional settings since the COVID-19 pandemic. We investigated the prevalence of mental health conditions, their risk-protective factors, the impacts of these mental health issues on workplace retention, and highlighted relevant recommendations.MethodsThis study followed PRISMA guidelines. A comprehensive search of major databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL, and Embase) was conducted and supplemented with citation chaining to identify eligible reports spanning January 1st 2020 up to March 18th, 2025. Article screening, full-text review, and data extraction were completed by two independent investigators. Study quality was assessed using the NIH tool for quantitative studies and the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) framework for qualitative studies.ResultsA total of 10,005 identified reports were screened, with seven fair-to-good eligible studies included in the final review. Both quantitative (n = 4) and qualitative (n = 3) studies were included, and spanned multiple states, with most studies (n = 3, 42.9%) conducted in California. Healthcare workers reported various mental health conditions such as depression (48%), anxiety (18.8–51.1%), sleep disorders (17.4%), burnout (47.2%) and PTSD (49.3%), albeit significant heterogeneity constrains comparative analysis. Qualitatively, workers experienced considerable isolation, personality shifts, and cognitive dissonance. Risk factors predictive of mental health conditions included increased workload (β = 0.18, p < 0.001), workplace conflict (β = 0.15, p < 0.001), female sex (β = 0.10, p = 0.04), younger age, chronic medical conditions (β = 0.09, p = 0.03), fears around COVID-19 (β = 0.14, p < 0.001), and a lack of pandemic safety training (p = 0.033). Protective factors included resilience, administrator and peer support, access to needed resources, and a sense of fulfilment and purpose from working with populations in forensic-correctional settings.ConclusionsSystemic reforms including decreased mandatory overtime, staffing, workload distribution, organizational support, training, improved communication, access to adequate resources and psychosocial interventions may help promote wellness and optimize the ability of healthcare workers to provide care in forensic-correctional settings. However, the preliminary nature of the study findings suggests caution in their interpretations. Further high-quality research is needed to support evidence-informed decision-making and translation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14242-6.

Background

Forensic-correctional healthcare workers provide essential services to populations involved in the criminal justice system, often at the expense of their own mental well-being [1]. The existing literature has thus far shown that healthcare workers in forensic-correctional settings operate in a distinctly challenging environment that may predispose them to stress-related concerns and mental health issues [2]. Their professional roles typically involve working in a high-risk environment, exposure to traumatic events, limited access to resources, and handling the complex needs of a diverse population detained in the criminal justice system [3, 4]. Working among populations involved in the criminal justice system presents distinct challenges, exposing healthcare workers in forensic-correctional settings to unique risks and heightened occupational stress that may negatively impact their mental health and well-being significantly [5].

Nowhere are the above-mentioned issues more pressing than in the United States of America (US, USA). During the COVID-19 pandemic, the US held an estimated 1,827,600 individuals in jail or prison custody as of 2022, which is substantially higher than other high-income Western nations during this time period [6]. Moreover, the US has several unique characteristics, including higher incarceration rates and longer prison sentences, averaging 52 months compared to European Nations which averaged less than 12 months [7], making it necessary to explore the experiences of US healthcare professionals working in the criminal justice system. In the US, a landmark Supreme Court ruling in 1976 made healthcare for individuals in the criminal justice system a legal requirement [8]. In spite of this, the US has notable mental healthcare gaps in forensic-correctional settings, with only 9% of prisons in certain regions in the Southeast having on-site psychiatry staff [8]. Furthermore, US clinicians and mental health staff are often deterred from working in forensic-correctional settings due to stigma around the patient population, perceived complicity in these aforementioned systemic issues, and safety concerns [9]. As such, a synthesis of existing literature on the experiences of US healthcare professionals in the criminal justice system would be beneficial to learn from and inform action plans. Today, the US judicial system relies on tens of thousands of forensic-correctional nurses, psychiatrists, other physicians, psychologists, occupational therapists, social workers, and other allied healthcare professionals to deliver critical medical and biopsychosocial care to individuals in the criminal justice system [3]. However, patients in forensic-correctional settings often have complex and unmet healthcare needs, and together with working in an overwhelming environment, can further strain the healthcare workforce in these settings [8].

More recently, studies have shown that the Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) pandemic, leading to the spread of Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), has significantly intensified the global mental health burden, with healthcare workers bearing a disproportionate share of this impact [9–15]. In the same vein, past outbreaks, such as Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome (SARS) and Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) have demonstrated that frontline healthcare workers faced unique stressors, including extended working hours, heightened emotional strain from treating critically ill patients, and the looming risk of infection for themselves and their families [16, 17]. Moreover, healthcare professionals working during the pandemics frequently face stigma and discrimination due to their perceived exposure to the virus, compounding their experience of isolation and anxiety [18–20]. These compounded pressures during the COVID-19 pandemic likely exacerbated the pre-existing challenges faced by forensic-correctional healthcare workers, who already operate in high-stress environments [8, 18–20]. As such, our team hypothesized that the COVID-19 pandemic likely amplified these stressors, leading to an increased prevalence of diverse mental health concerns and disorders, including depression, anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and burnout, among others, for healthcare workforce in forensic-correctional settings.

Despite the growing awareness of the unique risks and mental health challenges faced by healthcare professionals since COVID-19, limited attention has been given to the unique experiences of US healthcare workers in forensic-correctional facilities since the start of the pandemic. To our knowledge, no study has yet synthesized the existing literature on the mental health of healthcare workers in US forensic-correctional settings since COVID-19. As such, this systematic review aims to address this gap in the literature by synthesizing findings in existing empirical studies on this subject.

In this study, firstly, we seek to summarize findings in extant literature regarding the prevalence and burden of mental health disorders based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [21] or International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [20] guidelines and other mental health issues (including stress-related conditions, anxiety, depression, fatigue, and substance use disorders among others) that are prevalent amongst healthcare professionals working in the US forensic-correctional settings during the defined period. Secondly, we described the risk and protective factors associated with these mental health outcomes in this population of healthcare workers. Thirdly, we highlighted the impacts of these mental health problems on healthcare professionals’ willingness to continue working in forensic-correctional settings by interpreting published narratives. Ultimately, we developed relevant recommendations, including potential policy strategies to promote the mental wellbeing of health professionals in forensic-correctional settings and provided guidance on future research.

Methods

This review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [22]. The review protocol was published in Open Science Framework (10.17605/OSF.IO/ZXVJD).

Eligibility criteria for study selection

We considered studies that examined mental health problems in forensic-correctional healthcare workers in the US since the start of the COVID-19 pandemic. Studies were included based on the criteria that (1) they examined population working within healthcare in forensic and/or correctional settings within the US, (2) the study obtained data for the period spanning January 1st, 2020 to March 18th, 2025 to assess the impacts of the pandemic, and (3) the studies published original empirical data. Studies were excluded if they (1) did not include relevant mental health outcomes (i.e.: depression, anxiety, fatigue, substance use, stress, sleep disturbances, and burnout etc.), (2) only assessed data obtained before January, 2020, or (3) did not publish empirical data as they were conference abstracts, or lacked full texts. There were no language or study design restrictions in our selection criteria.

Search strategies and information sources

A systematic search was conducted to identify potential articles in major databases (including PubMed/MEDLINE, PsycINFO, Web of Science, CINAHL, and Embase) published from January 1st, 2020 up to March 18th, 2025. We used a search strategy that included Medical Subject Headings and keywords, which were placed into the following categories: (1) Mental Health Conditions, (2) Forensic-Correctional Healthcare Workers, and (3) Correctional Workers. Search strategy

Citation chaining of relevant articles and references of included articles and systematic reviews was also completed to identify additional studies. Study authors were contacted where necessary to request access to their full texts or data.

All identified articles were exported into Covidence and duplicates were removed. Screening of title, abstract and full-text review of all identified articles was then conducted by at least two independent investigators and conflicts were resolved by discussion between the investigators or with consultation with the senior author on the paper where necessary.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data extraction from eligible reports was completed by at least two independent investigators and conflicts were resolved by discussion between the investigators and with consultation with the senior author where necessary. Data extraction focused on narrative synthesis using framework and guiding principles underpinning thematic grouping and outcome-driven synthesis [22–24]. Study information was collected with a data extraction table including items such as the name of the author, year of publication, characteristics of the study population, study design, setting of the study, mental health problems/outcomes (e.g., depression, anxiety, burnout, sleep problems, fatigue, substance abuse, and other mental health issues), and other findings or recommendations. The data extracted on main findings included the prevalence of mental health problems, associated factors, scales used for assessments, information on outcomes and specific study-defined interventions. Heterogeneity in professional roles and facilities was managed conceptually by synthesizing and grouping narrative themes using shared experiences across professions. Themes were identified across studies based on reported stressors, mental health outcomes, and protective factors, allowing narrative synthesis despite variation in roles and settings. The extracted information addressed most specific aims of this systematic review, though unfortunately no study trialed any specific interventions or reported data on assessments of substance abuse.

Appraisal of study quality

A quality assessment was conducted for each study included in the final review, including both qualitative and quantitative studies. Quality assessment (risk of bias assessment) evaluated the methodological rigor and potential biases of each included study, informing the interpretation of the review’s overall findings [23, 24]. The quality assessment tools were applied to assess study design, conduct, and reporting, thereby determining the strength and reliability of the aggregated results for evidence-based decision-making. For qualitative studies, the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) framework [25] was utilized and quantitative studies were assessed with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Study Quality Assessment Tools [26]. These scales evaluated each article on a range of 10 or 14 items respectively. The items unable to be assessed were considered as not reported or met the criteria. Items not applicable were dropped from the final calculation. The quality of each study was then systematically classified into three categories: poor (below 40%), fair (40–75%), and good (above 75%), based on the percentage of the criteria met. The scores on quality evaluation were calculated by dividing the positive responses by the total criteria used. Two authors conducted independent evaluations, and the average of their scores was used for the final assessment.

Additional assessment was conducted on Gangemi et al. [3] to highlight significant concerns based on other factors (e.g., limited sample size relative to multiple analysis conducted, no adjustment for multiple testing, and clustering of data around a single site) not fully captured by the CASP and NIH frameworks. Both scoring authors agreed that this particular study lacked the necessary rigor, and in consultation with the senior author was rated as fair in quality.

Results

Screening and selection of eligible articles

In total, 12,693 reports were identified from database search, with 10,005 articles remaining after the removal of duplicates. Screening of titles and abstracts produced 167 studies for full-text review. Of these, seven studies were included for data extraction in the final report [2, 3, 27–31]. Study characteristics

The included studies (n = 7) spanned various US states, including California, Colorado, Missouri, Maryland, New York, Pennsylvania, and West Virginia, with 42.9% of the research (n = 3) and 34.0% of the overall study population (n = 365) located in California [3, 27, 28]. Included studies were of both quantitative (57.1%, n = 4) and qualitative (42.9%, n = 3) design. Of the quantitative studies, two were descriptive cross sectional (25.6%, n = 2) and two were comparative cross-sectional studies (25.6%, n = 2) [2, 3, 28, 30]. The comparative studies compared healthcare workers in forensic-correctional settings working during the pandemic with healthcare workers in general settings during both the pandemic and pre-pandemic period, as well as non-forensic-correctional healthcare workers [2, 3]. All qualitative studies (42.9%, n = 3) were descriptive and cross sectional in nature. Two used a general descriptive approach (25.6%, n = 2), while one used a specific phenomenological lens (n = 1, 14.3%) [27, 28, 31]. Sample sizes in the included studies ranged widely (n = 8 to 486), with a total of 1073 healthcare professionals assessed across all included studies.

Analyzed participants came from diverse healthcare professionals’ occupational backgrounds, including psychiatrists, other physicians, social workers, psychologists, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, and nurses (n = 870). Some studies investigated specific sub-groups including nurses working in prisons (n = 181) [3, 27, 29], and mental health professionals with graduate degrees (n = 22) [27]. The majority of participants were female across studies where sex was reported (74.4%), and the weighted average age across all studies that reported this variable was 45.16 years (n = 587). Burhanullah et al. reported the mean age and sex distribution of their total population [2]. 40% of the participants were between the ages of 35–49 years, and 76.3% of participants were females [2]. Main study findings

Mental health outcomes and assessment tools used in quantitative studies

The mental health outcomes reported across studies varied, with a notable prevalence of depression, anxiety, PTSD, burnout and sleep problems among the study population. The construct of the mental health outcomes measured in the included studies are significantly heterogeneous, making comparison between studies limited and warranting narrative descriptive as we have done below, although there is a need for caution in the interpretation. A range of assessment tools, including the nine and four-item versions of Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) [2] and (PHQ-4) [3] respectively were used to measure levels of depression. To measure levels of anxiety in participants, the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) [2, 3, 30] and a study-defined questionnaire with Likert rating of items [26] were used. The two studies that screened for PTSD used different screening methods including the Impact of Event Scale - Revised (IES-R) [2] and the Abbreviated PTSD Checklist (PCL) [3]. Other validated scales used include the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI-2) [32], the Effort-Reward Imbalance Scale, study-defined questionnaires with Likert rating scales for burnout [2, 3, 28] as well as the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) for sleep disorder assessment [2, 3]. A description of findings in each of the included studies is summarized below and additional details are presented in Table 2.

Burhanullah et al. [2] reported clinical depression in 48% of forensic-correctional mental health staff compared to 32% of forensic-correctional officers in a multi-state study in the Eastern US. Of the total sample, 22.33% of healthcare workers had moderate-severe depression on PHQ-9. The study also reported that 37% of healthcare staff had clinical anxiety, with 12.84% having moderate-severe symptoms, rated with GAD-7. Using the IES-R, 49.3% had mild to severe PTSD symptoms, with 20.29% having moderate to severe symptoms. Additionally, 47.2% had high burnout, and 17.4% met the criteria for a sleep disorder, rated with PROMIS [2]. Guardiano et al. [3] found that 16% of forensic-correctional nurses working in California prisons had depression and 18.75% had anxiety based on assessment with PHQ-4. Furthermore, there were high mean scores on their burnout scale (9.62 effort to reward imbalance) and average sleep decreased from 6.60 to 5.36 h per evening, which was a net loss of 1.24 h from pre-pandemic levels. In total, 49% of nurses in the prison experienced clinical PTSD during the pandemic [3]. In their study, Leach et al. [28] reported significant stress and anxiety among various California forensic-correctional healthcare workers using a study-defined questionnaire with items rated on a seven-point Likert scale. This study noted scores of 5.9 for workplace and 5.5 for home stresses since the onset of the pandemic. They further noted a rise of 2.4 points in burnout and fatigue (3.3 to 5.7, p < 0.001) in the participant populations since the start of the pandemic [28]. Finally, Tomkus et al. [30] noted that various forensic-correctional healthcare workers in Missouri had mild (24%), moderate (16.7%) and severe (10.4%) anxiety on the GAD-7. This amounted to a net total of 51.1% prevalence of anxiety among the participating staff, with 27.1% of workers suffering from moderate-severe anxiety [30].

Mental health outcome from qualitative studies

Keller et al. [27] found that forensic-correctional nurses had increased stress leading to anxiety, sleep disturbances, hypertension, and gastrointestinal (GI) comorbidities. This survey also noted significant burnout leading to fatigue, exhaustion, weight gain, migraines, and emotional withdrawal. Finally, 75% of the nurses endorsed the negative social consequences of working in forensic-correctional settings, such as an increase in social withdrawal and a strained homelife and relationships due to the uncertain nature of their work scheduling and timeframes [27]. Gangemi [29] discovered that masters and doctoral professionals (psychologists) in forensic-correctional mental health reported significant pushback from other staff when attempting to empathize with inmates (59%), feelings of isolation from their loved ones (72.7%), and personality and outlook shifts (36%). A significant but unspecified number of participants in this study reported PTSD symptoms from workplace trauma exposure [29]. In semi-structured interviews, Cukale-Matos & Champion [29] highlighted that 87.5% of nurses in forensic-correctional facilities were experiencing unique stressors as they navigated the dilemma of viewing inmates as patients and vice-versa. Notably, 50% of these nursing staff reported both personality shifts, and outlook changes since working in corrections [29].

Study-defined risk factors for mental health conditions

Across the included studies, several recurrent risk factors (listed in relevant subcategories below) were identified for the observed burden of mental disorder, psychological distress, and burnout. Please see Table 2 for fulsome details.

I) Increased workload demands

Excessive job requirements were reported across multiple studies, often exceeding manageable levels for individual healthcare professionals. For example, quantitatively, nurses working in prison experienced significantly higher work hours (54.73 weekly, p < 0.0001) compared to community nurses, and there was a significant reduction in their sleep hours (5.36 nightly, p < 0.0001) [3]. Along the same vein, Keller et al. highlighted the qualitative effects of the higher work burden, noting that long and mandatory overtime shifts and poor staffing further increased stress and burnout among nurses in forensic-correctional settings [27]. Importantly, uncertain shift schedules had a deleterious effect on nurses’ social and home life. Increased workload demands affected other forensic-correctional healthcare workers as well. Being asked to stay late (rs = 0.41, p < 0.001) was associated with increased clinical anxiety prevalence [30] and concerns around staffing (5.6, IQR = 4–7) correlated with increased workplace stress, burnout, and fatigue [28]. Moreover, chronic understaffing and increased workload (β = 0.18, p < 0.001) correlated with depression, anxiety, burnout, and PTSD [2]. Across both quantitative and qualitative studies, there was frustration around staffing ratios, long working hours, and heavy workload [2, 3, 27, 28].

II) Workplace-related concerns and professional issues

Besides increased workload, several workplace related issues, including concern with places of employment were reported by forensic-correctional healthcare professionals. Quantitatively, increased workplace conflict (β = 0.15, p < 0.001) correlated with clinical anxiety, depression, and PTSD [2]. Qualitatively, a lack of trust between nurses and correctional staff [29], and poor nurse-management support and relationships [27] were identified. Other mental health professionals noted an ‘us-versus-them’ mentality with correctional officers alongside distrust in clinical supervisors. Up to 63.6% endorsed feelings of betrayal by the system [29].

Direct conflicts with inmate populations were also noted to deleteriously impact healthcare worker mental health. Witnessing inter-inmate violence was noted to be a unique stressor alongside uncontrolled physical contact during physical exams and procedures [2]. Hearing about violent or sexual actions that inmates had conducted during interviews was also noted to be a significant contributor to vicarious trauma symptoms [29].

Profession and location-specific concerns were also noted. For example, a higher prevalence of anxiety was reported by healthcare professionals working in state/federal prisons or county jails (35.1%) compared to those in immigration detention centers (15.4%) [30]. Interestingly, there was increased burnout, fatigue, and stress in participants who are registered compared to unregistered nurses (6.2 vs. 5.7, p = 0.012) [28].

III) Shifting personal values

Working in forensic-correctional healthcare settings was linked with distressing changes in both behaviour and personal values among the study population, especially significant concerns around work-related behavioural shifts [28]. Qualitatively, nursing professionals in forensic-correctional settings described an emotional hardening effect on both themselves and their coworkers; characterized by diminished empathy, relational difficulties, and reduced ability to connect with patients meaningfully [29]. This study further attested that this shift was brought on through unique workplace stressors, including lack of resources and the burden of being unable to trust one’s patients. In total, 36% of forensic-correctional mental health professionals attested that their worldviews and personalities had become harsher [29].

IV) Cognitive dissonance

Qualitative studies emphasized the risks of cognitive dissonance. Cukale-Matos and Champion [29] noted that nurses reported emotional discomfort and cognitive dissonance when required to suppress their emotions and struggled to maintain a caring demeanor toward individuals known to have committed crimes, likely due to difficulty separating an inmate’s identity from that of a patient [29]. On the other hand, attempting to perform therapeutic mental health interventions and care in a correctional system designed to punish inmates also leads to burnout and a sense of hopelessness. When attempting to empathize or care for inmates, 59% of forensic-correctional mental health staff found significant pushback from their correctional officer colleagues, who accused them of being overly sensitive [29].

V) Demographic and personal risk factors

Quantitative studies noted that female sex was a statistically significant predictor for anxiety (β = 0.10, p = 0.04) [2, 28, 30], stress [30] and lower resilience (β=-0.14, p = 0.008) [2]. Furthermore, younger age was significantly correlated with mental health burdens including depression, anxiety, and burnout (β=-0.10, p = 0.02) [2, 30]. The presence of chronic health conditions was identified as a broad risk factor for depression (β = 0.09, p = 0.03) and PTSD (β = 0.09, p = 0.03) during the pandemic [2]. For instance, cardiac comorbidities (U = 23.0, p = 0.031), prior obesity (U = 1,957, p = 0.003), and receiving behavioural health services correlated with anxiety symptoms among the assessed healthcare professionals [30].

VI) Pandemic-related challenges

Pandemic-related factors, including fear of the pandemic (β = 0.14, p < 0.001) and concerns around the well-being of coworkers who had been diagnosed with COVID-19 (4.9, IQR = 4–6) were positively correlated with overall worse mental health outcomes [2, 28]. Knowing a coworker was hospitalized significantly worsened anxiety scores (p < 0.001) [30]. Participants who desired resignation due to the pandemic had significantly higher anxiety scores [30]. Direct contact with COVID-19-positive patients (91.4%, p < 0.0001) and general exposure to the virus worsened stress and burnout [3, 28]. Numerous studies found that improper access to personal protective equipment (PPE) was associated with a higher mental burden, while anxiety scores increased (p = 0.033) if there was inadequate or improper education around PPE [28, 29]. Even positive preventative steps taken by institutions to address the pandemic often had deleterious mental health effects. For instance, enforced institutional isolation practices (β = 0.17, p < 0.001) worsened mental health burden, while the implementation of a patient immunization program (p = 0.035) significantly raised anxiety scores [2, 30].

Study-defined protective factors for mental health conditions

Several protective factors that directly mitigated the impact of occupational stress and burnout were identified in the included studies and elaborated below.

I) Fostering resilience

One key protective factor was resilience, described as the ability to perform and succeed in stressful situations [33]. Approximately, 55% of forensic-correctional healthcare workers had high resilience on the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale 2 (CD-RISC2), allowing them to thrive in their positions while mitigating psychological distress. Notably, resilience scores were quantitatively higher in males and older healthcare professionals [2].

II) Pride, fulfilment and purpose from work

The qualitative studies identified the perception of fulfillment and pride from working among the population in forensic-correctional settings as a protective factor among healthcare professionals. For instance, all nurses in a study cited their recognition of how important their work was and the satisfaction of helping their patients as significant pull factors for retention in forensic-correctional healthcare work [29]. Closely related is that a significant number of psychologists in forensic-correctional settings reported that their “work-related sufferings or stress” were offset by the rewarding experience of helping their clients [29].

III) Peer and administrative support

One qualitative study noted that peer-led support groups to debrief shared experiences and mitigate feelings of isolation and helplessness were identified as protective against mental health issues [29]. Again, feelings of companionship and friendship with fellow staff members were highlighted as key factors for job retention [29].

IV) Access to appropriate resources, including PPE

Adequate PPE was noted as a necessity by healthcare workers across the majority of the included studies [3, 28, 29]. Importantly, access to sufficient PPE was shown to significantly reduce anxiety (rs=-0.19, p = 0.008) among healthcare workers [30].

Interventions and strategies to mitigate mental health burden

Across the included studies (n = 7), no intervention or treatment trials were reported, albeit a number of strategies with protective effects against mental health issues were identified. Importantly, some studies suggested reworking overtime policies to address mandatory overtime, staffing, and workload distribution [3, 27]. Improvement in staffing ratios, limiting the number of hours per shifts (e.g. 12 h or less), and implementing self-scheduling [27] were recommended. The creation of a healthy work environment that promotes organizational transparency, mentorship, validation and support for health care providers from administrative-management staff was canvassed [27, 28]. Open forums were suggested to facilitate practitioners to articulate their experiences and seek support without stigma [29]. Updates were deemed necessary around communication of ethical guidelines and standards for mental health professionals. Integrating civilian oversight and consulting individuals with lived and learned experiences was also recommended [29].

Further recommendations centered in-depth education for nursing students around a career in forensic-correctional nursing and implementing opportunities for debriefing and reflection following traumatic or difficult events [31]. Trauma-informed care and mindfulness interventions should be offered through routine training and policy implementation and be accessible online if necessary [3, 29]. Clear communication about changes in workplace pandemic protocols and addressed worker concerns over potential administrative legal action over poor COVID-19 patient outcomes was very important [34]. Studies recommended improving efficiency regarding COVID-19 testing to less than 6 h [34]. For studies that listed it as a protective factor, all cited the importance of acquiring adequate PPE [3, 28, 30]. Finally, studies recommended that future research be conducted to mitigate the effects of the next pandemic [4, 29, 30], involve numerous facilities with healthcare professionals around the country [3, 29], and involve more robust qualitative methods to better capture the full spectrum of causes of mental burden [27].

Assessment of study quality

The quality of included studies (n = 7) was assessed by two independent reviewers (inter-reviewer agreement = 88.4%). The quality assessment determined all studies to be of either good (n = 1) or fair (n = 6) quality. Among the quantitative studies (n = 4), several concerns were frequently noted which include participation rate of eligible persons (n = 3), a lack of sample size justification or power description (n = 4) and a lack of statistical adjustment to control for confounding factors (n = 3). All quantitative studies were cross-sectional resulting in data collected from a single period of time and lack of follow up. Two quantitative studies have possible timing bias and there is a concern around the limited validity of some of the COVID-19 related measures and the use of generic tools. Assessment of qualitative studies (n = 3) found concerns relating to rigorousness of sample recruitment strategies (n = 2) and limited consideration of potential researcher bias (n = 2). One study is an extension of a parent study and only includes data from three optional, open-ended, written questions. Furthermore, three of the included studies collected data from only one US prison, suggesting a potential source of bias due to limited geographical clustering of populations and heavy emphasis on California. Discussion

This systematic review provides an in-depth synthesis of extant literature investigating the mental health issues among forensic-correctional healthcare workers in the United States, focusing on the peri-pandemic period. Several important findings are highlighted in this review, including the amplifying effects of COVID-19 pandemic on the inherent stressors, risks and mental health burden experienced by healthcare professionals caring for individuals in the criminal justice system. Overall, this study’s results are contextually comparable to existing literature highlighting the mental health vulnerabilities of healthcare professionals working in high-stress, high-risk environments and underscore the need for context specific and scalable solutions [35, 36]. Of the current included literature, 34.0% of the overall study population (n = 365) was located in California [3, 27, 29], resulting in limited geographical clustering of populations and a potential source of bias. It is important to note that unlike the rest of the USA, California’s prison population has continued to decline throughout the pandemic, decreasing by 23% and maintaining this trend in recent review [37]. On the other hand, the total number of individuals in US prisons decreased by 14% at the beginning of the pandemic but the number had started to increase since 2022 with as much as 2% increase [6].

Burden of mental health conditions among healthcare workers

Findings across the included studies underscored elevated levels of depression, anxiety, PTSD, burnout, and sleep disturbances among healthcare professionals in forensic-correctional facilities in the US. Notwithstanding, the heterogenous nature of the instruments and cut-off scores used across the included studies to assess mental health issues represents a limitation that constrained comparability.

A disturbingly high burden of mental health conditions was reported among forensic-correctional healthcare professionals across the included studies [2, 3, 27–31]. For example, the reported rate of depression was high, as approximately one in every two healthcare professionals in a forensic-correctional setting had depression compared to about one in every three correctional officers [2]. The rate of depression in healthcare professionals working in forensic-correctional settings is markedly elevated. Interestingly, the estimated prevalence among healthcare workers globally during the COVID-19 pandemic across different settings was significantly elevated [30], albeit these findings need to be interpreted contextually and with caution. Symptoms of anxiety were similarly pervasive, with about 18.8% to 51.1% of healthcare workers having anxiety [2, 30]. Notably, PTSD emerged as another striking concern with about half (49.3%) of healthcare workers having mild to severe PTSD [2] and a disaggregated analysis on nurses showed a similar prevalence rate for moderate to severe PTSD [2, 3]. Lower prevalence rates for PTSD, at 22% and 34%, have been reported in other forensic-correctional staff and correctional officers working in the US in previous studies [36, 38, 39]. Lastly, our findings indicate that over a sixth of mental health staff meet the criteria for a sleep disorder [2], with an average of about 75 min decrease in sleep duration reported in forensic-correctional nurses working during the pandemic [3]. Taken together, the results from this systematic review suggest that the COVID-19 pandemic created a uniquely salient environment for exposure to stress and trauma among forensic-correctional healthcare workers.

The aforementioned findings are built on a significant level of occupational stress and diminished well-being, which was already observed among general setting healthcare workers in the US prior to the COVID-pandemic [40]. Forensic-correctional healthcare workers have been shown to have high levels of mental health burden, including elevated levels of occupational and psychological stress and burnout, which are often linked to constant exposure to disturbing social issues and managing clients with challenging behaviours [41, 42]. These findings suggest the pandemic may have further magnified pre-existing challenges faced by forensic-correctional healthcare workers. Elevated mental health burden among healthcare workers in forensic-correctional settings can have profound ramifications on staff, patients, and society as a whole. The effects of depression include impaired cognitive functioning, decreased motivation and difficulty with concentration [43]. In the workplace, these depressive symptoms can impair work performance, decrease productivity and increase absences, all of which can correlate with the severity of depression [44, 45]. A high level of stress, and depression in healthcare workers have also been linked with poor interest in work related tasks and performance, which can significantly compromise their ability to perform clinical tasks and responsibilities effectively and may affect patient care and safety [46]. Similarly, anxiety can decrease cognitive function, attention, coordination and problem-solving abilities, all of which can impact the quality healthcare professionals deliver [47]. Anxiety was heightened among healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic, especially for those caring for patients on the front lines, such as forensic-correctional healthcare workers. One in five individuals in state and federal prisons tested positively for COVID-19, a rate that was four times as high as the general population [48]. Hence, healthcare workers were exposed to additional vulnerability due to the fear of contracting COVID-19 or becoming a carrier to the people close to them and limited availability of PPE in some settings [47]. Such elevated levels of anxiety are associated with increased risk of occupational injuries, illnesses, and burnout among others [49].

Another study-defined contributor to the mental health burden in healthcare professionals was the significantly increased exposure to traumatic events, such as violence and disturbing patient behaviours, leading to a high burden of PTSD [38, 39, 50]. Exposure to traumatic events can often result in adverse outcomes in healthcare workers such as burnout, compassion fatigue, secondary trauma, increased staff turnover, and poorer psychological and physical wellbeing [51]. Additionally, the violent behaviours of patients, ranging from verbal abuse to physical aggression, pose a serious risk to safety and can trigger psychological distress among healthcare workers [52, 53]. Closely linked is the personal conflict many healthcare workers face in ensuring organization policies of strict enforcement of custody and rules while providing care and treatment to clients in detention [39, 50]. This conflict between personal principles and policy requirements from the healthcare system provide the perfect trigger for moral injury; a form of psychological distress characterized by feelings of guilt and shame related to acts that defy one’s moral values [50]. In general, healthcare workers in secure and forensic-correctional psychiatric settings face greater risk for moral injury compared to other healthcare professionals given the unique moral challenges faced on a daily basis in their workplace [54].

The substantial rates of burnout (characterized by emotional, physical and mental exhaustion, often due to excessive and prolonged stress) among healthcare workers in this study is not surprising given the cumulative effects of the mental health burden observed [2, 3, 29]. Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) notes that nearly half of healthcare workers reported feeling burnt out and intended to look for new work in 2022 compared to roughly a third in 2018 [55]. Among professionals working in corrections, emotional exhaustion has been identified as the most common manifestation of burnout [56]. Common consequences of burnout in healthcare professionals include decreased life satisfaction, increased work turnover and higher levels of depersonalization [57]. Furthermore, burnout can increase the vulnerability to develop physical and mental health problems, potentially leading to a vicious cycle between burnout and mental health burden [39].

Factors associated with mental health conditions among healthcare workers

A diverse range of risk factors were associated with elevated mental health burden among forensic-correctional healthcare workers [2, 3, 29, 30]. Prominent among these factors are increased workload demands, workplace conflicts, shifting personal values, cognitive dissonance, demographic factors, and specific pandemic-related challenges. However, the studies also found several protective factors that could prevent adverse mental health conditions from occurring or worsening.

An increased risk of stress and anxiety was reported in female forensic-correctional healthcare workers [4, 27, 30]. Several studies have shown that female workers are more likely to be diagnosed with mental health conditions including PTSD, GAD, panic disorder, agoraphobia, and specific phobias compared to their male counterparts [58–60]. Similarly, younger age was consistently linked with higher levels of anxiety, which may be linked to increased vulnerability or less experience coping in high stress situations [2, 30]. Studies found that individuals with prior chronic mental health conditions experienced greater mental health challenges including PTSD and depression [2]. These results suggest that the stress from the work environment may have amplified their pre-existing mental health issues [61]. Similarly, studies found higher GAD-7 scores for individuals with cardiac comorbidities, and obesity, suggesting that physical health may also play a role in mental well-being in stressful environments [30].

Working in a forensic-correctional setting brings about unique challenges and stressors. Almost every study highlighted stressors directly tied to the workplace environment, including longer hours, staffing shortages, increased night shifts, and unpredictable schedules, shed light on a shared exhausting experience among forensic-correctional workers [3, 4, 27, 29, 30]. Furthermore, forensic-correctional nurses experience unique stresses compared to other healthcare settings. In traditional workplace environments, nurses are taught to believe their patients, however, in a forensic-correctional environment nurses often cannot fully trust the patient’s histories as numerous factors may limit accuracy [31]. Some inmates may malinger by altering their behaviour, or details about themselves to receive special treatment, adding an additional layer of stress to this workforce [31].

One of the primary concerns of health professionals was fear of getting infected with COVID-19 [3, 4, 29]. Not only did forensic-correctional healthcare providers worry about their own health, but they also expressed concern about the health of their co-workers who had been diagnosed with COVID-19 [29]. This fear had an impact extending beyond immediate health concerns and characterized a major push factor for many healthcare workers. Participants who resigned or considered resigning from their positions due to the pandemic had higher anxiety levels than those who did not resign nor consider resigning [30]. Another major pandemic-related factor that contributed to anxiety among forensic-correctional workers was access to and use of PPE. Anxiety levels were greater in healthcare workers who were not taught proper donning and doffing techniques, and improving access and availability to necessary PPE was an important way to reduce pandemic-related anxiety in forensic-correctional facilities [29, 30]. Both of these studies emphasized the importance of providing appropriate PPE and ensuring staff receive adequate training.

A significant amount of study participants expressed their frustration with a system that claims it wants to help but ultimately falls short. This seems to stem from the negativity in the forensic-correctional environment, much of which they attributed to institutional correctional policies [29]. Several other studies also highlight a decrease in empathy and a broader shift in personal identity and values resulting from forensic-correctional work [27, 31]. This suggests that working in a forensic-correctional environment affects individuals’ mental health while also shaping their overall identity.

While a substantial number of risk factors was identified, this review also highlights a range of protective factors that can buffer against occupational stress and improve overall well-being among forensic-correctional healthcare workers. Resilience was an important factor in mitigating stress and anxiety levels in participants. Mirroring previous statements on demographic risk factors, it was noted that resilience was higher in male participants as well as older participants [2]. However, this may pose a double-edged sword as data shows continued stigma for seeking care amongst this population. One study showed that only a minority of the one in ten men that experienced depression sought help [58]. In general, higher levels of resilience have been associated with increased job satisfaction and purpose [62]. Many forensic-correctional healthcare workers have a sense of pride and purpose in their job. Patients in forensic-correctional systems often do not get the care they need, so providing this essential service for them is seen as rewarding [29, 31]. Generally, having a sense of purpose in a job is associated with decreased stress [63]. Individuals with a stronger sense of purpose at work may be better equipped to manage stressors, preventing them from disrupting their life [63]. When former forensic-correctional healthcare workers looked back on their job, some of them felt grateful for the experience they had and the skills they learned [29]. Although numerous study participants described their work experiences as difficult, many found it enriching, constituting a major pull factor.

Implications and recommendations

Findings in this review need to be interpreted with caution due to the preliminary nature of these results. Implicitly, the study findings in this review underscores the potential amplifying effects of COVID-19 pandemic on the mental health burden experienced by healthcare professionals in forensic-correctional settings, and revisits the urgent need for scalable solutions to promote wellness among health workforce in these settings. Although, none of the studies in the review trialed specific interventions to target the mental health of forensic-correctional healthcare workers, the findings in the review highlight important insight into promising directions for future research to generate quality evidence towards fostering wellness and addressing mental health risks associated with working in these settings.

Chronic understaffing, extended shifts, excess overtime, and unpredictable schedules emerged as prominent stressors both before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, leading to worsened mental health outcomes [3, 4, 27, 30, 64]. To address this, we hypothesize reworking of overtime policies while maintaining equitable nurse-to-patient ratios in forensic-correctional institutions may promote staff mental health and wellbeing. Similarly, increasing the budget for additional healthcare workers can help improve staffing ratios and lower the amount of required shift hours. We envisage that the above-mentioned action plan may help preserve work-life balance and mitigate the associated burnout, sleep disturbances, and feelings of diminished well-being. Closely related is that implementation of self-scheduling, whenever possible, can allow staff collaboration, strengthen cohesion, facilitate effective teamwork, and ensure continuity of care [27, 30].

A high prevalence of burnout and poor retention of healthcare workers were observed [13]. Hence, we hypothesize that recruitment and retention incentives are needed with a consequently stronger administrative push towards shifting the workplace cultures and systematic reformations. Implementing policies that encourage paid mental health leave and culture campaigns that prioritize well-being may be beneficial. One such campaign could involve improving staff break policy and rest areas. We hypothesize that by adding relaxing amenities such as gyms, yoga rooms, sleeping pods, as well as technologies such as virtual reality and gaming systems, the workplace may become more adaptable and facilitate staff bonding. Effective vertical and horizontal communications to help with unresolved conflicts may also prove beneficial to wellness. To break existing harmful cultural paradigms, there is need to implement transparent and effective communication from institutional leadership on the importance of mindfulness, wellness, and what is expected of each worker. Another key recommendation we wholeheartedly endorse is integrating civilian oversight and consulting individuals with lived and learned experiences [29].

Across the included studies, authors underscored the need to foster a more psychologically safe climate, where staff feel encouraged to discuss their mental health concerns without stigma [27–29]. This reinforced the potential benefits of organizational-led and informal peer support groups to promote debriefing sessions following stressful incidents [29, 31]. These supports may allow staff to better process high-stress incidents and share coping strategies. Institutions that normalized these conversations might create safer environments, encouraging staff to address mental health challenges proactively. Capacity development and training for healthcare workers are needed to better manage the lasting effects of traumatic events [29, 31]. In this respect, trauma-informed curricula may be beneficial, particularly given the recurrent exposure of health professionals to violence and other distressing events. Furthermore, there is a need for improved access to mental health training and psychological support using both physical and digital resources [27, 29]. Such training could incorporate modules on trauma-informed care, resilience-building, and effective de-escalation techniques to better equip workers for unique challenges in their settings. Importantly, individualized training protocols and simulation-based learning, followed by structured debriefing sessions after critical incidents have shown good evidence to foster a reflective practice to improve clinical competence and support psychological recovery [65]. Beyond debriefing, findings in this review indicates the potential benefits of proactive routine mental health screening and early intervention programs [3, 4, 64], although more research evidence is needed. Recent novel technologies such as mental health apps, and screening through digital format to expedite this process can be leveraged.

Finally, the rapid shift in infection control measures during the COVID-19 pandemic added another layer of stress, underscoring the need for pandemic-specific adaptations [3, 66]. The beneficial role of adequate supply and training on the use of PPE cannot be overemphasized [3, 29, 30]. Again, transparent administrative communication regarding testing availability and infection rates may alleviate confusion and fear [28]. Although these strategies are more applicable in the context of pandemic-related challenges, constructive institutional reforms can be beneficial in the long term, helping healthcare workforce be better prepared for the next pandemic. Implementing these changes may better safeguard the mental well-being of forensic-correctional healthcare staff, allowing them to better serve incarcerated individuals.

Study limitations and next steps

Despite the importance of the findings in this review, a number of shortcomings are noted. First the mental health outcomes measured across the studies are not exhaustive and some of the findings are preliminary. For instance, no study looked at substance use disorders among this population and there is limited information on drivers of vicarious trauma. It is also important to highlight that more needs to be done to improve study quality through the use of standardized mental health assessment tools and use of robust-diverse samples to improve generalizability of the findings in future studies. Other limitations include the need for better reporting and analysis, controls for potential confounding factors, and conducting longitudinal research to address the constraints around causal inferences associated with cross-sectional studies.

Again, despite the identification of numerous risk and protective factors, gaps remain in the literature concerning the long-term efficacy of interventions targeting these risks to mitigate the mental health burdens. The strength of the conclusions is limited by the relatively small number of included studies, comprising only four quantitative and three qualitative papers. Moreover, most studies relied on cross-sectional designs and heterogeneous methodologies, warranting caution in the interpretation of the findings and limiting causal inferences. The scarcity of longitudinal studies further restricts the ability to assess trajectories of mental health outcomes over time. Additional limitations, including suboptimal reporting, limited precision, restriction in the contexts covered as most of the studies were set in California, and inadequate control for potential confounding factors, further limit the generalizability of the findings. Accordingly, caution is warranted when interpreting the results of this review, and these limitations should be addressed in future evidence-based research.

Additionally, inconsistencies in intervention protocols across institutions make it difficult to generalize findings on workplace best practices for widespread implementation. Analyzed institutions had wide organizational heterogeneity, given the broadly varying processes for management across different US states. This would pose a significantly large barrier to implementation. Of note, none of the studies assessed or investigated healthcare workers operating overseas US prisons including military detention facilities.

Future research on this subject should prioritize multi-site longitudinal studies employing validated tools to assess mental health burden and standardized intervention frameworks to better evaluate the long-term impacts of protective strategies and refine policies for forensic-correctional healthcare environments. These studies should consider the heterogeneity of forensic-correctional settings across different US state lines and consider unique professional roles. Specific studies to test the long-term efficacy of strategies and interventions aimed at reducing occupational stress and burnout are indicated. Such research will be crucial in informing evidence-based initiatives and guidelines to protect the mental wellbeing of workers in these high-risk settings and promote retention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review highlights a potentially high mental health burden and related concerns among healthcare workers in forensic-correctional settings, which may adversely affect their well-being, workplace cohesion, and the quality of care. However, the overall quality of the evidence remains limited. Hence, there is need for caution in the interpretation and generalizability of the findings.

While multiple risk factors that may exacerbate these challenges were identified, several potential protective factors were also described, offering direction for the development of targeted interventions to enhance workforce resilience and psychological health. Given the limited quality of the available evidence and the exploratory nature of the included studies, these conclusions are preliminary and underscore the need for further high-quality research to support evidence-informed decision-making.

Overall, it is imperative to understand the experiences of healthcare workers in forensic-correctional settings to determine the best ways to provide support. Identifiable risk factors for mental health issues may help stratify at-risk populations for targeted intervention. Finally, there is a need for systemic changes to protect the mental health of health professionals and future high-quality empirical research is needed.
