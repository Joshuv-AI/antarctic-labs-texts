Appropriateness of perioperative albumin administration in gastrointestinal tumor surgery patients without hypoalbuminemia: a propensity score-matched study

Introduction

Human albumin (HA) is a widely used colloid solution in perioperative care because of its physiological roles in maintaining plasma oncotic pressure, facilitating substance transport, modulating oxidative and inflammatory responses, regulating coagulation, and supporting tissue repair [1, 2]. These properties have contributed to its extensive use in surgical and perioperative settings in humans. However, accumulating evidence suggests that HA is frequently prescribed outside the guideline-recommended indications. A study evaluating the rationality of HA administration in patients with tumors demonstrated that only 5.7% of prescriptions complied with the indication criteria specified in the guidelines of the Italian Society of Transfusion Medicine and Immunohematology (SIMTI) [3]. Given its high acquisition cost, inappropriate HA use is associated with a substantial increase in healthcare expenditures [4]. Observational reports from routine clinical practice have similarly identified the frequent perioperative use of HA, which is not fully aligned with guideline recommendations for surgical patients [5–7]. In response to growing concerns regarding inappropriate prescribing, regulatory authorities have increasingly emphasized the need for strengthened oversight and clearer criteria for HA use in clinical practice [8].

Most clinical guidelines and consensus statements do not explicitly recommend routine HA administration in surgical patients [9–12]. Existing recommendations generally restrict HA use to selected clinical scenarios, such as critically ill patients undergoing abdominal surgery when serum albumin (ALB) concentrations are ≤ 25 or 30 g/L [2, 13]. In patients undergoing major surgical procedures (e.g., extensive hepatic or intestinal resection), HA may be considered only after adequate volume resuscitation when ALB concentrations fall below 20 g/L [3]. Despite these recommendations, the clinical benefits of HA administration in the perioperative setting are controversial. Several studies have reported that perioperative HA supplementation does not reduce the risk of surgical site infection and may be associated with an increased incidence of postoperative adverse events [14–16]. Although preoperative hypoalbuminemia (ALB < 30 g/L) is consistently associated with prolonged postoperative hospital stay and an increased risk of surgical complications in patients with malignancies [17–20], it remains unclear whether exogenous HA administration improves postoperative recovery or clinical outcomes.

The currently approved indications for HA therapy are generally limited to the management of severe hypoproteinemia, typically defined as ALB concentrations of < 30 g/L [2]. Nevertheless, perioperative HA continues to be administered in routine clinical practice to patients with relatively preserved preoperative ALB levels. Robust clinical evidence remains limited regarding whether perioperative HA administration in patients with preoperative ALB concentrations ≥ 31 g/L can meaningfully increase postoperative ALB levels, reduce postoperative incision drainage, alleviate tissue edema, improve wound-related outcomes, or shorten postoperative hospital stay. From a medication-use perspective, this uncertainty complicates prescribing decisions and highlights the need for evidence to evaluate the clinical outcomes associated with perioperative HA administration in patients without hypoalbuminemia.

Aim

This study aimed to evaluate the association between perioperative human albumin administration and short-term postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery with preserved preoperative serum albumin concentrations (≥ 31 g/L). By focusing on patients without hypoalbuminemia, this study aimed to provide evidence to inform the appropriateness of perioperative HA use in routine clinical practice.

Method

Study design and setting

This retrospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the association between perioperative HA administration and short-term postoperative outcomes in patients who underwent gastrointestinal tumor surgery with preserved preoperative serum albumin levels. The study was performed at a tertiary teaching hospital and aimed to reflect the real-world perioperative medication use and clinical practice.

Data source and study population

Electronic medical records were the primary data source. Adult patients admitted to and discharged from the General Surgery Ward between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were adult patients (≥ 18 years) who underwent surgical resection for gastrointestinal tumors during the study period. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) preoperative ALB concentration < 31 g/L; admission or discharge diagnoses requiring emergency management for hypovolemia, acute respiratory distress syndrome, cardiopulmonary bypass, special hemodialysis, or adjuvant plasma exchange; (3) non–gastrointestinal tumor–related surgeries (e.g., cholecystectomy, appendectomy, thyroidectomy, subcutaneous tumor excision, hernia repair, hemorrhoid surgery) or other procedures (e.g., radiofrequency ablation, exploratory laparotomy) or pharmacologic interventions (e.g., chemotherapy perfusion); (4) critically ill patients transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) during or after surgery; (5) partial hepatectomy; and (6) patients who received HA administration before surgery or beyond three postoperative days.

Data collection and study variables

Demographic, laboratory, surgical, and perioperative variables were extracted using standardized data collection forms. The collected demographic variables included age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Baseline laboratory parameters included serum ALB, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and serum creatinine (Scr), which were defined as the last measurements before surgery. The comorbidities included hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Operative variables included operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion volume, and surgical incision classification [21].

The postoperative outcomes included incision drainage volume on postoperative day 2 (POD2), cumulative incision drainage volume during postoperative days 1–3 (POD1–3), recovery-period ALB concentration (measured on the day of discharge or the day prior to discharge), and duration of postoperative hospital stay. The normal reference ranges were as follows: ALT, 7–40 U/L; AST, 13–35 U/L; and Scr, 45–84 μmol/L. To account for potential repeated admissions, each hospitalization was treated as an independent record, and a unique identifier was assigned.

Perioperative human albumin exposure

Patients in the exposure group received intravenous human albumin (generic name: human albumin; proprietary formulation: FLEXBUMIN®, 20%, 10 g/50 mL per bottle) during the perioperative period. HA was administered at a dose of 10–20 g once daily starting on the day of surgery or the first postoperative day and continued for 2–7 consecutive days according to clinical judgment. HA administration was discontinued before hospital discharge. Patients in the control group did not receive HA at any time during the perioperative period. All patients in both groups were allowed to receive other perioperative treatments according to the standard clinical practice.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the incision drainage volume on POD2, obtained from nursing or progress notes in electronic medical records. When bilateral drainage was present, the volumes were added. The secondary outcomes included (1) cumulative incision drainage volume during POD1–3, (2) recovery-period serum ALB concentration, and (3) postoperative hospital stay duration, defined as the number of days from surgery to hospital discharge.

Statistical analysis

Based on previous studies [22], the expected postoperative day 2 (POD2) incision drainage volume was 320 ± 60 mL in the HA group and 350 ± 80 mL in the control group. Assuming a two-sided significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 80%, a minimum of 89 patients per group was required. The detailed sample size calculation is provided in Appendix 1 of the Supplementary Material. To account for potential data loss, 415 patients were enrolled.

To minimize confounding, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement and a 0.2 caliper width. The propensity score model included sex, age, BMI, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, baseline ALB, ALT, AST, Scr, operation time, intraoperative blood loss, intraoperative blood transfusion volume, and surgical incision type. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an SMD < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. After matching, 248 patients (124 per group) were included in the final analysis of the study. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to compare baseline ALB levels between retained and excluded patients before and after PSM within both the HA and control groups.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Between-group comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U-tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Outcome variables with non-normal distributions were log10-transformed before regression analyses.

Univariate analyses were initially performed, and variables with statistical significance were subsequently entered into multivariable linear regression models using backward elimination to evaluate the association between HA administration and the study outcomes. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Hospital in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Approval No. 2025BJYYEC-KY022-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective nature of the study.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

Between January 1 and December 31, 2023, 2,709 patients were discharged from the General Surgery Ward. After screening, 737 patients underwent gastrointestinal tumor surgery. Following the application of the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 415 adult patients were eligible for analysis, of whom 194 received perioperative HA and 221 did not (Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analyses comparing baseline ALB levels between retained and excluded patients showed no significant difference in the HA group (t = 0.705, p = 0.482), suggesting that patient exclusion was unlikely to introduce selection bias with respect to baseline ALB levels in this group. In contrast, a significant difference was observed in the control group (t = 11.25, p < 0.001), indicating that the excluded and retained patients differed in baseline ALB levels.

Incision drainage volume on postoperative day 2

In the PSM cohort, the median incision drainage volume on POD2 was 29 mL (IQR 18–125 mL) in the HA-treated group and 24 mL (IQR 12–87.5 mL) in the control group, indicating a higher POD2 drainage volume in the HA-treated group. Because the drainage volume data were positively skewed, a base-10 logarithmic transformation was applied to improve normality and satisfy the assumptions required for the linear regression analysis. After log10 transformation, the mean POD2 drainage volume was 1.60 ± 0.58 in the HA-treated group and 1.51 ± 0.56 in the control group.

Univariate regression analysis demonstrated that operation time (t = 3.426, p < 0.001) and intraoperative blood transfusion volume (t = 2.022, p = 0.044) were significantly associated with the POD2 incision drainage volume (Table 2). These variables, along with HA treatment status, were included in the multivariable linear regression model. After adjustment using backward elimination, operative time remained independently associated with POD2 incision drainage volume (t = 3.346, p < 0.001), whereas perioperative HA administration showed no significant association with POD2 drainage volume (p > 0.05).

Incision drainage volume during postoperative days 1–3

In the PSM cohort, the median cumulative incision drainage volume during POD1–3 was higher in the HA-treated group than in the control group [147 mL (IQR 46–382.75 mL) vs. 66.5 mL (IQR 41–245 mL)]. Because the distribution of cumulative drainage volume was skewed, a log10 transformation was performed before the regression analysis to improve normality and satisfy the assumptions of linear regression. After logarithmic transformation, POD1-3 was 2.09 ± 0.58 in the HA-treated group and 1.95 ± 0.60 in the control group. Univariate analysis identified operation time as the only variable significantly associated with POD1–3 incision-drainage volume (t = 4.090, p < 0.001) (Table 3). In the multivariable linear regression analysis, operation time remained independently associated with POD1–3 drainage volume (t = 4.115, p < 0.001), whereas HA treatment status was not significantly associated with the cumulative drainage volume (p > 0.05).

Recovery-period serum albumin concentration

After propensity score matching, the mean recovery-period serum ALB concentration was 36.36 ± 4.04 g/L in the HA-treated group and 34.14 ± 2.80 g/L in the control group. In the univariate analyses, age (t = − 3.218, p = 0.001), baseline ALB (t = 5.798, p < 0.001), and HA treatment status (χ2 = 5.043, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with recovery period ALB levels (Table 4). In the multivariable linear regression model, perioperative HA administration remained independently associated with higher recovery-period ALB concentrations (t = 3.996, p < 0.001) after adjusting for age and baseline ALB. Age and baseline ALB levels were also independently associated with recovery period ALB concentrations (all p < 0.05).

Postoperative hospital stay duration

In the matched cohort, the mean postoperative hospital stay was 10.1 ± 6.24 days in the HA-treated group and 8.08 ± 3.81 days in the control group. Univariate regression analysis showed that baseline ALB (t = 2.034, p = 0.043), baseline AST (t = 2.457, p = 0.015), baseline ALT (t = 2.322, p = 0.021), operation time (t = 3.312, p = 0.001), and HA treatment status (χ2 = 3.084, p = 0.002) were significantly associated with the duration of postoperative hospital stay (Table 5). In the multivariable linear regression analysis, perioperative HA administration remained independently associated with a longer postoperative hospital stay (t = 3.167, p = 0.002). Baseline AST levels and operation time also remained significantly associated with hospital stay duration after adjustment (all p < 0.05).

Discussion

Effects of perioperative albumin administration on postoperative drainage outcomes

To date, evidence remains inconclusive regarding whether perioperative HA administration improves short-term postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery with preserved preoperative ALB concentrations (≥ 31 g/L). Serum ALB typically reaches its lowest level within 6–12 h after surgery and increases substantially by 48 h postoperatively [23]. In the present study, perioperative HA administration was not associated with a reduction in incision drainage volume on POD2 or during POD1–3. After PSM, the baseline ALB was significantly higher in the HA group than in the control group (34.44 ± 2.73 vs. 33.24 ± 2.07, p < 0.001). Patients with higher baseline albumin who received HA were theoretically more likely to be affected by this medication. However, our results indicated that HA administration was not a significant factor influencing incision drainage volume on POD 2 or in POD 1–3. And the incision drainage volumes on POD 2 and during POD 1–3 were both higher in the HA group than in the control group. These findings suggest that HA supplementation does not confer measurable benefits in terms of early postoperative fluid-related outcomes in this patient population.

A previous meta-analysis by Chen et al. [24] demonstrated that compared with crystalloid solutions, colloid-based fluid therapy (including HA) was associated with higher postoperative pleural effusion drainage volumes. From a physiological perspective, approximately 30–40% of endogenous albumin is distributed within the intravascular compartment, and albumin accounts for nearly 80% of normal plasma colloid osmotic pressure. When serum albumin concentrations exceed 20 g/L, colloid osmotic pressure is generally maintained under normal physiological conditions [3]. Albumin increases intravascular oncotic pressure, limits fluid extravasation, interacts with endothelial cells to narrow intercellular junctions, and exerts antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects that may reduce microvascular permeability [25]. However, in patients experiencing surgical stress, inflammation, or malignancy-related hyperpermeability, capillary leakage is enhanced, and up to 85% of exogenously administered HA may rapidly redistribute to the extravascular compartment. This redistribution may increase interstitial oncotic pressure and exacerbate tissue edema [26–28].

These pathophysiological mechanisms may explain the lack of association between HA administration and reduced incision drainage observed in this study. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that HA does not promote wound-related recovery in surgical patients [2, 3, 29]. Notably, operation time was a consistent determinant of incision drainage volumes POD2 and POD1–3. The operation time is an important independent risk factor for postoperative capillary leakage and hypoalbuminemia [30, 31]. Prolonged surgery can trigger sustained systemic inflammatory responses, disrupt vascular endothelial barrier integrity, and promote the extravasation of plasma albumin into the interstitial space, leading to transient postoperative hypoalbuminemia [31]. From a clinical perspective, the operation time may be used for perioperative risk stratification. Pharmacists can contribute to a multidisciplinary management strategy by identifying high-risk patients preoperatively, supporting appropriate intraoperative anti-inflammatory and fluid management strategies, and promoting the judicious use of HA based on postoperative clinical indications. In addition, surgical complexity, rather than perioperative albumin supplementation, may play a more important role in influencing postoperative drainage outcomes.

It is worth noting that after PSM, although the P-value for surgical incision classification remained greater than 0.05, its standardized mean difference (SMD) was slightly above the conventional threshold of 0.1, indicating a potential residual imbalance. This is primarily attributed to the extremely low and unbalanced frequency of Grade III incisions (only 2 cases vs. 0 cases), which mathematically inflates the SMD value despite the negligible absolute difference. However, from a clinical perspective, Grade II incisions predominated in both groups (over 60%), and the overall distribution trend was highly comparable. Given that the absolute discrepancy was minimal and the traditional variance test yielded no significant difference (P > 0.05), this minor statistical imbalance is unlikely to introduce clinical bias or jeopardize the validity of our conclusions regarding perioperative albumin administration.

Serum albumin levels and clinical outcomes

When preoperative ALB concentrations are ≥ 31 g/L, further elevation of serum ALB levels may not translate into meaningful clinical benefits for the underlying disease process [32]. Although normal ALB concentrations in healthy individuals range from 35 to 50 g/L, this range reflects albumin synthesis, metabolism, and distribution rather than a threshold for optimal physiological function [33]. Hypoalbuminemia is often considered a marker of disease severity rather than a direct therapeutic target, and routine HA administration is generally not recommended for correcting low ALB concentrations [28, 34].

In the present study, patients with preserved preoperative ALB levels were selected. Although HA administration was independently associated with higher recovery-period ALB concentrations, this biochemical improvement did not correspond to improved postoperative drainage outcomes or shorter hospital stays. While ALB concentrations ≤ 35 g/L have been identified as risk factors for perioperative complications in some studies [19, 35, 36], a prior study by Cai et al. [23] demonstrated that postoperative ALB levels recovered spontaneously within 7 days, even in patients receiving only crystalloid therapy. Therefore, perioperative fluid shifts and hemodilution, rather than true protein deficiency, may be the primary contributors to transient postoperative hypoalbuminemia [37]. Furthermore, exogenous HA administration may suppress endogenous albumin synthesis, and its half-life is substantially shortened under postoperative stress conditions (12–16 h for infused HA versus approximately 3 weeks for endogenous albumin), particularly in the presence of increased capillary permeability [23].

Association between albumin use and hospital length of stay

In this study, no postoperative surgical complications were observed in either group after propensity score matching, suggesting that the cohorts represented a relatively low-risk surgical population. However, perioperative HA administration was independently associated with prolonged postoperative hospital stay. Baseline AST levels and operation time were also associated with longer hospitalization, indicating that postoperative recovery is influenced by multiple perioperative factors rather than HA use alone. These findings are consistent with perioperative expert consensus statements, which emphasize that HA should primarily be reserved for restrictive fluid strategies in critically ill patients requiring substantial volume resuscitation rather than for routine perioperative supplementation [38].

Implications for perioperative management and rational albumin use

From a perioperative management perspective, nutritional optimization, rather than albumin infusion, may be a more effective strategy for supporting postoperative recovery in patients with malignancies. HA supplementation does not constitute nutritional support, as albumin-derived amino acids lack essential components, such as tryptophan and isoleucine, and therefore cannot provide adequate protein replenishment [39]. Patients with a high nutritional risk are known to have increased postoperative morbidity [35, 36]. Multidisciplinary nutritional support protocols have been shown to increase serum ALB concentrations and reduce postoperative complications, whereas early postoperative nutritional intervention may further improve clinical outcomes [40, 41]. Perioperative nutritional optimization is also associated with reduced postoperative morbidity and improved survival [42, 43]. In addition, ω-3 fatty acid supplementation has been reported to favorably influence ALB concentrations in hospitalized patients [44]. These findings support a perioperative care strategy in which targeted nutritional assessment and intervention are prioritized, while routine HA administration in patients without hypoalbuminemia should be carefully evaluated in the context of clinical indications and overall treatment goals.

Clinical pharmacy implications

From a clinical pharmacy perspective, the findings of this study highlight the importance of evaluating the appropriateness of albumin prescriptions in perioperative practice. Albumin is a high-cost blood-derived product with specific clinical indications; however, it is frequently administered to surgical patients without clear evidence-based justification. The absence of measurable improvements in short-term postoperative outcomes among patients with preserved preoperative albumin levels suggests potential opportunities for medication stewardship. Clinical pharmacists can play an important role in optimizing perioperative albumin use through guideline implementation, prescription review, and interdisciplinary collaboration aimed at promoting rational and cost-effective pharmacotherapy.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, as a retrospective single-center study with a relatively limited sample size, its findings may not be fully generalizable to other settings and populations, warranting validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Second, minor measurement variability may have occurred for very low postoperative drainage volumes (≤ 10 mL) because of routine clinical documentation practices. Third, several potentially important factors, including the severity of surgical stress, perioperative inflammatory status, nutritional status, and clinician decision-making regarding human albumin administration, were not captured and may have influenced the postoperative outcomes. Although propensity score matching was performed to reduce confounding factors, residual imbalance remained for baseline albumin levels and surgical incision classification (SMD > 0.1). Furthermore, sensitivity analyses demonstrated significant differences in baseline albumin levels between the excluded and matched patients in the control group, suggesting the possibility of selection bias. Therefore, residual confounding and selection bias cannot be completely excluded from this study. Given the observational nature of this study, causal relationships between perioperative HA administration and postoperative drainage volume could not be established. Prospective multicenter studies and randomized controlled trials are needed to further evaluate the causal effects of perioperative HA use on postoperative drainage outcomes.

Conclusion

In adult patients undergoing gastrointestinal tumor surgery with preserved preoperative serum albumin concentrations (≥ 31 g/L), perioperative human albumin administration was not associated with reduced early postoperative incision drainage or shorter postoperative hospital stay. Although albumin administration increased serum albumin levels during the recovery period, this biochemical change did not translate into measurable improvements in short-term postoperative outcomes. These findings suggest that routine perioperative albumin administration in patients without hypoalbuminemia may provide limited clinical benefits. Therefore, careful evaluation of clinical indications and individualized prescriptions may be warranted when considering albumin administration in perioperative practice. Further multicenter prospective studies are needed to clarify the role of albumin therapy in patients undergoing surgery.
