A Case of Linear Hypertrophic Lichen Planus in a Pediatric Patient
Authors: Jin Ju Lee, Rosa Kim, Min Young Lee, Ji Yeon Byun, You Won Choi, Hae Young Choi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Lichen planus (LP) is a chronic inflammatory disease of the skin and mucosa. Of the various types, the hypertrophic type is characterized by thickened, purplish hyperkeratotic plaques and nodules. The course of hypertrophic LP tends to be more chronic than those of other types. A 12-year-old girl presented with a 2-year history of warty papules and plaques in a zosteriform configuration along one flank. Histopathology revealed hyperkeratosis and papillomatosis with wedge-shaped hypergranulosis. A lichenoid lymphocytic infiltrate with vacuolar change in the basal layer was evident. She was prescribed oral doxycycline, a topical corticosteroid, and tacrolimus. After 7 weeks, the skin lesions became significantly flattened and faded. LP is less common but more severe in children than in adults. The unilateral, linear hypertrophic type of LP is uncommon. Herein, we report a rare case of linear hypertrophic LP in a child.

INTRODUCTION

Lichen planus (LP) is an idiopathic inflammatory skin condition affecting the skin, mucous membranes, hair follicles and nails. The classic cutaneous lesions of LP present as small, flat-topped, shiny, polygonal, violaceous papules that may coalesce into plaques. The exact prevalence is still unknown, LP is known to affect 1% of the general population. Although LP affects patients of all ages, childhood LP is relatively more uncommon, accounting for only 1% to 5% of the total LP in the general population. Among pediatric LP patients, the classic type is known to be the most common subtype1. On the other hand, hypertrophic LP, which represents superimposed lichen simplex chronicus, commonly affects the lower limbs, particularly the shins. Moreover, hypertrophic LP only seen up to 7.7%~23.1% of cases123456.

We report a case of LP whose lesions showed clinically linear distribution in childhood like lichen striatus and that histopathologically was a rare variant of hypertrophic type and also that revealed dramatic improvement 7 weeks after treatment of oral doxycycline, topical corticosteroid and topical tacrolimus.

CASE REPORT

A 12-year-old female presented with verrucous papules and plaques on the left flank, exhibiting central clearing, less than 2 years in duration. She lacked any relevant contact or trauma history. She had undergone a skin biopsy at another clinic and a topical corticosteroid had been prescribed because of an impression of lichen striatus. As the lesions were refractory to this therapy, she was referred to our clinic. She lacked any underlying disease and her laboratory test data were normal. Physical examination revealed well-defined, discrete to confluent hyperkeratotic papules and plaques arranged in a linear manner along the Blaschko’s lines from the left side of the abdomen to the back. Mucous membrane, scalp, and nail examinations were normal (Fig. 1).

For further evaluation, we biopsied a lesion of the left flank. Histopathology revealed hyperkeratosis, papillomatosis, wedge-shaped hypergranulosis, and irregular acanthosis. In addition, band-like dermal lymphocytic infiltrates associated with vacuolar degeneration of the basal layer, forming a Caspary-Joseph space, were evident (Fig. 2). These findings were consistent with a diagnosis of hypertrophic LP.

We prescribed oral doxycycline 100 mg daily for 10 weeks and a topical corticosteroid (fluticasone propionate, Cutivate cream®) and 0.1% topical tacrolimus. A dramatic clinical response was evident after 7 weeks (Fig. 3). The papules and plaques became flatter and the left-side hyperpigmentation was greatly reduced. We received the patient’s consent form about publishing all photographic materials.

DISCUSSION

LP is not rare and its several clinical variants have been described. The hypertrophic variant is characterized by intensely pruritic, thick, hyperkeratotic plaques and nodules that may be verrucous in appearance. The lesions of hypertrophic LP tend to present symmetrically on the lower extremities. Hypertrophic LP is often refractory to treatment78, and squamous cell carcinoma can rarely develop in long-standing lesions9.

Linear LP, another variant, exhibits linear lesions along the Blaschko’s lines; lichen follicularis10, atrophic LP11, and LP pigmentosus12 can be also evidence for linear configurations. One epidemiological study reported that 3.4% of LP patients simultaneously exhibited more than one LP variant13.

The differential diagnosis includes lichen striatus, inflammatory linear verrucous epidermal nevus, and linear epidermal nevus. First of all our case needs to be clinically differentiated from lichen striatus because of childhood onset and also unilateral distribution. However, our patient histopathologically did not show any inflammatory infiltrates around hair follicles and sweat glands like lichen striatus. Also epidermal changes of our patient were marked with papillomatosis and hyperkeratosis.

Our review of the literature revealed only two case reports on unilateral, linear hypertrophic LP. The first case was 75-year-old male who was diagnosed with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) presented with pruritic, thick plaques on his right lower leg for 4 years. He showed complete response after 6 weeks of topical corticosteroid (Clobetasol propionate) treatment14. The second report was two 60-year-old females who presented with pruritic unilateral linear hyperkeratotic nodules and plaques along Blaschko’s line of the legs15. Disease duration was 15 years and 26 years respectively.

LP in pediatric patients makes up only 1%~5% of all LP cases. The prevalence of pediatric LP varies geographically; the peak onset time is from 8 to 12 years of age7. Of all pediatric LP, the hypertrophic variant accounts for 7.7% to 23.1%123456. Linear LP is more common in children, but the precise prevalence of linear hypertrophic LP remains unknown.

High-potency topical corticosteroids are the mainstay of treatment in patients with localized LP; systemic medications are often prescribed for those with severe, more protracted LP.

Some case reports have found that antibiotics have unexpectedly improved LP. Wahba-Yahav16 reported that a patient on oral metronidazole to treat amebiasis evidenced simultaneous improvement of cutaneous LP. It was suggested that the antibiotics might have suppressed immunological reactions, thereby improving LP. Oral doxycycline has been suggested to be a relatively safe alternative to conventional immunosuppressive agents for patients with ulcerative LP17. Doxycycline exhibits anti-inflammatory properties; it downregulates the production of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) involved in the innate immune response1819. It also inhibits several matrix metalloproteinases and granzyme B, which are involved in dermal matrix degradation of both collagen and elastic tissue. Moreover, doxycycline reduced infiltration of T cells through downregulation of chemokines and reduced expression of adhesion molecules in experimental cerebral malaria20.

We have treated our patient with doxycycline, a topical corticosteroid (fluticasone propionate, Cutivate cream®) and 0.1% topical tacrolimus because she told that the lesion did not respond to a highly potent topical corticosteroid which was prescribed at other local clinic. Her response to such triple therapy was excellent. In summary, we report a pediatric patient with linear hypertrophic LP, an uncommon variant.
