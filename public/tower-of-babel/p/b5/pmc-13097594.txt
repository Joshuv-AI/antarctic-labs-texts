The Impact, Role and Experiences of Nurses Working in Medical Quarantine During the COVID‐19 Pandemic: A Narrative Review
Authors: Tania Avila, Diana Jefferies, Lucie M Ramjan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo understand the impact, role and experience of nurses working in medical hotel quarantine during the COVID‐19 pandemic.BackgroundMedical Hotel Quarantine was staffed predominantly by nurses to prevent the spread of COVID‐19 when people who were COVID‐19 positive were unable to isolate safely in their communities.Review MethodsA narrative review was conducted to understand how nurses made meaning of their experience. Seven databases were searched (Google Scholar, CINAHL, COCHRANE, MEDLINE, Joanna Briggs Institute, PsychInfo and Scopus) following the PRISMA Methodological Guideline (between August 2021 and January 2022). A summary table was developed with the headings: author and year, country, study design and data collection, participants, critical appraisal rating and results. Using a Critical Realist lens, the data were analysed using thematic analysis and a stratified ontology of the Real, the Actual and the Empirical domains in medical hotel quarantine to understand how the complex nature of nursing care worked within this unique environment.ResultsNurses were pivotal to the success of medical hotel quarantine. The Critical realist lens demonstrated how this was a stressful environment as health information was updated and policies and requirements changed. Although there were reports of stigma outside work, many nurses reported that they felt a sense of duty caring for patients experiencing isolation.DiscussionThere is little research about nurses working in medical hotel quarantine during COVID‐19.ConclusionFurther research is required to understand how nurses managed these facilities to protect the community when future pandemics occur.Implication for the ProfessionIt is important that the lessons from medical hotel quarantine are recorded so that future nurses can be guided by the experience of nurses who worked in hotel quarantine if they are required to work in this unique area of practice.Keywords: COVID‐19, experience, hotel quarantine, nursing, role, stigma

Introduction

Nurses were at the forefront of the COVID‐19 Pandemic, providing patient care close to patients where it is difficult to adhere to social distancing guidelines. As a result, nurses have an increased risk of infection and transmission of the virus. Numerous studies have observed the pressures COVID‐19 has had on the physical and psychological health of nurses. Stressors such as fear of infection and transmission to family members compounded the regular stressors of caring for patients (Chaudhary et al. 2024; Dharra and Kumar 2021). However, one area where nurses cared for people in a unique environment, medical hotel quarantine, has received little attention in the literature. Medical hotel quarantine was a challenging environment that required complex problem solving with a team approach to ensure the safety and wellbeing of both nurses working within this environment and the patients they care for (Medway and Micik 2024). It is important to explore the impact, role and experience of nurses in medical hotel quarantine as often these facilities were nurse led (Jefferies et al. 2023).

Background

When COVID‐19 emerged in late 2019, policies and procedures were implemented in many countries affected by the virus to delay peak infection times and reduce the overall number of infections (Asongu et al. 2020). One example in Sydney, Australia was the Special Health Accommodation (SHA) which was established to provide care for travellers who were suspected or were confirmed cases of COVID‐19 and prevent transmission of the virus into the community. Its remit was expanded to travellers with complex health needs and isolated community patients requiring care beyond what could be provided in police‐run hotel quarantine (Jefferies et al. 2023). This included people who were homeless, with mental ill health or substance use issues who may have been at risk of transmitting the virus in the community (Longmore 2021). Nurses also contributed to the success of hotel quarantine for people experiencing homelessness in San Francisco and Madrid. However, anecdotal evidence from nurses working in quarantine facilities in New Zealand suggests that they have been socially ostracised due to fears of transmission when returning to the communities they live in.

Medical hotel quarantine was a public health strategy that was implemented to reduce the transmission of the COVID‐19 in the community (Fotheringham et al. 2021). Although there is little information about the impact, role and experience of nurses working in medical hotel quarantine, in other clinical areas the effects of stress caused by COVID‐19 had a negative impact on patient care by increasing absenteeism and burnout in nurses (Mokhtari et al. 2020). These findings have been reported by nurses caring for COVID‐19 positive patients and have been found in clinical settings across the world (Longmore 2021; Shan et al. 2021). For instance, nurses in Wuhan reported significant associations between stress, anxiety, insomnia and COVID‐19 (Shan et al. 2021). Yet, despite these stressors, nurses continued to provide care throughout the ongoing COVID‐19 crisis. However, the question of how nurses adapted to the experience of working in the medical quarantine hotel nurse has received little attention in the literature.

A Critical Realist Lens to Understand the Impact, Role and Experience of Nurses Working in Medical Hotel Quarantine

This study contributes to the limited research into nurses' work in the novel setting of medical hotel quarantine so that nurses can be prepared for this role in future pandemics. It takes a critical realist approach to understand the strategies, experience and outcomes of nursing practice in medical hotel quarantine and inform future nursing care during unprecedented disease outbreaks. Critical realism uncovers the complex nature of healthcare interventions and how they work in specific contexts (Williams et al. 2017) by using a stratified ontology, suggesting that in the study of ‘reality’ there are three different layers of knowledge, the ‘Real’, ‘Actual’ and ‘Empirical’ domains. The Real domain represents a reality that is independent of human thought and awareness where phenomena or generative mechanisms impact the world. The Actual domain seeks to understand how these generative mechanisms are experienced in the world. Finally, the Empirical domain is the human perception and experience of these generative mechanisms (Schiller 2016). Using this stratified ontology, this study seeks to understand how nurses adapted to the rapid nature of changing information about COVID‐19 and how they integrated new information when providing care to people in medical hotel quarantine. The purpose of this exploration is to understand the impact of COVID‐19 in medical hotel quarantine, the experience of working as a nurse and how they adapted their role in this unique environment.

The Review

Aims

This narrative review identifies and analyses studies reporting on the impact, role and experience of nurses working in medical hotel quarantine during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Methods

Design

A narrative study design was chosen because it enabled the researchers to explore how nurses described their experience of nurses and constructed meaning about their role in medical hotel quarantine (Drayton and Luck 2022).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This review included studies if they: (1) discussed the role, experience or impact of nurses working in hotel quarantine, (2) were peer reviewed and (3) were published in English, and after 2019. Studies were excluded if they spoke about nurses practicing in areas other than hotel quarantine during the pandemic.

Search Methods

An initial search was conducted in the CINAHL and MEDLINE databases. Indexed terms and keywords used within the title and abstract were used in the development of the search strings in the final search strategy. A search of the Google Scholar, CINAHL, COCHRANE, MEDLINE, Joanna Briggs Institute, PsychInfo and Scopus database was performed and completed 2 August 2021. An additional search was completed on 6 December 2021 and 17 January 2022 to capture any new articles. The following strategy was used: (MH ‘Practice Nurses’) OR (MH ‘Nursing Assistants’) OR Nurses OR ‘Nurse managers’ OR ‘Registered Nurses’ OR ‘Enrolled Nurses’ OR ‘Assistants in Nursin*’ OR (MH ‘Registered Nurses’) OR (MH ‘Nursing Management’) AND ‘Medical Hotel Quarantine’ OR ‘Hotel Quarantine’ OR ‘Health Hotel’ OR ‘Special Health Accommodation’ AND ‘COVID‐19’ OR (MH ‘COVID‐19’) OR (MH ‘COVID‐19 Pandemic’) AND Limit to: Academic Journals AND Limit to: Since 2019.

Search Outcome

Following this search, all 224 identified studies were uploaded into an EndNote X9 library and duplicates were removed. The remaining 214 articles underwent rigorous title, abstract and full text screening against the inclusion criteria. Reasons for exclusion were reported and recorded using the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines (Page et al. 2021) (insert Figure 1).

Synthesis

Thematic analysis identifies patterned responses within a data set (Braun and Clarke 2006). The literature meeting the eligibility criteria was read and reread, and initial codes were generated. Codes were collated and reviewed and grouped into overarching themes. Using the lens of critical realism within thematic analysis uncovered how individuals made meaning from their experience and the influence of societal factors on this meaning (Schilller, 2015).

Results

The literature review identified 6 studies from Australia [n = 2] (Fotheringham et al. 2021; Shaban et al. 2020), New Zealand [n = 1] (Jamieson et al. 2021), the USA [n = 1] (Fuchs et al. 2021), the Philippines [n = 1] (Sadang 2021) and Saudi Arabia [n = 1] (Alhamidi et al. 2020) that explored the role of nurses in medical hotel quarantine. The participants in three studies were nurses who had worked in hotel quarantine and had been interviewed by the researchers (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Jamieson et al. 2021; Sadang 2021). In two other studies, the participants were patients in the quarantine facility. One study was a quantitative review of patient records (Fuchs et al. 2021) and the other study was a qualitative study of patients in quarantine (Shaban et al. 2020). Another study was a discussion paper whose authors had established and managed a hotel quarantine facility (Fotheringham et al. 2021). Data analysis identified three overarching themes: (1) Information and governance structures; (2) the role of the nurse; and (3) the nursing experience. These themes are presented below according to the perceived domain—Real, Actual and Empirical in the Critical Realism framework (Schiller 2016) (Insert Table 3).

Theme 1: Information and Governance Structures

Within the ‘Real’ domain, the identified theme of ‘information and governance structures’ was considered generative mechanisms that influenced hotel quarantine nursing. As evidence emerged to inform health directives about the containment of COVID‐19, all the countries in these studies implemented hotel quarantine to prevent community transmission.

Subtheme 1.1: Factors Influencing Hotel Quarantine Nursing

Literature reporting on the influencing factors of hotel quarantine nurses was limited. Nurses described a lack of structure in hotel quarantine management, leading to nurse‐created policies and procedures.

We did not have administrative policies and procedures; we are the one[s] who created them. (Alhamidi et al. 2020, 102)

Another discussed the presence of comprehensive patient management strategies and governance structures (Fotheringham et al. 2021; Fuchs et al. 2021), however they also describe the difficulties in adapting to the changing health directives.

At times, issues were identified only when problems or challenges arose, requiring a flexible, dynamic approach to management. (Fotheringham et al. 2021, 6)

Participants reported how new evidence about COVID‐19 changed the rules as the pandemic evolved.

With COVID‐19 every day brings a new study about the mode of transmission. (Alhamidi et al. 2020, 104)

Every day they have a new rule… (Alhamidi et al. 2020, 102)

Another influencing factor on quarantine nursing practice was largely influenced by the level of experience and nursing knowledge they had previously developed in other roles. One nurse in Saudi Arabia who had worked during the Middle Eastern Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) pandemic observed a difference between the management of COVID‐19 through differing use of personal protective equipment (PPE). There was also a difference in their own practice and the practice of nurses who had graduated after the MERS pandemic (Alhamidi et al. 2020). The role of the nurse was influenced by these factors and the novel nature of COVID‐19 when hotel quarantine was established. This also influenced the experience of nurses working in these facilities.

Theme 2: The Role of the Nurse

The Critical Realist framework highlights the influence social structures have on individuals, such as the fear of community transmission of COVID‐19. The phenomena and events occurring in the ‘Actual’ domain were caused either directly or indirectly by the generative mechanisms in the ‘Real’ domain. Specifically, the role of the quarantine nurse was created as a direct result of the health directives and the need to prevent community transmission of COVID‐19. The fear of community transmission affected the families and friends of quarantine nurses.

Subtheme 2.1: The Role of the Nurse in Hotel Quarantine

Nurses working in hotel quarantine cared for patients who were overseas travellers, close contacts, or COVID‐19 positive patients to reduce community transmission (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Fotheringham et al. 2021; Fuchs et al. 2021; Jamieson et al. 2021; Sadang 2021; Shaban et al. 2020). In hotels, there could be between 150 and 280 patients at any time (Fuchs et al. 2021). Nurses reported that patient ratios in hotel quarantine could range from 1:50 depending on acuity, up to 280 guests (Alhamidi et al. 2020). Nursing staff received guests under transmission‐based precautions and escorted them to their rooms where they would stay for their quarantine period (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Fotheringham et al. 2021). In one study, the patients reported that twice daily welfare checks were conducted via telephone (Fuchs et al. 2021), escalating to an in‐person check when there was no response (Fotheringham et al. 2021). Nurses oversaw patient enquiries (Alhamidi et al. 2020), the accommodation of dietary restrictions, monitoring of symptoms (Fuchs et al. 2021) and psychological support (Alhamidi et al. 2020). Before entering the work site, nurses were screened for symptoms or possible exposures (Fotheringham et al. 2021). Nurses were required to care for patients with strict infection control procedures to prevent contracting and/or transmitting the virus (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Fotheringham et al. 2021; Jamieson et al. 2021; Shaban et al. 2020). If a nurse was identified as a close contact or in breach of PPE requirements, they were required to isolate for up to 14 days (Jamieson et al. 2021). Isolation could extend to close contacts of quarantine nurses, often friends and family.

They wanted me to get [children] out of uni and out of work and say you have to come home….They said, “Until you get a negative result, everybody's gotta stay home. [P9]” (Jamieson et al. 2021, p57)

Subtheme 2.2: How the Role Impacted Social Interactions Outside the Workplace

Only one study from New Zealand reported how social interactions were influenced when nurses worked in hotel quarantine. Family members and the community were hesitant or unwilling to interact with nurses working in hotel quarantine for fear of contracting COVID‐19 Employers also asked for additional ‘rules’ for families of nurses wishing to interact with society.

I live in a flat with several other young working professionals and one got told not to come into work when they found out where I worked… Both of their employers were wanting proof of my latest swab. (Jamieson et al. 2021, 58)

One nurse reported that her sister, an aged care employee, was contractually obligated to stay away from nurses working in medical hotel quarantine, highlighting the concern for the vulnerable population (Jamieson et al. 2021).

The fear of transmission adversely affected interactions between nurses and their families, friends and the community.

I invited my sister and her family over for a Christmas BBQ and she said she was sorry, but they wouldn't be able to come because her husband was very worried about COVID. (Jamieson et al. 2021, 58)

I said to one person, “oh well come and have lunch” and she went, “No, you're working in isolation. I don't want to have anything to do with you.” (Jamieson et al. 2021, 57)

The nurses here reported a lack of community confidence in nursing practice in hotel quarantine:

People trust [non‐quarantine hotel nurses] to do their job… They need to maybe have a little bit more faith in us and our practice. (Jamieson et al. 2021, 56)

They [the public] don't understand how seriously we take it and how we manage it… (Jamieson et al. 2021, 56)

Nurses were adversely affected by their practice context of their practice and the fear of community transmission.

Theme 3: The Nursing Experience

The themes within the ‘Empirical’ domain outlined the differing perspectives and experiences of the individual working as a quarantine nurse. Nursing viewpoints varied based on the demands of the role and the governance structures present with the quarantine establishment as well as personal experiences. Additionally, their personal values and the attitudes of the community influence the nursing experience.

Subtheme 3.1: Being a Quarantine Nurse

Nurses reported a sense of altruism or professional duty towards their role in the frontline, protecting the community against COVID‐19 (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Sadang 2021). Nurses in the Philippines reported a sense of fulfilment by assisting the community despite the challenges of the pandemic (Sadang 2021). Nurses in Saudi Arabia said it was their duty to provide professional nursing care “under all different situations” (Alhamidi et al. 2020). One nurse stated,

Working in community quarantine facility requires compassion, hard work, and strong patience to survive your daily responsibilities as a front liner nurse or else you will lose this battle. (Sadang 2021, 159)

Some nurses expressed confidence in their practice, using PPE, and their experience, no longer feeling afraid of the virus (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Jamieson et al. 2021). Others reported using their faith to overcome their fear of the virus (Alhamidi et al. 2020). In cases of increased risk such as outbreaks, some individuals reported taking additional measures such as wearing a mask while at home (Alhamidi et al. 2020), self‐isolating on days off, not seeing family or getting a COVID‐19 swab on their day off (Jamieson et al. 2021). In some instances, balancing work and family became unsustainable and the nurses resigned (Jamieson et al. 2021).

Subtheme 3.2: The Challenges of Quarantine Nursing

Nurses experienced several challenges working in hotel quarantine such as a lack of resources such as PPE (Sadang 2021), a lack of infection control knowledge and nursing skills such as using a ventilator (Alhamidi et al. 2020).

Now as a primary line to detect Corona I am ready. But as a hospital nurse and receiving cases of respiratory disease in critical condition on a ventilator I am not ready because I am not trained, not qualified. (Alhamidi et al. 2020, 104)

Several stressors were identified such as exhaustion due to the number of patients, a lack of sleep (Sadang 2021) and staff shortages (Alhamidi et al. 2020). There was also a constant risk of contracting the virus and transmitting it to family and friends (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Sadang 2021). One study reported nurses not trusting of the infection control capabilities of the hotel staff and the nurses cleaned the rooms of the patients to prevent transmission. This lack of trained staff added to the workload (Alhamidi et al. 2020). This included patients, even though they were in isolation in the quarantine hotel, who were anxious about transmitting COVID‐19 because of the lack of PPE:

Most of the time I would feel doubtful of my health and afraid of spreading the virus to my family, most especially my children, considering the lack of personal protective equipment. (Sadang 2021, 158)

Subtheme 3.3 Patient Challenges and the Nurses' Response

Nurses recognised the stress that patients experienced while in quarantine and managed a range of complex situations under difficult transmission‐based precautions (Shaban et al. 2020). Simple requests included the results of their swabs, and when they will be allowed to leave quarantine (Alhamidi et al. 2020). However, episodes of aggression required the help of security (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Fotheringham et al. 2021). Several nurses described patients not complying with health requests (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Sadang 2021).

…I went to her and I explained to her the situation and the protocols from the country and explained that we are here to serve you and if it was in our hands we would put you in the best hotel but for the confirmed cases there are specified hotels which we cannot change. (Alhamidi et al. 2020, 104)

Other challenges included nurses providing emotional support for patients in difficult situations (Alhamidi et al. 2020).

A patient's mother died in Germany because of cancer and he was with her for 7 months. It's painful really that he came in the same airplane with his mother's body. It's [was] really very hard and then he came here and they took the mother and he was alone. He was 22 years old and he started to cry and scream “my mom, I want to go and see my mom, I want to say goodbye to my mom”. So, these things are hard to be forgotten and when I talked [talk about it] it looks easy but when you are in the same situation and you are seeing everything it is not. (Alhamidi et al. 2020, 105)

Other incidents such as falls, occupation health and safety incidents and complaints were identified in the literature (Fotheringham et al. 2021).

Subtheme 3.4: The Impact on Nurses

Nurses experienced stigma and social exclusion from their families, the community and other healthcare workers because of their occupation (Jamieson et al. 2021; Sadang 2021). Nurses accessing health care were made to feel unwelcome and blamed as the source of a family member's illness (Jamieson et al. 2021).

The systems were probably as good as they could be at the time, and we were safe going to work, and hence the community's safe and we're not a risk to the community. It was a shock to me to get to New Zealand [to work as a nurse] and get a sense that that wasn't really the understanding in the community. [P11] (Jamieson et al. 2021, 56)

Nurses have reported working in hotel quarantine as a learning experience, learning time management, communication, leadership and computer skills. Nurses also reported a need for planning, prevention and follow up in the management of quarantine, as well as additional training in infection control (Alhamidi et al. 2020).

Discussion

Hotel quarantine was established to prevent the spread of COVID‐19, and ‘flatten the epidemiological curve’ by reducing the number of people infected within the community (Department of the Prime Minister and Cabinet 2021). Little attention has been paid to the role and experience of nurses working in hotel quarantine during the COVID‐19 pandemic.

A critical realist approach to data analysis was used in this review to understand the relationship between researchers and the generation of knowledge. Critical realism views reality as a social construct formed by previous experiences and what has been learned (Schiller 2016). In this review, critical realism was used to characterise the context in which the nursing role and experience were formed. This was important due to the novel nature of the COVID‐19 emergency management, as well as its significance for the nurses working within hotel quarantine. Using the critical realism approach added depth to the individual experience.

The Nursing Role

The greatest factor influencing the role of nurses working in hotel quarantine was the changing evidence base (Alhamidi et al. 2020; Fotheringham et al. 2021). To break the chain of infection, nurses need accurate information about the mode of transmission to guide the level of infection control precautions when caring for patients. Evidence regarding best practice was uncertain in the face of initial PPE shortages (Cook 2020). And often there was no policy and procedure available for infection control, and nurses took responsibility for decision making and coordination of care within these establishments (Alhamidi et al. 2020). This is reflected in the establishment of hotel quarantine in Spain that was launched with no guidance except community health regulations (Ramírez‐Cervantes et al. 2020). This finding was also reported by Jackson and Nowell (2021) where nurse managers found their management role extended to include greater duties or requests to fulfil roles with little to no training or orientation. In all cases whether there were structures and guidelines available, nurse needed to be flexible and knowledgeable when dealing with challenges and changes as they arose.

Quarantining for COVID‐19 involved physical isolation within a designated isolation establishment. Nursing tasks involved patient care under transmission‐based precautions including completing patient care from a distance, via telephone, or while wearing PPE if nurses were unable to work under social distancing recommendations. In their qualitative study, nurses were distressed by the intensive workload when caring for COVID‐19 positive patients as they were unable to provide holistic care (Silverman et al. 2021) and this caused feelings of moral distress as patient loads and acuity increased during the pandemic (Kumar and Rodney 2021). One example was patients being uncertain about the length of quarantine due to a lack of communication (Williams et al. 2022). Other examples included the shortage of medical and protective resources and the need to leave one patient because another patient's condition had become critical (Kumar and Rodney 2021). Nurses reported high levels of communication facilitated improved patient care and promoted adherence to medical quarantine. However, conflict arose when patients were dissatisfied with their accommodation at the quarantine hotel.

The Nursing Experience

While nurses reported the successful use of hotel quarantine to contain COVID‐19 infections in this review, nurses and their families were affected by their proximity with the virus and nurses experienced a high level of scrutiny. The fear of transmission often resulted in the stigmatisation of nurses (Jamieson et al. 2021; Sadang 2021).

Another stressor was a lack of resources and staffing leading to overwork and feelings of burnout and psychological distress when dealing with the COVID‐19 pandemic (Lorente et al. 2021). Additionally, nurses found themselves working with staff who were not trained in infection control, adding to the stressors when trying to prevent community transmission.

Patients in hotel quarantine required differing levels of care and support from nurses. Studies have shown patients experiencing isolation are at an increased risk for anxiety, anger and stress‐related disorder, higher in cases where individuals are quarantining for periods greater than 1 week (Henssler et al. 2021). This review found nurses understood that patients needed social companionship and required support in dealing with emotionally charged situations. However, the training or skills the nurses possessed in dealing with these issues are not discussed.

Despite the stressors, nurses have reported a high level of duty to their role in hotel quarantine using the hotel quarantine experience as a learning experience. Nurses also reported a need for future planning and patient management during viral outbreaks. These results are consistent with Goldfarb et al. (2021) where nurses who received pandemic‐specific training and emotional support from organisations had greater professional commitment.

The increasing prevalence of viral outbreaks such as the H1N1 Swine flu (Influenza A virus subtype) pandemic in 2009, Middle Eastern Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) in 2012 and the Zika virus epidemic in 2015, as well as the COVID‐19 pandemic, highlights the need for education and healthcare planning for future pandemics (Jackson and Nowell 2021; Tashkandi et al. 2021; Williams et al. 2022). Several studies have identified COVID‐19 quarantine hotels staffed by non‐medical personnel (Goh and Baum 2021; Teng et al. 2020), therefore, nurses add value to quarantine hotels through the provision of medical assessment and escalation of patient health issues. This review adds to the literature, defining the role of nurses and highlighting the nursing experience as a basis for future research.

This review was limited by the novel nature of COVID‐19 and the establishment of quarantine hotels. With only two years' worth of information, there was a limited number of studies to draw on. As a result, future research may confirm or deny the findings of this review.

Conclusion

The new role of nurses in hotel quarantine was developed to prevent the transmission of COVID‐19 within the community. Using critical realism, this review has characterised the nursing role, as well as the experience of the nurse working in hotel quarantine. Evidence and health policy informing practice changed rapidly as anxiety in the community increased, resulting in social stigma. Despite these sentiments, nurses continued to fulfil their duties and reported altruistic feelings towards their work notwithstanding a lack of resources and experience.

This review is one of a few reporting on the role and experiences of nurses working in hotel quarantine. Nurses must be appreciated and valued for the work that they do during difficult situations, such as during the COVID‐19 pandemic. The findings highlight the difficulties and stigma nurses experienced due to working in hotel quarantine. This emphasises the need for education in undergraduate and postgraduate nursing education programs, as well as clear policies and procedures. to improve practices and prevent stigma.
