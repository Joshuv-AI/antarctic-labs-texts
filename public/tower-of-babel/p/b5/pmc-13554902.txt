Clinical Heterogeneity of Recurrent Familial Cardiac Myxoma in PRKAR1A-Related Carney Complex
Authors: Alexis Vallejos Barrientos, Daniel Espinoza Alva, Diego Davila Flores, Renee Montesinos Segura, Cristian Aguilar Carranza, Romel Zamudio Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac myxomas are common benign primary cardiac tumors; hereditary forms are strongly associated with the Carney complex.Case SummaryA 42-year-old woman with familial Carney complex and a pathogenic PRKAR1A variant developed recurrent cardiac myxomas involving 3 of the 4 chambers. The first presentation featured left atrial and right ventricular myxomas causing cardioembolic cerebellar stroke. A first recurrence in the left atrium caused severe transmitral obstruction and decompensated heart failure. A second recurrence in the right atrium presented as an infected myxoma. A third recurrence arose from the tricuspid annulus 4 months after resection; because further reoperation after 2 prior sternotomies and a right thoracotomy was considered prohibitive, she was listed for transplantation but died suddenly while awaiting transplant.DiscussionThis case highlights the aggressive, recurrent, and clinically heterogeneous phenotype and fatal course of Carney complex–associated cardiac myxoma.Take-Home MessageEarly recognition, lifelong surveillance, and timely consideration of advanced strategies are essential in selected patients with recurrent disease.Key Words: Carney complex, computed tomography, echocardiography, genetic disorders, myxoma, stroke

History of Presentation

A 42-year-old woman with recurrent familial cardiac myxoma (CM) in the setting of PRKAR1A-related Carney complex (CNC) presented with several days of fever, intermittent sharp chest pain, and nausea. She denied dyspnea, syncope, palpitations, or focal neurologic symptoms. On examination, her temperature was 38.6 °C, heart rate was 110 beats/min, respiratory rate was 15 breaths/min, and oxygen saturation was 99% on room air. Cardiac auscultation revealed a left lower sternal border systolic murmur, without peripheral stigmata of infective endocarditis.

Past Medical History

At age 36, the patient presented with syncope followed by dysarthria. Brain computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a right cerebellar ischemic lesion consistent with cardioembolic stroke (Video 1). Cardiac imaging revealed 2 intracardiac masses: a left atrial mass attached to the interatrial septum and a second right ventricular mass initially suspected to arise from the interventricular septum (Videos 2 and 3). Intraoperatively, the right ventricular mass was attached to the posterior papillary muscle; radical base excision was performed, preserving the valvular apparatus. Histopathology confirmed papillary CMs (Figure 1).

Index Presentation With Cerebellar Stroke and Bichamber Cardiac Myxomas

Brain computed tomography showed a hypodense lesion in the right cerebellar hemisphere, consistent with cardioembolic ischemic stroke (A and B, white arrows). Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a left atrial mass protruding across the mitral valve and a second mass within the right ventricle, documenting bichamber cardiac myxomas (C, white arrows). Cardiac computed tomography confirmed the left atrial lesion (D, arrow). Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography after resection showed no residual intracardiac masses (E and F), establishing complete initial surgical clearance. Family history revealed CMs in her daughter and sister, both treated surgically. At age 38, the patient developed progressive dyspnea, orthopnea, and lower-extremity edema. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) demonstrated recurrence of a left atrial CM causing severe obstruction of transmitral flow, associated with severe mitral regurgitation and right ventricular dysfunction (Videos 4 and 5). She underwent repeat surgical resection with atrioseptoplasty and mitral and tricuspid annuloplasty (Figure 2). Histopathology again confirmed CM. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic PRKAR1A nonsense variant, c.286C>T (p.Arg96Ter), establishing the diagnosis of CNC.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a recurrent left atrial myxoma attached to the interatrial septum and prolapsing across the mitral valve, producing severe transmitral obstruction (B, white arrow). Continuous-wave Doppler confirmed a mean transmitral gradient of 20 mm Hg (C). Postoperative transesophageal echocardiography showed no residual intracardiac mass after repeat surgical resection (F). AO = aorta; LA = left atrium; LV = left ventricle.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included infected recurrent CM, mural infective endocarditis with superimposed vegetation, infected intracardiac thrombus, and recurrent noninfected CM with inflammatory features.

Investigations

Initial blood tests demonstrated a white blood cell count of 14,200 cells/μL (reference range [RR]: 3,500-10,500 cells/μL); inflammatory markers included a C-reactive protein level of 221 mg/L (RR: <5 mg/L) and a procalcitonin level of 13.29 ng/mL (RR: <0.05 ng/mL). Blood cultures were obtained.

TTE identified a large intracardiac mass within the right atrium protruding toward the right ventricle, raising suspicion for a recurrent cardiac tumor. The prior mitral annuloplasty showed mild residual mitral regurgitation without evidence of stenosis.

Transesophageal echocardiography demonstrated a heterogeneous right atrial mass measuring approximately 60 × 38 mm, attached to the tricuspid annulus and extending toward the anterior tricuspid leaflet, producing obstruction of tricuspid inflow with a mean gradient of approximately 7 mm Hg (Video 6). Cardiac CT confirmed the origin of the mass at the tricuspid annulus and its extension toward the right ventricular inflow tract, without evidence of extracardiac extension (Video 7). Blood culture results were positive for Streptococcus oralis.

Management

Targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated. Given the right atrial mass and suspected infected intracardiac tumor, the case was discussed by the multidisciplinary heart team, and surgical resection was recommended. To avoid a third sternotomy, the patient underwent resection through a right thoracotomy with cardiopulmonary bypass.

Dense pericardial-pleural adhesions were encountered. After caval clamping and right atriotomy, a 50 × 60-mm right atrial tumor adherent to the posterior tricuspid annulus and protruding toward the right ventricular inflow tract was identified. The mass was completely excised along with its attachment site, and the right ventricle was carefully explored. Intraoperative hydraulic testing demonstrated adequate tricuspid leaflet coaptation, confirming preserved valve function. The right atrium was repaired, and no intraoperative complications were reported. Histopathologic examination demonstrated an irregular, fragmented CM with prominent inflammatory infiltrates, fibrin deposition, microabscess formation, and Gram-positive bacterial colonies, confirming the diagnosis of infected CM (Figure 3).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient completed the planned 6-week course of targeted intravenous antibiotic therapy, and repeat blood culture results were negative. A TTE performed before discharge demonstrated no residual intracardiac mass and preserved tricuspid valve function. She was discharged clinically stable. Four months after discharge, follow-up TTE revealed a new right atrial sessile mass measuring approximately 41 × 27 mm, attached to the tricuspid annulus and protruding toward the right ventricle, raising concern for tumor recurrence (Videos 8 and 9, Figure 4).

Early Asymptomatic Recurrence at the Tricuspid Annulus After Resection of Infected Cardiac Myxoma

Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography 4 months after surgery demonstrated a new right atrial mass attached to the tricuspid annulus and protruding toward the right ventricle (A, white arrow). Continuous-wave Doppler showed persistent tricuspid inflow obstruction with an elevated mean gradient of 15 mm Hg (B). Cardiac computed tomography confirmed a recurrent right atrial tumor arising from the tricuspid annulus (C and D, red arrows). This early postoperative recurrence, despite apparently complete resection, underscores the aggressive relapsing phenotype of the PRKAR1A-related Carney complex. Abbreviations as in Figure 1.

Given the patient’s history of 2 prior sternotomies, a right thoracotomy with dense adhesions, prior valve repairs, and early recurrent disease, the multidisciplinary heart team considered further surgical resection prohibitive and recommended listing for orthotopic heart transplantation. Two months later, while awaiting transplantation, the patient experienced sudden out-of-hospital death at the age of 42 years.

Discussion

Primary cardiac tumors are rare, with CMs representing the most common benign subtype.1,2 Approximately 90% of CMs are sporadic and arise in the left atrium; however, early-onset, recurrent, multifocal, or atypically located tumors should raise suspicion for CNC.1 This familial neoplasia syndrome is most commonly associated with inactivating variants of the PRKAR1A tumor suppressor gene.3

The clinical presentation of CM is driven by tumor size, location, mobility, and surface morphology. Mobile, irregular, villous, or friable tumors are associated with embolization, whereas larger and less villous lesions tend to cause obstruction; constitutional symptoms may reflect interleukin-6 release.2,4,5 Multimodality imaging supports CM diagnosis by identifying a well-defined intracavitary mass with endocardial attachment, heterogeneous architecture, and no extracardiac extension.1,2 Internal necrotic or inflammatory heterogeneity suggests infected CM in the appropriate clinical context.1,6 This case illustrates a CNC phenotype with multichamber and valvular involvement, recurrence, clinical heterogeneity, and tumor infection. Diagnosis was established at the second recurrence, meeting 1 major (CM) and 2 supplemental criteria (family history and pathogenic PRKAR1A variant).7

The molecular pathobiology of CNC explains this phenotype. The PRKAR1A gene on chromosome 17q22-24 encodes the type Iα regulatory subunit of protein kinase A and functions as a tumor suppressor.7 Inactivating germline variants disrupt cyclic adenosine monophosphate signaling, increase protein kinase A activity, and promote mesenchymal proliferation.3,7 This dysregulation predisposes to multifocal, recurrent, atypically located CMs, often multichamber and early-onset,1,7,8 consistent with this patient’s recurrent, heterogeneous course.

The index cerebellar ischemic stroke reflects the embolic potential of left-sided CMs, reported in up to 29% of cases, with basal ganglia and cerebellar predilection.2,4 The second presentation was dominated by decompensated heart failure through a different mechanism: recurrent mass-related dynamic transmitral obstruction mimicking severe mitral stenosis with concomitant valvular involvement causing mitral regurgitation. Right ventricular dysfunction was most consistent with postcapillary pulmonary hypertension and chronic pressure overload, producing biventricular heart failure. This evolution highlights the anatomical plasticity of CNC-associated myxomas, with 29.4% of recurrences arising in different chambers,8 and their capacity for divergent hemodynamic manifestations over time.4,5,8

The third hospitalization represented infected CM, an exceptionally rare entity reported sporadically in the literature.6 It should be suspected with fever of unknown origin and an intracardiac mass; differential diagnoses include mural infective endocarditis, infected thrombus, and noninfected CM with paraneoplastic inflammation.2,6 Transesophageal echocardiography helps define mass location, valvular involvement, and management.2 In the Revankar and Clark framework, diagnosis is supported by microorganisms within inflammatory infiltrates on histopathology plus positive blood cultures.6 Combined surgical resection and prolonged targeted antimicrobial therapy achieved initial clinical resolution.

The new asymptomatic tricuspid mass 4 months after surgery underscored the therapeutic dilemma. In CNC, recurrence may approach 54%, requiring lifelong surveillance and family screening.8 Identification of a familial PRKAR1A variant mandates targeted genetic testing for first-degree relatives. Mutation-positive relatives require annual echocardiography starting in infancy. For patients with prior CM, surveillance should be biannual for 20 years postresection, followed by lifelong annual monitoring.9 Although cardiac CT is fundamental for tumor characterization and operative planning, validated tomographic predictors of CM recurrence are lacking; once recurrence is identified, management should focus on complete excision and systematic exploration of the remaining chambers, as occurred during the first 3 operative interventions.1,8 Surgical strategy must be anatomy-specific: septal lesions require excision of the implantation base, whereas ventricular or valvular recurrences necessitate complete clearance while preserving conduction tissue, ventricular function, and valvular competence.2,8 Early resection is generally preferred because of persistent embolic, obstructive, and sudden death risks; however, further surgery was considered prohibitive after 2 prior sternotomies, a subsequent right thoracotomy complicated by dense adhesions, and prior mitral and tricuspid valve repairs.2,4 Heart transplantation was considered an exceptional definitive option for unresectable recurrent disease, requiring multidisciplinary evaluation;10 prior surgeries and transfusional exposure suggested sensitization and greater operative complexity.

Her sudden death while awaiting transplantation highlights the aggressive, unpredictable course of recurrent CMs in CNC, where cardiac causes account for mortality. The terminal event likely reflected abrupt valvular obstruction (ball-valve phenomenon), systemic or coronary embolization, or malignant ventricular arrhythmia.2,4,7 These mechanisms underscore the fatal potential of unresectable CMs despite surveillance. Limitations include incomplete parental genetic testing, lack of autopsy confirmation, and absence of cardiac magnetic resonance. However, echocardiography and CT adequately defined tumor attachment, valvular involvement, and surgical anatomy, making further tissue characterization unlikely to alter management.

Conclusions

CNC should be strongly suspected in patients with CM presenting at a young age, with familial clustering, multifocal disease, recurrence, or atypical intracardiac location. This case shows that recurrence may persist despite apparently complete excision and that even asymptomatic lesions may carry substantial risk. Lifelong surveillance, family screening, and early multidisciplinary consideration of advanced strategies are therefore warranted in selected patients with recurrent, surgically challenging disease.
