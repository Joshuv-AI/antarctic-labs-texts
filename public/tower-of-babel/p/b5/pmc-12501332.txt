Privacy-preserving computation scheme for the maximum and minimum values of the sums of keyword-corresponding values in cross-chain data exchange
Authors: Xin Liu, Huize Gao, Dan Luo, Wei Ye, Likai Jia, Gang Xu, Xiubo Chen, Lanying Liang, Baohua Zhang, Yu Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the specific value computation scenario of blockchain cross-chain data exchange, participants can organize raw data in the form of data pairs, making the computed specific values become key information for cross-chain collaboration. The main challenges faced in this scenario include: the lack of appropriate privacy protection mechanisms leading to easy leakage of sensitive data; the vulnerability of unencrypted or unvalidated data to tampering or malicious attacks; a crisis of trust among users. To address the above problems, this paper introduces secure multi-party computation (MPC) into the cross-chain data exchange process to ensure the fairness of the computation process and protect data privacy. To address the problem of computing the maximum and minimum values of the sums of keyword-corresponding values in cross-chain interactions, it is transformed into the secure computation scheme of computing the maximum and minimum values of the sums of corresponding elements in the intersection of sets (MMSI). A secure computation scheme for maximum and minimum values based on the fully homomorphic NTRU (FH-NTRU) encryption algorithm is proposed. First, a secure computation protocol for MMSI without a universal set under the semi-honest model is proposed. Then, to address potential malicious behaviors in the protocol, a secure computation protocol for MMSI under the malicious model is proposed, using the cut-and-choose method. The protocol under the malicious model is analyzed for correctness and the security of the protocol is proved using the real/ideal model paradigm. Finally, the efficiency analysis and experimental simulations show that the protocol is efficient and reliable, it can resist malicious adversary attacks and ensure the correctness of the computation results while effectively improving the security during cross-chain interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-14629-1.Keywords: Blockchain, Secure multi-party computation, Cross-chain, Malicious model, Maximum and minimum valuesSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

Blockchain technology, as a decentralized distributed ledger technology, has the characteristics of non-tampering, transparency, security, etc.1, with the development of blockchain technology, more and more industries have benefited from it, which promotes the digital transformation and innovation in the fields of finance2,3, commerce4,5, healthcare6,7, etc. Despite the significant advantages of blockchain, different blockchain platforms often adopt different technologies, consensus mechanisms, and protocol standards, leading to an “island” effect between blockchains. In order to address this problem, cross-chain technology8 can realize the interactive operation between different blockchains.

Cross-chain technology is a technical solution that enables interoperability between different blockchain networks, allowing independently operated blockchains (such as Ethereum, Polkadot, etc.) to securely exchange data, assets, or trigger smart contracts without relying on centralized institutions. The adoption of cross-chain technology aims to break down the “information silos” between blockchains, as traditional blockchains operate independently with no interconnection of assets and data—for example, Bitcoin cannot be directly used in Ethereum’s DeFi ecosystem. Cross-chain technology unlocks this value through interoperability. Additionally, using cross-chain technology helps distribute risks by avoiding the concentration of all assets on a single chain, thereby reducing systemic risks (such as the impact of a single point of failure on Ethereum). Cross-chain technology not only realizes the exchange of assets and data, but also involves the processing, analysis and computation of data in cross-chain interactions. Especially when transferring data between multiple chains, computing some particular values as a result is unavoidable.

In cross-chain interactions, when the particular values computed are the maximum and minimum values of the sums of keyword-corresponding values, the problem can be transformed into the problem of computing the maximum and minimum values of the sums of corresponding elements in the intersection of sets (MMSI) without trusted third parties (TTP). For example, in financial joint risk control analysis, two financial institutions—Institution and Institution —each possess user loan information organized as (ID number, loan amount) data pairs. To ensure data privacy and security, both institutions have built private chain and private chain respectively. Using smart contracts, they upload hash values of user loan information and data pair indexes to selected blockchain platforms (such as Ethereum and Polkadot), while the actual raw data is organized in data pair format and stored encrypted in off-chain databases.

Within this framework, the two institutions aim to compute the maximum and minimum total loan amounts of their shared customers, which serve as crucial indicators for credit risk assessment. To achieve this, cross-chain technology is employed to verify and transmit data hash values. After receiving verification results, blockchain nodes perform off-chain data computation and decryption. Finally, Institution and Institution can securely query the required computation results through their respective chain interfaces.

Essentially, the logical core of the process is to securely compute the maximum and minimum values of the sums of corresponding elements in the intersection of the two sets. The private chains maintained by Institution and Institution can be viewed as two independent sets and , where the institutions’ user loan data (organized as data pairs) constitute elements in sets and respectively. The process of determining shared customers between the institutions fundamentally represents finding keyword intersections between elements of two sets without universal set information. When intersections exist, the associated values corresponding to intersecting keywords are summed, from which the maximum and minimum values are derived.

The input data in the above process covers the user’s identity information, amount and other sensitive information, if the operation is performed directly on the plaintext data or appropriate privacy protection measures are not taken, the attacker may infer the user’s transaction amount, asset status and other information by listening to the computation results and the intermediate process, and the data leakage will pose a serious privacy protection risk.

Therefore, in order to protect the privacy of data participants, secure multi-party computation (MPC) emerges on demand, aiming to enable multiple participants to perform computation and data sharing without disclosing sensitive information through the use of technological means such as cryptography and protocol design. MPC was first proposed by YAO9, and then developed by Goldreich10. MPC has been recognized and applied in practice in various aspects such as cloud computing11–17, internet of things (IoT)18–21, privacy-preserving data mining22–24, and e-auctions25, and provides an effective solution to achieve data cooperation, protect data privacy, and meet legal requirements.

This paper addresses potential malicious behaviors in the computation of maximum and minimum values of sums of associated values corresponding to shared keywords in cross-chain interoperability protocols, and designs the privacy-preserving MPC protocols for computing MMSI, which can be embedded into cross-chain interoperability protocols as their privacy computation module to detect malicious adversary attacks and ensure data privacy and computational security. Both the semi-honest model and malicious model protocols are applicable to interoperability protocols. Furthermore, the malicious model protocol can cover security requirements for most cross-chain scenarios. For example, in oracle scenarios where oracles need to obtain data off-chain, it must defend against data source tampering or malicious submissions. In decentralized atomic swap scenarios, where atomic swaps involve direct interaction between two or more parties (e.g., HTLC), it must prevent malicious users from canceling transactions or submitting invalid data. In these cases, using the malicious model protocol can ensure data security.

Regarding the overall storage scheme selection for cross-chain interoperability protocol execution, we adopt an approach where raw data and intermediate computation results are stored off-chain, while data digests and MPC protocol execution results are stored on-chain for verification. Performing MPC protocol computations off-chain for specific values ensures that raw data is not exposed on public chains, protects privacy through fully homomorphic NTRU (FH-NTRU) encryption, and avoids the gas costs associated with storing massive data on-chain as well as blockchain throughput limitations (e.g., Ethereum’s 15 TPS). This approach is suitable for large-scale, high-frequency cross-chain operations and meets practical requirements.

Therefore, in blockchain, the privacy preservation of the maximum and minimum values computation of the sums of keyword-corresponding values in cross-chain data exchange is a crucial issue. To address this problem, this paper contributes as follows:

(1) The problem of computing the maximum and minimum values of the sums of keyword-corresponding values in cross-chain data exchange is transformed into the problem of computing MMSI securely in the absence of a universal set, which uses MPC techniques to protect data privacy and enhance the security during cross-chain interactions.

(2) Based on the FH-NTRU encryption algorithm, the protocol for the secure computation of MMSI under the semi-honest model is designed using the method of adding obfuscated elements for the case without a universal set. The possible malicious behaviors in the protocol of the semi-honest model are analyzed, and the protocol for computing MMSI under the malicious model is designed using the cut-and-choose method. The correctness of the protocol is analyzed and the security of the protocol is proved using the real/ideal model paradigm. This is one of the few MPC protocols that can be applied in blockchain systems while resisting malicious adversary attacks.

(3) Comparing the performance with the existing schemes, the proposed protocols under both models are able to simultaneously find MMSI while protecting the private data and the cardinality of the sets. After summing and subtracting the maximum and minimum numbers in the protocol, it can also serve as a protocol for simultaneously finding the extreme value difference and the extreme value sum of the corresponding elements in the intersection of sets.

This paper is organized as follows: “Existing relevant works” provides an introduction to the existing research schemes; “Preparatory knowledge” describes the cryptographic knowledge required for protocol design and the multi-tiered trust establishment mechanism in cross-chain data exchange in combination with the MPC protocols; “Secure computation protocol for MMSI under the semi-honest model” and “Secure computation protocol for MMSI under the malicious model” respectively present the MPC protocols under the two models with proofs of correctness and security; “Protocol efficiency analysis” is a performance analysis of the protocols; and “Conclusion” is the conclusion.

Existing relevant works

Currently, numerous solutions have emerged that combine MPC with blockchain technology to address privacy protection issues in related fields. Yang et al.26 proposed an MPC framework for enabling privacy protection and public auditing in the industrial IoT by combining MPC and blockchain technologies. Bernabé-Rodríguez et al.27 provided a trusted data transaction scheme for users by applying MPC to blockchain to prevent malicious behavior and support privacy protection. Parthasarathy et al.28 proposed a secure distributed medical record storage and emergency sharing system based on blockchain and MPC technology. Mu et al.29 proposed a peer-to-peer (P2P) contract transaction method based on energy blocks, combining MPC and blockchain technologies for nano-grid applications. Khan et al.30 proposed the blockchain-enabled location identification and efficient validation with encryption framework, which combines MPC and blockchain technologies for real-time validation of connected self-driving vehicles and micro-travel data. While these studies have validated the effectiveness of integrating MPC with blockchain technology in scenarios such as industrial IoT auditing, they have not addressed the specific requirement of securely computing the maximum and minimum values of the sums of keyword-corresponding values in cross-chain data exchange, and thus cannot resolve the problem presented in our context.

In the field of private set computation, Zhao et al.31 constructed a secure determination protocol for set-set intersection relations that can resist collusion based on Lifted ElGamal threshold encryption algorithm. Xie et al.32 constructed a secure computation protocol that can output multi-party set intersection based on Gödel encoding and ElGamal threshold encryption algorithm. However, the above two protocols are only applicable to the case of having a universal set under the semi-honest model, and they cannot resist malicious adversary attacks, nor can they address the problem of this paper.

Li et al.33 constructed an MPC protocol for securely computing the sum of intersection elements based on the Lifted ElGamal threshold encryption algorithm. Tu et al.34 designed an unbalanced private set union (PSU) protocol using lattice-based leveled fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) and permutation matrix private equality testing (pm-PEQT). Yang et al.35 proposed an unbalanced quorum private set intersection (Quorum PSI) MPC protocol based on lattice-based FHE, which supports client-multi-server interaction and can detect elements appearing in at least server sets. While all these protocols were designed for no-full-universe scenarios, they still have certain security limitations—none can resist malicious adversary attacks.

Rindal et al.36 constructed a two-party set intersection protocol resistant to malicious adversary attacks based on oblivious transfer (OT) and garbled bloom filter (GBF), but with high complexity. Liu et al.37 constructed a secure computational set intersection protocol based on the Paillier encryption algorithm, which is able to resist malicious adversary attacks, but only solves the case where there is a universal set.

Li et al.38 constructed the MPC protocol to securely compute the sum of the association values of the intersection elements of a set based on the Paillier encryption algorithm, and this protocol is suitable for the case of no universal set under the semi-honest model, but it is not able to resist the malicious adversary attacks, and it can’t address the problem in this paper. Ma et al.39 constructed a protocol for computing the maximum value of the sums of elements corresponding to the intersection of sets securely, based on the Paillier encryption algorithm. This protocol is suitable for the case of no universal set under the semi-honest model, but the protocol is inefficient and cannot resist malicious adversary attacks.

Additionally, regarding research on privacy-preserving computation of maximum and minimum values, Zhang et al.40 proposed a protocol based on ElGamal homomorphic encryption and threshold decryption, which achieves joint computation of max/min values through one-time encoding. However, the protocol was designed only for the semi-honest model without considering malicious adversary attack scenarios, and it requires data to belong to a predefined universal set whose size affects computational complexity. Li et al.41 designed a protocol based on ElGamal threshold cryptosystem and zero-knowledge proof (ZKP). By encoding methods that map data to predefined ranges and incorporating privacy-preserving shuffling with ZKP to resist malicious attacks, the protocol can withstand malicious adversaries. Nevertheless, it requires data to belong to a known, small-range ordered universal set, limiting the protocol’s generality, while the ZKP and threshold decryption introduce significant computational and communication overhead. Park42 employed secret sharing schemes and secure equality (SEQ) protocols to propose an MPC protocol for privacy-preserving computation of maximum and minimum values. The protocol adopts a bit-by-bit processing approach that facilitates parallel computation. However, it only considers the semi-honest model and cannot resist active attacks. The protocol’s security relies on the SEQ protocol’s robustness—if vulnerabilities exist in the SEQ protocol, the overall protocol’s security would be compromised.

In summary, although there have been more research proposals for privacy computation of sets in blockchain, most of the protocols are proposed under the semi-honest model, and there are fewer studies on the problem of MMSI proposed in this paper. This paper designs a protocol for computing MMSI under the no universal set condition, which is resistant to malicious adversary attacks, and effectively improves the security of the process of computing the maximum and minimum values in cross-chain interactions.

NTRU encryption algorithm

The NTRU encryption algorithm was proposed by Hoffstein, Pipher and Silverman46 in 1998. In addition, the NTRU encryption algorithm relies on the shortest vector problem (SVP) on the lattice rather than the traditional discrete logarithmic or large number decomposition problems, which makes it effectively resistant to quantum computational attacks and is one of the most superior post-quantum encryption algorithms47. Adding obfuscation elements refers to the process where protocol participants insert non-authentic, randomly generated elements into the original set to conceal its true information. Remark 3

If during the simulation of adversary attempts, the adversary deviates from the protocol with malicious inputs, the simulator can extract these malicious inputs. Taking Case 2 as an example, the simulator can obtain the results and by decrypting and when executes step 19 of , then reversely compute the malicious inputs and to extract ’s malicious inputs. However, it must be clarified that this scenario falls under one of the three typical unavoidable cases listed in “Security of malicious model” (providing false inputs during protocol execution). Since the ideal model protocol itself cannot prevent such behavior, it can be disregarded in the security proof of the malicious model protocol. Consequently, Protocol 2 still supports UC-style security.

During the preparation phase of Protocol 2, a malicious participant may refuse to engage in the protocol execution by generating and publishing invalid keys, thereby compromising protocol availability. Although public key validity can be verified through methods such as ZKP verification based on Schnorr-style proofs. Alternatively, after a participant receives the other party’s public key , it tries to compute the inverse element in the ring , and if the inverse element exists, the public key is valid. Nevertheless, according to Goldreich’s theoretical framework44, this situation fundamentally remains one of the three unavoidable cases in the ideal scenario (refusing to participate in the protocol and providing false inputs). Consequently, such behavior is also disregarded in the actual protocol, with no special handling required.

Discussion on the impact of MPC protocols on blockchain efficiency

The impact of MPC protocols on blockchain efficiency is analyzed as follows: MPC protocol execution in the blockchain typically adopts a layered architecture, where computational tasks are completed off-chain, with the blockchain only responsible for verifying results. This design limits the blockchain’s role to being a “trust anchor” rather than a computational entity, thus having minimal impact on on-chain efficiency.

The cryptographic operations and multi-party interactions of MPC protocols are performed off-chain, consuming no on-chain resources. The blockchain only needs to verify ZKP (e.g., zk-SNARKs), whose complexity is significantly lower than that of MPC itself. The core constraints of blockchain efficiency lie in its consensus mechanism and block generation time, while off-chain MPC execution time is typically much shorter than block intervals, avoiding bottlenecks.

Experimental simulations demonstrate that, as shown in Appendix Figure S1, with varying numbers of set elements (the data volume held by blockchain nodes participating in MPC execution), when each party possesses 100 data entries, Protocol 2 with enhanced security achieves an execution time 250 ms. Assuming 50 intersecting elements and a cut-and-choose data volume of 20 in Protocol 2, the execution time for a single encryption/decryption operation during protocol execution is approximately 1.5ms, with a fixed number of communication rounds. Consequently, the off-chain MPC computation time (on the millisecond scale) is significantly shorter than blockchain block intervals (ranging from seconds to minutes), differing by orders of magnitude, thus rendering its impact on overall efficiency negligible. Furthermore, leveraging multi-core CPUs, GPU acceleration, or even distributed computing clusters for off-chain computations could substantially reduce actual execution time.

To roughly analyze the Gas consumption, assuming that the cross-chain MPC protocol is triggered once every 10 min, and 450,000 Gas is needed for on-chain verification (zk-SNARK), the daily Gas capacity of Ethereum is about 2.16e11 Gas (30 million Gas/block 7,200 blocks/day), the daily Gas consumption for MPC protocol execution would be about 6.48e7 Gas (450,000 Gas 144 executions/day). This represents merely 6.48e7/2.16e110.03% of total capacity, effectively negligible in practical terms.

Conclusion

This paper transforms the problem of computing the maximum and minimum values of the sums of keyword-corresponding values in cross-chain data exchange into the problem of computing MMSI in a secure manner, which is able to provide valuable information for many fields such as finance, healthcare, and records management under the protection of data privacy. Based on the FH-NTRU encryption algorithm, the protocols for computing MMSI under the semi-honest and malicious models are proposed respectively. Among them, the protocol under the malicious model avoids the appearance of unfairness and is resistant to malicious adversary attacks. The security of the protocol is proved using the real/ideal model paradigm, and the efficiency of the protocol is analyzed by means of complexity analysis and experimental simulations. The proposed protocol demonstrates high practicality and significant research value, particularly in enhancing the security of cross-chain data exchange and advancing the application of MPC in blockchain.
