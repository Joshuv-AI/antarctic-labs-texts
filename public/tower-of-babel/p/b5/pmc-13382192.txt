Brave New Frameworks: Synthesis and Structural Properties of Novel Covalent Triazine Frameworks From Commercial Dicyanobenzenes for Photocatalytic Applications
Authors: Mads Lund Nygaard Nielsen, Ronghui Lu, Melissa Jane Marks, Rui Shi, Anja Verena Mudring, Marcel Ceccato, Jørgen Skibsted, Jacopo Catalano, Nina Lock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Covalent triazine frameworks (CTFs) have promising photocatalytic applications including water treatment and the conversion of CO2. This study investigates the utilization of the commercially available precursors 1,2‐dicyanobenzene (1,2‐DCB) and 1,3‐dicyanobenzene (1,3‐DCB) to synthesise novel CTF‐type materials. The structure and properties of these materials are compared with those of the benchmark photocatalyst CTF‐1, which is synthesised from 1,4‐dicyanobenzene (1,4‐DCB). Although the materials derived from 1,2‐DCB and 1,3‐DCB as well as 1,4‐DCB contain triazine units characteristic of CTFs, the 2D and 3D structural properties differ substantially depending on the DCB precursor. The geometry of 1,2‐DCB prevents the formation of ordered porous networks, leading to materials that lack semiconducting properties, hence without potential use in photocatalysis. By contrast, materials derived from 1,3‐DCB show greater similarity to CTF‐1, with a proposed extended 2D network structure, a high thermal stability, and visible light absorption. However, the crystallinity and overall surface area of the 1,3‐DCB‐derived products are lower than values reported for CTF‐1. Photocatalytic activity towards the aerobic oxidation of benzylamine is demonstrated for the catalysts derived from 1,3‐DCB and compared with materials prepared from 1,4‐DCB. Overall, this study demonstrates that commercially available DCBs can form triazine‐containing CTF‐type materials with tuneable structural and optical properties.

Introduction

Covalent triazine frameworks (CTFs) are a subclass of the broader covalent organic frameworks (COFs) materials class, and have garnered significant attention for potential use in photocatalysis [1], as support for single‐atom catalysts [2], and in gas storage [3]. COFs have porous network structures and are composed of small organic building blocks assembled through reticular chemistry [4]. COFs have attracted much interest due to their properties, such as large surface areas and porosities, customizability, and abundant catalytic active sites. These properties are shared with the metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) materials class, but COFs generally exhibit higher thermal and chemical stability than MOFs [5]. CTFs are examples of such stable COFs and contain covalently linked triazine motifs [1]. CTFs have shown promising applications within photocatalysis [6] due to their visible light absorption and high surface areas.

The porosity of CTFs, and COFs in general, is governed by the size and shape of the building blocks. Most CTF studies are based on a limited number of monomers, such as 1,4‐dicyanobenzene (1,4‐DCB) or 1,3,5‐tricyanobenzene, which are used for the formation of either CTF‐1 or CTF‐0, respectively [7, 8]. CTF‐1 is perhaps the most widely investigated CTF due to its high surface area, which results from the hexagonal micropores in the material [7]. In comparison, CTF‐0 is constructed from denser layers with smaller micropores, resulting in a lower surface area [8]. However, CTF‐0 boasts the highest nitrogen content of CTFs made from commercially available DCBs. This large nitrogen content is beneficial for the anchoring of metal ions, for example, in catalytic applications [9].

Instead of using commercially available precursors, recent studies have targeted the synthesis of custom‐made monomers to tailor specific sought‐after properties in the resulting CTF materials [10, 11]. This is often done for areas of application such as sensing and gas storage, where the inclusion of specific functional groups can enhance interactions between a particular substrate and the CTF material [12, 14]. A specific example of this is 1‐naphthol sensing in water [12]. However, the development of novel CTF materials based on custom‐made monomers is often expensive and may require the design of novel synthesis procedures for CTF formation.

The potential development of novel CTF photocatalysts from commercially available DCBs other than 1,4‐DCB is virtually unexplored. Only 1,3‐dicyanobenzene (1,3‐DCB) has been reported in few studies for the formation of CTF‐based materials [15, 19], and they have been synthesised using either an ionothermal or a low‐temperature superacid‐catalysed route. Neither the structural nor the photocatalytic properties of these materials have been benchmarked against CTF‐1, and 1,3‐DCB‐derived CTFs have only been explored in a narrow set of catalytic applications, such as H2 evolution.

In this study, we investigate the potential of preparing CTF‐type materials from 1,2‐ and 1,3‐DCB using a triflic acid‐catalysed route. As part of the material preparation, this study also focuses on the potential of surface area optimisation via post‐synthetic heat treatment (HT), as inspired by a previous study proposing that the stacking configuration and surface area in CTF‐1 are strongly influenced by residual triflic acid from the synthesis [20]. Specifically, CTF‐1 containing triflic acid exhibits a low surface area due to micropore blocking, whereas triflic acid removal by thermal treatment leads to changes in the stacking configuration, hence a larger surface area [20].

This study compares the structural properties of the materials derived from 1,2‐DCB and 1,3‐DCB to those of CTF‐1, which is derived from 1,4‐DCB. The molecular‐ and long‐range structure, porosity, and light absorption and emission properties of the novel materials are compared to the benchmark CTF‐1, and the impact of post‐synthetic HT is explored. Lastly, the photocatalytic properties of selected CTF‐type materials are investigated against a model reaction, namely the aerobic oxidation of benzylamine to N‐benzylidenebenzylamine.

We suggest that CTFs, and COFs more broadly, based on commercially available monomers are promising for potential future applications. We consider that this study can serve as a generalizable procedure for the assessment of novel COFs in terms of synthesis, characterisation, and initial catalytic evaluation.

Results and Discussion

Part 1—Synthesis and Structural Characterisation

The formation of CTF‐type materials is explored with three precursors: 1,2‐dicyanobenzene (1,2‐DCB), 1,3‐dicyanobenzene (1,3‐DCB), and 1,4‐dicyanobenzene (1,4‐DCB). The synthesis protocol consists of two steps: (i) triflic acid catalysed condensation of DCB followed by (ii) heat treatment (HT) of the as‐synthesised powder, resulting in solid products with yields up to 80% relative to the precursor mass. Materials resulting from the polycondensation reaction (step i) are denoted 1,2‐CTF, 1,3‐CTF, and 1,4‐CTF herein, while compounds formed after subsequent HT of these powders (step ii) are denoted 1,2‐CTF (HT), 1,3‐CTF (HT), and 1,4‐CTF (HT) herein. It should be noted that 1,4‐CTF is typically referred to as CTF‐1 in literature. The synthesis conditions differentiating the CTF variants can be seen in Table 1.

The solid products derived from 1,2‐DCB clearly show carbonisation, as evidenced by their black colour, while the colour of the other materials ranges from yellowish (1,3‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF) to dark green (1,3‐CTF (HT) and 1,4‐CTF (HT)) (Figure S1). Such colour changes upon HT of pristine CTFs have been observed in other studies. It has been attributed to the removal of excess triflic acid associated with changes in the stacking configuration of the 2D molecular layers (see further discussion below) [20, 21].

This study mainly focuses on the structure and properties of materials prepared from 1,2‐DCB and 1,3‐DCB. Herein, these compounds are compared with materials synthesized from 1,4‐DCB under identical conditions, which were recently reported by us in ref. [22].

The 2D molecular structures of the solid products were investigated via 13C solid‐state magic‐angle spinning (MAS) NMR (13C MAS NMR) spectroscopy and Fourier‐transformed infrared (FT‐IR) spectroscopy. The results of these analyses are summarised in Figure 1.

(a) Solid‐state 13C{1H} CP/MAS NMR spectra (9.4 T, νR = 10.0 kHz) and (b) FT‐IR data for all synthesised CTF materials. In the solid‐state 13C‐NMR spectra, asterisks denote spinning side bands. In (a) and (b) peaks are assigned to functional groups: triazine (red), precursor nitrile (blue), aromatic carbon atoms (green), unknown alkene impurity or structural defect (orange), amide functionalities (magenta). Proposed structural motifs in (c) 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT), (d) 1,3‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF (HT), and (e) 1,2‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF (HT). In (e) the terminal X groups denote its unknown identity. The 13C‐NMR and FT‐IR spectra for 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF HT (previously published in ref. [22]) are included in Figure 1 for direct comparison with the 1,2‐ and 1,3‐CTF materials.

Formation of triazine groups in all pristine and heat‐treated CTF samples is confirmed by 13C MAS NMR (Figure 1a) and FT‐IR (Figure 1b) spectroscopy. Specifically, triazines are evidenced by the peak located at 170 ppm in the 13C NMR spectra and the vibrational bands in the FT‐IR spectra located at around 800, 1349, and 1501 cm−1 [20, 23, 24]. The conversion of the precursor nitrile groups for formation of triazine‐linked networks is supported by the small magnitude of the vibrational band at 2240 cm−1 in Figure 1b and of the peak at 115 ppm in Figure 1a (shaded in blue), indicative of the nitrile [20, 25].

Based only on topological arguments, the molecular geometry of the precursors, and the confirmed presence of triazines, we propose that the formation of triazines from a trimerization reaction could result in the molecular motifs displayed in Figure 1c–e. The proposed molecular structure of 1,4‐CTF is simply CTF‐1, and the molecular structure shown in Figure 1c agrees with the spectroscopic data presented in Figure 1a,b, which also matches published CTF‐1 studies [7, 23, 26]. Likewise, the FT‐IR spectra of the 1,3‐CTF samples match those reported in a different study [15]. We propose the 1,3‐DCB precursor could potentially form polymeric 2D triazine sheets, as sketched in Figure S8. To the best of our knowledge, the molecular structures of 1,2‐DCB‐derived CTFs have not previously been reported and will be discussed below.

Besides confirming the nitrile to triazine conversion, the spectroscopic data (Figure 1a,b) provide additional insight into the molecular structures. The FT‐IR and 13C MAS NMR spectra for the 1,2‐CTF samples differ notably from the other materials, as the FT‐IR data contain multiple vibrational bands in the 1700–1500 cm−1 region and a broad vibrational band above 3000 cm−1 (Figure 1, shaded magenta). Furthermore, the resonance assigned to the triazine group in the 13C MAS NMR spectra for the 1,2‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF (HT) materials is broader and has a lower intensity than the other triazine samples. This can be attributed to the steric hindrance related to condensation of 1,2‐DCB, which prevents the complete conversion of nitrile to triazine groups. Hence, while some nitrile groups can trimerize to form triazine groups, the remainder will remain unreacted or hydrolyse into amide groups upon reaction termination with water [23, 27]. This conversion of nitrile groups to amides would give rise to vibrational bands in the 1700–1500 cm−1 region due to carbonyl formation and a broad vibrational band above 3000 cm−1 due to N–H groups [23, 25]. However, no peaks have been assigned to amide groups in the NMR spectra, possibly as a result of low peak resolution and the close proximity between peaks associated with triazine and amide groups [23]. Instead, X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) confirms amide functionalities in 1,2‐CTF (HT) (Figure S5). We propose that by combining the triazine and bridging benzene units with the geometric structure of 1,2‐DCB through topological considerations, the general molecular structure presented in Figure 1e is the best description of the disordered 1,2‐CTF materials. As the molecular connectivity is not known beyond the proposed triazine building block (Figure 1e), an “X” has been used to indicate molecular (e.g., cyano‐, amide‐, or triazine units). This structural description of the 1,2‐CTF materials is also in good agreement with XPS (Figure S5) confirming the presence of triazine moieties, but in a comparatively lower concentration than in 1,4‐CTF (HT). Furthermore, XPS confirms unreacted nitrile in addition to the amide functionalities in 1,2‐CTF (HT), which are indicative of a comparatively low degree of polymerisation.

The black colour of both 1,2‐CTF samples suggests that carbonisation of the material occurred during formation. This phenomenon is often observed in CTF‐based compounds synthesised at high temperatures using ionothermal synthesis, where the harsh synthesis conditions result in a partial breakdown of organic constituents. Black‐coloured products are typically associated with a lack of semiconducting capabilities [28, 29]. It is noticeable that neither the 13C MAS NMR data (Figure 1a) nor the FT‐IR data (Figure 1b) show indications of graphite impurities or other elemental carbon side products, which are likely to form by carbonisation of CTF. However, this might be due to a low graphite amount and the data resolution, and similar missing indications can be noted in other studies of carbonised CTF materials [6, 7, 28, 30]. Based on these findings, we note that carbonisation might only be a surface effect.

A few discrepancies remain between the proposed molecular structures and the spectroscopic data. (i) The proposed motifs in 1,2‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF (Figure 1d,e) suggest that more than two distinguishable aromatic carbon atoms should be present in the bridging benzene groups, which should be observable in the 120–140 ppm region in the 13C MAS NMR spectra (Figure 1a, shaded green). However, the NMR data only show two partially resolved resonances. The inability to fully resolve all peaks in the aromatic region might be attributed to a low chemical shift difference between the aromatic carbon atoms, possibly in combination with material disorder and anisotropic effects often seen in solid‐state NMR spectra [31, 32]. (ii) Both the 1,2‐ and 1,3‐CTF samples contain a small unassigned peak at 113 ppm in the 13C MAS NMR spectra (Figure 1a, shaded in orange) and a small, narrow band above 3000 cm−1 in the FT‐IR data (Figure 1b), which may be assigned to an unknown alkene impurity. Alternatively, the peak at 113 ppm may be attributed to carbon atoms adjacent to unreacted nitrile groups, which have been observed in 13C‐NMR spectra in other CTF studies [8]. (iii) The FT‐IR data for the 1,2‐ and 1,3‐CTF materials display multiple unidentified vibration bands around 1200 cm−1 which we are unable to assign.

In conclusion, based on the above spectroscopic investigations of the molecular structure, all synthesised materials have shown clear indications of the formation of triazine‐containing motifs. The 1,2‐CTFs stand out as disordered materials with several unassigned peaks in the FT‐IR spectra and potential carbonisation. Disorder in 1,2‐CTFs is overall in agreement with the precursor geometry, which prevents complete condensation into triazines.

Powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) was used to evaluate the long‐range (crystalline) order of the CTF materials (Figure 2a,b). Additionally, the elemental composition was determined by CHNS elemental analysis (Figure 2c), and UV–vis DRS was used to characterise the light absorption properties of the materials (Figure 2d).

PXRD patterns for (a) all CTF samples and (b) for 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT) along with calculated X‐ray diffraction patterns for CTF‐1 based on published structural data [21]. (c) Data from CHNS elemental analysis of all CTF materials. (d) Tauc plots and linear fits for bandgap estimation of all 1,3‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF materials. The CHNS and Tauc plots for 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT) are shown for comparison with the other materials and were previously published in a separate study [22]. CHNS and UV–vis DRS raw data are presented in Sections S2 and S3.

The diffraction patterns presented in Figure 2a,b indicate that all CTF samples are either semicrystalline or amorphous. Both 1,4‐CTF materials display few broad but well‐defined Bragg peaks characteristic of a semicrystalline solid. Overall, the experimental pattern matches the calculated diffraction pattern based on the published structure of CTF‐1 with two different stacking configurations [20, 21, 33]. HT of 1,4‐CTF results in an increase in the relative intensity of the most intense peak at 7°, which is attributed to the removal of residual triflic acid and a transition in the stacking sequence of the CTF layers from AB staggered to AA eclipsed (Figure 2b), as first reported by Yang et al. [20] and also discussed in [22]. A schematic drawing of these different stacking configurations is presented in Figure S6. The transition in stacking sequence due to the removal of triflic acid was further supported by CHNS elemental analysis (Figure 2c), where 1,4‐CTF (HT) shows a negligible sulfur content, as previously reported by us [22].

Additionally, the removal of triflic acid from the 1,4‐CTF hints at a slight band gap narrowing from 2.5 eV (1,4‐CTF) to 2.4 eV (1,4‐CTF (HT)) in addition to a clear colour change of the samples (Figure S1), which is also reflected in the UV–vis DRS data [22] (Figure 2d and S2). This trend is consistent with previous studies on triflic acid removal from CTF‐1 [25, 33]. However, the reported band gaps should be considered only approximate, as the prerequisites for a proper band gap determination based on linear fitting of the Tauc plots are not given [34]. The increase in absorption is not continuous, but the spectra show multiple, rather broad step‐like features frequently observed in 2D materials or materials with platelet morphology like graphene, graphene oxides or carbon nitrides where quantization effects occur. In addition, structural analysis confirms that the samples do not consist of a uniform, defined material but rather a spread of polymerisation, stacking, and aggregation, and a content of impurities/terminal functional groups, which also give rise to multiple overlapping electronic transitions. Photoluminescence (PL) confirms this, as emission spectra are excitation wavelength dependent (Figures S3 and S4, Supporting Information). In the emission spectra there are two overlapping, but still distinguishable features that are reminiscent of exciton peaks. However, they can be observed in all samples at the same position (within errors), which points to a different, rather extrinsic origin. The complexity of electronic transitions becomes further evident in the comparison of excitation (Figures S3 and S4) and DRS spectra (Figure S2), as technically, the excitation spectrum should correlate with the absorption spectrum, which it does not. Broad emission bands have been reported for CTF‐1 and similar materials at approx. 400–650 nm with a maximum at around 480 nm [26]. Upon excitation at low wavelengths (415 nm or lower) both 1,3‐CTF (HT) and 1,4‐CTF (HT) display a similar local maximum in the emission spectra, but the emission band stretches to 800 nm for all excitation wavelengths and both samples. Hence, we propose the emission spectra to both represent features intrinsic to CTF‐type materials (in the approximate range 400–650 nm) overlapped with features of extrinsic origin at longer wavelengths.

According to PXRD data some degree of long‐range periodicity is observed for the 1,3‐CTF samples with two very broad but distinct peaks at 15° and 27°; however, it differs significantly from the 1,4‐CTF samples. The PXRD data of the 1,3‐CTF samples align with scattering data for similar 1,3‐DCB‐derived CTF materials reported in other studies [15, 35]. Interestingly, we recently reported a very similar PXRD pattern of a highly disordered variant of CTF‐1. The local order of this compound was investigated by X‐ray total scattering and pair‐distribution function analysis revealing a local structure (up to approx. 8 Å) identical to that of a semicrystalline CTF‐1 variant (denoted 1,4‐CTF herein) [22].

The heat‐treatment of 1,3‐CTF removed residual triflic acid as confirmed by CHNS analysis revealing a negligible sulfur content for 1,3‐CTF (HT) (Figure 2c), yet, the original “long‐range order” was maintained (Figure 2a). The removal of triflic acid resulted in a similar change in bandgap size as observed for in 1,4‐CTF, which is seen in the Tauc plots in Figure 2d. Here, the calculated bandgaps were also estimated to 2.5 eV for 1,3‐CTF and 2.4 eV for 1,3‐CTF (HT). It is noteworthy that survey XPS spectra reveal fluorine in 1,3‐CTF (HT) (Figure S5), which contrasts CHNS data (Figure 2c). One possible explanation might be that some fluorine impurities are present at the sample surface.

PXRD data of 1,2‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF (HT) samples indicate that no long‐range order is present, as no Bragg peaks can be observed. The amorphous nature of the 1,2‐CTF sample is readily explained by the inability of 1,2‐DCB to form large and uniform triazine‐based sheets, as discussed in detail in relation to the spectroscopic data. As the molecular structure of these compounds is disordered, the formation of a compound with long‐range order is unlikely, thus explaining the amorphous nature of the 1,2‐CTF materials. The 1,2‐CTF materials do not show semiconducting properties according to the featureless UV–vis DRS data (Figure S2b), which can be explained by carbonisation and/or a low degree of polymerisation of the compounds.

The degree of polymerisation of the CTF materials was investigated using gel permeation chromatography (GPC), providing information about the molecular masses of the materials. A low molecular mass is correlated with a low degree of polymerisation. Further, the thermal stability of CTF‐based frameworks is also influenced by the degree of polymerisation, hence the formation of large π‐conjugated networks. The presence of π‐conjugated systems has been shown to increase the thermal stability of other polymer‐based materials, and a similar effect is likely observable for CTF‐based frameworks [36, 37]. The thermal stability was investigated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). Results from both GPC and TGA are presented in Figure 3.

(a) GPC data for CTF materials, 1,4‐DCB and the solvent used for sample preparation (THF). (b) TGA data for all CTF materials. The GPC and TGA data for 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT) materials are plotted for comparison with the other compounds, and these data have been published in a recent study by us [22], as are GPC data for 1,4‐DCB and THF.

GPC is a valuable tool for quantifying small molecular mass ranges, whereas the absence of a signal indirectly identifies a polymeric material with a large molecular mass and limited solubility. For example, the 1,4‐CTF samples exhibit no GPC signals [22] in agreement with similar findings due to their limited solubility [38]. The 1,3‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF samples give GPC signals (Points A, B, and C) corresponding to molecular masses of around 465 g/mol (A), 176 g/mol (B), and 598 g/mol (C). Point B could originate from encapsulated monomer in the reaction, given its similar retention time compared to the monomer sample (Point D). However, the spectroscopic data in Figure 1a,b show no clear indication of this. The 1,3‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF materials contain similar molecular mass distributions, given the similar molecular masses of points A and C. This contradicts the expectations based on the spectroscopy (Figure 1a,b) and diffraction data (Figure 2a), suggesting that 1,2‐DCB does not form large networks. A polydisperse mass distribution in the 1,3‐CTF sample is likely an explanation for this observation. If this sample contained a portion of smaller and more soluble sheets, these would be detectable via GPC. In contrast, the larger insoluble networks would remain undetected due to their low solubility in THF, similar to that of the 1,4‐CTF samples. Given the molecular mass of the DCBs of 128 g/mol, it is likely that only the smaller CTF fragments with molecular masses corresponding to 3–4 DCB molecules have dissolved in THF. We note that the GPC analysis presented herein serves the purpose to investigate trends between different materials rather than their exact molecular masses.

Results from the TGA analysis reveal that both pristine and heat‐treated 1,4‐CTF [22] and 1,3‐CTF samples exhibit similar thermal stability, with a decomposition temperature of approx. 650°C. The mass loss at approx. 350°C correlates with the removal of triflic acid from the materials, which aligns with the CHNS data (Figure 2c), showing negligible sulfur content for the heat‐treated samples. When disregarding triflic acid removal, the 1,2‐CTF materials show lower thermal stability compared to the other samples, with an onset decomposition temperature of 350°C. The lower thermal stability is attributed to the lower degree of polymerisation, hence a smaller conjugated network [36, 39]. The low thermal stability of the 1,2‐CTF material also explains the differences in GPC data for this sample and 1,2‐CTF (HT), where no detectable signals are observed for 1,2‐CTF (HT) (Figure 3a). It is likely that HT of the 1,2‐CTF results in a breakdown of the already small components, thereby eliminating the constituents responsible for point C from the resulting 1,2‐CTF (HT) sample.

In conclusion, both 1,3‐DCB and 1,2‐DCB can form triazine‐based materials with several differences in structural and physical properties. We propose that samples derived from 1,3‐DCB exhibit some similarities in structure to those of 1,4‐DCB‐derived CTF‐1, including a 2D triazine‐connected framework and semicrystallinity. These materials also share similar light absorption properties and thermal stability, which are noteworthy as these properties are highly relevant for photocatalytic applications. However, 1,3‐CTF does not undergo any apparent crystal structure transformation upon HT, which differs from 1,4‐CTF. The structural properties of samples derived from 1,2‐DCB differ from the other materials. Precursor geometry prevents the 1,2‐DCB monomer from forming large networks of triazine‐linked aromatic groups, resulting in lower thermal stability and an amorphous crystal structure. Additionally, 1,2‐CTF samples undergo some degree of carbonisation during synthesis, producing black‐coloured products with no semiconducting behaviour.

Characterisation of the microstructural properties of the synthesised materials is augmented through investigations of their morphology and porosity (Part 2), followed by investigations of the photocatalytic performance of selected materials (Part 3).

Part 2—Characterisation of Microstructure and Porosity

The microstructure of the CTF materials was characterised using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), with images presented in Figure S7. All six CTF materials have similar microstructure being composed of large particle agglomerates with no noteworthy morphological characteristics. This is consistent with other DCB‐derived CTF materials [25, 33]. The CTF pores are not visible via SEM, given their small pore widths, e.g., 11 Å for CTF‐1 [20, 40]. N2 adsorption/desorption measurements were conducted to characterise the surface area and porosity of the CTF compounds (Figure 4).

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms for (a) 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT), (b) 1,3‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF (HT), and (c) 1,2‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF (HT). Note the BET surface area of 1,3‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF was below the detection limit. Data for 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT) are included for comparison with the other materials and have been published by us in a previous study [22].

The isotherm for the 1,4‐CTF sample [22] can be classified as a Type II isotherm, characteristic of nonporous solids (Figure 4a). This can be attributed to its AB stacking configuration, which results in the blocking of the micropores and thus a low surface area (3 m2/g). After HT, the stacking configuration is altered in the 1,4‐CTF (HT) sample to an AA configuration, which causes pore realignment and the induction of microporosity (Figure S6). This is evidenced by the significant increase in surface area for 1,4‐CTF from 3 to 524 m2/g upon HT to 1,4‐CTF (HT) [22]. As a consequence of HT and pore realignment, the N2 adsorption/desorption isotherm changes from a Type II to a Type I isotherm, which is characteristic of a microporous solid (Figure 4a). The experimentally derived pore width in this sample aligns with the theoretical pore width for CTF‐1 of 11 Å [22]. Structural changes of CTF‐1 upon HT are also observed in the PXRD data (Figure 2) and has been reported in other studies [20, 22, 25].

The isotherms of the 1,3‐CTF and 1,2‐CTF samples can also be characterised as Type II (nonporous solids), in agreement with the low surface areas (Figure 4b,c). The surface area of the 1,3‐CTF samples could not be determined by Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) from N2 adsorption, due to negative adsorption data in the relative pressure range generally used for surface area determination. While negative adsorption is unphysical, it underlines the negligible N2 accessible surface area of the 1,3‐CTF materials (see further details in the Experimental section). A low surface area of 3.2 m2/g was reported for this material in a study by Shao et al. [15]. The low surface areas for 1,3‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF (HT) can possibly be explained by the layer stacking in the materials, as no transition in the long‐range stacking configuration was observed upon HT (Figure 2a). Thus, presence of any micropores found in ‘single 2D layers’ could still be N2 inaccessible upon stacking, similarly to pore inaccessibility in AB stacked CTF‐1. Furthermore, the similarities between the proposed pore structures of the 1,3‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF (HT) samples (based on topology) with CTF‐0 prepared from 1,3,5‐tricyanobenzene (Figure S8) can also explain the low surface area. The theoretical molecular structure of CTF‐0 has a pore width of 5 Å [41]. For comparison, the kinetic diameter of N2 is 3.6 Å [42]. The proposed molecular structure of 1,3‐CTF contains two types of pores, where one is identical to the one found in CTF‐0 (P1 in Figure S8), while the other type of pore is wider (P2 in Figure S8). The small dimensions of the pores in 1,3‐CTF may also explain the polydisperse molecular mass distributions of 1,3‐CTF proposed via GPC (Figure 3a), as the narrow micropores may retain residual 1,3‐DCB or intermediate motifs formed during the synthesis. The surface areas of 1,3‐CTF reported in other studies are generally higher than the nondetected area reported herein, which might be attributed to the use of ionothermal synthesis procedures in other studies. Ionothermal synthesis might cause carbonised structures with enhanced surface areas, and a link between carbonisation and high surface areas has been reported for CTF‐1 [7, 43]. The low surface area of the 1,2‐CTF samples can most likely be attributed to the unsuccessful formation of an extended porous network as also suggested by GPC (Figure 3a).

Part 3—Photocatalytic Experiments

The photocatalytic performance of the 1,3‐CTF samples was investigated via photocatalytic oxidation of benzylamine to N‐benzylidenebenzylamine (see reaction scheme in Figure S9) and compared with 1,4‐CTF samples [22]. This reaction has previously been employed as a model reaction for CTF‐based materials as well as other photocatalysts [44, 45]. The model reaction is used to evaluate differences in photocatalytic activity under specific reaction conditions and is not optimised to ensure complete conversion of benzylamine for each catalyst. The 1,2‐CTF samples were excluded from catalytic screening, as these materials are not semiconductors according to UV–vis DRS data (Figure S2b).

The results of the photocatalytic experiments are presented in Figure 5 and Table S2. The experiments were carried out using two different irradiation wavelengths of 365 and 410 nm, respectively. Both 1,4‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF materials absorb light at these wavelengths, according to DRS data (Figure S2a).

(a) Obtained yield and selectivity when using CTF materials as photocatalysts for the aerobic oxidation of benzylamine to N‐benzylidenebenzylamine. (b) Photocatalytic stability studies using CTF materials for benzylamine oxidation upon repeated usage with 365 nm light. Data related to the photocatalytic screening of 1,4‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF (HT) have previously been published by us in ref. [22].

Figure 5 indicates that all four 1,4‐CTF [22] and 1,3‐CTF samples are active for the photocatalytic oxidation of benzylamine to N‐benzylidenebenzylamine, with the 1,4‐CTF samples exhibiting superior activity. Control experiments showed no noteworthy activity without either the CTF‐based catalysts or light (Table S2). The superior performance of the 1,4‐CTF materials is most likely attributed to the higher surface area of these samples, as increased surface area should increase the number of catalytic active sites [46]. This phenomenon can explain the increase in photocatalytic activity when heat‐treating 1,4‐CTF to 1,4‐CTF (HT), as this results in an increased surface area due to micropore alignment [22]. However, the increase in surface area does not translate directly into a proportional increase in photocatalytic activity, suggesting that not all the N2 accessible surface area is accessible for photocatalytic reactions. This may be a result of the microporous nature of the 1,4‐CTF materials, which may impose mass transport limitations, as also discussed in ref. [22]. Furthermore, we cannot rule out that other factors play a role on the observed differences in photocatalytic activity in addition to the surface area (e.g., differences in photophysical properties such as charge‐carrier lifetime and band positions). The similarities in photocatalytic activity of the 1,3‐CTF and 1,3‐CTF (HT) are likely a result of the negligible structural changes induced via heat‐treatment. The presence of amide functionalities has previously been proposed to improve photocatalytic activity in CTF‐1 [25]. However, according to XPS data (Figure S5), the amide content in both 1,3‐CTF (HT) and 1,4‐CTF (HT) is negligible, and we can rule out that the amide content leads to the observed difference in catalytic activity between the two materials.

The photocatalytic stability of the CTF samples was assessed by performing repeated oxidation reactions (Figure 5b), revealing some decrease in photocatalytic performance upon recycling. The results in Figure 5b are normalised to compensate for the partial loss of solid photocatalyst that occurs when the powder is recovered and transferred to a new batch. The observed reduction in photocatalytic activity upon recycling might be due to local structural changes in the catalyst. However, a high stability of the materials is expected at operating conditions, given the general high thermal and chemical stability of triazine‐based photocatalysts as widely reported in other studies [8, 47, 49]. Furthermore, post mortem characterisation by FT‐IR spectroscopy confirms a high resemblance between the spectra of samples before and after catalytic screenings and, specifically, that the triazines are preserved at catalytic conditions (Figure S10).

Conclusions

This study investigates the use of commercially available DCBs to synthesize CTF‐based photocatalysts. CTF‐based materials were prepared using 1,2‐DCB and 1,3‐DCB precursors, and their structural and photocatalytic properties were compared to those of benchmark CTF‐1 (denoted 1,4‐CTF herein), synthesised from 1,4‐DCB [22]. Additionally, the three samples were subjected to heat treatment for potential surface area enhancement [20].

The 1,3‐CTF materials show some similarities to 1,4‐CTF such as a triazine‐based network, some degree of long‐range periodicity, high thermal stability, and similar light absorption properties including comparable bandgap size. However, the stacking configuration of the 1,3‐CTF is not modified through HT, in contrast to 1,4‐CTF, resulting in very low surface areas for both 1,3‐CTF samples. We propose the differences in surface areas between the 1,3‐CTF and 1,4‐CTF samples to cause the superior photocatalytic activity of the 1,4‐CTF materials. 1,3‐CTF samples as well as 1,4‐CTF compounds show structural stability upon catalytic usage. Despite containing triazine moieties, the 1,2‐CTF samples differ significantly from the other CTF‐type materials. The geometry of the precursor prevents the full conversion of nitrile to triazine groups and, thus, the formation of a triazine‐linked network with long‐range order. The 1,2‐CTF materials are partly carbonised, show low thermal stability and do not have semiconducting properties.

This study demonstrates that commercial alternatives to 1,4‐DCB can form CTF‐type materials with properties somewhat similar to those of CTF‐1. However, future studies should establish methods for increasing the surface area and preventing carbonisation. Future studies could also focus on DCBs with larger molecular sizes to increase the surface area and pore widths, thereby enhancing their potential for catalytic applications.

Experimental

All chemicals used in this study were purchased from Merck and used without further purification.

Synthesis

The procedure used for synthesising the CTFs was adapted from a study published by Yang et al. [20], which centred around CTF‐1 synthesised from 1,4‐DCB. We also previously reported on the synthesis of 1,4‐CTF materials using this approach [22]. In the present study, the monomer (1,2‐DCB, 1,3‐DCB, or 1,4‐DCB) (0.256 g, 2 mmol) and triflic acid (89 μL, 1 mmol) were mixed in a glass test tube. The test tube was subsequently submerged in liquid N2 and connected to a Schlenk line. After being submerged for 5 min in the liquid N2, the test tube was evacuated under dynamic vacuum for 5 min. Afterwards, the test tube was extracted from the liquid N2 and purged with Ar for 5 min. Lastly, the tube was evacuated again, while placed in liquid N2, before being flame sealed. The closed‐off tube was placed in a muffle furnace and heated to 250°C using a ramp of 5°C/min. The tube was heated at 250°C for 12 h. After the tube had cooled to room temperature, it was submerged in liquid N2 for 5 min and opened. The solid product in the tube was isolated by filtration and washed three times with demineralised water and ethanol. To remove leftover demineralised water and ethanol, the washed products were dried in an oven for 12 h at 60°C. Products from this synthesis route were named according to the monomer used: 1,2‐CTF, 1,3‐CTF, and 1,4‐CTF.

Following, the pristine samples were subjected to HT aimed for triflic acid removal, also published by Yang et al. [20]. Here, the as‐synthesised CTF powders were placed in a ceramic crucible. The crucible was transferred to a tube furnace and purged with N2 for 30 min using a flow rate of 100 mL/min. Following, the CTF‐1 sample was heated to 350°C using a ramp of 5°C/min. The samples were heated at 350°C for 2 h before cooling to room temperature (without active cooling). Samples subjected to HT are designated with the postfix “HT.”

Characterisation

FT‐IR spectroscopy was performed with a Nicolet Summit Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific). The spectrometer was equipped with an attenuated total reflectance unit with a diamond crystal. All collected spectra were averaged from 64 scans with a resolution of 1 cm−1. Unless otherwise stated, the spectra were subject to baseline correction and wavelength‐dependent penetration correction by utilising methods built into the spectrometer software.

The solid‐state MAS nuclear magnetic resonance spectra were obtained on a Bruker Avance 400 (9.39 T) spectrometer, using a 4‐mm double‐tuned X‐{1H/19F}} MAS probe. The 13C MAS NMR spectra were obtained with 13C{1H} cross‐polarization using a spinning speed of νR = 10.0 kHz and a relaxation delay of 10 s. The spectra and all 13C chemical shifts are referenced to neat tetramethylsilane (Si(CH3)4).

PXRD data were collected on an Aeris Research Edition Benchtop diffractometer produced by Malvern Panalytical using a PIXcel1D detector. All measurements were carried out using Cu Kα1 and Kα2 radiation (λ = 1.54 Å), with powdered samples mounted on low‐background silicon sample holders, and scattering data was collected in Bragg–Brentano geometry. Data were measured in the 2θ range of 5°–90°, with a measurement time of 41 min.

CHNS Elemental Composition Analysis was performed using an Elementar Vario Macrocube analyzer. Samples were prepared by packing and sealing 20–25 mg of each CTF sample in tin foil using a manual press. For the measurements, sulfanilamide was utilised as a reference. The elemental composition data were corrected using thermogravimetric data, where each CTF sample was calcined at 1000°C for 2 h under atmospheric conditions.

UV–vis DRS spectra were measured on an AvaSpec 2048 fiber‐optical spectrometer made by Avantes. The spectrometer was equipped with an Ava Light‐DHS light source and an FCR 19UV200‐2‐ME reflection probe. BaSO4 was used as a reference material for all spectra, and all powdered samples were measured in their pristine form.

PL excitation and emission spectra were measured on an Edinburgh FLS1000 combined fluorescence spectrometer, equipped with a 450W xenon lamp for excitation. All the samples were measured in their powder form at room temperature.

GPC was performed using a Shodex KF‐806L column operating at 35°C. The instrument utilized a dual sensor setup: one sensor was an L‐4000 UV detector manufactured by Hitachi operating at 256 nm. The other sensor was a Knauer‐manufactured High‐Temperature Differential Refractive Index sensor. This sensor operated at 50°C with THF as a reference eluent. Sample preparation was conducted by suspending 20 mg of a CTF material in 4 mL of THF, and stirring the suspension at 50°C for 24 h. After this, the samples were allowed to settle overnight before the liquid phase was extracted using a syringe equipped with a particle filter.

TGA was carried out using a TG 209 T1 Libra instrument from Netzsch. Samples were prepared by weighing 5–6 mg of each CTF sample in alumina crucibles. The analyses were conducted by heating the samples at a heating rate of 10°C/min to 800°C under a N2 atmosphere.

N2 adsorption/desorption measurements were carried out using a Quantachrome Autosorb iQ2 Automated Gas Sorption setup. Samples were degassed at 120°C under dynamic vacuum for 12 h to remove water adsorbed on the sample surface before each measurement. The BET surface area was determined based on five N2 adsorption data points using the instrument software. Full N2 adsorption/desorption isotherms comprised 76 adsorption and 40 desorption data points for the 1,4‐CTF samples (data reproduced from ref. [22]) and 40 adsorption and desorption points for the 1,2‐CTFs and 1,3‐CTFs. Reliable determination of surface areas by BET requires samples with a minimum absolute surface area of ≈1 m2, i.e., large sample amounts are required for the collection of N2 sorption data for materials with low surface areas [50]. For measurements in this study a sample amount of ≈200 mg was used. Surface area determination of the low surface area 1,3‐CTF materials was repeated with a sample mass of more than 1 g. In both cases unphysical data were obtained as a consequence of the low surface area.

SEM was conducted using a Tescan Clara SEM employing a 4Q‐BSE detector. The detector choice permitted complete visualisation of topological features. For sample preparation, CTF powders were dispersed on conductive carbon tape mounted on SEM metal stubs. Samples were coated with 6 nm of platinum before analysis to prevent charge build‐up and damage to the sample during analysis. This was done by employing a Leica SCD500 sputter coater. SEM images were acquired using a low acceleration potential of 5 keV, as an additional strategy to prevent charge build‐up during analysis.

XPS was performed using a Kratos Axis UltraDLD spectrometer equipped with a monochromated Al Kα X‐ray source (1486.7 eV), typically operated at 225 W. The analysis chamber was maintained in the high‐vacuum range (4–5 × 10−9 torr). Survey spectra were collected at a pass energy of 160 eV with a 1.0 eV step size, and high‐resolution spectra were measured at 20 eV pass energy with a 0.1 eV step size. Spectral processing was carried out in CasaXPS (version 2.3.16, Casa Software Ltd., Wilmslow, UK), and all spectra were calibrated by setting the main C 1s peak to 284.8 eV. A charge neutralizer was applied in selected measurements to mitigate sample charging. Under these conditions, XPS probes the upper ≈10 nm of the sample surface. Quantitative analysis employed appropriate peak fitting using GL line shapes and a Shirley background, depending on the elements present.

Photocatalytic Screening Through Benzylamine Oxidation

The photocatalytic performance of the CTF materials was evaluated using the aerobic oxidation of benzylamine. The experiments were carried out in a custom‐built reaction cell with a quartz window for illumination. For these experiments, each CTF sample (25 mg) was dispersed in acetonitrile (10 mL). After the solid catalyst was dispersed, benzylamine (109 μL, 1 mmol) was added, and the solution was stirred for 5 min. The photocatalytic reaction was initiated through illumination and continued for 24 h in air. Low‐power (≈0.1 W) OFK‐8000 OPTIMAX Multi‐Lite lamps equipped with either 365 or 410 nm lamp heads were used as light sources for the photocatalytic screenings. After 24 h of irradiation, the solid catalyst was isolated using centrifugation. Excess acetonitrile was removed from 3 mL of the supernatant using a rotary evaporator, and product quantification and characterisation were carried out on the remaining liquid sample using 1H NMR. Product quantification employed 15 μL of mesitylene as an internal standard, and NMR spectra were collected on a 400 MHz Bruker Ascend Spectrometer.
