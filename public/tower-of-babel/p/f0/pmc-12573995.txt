Maternal–to–neonatal microbial transmission and impact of prenatal probiotics on neonatal gut development
Authors: Lulu Meng, Ge Fan, Haishan Xie, Kian Deng Tye, Lianyi Xia, Huijuan Luo, Xiaomei Tang, Ting Huang, Jiaxin Lin, Guangyu Ma, Xiaomin Xiao, Zhe Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaternal microbiota during pregnancy plays a crucial role in establishing the neonatal gut microbiota, which is essential for infant health. This study aimed to trace maternal microbial sources contributing to early neonatal gut colonization and assess the effect of prenatal probiotic supplementation on maternal–to–neonatal microbial transmission.MethodsA total of 26 mother–neonate pairs undergoing full-term vaginal delivery were enrolled. From gestational week 32 until delivery, the intervention group received a probiotic supplement containing Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus delbrueckii bulgaricus, and Streptococcus thermophilus twice daily, while the control group received no supplementation. Maternal fecal, vaginal, and placental samples were collected at full term, and neonatal fecal samples were collected longitudinally at Days 1, 3, 14, and 6 months postpartum. Microbial community profiling was performed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. Microbial source attribution was conducted using the FEAST algorithm.ResultsAlpha and beta diversity analyses showed that prenatal probiotics transiently altered the composition of neonatal meconium microbiota, with no significant differences observed at later time points. Volatility analysis revealed enhanced microbial stability in the probiotic group during Days 1 to 3 (P < 0.001). FEAST source-tracking indicated that maternal gut and placenta were the major contributors to neonatal meconium colonization, with gut-derived input increasing over time and vaginal contributions remaining minimal throughout. Probiotic supplementation significantly increased the placental contribution to meconium microbiota (P = 0.02), with a sustained but non-significant elevation observed through later stages. Meanwhile, gut-derived and vaginal-derived inputs were consistently reduced in the probiotic group, though these differences did not reach statistical significance.ConclusionThis study highlights the dynamic nature of maternal-to-neonatal microbial transmission and demonstrates that prenatal probiotic supplementation can transiently reshape early colonization patterns by modulating source contributions.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT06241222 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06241222); retrospectively registered on 2024-01-17 (study start 2018-01-01; completion 2021-12-31; first posted 2024-02-05).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07293-6.Keywords: Gut microbiome, Pregnancy, Probiotic, Vertical transmission, Neonatal gut microbiome, Placenta

Introduction

The development of the gut microbiota is crucial to individual health. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota may be associated with various diseases such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [1, 2], sepsis [3], food allergies [4], autism [5], and type 1 diabetes [6]. As a dynamically evolving community, the gut microbiota can be categorized into three stages: developmental, transitional, and stable [7]. The early life stage, being a critical window for metabolic and immune development, plays a significant role in the initial acquisition and succession of the microbiota, which may have long–term health implications [8, 9]. Maternal microbiota constitutes the primary source for neonatal gut colonization and is increasingly recognized as a key determinant of immune development and epigenetic regulation [10, 11]. However, the relative contributions of different maternal microbial reservoirs and the factors shaping vertical microbial transmission remain incompletely understood.

Probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host, are commonly used during pregnancy to alleviate gastrointestinal discomfort and support gut health. Prenatal probiotic supplementation has been associated with reduced allergic symptoms in offspring and improved immune outcomes [12, 13], as well as restoration of microbial balance disrupted by antibiotic exposure [14]. However, whether these effects are mediated through alterations in maternal–to–neonatal microbial transmission remains unclear. Given the foundational role of maternal microbiota in neonatal colonization, exploring how probiotics might affect this early transmission process is essential for understanding their impact on neonatal gut development.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate maternal–neonatal microbial transmission by profiling the microbiota of the maternal gut, vagina, and placenta, as well as neonatal fecal samples at multiple time points from birth to six months. Additionally, we investigated the impact of prenatal probiotic supplementation, initiated at the 32nd week of gestation, on this transmission process. By integrating longitudinal sampling and source–tracking analyses, our findings provide new insights into the origins of the neonatal gut microbiota and offer evidence to support microbiota–targeted strategies for improving maternal and infant health.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample collection

This clinical study was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, The First Affiliated Hospital of Jinan University, Guangzhou, recruiting participants before gestational 32nd weeks. Subjects were included based on the following criteria: singleton pregnancy, first-time pregnancy, gestational age ≥ 37 weeks at delivery. Subjects were excluded based on the following criteria: regular consumption of foods or beverages rich in probiotics or prebiotics during pregnancy, use of probiotics or antibiotics, presence of vaginal infections, and pregnancy complications.

Thirty pregnant women were enrolled and randomly assigned to either a probiotic group (14 participants) or a control group (16 participants). The probiotic group received oral Golden Bifid tablets (Inner Mongolia Shuang qi Pharmaceutical Company, China.) twice a day (two tablets per dose) starting from gestational 32nd weeks until delivery. Each tablet contained live Bifidobacterium longum not less than 0.5×107 CFU, live Lactobacillus delbrueckii bulgaricus and live Streptococcus thermophilus not less than 0.5×106 CFU. Participants were closely monitored for symptoms of diarrhea and gastrointestinal discomfort during the medication period. Participants were required to return the tablet packaging for compliance assessment, with unused tablets totaling less than 10%. No treatment was administered to the control group. Ultimately, only 26 pregnant women were included in the study (4 cases excluded due to failed vaginal delivery converted to cesarean section). Among them, 11 cases were in the probiotic group, and 15 cases were in the control group. All pregnant women had vaginal deliveries and exclusively breastfed their infants for the first 6 months postpartum (Fig. 1).

Sample collection

Fecal samples (TF) and vaginal samples (TV) were collected from each pregnant woman at full–term. Participants collected approximately 1–2 g of fresh feces in a 50 mL centrifuge tube during defecation. Vaginal samples were collected by trained professionals. Placental samples (TP) were collected by midwives within 10 min of placental delivery under strict aseptic conditions. The fetal side of the placenta was first rinsed with sterile saline to remove visible blood. The surface amniotic membrane was carefully removed using sterile surgical scissors, and approximately 0.2–0.5 cm of superficial placental tissue was excised. Then, 4–6 tissue blocks (approximately 1 × 1 × 1 cm each) were obtained from different fetal-side regions using sterile scalpels and immediately transferred to individual sterile cryovials. It is important to note that this study did not collect or process dedicated negative control samples (e.g., sterile saline swabs or extraction blanks) alongside the biological samples. This is a recognized limitation, as the potential for contamination from the environment cannot be empirically assessed or excluded from the results, particularly for low-biomass samples such as the placenta [15]. Neonatal fecal samples were collected on the first day (T1), third day (T2), second week (T3), and sixth month post-birth (T4). All collected samples were immediately placed in freezing boxes and transported to the laboratory under cold chain conditions, where they were stored at − 80 °C until further processing.

A total of 175 samples were collected from 26 mother–neonate pairs, including TF (n = 26), TV (n = 25; one sample was missing from the control group due to scheduling issues), and TP (n = 25; one sample was missing from the probiotic group). Neonatal fecal samples included T1 (n = 26), T2 (n = 26), T3 (n = 26), and T4 (n = 21; two neonates in the probiotic group and three in the control group were lost to follow–up). DNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

The genomic DNA from the samples was isolated employing the CTAB/SDS method. Subsequently, DNA purity and concentration were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 341 F (5′–CCTACGGGNGGCWGCAG–3′) and 806R (5′–GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT–3′). PCR was performed with Phusion® High–Fidelity PCR Master Mix (#M0531S, New England Biolabs, USA). The PCR protocol included an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. A no-template control (sterile ddH₂O) was included during amplification to monitor contamination, and only when no visible band was observed in this control were the subsequent steps performed. PCR products were mixed with a matching volume of buffer and confirmed by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. These mixed products were then purified employing the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (K0692, Thermo Scientific, USA). Library construction was carried out using the Ion Plus Fragment Library Kit (Thermo Fisher), and sequencing was performed on the Ion S5™ XL platform.

Data processing and analysis

After trimming the barcode and primer sequences from the Ion S5™ XL sequencing data, the sequences were then assembled to obtain raw tags. Following strict filtration, raw tags were converted into clean tags, and subsequently, by eliminating chimeric sequences, effective tags were finally achieved. The effective tags were clustered into Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) at a 97% similarity threshold using UPARSE software (Uparse v7.0.1001, http://www.drive5.com/uparse/). Finally, taxonomic annotations for the representative sequences of each OTU were conducted using the Silva Database and the annotation methods provided by Mothur [16]. This enabled microbial taxon identification and abundance analysis. Based on the annotation results, we identified the top 10 most abundant phyla and top 20 most abundant genera within each group. Relative abundance bar plots were then generated to visualize dominant taxa proportions across groups at the phylum and genus levels. In addition, genus–level relative abundances of specific taxa, including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus, were extracted from the annotated abundance table for subsequent statistical comparison between groups. To ensure comparability across samples, the OTU table was rarefied to an even sequencing depth of 5,338 reads per sample (the minimum sequencing depth in this dataset) using the function in the R package phyloseq. This rarefied dataset was used for alpha and beta diversity analyses. For microbial source tracking, we used the unrarefied OTU table to retain the full data structure and minimize information loss.

Metadata analysis

Clinical data of the participants were obtained from the electronic medical record system. Depending on the data type, Student’s t–test or Fisher’s exact test was performed using SPSS software (v25).

Microbial alpha diversity

Alpha diversity indices (Chao1 and Simpson) were calculated using QIIME software (V1.7.0), and statistical comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon.

Microbial beta diversity

The Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was calculated using the vegan package in R software (V4.0.5). The results were visualized through Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA). Differences between groups were compared using the PERMANOVA test.

Microbial volatility analysis

To evaluate temporal stability of the gut microbiota within individuals, microbial volatility was calculated based on Aitchison distances between consecutive fecal samples collected from the same neonate at three intervals: days 1–3, 3–14, and 14–180. The analysis followed the approach established by Henrik Andreas Eckermann et al. [17]. Group comparisons at each interval were performed using the Wilcoxon rank–sum test.

Microbial differential abundance

Microbial relative abundance at the phylum and genus levels were compared between the two groups using the Wilcoxon rank–sum test.

Maternal–neonatal microbial transmission analysis

First, we used the fast and accurate Fast Expectation–Maximization Microbial Source Tracking (FEAST) program to analyze the microbial sources of neonatal gut microbiota at different time points in both groups [18]. Full–term maternal gut microbiota, vaginal microbiota, and placenta microbiota were designated as sources, while neonatal fecal samples were designated as sinks. Statistical comparisons were performed using Wilcoxon rank–sum tests. In addition, we applied the method described by Rasmussen et al. [19] to trace the transmission of specific maternal OTUs to neonates. Specifically, Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate whether an OTU present in maternal microbiota was also significantly detected in neonatal fecal samples. An odds ratio (OR) >1 with a P < 0.05 was considered indicative of significant maternal–to–neonatal microbial transmission.

The dynamic changes in neonatal gut microbiota and the impact of probiotics

Microbial alpha diversity was assessed using the Chao1 and Simpson indices (Fig. 2). From birth to six months, both groups exhibited similar trends. The Chao1 index, representing species richness, showed that meconium samples had relatively high richness, which decreased sharply by T2 and gradually increased thereafter. No significant differences in Chao1 index were observed between the two groups at any neonatal time point (T1:P = 0.07; T2: P = 0.71; T3: P = 0.84; T4: P = 0.69) (Fig. 2A). In contrast, the Simpson index, reflecting overall microbial diversity, was significantly lower in the probiotic group at T1 (P = 0.04), while no significant differences were observed at later time points (T2: P = 0.16; T3: P = 0.75; T4: P = 0.52) (Fig. 2B). Prenatal probiotic supplementation had no significant impact on maternal microbial diversity in the gut, vagina, or placenta, as assessed by Chao1 (TF: P = 0.90; TV: P = 0.32; TP: P = 0.53) and Simpson indices (TF: P = 0.95; TV: P = 0.31; TP: P = 0.36) (Fig. 2).

Paired Bray–Curtis distance analysis between maternal and neonatal microbiota showed that the average distances between maternal sites (gut, vagina, and placenta) and neonatal fecal samples remained below 1 at all time points, suggesting the presence of shared OTUs (Fig. 3B). No significant differences in maternal–to–neonatal microbial distances were observed between the control and probiotic groups across all time points (TF: T1 P = 0.384, T2 P = 1.000, T3 P = 1.000, T4 P = 0.554; TV: T1 P = 0.222, T2 P = 0.893, T3 P = 0.317, T4 P = 0.339; TP: T1 P = 0.249, T2 P = 0.060, T3 P = 0.071, T4 P = 0.082). Across groups and sampling time points, the maternal vaginal microbiota was consistently more dissimilar to the neonatal gut microbiota than either the maternal gut or placental microbiota. This pattern was seen in both the control group (T1: TP vs. TV, P = 0.010; TF vs. TV, P = 0.006; T2: TP vs. TV, P = 0.001; TF vs. TV, P < 0.001; T3: TP vs. TV, P < 0.001; TF vs. TV, P < 0.001; T4: TP vs. TV, P = 0.002; TF vs. TV, P < 0.001) and the probiotic group (T1: TP vs. TV, P < 0.001; TF vs. TV, P = 0.001; T2: TP vs. TV, P = 0.001; TF vs. TV, P < 0.001; T3: TP vs. TV, P < 0.001; TF vs. TV, P < 0.001; T4: TP vs. TV, P = 0.002; TF vs. TV, P = 0.001). However, no significant differences were found between the maternal gut and placental microbiota in either group (control: T1 P = 1.000, T2 P = 0.272, T3 P = 0.418, T4 P = 0.566; probiotic: T1 P = 0.917, T2 P = 1.000, T3 P = 1.000, T4 P = 1.000).

Microbial volatility, reflecting within–individual fluctuations in gut microbiota over time, was significantly lower in the probiotic group than in the control group during days 1–3 (P < 0.001), suggesting enhanced early microbial stability. No significant differences were observed between groups during days 3–14 (P = 0.52) or days 14–180 (P = 0.76) (Fig. 4).

We further analyzed the neonatal gut microbiota composition from birth to six months at both the phylum and genus levels. While relative abundances varied across time points, Actinobacteria, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, and Proteobacteria were the dominant phyla throughout the study period in both groups (Figure S1A), together accounting for over 90% of the total abundance. Notably, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria peaked at T1 (48.72% in the control group and 63.97% in the probiotic group), sharply declining at T2 to approximately 20.8% in both groups, and then remaining relatively stable through six months. In contrast, Actinobacteria gradually increased from T1 to T4, rising from 1.45% to 41.91% in the control group and from 2.17% to 46.39% in the probiotic group.

At the genus level (Figure S1B), in the control group, the abundance of Ralstonia (Proteobacteria) was highest in T1 and gradually decreased thereafter. Meanwhile, Bifidobacterium (Actinobacteria) progressively accumulated as the neonate aged, becoming the dominant genus by the sixth month. Given that the probiotic formulation contained Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus, we examined whether the abundances of these genera differed between groups across multiple time points. Wilcoxon rank–sum tests revealed no significant differences in the relative abundances of these genera between the probiotic and control groups at any time point (all P >0.05; T1: Bifidobacterium P = 0.935, Lactobacillus P = 0.196, Streptococcus P = 0.643; T2: P = 0.305, 0.838, 0.799; T3: P = 0.919, 0.721, 0.683; T4: P = 0.464, 0.446, 0.056) (Fig. 5). It is noteworthy that our previous study [20] found that probiotic supplementation did not significantly alter the abundance of these three genera in the maternal gut microbiota. Consistently, in the present study, no significant differences in the abundances of these three genera were observed in maternal vaginal and placental samples between the probiotic and control groups (all P >0.05; Bifidobacterium: TF P = 0.281, TP P = 0.129, TV P = 1.000; Lactobacillus: P = 1.000, 0.160, 0.467; Streptococcus: P = 0.054, 0.216, 0.244) (Figure S2). This suggests that while the probiotic supplement contained these genera, their colonization and proliferation in the neonatal gut may be regulated by other factors.

Maternal–neonatal microbial transmission

Previous studies have suggested that neonatal gut microbiota colonization may begin in utero [21–24]. Consistent with this, our findings showed that meconium harbored a high species richness. As neonates grow, the composition of their gut microbiota changed in response to developmental needs. To investigate the potential origins and temporal dynamics of neonatal gut microbiota, we applied the FEAST source tracking method, considering the maternal gut, vaginal, and placental microbiota as possible sources for each paired mother–neonate dyad. Due to missing vaginal or placental samples in two pairs, these were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, the source tracking was performed on 24 complete mother–neonate pairs (14 in the control group and 10 in the probiotic group) for time points T1 to T3, and on 19 pairs (11 in the control group and 8 in the probiotic group) for time point T4.

We observed that neonatal gut microbiota inherited a substantial proportion of microbes from the maternal microbiota in the control group. From T1 to T4, the proportion of maternal–derived microbiota gradually increased (from 41.03% to 64.50%) (Fig. 6A). Moreover, the neonatal gut microbiota exhibited source selectivity. At T1, the dominant sources were the placenta (22.48%) and maternal gut (14.13%). By T2, the proportion of gut-derived microbiota doubled that of placental sources, a trend that persisted until T4 (Fig. 6A). Vaginal–derived microbiota consistently accounted for the lowest proportion (0.05%–4.42%) across all time points (Fig. 6A). These findings indicate that the maternal gut serves as the primary source shaping the neonatal gut microbiota throughout early development. Prenatal probiotic supplementation appeared to promote the colonization of placental–derived microbiota in the neonatal gut, especially at T1 (P = 0.02) (Fig. 6B). From T2 to T4, the probiotic group also showed higher levels of placental-derived microbiota compared to the control group, though the differences were not statistically significant (T2: P = 0.24; T3: P = 0.51; T4: P = 0.21) (Fig. 6B). Throughout the developmental stages, the proportions of maternal gut and vaginal–derived microbiota were lower in the probiotic group than in the control group, but these differences were not statistically significant (TF: T1 P = 0.371, T2 P = 0.212, T3 P = 0.752, T4 P = 0.238; TV: T1 P = 0.259, T2 P = 0.752, T3 P = 0.341, T4 P = 0.238) (Fig. 6B).

Given the limited resolution of FEAST in identifying specific taxa, we applied the method described by M. A. Rasmussen et al. [19] to quantify the probability of specific OTUs being transmitted between mothers and neonates. In the control group, OTUs affiliated with the genus Bacteroides were consistently detected across all time points, with maternal fecal and placental microbiota identified as the primary sources (Fig. 7A). At T1, multiple Bacteroides–related OTUs (OTU_14386, OTU_11945, OTU_14382, OTU_15496, and OTU_5944) exhibited high estimated transmission probabilities (OR = 22.89, P = 0.02; OR = 22.89, P = 0.02; OR = 100, P = 0.03; OR = 100, P = 0.01; and OR = 100, P = 0.01, respectively). From T2 to T3, maternal fecal–derived Bacteroides OTUs (OTU_14445, OTU_7395, OTU_2023, and OTU_8616) were likely transmitted to neonatal fecal samples, all showing high transmission likelihood (OR = 100) and statistical significance (P = 0.03, 0.03, 0.03, and 0.01, respectively). At T4, a Bacteroides–associated OTU (OTU_10303) remained significantly transmissible from maternal to neonatal gut (OR = 100, P = 0.04). In contrast, significant transmission of a Bifidobacterium–affiliated OTU (OTU_14624) was observed exclusively at the T2 stage in the control group (OR = 100, P = 0.02) (Fig. 7A, T2).

In the probiotic group, however, a distinct transmission pattern was observed. While Bacteroides remained detectable at T1 and T2 (e.g., OTU_2023 and OTU_8616 at T1; OTU_13956 at T2; all with OR = 100, P = 0.02), multiple Bifidobacterium–associated OTUs (OTU_1091, OTU_14558, and OTU_15552) were significantly transmitted from mothers to neonates at T3 and T4, with each showing high odds ratios (OR = 100) and strong statistical support (P = 0.03, 0.02, and 0.01, respectively) (Fig. 7B). This suggests that probiotics may influence the transmission of key microbial taxa between mother and neonate.

Discussion

For a long time, it was generally believed that the colonization of the neonatal gut microbiota began at birth. However, advances in sequencing technologies have challenged this view, with increasing evidence supporting the presence of microbial DNA in the placenta, amniotic fluid, and meconium, suggesting a possible intrauterine origin of the neonatal microbiome [21–24]. In our study, we focused on the placental microbiota as a potential source of neonatal colonization, although this concept remains controversial due to the high risk of contamination in low-biomass samples [25]. While PCR negative controls were included to monitor contamination introduced during amplification, dedicated extraction and sequencing blanks were not processed alongside the biological samples. Such controls are critical for distinguishing true microbial signals from background noise introduced during sampling, DNA extraction, or library preparation. Without them, we cannot definitively rule out the possibility that some sequences detected in placental samples arose from contamination, and caution is therefore warranted when interpreting the absolute diversity and abundance of placental taxa. Nevertheless, several aspects of our study support the biological relevance of our findings. Placental samples were collected within 10 min of delivery under strict aseptic conditions, with the fetal surface rinsed with sterile saline and the superficial amniotic membrane removed before excising sub-amniotic tissue blocks, minimizing potential contamination. FEAST source-tracking analysis relies not solely on the presence of taxa in the placenta but on shared features between maternal placental communities and neonatal meconium communities. While contamination could inflate overall diversity in placental samples, it is unlikely to generate consistent mother–neonate pair-specific similarity patterns, particularly when such patterns are absent in other environmental sources. Moreover, the observation that placental contribution to the neonatal gut microbiome was modulated by probiotic intervention adds a layer of biological specificity that cannot be attributed to random contamination. Placental and meconium samples were collected in distinct environments—the delivery room and the postpartum unit, respectively—yet compositional similarities were observed, suggesting that detected placental microbes are unlikely to arise solely from environmental contamination and may represent true symbionts [26]. Finally, our previous work demonstrated that these placental symbionts are functionally active, contributing to placental immune stimulation and systemic maternal immune responses [27], providing further evidence against the sterile womb hypothesis. In addition, emerging evidence indicates that maternal oral bacteria can translocate into the bloodstream and reach the placenta [28], and that the integrity of the fetal membranes is closely associated with the presence of microbes in amniotic fluid [29]. Therefore, the placental microbiota signals we detected may be influenced by these factors. Future studies should incorporate rigorous negative controls throughout the experimental workflow and include broader maternal sampling (e.g., oral swabs, skin swabs, blood) to robustly confirm the existence, composition, and vertical transmission of a placental microbiota.

Meconium, representing the first stool passed by the neonate and collected prior to substantial environmental exposure or feeding, exhibited the highest species richness among neonatal samples, as indicated by the Chao1 index. This richness markedly declined within the first few days after birth and then gradually recovered over time, consistent with findings by Wampach et al. [30]. When comparing groups, no significant differences in species richness (Chao1 index) were observed between probiotic and control groups in either maternal or neonatal samples, aligning with prior animal studies [31]. However, the Simpson diversity index, which reflects species evenness and overall community diversity, was significantly lower in meconium samples from the probiotic group compared to controls, suggesting a transient disruption in community balance. This finding contrasts with reports such as Halkjær et al. [32], who found no significant changes in microbial diversity following maternal probiotic supplementation, possibly due to differences in probiotic strains, dosages, or study populations. In our study, we selected a commercially available probiotic formulation (Golden Bifid tablets), which contains Bifidobacterium longum, Lactobacillus delbrueckii bulgaricus, and Streptococcus thermophilus. These strains are among the most commonly used probiotics in fermented dairy products and have been shown to withstand gastrointestinal transit and overcome colonization resistance [33, 34]. Regular consumption of these probiotics has been associated with various health benefits, including modulation of host immune responses and the maintenance of gut microbial homeostasis [35, 36]. Among them, Bifidobacterium longum is particularly noteworthy for its dominance in the neonatal gut and its well-documented ability to establish persistent colonization in the intestine [37]. Notably, although microbial evenness was initially reduced in the probiotic group, this difference did not persist over time. This temporal convergence suggests that the neonatal gut microbiota possesses a strong capacity for self–regulation and ecological stabilization.

The results of beta diversity analysis further confirmed that microbial community composition was significantly influenced by temporal dynamics. Notably, prenatal probiotic supplementation led to significant alterations in the beta diversity of meconium microbiota, indicating that microbial assembly had already been modulated before birth. In addition, microbial volatility between days 1 and 3 after birth was significantly lower in the probiotic group compared to the control group, suggesting enhanced microbial stability. This finding is of particular interest given that previous studies have reported increased microbial volatility in individuals under physiological stress or in neonates with colic, conditions often associated with adverse health outcomes [17]. The first few days of life represent a critical window for neonatal development, characterized by dynamic and complex host–microbe interactions [38, 39]. During this period, changes or disruptions in the gut microbiota could have increased the incidence of metabolic and immune system diseases [40, 41], and might also have affected cognitive development and behavior [42]. Although no significant differences in beta diversity or volatility were observed at later time points, the potential long–term effects of transient microbiota alterations induced by prenatal probiotic supplementation remain uncertain. Even short–lived shifts in microbial composition during this sensitive period may exert lasting effects on neonatal health trajectories [43].

Our findings also demonstrated that the structure of the neonatal gut microbial community changed dynamically over time, as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. For example, by the T2 time point, the relative abundance of Proteobacteria had declined, while Actinobacteria exhibited a gradual increase from T1 to T4 (Supplementary Figure S1A). These shifts likely reflect microbial adaptation to the evolving gut environment. In early life, the neonatal gut contains limited oxygen, favoring the growth of facultative anaerobes. As time progresses, the gut environment becomes more anaerobic, which is conducive to the growth of strict anaerobes [44]. Additionally, the gradual increase in Bifidobacterium, a key genus within Actinobacteria (Supplementary Figure S1B), aligns with the established pattern of gut microbiome maturation. Besides the suitability of the gut environment for their growth, this increase could also be attributed to breastfeeding, as breast milk provided oligosaccharides that promoted the growth of Bifidobacterium [40]. Crucially, despite the administration of prenatal probiotics, we did not detect significant differences in the abundance of probiotic-related genera (Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Streptococcus) between the control and probiotic groups in either maternal or neonatal samples (Supplementary Figure S2). This suggests that maternal probiotic supplementation may not manifest as direct colonization or competitive exclusion in the neonatal gut, but rather through more indirect pathways, such as by modulating microbial transmission dynamics or ecological interactions.

We employed the latest microbial source tracking method, FEAST, which provides distinct advantages such as higher accuracy, improved robustness to noise, and faster computational efficiency compared to traditional methods like SourceTracker. Although FEAST has been primarily applied in studies of environmental microbiomes, such as riverine and soil ecosystems [45, 46], recent work has demonstrated its utility in tracing human-associated microbiomes [43, 47]. These methodological strengths support its application in maternal–neonatal microbiome research and enhance the reliability of the source attribution results presented in our study [43, 48]. Our results supported the vertical transmission of microbiota from different maternal sites to the neonatal delivered via vaginal birth. Specifically, the placenta and maternal gut were the main contributors to the neonatal microbiota, while the vaginal microbiota played a smaller role. This finding challenges the traditional assumption that vaginal exposure during vaginal delivery is the primary driver of initial neonatal colonization, a view that underlies practices like vaginal microbial swabbing for cesarean–born neonates [49]. Consistent with this, longitudinal studies have found no lasting impact of vaginal microbial transfer on the neonatal gut microbiota [50, 51]. Nevertheless, the vaginal microbiota is highly dynamic, influenced by hormonal fluctuations, immune changes, and other perinatal factors [52]. Therefore, a single time-point assessment may not adequately capture its temporal variability or fully reflect its role in maternal–neonatal microbial transmission. Beyond the maternal gut, placenta, and vagina examined here, other maternal sites may contribute to neonatal microbial colonization. For example, the maternal oral cavity has been identified as the third major source of infant microbiota after the gut and vagina [53]. Maternal skin may also play a role, as skin-to-skin contact has been shown to stabilize infant gut microbial communities and delay premature maturation [17]. Additionally, breast milk represents a potentially important source, containing microbial genera such as Veillonella and Rothia that have been linked to a reduced risk of asthma in children [54]. However, logistical challenges during the postpartum period, along with technical limitations such as frequent failure of microbial DNA extraction and high levels of human DNA contamination, hindered reliable characterization of breast milk microbiota in our study. Consequently, the contribution of breast milk to neonatal gut colonization remains uncertain and warrants further investigation using optimized sampling and extraction methods. Notably, our FEAST analysis identified some unknown microbial sources that contribute to the colonization of the neonatal gut, which could be related to these unsampled areas. Although their relative contribution was low and varied slightly over time, these unknown sources likely reflect maternal and environmental inputs that were not captured in our study. In particular, as neonates are gradually exposed to additional microbial sources through feeding and environmental contact, the omission of these sources may have resulted in a modest underestimation of their overall contribution in our analysis, especially at later time points (T3–T4). To address this limitation, future studies should adopt a systematic sampling strategy, including longitudinal collection of breast milk (from colostrum to mature milk), maternal skin swabs (e.g., areolar, chest, and abdominal regions), and oral swabs throughout pregnancy and postpartum. Such an approach would enable the construction of a comprehensive library of potential maternal microbial sources.

Previous studies have reported that the meconium microbiota shares greater similarity with amniotic fluid than with other maternal sites [55], and that the microbiota of amniotic fluid closely matches that of the placenta [56]. Our results agree with these findings, showing that the placenta is the main source of microbes in meconium. Furthermore, we observed that placental microbiota continued to contribute to the neonatal gut microbiota even six months after birth, which argues against the idea that these findings are due to contamination and instead supports the concept of long–term colonization initiated before birth [57]. Beyond global source contributions, we examined microbial transmission at the OTU level to capture finer taxonomic dynamics. Although taxa affiliated with the same genus (e.g., Bacteroides) were detected across multiple neonatal time points, the specific OTUs differed over time. This indicates that microbial transmission is not a single event but rather a dynamic and selective process shaped by neonatal development. Factors such as gut maturation and immune system changes likely act as filters determining which microbes persist at different stages [58]. As neonates grow, their gut microbiota stabilizes and becomes more similar to maternal gut microbiota, while microbes originating from the placenta and vagina gradually decrease, likely reflecting adaptation to the neonatal gut environment. This view is supported by recent studies showing that transplanting maternal fecal microbiota into cesarean–born neonates can partially restore a microbiome similar to that of vaginally delivered neonates [59, 60].

Although no statistically significant differences in Bifidobacterium abundance were observed between groups at any time point, source–tracking analysis at the OTU level revealed significant differences in the transmission patterns of specific Bifidobacterium taxa. Notably, several Bifidobacterium–associated OTUs exhibited significantly higher transmission probabilities from mother to neonate in the probiotic group during the later neonatal stages (T3 and T4), suggesting that prenatal probiotic supplementation may selectively enhance the maternal transfer of these beneficial taxa. While previous studies have reported that delivery mode affects maternal–to–neonatal microbial transmission [61], our study provided the first evidence that prenatal probiotic intake may have also influenced this process, particularly during the meconium stage, when the contribution of placental microbiota was relatively higher and that of maternal gut and vaginal sources was lower. These observations may be linked to transient decreases in microbial evenness (Simpson index), alterations in beta diversity at this stage, and the early stabilization of the neonatal microbiome during the first days of life. In subsequent stages, although the continued decrease in maternal gut–derived microbes and the increase in placental contributions in the probiotic group did not reach statistical significance, these patterns suggest a potentially meaningful shift in microbial transmission dynamics. The absence of pronounced differences at later stages likely reflects the increasing influence of host development and environmental exposures as the neonate matures. Importantly, these transmission patterns may have implications for neonatal health, particularly immune development and disease susceptibility. Early-life microbial colonization is a key determinant of immune system maturation and has been linked to outcomes such as NEC, sepsis, and allergic diseases [62, 63]. Maternal microbial exposures during pregnancy have also been shown to influence the offspring’s immune phenotype and predisposition to allergic disorders [63]. In this context, the increased placental contribution and reduced maternal gut–derived transmission observed in the probiotic group may meaningfully alter microbial exposures during the critical window of immune programming. Although our study did not track clinical outcomes, such changes could potentially modulate the risk of NEC, allergic diseases, or metabolic disorders. Notably, recent clinical evidence suggests that prenatal probiotic supplementation may reduce the incidence of allergic disorders in offspring, supporting the biological plausibility of our findings [64]. Longitudinal studies with extended follow-up will therefore be necessary to clarify these associations and assess long-term health consequences. Beyond the effects of probiotic supplementation, factors such as maternal diet, antibiotic exposure during pregnancy, obesity, and metabolic conditions (e.g., gestational diabetes or preeclampsia) are known to shape maternal gut and vaginal microbiota [65, 66] and, consequently, the microbial pool available for neonatal colonization. Although participants with major complications were excluded and none received perinatal antibiotics, we did not collect detailed dietary or lifestyle information, which limits our ability to fully assess the influence of these factors on neonatal microbiome assembly. Future studies incorporating comprehensive maternal health, lifestyle, and medication data will be essential to clarify how these factors contribute to maternal–neonatal microbial transmission.

In non-pregnant populations, probiotic supplementation has been shown to strengthen gut barrier function through the modulation of tight junction protein expression and localization [67–69]. In our study, we observed a consistent reduction in microbial transmission from the maternal gut to the neonate in the probiotic group. Although our data do not provide direct mechanistic evidence, this observed reduction supports a plausible hypothesis: that prenatal probiotic supplementation may enhance maternal gut barrier integrity, thereby limiting microbial translocation to the placenta or other maternal sites. A similar speculative hypothesis might apply to the maternal vaginal environment. Although vaginal transmission appeared limited in both groups, its relative contribution was lower in the probiotic group. Prior research has similarly reported that probiotics can improve vaginal mucosal barrier integrity and attenuate local inflammatory responses [70, 71]. However, given the unique physiological context of pregnancy, including altered immune and hormonal regulation, these hypotheses remain speculative and require direct validation. Future studies could address this by incorporating functional assays, such as markers of epithelial barrier integrity (e.g., Zonulin, Claudin-3, or fatty acid-binding protein levels in maternal serum or stool [72–74]) and mucosal immunity (e.g., cytokines such as IL-6, IL-10, or TNF-α in maternal blood, stool, or vaginal secretions [73]). Such experiments would clarify whether prenatal probiotic supplementation directly modulates maternal barrier function and thereby influences microbial transmission to the neonate.

This study has several limitations. First, we did not collect samples from maternal skin, oral cavity, breast milk, or environmental contacts, which could potentially influence the neonatal gut microbiome [75–77]. Second, vaginal samples were collected only once at full-term. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs with sampling across multiple stages of pregnancy and delivery to better characterize vaginal microbiota dynamics and their potential influence on neonatal colonization. Third, our placental sampling protocol cannot completely exclude low–level contamination. Future studies should include rigorous blank controls throughout placental collection and processing to better distinguish true placental microbiota from contaminants. Fourth, 16 S rRNA gene sequencing lacks strain-level resolution, limiting our ability to confirm mother-to-neonate strain sharing. Future work should use strain-resolved metagenomics or isolate whole-genome sequencing to delineate transmission pathways. Finally, due to challenges in follow-up, the number of participants was limited, particularly in the probiotic group (n = 11), which reduced the statistical power to detect subtle but biologically meaningful differences. This limitation is especially relevant in microbiome research, where high inter-individual variability and the compositional nature of the data necessitate larger cohorts for robust inference. Consequently, non-significant results should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect insufficient power (type II errors) rather than a true absence of effect (e.g., lack of difference in Bifidobacterium abundance), while significant associations may be overestimated in small cohorts. Therefore, our findings should be regarded as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate these observations and to provide estimates of effect sizes and confidence intervals for maternal–neonatal microbial transmission. Moreover, longer–term follow–up is required to confirm the persistent impact of microbiota from different maternal sites on the infant gut microbiome.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides novel insights into the dynamic process of maternal–neonatal microbial transmission, highlighting the distinct contributions of the maternal gut, placenta, and vaginal microbiota across early developmental stages. We found that prenatal probiotic supplementation, while not significantly altering the abundance of key probiotic genera such as Bifidobacterium, appeared to influence the route and timing of microbial transmission, particularly by enhancing placental contribution during the meconium stage and transiently modifying neonatal microbial diversity. Over time, the maternal gut became the predominant microbial source, suggesting a selective, ecologically structured colonization process. These findings challenge traditional views on the dominance of vaginal microbiota during birth and support the role of placental and gut–derived microbes in shaping the early neonatal gut ecosystem. Larger studies with mechanistic exploration are needed to better understand how probiotics might shape early–life microbial colonization and long–term health outcomes.
