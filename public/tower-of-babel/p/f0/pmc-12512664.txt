Autograft bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation for collapsed necrotic femoral head: a mid-term retrospective study
Authors: Min-cong He, Bang-ning Gu, Peng Peng, Qing-wen Zhang, Qiu-Shi Wei, Wei He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the mid-term outcomes of surgical hip dislocation combined with bone graft impacting and non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap implanting in the treatment of post-collapse osteonecrosis of femoral head (ONFH).MethodsFrom October 2014 and April 2020, 111 patients (121 hips) with ONFH were retrospectively reviewed. Assessment parameters included the Visual analog scale (VAS) pain scores and Harris hip score (HHS). Follow-up end points were set as total hip arthroplasty (THA). Conversion to THA was selected as the definitive endpoint, representing an objective clinical failure requiring intervention, whereas radiologic re-collapse lacks standardized definition and may not consistently correlate with symptomatic progression or necessitate revision surgery. Therefore, it was not used in this study. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to examine the survival rate. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to investigate the risk factors associated with conversion to THA, including age, etiology, duration of pain, combined preserved angle (CPA), and hip joint medial space ratio (MSR). Pre- and postoperative variables of HHS in the different groups were assessed.ResultsThe average follow-up time was 53.82 ± 25.36 months. The mean VAS score and HHS were significantly improved at the final follow-up compared with preoperative values (p < 0.001). By the time of the last follow-up, 19 hips failed and reached the end point of observation. The overall survival rate was 84.30% at 60 months. Multivariate Cox regression analysis revealed that CPA and MSR were independent factor associated with conversion to THA. These findings establish CPA and MSR as crucial preoperative prognostic indicators and patient selection tools. Clinical outcomes was affected by age, duration of pain, JIC classification, combined preserved angle (CPA), and hip joint medial space ratio (MSR).ConclusionsSurgical hip dislocation combined with non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting is an effective treatment on post-collapse ONFH with satisfactory mid-term results, particularly when patients are selected based on favorable preoperative CPA (> 118.73°) and MSR (≤ 20.35%) values. Multicenter studies with longer follow-up are needed to further validate the efficacy of this procedure in post-collapse ONFH.

Introduction

Osteonecrosis of femoral head (ONFH) is a refractory orthopaedics disease which can make a great burden to the individual and society. The etiology includes corticosteroid intake, hip trauma and chronic alcohol abuse [1]. Femoral head collapse and secondary arthritis would occur if left untreated [2]. The treatment of ONFH mainly depends upon disease progress. Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is appropriate for senior patients or those in late stages [3]. However, in order to avoid multiple arthroplasty revisions in the future, most young patients prefer to delay or even avoid THA [4]. Swarup et al. reported that patients undergone THA before the age of 25 not only had worse implant survivorship, but also had poorer clinical outcomes [5]. THA revisions projected to surge 137% (2005–2030), with surgical traumas, delays, and costs escalating disability risks and straining healthcare/family networks [6]. Joint preserving techniques have shown promising outcomes in pre-collapsed patients [7–9]. In the initial phases of osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH), compromised vascular supply and reduced oxygenation induce cellular apoptosis and progressive architectural breakdown of osseous components in the femoral region [10]. Once femoral head collapse occurs, hip preserving treatments would become more complex and the clinical outcomes would be unpredictable [11]. At present, scholars have comprehensively reviewed various hip preservation strategies, including non-surgical treatment such as protective weight-bearing, hyperbaric oxygen, pulsed electromagnetic, extracorporeal shock wave, bisphosphonates, anticoagulants, antilipid-lowering drugs, vasodilators, and traditional Chinese medicine, and surgical treatment such as core decompression, osteotomy, bone grafting, mesenchymal stem cell transplantation, tantalum rod, and tissue engineering. Their review highlights the potential of these strategies to slow disease progression and improve clinical outcomes [6]. Therefore, there is a need of a reliable joint preserving treatment for patients with early collapse or even mild arthritic changes. In the last decade, hip preserving surgeries have been demonstrated effective in slowing disease progression [6, 12, 13].

In 2001, Ganz et al. described a technique of surgical hip dislocation approach based on their studies of the vascular anatomy of the proximal femur [14]. Surgical hip dislocation enables full acetabular labrum, bone surface, and femoral head exposure, treating femoroacetabular impingement, fractures, and hip pathologies [15, 16].The clinical results of treatment for late stage ONFH by the approach of surgical hip dislocation remain unclear. Steppacher et al. reported favorable drilling/bone grafting with femoral osteotomy results via this approach, but limited sample size and short follow-up weaken reliability [17]. Sun et al. found surgical hip dislocation with femoral neck rotational osteotomy effective for ONFH, linking neck-shaft angle differences to postoperative biomechanical changes. However, small cohort size and absence of prior biomechanical research weaken the study’s credibility [18]. Xia et al. reported that surgical dislocation with bone grafting or rotational osteotomy had promising short-term efficacy in relatively late stages ONFH [19]. However, the sample size is still small and the longer follow up results are unknown.

While these prior studies utilizing surgical hip dislocation with various bone grafting or osteotomy techniques demonstrate potential, limitations persist, particularly regarding small cohorts and limited follow-up. Critically, to our knowledge, no study has specifically reported the systematic combination of a structural nonvascularized iliac crest bone flap with impacting cancellous bone grafting harvested from the greater trochanter within the surgical hip dislocation approach for post-collapse ONFH. The non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap aims to provide critical structural support beneath the subchondral bone to prevent further collapse and potentially allow articular surface reduction, while the impacting cancellous graft from the trochanteric site fills the debrided necrotic cavity, creating an osteoconductive and osteoinductive environment to facilitate bone regeneration. This study represents the first investigation to combine these two techniques via surgical hip dislocation, addressing both mechanical instability and biological deficits inherent in post-collapse ONFH. This novel dual-grafting strategy, facilitated by the comprehensive exposure afforded by surgical dislocation, represents a distinct approach aimed at addressing both the mechanical instability and biological deficit inherent in post-collapse ONFH.

The purposes of our study were as follows: (a) to investigate the mid-term hip survivorship of a group of patients with post-collapse ONFH treated with non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation approach; (b) to assess the clinical outcomes with Harris hip score (HHS); (c) to explore the risk factors leading to its conversion to THA.

Materials and methods

Research object

This study was approved by the ethics committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou, University of Chinese Medicine, and all methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent for inclusion in the study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) radiographic criteria of ARCO stages III [20]; (b) age above 18 years old at time of surgery; (c) Collapse Severity & Lesion Characteristics: Presence of symptomatic collapse. Extensive collapse was defined as depression > 4 mm on preoperative frog-leg lateral radiographs. Patients meeting this criterion, those with severe secondary osteoarthritis (Tonnis grade ≥ 2), or lesions involving > 30% femoral head volume underwent critical evaluation for surgical candidacy; (d) Symptoms & Functional Demand: Significant hip pain (mean preoperative VAS 3.45 ± 1.03) and functional impairment (mean preoperative HHS 65.31 ± 6.39) affecting quality of life and activity levels. Patients with high functional demands seeking to avoid or postpone THA were prioritized; (e) Radiographic Integrity (CPA & MSR): Assessment of the Combined Preserved Angle (CPA) [21] and Hip Joint Medial Space Ratio (MSR) [22]. While not absolute exclusion criteria, a higher CPA (> 118.73°) and lower MSR (≤ 20.35%) were considered favorable prognostic indicators (as later confirmed in our results) and factored into surgical decision-making, suggesting sufficient residual viable bone and joint congruity to support reconstruction. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) patients underwent previous hip preserving surgeries; (b) active infectious disease in the affected hip; (c) secondary arthritis. A total of 111 patients (121 hips) with ONFH treated in the Department of orthopaedics between October 2014 and April 2020 were retrospectively reviewed. The minimum duration of follow-up was 10 Months, and the number of hips with less than 24 Months of follow-up was 12.All the patients underwent necrotic tissue debridement, non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation approach. Radiological (plain radiographs, computerized tomography) evaluations were performed before and after the operation. Surgical treatment

All operations were performed by professor Wei He. The detailed procedures were as follows: After the general anesthesia was effective, the patient was placed in the lateral position. The skin, subcutaneous tissue and fascia were incised to expose the iliac bone (Fig. 1A). The full-thickness bone flap was about 3.5 cm x 1.5 cm (Fig. 1A). At the same time, part of the spongiform bone was taken for further use. The greater trochanter of the femur was exposed via a lateral incision of approximately 18 cm. The anterior greater trochanteric osteotomy (with a thickness of 1.5 cm) was performed with the pendulum sawing from the posterior direction and lifted to the front (Fig. 1B). Meanwhile, the greater trochanteric cancellous bone was taken for bone preparation. The joint capsule was incised by the shape of “Z”, and round ligament was cut to make the femoral head dislocate forward (Fig. 1C). An approximate 2.0 cm x 1.5 cm window was made at the anterior part of the femoral head and neck border slot. Through the bone window, a power burr was used to remove the necrotic bone until a bleeding surface was encountered at the base of the cavity. In cases with extensive necrosis (e.g., JIC C2 type involving > 30% femoral head volume), viable bleeding bone might not be fully achieved at the deepest region due to compromised vascularity. In such scenarios, we prioritized: (i) Removing all soft, necrotic tissue until a relatively firm bony surface was reached, even if bleeding was minimal; (ii) Preserving the sclerotic boundary in subchondral regions to avoid structural compromise; (iii) Extending debridement to the periphery where viable bone was confirmed. The autologous cancellous bone prepared before were used for the impacting bone graft (Fig. 1D). The free iliac bone flap was properly trimmed and inserted into the bone groove for supporting bone grafting. After that, the collapsed cartilage surface recovered to normal, and the collapse is corrected. The original position of the bone window should be coated with bone wax without screw fixation. After washing the joint cavity, the affected hip was reduced by traction and the joint capsule was sutured. Subsequently, the femoral greater trochanter was anatomically reduced, and fixed with three 3.5 mm cortical bone screws. Finally, the wound was irrigated and sutured. After the surgery, conventional analgesic and thromboprophylaxis were conducted. We also guided the patients to take functional exercises. Patients were kept non-weight bearing for the first three Months following surgery. After three Months, patients were progressed to approximately 50% weight bearing if there were radiographic evidence of graft healing. After six months, they began full weight bearing.

Follow-up and assessment

All the patients were required for follow-up. Anteroposterior and frog leg lateral views in X-ray were required. The follow-up times are as follows: every 3 Month in 1 year after surgery, every six Month in 1–2 years after surgery, and once a year after 2 year.

Clinical outcomes were assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS) and HHS before surgery and at each follow-up time [23, 24]. The primary end point was defined as conversion to THA. The procedure was deemed successful if the HHS was > 70 points at the last time follow- up. The radiological evaluation included the JIC classification, combined preserved angle (CPA), and hip joint medial space ratio (MSR). The CPA indicate the area of intact femoral head unaffected by necrotic lesion, it can reflect the integrity of the anterior and lateral portion of the femoral head [21]. MSR is defined as the ratio of the hip joint medial space to the distance from the pelvic teardrop to the outermost margin of the acetabulum [22]. To assess the reliability of CPA and MSR measurements, inter- and intra-observer reliability analyses were performed. Two independent orthopedic surgeons (blinded to patient outcomes and each other’s assessments) measured CPA and MSR on preoperative radiographs of 30 randomly selected hips. Each observer repeated measurements twice with a 4-week interval to minimize recall bias. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two-way random-effects model for absolute agreement, with ICC(2,1) for single measures and ICC(2,k) for average measures. ICC values were interpreted as: <0.50 (poor), 0.50–0.75 (moderate), 0.75–0.90 (good), > 0.90 (excellent).

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were analyzed using SPSS 28.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was used to examine the survival rate. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to investigate the risk factors associated with conversion to THA, including age, etiology, duration of pain, CPA, and MSR. Pre- and postoperative variables of HHS in the different groups were assessed using independent samples T test and the two tailed ANOVA test when multiple groups were compared. The primary clinical outcome was the HHS, dichotomized into good/excellent and other, compared using the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Survival rate

The average follow-up time was 53.82 (10–93) Months. By the time of the last follow-up, 19 patients with poor HHS were defined as clinical failure. Nineteen hips received THA (11 in the first 2 years following index surgery and eight between 3 and 5 years following index surgery). In 19 cases, follow-up time is ranged from 10 to 59 Months, with an average follow-up time of 25.26 Months. The survival rate was 97.52% (55/121) at 12 Months, 87.60% (106/121) at 36 Months. The overall survival rate was 84.30% (Fig. 2).

Clinical results

The operation was successfully conducted in all 111 patients. There have no serious postoperative complications such as deep venous thrombosis, deep infection, and nonunion. No patients were lost to followed-up. Preoperative HHS point improved from 65.31 ± 6.39 to 84.81 ± 10.50 at final follow-up (p < 0.001); the VAS point decreased from 3.45 ± 1.03 preoperatively to 0.73 ± 0.74 at final follow-up (p < 0.001) (Table 1). Sixty hips were excellent, 37 hips were good, five hips were fail, and 19 hips were poor according to the HHS evaluating system. The clinical excellent and good rate was 80.17%. According to the previous studies, CPA and MSR were classified in two categories [21, 22]. There were no significant differences in the preoperative HHS between different age groups, etiology, JIC classification, pain duration, PCA and MSR. The postoperative HHS and the rates of success were greatly higher in younger patients compared to those beyond 40 years, and in those had reported hip symptoms for ≤ 6 months compared to those reported a longer duration of symptoms. Patients with JIC classification type C1, CPA > 118.73°, and MSR ≤ 20.35 had better outcomes (Tables 3 and 4). There were no differences (p > 0.05) among etiologies on postoperative efficacy (Tables 3 and 4). On the basis of this Cox regression analysis, Kaplan–Meier survival curve was further drawn by stratification according to CPA (Fig. 3A), and MSR (Fig. 3B).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the mid-term clinical outcomes and survivorship of non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap grafting via surgical hip dislocation for the treatment of post-collapse ONFH. Our results demonstrate significant improvements in VAS pain scores and HHS postoperatively. The overall survival rate was 84.3% at a mean follow-up of 53.82 months. Furthermore, we identified that patients with preoperative CPA > 118.73°and MSR ≤ 20.35% exhibited superior mid-term outcomes.

The optimal treatment of ONFH depends on disease stages. Hip preserving surgery is often used in young patients with pre-collpase phase of osteonecrosis [7]. Currently, the treatment for the post-collpase stages of ONFH remained tough and limited. Bone grafting and osteotomies were the most common joint preserving procedures used in the post-collapse ONFH [12]. But the outcomes of hip preserving techniques in post-collapse ONFH remained controversial (Table 5) [17, 19, 25–29]. In 2017, Yao C et al. reported surgical hip dislocation with sequestrum clearance and impacting bone graft for late stage ONFH, the survival rate with 36.6 Months follow up was 87.5% [25]. In a retrospectively study, 12 patients with ONFH underwent surgical hip dislocation, and three of the 13 hips received further surgery after a three-year follow-up [17]. After that, Xia et al. reported the short-term outcome of the surgical dislocation with bone grafting or rotational osteotomy in relatively late stages ONFH, the survival rate was 76.5% [19].

Unlike the previous bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation approach, we used the autogenic non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap as the grafting material. The autogenic non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap provides superior structural support to the subchondral bone, reducing collapse risk compared to pure cancellous bone grafting. This is consistent with the mechanical advantage of structural bone flaps reported in modified core decompression techniques [30]. Furthermore, the impacted cancellous graft creates an osteoconductive microenvironment, analogous to the bone induction effects observed in combined core decompression and bone grafting procedures [31]. According to our findings, the surgery procedure considerably reduced pain and improved the HHS, demonstrating that non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation approach had effects on improving the clinical outcomes. Then, at our average follow-up of 53.82 Months, 84.3% hips did not convert to THA with acceptable hip function. This helps to demonstrate that this surgery procedure contributed to slowing disease progression in ONFH. In addition, the overall survival rate in this study was greater than many of the previous researches.This is consistent with the findings of Qi et al. [6], who reported that surgical treatments, especially those incorporating regenerative medicine approaches, offer better survival rates and clinical outcomes compared to nonoperative treatments. This further highlights the effectiveness of our surgical technique in preserving hip function and delaying the need for THA.

The non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap provides superior structural support compared to cancellous grafts alone, owing to its corticocancellous composite architecture. The cortical layer acts as a ‘biological buttress’ immediately resisting subchondral compressive stresses, while the cancellous component facilitates osteoconduction [32]. This contrasts with pure impacted cancellous grafts [19], which rely on frictional interlock between bone particles—a mechanism prone to early micromotion and collapse under cyclic joint loading. Our dual-graft strategy thus addresses both mechanical instability and biological deficits in post-collapse ONFH.“Although impacting bone grafting alone [19] achieved a 76.5% survival rate at 36 months, it showed limitations in hips with JIC C2 lesions (> 30% head involvement) where structural support is critical. Our technique’s 84.3% survival at 60 months may be attributed to the iliac flap’s ability to restore load-bearing capacity in the anterolateral femoral head—the primary weight-bearing zone. The flap’s cortical integrity maintains joint congruity during the vulnerable remodeling phase (6–12 months), reducing secondary collapse risk.

Surgical hip dislocation is a safe approach used to address pathologic lesions around the hip joint. This approach can offers full access to the entire hip joint and direct treatment of the necrotic lesion [14]. In an anatomical study, researchers found that the blood supply of the femoral head was mainly from the deep branch of the medial circumflex femoral artery [33]. Surgical hip dislocation technique can protect this vessel and prevent necrosis of the femoral head in hip surgery [14]. We described the non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation approach technique based on the notion. Comparing with the traditional bone graftting skill without exposing the hip joint, surgical hip dislocation allows a full evaluation and debridement of necrotic lesion. Meanwhile, a precise non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting can provide a stable support to the anterolateral column of the femoral head. Furthermore, cancellous bone from greater trochanteric is an ideal autograft transplantation bone source for fully grafting of lesion site in femoral head. In our case, no patients need allograft or other bone graft materials.

Several risk factors, such as stage of the disease, age, lesion size and location, and duration of symptoms, play an important role in selection of the treatment choice [8, 34]. In this study, we investigated the risk factors for the failure of non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation approach to convert to THA. The CPA was defined as the sum of the lateral preserved angle and the anterior preserved angle, it reflect the integrity of the anterior and lateral portion of the femoral head. Previous study revealed that CPA has a potential value in predicting femoral head collapse [21]. Accordingly, we hypothesized that CPA might have predictive value on the efficacy of this surgery procedure. Multivariate Cox regression analyses suggested that CPA was a independent factor associated with conversion to THA. The superior outcomes in patients under 30 years—reflected by significantly higher Harris Hip Scores (89.02 ± 7.22 vs. 79.43 ± 12.01 in > 40-year-olds, P < 0.001) and implant survivorship—are primarily attributable to age-related biological advantages. Histological analyses confirm that younger patients exhibit 75% greater microvessel density in grafted necrotic lesions (P < 0.01), accelerating revascularization and bone repair [35]. This enhanced healing capacity stems from preserved function of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in individuals under 30 years, which demonstrate 3-fold higher proliferative activity and osteogenic differentiation potential compared to older cohorts [36]. For patients over 40 years, adjunctive autologous bone marrow cell implantation may partially reverse age-related deficits, as evidenced by a 48% reduction in THA conversion risk at 5-year follow-up [37].

In addition, patients with CPA more than 118.73° had better HHS compared to patients with less than 118.73°. Therefore, it was evident that the maintenance of the lateral and anterior wall of the femoral head greatly contributed to the rate of success. MSR reflects how well the acetabulum covers the femoral head. Previous studies have demonstrated that less acetabular coverage was associated with higher pressure in hip joint capsules and the development of ONFH [38, 39]. MSR has been proven to be related with the collapse of ONFH [22], but the relationship between the MSR and the success of hip preservation is still unclear. We found that MSR was a independent risk factor associated with conversion to THA, and patients with MSR less than 20.35 have better hip function in the follow-up period. We speculate that proper acetabular coverage represents good joint stability, which may contribute to provide stable environment for bone repair in femoral head after bone grafting. In Biomechanically, the predictive power of CPA (> 118.73°) lies in its reflection of critical structural support in the anterolateral femoral head: the primary weight-bearing zone [21]. A larger CPA indicates greater preservation of the bony columns that resist compressive loads during gait, functioning as a biological buttress to prevent subchondral collapse [11, 40]. Conversely, MSR (≤ 20.35) serves as an indicator of optimal joint congruity [22, 39]. Lower MSR values promote uniform load distribution across the articular surface, reducing focal stresses at the graft-host interface that impede bone regeneration [38]. The high ICC values for CPA and MSR (> 0.90 for both intra- and inter-observer reliability) confirm their robustness as radiographic predictors. This aligns with prior validation of CPA by Wei et al. [21], who established its predictive utility in collapse risk assessment. The excellent reliability of MSR (a novel parameter in our study) further supports its clinical applicability for joint congruity evaluation in ONFH management.

In this study, we also determine the optimal indication for this surgery procedure. The follow-up results of this study showed that patients with age less than 30 years old had higher postoperative HHS and survival rate, suggesting that younger age was a protective factors of patients underwent this surgery procedure. This age-related outcome disparity is likely attributable to superior bone metabolic activity in younger patients. Enhanced osteogenic capacity (e.g., mesenchymal stem cell recruitment, osteoblast differentiation) and angiogenic potential accelerate necrotic bone resorption and graft revascularization [34, 41]. These biological advantages are critical for procedures reliant on biological incorporation of nonvascularized grafts. The occurrence of pain was associated with the progression of necrosis and the occurrence of collapse of the femoral head [40–42]. Therefore, we hypothesized that shorter duration of pain could be a potential indication for this procedure. Consistent with these studies, we found that patients with less than 6 months of hip pain had better outcomes than those patients with longer duration. Researchers reported that JIC classification was associated with functional results in hip preserving surgery for ONFH [8, 43]. Our results show that patients with JIC type C1 had better clinical outcomes than that with JIC type C2. The lack of association between etiology and surgical failure aligns with multivariate analyses from large cohort studies, where factors like lesion location and collapse extent outweighed etiology in predicting outcomes [44].

Our study has some limitations. First, all cases were from a single medical center. Multi-center clinical trials are needed to further evaluate the efficacy of this procedure. Second, the lack of a comparative control group (e.g., core decompression, vascularized bone grafting, or nonsurgical treatment) limits our ability to determine the superiority of this technique over currently available alternatives. Third, the retrospective design introduces an inherent risk of selection bias, as patients were not randomized and may represent a cohort with favorable anatomical characteristics (e.g., higher CPA/lower MSR) or shorter duration of symptoms. Third, although we followed these patients mid-term, a follow-up of 53.82 months is insufficient to assess long-term (> 10 years) survival, especially for younger patients seeking delayed THA. In addition, as a single-center study, our findings may not be generalizable to a broader population with different surgical skills and patient demographic characteristics. Functional outcome was assessed only by HHS and VAS scores. A comprehensive assessment of functional recovery is limited by the lack of objective measures such as range of motion (ROM), gait analysis, or patient-reported activity levels such as the UCLA activity score. Future multicenter prospective studies incorporating advanced functional assessments are needed to validate these findings. Finally, the clinical efficacy of secondary THA compared with primary THA is currently unclear. Further investigation is needed.

In conclusion, this study revealed encouraging clinical outcomes and survival rates of non-vascularized iliac crest bone flap bone grafting via surgical hip dislocation in symptomatic post-collapse ONFH patients, with an 84.3% survival rate at 60 months. This efficacy compares favorably to other hip-preserving techniques for advanced ONFH, such as impacting bone grafting alone (76.5% survival at 36 months) [19] and core decompression with structural support (≤ 75% survival beyond 5 years) [30]. It also significantly delays THA compared to the natural history of untreated collapse (60–80% THA conversion within 2 years) [34]. Crucially, our study identifies Combined Preserved Angle (CPA > 118.73°) and Hip Joint Medial Space Ratio (MSR ≤ 20.35%) as robust preoperative radiographic selection tools. Mechanistically, CPA reflects biomechanical support integrity in the weight-bearing zone, while MSR indicates protective joint congruity. Younger age (< 30 years) confers enhanced bone regeneration capacity. Studies with larger cases and longer follow-up times are needed to evaluate the potential effect of this surgery procedure.
