Development and validation of a vision transformer model for radiographic assessment of pediatric humeroradial joint alignment
Authors: WenTao Wang, ShengHua He, YiFan Rao, Federico Canavese, ShengPing Tang, ChongZhi Zhao, Hang Liu, Antonio Andreacchio, Maria Beatrice Michelis, Chiara Arrigoni, Nunzio Catena, Sandro Gregorio, Lorenza Marengo, ShunYou Chen, SuiGu Tang, QianQian Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhether the radiographic assessments of pediatric anatomical (A-HRJA) and nonanatomical humeroradial joint alignment (NA-HRJA) among pediatric orthopedic surgeons (POS) relies on clinical experience remains unknown. No vision transformer (ViT) model for automated and accurate radiographic pediatric HRJAs categorization has been developed and validated. This study aims to evaluate the radiographic assessment outcomes of pediatric A-HRJAs and NA-HRJAs among POS with varying levels of experience, and to develop and validate a model for automated and accurate categorization of pediatric HRJAs on radiographs.Materials and methodsA total of 294 pediatric radiographs were independently recorded by four junior-level, four mid-level, and three senior-level POS. Then, the performance of the POS with various levels of experience was evaluated. A total of 5118 pediatric radiographs (2932 A-HRJAs and 2186 NA-HRJAs) were prepared to construct the vision transfer (ViT) model. The ViT model’s clinical efficacy was evaluated using an external testing dataset (84 A-HRJAs and 132 NA-HRJAs). A total of 11 POS independently recorded the HRJA types of the external testing dataset twice with a 1-month interval: first without ViT model assistance, and second with assistance. The rating performances of the POS without and with ViT model assistance were compared.ResultsThe accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were similar among junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS (P > 0.050).The ViT model’s area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was 0.944. The ViT model’s rating performance was significantly superior to that of POS in the first round regarding accuracy (P = 0.001), sensitivity (P = 0.001), and NPV (P < 0.001). However, no significant differences were found between the ViT model and POS in the second round. The accuracy (P = 0.008), PPV (P = 0.043), and NPV (P = 0.030) improved significantly in the second round compared with the first, as demonstrated by the following values: 86.9–92.6%, 88.3–93.6%, and 85.8–93.2%, respectively. The kappa value reflecting the reliability among POS improved by 24.8% in the second round.ConclusionsThe accuracy of manually classifying radiographic pediatric HRJA types did not depend on clinical experience. The ViT model can accurately categorize radiographic pediatric HRJA types and significantly improve the POS’s rating performance when categorizing A-HRJAs and NA-HRJAs.Level of evidenceIIISupplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10195-026-00955-1.

Introduction

Humeroradial joint alignment (HRJA) refers to the spatial congruency between the humeral capitulum and the radial head [1, 2]. HRJA is an important anatomical landmark for accurately diagnosing and treating various pediatric elbow conditions, such as Monteggia fractures, as well as for evaluating their prognosis [3–5]. However, pediatric HRJAs are frequently misdiagnosed in clinical practice, particularly in patients with Monteggia fractures, with the rate of HRJAs misdiagnosis up to 20–50%, which can result in elbow pain, deformity, and impaired function [6–9].

Plain radiographs are the main reference for assessing HRJA in children [2, 6, 10]. However, the irregularity of the radius, incomplete ossification of the radial head and humeral capitulum on radiographs, and limited feasibility of younger or traumatized patients to undergo clinical examinations, always result in the inaccurate and subjective radiographic HRJA assessments in pediatric patients, especially in cases involving subluxated humeroradial joints [11–14]. Currently, there are few reports on whether clinical experience improves the ability to correctly evaluate pediatric HRJA on radiographs.

Deep learning (DL) technology is excellent at accurately and automatically assessing medical images through visual networks [15]. However, there are no DL models that focus solely on automatically recognizing HRJA types on pediatric radiographs . Previous DL models were primarily developed on the basis of visual convolutional neural networks, which have limited ability to capture precise spatial information [21]. Thus, DL models must enhance their capacities to extract and learn the spatial radiographic features of the radial head and humeral capitulum to achieve precise, automated pediatric HRJA assessment.

The vision transformer (ViT) model is a type of DL model that is adept at capturing spatial positional information in images through its self-attention mechanisms [22–25]. However, it has limited software accessibility and usability, as well as associated costs [22–25]. Overall, the application of the ViT model to categorize HRJA on radiographs is lacking.

Therefore, this multicenter study aims to evaluate the radiographic assessment outcomes of A-HRJAs and NA-HRJAs among pediatric orthopedic surgeons (POS) with different levels of experience. Furthermore, the study aims to develop and validate a ViT model for accurately and automatically classifying HRJA types on pediatric elbow radiographs.

Materials and methods

After receiving the ethical approval (approval number: 2025227), we conducted a retrospective review of the clinical records, and pre- and postoperative radiographs of the elbow joints of 1938 pediatric patients (5793 radiographs) with Monteggia fractures who were treated surgically in five institutions.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age of < 17 years at the time the radiographs were taken; (2) radial head reduction within 14 days of trauma; (3) radiographs with A-HRJAs or NA-HRJAs; (4) radiographs taken with standard anteroposterior (AP), oblique, or lateral projections; and (5) complete clinical and radiographic data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of congenital radial head dislocation, concomitant metabolic bone diseases, syndromes, or neuromuscular diseases; (2) presence of fractures of the proximal radius or distal humerus; (3) poor-quality radiographs (low resolution, cast or brace masking the joint); (4) presence of hardware in the proximal one-third of the forearm; (5) incomplete clinical and radiographic data; and (6) unavailability of signed consent forms for participation in the study.

A total of 5582 (96.4%) elbow joint radiographs were included in the current study. In particular, there were 1873 (32.3%) AP, 1578 oblique (27.2%), and 2131 lateral (36.8%) radiographs of the elbow joint. The remaining 211 radiographs (3.6%) were excluded for the following reasons: 38 (0.7%) were in nonstandard projections, 20 (0.3%) lacked corresponding clinical records, 31 (0.5%) had radial head reduction > 14 days after trauma, 7 (0.1%) had concomitant radial neck fractures, 65 (1.1%) were of poor qualities, and 50 (0.9%) had hardware in the proximal one-third of the forearm.

HRJA types

On radiographs, HRJAs were categorized as either anatomical (A-HRJAs) or nonanatomical (NA-HRJAs). Specifically, an A-HRJA was diagnosed when the radius axis passed through the center of the humeral capitulum, and an NA-HRJA was diagnosed when it did not [1, 2].

Three senior POS, each with over 20 years of experience, independently evaluated the HRJA types of all 5582 radiographs. Notably, before determining the radiographic HRJA types, they referred to the pediatric patients’ clinical records, which included information on pain, swelling, and bruising locations. The HRJA type of each radiograph was confirmed if all three POS agreed on the radiographic HRJA type.

The HRJA types of 5334 radiographs (95.6%) were classified consistently by the three POS, including 3016 (56.5%) with A-HRJAs and 2318 (43.5%) with NA-HRJAs. The remaining 248 (4.4%) radiographs were excluded owing to incongruent classification of the HRJA types among the three POS.

HRJA assessment among POS with different levels of experience

A total of 294 radiographs (5.5%) were randomly selected from the pool of 5334 radiographs with identified HRJA types. Subsequently, 11 raters, including 4 junior-level (< 5 years of experience), 4 mid-level (5–10 years of experience), and 3 senior-level raters (> 10 years of experience), were asked to independently record the HRJA types of the 294 radiographs.

The accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive values (PPV), and negative predictive values (NPV) of the raters’ rating performances were then calculated. Higher accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV indicated better radiographic assessment performance.

ViT model construction

Radiograph preparation

The pool of 5334 pediatric radiographs displaying identified HRJA types were reoriented to position the humerus superiorly and the forearm inferiorly. Then, the radiographs were cropped to include the distal one-third of the humerus (on the top), the proximal one-third of the forearm (on the bottom), and the skin (laterally and medially).

Datasets division

The radiographs were randomly divided into two parts at the patient level: a dataset for ViT model construction (n = 5118; 95.9%) and an external testing dataset for evaluating the ViT model’s clinical efficacy (n = 216; 4.1%). Furthermore, the dataset for ViT model construction (5118 radiographs) was randomly split into training (n = 4095; 80.0%) and internal testing (n = 1023; 20.0%) datasets with an approximate ratio of 4:1. In particular, the dataset for ViT model construction was collected from four institutions (80.0%), and the external testing dataset was from another independent institution (20.0%).

Comparison between the ViT model and POS assessment performances of the external testing dataset

First round without the assistance of ViT model

The 11 POS with varying levels of experience, who were blinded to the HRJA types of the external testing dataset and the clinical records corresponding to each radiograph, were asked to review the dataset and record the HRJA type of each radiograph. The radiographs were randomly distributed.

Second round with the assistance of ViT model

One month after the first round, the 11 raters, who were masked to the HRJA types as well as the associated clinical records for each radiograph, were instructed to classify the HRJA types of radiographs of the external testing dataset with X-rays newly randomly distributed. Before determining their assessment outcomes (A-HRJAs or NA-HRJAs), the raters referred to the ViT model’s rating results for each radiograph of the external testing dataset.

Notably, the HRJA classification descriptions in the form of drawings and radiographs were sent to raters to be familiar with it 1-week ahead of reviewing the dataset in each round.

Evaluation of the radiographic assessment performance

The performances of the ViT model and POS in classifying HRJA types were evaluated by calculating accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Data were expressed as numerical variables, frequencies, percentages, and means with standard deviations.

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the differences in HRJAs categorization performances among junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS regarding accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV.

One-sample t-tests were performed to compare the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the ViT model with those of the POS following each round of HRJA type classification.

Paired-sample t-tests compared the differences in HRJAs categorization performances of POS between the first and second rounds regarding accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV.

The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated on the basis of receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to assess the rating performances of the ViT model, junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS, respectively.

Fleiss-kappa analysis was used to evaluate the reliability (poor: 0.0–0.2; fair: 0.21–0.4; moderate: 0.41–0.6; substantial: 0.61–0.8; excellent: 0.81–1.0) of each round of HRJAs classification performances among POS [26].

The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

In general, increased clinical experience is critical to accurate diagnosis [27, 28]. However, our study found that pediatric HRJA type classification on radiographs was comparable among POS with different levels of experience; these POS did not achieve 100% accuracy. This emphasizes the difficulties of precisely classifying HRJA types on elbow radiographs. In this context, we developed a ViT model to accurately and automatically categorize pediatric HRJA types on radiographs. The outcomes of its clinical application showed that the ViT model significantly improved the rating performances of POS when classifying radiographic pediatric HRJA types (A-HRJAs or NA-HRJAs).

Our results showed that clinical experience did not significantly improve the performances of POS in categorizing HRJA types on radiographs, which differs from what was reported by Choi et al. [29]. This discrepancy may be owing to the inadequate sample size and limited number of raters in their study. In their study, only 40 NA-HRJA cases were reviewed, and only one rater was included in the junior-level group [29]. In our study, 294 radiographs were randomly selected and reviewed by 11 raters with varying levels of experience, and one-way ANOVA analyses were then conducted to evaluate the raters’ performances.

Similar to previous research, the accuracy of classifying pediatric HRJA types on radiographs among senior-level radiologists did not reach 100% in this study. Gleeson et al. reviewed 220 pediatric forearm fractures and found that senior radiologists misdiagnosed 25% of fractures with NA-HRJAs [7]. Hubbard et al. and Tan et al. also reported similar results [30, 31]. Difficulties in classifying pediatric HRJA types on radiographs may be due to the irregularities in the radius, as well as the incomplete ossification of the radial head and humeral capitulum on radiographs among pediatric patients. This makes it challenging to discern whether the radius axis passes through the center of the humeral capitulum on radiographs, especially in cases with subtle anatomical changes [11–14]. Furthermore, in our study, raters classified pediatric HRJA types without referring to physical examination results, which are critical for increasing the accuracy of radiographic classification of pediatric HRJAs [2, 12, 29].

Despite the importance of physical examination, radiographs were still the main reference for identifying pediatric HRJA types because it was always difficult to physically examine pediatric patients with elbow injuries [2, 6, 10, 12, 29]. Furthermore, it was also challenging to arrange for multiple POS to assess the pediatric HRJA types of every patient even given the critical role of multi-rater assessment in enhancing the accuracy of radiographic HRJA typing [29]. Consequently, to develop a DL model to precisely classify pediatric HRJA types on radiographs is very important. To achieve this goal, our study only included cases with definite physical examination results to create the dataset for ViT model construction. Additionally, the HRJA types in the dataset for ViT model construction were consistently identified by three senior POS with > 20 years of experience, who had access to the physical examination data.

The ViT model excelled at classifying radiographic pediatric HRJA types in the testing dataset, as shown by the AUC value, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and F1 scores. This may be owing to its self-attention mechanism and global context modeling capacity, enabling it to capture global image information, particularly spatial features across the image plane, as reported in previous studies [32–35]. Differentiating pediatric HRJA types on radiographs relies on the relative spatial position of the radial head and humeral capitulum. Consequently, the ViT model, which was trained on a large-scale dataset of 2932 A-HRJA cases and 2186 NA-HRJA cases, can accurately recognize the radiographic features of the radial head and humeral capitulum, as well as their spatial relationship, thus achieving the accurate pediatric HRJA type assessments on radiographs.

The ViT model performed better than the POS in the first round but not in the second. This was mainly owing to the uncertainty of the POS’s rating results, which were affected by the difficulties of classifying pediatric HRJAs on radiographs [11–14]. Additionally, the ViT model was trained on a dataset with defined HRJA types, enabling the ViT model to perform better at classifying radiographic pediatric HRJA types. Therefore, POS tended to trust the ViT model’s results when classifying pediatric HRJAs on radiographs.

The most significant finding was that the ViT model improved the accuracy, PPV, NPV, and reliability of POS’s rating performances of pediatric HRJA type classification on radiographs. Our findings reduce the time cost of manually evaluating pediatric HRJA types on radiographs, and overcome the difficulties of manually classifying radiographic pediatric HRJA types, thus potentially decreasing the pediatric elbow joint complication rates due to the HRJA types misdiagnosis.

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, we only evaluated the clinical efficacy of the ViT model among POS with various levels of experience, while radiologists and orthopedic surgeons specializing in other areas were not involved in the analysis. Secondly, the ViT model’s performance might be further improved. Thirdly, the ViT model’s rating performances were not compared with those of other models. Fourth, ultrasound was not used to determine or confirm the types of pediatric HRJAs because it was not routinely used in our settings. Nevertheless, three senior POS with over 20 years of experience consistently identified the radiographic types of HRJA in the included patients by referencing their clinical records. Despite these limitations, our study was the first to use a ViT model to accurately and automatically classify pediatric HRJA types on radiographs. Furthermore, the ViT model significantly improved POS’s rating performances in classifying radiographs with A-HRJAs and NA-HRJAs, demonstrating strong clinical significance. In the future, a DL model with superior performance will be necessary to precisely classify radiographic pediatric HRJA types on the basis of larger sample sizes. Additionally, its performance will need to be evaluated against other models across raters with various specialties from multiple institutions.

In conclusion, manually classifying pediatric HRJA types on radiographs can be challenging, and the accuracy did not depend on clinical experience. We developed a ViT model that demonstrated superior capabilities in automatically and accurately categorizing pediatric HRJA types on elbow radiographs. This model has been validated to improve POS’s rating performances in categorizing pediatric A-HRJAs and NA-HRJAs on elbow radiographs.

Our model can significantly improve the accuracy with which pediatric HRJA types are identified on elbow radiographs. This would reduce the misdiagnosis rate, particularly among pediatric patients with Monteggia fractures. Furthermore, our model enables less experienced physicians to accurately evaluate HRJA types on radiographs. However, the clinical translation of our model is hindered by several challenges, including limited software accessibility, usability barriers, and substantial implementation costs. Further investment and dedicated efforts are necessary to address these challenges and facilitate the translation of this model from research to clinical practice.
