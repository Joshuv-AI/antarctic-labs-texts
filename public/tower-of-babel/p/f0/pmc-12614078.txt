Representation of Older Adult Patients in Clinical Trials
Authors: Sebastian Haertter, Navita L Mallalieu, Soundos Saleh, Paul Goldsmith, Rucha Sane, Jack Cook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTOlder adults remain significantly underrepresented in clinical trials. This is concerning because age‐associated physiological changes can alter drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. This paper examines the status of older adult representation in clinical trials, analyzes contributing factors, and discusses initiatives to address gaps. Data were collected from studies completed between 2010 and 2025 using ClinicalTrials.gov to assess age‐related inclusion/exclusion criteria. Further, two surveys of pharmaceutical companies were initially conducted to assess age ranges of randomized patients in pivotal trials, what clinical pharmacology approaches were applied to potentially support dosing in older adult patients, whether exclusion criteria disproportionally excluded older patients, reasons older adult patients were not randomized, and whether older patients more often discontinued trials prematurely. Exclusion criterion based on an age cut‐off was rarely applied. Pharmacokinetic analyses were performed for most trials, but only one age‐related dosing recommendation was reported out of 29 products. In a survey of 41 clinical studies, exclusion criteria showed no bias toward exclusion of patients > 80 years. Reasons for not randomizing older patients were diverse and trial dependent. Subjective reasons for non‐randomization at higher frequencies in older patients were patient wish and doctor advice. Discontinuation rate did not reveal a clear age‐related trend. When comparing trial age composition to disease prevalence by age, a mismatch was evident with fewer than expected older patients enrolled. This research identifies the gap between trial participation and disease prevalence by age groups, offering insights for sponsor companies to enhance older adults' inclusion in clinical research.Keywords: clinical trials, discontinuation, exclusion criteria, older adult patients, screening failure

Introduction

The aging of the global population presents significant challenges and opportunities for drug development. By 2034, adults aged 65 years and older (77 million) will outnumber under 18 years old (76.5 million) in the United States, with the adults aged 85 years and above expected to triple from the current 6 to 18 million by 2060 [1]. This demographic shift has profound implications for medication use and drug development, as older adults constitute the primary consumers of prescription medications due to higher rates of chronic diseases and multimorbidities [2, 3].

Despite being the largest group of medication users, older adults—particularly those over 80 years—remain significantly underrepresented in clinical drug trials that inform drug approvals and prescribing practices [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. This underrepresentation is particularly concerning because aging is associated with physiological changes that can alter drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, potentially affecting both safety and efficacy profiles [9, 10]. These age‐related changes include decreased renal and hepatic function, alterations in body composition, reduced cardiac output, and changes in receptor sensitivity, all of which can influence drug disposition and response to medication response [10, 11].

Since the 1980s, the FDA and ICH have implemented initiatives to improve geriatric medication information, including guidelines and reporting requirements for age‐related data in drug applications [12, 13, 14]. More recently, in 2021, the FDA launched the “Roadmap to 2030 for New Drug Evaluation in Older Adults” initiative, aiming to improve the evidence base for medication use in older populations [15].

To support this initiative, the International Consortium for Innovation and Quality in Pharmaceutical Development (IQ Consortium), a not‐for‐profit organization of pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies, established a dedicated working group within the Clinical Pharmacology Leadership Group (CPLG) which worked together with FDA on a Webinar in 2021 [15]. This working group was formed to examine whether the inclusion of older patients in pivotal studies reflects disease prevalence in this age group and to identify factors driving potential underrepresentation. The working group's mission aligns with the IQ Consortium's broader goal of advancing science and technology to augment the capability of member companies to develop transformational solutions that benefit patients, regulators, and the broader research and development community.

A nationally representative survey of older adults with multimorbidity revealed that major barriers to participation include fear of side effects (48%), concerns about taking too many medicines (44%), and difficulties with video‐based trial activities (33%) [16]. While remote participation has been suggested to reduce burden, 65% of surveyed older adults preferred in‐person visits over video‐only trials (49%). These patient‐reported preferences often conflict with proposed changes in clinical trial design intended to increase enrollment, but being unknowingly detrimental to that goal.

This paper examines the current status of older adult representation in clinical trials and describes the potential factors contributing to their underrepresentation, which may in turn help regulatory agencies and industry to address these gaps. This manuscript presents findings from the IQ Consortium's working group analyses of screening failure rates, discontinuation patterns, and the impact of exclusion criteria on older adult participation across multiple therapeutic areas. The analysis is novel in that it examined the potential impact of inclusion and exclusion criteria, other than age, on potential enrollment of older adults and examined reasons for screen failures from individuals considered to participation in studies rather than opinions from older adult focus groups.

Methods

ClinicalTrials.gov Survey

A search on ClinicalTrials.gov (Home|ClinicalTrials.gov) [17] was conducted using the filters of age range 80–150 years; only interventional trials; only Phase II and III; with results; study documents: study protocols; only funded by industry; study completion between January 2010 and December 2024.

The same search was repeated but instead of specific age ranges, adults (> 18 years) and older adults (≥ 80 years) were used as age filter criteria. The difference between these results should reflect the percentage of trials with a potential age limit < 80 years.

Pharmaceutical Industry Survey

In a first survey across IQ pharmaceutical industry members, the age range allowed per protocol was compared with the age range of actually randomized patients from recent pivotal trials across indications. Second, the survey aimed at gathering information on dose modifications for older adult patients enrolled in the pivotal trial due to safety concerns or later at launch, and what clinical pharmacology/pharmacometrics techniques were applied in these trials in support of dosing in older adult patients. All included trials were carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization Good Clinical Practice guidelines, approved by the respective ethics committees, and all patients had provided written informed consent.

Detailed View on Clinical Trials

In a second survey, between May 2023 and August 2024, IQ pharmaceutical industry members were asked to provide information from pivotal trials completed within the last 6 years on: screening, randomization, and discontinuation by age, patient's identified sex, and region, on potential reasons for screening failure by age, gender and region, and the respective inclusion and exclusion criteria. The age categories were defined as 18 to < 65, 65 to < 70, 70 to < 75, 75 to < 80, 80 to < 85, and ≥ 85 years. In case the latter two groups were too small, they were pooled to a group of patients ≥ 80 (80+) years. Discontinuation or the reason for not being randomized was not recorded or retrievable for some of these trials. Hence, the information summarized in this paper is only given for a subset of studies (n = 9 for screening failure reasons and N = 12 for discontinuation); in three cases, the company only provided the discontinuation rate by age for the placebo groups.

The EHR (Electronic Health Records) tool, TriNetX (TriNetX LLC) was applied to estimate the size of the base population per disease which would meet each trial's inclusion criteria and the attrition due to exclusion criteria. This was compared to the actual representation of older adult patients in the respective trial (randomized set) giving the participation/prevalence ratio. The TriNetX database is a global health‐collaborative clinical research platform collecting real‐time electronic medical data from a network of HCOs. For further information on TriNetX, please refer to real‐world data for the life sciences and healthcare|TriNetX and Paljarvi et al. [18, 19]. All data from the TriNetX platform and all data from the individual trials were aggregated data. The TriNetX data were de‐identified per the de‐identification standard defined in Section §164.514(a) of the HIPAA Privacy Rule (Publication Guidelines—TriNetX) [20]. TriNetX was accessed in July 2024, using the network “global” and ICD‐10 criteria for the definition of the disease. Disease per ICD‐10 definition and the inclusion criteria were used as “Must Have” and exclusion criteria as “Cannot Have.” To estimate the dedicated effect on an older patient population, the analysis was repeated twice for all patients ≥ 18 years and using a filter for age ≥ 80 years. The age cut‐off of 80 years was chosen because in some of the available studies, patients ≥ 85 years in the randomized set were very small and a comparison with the estimated disease patient group of that age was considered not meaningful.

Results

ClinicalTrials.gov Survey

Two thousand seven hundred and seventy‐eight interventional industry‐funded phase II and III studies completed between January 2010 and December 2024 with adult patients, that is, ≥ 18 years were identified in ClinicalTrails.gov; 2150 or 77.4% of these studies did allow inclusion of older adult patients of ≥ 80 years. Given that most trials allowed the inclusion of adult patients of ≥ 80 years, it was concluded that age‐specific exclusion criteria were not a major reason for the low participation of older adults and no further analysis for other possible covariates (e.g., molecule type, therapeutic area, stage of development, company, etc.) was undertaken.

Pharmaceutical Industry Survey

The first IQ internal survey comprised a total of 36 pivotal trials across indications (Supplementary Table S1). In 30 trials (83%), the only requirement for inclusion was an age of 18 years or above but without any upper age limit and four studies were preferentially conducted in older adult patients. In 29 trials, patients > 75 years were randomized to any of the study treatments. Population pharmacokinetics (PPK) analyses were performed on data from 29 trials while exposure‐response analyses were conducted in only 17 trials. Mechanistic disease‐ or disease progression models and/or other QSP models were reported for seven studies. In only one case, a dosing recommendation for older patients (> 80 years) different from younger adult patients was derived (Table S1).

Detailed View on Clinical Trials

In the second survey, overall, 41 pivotal studies across therapeutic areas of cardiovascular, neurology (pain symptoms), infectious disease, ophthalmology, and oncology including 112,471 patients were available. Each of these trials comprised between 184 and 19,381 patients. While exclusion criteria could generally reduce the potential patient participation (Figure S1), this effect was not preferentially biased against older patients, 80 years or older, based on the proportion of older adults screened being generally similar to the proportion of older adults being eventually randomized to the trial, after the application of exclusion criteria (Figure S1). These data were the results of a simulation by means of TRiNetX using the inclusion/indication and exclusion protocol information of the respective trials.

While overall, there was a slight trend to higher screening failure rates with age (Figure 1, average over all trials included) in some indications (HCV, CKD, and transcatheter valve replacement) the screening failure rate did not reveal higher numbers for patients older than 80 years, as shown in Figure S2.

Percent of patients who were screened but not randomized by age categories across indications. Observed data from the respective trials.

The reasons for not randomizing a screened patient were quite diverse and could not be pooled across trials except for some clinical laboratory parameters like renal function. In the case of a COVID‐19 study, missing the criterion of creatinine clearance of ≥ 50 mL/min was the exclusive reason for screening failure for patients of ≥ 85 years (100% of screening failure) but accounted for only 19% in patients younger than 65 years. The overall screen failure rate in these COVID‐19 trials was, however, exceptionally low at 1.35%. A high rate of screen failure due to impaired renal function (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2) in the oldest patients' category was also observed in trials for osteoarthritic pain, chronic lower back pain, and heart failure with reduced or preserved ejection fraction.

Other more subjective reasons for non‐randomization with higher frequencies were patient wish and doctor advice as shown in the example of a chronic kidney disease trial (Figure S3).

When patients were eventually randomized to treatment, the discontinuation rate did not reveal a clear trend of higher discontinuation numbers with increasing age (Table S2).

Investigation of the participant age ratio, that is, the age composition in a trial compared to the prevalence of the disease in real‐world population, majority of the studies had a mismatch between the prevalence of the disease in patients older than 80 years and the participation of this age composition of patients randomized in a trial (Figure 2). The only exception to this was the transcatheter heart valve (transcat valve) replacement.

Proportion of older adults (age ≥ 80 years) relative to all adults (age > 18 years) who were randomized in an individual trial (in red, observed data) versus the overall older adult population with the respective diagnosis (base population) who could qualify to participate in that trial (in black, simulated using TriNetX data). In the HF trial, the patient population was diagnosed with HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) while TriNetX only provided a general HF category. CKD1 and 2 and HCV1 and 2 are different trials with different datasets.

Discussion

There are numerous reasons why older adults are underrepresented in clinical trials. These reasons can be categorized into two main perspectives: those of the patients and those of the study sponsors.

From the patient's perspective, a study involving two trials with over 1000 participants [21]—one including veterans and the other non‐veterans—identified key deterrents such as health concerns, mistrust, and lack of interest. Other research highlights additional factors, including cognitive challenges, the perceived burden of participation [22], logistical barriers, restrictive eligibility criteria, and family influence [23]. Additionally, increased physician engagement has been cited as a key factor in increasing the participation of older adults [24, 25]. Our analysis also revealed distinct differences in participants' preferences, especially in one case where older adults expressed unique concerns compared to other age groups (Figure S3).

On the sponsor's side, we examined various factors contributing to low participation rates among older adults, such as exclusion criteria, reasons for screening failures, and discontinuation. To provide context, we utilized a database representing a broad and diverse patient population. Similar to the patient's perspective, the analysis revealed a significant gap between the demographic composition of trial participants and the actual prevalence of conditions in the real‐world population, indicating a lack of representativeness.

If the primary cause for poor representation of the older adult subgroup is the reason identified from the patients' perspective, future approaches should focus on addressing these. Logistics, such as transportation, reimbursement for travel expenses, or home visits, are potentially the easiest to implement, reducing the burden of clinical trial participation. Clear and accessible communication considering age‐specific impairments could potentially help. Lastly, building trust is a key element for establishing relationships between patients, health care providers, and community leaders. It was noteworthy that, in our survey, exclusion criteria did not emerge as a primary factor for the reduced participation of older adults. While our research identified that exclusion criteria were, in fact, not a key factor during the examination of the data, we felt the need for a deeper classification of causes for screen failures. Unfortunately, these reasons were not systematically recorded in all trials and would require some further research to identify potential patterns.

Understanding the underlying causes of this disparity is essential for addressing the underrepresentation of older adults in clinical trials. This research identifies the gap between trial participation and disease prevalence, offering insights for sponsor companies to enhance older adults' inclusion in clinical research.
