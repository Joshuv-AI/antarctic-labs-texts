Switching on the behavioral and neural rhythmicity to retrieve memories when the number of retained items exceeds four
Authors: Takuya Ideriha, Junichi Ushiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDoes our nervous system rely on the same neural mechanism for memory retrieval across all contexts? Here, we report an intriguing phenomenon where the nervous system “switches on” behavioral and neural rhythms under specific conditions, such as when the number of items being learned concurrently exceeds four. In our experiments, participants repeatedly learned and retrieved 2, 3, 4, or 5 color-letter pairs. Analyses of hundreds of reaction times revealed a significant tendency for memory recall to occur at discrete timings corresponding to theta-alpha (4–13 Hz) cycles in the 5-pair condition, while no such behavioral rhythmicity was observed in the 2, 3, and 4-pair conditions. The behavioral rhythmicity showed a clear increasing trend as the trials progressed. This rhythmicity was more prominent when recalling a different item from the immediate previous trial. Analyses of electroencephalographic (EEG) data also revealed an increased cue-locked neural theta-alpha rhythm in the 5-pair condition. Interestingly, the behavioral and EEG rhythms exhibited distinct characteristics, suggesting they each visualize different aspects of the underlying neural rhythm. Our results indicate that our nervous system adaptively engages a neural rhythm, as if switching it on, to achieve memory retrieval in specific contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40959-9.Keywords: Behavioral fluctuation, Event-related potentials, Magical number, Neural oscillation, Working memorySubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

We often experience difficulty in recalling information that we believe we have learned. This phenomenon is particularly pronounced when learning a large amount of information simultaneously, such as new vocabulary. Nevertheless, we can often manage to retrieve the target memory even under these challenging conditions. In contrast, memory retrieval is effortless when learning only a few items or recalling well-established knowledge. These contrasting situations suggest that different memory retrieval mechanisms may be engaged depending on the context. However, it remains unclear how these mechanisms differ across such varying conditions.

Recent electrophysiological and intracranial studies converge on rhythmic coordination in the theta (4–8 Hz) and alpha (8–13 Hz) bands as a central mechanism of memory retrieval1,2. Generated by the synchronous activity of large-scale neuronal ensembles, these oscillations are even non-invasively measurable from the scalp using electroencephalography (EEG)3. Previous reports have shown that theta-alpha synchronized activity increases around the time of retrieval4–6. Also, retrieval events are reported to occur at specific phases of these oscillations7, implying a discrete sampling mechanism that impacts reaction times (RT). As a behavioral consequence of this neural periodicity, RT distributions for recall typically exhibit a multimodal structure with several peaks, rather than a smooth, unimodal shape8. Thus, convergent evidence indicates that memory retrieval relies on a periodic mechanism, the signature of which is observable in both neural dynamics and behavioral patterns.

A critical limitation of this body of work, however, is that the context-dependency of these retrieval mechanisms has been largely overlooked. First, cognitive load is a critical contextual factor that may modulate neural activity. For instance, attempting to encode a large amount of information simultaneously, such as when cramming for an exam, would likely cause interference among items and make memory retrieval difficult9,10. Conversely, if dealing with only two or three items at a time, they can be easily managed within the capacity of human working memory11,12, making recall effortless. Second, the consolidation state of a memory represents another key variable. As memories gradually stabilize and the underlying neural circuitry and the brain regions where they are stored change accordingly13,14, the retrieval mechanism required to access them likely differs depending on their degree of consolidation. Third, our sensory perception and responses are influenced by recently viewed stimuli15,16. It is known that a recent stimulus can both facilitate faster responses16 to and introduce bias in the processing of subsequent stimuli17. Therefore, it is plausible that under these differing conditions, distinct neural mechanisms for memory retrieval are engaged accordingly.

Here, we report an intriguing phenomenon where theta-alpha rhythms are selectively engaged only under specific memory retrieval contexts. In our experiment, participants were required to learn and recall memory items in parallel as if when learning vocabulary (Fig. 1a), and we analyzed their RTs and scalp EEG across hundreds of trials. Our results indicate that the engagement of these theta-alpha rhythms depends on factors such as the number of items being learned concurrently, the consolidation state of the memory, the recency of the memory item, and whether the recall was correct or incorrect. Taken together, our findings indicate that memory retrieval mechanisms are highly context-dependent, highlighted by the selective engagement of theta-alpha oscillations only in specific situations.

Materials and methods

Participant details

We conducted two experiments. In Experiment 1 (see Experimental design for detail), 15 participants carried out 2, 3, 4, 5-pair conditions (2-pair: mean age ± SD, 22.2 ± 1.7 years; 10 males, 5 females, 2 left-handed; 3-pair: 22.0 ± 1.9 years; 9 males, 6 females, 1 left-handed; 4-pair: 22.3 ± 2.0 years; 10 males, 5 females, 1 left-handed; 5-pair: 23.8 ± 2.7 years; 8 males, 7 females, 1 left-handed). In Experiment 2, 25 participants (25.2 ± 3.7 years; 17 males, 8 females, 2 left-handed) carried out both 3 and 5-pair conditions. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity and reported no color vision deficiency. Participants gave informed consent before the experimental session and received monetary compensation. All experimental protocols and procedures were approved by the SFC Research Ethics Committee, Keio University (Approval Number 293). All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations outlined in the journal’s editorial policies, and with full respect for the dignity, rights, and welfare of the participants.

Experimental design

Throughout all experiments, the stimuli and program were generated using MATLAB (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and the Psychophysics Toolbox (Psychtoolbox-3)18. The monitor was positioned approximately 57 cm from the participant. Experiments 1 and 2 were identical, except that we recorded EEG in Experiment 2. 2, 3, 4, and 5-pair conditions were conducted in Experiment 1, while only 3 and 5-pair conditions were carried out in Experiment 2.

In the experiment, participants performed a task where they learned and recalled color/letter pairs in parallel as if when learning vocabulary in random order (Fig. 1a). In each trial, a colored circle was presented in the center of the screen for 1,500 ms, followed by the corresponding letter. The number of pairs to remember in each set varied by condition, ranging from 2 to 5 pairs. Each pair appeared 20 times per set, allowing participants to gradually form a strong memory. The order of stimulus presentation was randomized. Participants were instructed to accurately and quickly type the corresponding letter on the keyboard when a color appeared. If they could not recall the paired letter (including the first encounter with the color), they were instructed to wait for 1,500 ms and then re-memorize the corresponding letter. Conversely, if they remembered the paired letter, they were asked to respond as quickly as possible within the 1,500 ms during which the colored circle was displayed. The RTs here were analyzed later. When participants responded within 1,500 ms, correct or wrong feedback quickly appeared on the screen. In instances of correct reactions, the corresponding letter was displayed in blue, whereas for incorrect reactions, the correct letter was displayed in red for 500 ms, which worked as a correct or wrong feedback. If a reaction was not given within 1,500 ms, the correct letter was displayed, and participants were instructed to memorize the color/letter pair again and press the key. After a 1,000 ms inter-trial interval, the next trial began. A specific color/letter pair appeared 20 times within a single set. Thus, for example, in the 3-pair condition, there were 3 × 20 = 60 trials per set, and the order of presented pairs was randomized. Participants learned color/letter pairs through such sets and gradually responded more quickly. The number of trials and sets per condition are listed in Table 1. While the color/letter pairs were fixed within each set, they were changed between sets. Consequently, participants were required to learn new pairs with each set. Between sets, participants could take breaks. The color codes for the eight candidate colors were [230,0,18], [243,152,0], [207,219,0], [34,172,56], [0,160,233], [29,32,136], [146,7,131], and [228,0,127] in RGB format. The candidate letters for key inputs were ‘s’, ‘d’, ‘f’, ‘j’, ‘k’, and ‘l’. In each set, colors and letters were chosen from the candidates randomly. The fingers used for reactions were the left and right ring fingers, middle fingers, and index fingers.

EEG recordings

Scalp EEG was recorded from 19 scalp positions based on the International 10–20 system in Experiment 2. 19 Ag/AgCl electrodes with a diameter of 18 mm (g.LADYbirdPASSIVE 1035, Guger Technologies, Graz, Austria) were affixed to an EEG cap (g.GAMMAcap 1027; Guger Technologies, Graz, Austria) covering the whole head. Reference and ground electrodes were placed on the left and right earlobes, respectively. EEG was amplified and band-passed (0.5–200 Hz) via an analog biosignal amplifier (g.BSamp 0201 A; Guger Technologies). Analog EEG signals were converted into digital signals by an analog-to-digital converter (NI USB-6259, National Instruments) at a sample rate of 1000 Hz, controlled by a data-logger program designed using MATLAB software (The MathWorks, Inc.).

Behavioral data analyses

Trials included

Basically, only data from correct trials (correct reactions within 1,500 ms) were analyzed. Also, we analyzed trials after all pairs appeared at least once. For example, if there were pairs A, B, and C in the 3-pair condition and the order of trials was “A, B, B, A, C, B, …”, trials after the sixth trial (B) were included in the analysis. In each participant, the analysis for each condition was performed by merging the data across all sets within the same condition. For example, in the 3-pair condition where five sets of 60 trials were conducted, data from 300 trials were used for the same analysis. Of these, data that met the above criteria, correct trials within 1,500 ms after all pairs appeared at least once, were analyzed according to the following procedure. Hit rate (HR) and median RT were also calculated using these trials.

Successive and non-successive trials

In each condition, we divided trials into “successive” and “non-successive” trials. Successive trials are the trials whose stimuli are the same color as the previous trials, and non-successive trials are the other trials. Because the data from successive trials are so easily recalled and likely to exhibit different characteristics than the other trial (see Fig. 1b). we separated the analyses from the other trials.

RT distribution analyses

Of the RT data that met the above criteria, data exceeding 3 MAD (median absolute deviation) were excluded from the analysis as outliers. RT distributions were calculated using the Kernel density estimation by sliding a 50-ms rectangular window from 1 to 1,500 ms with 1 ms resolution. The obtained distribution was smoothed by a moving average using a 20-ms window. To analyze the time-resolved frequency representation in the non-stationary time course of RT distributions, we applied the Morlet wavelet decomposition with the cwt function from the Wavelet Toolbox for MATLAB. The above method was used to assess the oscillatory characteristic of the behavioral rhythmicity instead of the Fourier transform because the wavelet decomposition does not need the assumption of the stationarity of the time courses. The average value in the 4–13 Hz range over the entire time period of the obtained decomposition was calculated as the quantitative theta-alpha value. This value was Z-scored together with the results of the Surrogate analysis described below and used for statistical analysis. We defined this value as “behavioral theta-alpha value”.

Surrogate analyses

To examine the existence of theta-alpha oscillation in RT distributions, we generated null-hypothesis surrogate distributions for each participant’s data as follows. First, we fitted an ex-Gaussian distribution to the original RT distribution. Generally, the ex-Gaussian distribution well fits human RT distribution and has been used to examine human reaction characteristics, including retrieval of memories19–21. Then, an equal number of simulated RTs as the corresponding trials were randomly sampled from the ex-Gaussian distribution. After that, the same frequency analyses as the original one were conducted for the simulated RTs. This process was repeated 500 times, and the mean and the standard deviation of null-hypothesis data were obtained. Using this data, the original theta-alpha value was transformed into Z-score.

Comparison between the early and the late trials

To test the effect of learning on rhythmicity, we compared data from non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition between the early and the late trials in each set. We first extracted non-successive trials based on several criteria (see *Trials included). The first and the last 10 trials of each set were extracted, resulting in the first and the last 30 trials because each participant carried out three sets of the 5-pair condition. Note that the above criteria reflected how well each memory was consolidated because participants should have learned new pairs in each set. Because the subsequent analyses require a precise extraction of phase in the RT rhythmicity, only data from participants with robust behavioural theta–alpha value (the peak value in the theta–alpha band > 1.96) were retained. This selection was made solely to ensure analytical accuracy and does not constitute statistical double dipping. Importantly, most excluded participants still showed positive behavioural theta–alpha values; thus, their omission should not be taken as evidence of absent rhythmicity. To ensure the number of participants included in this analysis, we aggregated data from Experiment 1 and 2, resulting in 17 participants’ data who showed significant behavioral oscillations. For each participant, the peak frequency in the 4–13 Hz range of the oscillations was calculated, and a bandpass filter was applied in the 0.5 Hz before and after the peak frequency, resulting in a 1 Hz range (second-order Butterworth filter). A Hilbert transform was applied to the obtained waveform to extract the phase of the oscillation. Phase 0 corresponds to the rhythmic peak of the behavioral oscillations. Using this, we calculated the phase where the reaction was made in a certain trial. The V-test was used to calculate whether RTs of the first and the last 30 trials were biased toward phase 0 of the oscillation. These V-tests were performed for each individual. In addition, phase data for the first and the last 30 trials were aggregated across individuals, and whether the phase was biased toward 0 degrees was also calculated using V-test. This analysis calculated how strictly reactions of the extracted first/last 30 trials were phase-locked to the behavioral oscillations. After that, we statistically compared the first/last 30 trials. However, results from the above-mentioned analysis should be carefully interpreted because it did not avoid the circularity issue where the trials to calculate the phases of reactions were also included in the calculation of RT distributions. Therefore, we conducted an additional analysis by excluding the extracted first/last 30 trials from calculating the RT distribution. In this additional analysis, the number of participants who showed significant behavioral oscillations decreased from 17 to 14 because fewer trials were used for the calculation of RT distributions. Hence, data from the 14 participants were included in the statistical analysis. Other processes were carried out in the same manner as the aforementioned analysis.

Comparison between the correct and the incorrect trials

We examined whether RTs in incorrect trials within 1,500 ms were phase-locked to behavioral oscillations of correct trials. Data were retained only for participants whose correct-trial RTs exhibited a robust theta–alpha oscillation (peak value > 1.96) and who produced ≥ 10 incorrect responses within 1,500 ms. Incorrect responses in this time window are interpreted as retrieval of an incorrect letter, not omissions of recall. Applying these criteria left 13 participants (Experiment 1: n = 5; Experiment 2: n = 8). As noted in “Comparison between early and late trials,” this selection of participants serves solely to ensure the accuracy of the subsequent phase-extraction analyses; it is not a statistical manipulation and should not be taken to imply that excluded participants lacked rhythmicity. In the analysis, we first randomly extracted as many correct trials as incorrect trials. The RTs of the correct trials that remained unextracted were used to calculate behavioral oscillations to examine whether the RTs of the incorrect and extracted correct trials were locked to the phase of that oscillation, to avoid circularity. Similarly to comparison between the early and the late trials, the waveform of the peak frequency in the 4–13 Hz range was extracted with a bandpass filter (second-order Butterworth filter) in the 0.5 Hz range before and after the peak frequency. A Hilbert transform was applied to obtain the phase of the oscillation. Using this, we calculated the phase where the reaction was made in a certain trial. The V-test was applied to examine whether RTs of incorrect and extracted correct reactions were biased toward phase 0 (peak phase) of the oscillation. These V-tests were performed for each individual. In addition, phase data for incorrect and extracted correct reactions were aggregated across individuals, and whether the phase was biased toward 0 degree was calculated using V-test.

EEG data analyses

EEG preprocessing

Recorded EEG signals were processed with MATLAB and EEGLAB toolbox version 2023.0 22. The EEG signals were high-pass filtered at 1 Hz, and band-stop filtered at 49–51, 99–101, 149–151 Hz by third-order Butterworth filter. Data segments with visible noise due to body movement, muscle activity, or poor contact were visually identified and excluded from the analysis. The remaining data were decomposed with independent component analysis and components with eye blinks and eye movements were removed using “runica” function of EEGLAB22. The data were then separated into epochs to calculate event-related potentials (ERPs). The epoch-length was 6 s spanning from 2 s before to 4 s after the stimulus.

EEG analyses

In this study, we calculated ERPs to examine stimulus-locked rhythmicity of EEG. The rationale for using ERPs was that the behavioral oscillation observed in RT distributions suggests a transient, cue-locked neural process. Specifically, if behavioral rhythmicity reflects a phase resetting or evoked response at the moment of the retrieval cue, such activity should be detectable as a phase-aligned potential in the ERP waveform. Therefore, ERP analysis allowed us to directly test whether the rhythmic dynamics underlying behavioral oscillations are reflected in cue-locked neural activity. As to EEG analyses, we only analyzed correctly answered non-successive trials because the number of successive or incorrect trials would not be sufficient for calculating ERPs. The quantitative value from ERPs was derived within a 500 ms time window: from 0 ms to 500 ms post-stimulus for stimulus-locked potentials. Considering that the time window was 500 ms, trials where the RT was within 500 ms were excluded from the analysis. To unify the analysis conditions for both the 3-pair and 5-pair conditions, the number of trials analyzed was aligned with the condition having the fewer trials. For conditions with more trials, data were randomly sampled to match the number of trials in the condition with fewer trials. For instance, if a participant had 176 analyzable trials in the 3-pair condition and 120 in the 5-pair condition, the analysis was standardized to 120 trials of the 5-pair condition. From the 3-pair condition, 120 trials were randomly selected for analysis, and the rest were excluded. The final number of epochs used in the analysis for each participant’s data was 112.3 ± 28.5 (mean ± SD) trials. For the calculation of ERPs, the data of each epoch was low-pass filtered at 50 Hz using a third-order Butterworth filter. The ERP analysis was performed for each electrode individually. Stimulus-locked potentials were calculated by averaging the waveforms of each epoch aligned with the moment of stimulus presentation. Thus, stimulus-locked potentials resulted in 19 waveforms per participant because we recorded data from 19 channels. For each resulting ERP, Morlet wavelet transform was applied using MATLAB’s cwt function, similar to the calculation of behavioral oscillations. This was averaged over time from 0 ms to 500 ms. Subsequently, values in the 4–13 Hz range were averaged, and the proportion of the theta/alpha band was obtained by dividing by the total value below 50 Hz. These values was used as quantitative EEG metrics. We defined this value as “EEG theta-alpha value”. When focusing on the theta band, we defined it as the “EEG theta value,” and likewise, when focusing on the alpha band, we defined it as the “EEG alpha value. Furthermore, the peak frequency was defined as the frequency with the highest power within the 4–13 Hz range.

In addition to the ERP-based analysis, we also conducted a single-trial analysis to examine rhythmicity. In this analysis, the order of procedures was reversed: time–frequency decomposition was first applied to each single trial using the Morlet wavelet transform, and then the resulting power spectra were averaged across trials. All other analytical procedures and the epochs used were identical to those in the ERP analysis. Specifically, only non-successive correct trials with RTs longer than 500 ms were included in the analysis. The proportion of theta and/or alpha band power within the 0–500 ms time window was used as the quantitative measure, calculated by dividing the theta/alpha band power by the total spectral power below 50 Hz.

Statistical analyses

Unless stated otherwise, we applied non-parametric statistics because not all data did not meet the assumption of normal distribution. When comparing data between two conditions, we adopted Wilcoxon’s signrank test. To examine correlation between two variables, we adopted Spearman’s rank correlation test. Before applying two-way ANOVA, we performed aligned rank transform (ART)23, which enables us to apply an ANOVA for non-parametric data. When the interaction was significant, we applied Kruskal-Wallis tests to examine the simple main effects. After that, to conduct statistical comparison among several conditions, we applied Wilcoxon rank sum test by correcting p value with Shaffer’s method24 and to conduct statistical comparison between two conditions, we applied Wilcoxon’s signrank test. When examining whether the phase at the moment a reaction was made was biased towards the 0-degree phase of behavioral oscillations, a V-test was applied using the CircStat toolbox25 in MATLAB.

When comparing theta-alpha value in EEG between the 3-pair and 5-pair conditions, we carried out a paired-samples, cluster-based permutation test implemented in FieldTrip, an open source MATLAB toolbox26. In this analysis, a paired-sample t-test was first performed at each electrode to compute p-values for each channel. Electrodes with p < 0.05 that were spatially adjacent were grouped into clusters. For each cluster, the sum of t-values was computed. The cluster with the maximum cluster-level statistic was selected for constructing the null distribution to conduct nonparametric statistical testing. To construct the null distribution, a permutation test was performed by randomly exchanging condition labels within each participant. This procedure was repeated 5000 times to estimate the distribution of the maximum cluster-level statistic under the null hypothesis. The corrected p-value was obtained by comparing the observed statistic from the real data against this permutation distribution. The test was performed across the 19 electrodes. The spatial adjacency between electrodes was defined based on a triangulation approach that follows the default logic implemented in the ft_prepare_neighbours function of the FieldTrip toolbox.

To examine whether RT distributions were temporally associated with neural ERP activity, cross-correlation analysis was performed. Both RT distributions and ERP signals were derived using the methods described above. RT distributions were analyzed within the 0–1500 ms post-stimulus window, whereas ERPs were based on the 0–500 ms window but zero-padded by 1000 ms on the right side to match the temporal span. To ensure consistent temporal resolution, the RT distributions were resampled to 500 Hz. For each participant, the maximum value of cross-correlations were computed between the RT distributions and ERPs from each of the 19 EEG channels, yielding 19 values per participant. To assess the statistical significance of the observed correlations, participant labels were randomly shuffled such that RT distributions and ERPs from different participants were paired, and the maximum cross-correlations were recomputed 500 times per channel to construct a null distribution. Empirical p-values were then estimated as the proportion of null correlations whose absolute values were equal to or greater than the observed cross-correlation, which was first computed for each participant and then averaged across participants. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons across channels using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

To examine whether recall responses were jointly modulated by neural and behavioral oscillatory phases, we analyzed the phase relationship between single-trial EEG and behavioral signals using a two-dimensional phase histogram approach. For each participant, behavioral oscillatory signals were band-pass filtered between 4 and 13 Hz using a third-order Butterworth filter, and the instantaneous phase was extracted using the Hilbert transform. The behavioral phase at recall was defined as the phase value at the response time of each trial. EEG signals from each electrode were processed in the same frequency band, and the instantaneous phase was extracted on a trial-by-trial basis. For each trial, the EEG phase corresponding to the recall time point was sampled for each electrode. For each electrode, recall events were aggregated across participants and binned into a two-dimensional histogram defined by behavioral phase and EEG phase, using 12 equally spaced bins spanning − π to π for each dimension. This yielded a joint phase distribution representing the number of recall events occurring at each combination of behavioral and neural phases. To test whether recall occurrence depended on the joint phase relationship between behavioral and EEG oscillations, we performed a chi-square test of independence. Under the null hypothesis of independence, recall events are assumed to occur according to the product of the independent phase-dependent probabilities of behavioral and EEG oscillations. Expected cell counts were computed from the corresponding marginal distributions, and chi-square statistics and Cramér’s V were calculated for each electrode. Statistical significance was assessed using a Monte Carlo permutation procedure (5,000 iterations), in which trial-wise correspondences between behavioral and EEG phases were randomly shuffled while preserving their individual phase distributions. Empirical p-values were computed as the proportion of surrogate chi-square values exceeding the observed chi-square statistic. The resulting p-values were corrected for multiple comparisons across channels using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure.

Results

Behavioral results

Overall task performance

In the experiment, participants remembered color/letter pairs and answered the corresponding letter quickly when a colored circle appeared on the screen (Fig. 1a). Basically, task performance was the worst when participants were required to recall the non-successive items among a large amount of information (Fig. 1b, c). The result of 2-way ANOVA for median RT showed significant main effect of condition (the number of retained pairs) (F(3, 112) = 71.82, p < 0.001), main effect of successiveness (whether the trial was successive or non-successive) (F(1, 112) = 79.01, p < 0.001), and the interaction (F(3, 112) = 13.87, p < 0.001). Thereafter, we conducted Kruskal-Wallis tests to examine the simple main effects of condition. In both successive and non-successive trials, the results showed significant simple main effects of condition (successive: p < 0.001; non-successive: p < 0.001). As to successive trials, Wilcoxon rank sum tests (Shaffer-corrected) showed that RT in the 2-pair condition was shorter than in the 3, 4, 5-pair conditions (p < 0.001 in 2-pair and 4-pair, and 2-pair and 5-pair; p < 0.01 in 2-pair and 3-pair comparisons), and RT in the 3-pair condition was shorter than in the 5-pair condition (p < 0.01). The other comparison showed no significant differences (p > 0.05). As to non-successive trials, the same tests showed that all comparison was significant and RT in larger-pair conditions was longer than that in smaller-pair conditions (p < 0.05 in 4-pair and 5-pair; p < 0.001 in all other comparisons). Also, we performed four Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to examine the simple main effects of successiveness of trials on RTs. The results showed no significant difference in the 2-pair condition (p > 0.05), but RTs in non-successive trials were longer than those in successive trials in the 3, 4, and 5-pair conditions (p = 0.0015 in the 3-pair condition; p < 0.001 in the 4 and 5-pair conditions). The result of 2-way ANOVA for HR showed a significant main effect of condition (the number of retained pairs) (F(3, 112) = 4.40, p = 0.0057), the main effect of successiveness (whether the trial was successive or non-successive) (F(1, 112) = 26.62, p < 0.001), and the interaction (F(3, 112) = 6.55, p < 0.001). Thereafter, we conducted Kruskal-Wallis tests to examine the simple main effects of the condition. In successive trials, the simple main effects of the condition were not significant (p > 0.05), whereas in non-successive trials, the simple main effects of the condition were significant (p = 0.0024). As to non-successive trials, Wilcoxon rank sum tests showed that HR in the 2-pair condition was higher than in the 4-pair condition (p = 0.0083) and the 5-pair condition (p = 0.0027), but no other comparison showed significant differences (p > 0.05). We also performed four Wilcoxon signed-rank tests to examine the simple main effects of the successiveness of trials on HR. The results showed no significant difference in the 2-pair and 3-pair condition (p > 0.05), but HR in non-successive trials was lower than those in successive trials in the 4 and 5-pair conditions (p = 0.0011 in the 4-pair condition; p < 0.001 in the 5-pair conditions). These results demonstrate that memory retrieval of non-recently encoded items among a large amount of information was the most difficult.

Memory items were retrieved rhythmically in the 5-pair condition

Next, we visualized individual RT distributions and found behavioral rhythmicity in the distributions in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition. Four typical RT distributions for each memory load condition are shown in Fig. 2a. We found clear periodicity in the RT distributions, especially for RT to non-successive stimuli in the 5-pair condition. To examine the oscillatory characteristics of RT distributions, we conducted a time-frequency decomposition and compared them with non-oscillatory surrogate RT distributions (500 iterations). The obtained mean and standard deviation were used to calculate the z-score of the oscillatory value. Figure 2b and c show a clear peak around the theta-alpha band in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition. The z-score of theta-alpha value was significantly larger than zero in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition (Fig. 2d; one-tail Wilcoxon’s signrank test, p = 0.0034, respectively), whereas no other trials/conditions showed significance (p > 0.05). To confirm that the main findings were not dependent on the specific choice of kernel width, we repeated the analysis using a shorter 10-ms window. The results were highly consistent with those obtained using the 50-ms kernel (see Fig. S1). These results suggest that memory retrieval becomes rhythmical when the number of retained items exceeds four. Examining the relationship between the rhythmicity and memory performance

We examined the inter-individual relationship between the theta-alpha rhythmicity and the task performance in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition, where significant theta-alpha rhythmicity was observed. Interestingly, the theta-alpha values were not correlated with either the HR (rho = 0.039, p = 0.89) or the average RT (rho = − 0.33, p = 0.23). Also, we examined the correlation between the peak frequency of the behavioral rhythmicity and the task performance, resulting in no significant correlation (HR: rho = 0.29, p = 0.30; RT: rho = − 0.067, p = 0.81). These results suggest that subjective difficulty of memory recall and prolonged RTs themselves are not the determining factors of rhythmicity in memory recall in inter-individual level. In addition, though peak frequency showed individual differences in the theta-alpha band, it would not be related to the memory performance.

Later trials were more phase-locked to the behavioral rhythmicity

Does this rhythm appear when the number of items exceeds 4 or when recall is difficult due to insufficient learning? In a typical RT distribution and RTs from early/late 30 trials, RTs from late 30 trials seem to be more strongly clustered around the peaks of RT distribution than early 30 trials (Fig. 3a). When averaged RTs were calculated for each 30-trial bin, RTs showed a decreasing trend as trials progressed, indicating a learning process of participants (Fig. 3b). In contrast, V-value, which computes how well reactions were biased toward phase zero (peak phase) of behavioral rhythmicity, showed an increasing trend as trials progressed, indicating reactions were more locked to the behavioral rhythmicity as participants learned the memory pairs. To statistically examine the trend, we compared the averaged RT and the rhythmicity between the first and the last 30 trials. Averaged RT was significantly shorter in the last 30 trials for non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition (Fig. 3b, Wilcoxon’s signrank test, p = 0.0017), showing that participants could retrieve memory items easily in the last 30 trials compared with the first 30 trials. Importantly, reactions in the last 30 trials showed significantly larger V-values than the first 30 trials (Fig. 3a, c, Wilcoxon’s signrank test p = 0.0014). This indicates that reactions in the late trials showed a stronger tendency for rhythmic recall than in earlier trials. Both V-values for the first (Wilcoxon’s signrank test p = 0.0031) and the last (Wilcoxon’s signrank test p < 0.001) 30 trials were significantly larger than 0. When aggregating the phases of 30 trials across the 17 individuals, resulting in 510 reactions each for the first and the last trials, both the phases of the first (V = 45.67, p = 0.0021) and the last (V = 128.24, p < 0.001) 30 trials were significantly biased toward 0 degrees (Fig. 3f).

In these analyses, we examined how greatly the first and last 30 trials contributed to the rhythmicity of the RT distribution. For this purpose, the extracted 60 trials were also included in creating the RT distribution. However, this approach may overestimate the degree to which the extracted trials are locked to the phase of the RT distribution’s rhythm due to the issue of circularity. To address this concern, we also conducted analyses by excluding the first and last 30 extracted trials from creating the RT distribution. As a result, regarding Fig. 3e, while the V-value for the last 30 trials was again significantly larger than 0 (Wilcoxon’s signrank test p = 0.0040), the V-value for the first 30 trials was not (Wilcoxon’s signrank test p = 0.90). The difference in the V-values between the first and last 30 trials was not significant (Wilcoxon’s signrank test p = 0.058). In aggregated phase analysis, regarding Fig. 3f, while the V-value for the last 30 trials was again significantly larger than 0 (V = 42.54, p = 0.0017), the V-value for the first 30 trials was not (Fig. 3f, V = -1.52, p = 0.54). These results suggest that this rhythmicity does not emerge when memory recall is difficult due to insufficient learning but does emerge when retrieving a memory from 5 or more items that are well consolidated.

Phase inconsistency of behavioral rhythmicity in incorrect trials

Next, we examined whether incorrect responses within 1,500 ms were phase-locked or not to the peak of oscillations of correct trials in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition. By avoiding problems of circularity (see Materials and Methods), we analyzed whether correct and incorrect trials were biased towards the peak phase of the recall rhythm. As a result (Fig. 4a), V-value (non-uniformity around 0°) was significantly larger than 0 in correct trials (Wilcoxon’s signrank test, p = 0.017), but not in incorrect trials (p = 0.45). As an aggregated phase analysis (Fig. 4b, c), again, RT tended to be biased toward 0 degree (peak phase) in correct trials (V = 26.28, p = 0.012), but not in incorrect trials (V = 14.50, p = 0.11). These results suggest that the phase-locking of responses to behavioral oscillations was not consistent across participants. However, the results should be carefully interpreted due to relatively small samples, so the conclusion must be regarded as provisional and replication with larger cohorts will be needed.

EEG results

Replication of behavioral rhythmicity

In Experiment 2, 25 participants carried out the 3 and 5-pair conditions while recording neural activity with EEG. First, we replicated the behavioral rhythmicity obtained in Experiment 1 (Fig. 5). Again, we found theta-alpha value of the behavioral rhythmicity in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition was significantly larger than 0 (Fig. 5a, b, one-tail Wilcoxon’s signrank test, p = 0.0062), but not of successive trials and/or the 3-pair condition (p > 0.05). The theta-alpha values were not correlated with either the HR (rho = 0.055, p = 0.79) or the average RT (rho = -0.34, p = 0.095) at inter-individual level. Peak frequency did not correlate with either the HR (rho = 0.14, p = 0.50) or the average RT (rho = 0.13, p = 0.55) across individuals. These results are consistent with the behavioral results of Experiment 1, supporting the existence of behavioral theta-alpha rhythmicity in non-successive trials of the 5-pair condition.

Stimulus-locked potentials of EEG

The behavioral rhythmicity observed in the RT distribution implies the presence of phase-locked theta–alpha oscillations triggered at the moment of stimulus onset. Such cue-locked neural synchronization would be reflected as transient, stimulus-locked activity in the EEG. Therefore, to test this possibility, we calculated the theta–alpha value in stimulus-locked potentials and compared them between the 3-pair and 5-pair conditions in non-successive trials (Fig. 6). Successive and incorrect trials were not analyzed due to small number of trials for EEG analyses. In a typical individual, the EEG trace showed more visible theta-alpha rhythmicity in the 5-pair condition compared with the 3-pair condition around a parietal electrode (Fig. 6a, b). The difference in theta-alpha power between the 3-pair and the 5-pair conditions is shown in Fig. 6c. A cluster-based permutation test identified a significant left‐parietal cluster, within which theta–alpha power was significantly greater in the 5-pair than the 3-pair condition (p = 0.021). Although an uncorrected cluster was found at the right‐parietal electrode (P8), it did not survive correction in the permutation test (p = 0.11). The time–frequency representation for the left‐parietal cluster (Fig. 6d) exhibited a pronounced post-stimulus increase in theta–alpha power, with distinct peaks observed in both the theta and alpha bands. Together, these findings demonstrate that, at the level of scalp‐recorded EEG, rhythmic activity in the theta–alpha range is selectively “switched on” under the 5-pair condition.

Although both scalp EEG and RT distributions exhibited clear periodicities, our results indicate that they do not simply mirror one another (Fig. 7). First, we tested whether participants with stronger behavioral oscillations also showed greater EEG power in the theta–alpha range; no significant correlation was found (Fig. 7a; rho = 0.16, p = 0.45). Likewise, there was no reliable relationship between the peak frequencies of behavioral and neural oscillations (Fig. 7b; rho = -0.32, p = 0.11). Next, we asked whether EEG rhythmicity, like behavioral periodicity (Fig. 4), became more pronounced in the second half of the task. Contrary to this expectation, EEG power did not differ significantly between the first and second halves of the experiment (Fig. 7c; p > 0.05), indicating distinct temporal profiles between behavioral and EEG rhythmicity.

To examine whether the behavioral oscillations observed in the RT distributions shared common temporal characteristics with ERPs within individuals, we conducted a cross-correlation analysis between the two signals. A permutation test was then performed by shuffling participant labels to evaluate the statistical significance of the observed correlations. A representative waveform of cross-correlation from a single participant is shown in Fig. 7d for visualization purpose. The cross-correlation waveform between the RT distribution and EEG signal from this representative participant fell well within the 95% confidence interval obtained from the permutation test, which was computed by cross-correlating RT distributions from one participant with EEG signals from different participants. This indicates that the EEG responses of an individual were not directly reflected in the corresponding individual’s RT distribution. Also in a group data, none of the 19 channels showed a significant cross-correlation (all FDR-corrected p > 0.05 ).

To further examine the relationship between neural and behavioral oscillations, we visualized where recall responses occurred within the single-trial phase space of EEG and behavioral oscillations using a two-dimensional histogram (Fig. 7e). This analysis allowed us to assess whether recall events preferentially emerged at specific combinations of neural and behavioral phases. If neural and behavioral oscillations are truly independent, as suggested by the preceding analyses, the joint probability of recall occurrence across their phases should be well explained by the product of the marginal phase-dependent probabilities of each signal alone. In contrast, any systematic coupling between the two oscillations would lead to deviations from this independence assumption. To test this, we applied a chi-square test of independence to the joint phase distributions. Consistent with the independence hypothesis, the chi-square tests did not reveal significant associations at any electrode (Fig. 7f; all FDR-corrected p > 0.05), indicating that recall responses were not jointly modulated by the phases of neural and behavioral oscillations.

These results indicate that behavioral and EEG oscillations reflect different aspects of rhythmicity in memory retrieval. The theta-alpha power in the EEG data was consistent from the early to late stages of learning, suggesting it may primarily reflect changes in retrieval strategy related to the number of memory pairs. Behavioral oscillations, in contrast, became more pronounced during the later stages of learning. This may suggest that they reflect the influence of memory consolidation in subcortical regions, such as the hippocampus which is difficult to record with scalp EEG.

Single-trial EEG analyses

To facilitate comparison with previous studies and address several potential concerns, we additionally performed single-trial EEG analyses instead of relying solely on ERP-based measures. For each trial, we conducted frequency analyses within the post-cue period and then averaged the resulting power spectra across trials. The between-condition difference in the ratio of theta–alpha power to the total power (1–50 Hz) was most pronounced over lateral parietal electrodes (Fig. 8); however, cluster-based permutation tests revealed no significant differences between conditions.

Single-trial analyses of theta-alpha oscillations of 3-pair (left), 5-pair (center), and the difference between them (right), which can be compared with Fig. 3. No significant cluster was found.

To further explore whether the strong rhythmicity observed in the 5-pair condition could alternatively be attributed to increased cognitive demand, we examined frontal-midline theta (FMT) activity across conditions. FMT is a theta oscillation originating primarily from the anterior cingulate and medial prefrontal cortices27, known to emerge robustly during high-load working memory28–30 or attentional control31, conflict resolution32,33, and mental fatigue34. Accordingly, we visualized the scalp distribution of theta oscillation at the single-trial level for each condition (Fig. S2). Interestingly, a cluster-based permutation test identified a fronto-central midline cluster, within which the theta value was significantly greater in the 3-pair than in the 5-pair condition (p = 0.0024). This pattern suggests that participants may have engaged their working memory and attentional control systems more actively in the 3-pair condition, and that the rhythmic activity in the 5-pair condition does not necessarily reflect increased cognitive resource allocation, conflict resolution, or mental fatigue.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated whether the neural mechanisms of memory retrieval are context-dependent. The novel findings are as follows: (1) In the 5-pair condition, the RT distribution showed distinct peaks with cycles corresponding to theta-alpha rhythmicity (Fig. 2); (2) In the 2, 3, or 4-pair conditions, where recall performance was higher, the RT distribution showed no such significant rhythmicity; (3) Even in the 5-pair condition, recall was not phase-locked to the behavioral rhythmicity in earlier trials where memory was not well consolidated. The phase-locking became stronger as the trials progressed and memory items were consolidated (Fig. 3); (4) In incorrect trials, recall was not significantly phase-locked to the behavioral rhythmicity (Fig. 4); (5) EEG analyses showed stronger theta-alpha stimulus-locked oscillations in the 5-pair than in the 3-pair condition, mostly around parietal electrodes (Fig. 6); and (6) The characteristics of the behavioral oscillations and EEG rhythms did not correlate; furthermore, unlike the behavioral data, the power of the EEG rhythm showed no change between the first and second halves of the task. Our results demonstrate that our nervous system “switches on” rhythmicity to retrieve memories depending on specific contexts.

Multiple peaks observed in distributions of RT to recall memory items suggest the involvement of neural oscillations in memory retrieval. Hundreds of memory recall per participant in the 5-pair condition showed that memory recall tended to occur at specific/discrete timings after memory cues, with the cycle corresponding to the theta-alpha range. As shown in Fig. 1, the discrete peaks in the RT distributions imply that the memory cue triggered the phase reset of neural oscillation35 and the phase/frequency where the phase resetting occurred was almost the same across trials. Based on this assumption that the phase of neural oscillation is the same across trials, we calculated stimulus-locked potentials of EEG signals across trials (Fig. 6). As a result, such phase-locking was mainly observed around left parietal electrodes in the 5-pair condition, supporting the existence of phase-resetting of neural oscillation triggered by the memory cue when the number of candidate items is 5. In recent years, a growing body of research has investigated the nature of behavioral rhythmicity and gained implications for neural mechanisms of attention36–39, visual perception40, working memory41–45, and long-term memory8. Our results are the first to add to this body of research by demonstrating that behavioral rhythms to retrieve memories switch to appear when the number of memorized items is beyond 4.

We found an invisible boundary between the numbers 4 and 5, which affects memory retrieval. As the term “the magical number 4” indicates, our cognitive ability has long been known to be severely limited by the boundary of the number 411,46. The number of items we can actively hold in short-term memory at the same time46,47 and the number of items we can pay attention to at the same time are only about 448. While 4 or fewer items can be instantly and accurately recognized, the identification of items above 4 has been shown to rely on vague estimation49,50. It is suggested that our nervous system possesses specific neurons reacting to numbers 1, 2, 3, or 4, while there are no such neurons for numbers beyond 450. The current results showed a novel observation that the non-short-term memory retrieval process is also affected by the cognitive boundary of 4. When the number of candidate items is within 4, retrieving them is easy and smooth, as reflected in the short RT, high HR, and no rhythmicity. Notably, when the number of candidate items exceeds 4, where easy memory retrieval would be limited, our nervous system apparently employs an alternative strategy, recruiting behavioral/neural rhythmicity. What is surprising about current results is that the non-short-term memory process was affected by the boundary, which has not been reported. Combined with previous reports, our result indicated that our nervous systems have a specialized neural infrastructure for processing items within 4. An interesting future direction is to examine the relationship between individual cognitive capacity and the emergence of rhythmicity. Cognitive capacity is not perfectly fixed across individuals; therefore, those with a larger capacity than four may not show rhythmicity even when the number of retained items exceeds four.

Our results suggest that our nervous system recruits theta-alpha rhythmicity when candidate memory items overflow the working memory capacity of 4 and should be retrieved from long-term memory. Working memory is the short-term memory system that actively maintains and utilizes items in mind51. When the number of items is within 4, which is known to be the capacity of working memory, all candidate items can be actively retained in the working memory storage throughout the memory task46. On the contrary, when the number of candidate items exceeds 4, they overflow from the working memory capacity and should be stored in the long-term memory system to deal with memory retention and retrieval. The clear behavioral/neural rhythmicity in the 5-pair condition, where memory items overflowed from working memory capacity, suggests the involvement of theta-alpha rhythmicity in long-term memory retrieval. Supporting this view, the theta-alpha behavioral rhythmicity progressively got stronger in the latter trials, where memory items were well consolidated in long-term memory, while the rhythmicity was not observed in earlier trials (Fig. 3). This indicates that our nervous system possesses such a very flexible switching mechanism to deal with our cognitive capacity.

A recent study showed similar theta rhythmicity in a long-term associative memory task8. In their experiment, participants encoded more than 100 cue-target memory pairs, and after a distractor task (judging whether numbers displayed on a screen are even or odd), they retrieved the encoded items on seeing a memory cue. In such a task, which depended clearly on the long-term memory system, RT distributions showed behavioral rhythmicity similar to our observation. Based on this result, the behavioral rhythmicity observed in our tasks would reflect the recruitment of the long-term memory system. Notably, there are two additional findings from our study compared with the previous one. First, our study found that the appearance of behavioral rhythmicity to retrieve memories is context-dependent: they appeared when the number of retained items was beyond 4 and when the items were well consolidated. Second, the frequency range was different between our results (4–13 Hz) and the previous study (1–5 Hz). This difference in frequencies may be related to the difficulty of target recall. For instance, a prior study included a filler task between the learning and recall phases, which made retrieval more challenging than in our present work and could, in turn, have induced a slower frequency.

Although it is challenging to precisely identify the neural sources from scalp EEG, the topography of the theta-alpha oscillations also supports the involvement of the long-term memory system in the 5-pair condition. The difference in theta-alpha oscillation between the 5-pair and 3-pair conditions was the strongest around the left parietal electrodes (Fig. 6). These regions would include the posterior parietal cortices such as the angular gyrus, which is activated during episodic memory retrieval52–54 and has connectivity with the hippocampus55,56. A possible function of the theta-alpha neural oscillation would be to synchronize neural activity across brain regions57,58 related to episodic memory retrieval: the posterior parietal cortex, hippocampus, and other areas, which are difficult to capture with surface EEG recordings. With human intracranial recordings, Watrous et al. reported synchronization of the phase of the slow oscillations (1–10 Hz) among the above-mentioned regions during long-term memory retrieval tasks55. Therefore, the theta-alpha oscillation around parietal electrodes would indicate the synchronization of brain regions related to the long-term memory retrieval system, including the posterior parietal cortex and hippocampus.

The absence of theta–alpha rhythmicity under the within-four-pair condition may appear inconsistent with previous reports showing the presence of theta–alpha rhythms during working memory maintenance, even with a small number of items30. However, a crucial distinction lies in the task phase: the present theta rhythmicity was observed during the “retrieval” phase, whereas prior studies have primarily examined theta–alpha activity during “maintenance”. Theta–alpha oscillations during working memory maintenance have been observed at both the EEG28,30,59 and behavioral levels42,43,45, and thought to support the active maintenance of multiple items60,61. In contrast, the present study focused on neural and behavioral oscillations following the presentation of the retrieval cue, and a prominent theta–alpha rhythm was detected specifically in the 5-pair condition. A second important difference from previous research concerns the analytical approach. Whereas earlier studies have predominantly targeted “induced” (i.e., non–phase-locked) theta–alpha activity elicited by each stimulus, the current study employed ERP analyses time-locked to the retrieval cue to visualize oscillations that were “evoked” or phase-locked by the cue. Consistent with this methodological distinction, induced frontal theta oscillations were also observed here and were actually stronger in the 3-pair than in the 5-pair condition (Fig. S2). Finally, the paradigm used in the present study was not a working-memory task but rather a learning task in which the same memory items appeared repeatedly. Such differences in task structure and cognitive demands further contribute to the apparent discrepancies between the current findings and those of earlier studies.

Nevertheless, the theta rhythm observed in the present study—presumably related to long-term memory retrieval—can be discussed within a unified framework together with theta rhythms previously reported in the contexts of working memory and attention61,62. In short, both working memory maintenance and long-term memory retrieval would rely on theta rhythms to temporally segregate information through phase- and cycle-dependent activation, which serves to minimize interference. Theta oscillations observed during working memory tasks have frequently been linked to the rhythmic activation of information63–65. According to this line of research, items maintained in working memory are activated at distinct phases or cycles of the theta rhythm. Such temporal segregation is thought to prevent interference between different items or processes while simultaneously protecting all items from decay. This kind of theta-rhythmic activation/readout of working memory has gained empirical support based on neural65,66 and behavioral42,45 oscillatory studies. Importantly, the functional relevance of theta rhythms has also been demonstrated in long-term memory retrieval where the target memory item is assumed to be inactive prior to retrieval. Prior work has shown that, when retrieving previously encoded memories in the presence of competing memory representations, target memories and competing memories are reactivated at different phases of hippocampal theta oscillations and interference between them are resolved67. Taken together, these lines of evidence suggest that theta rhythms serve a functional role in segregating and activating information while preventing interference, both during short-term memory maintenance and long-term memory retrieval. Viewed within these frameworks, the theta (and possibly alpha) rhythmic activity observed in the present study may reflect a state in which long-term memory representations that had once settled into a non-active state were transiently reactivated into a rhythmic and active state (potentially within a working memory storage as theorized by Atkinson and Shiffrin68) without interference from other items. An important direction for future research is to determine whether the rhythms observed here are homologous to, or distinct from, those previously reported in studies of working memory and attention.

Interestingly, the single-trial analysis of frontal-midline theta (FMT), a well-established marker of cognitive load69 and mental fatigue34, revealed weaker theta power in the 5-pair condition than in the 3-pair condition. This pattern emerged despite the fact that the 5-pair condition required participants to handle a larger amount of information. The finding therefore suggests that participants did not rely on prefrontal resources typically associated with working memory28–30, attentional control31, or conflict resolution32,33 during the more demanding 5-pair task, and that the increased load did not induce measurable mental fatigue. Taken together, these results raise the possibility that, in the 5-pair condition, participants may have shifted away from prefrontal, resource-intensive control processes and instead allocated information to alternative systems. One plausible candidate is the hippocampal–long-term memory network, which may have supported memory organization and retrieval when the prefrontal system was not engaged. It should be noted that, as shown in a previous study67, interference resolution among multiple items can also occur within the hippocampus and other long-term memory systems, suggesting that the theta–alpha rhythmicity observed in the present study might still reflect such processes.

The behavioral oscillations observed in this study likely reflect rhythmicity within the whole processing stream, encompassing visual perception70, memory recall centered on subcortical regions like the hippocampus4,5,7, and motor output from motor-related areas71. In contrast, scalp EEG is primarily sensitive to neural oscillations originating from the cortical surface, making it difficult to visualize activity from deeper, subcortical structures. This methodological difference suggests that behavioral and EEG oscillations capture distinct aspects of neural processing. Our findings support this view, revealing a clear dissociation between behavioral oscillations and scalp-level theta-alpha rhythms. The power and frequency of these two sets of oscillations were uncorrelated; while behavioral oscillations strengthened as memory consolidation progressed during the later stages of the task, EEG oscillations did not exhibit this change; furthermore, cross-correlation analyses revealed no significant temporal coupling between behavioral and neural oscillations at any electrode, and phase-based independence tests using two-dimensional histograms and chi-square statistics likewise failed to detect a reliable association between behavioral and EEG phases (Fig. 7). The enhancement of behavioral oscillations with memory consolidation suggests their association with the long-term memory system, including the hippocampus. As scalp EEG did not capture this feature, it likely reflects cortical activity related to either visual processing or motor output. In sum, behavioral and scalp EEG oscillations appear to reflect different facets of rhythmic neural activity. Therefore, they offer complementary insights, and their combined use can contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying neural mechanisms72.

In the present study, we discovered that when the number of items exceeded the typical working memory capacity of four, their retrieval exhibited rhythmic fluctuations. This rhythmicity may represent a neural dynamic that facilitates the cooperative engagement of the long-term memory system, a process required when the capacity of short-term memory is exceeded. To identify the precise neural sources and functional significance of this phenomenon, future studies should employ methods capable of directly measuring deep-brain activity, as well as longitudinal paradigms that can assess long-term memory consolidation. Moreover, it will be important to investigate how this rhythmicity changes across more diverse learning contexts—such as when more than six items are learned simultaneously, when learning extends across multiple days, or when interference73 or multitasking74 conditions are introduced. As a related finding, ter Wal et al.8 observed prominent rhythmicity during a task in which participants learned tens of associative memories followed by a delayed recall after a filler task. Notably, the frequency observed in their study was substantially slower than that found here. These observations together suggest that the rhythmicity of memory retrieval may vary flexibly depending on learning context. Elucidating such contextual variability represents an important direction for future research into the neural mechanisms of memory and learning. Finally, these findings raise the possibility that simple behavioral measures such as reaction time could provide a new window into the underlying neural states of memory—offering potential insight into whether a memory trace is consolidated, or what strategies an individual employs during retrieval.
