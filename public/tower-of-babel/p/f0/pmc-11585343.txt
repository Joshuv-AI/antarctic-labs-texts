The American Cancer Society National Lung Cancer Roundtable strategic plan: Promoting guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging
Authors: Farhood Farjah, Julie A Barta, Douglas E Wood, M Patricia Rivera, Raymond U Osarogiagbon, Robert A Smith, Timothy W Mullett, Lauren S Rosenthal, Louise M Henderson, Frank C Detterbeck, Gerard A Silvestri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate staging improves lung cancer survival by increasing the chances of delivering stage‐appropriate therapy. However, there is underutilization of, and variability in, the use of guideline‐recommended diagnostic tests used to stage lung cancer. Consequently, the American Cancer Society National Lung Cancer Roundtable (ACS NLCRT) convened the Triage for Appropriate Treatment Task Group—a multidisciplinary expert and stakeholder panel—to identify knowledge and/or resource gaps contributing to guideline‐discordant staging and make recommendations to overcome these gaps. The task group determined the following: Gap 1: facilitators of and barriers to guideline‐concordant staging are incompletely understood; Recommendation 1: identify facilitators of and barriers to guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging; Gap 2: the level of evidence supporting staging algorithms is low‐to‐moderate; Recommendation 2: prioritize comparative‐effectiveness studies evaluating lung cancer staging; Gap 3: guideline recommendations vary across professional societies; Recommendation 3: harmonize guideline recommendations across professional societies; Gap 4: existing databases do not contain sufficient information to measure guideline‐concordant staging; Recommendation 4: augment existing databases with the information required to measure guideline‐concordant staging; Gap 5: health systems do not have a performance feedback mechanism for lung cancer staging; Recommendation 5: develop and implement a performance feedback mechanism for lung cancer staging; Gap 6: patients rarely self‐advocate for guideline‐concordant staging; Recommendation 6: increase opportunities for patient self‐advocacy for guideline‐concordant staging; and Gap 7: current health policies do not motivate guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging; Recommendation 7: organize a representative working group under the ACS NLCRT that promotes policies that motivate guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging.Plain language summaryStaging—determining the degree of cancer spread—is important because it helps clinicians choose the best cancer treatment. Receiving the best cancer treatment leads to the best possible patient outcomes. Practice guidelines are intended to help clinicians stage patients with lung cancer. However, lung cancer staging in the United States often varies from practice guideline recommendations. This report identifies seven opportunities to improve lung cancer staging.Keywords: lung neoplasms, mediastinal staging, neoplasm staging, non–small cell lung cancer, quality improvement

BACKGROUND

We are turning the tide in the battle against non–small cell lung cancer. The 1990s kicked off a decline in lung cancer incidence and deaths as a result of tobacco control efforts initiated in the 1960s that are projected to extend to 2065. 1 , 2 , 3 More recently, the evidence supporting screening high‐risk individuals has ushered in a new era of early detection and treatment of lung cancer, which is anticipated to further reduce lung cancer mortality. 4 , 5 Finally, rapidly emerging therapeutics are associated with longer survival. 6

To fully reap the benefits of these recent achievements, patients with lung cancer must be accurately staged. Staging is a clinical algorithm that uses noninvasive and invasive diagnostic tests—such as computed tomography (CT), positron emission tomography (PET), mediastinoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound‐guided lymph node aspiration (EBUS), brain magnetic resonance imaging, and/or percutaneous biopsies of lymph nodes or nonthoracic organs (e.g., bone, liver, adrenal gland)—to characterize the extent of disease (i.e., stage).

Lung cancer treatment varies by stage—local therapy only for stage I (e.g., surgery or radiation), multimodality therapy for stages II (surgery and adjuvant systemic therapy) and III (chemoradiation therapy and immunotherapy or induction therapy and surgery), and systemic therapy (including immunotherapy and targeted therapies) for stage IV. Inaccurate staging may lead to inappropriate treatment selection (Table 1). Inappropriate treatment selection can lead to poor patient outcomes (Figure 1).

Conceptual framework linking staging to patient outcomes.

Practice guidelines from the American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP) and the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) help clinicians navigate lung cancer staging. 7 , 8 However, actual staging often deviates from recommended staging. For example, practice guidelines recommend brain imaging and PET scans for all patients with locally advanced disease, but only 75% and 60% of patients, respectively, receive these tests. 9 Approximately 75% of patients with lung cancer would be expected to undergo invasive pretreatment lymph node staging, 10 , 11 yet actual rates are 20%–33%. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Practice guidelines recommend routine intraoperative lymph node evaluation for surgically treated patients, yet 18% do not undergo intraoperative lymph node evaluation. 17 Even staging protocols for lung cancer treatment trials deviate from guideline recommendations, with up to 34% of patients being understaged. 18

Of particular concern, guideline‐discordant staging likely contributes to disparities in lung cancer treatment and outcomes. 19 There are unexplained racial differences in the utilization of PET scans and invasive staging. 20 , 21 There is no biological basis for staging patients differently based on their age, race, ethnicity, sexual orientation, or gender identity. We believe social determinants of health (i.e., social class, systemic racism, and implicit biases among clinicians) likely explain variation across demographic subgroups. Additionally, places and providers of care for the demographically disadvantaged are typically resource‐challenged. 22

Collectively, the evidence demonstrates significant gaps in lung cancer staging and motivates urgent, systematic action. There are legitimate reasons (e.g., comorbid conditions, treatment preferences) to pursue guideline‐discordant staging for some patients. However, the frequency of deviations exceeds clinical expectations. Because staging links to patient outcomes, health equity, and high value care, multiple stakeholders—including patients, clinicians, health systems, insurers, employers, policymakers, and funding agencies—are vested in promoting guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging. Our objective was to catalyze a dialogue about how best to improve lung cancer staging among diverse lung cancer stakeholders.

METHODS

The American Cancer Society National Lung Cancer Roundtable (ACS NLCRT) is a coalition of public, private, and voluntary organizations and invited individuals whose mission is to reduce the incidence of lung cancer and promote lung cancer survivorship through awareness and prevention, early detection and assurance of optimal therapy. 23 Lung cancer staging falls under the purview of the Triage for Appropriate Treatment Task Group. This task group sought to identify knowledge and resource gaps that contribute to guideline‐discordant lung cancer staging and make recommendations to overcome these gaps.

These goals were achieved through a multi‐step process. First, we reviewed key literature. Next, we drafted and distributed a manuscript outline to the task group and the ACS NLCRT Steering Committee. A revised outline was reviewed and discussed with 25 task group members and revised further, after which a writing group was established. Relevant new literature was included during the period of manuscript development. The stakeholders on the writing committee reached a uniform consensus on gaps and recommendations.

RESULTS

Gap 1: facilitators of and barriers to guideline‐concordant staging are incompletely understood

Organizations that produce practice guidelines strive to develop and promote evidence‐based recommendations but infrequently pursue implementation strategies. Understanding facilitators of, and barriers to, guideline‐concordant staging is the first step toward better implementation. 24 , 25 For instance, the use of advanced diagnostic tests requires access to facilities, equipment, and skilled diagnosticians. 26 To some extent, practice guidelines recognize these needs and provide alternative staging recommendations (e.g., CT and a bone scan when a PET scan is not accessible) or recommend referral to specialized centers. 7 , 8 Yet, approximately 60% of physicians report experiencing barriers to making specialty referrals. 19 , 27 Given the heterogeneous care delivery in the United States, we have a limited understanding of facilitators of, and barriers to, guideline‐concordant staging.

Recommendation 1: identify facilitators of, and barriers to, guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging

Researchers should identify factors underlying guideline‐discordant staging. The ACS NLCRT recently surveyed pulmonologists and thoracic surgeons to better understand knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, and perceptions of facilitators and barriers to invasive mediastinal staging. 28 Key findings were: (1) 29% of respondents were unaware of nodal staging guidelines, (2) 50% reported that there was a lack of evidence linking guideline recommendations to better patient outcomes, and (3) 90% of clinicians experienced at least one barrier to implementing guideline‐concordant staging in their practice. This and other studies can lead to interventions designed to promote guideline implementation. For instance, the ACS NLCRT could collaborate with professional societies to design education interventions, such as adoption of a thoracic oncology curriculum and diploma regardless of clinician specialty, 29 development of standardized curricula for invasive staging procedures for pulmonary medicine and thoracic surgery trainees, 30 and development of continuing medical education activities that specifically address lung cancer staging. 31 Education interventions along with others gleaned from this type of research would likely lead to multi‐level interventions designed to increase guideline‐concordant staging.

Gap 2: the level of evidence supporting staging algorithms is low‐to‐moderate

Practice guidelines from ACCP and NCCN acknowledge that the levels of evidence supporting their recommendations are low‐to‐moderate. 7 , 8 Much of the scientific literature examines individual tests' performance characteristics (e.g., sensitivity/specificity) rather than the relationship between staging algorithms and outcomes. Lung cancer staging trials have only examined the effect of individual diagnostic tests on outcomes. 32 , 33 Another important limitation of trials is the use of controversial intermediate outcomes (e.g., “nontherapeutic thoracotomy”) rather than widely accepted oncologic and patient‐centered outcomes (e.g., survival, mortality). 34 Several recent observational studies report associations between guideline‐concordant nodal staging and fewer overall staging tests, procedure‐related complications, and higher survival rates. 15 , 16 However, these investigations used existing databases with limitations (Gap 4) that raise the possibility of bias. 35 Low‐to‐moderate levels of evidence are a known barrier to guideline‐concordant lung cancer treatment 25 and may also be a barrier to guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging. There is an urgent need to strengthen the supporting evidence to provide greater confidence in the recommendations.

Recommendation 2: prioritize comparative‐effectiveness studies evaluating lung cancer staging

Comparative‐effectiveness evaluations of lung cancer staging that leverage existing data sources will likely generate a higher level of evidence relatively quickly and inexpensively. Modifications to existing data sources can provide investigators with the granularity needed to measure guideline‐concordant staging, confounding variables, and longitudinal outcomes of interest (Recommendation 4). Statistical advancements leveraging machine learning enhance the ability to minimize confounding variables when using observational data. 36 Additionally, data from observational studies can be used as empirically derived inputs for models that simulate clinical trials. 37 Although randomized trials are the gold standard for determining efficacy; they require substantial resources. Observational studies are a reasonable first step in comparing lung cancer staging strategies. Funding agencies play a critical role in supporting comparative‐effectiveness research and should prioritize studies that evaluate lung cancer staging.

Gap 3: guideline recommendations vary across professional societies

Although more alike than not, there are important differences in lung cancer staging guidelines across professional societies (Table 2). These differences may result from divergent methodologies to evaluate and synthesize evidence, varying frequencies for updates, and conflicts of interest. One potential consequence of having different guideline recommendations is practice variation. For example, there is a 25‐fold center‐level variation in invasive mediastinal staging not explained by case‐mix, access to specialists and facilities, or chance. 13 Variation in guideline recommendations may partly explain variation in lung cancer staging across institutions.

Recommendation 3: harmonize guideline recommendations across professional societies

The purpose of a practice guideline is to provide trustworthy, expert guidance based on the best available evidence. Uniform guideline recommendations across professional societies may reduce variability and promote guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging. Aligning methodologies for evidence review and synthesis, synchronizing update schedules, and providing greater transparency about conflicts of interest represent the ideal. Professional societies should amend their guideline development methodology to allow for reconciliation of differences, for example by defining mutually agreeable and transparent reconciliation processes intended to lead to a unified recommendation. When reconciliation is not possible, the professional societies should agree to jointly publish statements explaining the reasons for disparate recommendations, with a list of pros and cons associated with each recommendation. Published debates over discordant staging recommendations can promote transparency and demonstrate scientific equipoise, which can guide research efforts and funding prioritization to reconcile guideline differences. 34 , 38 The ACS NLCRT will work with practice guideline organizations to facilitate agreement and explanation of disagreement.

Gap 4: existing databases do not contain sufficient information to measure guideline‐concordant staging

To measure guideline‐concordant staging, one needs to collect information on the utilization and sequence of diagnostic tests and results. For example, guidelines recommend invasive mediastinal staging for patients with centrally located tumors or >3 cm and/or lymphadenopathy on CT or PET. 7 , 8 To know whether guideline‐concordant staging occurred, one must first determine whether a CT and PET scan were performed and have access to the radiographic findings. For patients with an indication for invasive mediastinal staging, one would also need to know if the proceduralist sampled lymph node tissue. On pathology review, reports of patients undergoing invasive mediastinal staging indicate that between 7% and 31% of patients have inadequate or no lymph node tissue sampled. 16 , 39 This example illustrates the granularity of information needed to determine guideline‐concordant staging. No existing database collects sufficient information to measure guideline‐concordant staging.

Recommendation 4: augment existing databases with the necessary information to measure guideline‐concordant staging

There are opportunities to modify existing databases to measure guideline‐concordant staging. One strategy is to link multiple sources of information, including administrative data, tumor registries, and electronic health records (EHRs). The Cancer Research Network, a consortium of mixed‐model and integrated health care delivery organizations, makes such data linkages for observational research. 41 In the research setting, the money, time, and expertise needed to perform such linkages, train abstractors and perform targeted EHR abstraction pales by comparison to the cost of trials. Consequently, funding such studies should be cost‐efficient for funding agencies. The National Cancer Institute recently funded a comparative‐effectiveness study of various pretreatment invasive nodal staging strategies that leverage this data infrastructure and may offer insights into scaling such efforts beyond research activities. 42 The resources and time needed for granular data collection may be infeasible in the quality improvement setting. However, the burden of manual abstraction could be offset by using random sampling techniques such as those used by the American College of Surgeons National Surgical Quality Improvement Project. 43 Automated data abstraction an alternative, for example, natural language processing, a form of automated data abstraction that requires development and site‐specific validation resources. In contrast, standardized reporting using structured fields is highly amenable to automated data abstraction but often requires a substantial change in clinician behavior. There is precedent for standardized reporting of test results, for instance, the American College of Radiology LungRads system for lung cancer screening 44 and the College of American Pathologists synoptic reporting for pathology findings. 45 National organizations such as the ACS NLCRT can partner with professional societies to advocate for standardized radiology and pathology reporting and routine entry of lung cancer clinical staging data into structured EHR fields, allowing for research, evaluation, and feedback with little effort. Collaboration with industry, such as developers of EHR systems, can efficiently foster the use of medical informatics to collect data for research and quality improvement purposes.

Gap 5: health systems do not have a performance feedback mechanism for lung cancer staging

A bedrock principle of quality improvement is performance feedback using high‐quality data. Known variability in the use and quality of staging procedures argue for metrics that assess structure, process, and outcome. 12 , 13 , 16 , 26 , 30 , 39 , 46 The STS General Thoracic Surgery Database 40 currently measures utilization of noninvasive and invasive staging modalities among surgically treated lung cancer patients. The ACCP piloted a multi‐institutional registry for bronchoscopy called the ACCP Quality Improvement Registry, Evaluation, and Education Program. 47 This database can measure diagnostic yield among patients who underwent a bronchoscopic procedure. A strength of both programs is the granularity of their data collection; however, both share the limitation of being procedure and specialty‐based and unable to provide information on the appropriateness of staging. The National Cancer Database (NCDB)—jointly sponsored by the American Cancer Society and American College of Surgeons—provides center‐level performance measures for cancer care using hospital registry data from Commission on Cancer accredited sites. Current measures relating to lung cancer staging are limited to intraoperative lymph node staging and synoptic pathology reporting. 48 An important limitation of this feedback mechanism is that it is limited to the minority of patients with lung cancer who undergo surgical therapy. In addition, health systems do not have a performance feedback mechanism for their clinicians practicing in the hospital and ambulatory care settings where lung cancer staging occurs.

Recommendation 5: develop and implement a performance feedback mechanism for lung cancer staging

Performance feedback mechanisms can help health systems and clinicians achieve higher guideline‐concordant staging levels. The ACCP Thoracic Oncology Network and Quality Improvement Committee published a framework for developing and validating quality measures for lung cancer care. 10 Thus far, the group has identified three process measures: appropriate utilization of invasive mediastinal staging and brain imaging and histologic documentation of stage IV disease. This framework could also be used to identify structural and outcome quality measures. The ACS NLCRT can help facilitate a collaboration leveraging the combined experience and resources of the STS, ACCP, and American College of Surgeons Commission on Cancer to implement structure, process, and outcome measures for lung cancer staging using the NCDB.

Gap 6: patients rarely self‐advocate for guideline‐concordant staging

Awareness of practice guideline recommendations is highly variable across patients, and patient perceptions about the intent of guidelines range from empowerment to rationing of care. 49 The NCCN has a patient‐facing version of their guidelines covering topics such as the overall rationale for staging and the diagnostic tests used to stage lung cancer. However, the content does not help patients understand specific staging recommendations. 50 An alternative source of information about lung cancer staging guidelines is patient advocacy groups, including the numerous member organizations of the ACS NLCRT. 23 The focus of these groups includes important topics such as tobacco cessation, screening, comprehensive biomarker testing, targeted therapy, patient support groups, financial support, scientific research, and health policy. Some groups educate patients on the importance of cancer staging, but there is little to no content on how patients should be staged or how the staging process should be discussed with their physician.

Recommendation 6: increase opportunities for patient self‐advocacy for guideline‐concordant staging

Professional organizations and patient advocacy groups have an opportunity to develop common, regularly updated, evidence‐based educational content and to promote patient awareness and patient‐provider dialogue about staging. Because lung cancer staging involves complex clinical decision‐making, these education and empowerment interventions should be designed in collaboration with patients. The interventions require careful design with attention to reading levels, health literacy, numeracy, and how the content will be disseminated to avoid unintentional promotion of health inequities. Additionally, these interventions should not impose the burden and responsibility of appropriate staging upon the patient. There should be a commitment to ensure every patient receives these materials at the time of diagnosis. Importantly, scientists should evaluate the effectiveness of these patient education interventions regarding their ability to increase knowledge and self‐efficacy. Greater awareness of practice guideline recommendations and tools to promote patient‐physician dialogue should increase opportunities for self‐advocacy. Professional organizations such as the ACS NLCRT can help facilitate collaborations between groups that produce patient education materials.

Gap 7: current health policies do not motivate guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging

There are no health policies that specifically address adherence to lung cancer staging guidelines. Health policy in the United States largely determines reimbursement for specific services, such as the utilization of diagnostic tests and cancer therapies. Underutilization of staging modalities occurs despite a fee‐for‐service system where diagnostic tests are discretely billable. One explanation is that recipients of diagnostic‐related reimbursements (e.g., radiologists) are not necessarily the same clinicians requesting diagnostic tests and/or subsequently treating the patient (e.g., oncologist). Another explanation is the perceived opportunity cost of lung cancer staging when the clinician can participate in another billable aspect of care that reimburses at a higher level (e.g., cancer treatment). Policies could be implemented that use financial levers to motivate behavior among clinicians who directly influence staging, or that modify the reimbursement structure (e.g., pay‐for‐performance) for the care of patients with lung cancer. Health policy and advocacy organizations will need to address legitimate concerns over the costs of cancer‐related diagnostics, unnecessary testing, low‐to‐moderate levels of evidence supporting guidelines (Gap 2), and variable guideline recommendations (Gap 3). Innovation in health policy will require multi‐stakeholder engagement and rigorous evaluation to maximize population‐level benefit while avoiding unintended consequences for individual lung cancer patients.

Recommendation 7: organize a representative working group under the ACS NLCRT that promotes policies that motivate guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging

The ACS NLCRT can bring together various stakeholders (clinicians, researchers, professional societies, patient advocates, industry representatives, and government agencies, all represented on the ACS NLCRT) to facilitate policy innovation, implementation, and evaluation. One example is to include lung cancer staging adherence measures as part of broader quality improvement efforts to improve lung cancer care and outcomes, 10 and link payment to participation in these quality improvement programs. Another example is advocacy for restructuring payments for lung cancer care (e.g., disease‐specific bundled payments). Regardless of the specific intervention, the ACS NLCRT may have unprecedented success in influencing policy because it represents the views of diverse stakeholders.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, staging is a critically important component of lung cancer care that applies to any eligible patient interested in curative‐ or palliative‐intent therapy. We identified seven knowledge and resource gaps that pose barriers to guideline‐concordant staging and offered seven recommendations to help bridge these gaps (Table 4). These gaps and recommendations do not exist in isolation. Pursuing one recommendation can positively affect multiple gaps and cause a “ripple effect.” For instance, augmenting existing databases can facilitate both comparative‐effectiveness research and the development and implementation of performance feedback mechanisms. Similarly, harmonization of guidelines can facilitate the development of quality metrics necessary to implement performance feedback mechanisms. If professional organizations cannot reconcile differences for specific recommendations, the disagreement becomes fodder for researchers. Over time, higher levels of evidence supporting guidelines, harmonized guideline recommendations, and performance feedback mechanisms can facilitate the development of health policies that promote guideline‐concordant staging. Because of the synergistic effects of our recommendations and the variable resources (i.e., money, time, and human capital) needed to ensure their success, we do not see a pragmatic way to prioritize these recommendations. Instead, we recognize that all are important; success will be measured incrementally over time, and some recommendations will be pursued in parallel whereas others will be pursued in series. We acknowledge and look forward to the hard work ahead in achieving our goal of promoting guideline‐concordant lung cancer staging as a means of ensuring appropriate and equitable treatment, optimal patient outcomes, and high value care.
