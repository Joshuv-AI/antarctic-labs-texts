Exploring Emotional Communication Between Older Persons and Nursing Assistants in Home Care: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Tanja Gustafsson, Elisabeth Lindberg, Hanna Maurin Söderholm, Lena Hedén, Sandra van dulmen, Annelie J Sundler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCommunication between older persons and nursing assistants is important in providing high‐quality, person‐centred home care. While previous research has reported on older persons' emotional concerns during home care conversations, there is a need to gain an in‐depth understanding of emotional concerns' role in such conversations and how to support the older persons' comfort and well‐being. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the nature and focus of emotional communication between older persons and nursing assistants in home care.MethodsA descriptive qualitative design using observational data from 83 audio‐recorded home care visits was used to identify and analyse sequences of distressing emotional communication, analysed through thematic analysis.ResultsIn this study, four themes were described illustrating how emotional communication between older persons and nursing assistants during home care visits creates opportunities for empathy: (1) recognising empathic opportunities, (2) identifying subtle and vague signs of emotional distress, (3) expressing needs for emotional support, and (4) striving to restore comfort in challenging conversations.ConclusionsConversations about older persons' emotions and worries can be complex. The findings indicate a fine line between meaningfulness and discomfort in emotional sequences. Expressions of emotional distress provide empathic opportunities that call for attentiveness and sensitivity from nursing assistants, implying a need for education and training in person‐centred communication.Implications for PracticeEmotional communication acknowledging empathic opportunities enhances person‐centred care by promoting trust, comfort, and well‐being. Ultimately, emotional communication reinforces the importance of empathy in home care and creates space for emotional interactions, leading to more supportive relationships.Keywords: emotional communication, home care, nursing assistants, observations, older persons, person‐centred care, thematic analysis

Introduction

Communication between older persons (OPs) and nursing assistants (NAs) is central to home care. NAs are a key part of nursing staff working at home care services (HCS) in Sweden, often providing most of the daily support and interaction (The Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare 2025). In nursing and care processes, communication about emotions, i.e., emotional communication (EC), can help staff understand patients' worries and concerns (Hafskjold et al. 2017; Höglander et al. 2017; Sundling et al. 2020). EC is essential to person‐centred care (PCC), as it helps build trust, foster connection, and promote partnership between nursing staff and OPs (Ebrahimi et al. 2021). In addition, EC provides opportunities for empathy (Suchman et al. 1997). However, it can be challenging for nursing staff to communicate about emotions, especially when addressing existential issues, fragility, dependency, and dissatisfaction (Sundler et al. 2016), or when emotions are expressed indirectly through hints or vague cues (Hafskjold et al. 2017). While previous research has reported on the occurrence of OPs' emotional concerns, this study was performed to gain in‐depth knowledge of the role of such EC in nursing home care.

In Sweden, depending on individual needs, HCS involve nursing and social care provided by healthcare professionals with varying education levels, mainly NAs (Ulmanen and Szebehely 2015). HCS include help with fundamental care needs, such as hygiene and nutrition, as well as more complex care. Over time, the responsibilities of NAs have expanded to include care tasks that were previously carried out by licensed healthcare professionals. However, there are still gaps in the support available to NAs in their evolving roles, which can contribute to workforce challenges and may impact patient safety and quality of care (Afzal et al. 2018). NAs often face the challenge of balancing the number of visits with providing high‐quality care, including emotional support (Choe et al. 2015). From the OPs' perspective, home care offers opportunities for meaningful conversations and relationships with nursing staff (Jarling et al. 2018). Although EC is not always formally emphasised in NA training or documented in care routines, it is implicitly expected as part of care delivery. NAs, therefore, play a central role in shaping OPs' daily care experiences through their emotional engagement.

Empathy is foundational to PCC (Håkansson Eklund et al. 2019), involving understanding, feeling, and sharing another person's emotional state while recognising that the emotions belong to the other person and not oneself (Håkansson Eklund and Summer Meranius 2021). Empathic opportunities arise when emotions are expressed and create an opportunity for an empathic response (Suchman et al. 1997). Empathic communication, along with shared decision‐making and a supportive environment, ensures that patients feel valued and actively involved, which are key components of PCC (McCormack and McCance 2006). The ability to listen and be sensitive is essential as it includes the acknowledgment and confirmation of emotions (McCabe 2004).

Emotions are subjective experiences of pleasant or unpleasant feelings that serve various functions and provide healthcare providers with information (Ekman 1992). EC individualises care by revealing what matters most to a person. Research on EC in home care has grown in recent years, exploring both negative (e.g., distress, worry) and positive emotions (e.g., joy, comfort). Negative emotions provide insight into individual needs (Hafskjold et al. 2017; Höglander et al. 2017; Sundling et al. 2020); positive ones support well‐being (Terrill et al. 2018) and enhance memory in persons with dementia (Van Dulmen et al. 2017). Still, EC can be demanding for nursing staff, making them feel vulnerable (Angel and Vatne 2017) and difficult to encounter when involving existential aspects of caring (Sundler et al. 2016).

Although the importance of EC is well documented, little is known about its role, function, and potential impact on home‐dwelling OPs. More research is needed to understand how EC actually unfolds in daily practice and how it might be acknowledged and responded to meaningfully. This study aimed to explore the nature and focus of EC between OPs and NAs during home care visits.

Methods

Study Design

This descriptive qualitative study used observational data from audio‐recorded communication during home care visits. Audio recordings (ARs) were collected and analysed using thematic analysis grounded in descriptive phenomenology (Sundler et al. 2019), an approach that supports an in‐depth understanding of real‐time EC between OPs and NAs.

Setting and Participants

A convenience sample of OPs and NAs was recruited from two home care units in a medium‐sized municipality in western Sweden in 2017. The manager of the municipal units expressed interest in participating during a meeting within the Health and Care Administration and gave permission for recruitment. NAs with permanent employment (n = 27) were informed about the study, both verbally and in writing, and invited by the first author to participate. OPs fluent in Swedish aged 65 years or older, living in their homes, without severe cognitive impairment were contacted via telephone by administrative personnel familiar to them. They also received written information and were approached again for consent by NAs they knew. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data Collection

The data collected for this study included 83 ARs of real‐time communication during home care visits, involving 13 NAs and 22 OPs. Each participating NA was asked to record five to seven visits using a portable digital audio recorder. They were instructed to select visits to OPs that had provided informed consent.

A purposeful sampling of ARs resulted in various types of home care visits being recorded, including morning and evening visits, both long and short. The NAs informed the OPs about the recording at the start of the visit, began recording upon entering the home, and stopped it upon leaving. NAs were instructed to behave and speak as they normally would. The recordings ranged from 2 to 58 min (mean = 13 min), totaling approximately 17 h of data collected between December 2017 and November 2018. Data Analysis

Two researchers conducted the data analysis: the first author, an RN and PhD student, and the last author, a professor in caring science with experience in qualitative and communication research. To become familiar with the data, the first author listened to all recordings. Next, sequences of EC were identified using the Verona coding definitions of emotional sequences (VR‐CoDES) (Del Piccolo et al. 2011). According to VR‐CoDES, emotional distress is expressed either as a cue or a concern. A cue is: ‘a verbal or non‐verbal hint which suggests an underlying unpleasant emotion and would need a clarification from the health provider,’ while a concern is ‘a clear and unambiguous expression of an unpleasant current or recent emotion where the emotion is explicitly verbalized’ (Del Piccolo et al. 2009, 4–6).

The first and last authors listened to the recordings separately, then together, to identify emotional sequences. EC sequences were found in 42 of the 83 ARs, involving 12 NAs and 18 OPs (see Table 1). By listening to both verbal and non‐verbal expressions, such as intonations, an overall sense of the meaning and interaction was obtained. Thereafter, the communication between OPs and NAs in these emotional sequences was transcribed verbatim. While listening, the first author noted non‐verbal cues (e.g., tone of voice, sighing, crying, and silences) and contextual elements (e.g., time and purpose of the visit, ongoing activities). These contextual notes were based on either what was said or inferred from the audio. The transcripts yielded approximately 70 A4 pages of text.

The analysis followed a phenomenological approach (Sundler et al. 2019), which includes maintaining openness, questioning pre‐understanding, and adopting a reflective attitude. First, the transcripts were read multiple times by both researchers with an open mind for familiarisation with the data. Then, content related to EC was labelled with notes. Patterns of meanings in the text were further explored and elaborated on by comparing similarities and differences and further developed into descriptive text. The process of analysis involved a back‐and‐forth movement between parts and the whole. Finally, meanings were organised and developed into themes.

Themes were illustrated with direct quotations, each attributed by the AR (1–42), NA (1–12), and OP (1–18) numbers. Since the original data were in Swedish, the analysis was conducted in the original language. Theme labels and quotes were translated into English in the final phase, with careful attention paid to preserving meaning and tone. Throughout the process, the analysis and development of themes were discussed among all authors.

Rigour

To ensure the rigour and trustworthiness of the analysis, researchers need to discuss and reflect on their understanding of the data and derived themes (Malterud 2001). Therefore, we adopted a reflective attitude in which pre‐understanding was questioned throughout the process, as suggested by Sundler et al. (2019). The credibility and trustworthiness of the findings were enhanced by using quotes from the ARs to illustrate the link between the empirical data and the themes. Transferability, a key indicator of scientific quality (Sundler et al. 2019), was strengthened by collecting rich, varied data that allowed for a comprehensive understanding of the EC in home care. Although the study was conducted in a specific setting with a limited number of participants, the findings offer insights applicable to other healthcare settings where EC plays a critical role.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board of Gothenburg (Dnr. 260/17) and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. They were assured of confidentiality, the right to withdraw at any time without consequences, and that declining participation would not affect their care. Data were securely stored and managed according to the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) 2016 and local university protocols.

Findings

The findings describe four themes (see Figure 1) that illustrate how EC between OPs and NAs during home care visits creates opportunities for empathy. Emotional expressions, such as signs of discomfort, sadness, or stress, metaphorically serve as crossroads, guiding interactions in multiple possible directions. These expressions evoke varied responses shaping subsequent conversations through turns and actions that may carry dual or ambiguous meanings. Such interactions present challenges for NAs but also open up complex, potentially empathic opportunities.

Overview of the themes describing empathic opportunities during emotional communication between older persons and nursing assistants.

Recognising Empathic Opportunities

Some homecare conversations lead to trustful interactions, creating empathic opportunities. Emotional expressions in these conversations were often tied to OPs' expressions of discomfort, insecurity, sadness, irritation, or anger. These emotional expressions provide entry points for engagement. When NAs recognise and respond to the OPs' expressed emotions with sensitivity and attentiveness, they can foster mutual comfort. For example, an older woman expresses sadness about her loneliness and the death of her husband, wishing for more time to talk with NAs. The NA responds empathically, offering verbal comfort, giving her hugs and suggestions for how to request more social time from the HCS. During the conversation, the woman says:

OP: I told [another NA] that I have lost my desire to live.

NA: No, you cannot. Sometimes it is okay to feel sad, it is okay to feel slightly sad and tired, it is okay to feel like that; no one can be positive all the time.

(OP11 & NA11, AR41)

Here, the NA acknowledges and normalises the woman's feelings, continuing the emotional conversation.

Empathic communication was observed to involve both verbal and non‐verbal behaviours, such as comforting words, physical contact (e.g., hugs). NAs respond to emotions by inviting conversations, asking questions, and tolerating silence. For instance, during a visit, an older woman discusses her near‐blindness with an NA while creating a shopping list. The conversation turns toward the woman's eye problems. Being almost blind affects her daily life, which at times makes her sad and diminishes her desire to live. As she reflects on the sadness and challenges related to her vision loss, the NA responds with an encouraging tone of voice:

OP: I told my daughter once that now I do not want to be around anymore, and then the tears came, you know.

(OP11, AR28)

NA: You need to keep on fighting.

(NA6, AR28)

Emotional talk and eliciting feelings and experiences seem to instill trust and meaningful connections, going beyond the completion of practical tasks.

Health disparities also evoke emotional responses. For example, an older woman expresses worry, coping with pain and difficulty walking, downplaying her health problems. When the NA recognises this, their communication develops into an interplay:

NA: You have been through a lot of amazing things in life!

OP: Yes, I have, but nowadays, nothing is happening to me.

(NA5 & OP6, AR27)

By affirming her value, the NA comforts her, demonstrating how emotional expressions can develop into empathic opportunities when recognised and addressed.

Identifying Subtle and Vague Signs of Emotional Distress

Emotional concerns during home care visits can emerge as subtle or vague signs that are difficult to detect, as observed in the ARs. OPs sometimes conceal their concerns behind metaphors, humour, or ambivalent expressions during everyday conversations and ongoing activities. For example, an older man uses a metaphor to express sadness:

OP: It is a bit dark today.

(OP10, AR17)

His tone of voice conveys an underlying feeling of sadness. Similarly, humour sometimes masks emotional distress. One older woman speaks to an NA about the deterioration of her physical capacity. During their conversation, she jokes about the loss of her singing abilities:

OP: Nowadays I cannot sing, I sound like a crow; caw, caw, caw.

(OP6, AR14)

Emotional distress hidden in metaphors or humorous façades can discount the seriousness of the expressions and require explicit attention to underlying cues.

Emotional conversations occasionally reveal ambiguity during conversations, where verbal statements and non‐verbal signals, such as the tone of voice, do not align. For example, during the end of a homecare visit, an older man tells an NA that he is about to go to the doctor because of his health problems, and insists that he is not worried about his upcoming surgery:

OP: I am not worried at all, but I am working on getting well.

NA: That is right!

OP: I am not worried in any way, but I just want the surgery to be done.

(OP2 & NA2, AR5)

When the NA tries to encourage him, the man repeats himself by saying he is not worried. Despite his verbal denial, the tone of his voice suggests concern. These ambiguous expressions of negative emotions are sometimes difficult to notice.

Expressions of emotional distress can surface alongside practical tasks, making it easy for vague expressions to be overlooked and opportunities for interpersonal communication to be missed. For example, while helping an older woman to the bathroom, an NA hears her express being worried about her wetting the bedclothes due to urine incontinence:

OP: I am sorry, but I cannot help it // It is so troublesome when I cannot fix it by myself.

NA: Yes, that is not something you can control.

(OP13 & NA10, AR40)

While responding, the NA continues the practical tasks of changing the bedclothes. Although some NAs may recognise subtle emotional cues, there remains a risk that such expressions could go unnoticed or unaddressed, as attention shifts toward task completion.

Expressing Needs for Emotional Support

In some interactions, OPs explicitly seek attention for their emotional concerns, but NAs, focused on practical tasks, sometimes overlook these needs. When emotional needs go unacknowledged, OPs sometimes repeat their concerns. For instance, an older man expresses concern over his increasing dependence:

OP: If it had not been for the [stoma] bag, I would have managed on my own.

(OP4, AR6)

The NA briefly confirms his thoughts. As the NA assists him with showering, the man continues to express ambivalence about his reliance on HCS, worrying about how he will cope as his frailty increases. Despite sparse responses from the NA, he repeatedly returns to the topic, signalling his need for acknowledgement.

Negative emotions often challenge NAs, particularly when practical tasks set on the homecare agenda take precedence. During one visit, an older man reflects on being in an end‐of‐life phase while the NA is occupied with washing dishes. He recalls flipping through an old photo album and saw a photo of himself in his 30s. He had then looked in the mirror. He says:

OP: I should not look at myself in the mirror.

(OP4, AR11)

The NA reassures him about his appearance, but the man disagrees and says he should not complain because he is doing fine. He sighs and reflects over the passing of years:

OP: I think of all the years that have passed when looking back on my life.

(OP4, AR11)

His existential concerns are left unacknowledged as the NA continues humming and completing tasks.

Similarly, in another visit, an older woman repeatedly voices pain in her fingers. While administering medication, the NA responds by saying that she can imagine how difficult it is for her. After a short while, when helping her with compression socks, the woman mentions her pain again. The NA reminds her to take painkillers, and the woman replies:

OP: Maybe they will help a bit, eventually.

(OP6, AR19)

When the woman reiterates her concerns, the NA replies in an irritated tone:

NA: This [the problem with the fingers] seems to be the problem every day.

(NA3, AR19)

The visit ends without the woman's concerns being fully addressed.

These examples illustrate how emotional needs often compete with the demands of mandatory care tasks, making it difficult for NAs to engage fully in emotional conversations. In such situations, OPs may continue to seek acknowledgment even when responses are minimal or dismissive.

Striving to Restore Comfort in Challenging Situations

Conversations involving emotional distress can provoke discomfort and frustration for both OPs and NAs. When unexpected emotions arise during everyday interactions, they often change the atmosphere, making communication tense and challenging. A common approach NAs use to restore comfort is to redirect conversations away from distressing topics. For example, during an interaction between an older woman and an NA, they discuss the upcoming Christmas holiday. While making the bed, the NA cheerfully asks the woman about her plans. The woman quietly reveals that she will spend Christmas alone, her tone altering the atmosphere. To lighten the mood, the NA jokes:

NA: I will come and pick you up, and you will celebrate at my house.

(NA7, AR29)

They both laugh briefly, but the woman maintains a sad tone, saying she is okay with being alone. After a short pause, the NA shifts the focus by asking the woman if she is happy with how the bed is made and saying she has been meticulous. The conversation continues with other topics. This illustrates how tense situations are often eased by changing the subject to restore a more comfortable atmosphere.

Frustration can also surface when OPs express dissatisfaction with HCS. For example, an older woman complains about perceived neglect in her wound care:

OP: It [the wound] should be taken care of and washed and… but nobody cares about it.

(OP13, AR39)

The NA tries to comfort her by suggesting they contact the nurse responsible for her care. Similarly, frustration arises when NAs do not adhere to scheduled time. In another visit, an older woman expresses irritation when the NA arrives too early for a scheduled visit:

OP: I asked you to come at half past nine! This is because I need to eat my sandwich and take my medication first. I do not have the energy to get up at half past six.

NA: Okay, I am sorry for coming too early today. It was not my intention to ruin that.

(OP12 & NA7, AR35)

Although the NA apologises several times, the woman repeatedly voices her irritation, and the tension affects their interaction throughout the visit. These examples demonstrate how negative emotions expressed during home care visits can challenge communication, disrupt trust, and strain the caring relationship.

Discussion

The findings illustrate how EC in conversations between OPs and NAs during home care visits creates pathways for empathy. Emotional expressions often act as turning points, shaping subsequent interactions and offering complex, sometimes ambiguous, opportunities for empathic engagement. Empathy seemed to be fostered when NAs recognise and respond sensitively to OPs' emotional needs, acknowledging both explicit and subtle expressions of distress, such as sadness, loneliness, and frustration, conveyed through verbal and non‐verbal cues. These emotional aspects are central to human‐to‐human interactions in nursing care and align with the ethical responsibility of nursing staff to address individual needs thoughtfully. This responsibility draws on the concept of phronesis or practical wisdom, described by Paul Ricœur (Kristensson Uggla 2022).

However, the findings also reveal that NAs do not always recognise or respond to OPs' emotional signals. Without direct access to the NAs' perspective or intentions, it remains unclear whether these omissions reflect deliberate strategies (e.g., redirection) or result from task‐oriented focus and communication challenges. These findings should be interpreted with an awareness of the NAs' role, training, and working conditions in home care settings. Previous research indicates that NAs often balance OPs' emotional needs against tight home care schedules, where practical tasks take priority (Gustafsson et al. 2023). Addressing emotional concerns can be challenging and may require communication skills and training, particularly when approaching OPs with depressive symptoms. Collaboration among professionals, OPs, and their relatives is essential in addressing emotional needs and fostering mutual communication and support (Ebrahimi et al. 2021). Although challenging, managing OPs' emotional vulnerability can be rewarding for nursing staff, contributing to job satisfaction and professional growth (Larsson Gerdin et al. 2021). These findings highlight the need for both educational efforts and organisational support to equip NAs with the skills needed to address OPs' emotional needs. Investing in high‐quality home care, including support for developing communication skills, remains essential, especially in times of high staff turnover, a high percentage of staff with a different native language, and the need for efficient and person‐centred care.

The complexity of EC also underscores the importance of NAs' situational awareness. In this study, we observed a fine line between meaningfulness and discomfort in communication sequences with emotional distress. There is no single answer regarding how to best acknowledge or respond to these situations. It seems that emotional expressions are linked to what is happening in the actual situation, where the NAs need to respond to OPs' emotional distress. Emotional expressions often emerge within specific contexts, influenced by the nature of the visit, for instance, assistance with daily activities, medical administration, or wound care (Heyn et al. 2021). Communication and interaction form the basis of individualised and person‐centred care, emphasising sensitivity to the OP's emotional state, active listening, and emotional validation (McCormack et al. 2010; Sundler et al. 2020). However, hidden or vaguely expressed concerns may easily go unnoticed, as previous studies also have shown (Hafskjold et al. 2017; Höglander et al. 2017). These findings reinforce the core tenets of person‐centred communication and highlight the importance of NAs' ability to tailor their communication strategies to the emotional and psychosocial needs of each OP, recognising when to offer comfort, when to provide clear information, and when to listen actively.

In this study, efforts to restore comfort in challenging situations were identified. The findings suggest that NAs need skills in EC to recognise and respond to negative emotions, creating supportive conversations based on OPs' individual needs. Similarly to the NAs in this study, other studies report that registered nurses in home care settings express uncertainty and confusion about addressing emotional dimensions in their professional roles (Devik et al. 2013). Providing emotional support, listening attentively, and being alert to non‐verbal cues remain essential for promoting person‐centred communication, creating a supportive and empathetic care environment that enhances the overall well‐being of OPs (Ebrahimi et al. 2021). Research also suggests that focusing on OPs' relational and emotional needs can enhance job satisfaction among nursing staff (Edvardsson et al. 2011; Franzosa et al. 2019). However, there are barriers for NAs to develop relationships with OPs, such as casual or on‐call employments with low wages and unpredictable scheduling (Afzal et al. 2018). In an era of increasing digital and technological integration in home care, maintaining a focus on relational and emotional aspects becomes even more critical. Emotional conversations can serve as a meaningful pathway to build relationships, where empathy and understanding bridge the gap between task‐oriented care and compassionate, person‐centred care.

Study Limitations and Strengths

In this study, observational data from ARs were analysed as an efficient and highly valued method for data gathering (Salmon 2015). ARs have been found to be almost as efficient as video recordings in analysing emotional tone in communication research (Williams et al. 2013). Their study found high agreement of emotional tone ratings among audio and video data. While ARs are limited to verbal and nonverbal communication such as tone of voice and voice pitch, video recordings also capture communication behaviours such as body language and gestures. Still, both audio and video data were found to be efficient in capturing affective messages, and such observational data can capture real‐time interactions and communication in their natural setting. By analysing recorded communication, we aimed to observe dynamics of EC as it unfolds during home care conversations, which is a strength in this study. However, the study design did not allow for follow‐up questions or further descriptions of the participants' experiences, which is a limitation.

The NAs reported that the recordings did not disrupt their day‐to‐day work. From their point of view, they acted naturally and sometimes even forgot that they were being recorded. In addition, previous studies support the assumption that ARs have limited impact on the actual communication (Henry et al. 2015; Williams et al. 2013). One strength was that the researchers were not present during the recordings, which minimised observer influence. Observations have advantages: such data can provide valuable insights into interactions and the actual communication (Salmon 2015). Höglander et al. (2017) argue that further research involving real‐time audio and video recordings of communication is necessary, as discrepancies may exist between individuals' recollections during interviews about nursing care and the actual delivery of that care.

The study focuses on EC in home care, addressing enduring aspects of nursing care that remain unaffected by temporal changes. Empathic and person‐centred care are central to effective communication in home care settings and are unlikely to lose relevance over time. Despite data collected in the years 2017 and 2018, the insights into how EC fosters OPs' well‐being continue to offer significant value and can inform home care practices.

Not all NAs had Swedish as their native language; however, all were fluent in Swedish. Even though OPs were not asked about their native language, we observed that they also spoke fluent Swedish, with some speaking with an accent suggesting a different native language. A potential limitation of this study, given its focus on communication, is the absence of participants with any language difficulties. Additionally, while our study did not collect detailed information on participants' cultural, ethnic, or socio‐economic background, these factors may influence EC in home care. Many NAs in Sweden have a migrant background, whereas OPs receiving home care are typically born in the country (The Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare 2024, 2025). This asymmetry may shape relational dynamics in various ways, including both opportunities and challenges for empathy and mutual understanding. Future research should explore how such contextual factors influence EC, particularly in increasingly multicultural care settings.

A strength of the study is that the analysis was reflected upon by all authors, which helped maintain reflexivity throughout the research process. Reflections and discussions were continuously made to uphold a reflective attitude during the whole process, as described by Sundler et al. (2019). When uncertainties or interpretive challenges arose, they were addressed through joint discussions, contributing to analytic rigour and credibility. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into EC in home care and highlights important opportunities for empathic engagement in NA–OP interactions.

Conclusion

The results highlight the important yet complex phenomenon of EC during home care visits. EC opens empathic opportunities that support PCC, although a fine line was observed between fostering meaningful connections and provoking emotional distress. Nursing staff, particularly NAs, need education and training to identify and respond to empathic opportunities with empathy.

This study's findings point to the importance of NAs' emotional and empathic responses to OPs' emotional expressions in providing support and PCC. In practice, the findings underscore the need to create relational space within care routines, allowing for emotionally attuned interactions. At the policy level, the study highlights the importance of emotionally responsive, person‐centred approaches as part of HCS. Given that home care enables individuals to remain in their own homes while receiving support, promoting EC and emotional well‐being is essential for building socially and ethically sustainable care systems.
