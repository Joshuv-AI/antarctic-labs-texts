Trends of blood pressure control in metabolic syndrome among US adults: evidence from NHANES 1999 to 2020
Authors: Jiaming Huang, Lingrong Hu, Qingyan Ruan, Xiaoming Li, Shumin Zhao, Kaihong Chen, Liling Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetabolic Syndrome (MetS) involves several cardiovascular risk factors, with hypertension being a critical component that significantly impacts cardiovascular outcomes. As guidelines support blood pressure control in populations with high cardiovascular risk factors, we evaluated BP control in MetS patients.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional analysis of probability samples of adults aged ≥ 18 years with MetS from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 1999 to 2020 to assess changes in BP control. MetS was identified by meeting at least three of the following criteria: (1) waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men and ≥ 88 cm in women; (2) fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dl; (3) BP ≥ 130/85 mmHg; (4) triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dl; and (5) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) < 40 mg/dl in men and < 50 mg/dl in women. BP control was defined as systolic BP < 130 mmHg and diastolic BP < 80 mmHg. All analyses accounted for differences between survey years, complex sampling design, and survey weights.ResultsFrom 1999–2002 to 2015–2020, the prevalence of MetS increased from 19% to 24% (P < 0.001). Among MetS patients, the proportion of those self-reporting hypertension or taking BP medications increased from 65% in 1999–2002 to 72% in 2015–2020 (P = 0.001). Over time, both the use and quantity of antihypertensive medications increased significantly, and according to current guidelines, BP control among MetS patients has improved significantly. Similar trends were observed across subgroups stratified by gender, diabetes status, and CKD status.ConclusionBP management in MetS patients showed significant improvement from 1999 to 2020, with increasing control rates despite fluctuations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01027-7.

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) comprises multiple risk factors linked to diabetes and cardiovascular disease (CVD), characterized by visceral fat accumulation, insulin resistance, lipid abnormalities, and a heightened risk of developing type 2 diabetes, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) and hypertension [1, 2]. The overall rate of MetS in the United States is 34.7%, with rates increasing with age and exceeding 50% in certain populations [3–5]. A meta-analysis encompassing 87 studies demonstrated that MetS significantly increased the risk of CVD, all-cause mortality, and stroke, even among individuals with MetS but without diabetes [5–7]. The cardiovascular risks associated with MetS varied depending on the combination of its components. One study revealed that among all possible combinations of three MetS components, central obesity, elevated blood pressure, and hyperglycemia conferred the highest risk for CVD and mortality [5, 8]. Therefore, effective BP control has emerged as a crucial strategy for preventing cardiovascular events and reducing mortality rates in patients with MetS [9].

In recent years, with the advancement of clinical trials, the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA) conducted a systematic review in 2017 and subsequently recommended lowering BP targets to < 130/80 mmHg for individuals at elevated cardiovascular risk [10]. Multiple studies have demonstrated that controlling systolic BP to <120mmHg can enhance various important outcomes, such as overall mortality and cardiovascular events [11, 12].

Data from the Framingham Heart Study demonstrate that high-normal blood pressure (systolic 130–139 mmHg and/or diastolic 85–89 mmHg) significantly increases both the absolute and relative risks of CVD in normotensive populations [13, 14]. This finding underscores the critical importance of comprehensive BP control assessment in individuals with MetS, who inherently possess multiple cardiovascular risk factors [15]. The evaluation of BP management in this high-risk cohort is particularly crucial, as even modest elevations may confer disproportionate cardiovascular risk compared to the general population. Rigorous assessment of hypertension control in MetS patients represents an essential component of cardiovascular risk stratification and targeted preventive intervention strategies. While existing literature has analyzed blood pressure trends in the general population, studies specifically focusing on the MetS population are relatively scarce. Since various factors such as gender, geography, and healthcare resource distribution also affect BP conditions in MetS patients, it is necessary to focus on analyzing BP trends in this population for better assessment [16].

Through analyzing extensive epidemiological data, we sought to identify factors that may influence hypertension control in this population, contributing to individualized antihypertensive management for these patients. Furthermore, the disparities in BP control among subgroups—such as those defined by gender, race/ethnicity, and comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus and chronic kidney disease)—have not been adequately characterized in this high-risk population. Therefore, this study aims not only to evaluate the long-term trends in BP control among patients with MetS from 1999 to 2020, but also to investigate potential differences across key demographic and clinical subgroups.

Methods

The study utilized data from the NHANES database, spanning the years 1999–2020. NHANES is a periodically conducted cross-sectional health survey that employs a stratified, multistage, and probability cluster design to collect nationally representative sample data from the non-institutionalized civilian population of the United States. The NHANES, a health survey that represented the national population, was implemented by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS), a division of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Because of the COVID-19 pandemic, data collection for the NHANES 2019 to 2020 cycle stopped in March 2020, and the 2019 to 2020 data were made accessible in conjunction with the 2017 to 2018 data [17]. We examined data from the 1999 to 2002, 2003 to 2006, 2007 to 2010, 2011 to 2014, and 2015 to 2020 cycles for the current study.

Study population

In this research, we included patients aged ≥ 18 years with MetS and excluded participants with missing data on antihypertensive medication use and BP measurements. The final analysis comprised participants from five time periods: 1999–2002 (n = 1924), 2003–2006 (n = 1848), 2007–2010 (n = 2909), 2011–2014 (n = 2467), and 2015–2020 (n = 3496). The detailed exclusion criteria were shown in Figure S1.

Definition of MetS

MetS was defined according to the harmonized criteria established in 2009 by the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), American Heart Association (AHA), and National Heart, Lung and Blood Institute (NHLBI) [1]. Participants were diagnosed with MetS if they met three or more of the following five criteria: abdominal obesity (waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men or ≥ 88 cm in women); elevated blood pressure (systolic BP ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic BP ≥ 85 mmHg, or on antihypertensive medication); elevated fasting glucose (≥ 5.6 mmol/L [100 mg/dL], or on glucose-lowering medication); hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L [150 mg/dL], or on specific treatment); and low HDL cholesterol (< 1.0 mmol/L [40 mg/dL] in men or < 1.3 mmol/L [50 mg/dL] in women, or on specific treatment).

Blood pressure measurement and control

Trained physicians measured BP using a mercury sphygmomanometer and a properly fitted cuff. Three consecutive readings were obtained at each visit, with the first measurement taken after a 5-minute seated rest period, followed by two additional readings at 30-second intervals. A fourth measurement was attempted if any reading was interrupted or incomplete. For participants with multiple BP readings, the initial measurement was excluded from the calculations, and the mean of all remaining valid measurements was used in the data analysis [18]. BP control was defined according to the ACC/AHA guideline threshold of systolic BP < 130 mmHg and diastolic BP < 80 mmHg [10]. Recognizing the potential for temporal classification bias when applying the 2017 ACC/AHA blood pressure control threshold to earlier NHANES cycles, we conducted sensitivity analyses using the BP threshold (< 140/90 mmHg) established by The Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure (JNC 7) [19].

Variable definitions

Sociodemographic data were collected via standardized questionnaires, including self-reported information on age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational levels, and family income. The collection procedures for other clinical variables are in the Supplemental Material.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted with R software (version 4.3.1). Weighted analyses were conducted using NHANES-recommended sampling weights. Sampling weights were applied in all analyses to produce nationally representative estimates. All analyses accounted for the complex, multistage, probability sampling design of NHANES, incorporating strata, primary sampling units (PSUs), and sample weights, following NHANES analytic guidelines. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Clinical characteristics of patients with MetS were compared across different time periods. Categorical variables were presented as counts and their respective weighted proportions. Continuous variables were expressed as weighted means with their standard errors (SEs). Assessments of measure differences between time periods were made using ANOVA, Kruskal–Wallis, and Pearson’s chi-square tests as appropriate. The counts and percentages of individuals with MetS were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test by the following SBP categories: <120, 120–129, 130–139, and ≥ 140 across the time periods. To evaluate secular trends in BP control and changes in medication use, we performed survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression models, using the midpoint year of each NHANES cycle as a continuous variable to assess linear trends in BP control and temporal changes in medication use. Models were adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, CKD, and diabetes status. This approach follows NHANES recommendations for trend testing over time in binary outcomes. For continuous outcomes, we used survey-weighted linear regression models, incorporating time as a continuous predictor, to test for linear trends across cycles. Additionally, BP control was assessed among patients with MetS through stratification by demographic characteristics, insurance, CKD, and diabetes status. To further explore the potential moderating effects of these factors on BP control trends, we incorporated interaction terms between time periods and these key variables in our multivariable logistic regression models.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The clinical characteristics of included individuals are shown in Table 1. This study included 12,644 US adults with MetS. From 1999–2002 to 2015–2020, the prevalence of MetS increased from 19% to 24% (P < 0.001). The proportion of people aged ≥ 65 showed a slight increase over the years. The prevalence of DM increased from 29% in 1999–2002 to 44% in 2015–2020 (P < 0.0001), and the body mass index (BMI) showed a yearly increase. Notably, the prevalence of hypertension increased significantly over time, rising from 65.2% in 1999–2002 to 72.2% in 2015–2020. There were no significant changes in the distribution of gender, race, education level, or the proportion of patients with poverty income ratio (PIR) or chronic kidney disease (CKD).

Antihypertensive medication use among MetS patients

As shown in Fig. 1, between 1999–2002 and 2015–2020, the proportion of patients with MetS receiving treatment with at least one antihypertensive medication significantly increased, while the proportion of MetS patients not receiving any BP medication decreased from 46% in 1999–2002 to 34% in 2015–2020. Table 2. illustrates the changes in antihypertensive medication use over time, showing notable increases in the use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), and β-blockers. There was an overall increase in the use of diuretics, peaking in 2007–2010 before stabilizing, while there were no notable changes in calcium channel blocker use.

BP category distribution by gender and race/ethnicity

Figure 2 displays the percentages and 95% CI of the MetS population categorized by systolic BP and diastolic BP during 1999–2020. The data reveal significant differences in BP distribution across time periods. Specifically, the proportion of individuals achieving SBP < 120 mmHg increased significantly, while the proportion of those with SBP ≥ 140 mmHg showed a significant decrease. These distributional shifts were particularly pronounced in females and Non-Hispanic White populations. Changes in the proportion of people with DBP < 80 mmHg and DBP ≥ 90 mmHg varied slightly between time intervals. Table S2 provides detailed percentages of individuals with SBP < 120, 120–129, 130–139, and ≥ 140 mmHg across different gender and racial/ethnic groups during each time period, illustrating the changing patterns in BP category distribution over the study timeframe.

BP control in individuals with MetS

As shown in Table 3, while there were no significant changes in mean diastolic BP over time, systolic BP significantly decreased from 131 mmHg to 128 mmHg (P = 0.02). According to ACC/AHA guidelines, after accounting for relevant confounding factors, the percentage of patients with MetS achieving target BP increased from 37% in 1999–2002 to 48% in 2015–2020, with BP control rates even reaching 51% during 2007–2010 (P < 0.001). Similar results were observed using the JNC 7 guideline, with the proportion of MetS patients achieving target BP increasing from 65% in 1999–2002 to 72% in 2015–2020 (P < 0.001).The trends of BP control are shown in Fig.3.

Next, we assessed the proportion of MetS patients with controlled hypertension, categorized by age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, CKD status, and diabetes status. The results of this analysis are presented in Table 4. Notably, the percentages increased from 37% to 30% in 1999–2002 to 49% and 43% in 2015–2020 among those aged 45–64 years and ≥ 65 years, respectively (P < 0.001). Over time, significant improvements were observed across racial groups and PIR, though statistical significance was only reached among non-Hispanic Whites and those with PIR of 1.3–3.5 and > 3.5. Across education levels, with and without chronic disease, all groups showed a significant upward trend, peaking in the 2007–2010 period and then stabilizing. No statistically significant change in BP control was observed among uninsured individuals or those with other insurance types. Discussion

Based on NHANES data from 1999 to 2020, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of BP control trends among U.S. adults with MetS. Our findings revealed a significant temporal increase in hypertension prevalence among MetS patients. Correspondingly, the proportion of MetS patients receiving at least one antihypertensive medication has continued to rise. Encouragingly, according to the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines, the percentage of patients achieving target BP control showed substantial improvement, increasing from 37% in 1999–2002 to 48% in 2015–2020, despite some periodic fluctuations. Similar positive trends were observed across various demographic subgroups and among patients with coexisting conditions like diabetes and CKD, indicating a broad-based improvement in BP management.

Several factors may contribute to these observed improvements: first, the evolution of clinical guidelines has played a crucial role. The JNC7 in 2003 established foundational recommendations, suggesting that hypertensive patients maintain systolic BP below 140 mmHg. Subsequent research demonstrated that more intensive BP control (SBP < 120 mmHg) could significantly improve multiple clinical outcomes, including all-cause mortality and cardiovascular events [20]. However, this finding was tempered by other studies indicating that intensive BP control might lead to increased adverse events or show no significant impact on clinical outcomes in certain populations [11, 12]. In response to this evolving evidence base, the 2017 ACC/AHA guidelines made a landmark change, adjusting the hypertension diagnostic criteria to ≥ 130/80 mmHg for patients with high cardiovascular risk, rather than maintaining the traditional threshold of 140/90 mmHg [10]. These ongoing refinements in clinical trial insights and guideline updates have provided clinicians with more sophisticated tools for BP monitoring and management in MetS patients, enabling more comprehensive and individualized care approaches.

Second, increased use of antihypertensive medications. The proportion of patients not using antihypertensive medications has decreased year by year, with particularly notable increases in the use of ACEIs/ARBs and β-blockers. Multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that while antihypertensive medications (particularly ACEIs/ARBs) may induce minor alterations in metabolic parameters such as lipid profiles and blood glucose, they have not been associated with deterioration in cardiovascular or renal outcomes [21–24]. In fact, some research has indicated that high-dose ARB treatment can effectively reduce arterial stiffness in MetS patients with hypertension, providing additional cardiovascular benefits beyond BP control [25]. Furthermore, the introduction of newer antihypertensive agents (such as carvedilol, labetalol, and nebivolol) has demonstrated neutral or positive metabolic effects compared to conventional β-blockers, broadening the treatment options for clinicians managing hypertension in MetS patients [2]. This broader range of treatment options has enabled more personalized approaches to BP management, potentially contributing to improved control rates. Despite our study not finding an increase in the use of CCBs, previous research has demonstrated that the combination of enalapril and lercanidipine (ENA/LER) offers superior antihypertensive efficacy and potential cardiovascular protection in patients with MetS compared to enalapril plus hydrochlorothiazide (ENA/HCT) [26]. This provides important clinical implications, suggesting that appropriate augmentation of CCB utilization or implementation of combination therapy could achieve enhanced BP reduction and further improve hypertension control rates. The synergistic effects of ACEIs and CCBs may offer comprehensive cardiovascular benefits beyond BP reduction alone, particularly in this high-risk population with complex metabolic abnormalities.

Third, the implementation of healthcare policies may have contributed to improved BP control in MetS patients. The passage of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) in 2010 represented a watershed moment, planning to extend insurance coverage to 32 million previously uninsured individuals [27]. The implementation of the Affordable Care Act (ACA) is temporally associated with improvements in BP control rates. The comprehensive reforms of the ACA not only expanded coverage but also implemented payment reforms that strengthened primary care services and promoted more effective chronic disease management and treatment protocols, which may be one of the factors contributing to the improvement in BP control rates [28]. However, despite these advances, current healthcare policies continue to grapple with persistent challenges in medical resource distribution, particularly concerning health disparities among different racial and socioeconomic groups [29–32]. Our research findings corroborated these disparities, demonstrating that except for non-Hispanic whites and individuals with poverty-income ratios ≥ 1.3, BP control improvements were not uniform across all population segments. The results emphasize the need for focused efforts to tackle healthcare disparities.

Fourth, our analysis revealed a significant correlation between education levels and BP control outcomes. Over the study period, overall education levels showed a steady increase, with particularly notable improvements in BP control among highly educated groups. This finding aligns with previous research, including surveys of market female vendors, which demonstrated that higher education levels correlate with better understanding and management of MetS [33]. Additional studies have shown that active communication about MetS knowledge and clinical assessment information can effectively enhance patients’ health risk awareness and motivation to adopt healthy lifestyle modifications [34]. Notably, awareness of MetS has been steadily increasing across all population segments, exceeding 50% in certain demographics [35, 36]. This improved health literacy has enabled patients to better recognize disease risks and actively engage in self-management strategies, leading to more effective control of various cardiovascular risk factors.

Additionally, we found that the BP control rates showed a positive trend in the elderly (≥ 65y) and non-Hispanic White populations compared to younger people and other groups, which aligns with findings from a previous study on BP control rates [37, 38]. These disparities suggest that developing more targeted intervention strategies for younger populations and ethnic minorities remains a significant challenge. Meanwhile, populations with comorbid diabetes or CKD status have shown positive changes in BP control, which may be related to increased awareness among these chronic disease populations in recent years, enabling effective specialist diagnosis, treatment, and recommended medication prescriptions [39, 40], thereby placing greater emphasis on controlling relevant cardiovascular risk factors.

Our study’s methodological strengths deserve mention. By utilizing NHANES data, which is intended to reflect the U.S. population, our findings are applicable to the overall non-institutionalized adult population in the U.S. The data collection process was rigorously conducted by trained personnel following standardized protocols, ensuring high-quality data. Additionally, our study employed the latest MetS diagnostic criteria and analyzed nearly two decades of survey data, allowing for robust longitudinal observation of BP control trends among MetS patients.

However, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design of NHANES data precludes the determination of causality for the observed trends. In addition, due to the outbreak of the COVID-19 pandemic, data collection for the 2019–2020 NHANES was prematurely terminated. As a result, our analysis utilized combined data from 2017 to March 2020, which may limit the representativeness of the most recent years. This methodological adjustment and the shortened sampling period could have introduced temporal bias into trend estimates for the final survey cycle. Moreover, we were unable to include all potential factors influencing BP control, such as health conditions and comorbidities not captured by NHANES, as well as important confounders like smoking and physical activity, which may have affected the accuracy of our results. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into patterns of BP control among MetS patients in a nationally representative large sample.

Conclusion

From 1999 to 2020, significant progress has been made in BP management among MetS patients, with overall control rates showing a continuous upward trend, albeit with a slight stagnation in 2010. These findings highlight the need for continued efforts to improve BP control while addressing disparities in healthcare and maintaining vigilance regarding the rising prevalence of MetS.
