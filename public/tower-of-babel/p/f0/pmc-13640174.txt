Insights into programmed cell death from multiple imaging modalities
Authors: Siqi Li, Xinyang Zhang, Dingding Li, Yiwen Hou, Yi Qian, Jinghan Xu, Qihong Gu, Minfeng Yang, Minjie Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Programmed cell death (PCD) is a fundamental, genetically regulated process crucial for development and homeostasis in multicellular organisms. Despite its importance, studying PCD presents significant challenges. Traditional methods relying on static, fixed samples cannot capture the dynamic progression of PCD and rarely reproduce the complex in vivo microenvironment. This review provides a systematic comparison of molecular probes and imaging modalities for detecting and differentiating major PCD subtypes, including apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis. We systematically summarize recent advances, emphasizing their utility for imaging‑based identification of distinct cell death forms rather than solely focusing on dynamic visualization. Practical guidance is offered for selecting appropriate probes and imaging platforms according to specific biological questions. By enabling real-time monitoring of hallmark markers and deep-tissue dynamics in living organisms, in vivo imaging provides novel insights and advanced technical support for elucidating the biological roles and functional significance of PCD. Nevertheless, this review highlights that the current strength of these technologies lies primarily in their ability to detect and differentiate PCD subtypes, with real‑time monitoring of full spatiotemporal dynamics remaining an ongoing challenge.

Introduction

In multicellular organisms, the precise regulation of cellular events, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and death, is fundamental to the growth, development, and the maintenance of the physiological equilibrium. Cell death is a critical biological phenomenon essential for eliminating senescent, damaged, or potentially dangerous cells, thereby ensuring tissue integrity and systemic homeostasis [1]. Traditionally, cell death is categorized into two broad types: programmed cell death (PCD), a regulated, encoded process, and unprogrammed death, which often occurs accidentally in an uncontrolled manner. PCD is a fundamental process in developmental biology and physiology. It is an active, genetically regulated, and highly orchestrated form of cellular demise that enables organisms to shape tissues, control cell numbers, and respond to external stimuli and internal damage [2]. This process encompasses several distinct subtypes, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis (Fig. 1) [3–18]. Each subtype is initiated and executed through unique molecular signalling pathways; for instance, caspase activation in apoptosis [19, 20], gasdermin pore formation in pyroptosis [21, 22], and RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL activation in necroptosis [23, 24]. However, they all share certain defining features of PCD, including increased cellular volume, swelling of organelles, and eventual disruption of the plasma membrane [2].Fig. 1Historical development of programmed cell death

Historical development of programmed cell death

For decades, researchers have observed and analyzed the molecular mechanisms underlying PCD through microscopic observation and biochemical analysis of fixed samples. However, this is essentially a static endpoint analysis and cannot reveal the dynamic patterns and spatiotemporal progression in a living environment. Meanwhile, the in vitro experimental system cannot fully simulate the complex microenvironment in vivo [25]. The emergence of advanced in vivo imaging technologies has bridged this critical methodological gap, enabling a shift from static analysis to dynamic, longitudinal studies [26]. This transformation includes several key developments: the design of specific fluorescent molecular probes and genetically encoded biosensors for real-time tracking of PCD-specific hallmark events, such as caspase activity [27]. The application of deep-tissue modalities, like two-photon excitation and photoacoustic imaging [28] for non-invasive, quantitative monitoring within deep-seated organs, and the integration of multimodal imaging, has also expanded the observation scale from the macroscopic scale to the microscopic scale.

Unlike previous reviews that have focused on either the molecular mechanisms of PCD or individual imaging techniques in isolation, this review provides an integrated comparison of molecular probes and multiple imaging modalities across apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis. We concisely describe each pathway and then systematically evaluate in vivo imaging techniques (nuclear, ultrasound, NIR‑II, photoacoustic, microendoscopy) with emphasis on their ability to detect and differentiate PCD subtypes. Practical guidance is provided for selecting appropriate tools based on specific biological questions. While capturing full spatiotemporal dynamics remains challenging, we also highlight the potential of these techniques for longitudinal and real‑time monitoring. Our aim is to offer a useful resource for researchers seeking to apply imaging technologies to PCD research.

The molecular mechanism of PCD

Apoptosis

Apoptosis, the most representative and extensively studied form of programmed cell death, has long been regarded as a highly conserved, immunologically silent mechanism involved in cellular clearance that does not provoke inflammation [19, 29, 30]. However, emerging evidence indicates that under certain conditions (e.g., specific death stimuli or cellular contexts), apoptosis can also elicit adaptive immune responses [31, 32]. Several stages have been implicated in its morphological evolution. After cellular shrinkage and loss of intercellular adhesion, cytoplasmic density increases. Thereafter, plasma membrane blebs form, followed by disruption of the nuclear envelope and nucleolus, leading to cellular fragmentation into apoptotic bodies. Conceptually, apoptosis proceeds through four overarching phases: reception of death signals, coordinated actions among apoptotic regulators, activation of cysteine-aspartate proteases (caspases), and entry into a self-propagating execution cascade. Apoptosis is initiated by two tightly coordinated signalling routes: the intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway and the extrinsic (death-receptor) pathway, with caspase proteases serving as the core executors [2, 33].

The intrinsic pathway is triggered by intracellular signals, such as DNA damage, oxidative stress, or chemotherapeutic agents. This pathway relies on stress-induced disequilibrium within the BCL-2 protein family, which can be divided into three categories that control and balance each other: anti-apoptotic members (e.g., BCL-2, BCL-XL, BCL-w, MCL1, and BFL1), pro-apoptotic members (e.g., BAK, BAX, and BOK), and the sentinel BH3-only proteins (e.g., BIM, PUMA, and BID) [34]. Cellular stress activates BH3-only proteins, which then neutralize the anti-apoptotic members and/or directly activate BAX and BAK. This triggers BAX/BAK oligomerization and mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP). This irreversible, node-like event releases proteins, particularly cytochrome c, from the mitochondrial intermembrane space into the cytosol. Cytochrome c promotes the assembly of apoptotic protease-activating factor 1 (APAF1) with initiator caspase-9 to form the apoptosome. The apoptosome activates downstream effector caspases, specifically caspase-3 and caspase-7, to systematically degrade key cellular substrates. This leads to the appearance of hallmark apoptotic features [35, 36].

The extrinsic pathway is initiated by extracellular signals: death ligands (e.g., FasL and TNF-α) bind to their cognate death receptors on the plasma membrane (e.g., Fas and TNFR1). This leads to the recruitment of adaptor proteins, such as Fas-associated protein via a death domain (FADD) and initiator caspases, to form the death-inducing signalling complex (DISC), which subsequently activates effector caspases [37]. Importantly, the two pathways intersect: in certain cell types, caspase-8 cleaves the BH3-only protein BID, generating its truncated, potent form (tBID). tBID engages the mitochondrial pathway, forming an irreversible feed-forward loop that amplifies apoptotic signalling [38].

The core of the apoptotic process lies in the proteolytic functions of caspases. The initiator caspases (caspase-8/-9/-10) activate the effector caspases (caspase-3/-6/-7), which subsequently cleave more than 600 cellular substrates [39]. In particular, they cleave structural proteins, such as nuclear lamins, leading to nuclear fragmentation, inactivation of DNA repair enzymes, such as PARP, and activation of caspase-activated DNase (CAD) through cleavage of its inhibitor, ICAD [40]. Several signature events are associated with these cascades. Gold-standard readouts comprise caspase activation and the externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS) from the inner to the outer leaflet of the plasma membrane, an “eat-me” signal for phagocytes [41]. FRET-based probes or fluorogenic substrates can help monitor caspase activity in a real-time manner. PS exposure, an early apoptotic event, is commonly detected using annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) staining [42]. Research on apoptosis not only guides early cancer detection and improves survival but also advances the study of autoimmune diseases [43] (Fig. 2).

Necroptosis

As a regulated form of programmed necrosis, necroptosis is activated as a crucial alternative when apoptotic cell death is blocked. It clears cells and activates the immune system through the release of their contents. Morphologically, it resembles necrosis, characterized by a marked increase in intracellular peroxide levels, heightened phosphorylation of mitochondrial membranes, and enhanced plasma membrane permeability [44, 45]. This progression culminates in cell swelling, plasma membrane rupture, and the leakage of intracellular contents (e.g., HMGB1, ATP, and inflammatory factors) into the extracellular space, thereby potently triggering inflammatory and immune responses [46–48]. Thus, unlike apoptosis, which is immunologically silent, necroptosis is intrinsically a pro-inflammatory process.

The initiation of necroptosis is contingent upon a precise set of molecular conditions. When cellular apoptosis is inhibited in response to inflammatory, oxidative, or ischemic stress, necroptosis occurs as an alternative cell death pathway. Conceptually, the process can be summarized into an orchestrated sequence of three stages: reception of specific death signals during apoptotic deficiency, coordinated assembly of the necrosome core complex, and the terminal execution phase characterized by plasma membrane disintegration. This process is mainly initiated when death receptors, such as TNFR1, or pattern recognition receptors, such as TLR3 and ZBP1, are activated, while the function of caspase-8, the apoptotic executor, is inhibited [49].

Necroptosis can be triggered by members of the tumor necrosis factor family (such as TNF), Toll-like receptors (TLR3 and TLR4), and specific nucleic acid sensors, including ZBP1 and potentially RIG-I and MDA5 [50–52]. While TNF is traditionally known to induce apoptosis, this process highly relies on caspases. In contrast, TNF-mediated activation of necroptosis requires the suppression of caspase-8 activity, which promotes the assembly of the RIPK1–RIPK3 signalling platform, also known as the necrosome.

Ligands, such as TNF-α, FasL, and TRAIL, bind to TNFR1 [53]. TNFR1 recruits a series of proteins to form two complexes with opposite signal transduction on the cytoplasmic side to induce necrotizing apoptosis [54]. Complex I is more commonly observed, which includes the NFR-related death domain (TRADD), TNFR-related factor 2/5 (TRAF2/5), apoptosis protein 1/2 inhibitors (cIAP1, cIAP2), receptor-interacting protein kinase 1 (RIPK1), immunosuppressive acidic protein (IAP), linear ubiquitin chain assembly complex (LUBAC), etc. RIPK1 can be ubiquitinated by cIAP1/2 or deubiquitinated by cylindromatosis (CYLD). After polyubiquitination, RIPK1 will form complexes and activate signalling pathways mediated by nuclear factor -κB (NF-κB) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) (such as JNK and p38), thereby inhibiting cell death. Deubiquitination will promote the dissociation of TRADD and RIPK1 from complex I, thereby triggering apoptosis or necroptosis. Complex II is formed through the release of receptor-related complexes in TNFR1 and the recruitment of FADD and procaspase 8 [55]. There are two types of complex II. When caspase-8 inactivates RIPK1 and RIPK3, complex IIa can trigger apoptosis. After caspase-8 inhibition, RIPK1 and RIPK3 bind to each other through their respective RIM domains. RIPK3 undergoes auto-phosphorylation, recruits and phosphorylates MLKL. Complex IIb, which is the necrotic body, triggers necrotic apoptosis [56].

Overall, MLKL activation by RIPK3 is a defining event in necroptosis, and the expression and modification of key pathway components, such as RIPK1, RIPK3, and phosphorylated MLKL (at Ser358 and Thr357), can be detected using Western blotting, immunohistochemical staining, or flow cytometry [57]. Furthermore, a high ratio of RIPK1 to procaspase-8 suggests a cellular context permissive for necroptosis. Notably, the molecular components of necroptosis can be specific to a cell type or disease condition. For example, a recent study demonstrated that in mouse and human cardiomyocytes, RIPK1 is absent, and ZBP1 directly activates MLKL downstream to induce necroptosis [58]. Physiologically, necroptosis has been implicated in various inflammatory and pathological processes, including ischemia reperfusion injury, inflammatory bowel disease, psoriasis, and pancreatitis-associated tissue injury [58]. Pyroptosis

Pyroptosis, also known as inflammatory programmed cell death, is a unique cell death program identified in response to the lysis and death of mouse macrophages caused by invasive pathogenic bacteria (e.g., Shigella flexneri and Salmonella typhimurium). It is different from apoptosis that maintains membrane integrity and immune silencing, also distinct from other forms of necrosis. It is a regulated cell death that relies on the formation of plasma membrane pores by Gasdermin family proteins [59, 60]. The core feature of pyroptosis is the formation of plasma membrane pores, which allows the release of large amounts of pro-inflammatory factors (e.g., IL-1β and IL-18), thereby triggering a strong inflammatory response [2, 60].

Pyroptosis is caused by the activation of inflammasomes and is an important innate immune response of the body, playing a significant role in inflammation, immunity and the fight against infection. It mainly has two activation pathways: the classical pathway and the non-classical pathway, which eventually converge in a common execution stage. Classical pathways mainly respond to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). When cells sense danger signals (such as bacterial flagella, viral RNA, ATP, etc.), pattern recognition receptors in the cytoplasm (such as NLRP3, AIM2, NLRC4 / AIM2 and pyrin) assemble into a multi-protein complex called an inflammasome, which recruits and activates caspase-1 [61]. After activation of caspase-1, a heterodimer with enzymatic activity is formed, which can specifically cleavethe GSDMD in the Gasdermin domain protein family to release the N-terminal domain [62]. This domain translocated to the inner side of the cell membrane and is prone to oligomerization to form pores, leading to ion imbalance, water influx, cell swelling and membrane rupture, resulting in pyroptosis of the cell. Activated caspase-1 can also cut pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their mature and biologically active forms. The mature IL-1β and IL-18 are released in large quantities outside the cells through the pores, which can rapidly recruit and activate immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages, directly triggering a strong inflammatory response. The non-classical pathway is an indirect pro-inflammatory approach, mainly targeting intracellular lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which is an important defense mechanism against Gram-negative bacterial infections [63]. When Gram-negative bacteria invade the cytoplasm, their cell wall component LPS is directly recognized. Human caspase-4/5 or mouse caspase-11 directly act as intracellular LPS receptors. After binding with them, oligomerization and self-activation occur. Activated caspase-4/5/11 cleavedGasdermin D, also causing the formation of membrane pores in its N-terminal domain, leading to osmotic lysis of cells and triggering a special potassium ion efflorescence [64]. This potassium ion outflow activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, which in turn activates caspase-1, and then caspase-1 processes and releases IL-1β and IL-18. In addition to GSDMD, another gasdermin family member, GSDME (also known as DFNA5), has been identified as a key executor of pyroptosis. Unlike GSDMD, which is cleaved by inflammatory caspases, GSDME is specifically cleaved by effector caspase-3 during apoptosis. Upon activation of caspase-3, it cleaves GSDME at the Asp270 residue, releasing its cytotoxic N-terminal pore-forming domain. This domain then oligomerizes and inserts into the plasma membrane, forming pores that cause cell swelling and lysis, thereby converting non-inflammatory apoptosis into secondary pyroptosis. This process is particularly prominent in certain cancer cells treated with chemotherapeutic agents and in some normal tissues such as the small intestine [66, 67]. The discovery of GSDME-mediated pyroptosis reveals a direct mechanistic link between the apoptotic and pyroptotic pathways, and highlights that caspase-3 activation can lead to either apoptosis or pyroptosis depending on the expression level of GSDME [68, 69] (Fig. 4b).

Other pathways involved in programmed cell death

Apoptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis are the three most important types of PCD, and their manifestations vary greatly in different conditions. In physiological conditions, apoptosis is the main type of PCD. Pyroptosis and necroptosis are significantly upregulated in immune and inflammatory responses, making them the dominant types of PCD. Therefore, the development of different pathways of PCD depends on environmental changes. Thus, there are regulatory methods beyond the three types of PCD that play specific roles in physiological and pathological conditions. Thus, expanding the discussion to cover these patterns can help better understand the process of PCD.

Ferroptosis, first proposed in 2012, is a regulated, non-apoptotic form of cell death caused by iron-dependent oxidative damage [70–72]. It is associated with the massive accumulation of lipid peroxides on the cell membrane, leading to unique atrophic morphological changes in the cell membrane. During ferroptosis, mitochondria will continuously shrink, mitochondrial cristae will decrease, and mitochondrial membrane density will increase. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is the core factor against ferroptosis, which uses glutathione to reduce lipid hydroperoxides to non-toxic alcohols. Inhibition of GPX4 activity, depletion of its substrate glutathione, or excessive generation of lipid peroxides through the iron-mediated Fenton reaction can all trigger iron-associated ferroptosis. Unlike apoptosis, ferroptosis occurs independently of caspase. Unlike necrotic cell death, ferroptosis does not rely on RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL. Iron-associated cell death is associated with various pathological processes, including neurodegenerative changes, such as Parkinson’s disease [73–75], ischemic organ damage, such as heart and kidney infarction [76, 77], and tumor suppression [78, 79]. It is worth noting that certain types of cancer cells with high metastatic potential or acquired resistance to chemotherapy and targeted therapy are more susceptible to iron-associated cell death, making it a promising therapeutic target [80].

Lysosomes are considered key organelles involved in degradation. However, accumulation of intracellular stress, metabolic abnormalities, DNA damage, or certain toxins, drugs, and heavy metals can affect the stability and function of lysosomes and impair the integrity of the lysosomal membrane, resulting in lysosome-dependent cell death (LDCD) [81]. Rupture of the lysosomal membrane leads to the release of digestive enzymes into the cytoplasm. These enzymes digest other cellular components and destroy cellular structures, such as the cytoskeleton and cell membrane, resulting in cell death. Crucially, lysosomal membrane permeation can also amplify cell death signaling in apoptosis, autophagy-dependent cell death, and ferroptosis, thereby increasing the complexity of the cell death pathway [82]. For instance, the release of cathepsins activates the lysosomal apoptotic pathway by cleaving the Bid protein and degrading the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 homologous protein [83].

Neutrophil extracellular trap necrosis (NETosis) is a highly specialized form of cell death unique to neutrophils [84]. Multiple pathogens or host-derived inflammatory signals trigger this phenomenon, initiating a cascade reaction centered on the overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) after activation of cell surface pattern recognition receptors, such as cytokine receptors, Fcγ receptors (FcγRs), and Toll-like receptors (TLRs). ROS-driven peptidyl arginine deiminase 4 (PAD4)-mediated histone citrullination leads to vigorous depolymerization of nuclear chromatin. Meanwhile, activation of neutrophil elastase (NE) and other granular proteins, with the assistance of the pores formed by gasdermin D (GSDMD), can promote the dissociation of the nuclear membrane from the granular membrane. This enables the dissociation of chromatin to mix with various antibacterial proteins in the cytoplasm. Finally, it completely ruptures the cytoplasmic membrane, releasing this mixed reticular structure to the extracellular space and forming neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) [85]. NETs comprise DNA as the backbone, as well as toxic proteins, such as histones, NE, and myeloperoxidase (MPO), thereby physically capturing and efficiently killing pathogens. The occurrence of NETosis is a dynamic and plastic process. However, this mechanism of action is also a “double-edged sword” [86]. Excessive or uncontrolled release of NETs can damage host tissues and is closely associated with various pathological processes, such as thrombosis, autoimmune diseases, and chronic inflammation [87–89]. In conclusion, the concept of PCD has advanced, which indicates that organisms possess complex and multi-functional self-elimination mechanisms for their own development (Fig. 5). The research strategies for PCD

The demands and challenges of PCD regarding in vivo imaging

While the molecular machineries of PCD have been extensively characterised and well-studied so far, a comprehensive understanding of their spatiotemporal dynamics within the intact physiological context remains a formidable challenge. Traditional endpoint assays offer only static snapshots of these dynamic processes, failing to capture the initiation, propagation, and resolution of PCD in a real-time mode. The advent and integration of advanced in vivo imaging technologies has helped bridge this critical gap, transforming our observational capacity from static analysis to dynamic, longitudinal investigation and enabling multi-scale visualization from molecules to living organisms. However, real-time visualization relies on specific molecular probes to label PCD events and imaging patterns capable of capturing these signals deep within living tissues. Therefore, the research strategy of PCD relies on the synergy between molecular detection and advanced imaging. The following section will first provide an overview of the main categories of molecular probes for PCD, and then discuss the key imaging modes that enable their in vivo application.

Molecular probes for detecting PCD

Effective labeling is essential for visualizing PCD in living organisms, whether in vitro or in vivo. Therefore, the development and optimization of molecular probes constitute the foundation for the specific detection of PCD. The plasma membrane of eukaryotic cells is a highly specialized and asymmetrically distributed phospholipid bilayer [90]. PCD brings significant changes in cellular morphology and composition. A key early morphological change during apoptosis is the translocation and externalization of anionic phospholipids, such as phosphatidylserine (PS) [91]. This loss of membrane asymmetry occurs early in the cell death cascade. Due to the broad expression and accessibility of PS, researchers have developed probes targeting the loss of membrane asymmetry as primary tools for cell death imaging. These probes include annexin V [92], synaptotagmin I derivatives [92], lactadherin [93, ], PS-binding peptides, monoclonal antibody fragments [94, 95], Zn-DPA complexes, and duramycin [96, 97]. Among these probes, 99mTc-labelled annexin V (99mTc-i-annexin V) is currently the only agent that has been extensively studied and advanced to the clinical stage. However, this class of probes exhibits significant non-target radiation loads, particularly in the kidneys and liver, and cannot reliably distinguish apoptosis from necrosis since PS exists on necrotic cells as well [91, 98, 99]. These limitations have hindered the development of novel 99mTc-annexin V mutants with reduced renal retention and decelerated the exploration of PS-targeted peptide probes anticipated to offer simpler synthesis and faster clearance [100, 101].

Activity sensors targeting the core molecule caspase-3 are among the most widely used and relatively specific probes for the identification of apoptosis, as they can identify apoptotic cells before obvious morphological changes [90, 102, 103]. Current strategies often utilize caspase inhibitors or substrates to synthesize various radiolabeled ligands. For example, 18F-ICMT-11 is a small-molecule caspase-3 inhibitor that can specifically distinguish apoptotic cells from necrotic ones [104]. However, its application is constrained by limited binding site availability, as its accumulation depends on both the concentration of active caspase and the binding affinity of the inhibitor. Its analogues, such as FITI, have been developed to obtain more favourable biodistribution [105, 106]. Additionally, the development of radiolabeled caspase substrates, such as 18F-CP18 and 18F-C-SNAT4, offers a promising alternative to overcome the issue of binding site saturation. Recently, an improved tracer targeting caspase-3, namely 18F-C-SNAT4, has been developed for monitoring therapeutic responses in mouse models [107]. The tracer exhibited enhanced serum stability and high sensitivity in detecting apoptotic cells. A Phase I/II clinical trial [108] is currently underway to investigate the biodistribution and pharmacokinetics of this radiotracer among patients suffering from lung cancer.

Beyond the well-established probes for apoptosis, the molecular toolkit has grown significantly for imaging other modalities of programmed cell death, enabling a more comprehensive view of cell death in vivo. For necroptosis, in which the core event is MLKL phosphorylation by RIPK3, the gold-standard detection method remains the immunodetection of phosphorylated MLKL (p-MLKL) in vitro [109]. The low permeability of antibodies makes in vivo application challenging. Thus, studies are actively searching for small-molecule inhibitors or activity-based probes that target RIPK3 kinase activity and genetically encoded FRET-based reporters to visualize necroptosis initiation in live models, offering a promising strategy for future in vivo imaging [110]. Pyroptosis, a highly inflammatory form of cell death, is characterized by the cleavage of gasdermin family proteins (primarily GSDMD) by inflammatory caspases (caspase-1/4/5 in humans, caspase-11 in mice). The cleaved N-terminal fragment of GSDMD (GSDMD-NT) oligomerizes and forms pores in the plasma membrane. Therefore, antibodies specifically targeting the GSDMD-NT fragment provide direct molecular evidence of pyroptosis, analogous to p-MLKL for necroptosis [22]. FRET-based probes containing the caspase-1 cleavage sequence (e.g., YVAD) can specifically report inflammatory caspase-1 activation, which is a key feature of the canonical pyroptosis pathway. However, it should be noted that pyroptosis can also be triggered via caspase-3-mediated GSDME cleavage, limiting the specificity of YVAD probes for distinguishing pyroptosis from apoptosis. Furthermore, the pores formed by GSDMD-NT allow the influx of membrane-impermeant dyes, like PI or SYTOX green. While not specific to pyroptosis, a rapid PI/SYTOX signal coupled with GSDMD cleavage can strongly confirm pyroptotic cell death

The choice of probe target entails a fundamental trade‑off between specificity and applicability. PS‑targeting probes are pan‑PCD but cannot distinguish subtypes; caspase‑3 probes are apoptosis‑specific but may cross‑react with GSDME‑mediated pyroptosis; p‑MLKL probes are necroptosis‑specific but face antibody permeability issues. Activity‑based probes (caspase, p‑MLKL) capture a narrow execution phase, whereas PS probes detect a broader window from early to late death. Radiotracers offer excellent depth but suffer from renal/hepatic background; optical probes provide better resolution but limited penetration. 99mTc‑annexin V has reached clinical trials, whereas most emerging probes remain preclinical. Readers should note that no single modality excels in all categories; the optimal choice depends on the specific biological question.

Advanced imaging modalities and their applications in PCD research

Although molecular probes provide sufficient specificity for differentiating PCD subtypes, their in vivo application relies on imaging techniques that can capture these signals deep within living organisms. The synergistic association between probes and imaging modalities is crucial for transforming molecular specificity into observable dynamic activities. The following sections provide a comparative overview of major imaging platforms, emphasizing their ability to detect and differentiate PCD subtypes, while also highlighting their potential for longitudinal and real‑time monitoring where applicable.

As the cornerstone of molecular imaging, nuclear imaging technology encompasses several modalities, such as X-ray tomography (XCT), positron emission tomography (PET), and nuclear magnetic resonance computed tomography (NMR-CT) [111]. It acquires internal data of objects by attenuating the law of nuclear radiation physical quantities and reconstructs tomographic images in combination with computer processing. Integrating their quantitative nature with the potential of whole-body scanning, they can serve as powerful clinical and preclinical tools for non-invasive assessment of PCD in vivo. This imaging technique detects radiation emitted from system-managed radiolabeled probes that accumulate at specific molecular active sites [112, 113].

In addition to the PS and caspase-3 targets mentioned above, new radioligands are expanding the range of PCD nuclear imaging. For instance, another “eat me” signal, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), which is translocated during apoptosis, can be targeted [114, 115]. Radio-labelled duramycin, a peptide with high affinity for PE, has shown great potential as a SPECT tracer (99mTc-duramycin). Specifically, it can be applied to image cell death and tumor response to treatment in myocardial infarction, providing earlier signals before the PS-targeted probe response [116, 117]. Another probe targets transglutaminase 2 (TG2), a cross-linked protease that is activated in both apoptosis and necroptosis [118]. This probe rapidly accumulates in the tumor cell death zone after treatment, providing a broad-spectrum cell death imaging method. For specific imaging of necrotic tissues, researchers have focused on the development of radiolabeled small molecules, such as RIPK1 [119]. Although it is still in the early stage of clinical development, a PET probe targeting RIPK1 has the potential to uniquely visualize this inflammatory cell death pathway in vivo [120].

High sensitivity and in-depth quantification are the main advantages of nuclear imaging. However, compared to MRI or CT, it offers relatively low spatial resolution and usually demands hybrid imaging (PET/CT or PET/MRI) for precise positioning [121, 122]. In addition, the use of ionizing radiation and radiochemistry limits its application in longitudinal studies. Unlike endpoint histology, PET/SPECT enables longitudinal tracking of PCD evolution in the same animal, revealing the onset, peak, and resolution phases of cell death in response to therapy, information that is lost in single-time-point analyses. Ultrasound imaging uses reflections of high-frequency sound waves to provide real-time, high-resolution data, without ionising radiation. Although traditional B-ultrasound and Doppler ultrasound can effectively determine tissue morphology, vasculature, and blood flow, they lack sufficient molecular specificity for directly detecting early PCD events. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), especially ultrasound molecular imaging (UMI), represents a powerful imaging method that can help address this deficiency [123].

The key to US imaging lies in the contrast agent, and the most common contrast agent is the microbubble contrast agent. When these microbubble contrast agents are injected intravenously, they can circulate through the vascular system and provide powerful sound wave backscattering to enhance blood flow and tissue contrast [124]. By functionalizing microvesicles with ligands, they can be designed to target molecular features on the vascular endothelium that are upregulated in adjacent cellular processes, including PCD. For instance, in cancer treatment, tumor vascular endothelial cells may express PS or vascular adhesion molecules, reflecting the presence of apoptotic tumor cells around them. A new strategy employs dynamically responsive microbubbles [125]. Exposure to specific enzymes released during PCD, such as activated caspases, can alter the acoustic characteristics of microbubbles, such as their resonant frequency or fragmentation, thereby providing an activating signal [126].

Advanced ultrasonic technology has expanded its application in PCD research. It not only helps monitor microvascular dynamics but also potentially visualizes the perfusion changes and vascular remodelling in regions subjected to apoptosis [127]. In addition, the integration of ultrasound with other modes is a growing trend. For instance, microbubbles can carry drugs or tiny gene fragments and deliver therapeutic agents to specific sites and induce PCD (sonodynamic therapy) [128]. Real-time ultrasound allows visualization of vascular changes that accompany PCD (e.g., altered perfusion, microvascular remodelling), providing dynamic functional information that is lost in histological sections. Traditional imaging methods, such as US and PET, have several limitations, including poor tissue penetration, obvious light scattering, low spatiotemporal resolution, and high background autofluorescence. Due to these limitations, studies are continuously developing new technologies [129]. Among them, near-infrared II region (NIR-II) fluorescence imaging and photoacoustic (PA) imaging are non-invasive, dynamic and high-resolution PCD visualization platforms [130].

Based on the traditional near-infrared region I (NIR-I), researchers have also explored and developed molecular probes that operate in the second near-infrared window (1000–1700 nm), the near-infrared region II (NIR-II) [131]. Compared to traditional NIR-I imaging, the longer wavelength of the NIR-II region can help effectively reduce molecular scattering and light absorption in tissues and minimize self-fluorescence to the greatest extent. Therefore, NIR-II fluorescence imaging (FI) can achieve deeper penetration, higher signal-to-background ratio (SBR), and better spatiotemporal resolution [132–135], thereby precisely assessing the therapeutic effect in the tumor tissue in a real-time manner. The development of this imaging method has advanced in tandem with the design of compatible molecular probes. For instance, NIR-II probes covalently linked to caspase-3-cleavable peptides or PS-targeted motifs helped non-invasively monitor tumor apoptosis in mouse models, providing sub-millimetre resolution and SBR far exceeding that of traditional NIR-I probes [103]. This mode can be used not only for imaging but also for intervention. Tang et al. found that the NIR-II nanomedicine platform based on J-aggregates can undergo in situ secondary self-assembly after targeting tumors, transforming from nanoparticles into micrometer-sized fibre bundles. It not only achieves long-term high signal-to-noise ratio NIR-II imaging but also effectively triggers tumor cell pyroptosis in the hypoxic microenvironment through the synergistic effect of phototherapy, thereby activating the anti-tumor immune response and simultaneously offering persistent and high SBR imaging [136]. The high temporal resolution of NIR-II imaging (milliseconds) captures caspase activation kinetics in living tumors, distinguishing between rapid apoptotic execution and slower necroptotic progression in real time.

As a supplement to NIR-II imaging, photoacoustic imaging offers a unique fusion mode that combines the high contrast of optical imaging with the deep penetration of ultrasound [137, 138]. In PA imaging, a pulsed laser is absorbed by the chromophore to generate ultrasonic waves (photoacoustic signals). These waves are then detected to produce an image. This technology can directly sense intrinsic biomarkers, such as hemoglobin oxygenation, thereby monitoring vascular changes and hypoxia associated with tumor cell death, significantly enhancing PCD visualization. More importantly, using PA active molecular probes, such as near-infrared dyes with high absorbance or specially designed nanoparticles, researchers can achieve high sensitivity and specificity for detecting PCD actuators. Caspase-3-activated PA probes, which alter their absorption spectra during the cutting process, enable the quantitative mapping of apoptotic activity in deep organs, such as the liver and the brain, which cannot be easily imaged using pure optical methods [139]. Although these advanced imaging methods offer a perspective for in vivo visualization, the ultimate biological goal lies in linking these observations to the potential molecular and cellular microenvironment. This necessitates combining imaging data with multi-omics analysis techniques. PA imaging simultaneously provides molecular (probe-based) and functional (hemoglobin oxygenation) information, allowing direct correlation between PCD events and tissue hypoxia without the need for separate assays.

Although non-invasive imaging techniques, such as NIR-II fluorescence imaging and PA imaging, have greatly enhanced our ability to visualize PCD in deep tissues, significantly improving resolution and specificity, they still offer limited penetration depth and spatial resolution. These limitations prevent direct and dynamic observation of PCD in specific areas and deep structures. Researchers have adopted microendoscopic imaging to overcome this limitation and explore PCD in regions not accessible to traditional microscopes at the cellular level. This technique provides a “window” for PCD events in living tissues.

The microendoscopic imaging technology combines the features of endoscopes and microscopes, providing a new approach for visualizing PCD at cellular and subcellular levels in deep tissues and organs that are not typically accessible to traditional microscopes. This technology can also be implanted in the body for long-term studies, enabling a form of assessment similar to in vivo histological examination [140]. Similar to the imaging methods mentioned earlier, fluorescent molecular probes are crucial in the application of microendoscopy [141]. Using confocal laser endoscopy (CLE) or two-photon endoscopy techniques, researchers can detect the spatial distribution of early-stage cancer and the dynamic changes in corresponding phenotypes in a real-time manner. For example, in models of colitis or Barrett’s oesophagus, CLE was applied to map images of mucosal apoptosis by employing fluorescent apoptotic protein V or caspase-3/7 activity reporter genes administered locally or systemically. These findings directly link PCD signals to inflammatory infiltration [142, 143]. Additionally, gene-encoded biosensors for early-stage cancer, such as FRET-based caspase-3 reporter genes, can be repeatedly imaged using implantable endoscopes, thereby continuously monitoring cellular changes in the same animal [144]. This has now become a powerful method for studying neurodegenerative diseases, intestinal epithelial activation, or the dynamic phenotypes of tumor microenvironments [145–147]. Therefore, microendoscopic imaging is not only a tool for screening systemic diseases but also an effective method for mechanistic, deep tissue microscopic examinations at predetermined locations, providing unparalleled insights into the spatial and temporal coordination of cellular heterogeneity and PCD in their natural tissue environment [148–150]. Microendoscopy enables direct observation of single-cell fate decisions over days to weeks, determining whether a cell survives, undergoes apoptosis, or switches to necroptosis – information that is lost in conventional endpoint histology.

A critical comparison across imaging modalities reveals distinct trade‑offs that should guide modality selection. Nuclear imaging offers whole‑body depth and excellent quantification but suffers from low spatial resolution and requires ionizing radiation, making it suitable for clinical therapy monitoring but less ideal for routine longitudinal studies in small animals. Ultrasound provides real‑time, portable, and non‑ionizing imaging with good depth, yet its molecular specificity relies heavily on vascular targets, limiting direct visualization of parenchymal cell death. Optical techniques such as NIR‑II fluorescence achieve high spatial and temporal resolution but are depth‑limited to approximately one to two centimeters, which restricts their use in deep abdominal or brain imaging. Photoacoustic imaging partially overcomes this depth limitation by combining optical contrast with ultrasound detection, but its quantification remains sensitive to tissue optical properties and requires specialized probes. Microendoscopy attains cellular or subcellular resolution in deep tissues, yet it is invasive and has a narrow field of view, making it best suited for mechanistic studies in predefined anatomical locations rather than screening applications. Integrated approaches

PCD imaging aims not only to detect the occurrence of final events but also to gain a systematic understanding of PCD. This requires going beyond a single imaging mode. Spatial omics first provides a molecular map of the death microenvironment, and further biosensors capture the real-time dynamics of upstream signals. Thus, artificial intelligence integrates these multi-scale datasets to generate predictive models. Integrating the three elements enables the connection of PCD with dynamic environmental changes at the molecular level, facilitating detailed research.

First, spatial transcriptomics techniques, such as 10x Genomics Visium, MERFISH, and Seq-Scope, represents a significant technological innovation. It transforms the simple localization of cell death into the generation of high-resolution molecular maps of the tissue microenvironment, revealing the immunological consequences of PCD [151, 152]. While analyzing genome-wide expression, these techniques retain spatial information, enabling researchers to precisely observe cells undergoing PCD, which cells undergo PCD and which immune cells are recruited around them [153, 154]. For example, in the hemorrhagic stroke (ICH) model, spatial transcriptomics indicated that ferroptosis is the main type of PCD in oligodendrocytes [155]. In hepatocellular carcinoma, it revealed the association between the high abundance of apoptotic cells and T cell exhaustion [156]. New genes linked to specific types or stages of PCD could be discovered using this unbiased whole-transcriptome analysis method, providing ideas for the development of next-generation cancer treatment probes and targets.

At the same time, it has been discovered that PCD is not an isolated event but rather the cumulative result of changes in the microenvironment. Second, next‑generation biosensors shift focus from death executors to upstream microenvironmental signals that drive PCD. FRET‑based metabolic sensors monitor ATP/ADP ratios to distinguish between ATP‑dependent apoptosis and ATP‑deficient necrosis [157–159]. Oxidative stress sensors, such as the genetically encoded H2O2 sensor (HyPer), can visualize the outbreak of oxidative stress signals in the early stages of PCD [160]. Organelle‑targeted activity sensors for caspases or gasdermins have elucidated compartment‑specific control of death pathways [161]. Importantly, these tools have revealed the basic regulatory mechanisms involved in PCD. For instance, they have shown how intracellular SAM levels regulate the activation of the key molecule RIPK1 to exert anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects [162], or how Z-RNAs accumulation in the cytoplasm is perceived by ZBP1 to trigger necroptosis [163]. These findings not only clarify biological phenomena but also define a new class of biomarker targets for predictive imaging, enabling intervention before the initiation of irreversible execution.

Third, artificial intelligence (AI) serves as the unifying engine. The vast, complex data generated by multimodal and multi‑omics methods will increasingly rely on AI (especially deep learning and machine learning) to extract patterns beyond human cognition. Critically, these approaches are interdependent: spatial transcriptomics identifies novel biomarkers that can be converted into biosensors; biosensors provide real‑time dynamics that validate spatial omics predictions; and AI learns from both to build predictive models of PCD outcomes. When AI detects early signs of atypical cell death in a specific tissue, it could guide multiphoton microscopy to that location for higher‑resolution scanning, or even trigger local light‑activated intervention, establishing a dynamic feedback loop between observation and perturbation. Summary

In conclusion, the field of PCD imaging has matured to offer a diverse toolbox of molecular probes and imaging modalities (Table 2). This review has systematically compared these tools with an emphasis on their practical strengths and limitations for detecting and differentiating apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis. Currently, nuclear imaging (PET/SPECT) remains the gold standard for whole-body, quantitative assessment of cell death, particularly with 99mTc-annexin V, despite its limitations in subtype specificity and spatial resolution. The key practical recommendation for researchers is to align probe selection with the specific biological question. PS‑targeted probes (annexin V, duramycin) are suitable for broad detection of cell death regardless of subtype, whereas caspase‑3‑targeted probes (e.g., 18F‑C‑SNAT4, FRET sensors) are preferred for specific apoptosis identification. For necroptosis, p‑MLKL or RIPK1‑targeted agents represent emerging options, and for pyroptosis, GSDMD or GSDME‑targeted reporters are available, though each choice carries trade‑offs in specificity, temporal window, and translational maturity.

Despite significant progress, key obstacles remain, including the lack of a single probe that cleanly separates all PCD subtypes in vivo, the difficulty of imaging multiple death pathways simultaneously, the inherent trade‑off between imaging depth and resolution, and the limited clinical translation of most probes beyond preclinical stages. Addressing these challenges, together with the integration of spatial transcriptomics, real‑time biosensors, and AI‑driven data fusion, may transform PCD imaging from a descriptive tool into a predictive and personalized diagnostic platform in the coming years.
