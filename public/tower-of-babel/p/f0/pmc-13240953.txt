Immune response to rotavirus vaccines in low- and middle-income countries: Challenges and perspectives
Authors: Elena Morandi, Melania Della Peruta, Francesca Mancini, Omar Rossi, Rebecca Jo Loomis, Miren Iturriza-Gómara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRotavirus is a highly contagious pathogen and a leading cause of severe diarrhea cases in children under 5 y of age worldwide. Although effective oral rotavirus vaccines have been developed and implemented globally, their performance varies substantially by setting. In high-income countries (HICs), these oral vaccines exhibit 85–90% efficacy in preventing rotavirus-associated hospitalizations, with protection lasting until at least 2 y of age. In contrast, vaccine efficacy in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is considerably lower, ranging from approximately 45% to 65%. To address this disparity, efforts are underway to develop next-generation rotavirus vaccines. In this review, we critically examine the immune responses elicited by natural rotavirus infection and by oral vaccination in both HICs and LMICs. The goal is to identify immunological factors associated with durable protection and to define key characteristics that an improved rotavirus vaccine should possess to achieve sustained high efficacy in LMICs.KEYWORDS: Rotavirus, oral vaccines, parenteral vaccine, neutralizing antibodies, T cells, correlate of protection, low- and middle-income countries

Introduction

Rotavirus (RV) is a highly contagious enteric virus and a leading cause of severe diarrheal disease in children under 5 y of age worldwide.1 RV infects mature enterocytes in the small intestine, resulting in villus blunting and impaired intestinal absorption. To prevent RV disease, the WHO recommends inclusion of oral rotavirus vaccination (ORV) in national immunization programs. ORVs have now been introduced in more than 100 countries and, together with improved sanitation infrastructure and widespread use of oral rehydration therapy, have contributed to a substantial reduction in global childhood diarrheal mortality. Nevertheless, rotavirus remains a major public health burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where it still accounts for approximately one-third of diarrhea-related hospitalizations and causes an estimated 120,000 to 200,000 deaths annually among children under 5 y of age.2

The rotavirus genome consists of 11 double-stranded RNA (dsRNA) segments encoding six structural and six non-structural proteins.3 Structurally, RV is a non-enveloped virus composed of three concentric icosahedral layers. The innermost layer is formed by the viral protein VP2 and encloses the viral genome together with the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (viral protein 1, VP1) and the capping enzyme VP3. This core is surrounded by the middle layer or double-layer particle (DLP), which is primarily composed of VP6. The outermost layer, forming the triple-layered particle (TLP), consists of the spike protein VP4 and the glycoprotein VP7. VP4 mediates viral attachment to the host cells and is cleaved by trypsin into VP8* and VP5*, a process essential for infectivity. VP8* facilitates initial binding to host cell receptors, including sialic acid and histo-blood group antigens, while VP5* undergoes conformational changes that enable membrane penetration. VP7 assembles into trimers maintaining the structure of the virion, stabilized by calcium ions. Based on VP6 sequence diversity, rotaviruses are classified into nine groups, of which group A (RV-A) causes the vast majority of human infections.4 Further classification is based on the genetic diversity of the outer capsid proteins, VP7 (G types) and VP4 (P types), both of which elicit neutralizing antibody responses. Globally, >90% of circulating RV strains belong to six G/P combinations: G1P[8], G2P[4], G3P[8], G4P[8], G9P[8], and G12P[8]. However, strain prevalence varies by geography and over time, influenced by factors such as viral evolution, age patterns of infection, and vaccine coverage.3

Since 2004–2006, two ORVs have been widely licensed and implemented globally. The monovalent live-attenuated Rotarix (RV1, GSK) contains a human G1P[8] strain and is administered in a two-dose schedule starting at 6 weeks of age. The pentavalent Rotateq (RV5, Merck & Co., Inc.) contains five human-bovine reassortant strains expressing G1, G2, G3, G4, and P[8] antigens and is administered in a three-dose schedule beginning at 6 weeks of age. In addition, other ORVs have been licensed in selected countries, including RotaVac (Bharat Biotech International Ltd.), a monovalent G9P[11] human-bovine reassortant (strain 116E), and Rotasiil (Serum Institute of India Pvt. Ltd.), a pentavalent human-bovine reassortant vaccine (https://extranet.who.int/prequal/vaccines/prequalified-vaccines). In high-income countries (HICs), RV1 and RV5 demonstrate high efficacy (85% to 90%) in preventing hospitalizations due to rotavirus gastroenteritis (RVGE) with protection sustained through the first 2 y of life.5 In contrast, vaccine efficacy in LMICs – particularly in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia – is substantially lower, ranging from approximately 45% to 65%,1,6,7 with real-world effectiveness estimates between 46% and 52%.8 Moreover, protection appears to decline during the second year of life, and herd effects, such as reduced rotavirus hospitalizations in unvaccinated populations, have been observed primarily in high-income settings.7,9

To address the reduced performance of ORVs in LMICs, significant efforts have focused on the development of alternative vaccine strategies, including non-replicating parenteral vaccines that may bypass factors limiting oral vaccine replication and immunogenicity. However, progress is hindered by several key challenges, including the absence of a well-established correlate of protection (CoP), incomplete understanding of cellular and mucosal immune responses to RV, and limitations of current in vivo and in vitro models. In this review, we examine the immune response induced by natural rotavirus infection and by ORV, discuss the limitations of current vaccines, and review the status of injectable non-replicating parenteral vaccines (NRRV) in preclinical and clinical development, highlighting research priorities.

Natural infection immunity

Human studies show that the immunity acquired during natural RV infection does not prevent RV re-infection but does protect against symptomatic disease,9 forming the rationale for attenuated ORVs. Most infections in children aged 6 and 24 months are asymptomatic, with even higher rates below 6 months, likely due to protection by maternal antibodies.10 However, protection varies across settings. While naturally acquired immunity against re-infection appeared similar in Mexico City and Vellore (India), protection against moderate-to-severe RV gastroenteritis (RVGE) was lower in India.11 In Mexico City, two infections conferred complete protection,12 whereas in Vellore, severe disease occurred even after multiple infections13 and non-primary infections were more likely to cause disease.11 These differences may reflect variations in maternal antibody protection, transmission intensity, malnutrition, and co-infections, and may help explain differences in vaccine performance across settings.

Humoral immune response

Available evidence suggests that serum IgG and IgA antibodies are associated with partial protection from RV infection or RVGE, although findings across studies are inconsistent. For example, a study in Mexico found protection against RV infection and diarrhea was primarily associated with IgA titers,14 while a study in Texas reported that both IgA and IgG titers correlated with reduced infection and diarrhea.15 In contrast, the Danish study found no association between serum IgA or IgG titers and protection from diarrhea.16

The mechanisms underlying antibody-mediated RV protection remain incompletely understood, in part because different antibodies can target distinct viral proteins depending on the number of exposures.17 Primary RV infection mainly induces homotypic neutralizing antibodies directed against the surface proteins VP7 and, to a greater extent, VP4 proteins. Broader heterotypic responses generally arise only after repeated exposures and target conserved epitopes on VP7 and VP4 or internal viral antigens. Antibodies against VP2, VP6, and nonstructural proteins NSP2 and NSP4 are commonly detected in convalescent sera, with VP6-specific antibodies being most abundant. VP6-specific IgA has been experimentally correlated with protection in murine challenge models.18 However, because VP6 is not exposed on intact virions, these antibodies are thought to mediate protection through non-canonical, intracellular mechanisms. One study showed that anti-VP6 antibodies can bind DLPs and inhibit viral transcription.19 Consistent with this, VP6-specific IgA has been shown to transcytose across intestinal epithelial cells via the poly-immunoglobulin receptor (pIgR) and co-localize with DLPs in the cytoplasm.20 VP6-specific IgG may have a role in the intracellular neutralization through uptake into epithelial cells via the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn). Once inside the cell, the Fc region of antibodies can engage TRIM21, an E3 ubiquitin ligase that targets the antibody-bound virions for proteasomal degradation.21 Notably, these intracellular neutralization mechanisms have been demonstrated in mouse models but have yet to be confirmed in humans.

Few studies have examined the antibody responses at the intestinal mucosa, the primary site of RV infection. In Danish children with acute RVGE, elevated levels of IgA and secretory immunoglobulins were detected in duodenal fluids during the first 10 d post-infection and correlated with serum antibody levels.16 Stool-based studies have further shown that fecal RV-specific IgA correlates with duodenal IgA, making copro-IgA a good predictor of mucosal immunity. Several studies have reported an association between higher copro-IgA levels and protection from infection.22 However, the copro-IgA response appears transient, as fecal IgA titers often decline to near baseline levels within 2 to 4 months. Additionally, high IgA levels did not consistently predict protection or asymptomatic infection at the individual level, while some children with low IgA titers remained asymptomatic.22

Maternally derived antibodies provide early-life protection against RV infection, but can also interfere with ORV performance, particularly in populations where vaccine efficacy is reduced. Longitudinal studies in neonates show that RV infections are frequently asymptomatic in the first 3 months of life, with peak of RV diarrhea incidence occurring at 12–14 months of age.10 This early protection for RVGE likely reflects maternal antibodies transferred prenatally or through breastmilk. In studies conducted in Mali, Kenya, and Ghana, high neutralizing antibody titers were reported in breastfed, unvaccinated infants aged 4–10 weeks, suggesting effective maternal antibody transfer.23 While only IgG crosses the placenta,24 IgA is the predominant antibody transferred through colostrum and breastmilk during lactation.25 Breastfeeding has been associated with protection against severe RV disease, but not complete prevention of infection. In a case-control study from Bangladesh, exclusive breastfeeding was linked to reduced severe RV diarrhea, although it appeared to delay rather than prevent RV disease onset.26 Maternal antibody levels vary geographically and socio-economically. Higher IgA and neutralizing antibody titers have been reported in breastmilk samples from women in India and Vietnam compared with the USA,27 whereas more recent data showed higher RV-specific IgA levels in breastmilk from the UK than India.28 Lower maternal antibody levels in some LMIC settings may reflect nutritional stress, which has been associated with reduced secretory IgA production.

To summarize, natural RV infection induces strong systemic and mucosal IgG and IgA responses with heterotypic neutralizing potential and the ability for maternal transfer. However, antibody CoP are variable across populations, highlighting the complexity of immune protection against RV and the challenges in vaccine design.

Cellular immune response

Although cellular immunity has not been deeply investigated during RV infection, both T- and B-cell responses have been detected. In particular, chronic RV infection has been reported in two children with congenital T-cell deficiency,29 highlighting the role of cellular immunity for virus clearance and disease resolution.

A recent review summarized the main findings of T-cell induction in humans.30 RV-specific T-cells recognize epitopes shared by different RV serotypes, suggesting that the RV T-cell response is cross-reactive. T-cell immunity to RV increases with age and is boosted after repeated exposures correlating with strength and magnitude of the antibody response, although it is more transient.31 Moreover, different studies showed that children with acute RV diarrhea had a decreased proportion of CD4+ T-cell and a low CD4+/CD8+ T-cell ratio compared to asymptomatic children.32,33 The low frequency of RV-specific T-cells detected in the blood early after infection may reflect their migration from the periphery to the gut mucosa. Supporting this, a higher proportion of RV-responsive CD4+ T-cells express the gut-homing markers α4β7 or CCR9.34,35 α4β7 is an integrin that binds to the mucosal addressing cell adhesion molecule-1 (MAdCAM-1), which is expressed throughout the intestine by endothelial cells in the lamina propria and associated lymphoid tissues.36 In contrast, CCR9 is a chemokine receptor whose ligand, CCL25, is uniquely expressed in the small intestine.37 As a result, the T cells that express both α4β7 and CCR9 are specifically directed to home to the small intestine. CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses to RV in children with diarrhea include predominantly Th1 and Th17 responses, associated with increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, IL-17, and IL-6. On the contrary, the proportion of Treg cells and level of TGF‑β in the peripheral blood were significantly decreased.38 In general, a stronger Th1 and cytotoxic T-cell response was detected in a larger proportion of adults compared with children, probably due to an increased number of re-infections.33,35,39,40 In a murine Rotavirus infection model, T cells are present in the intestinal mucosa,41 and mice lacking T-cells or T-cell receptors show delayed viral clearance,42 yet are still able to clear chronic RV infection in the absence of RV-specific antibodies.43,44

High frequencies of RV-specific B cells have been identified in both children and adults. RV-binding B cells appear to represent a distinct subset that, shortly after acute RV infection, begins to express the gut-homing marker α4β7.45,46 These cells have been shown to predominantly recognize the RV VP6 protein, with VP6-specific naive and memory B cell enriched in the CD27+ IgD+ IgM+ subset.47–49 As expected, the frequency of circulating RV-specific IgA+ memory B cells correlates with plasma RV IgA levels.47

Overall, different studies underlie the activation of cellular immunity during RV infection, although it is unclear how persistent these responses are.

Oral live-attenuated vaccine immunity

Humoral immune response after ORV

All licensed ORVs have demonstrated comparable efficacy/effectiveness regardless of strain composition, suggesting that they all induce homotypic and heterotypic protection. Protection correlated mostly with RV IgG 50–52 and more strongly with IgA antibodies at both the individual and population level.53 In Malawi and South Africa, both with high disease burden populations, lower RV-specific IgA at the onset of disease symptoms was strongly associated with RV clinical vaccine failure.54,55 In particular, IgA seroconversion (defined as anti-RV IgA ≥ 20 U/mL, or three- to four-fold increase in titer) has been widely used as a CoP against RVGE among ORV vaccinated infants. For any given IgA threshold, higher protection was conferred in low child-mortality countries compared with the high-mortality ones.56 In LMICs, RV-IgA has been suggested to be a sub-optimal non-mechanistic correlate of protection, as post-immunization RV-specific IgA levels were shown to be insufficient predictors of protection in infants in Bangladesh57 and Malawi.58 Most infants with clinical RV vaccine failure showed lower immune responses to the vaccine booster doses compared to immune responses to natural infection. This could reflect vaccine-specific inhibitory factors persistent in later infancy or a higher virus inoculum in natural infection.

In an early reassortant-vaccine study related to the RV5 program, stool rotavirus IgA rose after each of three doses, and no placebo infant showed a comparable increase.59 Similarly, stool IgA was detected after RV1, but correlations with protection were weak.60

Serum neutralizing antibodies were generated after oral vaccination, but they are not a universal CoP.61 In contrast to natural infection, ORVs primarily induce neutralizing antibodies against VP7,62 and RV5-induced titers consistently underestimate vaccine protection,63 suggesting that type-specific antibodies to VP7 or VP4 epitopes alone do not fully account for vaccine-mediated protection. Indeed, following oral vaccination, VP6 remains the immunodominant target of serum and intestine IgA.64

In conclusion, post-ORV serum anti-RV IgA is the dominant measured response. Higher IgA tracks lower RVGE risk across trials, yet the same IgA threshold predicts much better protection in low- than high-mortality settings, indicating that serum IgA captures only part of vaccine-induced immunity.

Cellular immune response after ORV

There is limited information on cell-mediated immune responses following ORV vaccination. One study using VP6-7 tetramer staining showed that ORV vaccination can induce VP6-7-specific CD4+ T-cells. Although these cells were detected at very low levels, all expressed the gut-homing α4β7 marker, and some also expressed CCR9, suggesting targeting to the intestinal mucosa.35 More recent work indicates that RV1 induces varied immune responses in infants across different socioeconomic settings.65 The vaccine elicits robust, RV-specific CD4+ T-cell responses in the periphery that are biased toward a Th2 profile and display a memory phenotype. However, these responses decline to pre-vaccination levels within 8 months. CD8+ T-cells are infrequent and functionally compromised. In infants with preexisting immunity, vaccination does substantially boost RV-specific IgA or T cell responses. For B-cell immunity, protection from disease has been linked to both plasma RV IgA measured after the second dose and the presence of circulating RV memory B cells (IgD−CD27+α4β7+CCR9+) detected after the first dose.60

Limitations of current oral vaccines in LMIC

In populations with a high burden of RV disease and mortality, the reduced efficacy of ORVs is likely driven by insufficient viral replication in the gut mucosa, leading to a reduced immune activation (Figure 2). Although host-genetic factors may contribute, studies in twins suggest that genetics account for less than 30% of immune variability,66 underlying the dominant role of environmental factors. These environmental factors include altered gut microbiota, micronutrient deficiencies, intestinal damage from frequent enteric infections, preexisting immunity to cross-reactive antigens, and interference by maternal RV-specific antibodies. Together, these factors may limit replication of live-attenuated vaccine viruses and impair mucosal immune priming. In addition, prevalent immune-modulating infections (e.g. tuberculosis, malaria, HIV) and persistent inflammation may drive immune exhaustion and skew responses toward a regulatory or Th2 phenotypes, contributing to vaccine hyporesponsiveness.67 Apparent reductions in vaccine efficacy or durability in LMICs may also be partially explained by study design, as natural high exposure leads to immunity acquisition in unvaccinated children, narrowing observable difference in clinical trials.68

Conceptual overview on the immune response to oral live-attenuated vaccines (ORVs) in different settings. A) high-income countries: ORV efficiently replicate in the intestinal mucosa, leading to robust mucosal and systemic immune activation and effective protection. B) low-and middle-income countries: multiple factors can limit vaccine replication in the gut, including increased microbiota diversity, intestinal damage from recurrent enteric infections, co-existing infections, cross-reactive immunity, interference from oral polio vaccine, and higher levels of maternally derived RV antibodies. Persistent intestinal inflammation may promote immune exhaustion and skew cellular responses toward regulatory or Th2 phenotypes, reducing vaccine responsiveness.

Comparative cohort studies highlight these influences. In Malawian and Indian infants, ORV shedding and seroconversion rates were significantly lower than in European infants and were associated with higher maternal RV-antibodies in serum and breastmilk. In contrast, ORV shedding in UK infants was not inhibited despite comparable maternal antibody levels.28 Marked differences in gut microbiota were also observed: Malawian infants exhibited higher microbial diversity, Indian infants were enriched for high Bifidobacterium, and UK infants – who showed strongest vaccine responses – had the lowest Bifidobacterium abundance. Notably, asymptomatic neonatal RV infection occurred in nearly half of Indian infants and was the strongest predictor of later RV-specific IgA responses.28 Asymptomatic neonatal P[6] RV strains are structurally and functionally distinct from disease-causing strains and can replicate despite breastfeeding, conferring protection against RVGE during early childhood.69 Consistent with this, the human neonatal RV vaccine (RV3-BB) demonstrated good efficacy against RVGE in Indonesia when administered at birth70 and showed strong immunogenicity in Malawian infants.71 In this setting, higher maternal RV-specific IgG (but not IgA) was associated with reduced vaccine take after early doses, though this effect was no longer evident at study completion (week 18). Interestingly, maternal IgG and IgA antibody levels were inversely associated with IgA seroconversion but did not affect vaccine shedding, suggesting limited impact on the mucosal replication in the neonatal schedule.72

Many other interventions aimed at improving ORV performance – including additional doses, delayed schedules, higher doses, micronutrient or probiotic supplementation, and breastfeeding withholding – have generally been unsuccessful.73 Delaying the first ORV dose from 6 to 10 weeks or separating ORV administration from oral polio vaccine (OPV) modestly improved RV-IgA seroconversion,74 likely by reducing interference from maternally derived neutralizing antibodies74,75 or OPV replication.76 However, pooled analyses show only a moderate improvement in protection against severe RVGE, insufficient to justify delaying vaccination or leaving young infants unprotected.75 The limited success of these interventions in increasing ORV efficacy does not support major changes to existing immunization schedules and instead underscores the need to develop next-generation vaccines.

Beyond immunogenicity, safety and cost considerations further limit ORVs. Live-attenuated vaccines pose risks in infants with severe immunodeficiencies and carry a theoretical risk of reassortment with wild-type strains. In addition, high manufacturing costs present challenges for sustainable global supply. Together, these limitations reinforce the need for the development of alternative vaccine approaches with improved efficacy, safety, and affordability, particularly for LMIC settings.

Next-generation vaccines

Developing injectable NRRVs could overcome key limitations of ORVs by bypassing the need for intestinal replication, thereby enhancing systemic immune activation and potentially improving efficacy. Non-replicating parenteral vaccine candidates in preclinical and clinical research.

Subunit vaccines represent a safe and manufacturing friendly approach, particularly for use in LMICs. These vaccines reliably induce systemic neutralizing antibody responses, but their ability to elicit mucosal immunity or robust cellular responses remains uncertain. A leading example is the intramuscular, non-replicating trivalent VP8* subunit vaccine developed by PATH, which targets P[8], P[6] and P[4] strains and includes tetanus toxoid-derived T-cell epitope (P2) with Alum as adjuvant. Although the vaccine was generally well tolerated, a phase III efficacy trial in African and Indian infants (NCT04010448) was terminated due to a lack of superiority compared to the oral RV1 vaccine.77 While the vaccine induced neutralizing IgG responses against multiple P types, serum IgA responses were detected in only 20–34% of participants.76 The limited efficacy may reflect insufficient mucosal immune induction or the narrow antigenic focus on VP8* alone, and the underlying reasons for low efficacy are still under investigation. Consistent with this, a phase II study showed reduced RV shedding following the RV1 challenge in some vaccine groups.88 Ongoing trials are evaluating the combined NRRV and ORV administration in prime/boost regimens with RV1 or RV3-BB (NCT04344054). Beyond VP8*, additional antigens are under investigation. VP6 is particularly attractive due to its high conservation and intrinsic adjuvant-like properties, including self-assembly into nanotubular structures. In preclinical studies, VP6 combined with norovirus virus-like particles (VLPs) induced mucosal antibodies and multifunctional T-cell responses, conferring protection against RV challenge in mice.79,89 The nonstructural protein NSP4, a RV enterotoxin, has been explored, when combined with VP8* and Norovirus P particles, it provides protection against diarrhea in murine models.78,90

VLPs-based RV vaccines have shown promise in preclinical models due to their strong immunogenicity and favorable safety profile, although production costs could limit their use in LMICs. Double- or triple-layered VLPs comprising various combinations of VP2, VP6, VP7, and/or VP4 have protected rabbits and gnotobiotic piglets against RV challenge.91 VP8*-based vaccines expressed using the Norovirus P (protrude) and S (shell) platforms also induced high IgG titers and reduced viral shedding in murine models.83 A plant-produced VLP containing VP2 and VP6 from the Wa strain and VP7 from RIX 4414 demonstrated immunogenicity in rats and was safe in phase I trials in adults and infants, inducing strong, homologous neutralizing antibody responses and no heterotypic immunity.92

More recently, mRNA vaccine platforms have been explored following their success during the COVID-19 pandemic. Although costs and cold-chain requirements may challenge implementation in LMICs, mRNA vaccines offer advantages in the induction of cellular immunity and potentially targeting intestinal tissues. mRNA vaccines encoding VP8* antigen (CureVac)84,93 and VP785 have induced neutralizing antibodies, CD8+ T-cell responses, IFN-γ production, and protection in challenge models.

Finally, inactivated RV vaccines (IRV) provide another promising alternative, offering superior safety and stability while retaining the full repertoire of RV structural antigens. Two IRV candidates are currently in preclinical and early clinical development: a heat-inactivated human CDC-9 G1P[8] (Serum Institute of India Pvt. LtdZhifei Lvzhu Biopharmaceutical Co) strain86 and a Vero cell-adapted ZTR-68-A G1P[8] (Institute of Medical Biology, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences) strain.87 In animal models, the CDC-9 RV vaccine induced homotypic and heterotypic neutralizing antibodies, reduced fecal viral shedding, and generated gut-homing memory B and effector memory T helper cells (Th1 and Th2) when administered intramuscularly.94 The ZTR-68-A vaccine demonstrated favorable safety and immunogenicity in a phase I trial of healthy adults in China, eliciting neutralizing antibodies as well as RV-specific IgG and IgA.95,96

To summarize, NRRVs allow precise control over antigen selection and immune targeting, with the nature of humoral and cellular response largely dependent on the vaccine platform. While many candidates induce strong systemic IgG and neutralizing antibodies and protection in animal models, their ability to elicit durable mucosal and cellular immunity remains unclear, underscoring the need for continued clinical evaluation and optimization.

Challenges and priorities for next-generation vaccines

In the absence of a validated CoP, rotavirus vaccine efficacy has largely been assessed using clinical outcomes such as RV disease or mortality. While necessary, this approach hampers our ability to compare protective mechanisms across different vaccine platforms and obscures the immunological basis of protection. This knowledge gap is particularly problematic for the development of NRRVs, for which new research priorities must be defined (Key Highlights: Research priorities for non-replicating vaccines). Indeed, clinical trials of candidate vaccines have repeatedly shown that high antibody titers do not consistently correlate with protection. It remains unclear whether protection is mediated by neutralizing or non-neutralizing antibodies, whether serum or mucosal antibodies are more relevant, and which antibody isotypes or subclasses are critical.61 RV-specific antibodies, especially total serum RV-IgA, are widely used as a CoP for natural infection and oral vaccination.53,56 Although RV-IgA can be a valuable epidemiological marker at the population level in HICs, it performs poorly in LIMCs, where it does not reliably predict individual protection and lacks a clear mechanistic basis.61,97 Moreover, because ORVs replicate in the gut and induce intestinal IgA, whereas NRRVs will likely rely on different immune pathways for protection, it is unclear whether IgA is an appropriate CoP for parenteral vaccines. Multiple lines of evidence indicate an important role for RV-specific IgG in protection. Passive transfer of IgG suppresses or delays viral infection in non-human primate models;98 maternally derived transplacental IgG appears protective95 but interfere with ORV take; and individuals with IgA deficiency are still able to recover from RV infection.99 Since NRRVs consistently induce serum IgG responses, determining whether RV-specific IgG could serve as a meaningful CoP for parenteral vaccination is a critical research priority.

Homotypic and heterotypic neutralizing antibodies have also been proposed as candidate CoPs, but, although often associated with protection, they do not fully predict it. One limitation is that standard neutralization assays rely on two-dimensional cultures of immortalized cell lines to assess inhibition mediated by surface viral proteins VP4 and VP7.17 These assays fail to capture intracellular neutralization mechanisms, such as those involving VP6 during antibody transcytosis. Human intestinal enteroid models more closely mimic the human gut epithelium and may provide a more physiologically relevant system to assess how circulating antibodies control human infection.

Beyond antibodies, durable and maternally independent markers of immunity – such as cellular immune responses – may offer alternative CoPs. However, vaccine-induced cellular immunity remains poorly characterized. In particular, little is known about how T-cell immune homeostasis is established in early life17,96 or about the presence and function of gut-resident memory T cells following natural infection or vaccination. Circulating antigen-specific T cells or antibody-secreting B cells that traffic to lymphoid or mucosa tissues may represent promising CoP.100 In infants, such cellular responses could better reflect active immune memory and discriminate vaccine responses from passively acquired maternal immunity. Nevertheless, these responses are often transient and infants with limited prior exposure have a reduced capacity to generate detectable memory cell populations.31,96

Controlled human infection models (CHIMs) offer limited utility for identifying CoPs for RV vaccines, as they are not ethical in infants and rely on surrogate challenge models. ORVs themselves have been used as challenge agents, with fecal vaccine shedding following RV1 administration serving as a proxy for vaccine take. While ORV shedding has been associated with protection against subsequent RV diarrhea through early childhood,57 high RV maternal antibody levels or a cross-reactive immunity can inhibit vaccine shedding in LMIC infants. Moreover, the detection of shedding after later doses may reflect vaccine inoculum rather than true replication. For NRRVs, using ORVs as challenge agents would be particularly problematic, as it does not directly reflect the parenteral vaccine immunogenicity. Although reduced RV shedding was observed in infants vaccinated with P2-VP8* compared to placebo recipients,101 no clear association was found between RV shedding and anti-P2-VP8* IgA responses88 and other factors such as gut microbiome may further confound interpretation.102

Compounding these challenges is the limited translational value of existing animal models. Available in vivo models do not reliably recapitulate human RV infection or disease, and results vary widely depending on animal strain, age, and virus used. While RV-specific antibodies, especially IgA, are important in mice and pigs, gut physiology, antibody transmigration, and susceptibility to disease differ substantially across species. In mice, only mouse pups younger than approximately 15 d are susceptible to RV disease, making longitudinal immune studies difficult. Importantly, animal models do not capture key human factors influencing vaccine performance in LMICs, such as coinfections, microbiota composition, preexisting immunity, and maternal antibody interference, limiting their value for comparing ORVs and NRRVs.

In conclusion, a deeper understanding of the humoral and cellular immune responses elicited by NRRV vaccines is urgently needed across both preclinical and clinical settings. Integrating detailed immunoprofiling approaches, including systems serology, will be essential to identify reliable and mechanistic CoP that can inform future vaccine development and distinguish immune responses across HIC and LMIC environments.
