Glycaemic penalty index as an independent predictor of healthcare‐associated infection in surgical inpatients: A nested cohort study from the STOIC‐D surgery trial
Authors: Gabrielle Blomson, Rahul D Barmanray, Mervyn Kyi, Peter G Colman, Leon J Worth, Minh V Le, Emily Sun, Spiros Fourlanos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsTo evaluate the utility of the Glycemic Penalty Index (GPI), a validated tool for assessing risk of adversity associated with inpatient dysglycaemia in surgical intensive care patients, in predicting adverse clinical outcomes among surgical inpatients at a quaternary Australian hospital.MethodsThis nested cohort study analysed data from the control arm of the Specialist Treatment of Inpatients: Caring for Diabetes in Surgery (STOIC‐D Surgery) randomised controlled trial. The GPI was calculated using blood glucose values to derive patient‐day weighted mean GPI (PDWMGPI). Univariable and multivariable analyses assessed associations between GPI and the outcomes of healthcare‐associated infection (HAI) and acute kidney injury.Results691 surgical inpatients with diabetes receiving standard care were included. On multivariable analysis, increasing PDWMGPI was independently associated with healthcare‐associated infection (odds ratio 1.02 per one‐unit increase, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, p = 0.008).ConclusionThe association between PDWMGPI and HAI suggests that linearised summary glycaemic measures may support clinicians in identifying patients at increased risk of adverse inpatient outcomes related to glycaemic patterns when interpreted alongside clinical factors. The simplicity of GPI as a single score may assist risk stratification and prioritisation of specialist diabetes input in surgical inpatient settings.Keywords: acute kidney injury, blood glucose, cohort studies, cross infection, diabetes mellitus, inpatients, operative surgical procedures

What's new

What Is Already Known?

Inpatient dysglycaemia is associated with adverse clinical outcomes in hospitalised patients with diabetes, but clinically practical summary metrics to quantify glycaemic risk remain limited.

What Has this Study Found?

In a cohort of surgical inpatients with diabetes, a higher patient‐day weighted mean Glycaemic Penalty Index (PDWMGPI) was independently associated with healthcare‐associated infection.

What Are the Implications of this Study?

Linearised glucometric summary measures such as the GPI may support risk stratification and help prioritise specialist diabetes input in surgical inpatient settings.

INTRODUCTION

The high burden and growing prevalence of diabetes among hospital inpatients present significant challenges for healthcare systems, with profound implications for patient health outcomes. 1 Diabetes is associated with adverse glycaemia, with both hyperglycaemia and hypoglycaemia common among hospitalised patients and associated with suboptimal prescribing, monitoring and communication practices. 2

There is a known association between hyperglycaemia and an increased risk of healthcare‐associated infection (HAI) in hospitalised patients. 3 , 4 Further, HAIs are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, length of hospital stay and healthcare costs. 5 , 6 Early identification and management of adverse glycaemia can prevent complications and reduce the high burden of HAI. Specifically, aggressive early post‐operative glycaemic control can reduce adverse clinical outcomes, including HAIs. 7 , 8

Abnormal glucose levels in hospitalised patients can range from isolated episodes of hyperglycaemia or hypoglycaemia to prolonged patterns of glycaemic instability. To address this complexity, glucometrics provide a more comprehensive approach by quantifying glucose variability and assessing overall glycaemia, moving beyond the interpretation of individual blood glucose (BG) values. 9 With the increasing adoption of the Electronic Medical Records (EMR) and rising clinician workload, tools that reduce data overload are becoming increasingly important.

The Glycemic Penalty Index (GPI) is one such validated tool serving this purpose. GPI summarises the frequency and magnitude of both hyperglycaemic and hypoglycaemic excursions into a single value reflecting overall glycaemic burden and associated risk of glycaemia‐related adversity. 10 Initially developed in a European cohort of surgical intensive care unit patients, GPI offers an objective and clinically relevant summary measure of glycaemic burden compared with traditional metrics. However, its applicability in other clinical settings remains to be evaluated.

The Specialist Treatment of Inpatients: Caring for Diabetes in Surgery (STOIC‐D Surgery) study was a large, randomised control trial that investigated the effect of early intervention with an electronic specialist‐led “proactive” model of care on glycaemic and clinical outcomes. 7 Through utilising the data collected from this well‐characterised cohort, the present study aims to apply the GPI in the STOIC‐D Surgery inpatient cohort to identify the value of GPI in predicting risk of adverse clinical outcomes, including HAI and acute kidney injury (AKI), in general surgical inpatients.

METHODS

Study design

This study was conducted as a nested cohort study of the Specialist Treatment of Inpatients: Caring for Diabetes in Surgery (STOIC‐D Surgery) standard diabetes care (control) group to examine the association between glycaemic burden and clinical outcomes in the absence of specialist‐led glycaemic intervention. The STOIC‐D Surgery randomised control trial was performed from February 2021 to December 2021 at the Royal Melbourne Hospital (RMH), a quaternary teaching hospital. The trial protocol and major results have been previously reported. 7 In brief, the STOIC‐D Surgery trial investigated the effect of early intervention with an electronic specialist‐led “proactive” model of care.

Participants

The STOIC‐D Surgery trial included 1371 participants admitted to surgical inpatient units at RMH with either pre‐existing diabetes (excluding Type 1 diabetes mellitus) or newly detected hyperglycaemia. Hyperglycaemia was defined as at least one random BG result ≥11.1 mmol/L recorded during the admission. Participants were randomly assigned (1:1) by computer‐generated random sequence to early proactive specialist diabetes care (intervention) or standard diabetes care (control) during their inpatient stay. The trial achieved the primary outcome of a lower median patient‐day mean glucose (PDMG) in the intervention group and the key secondary outcome of a lower incidence of HAI. The present analysis was restricted to participants in the standard diabetes care (control) group.

Data collection and glucose measurement

Relevant data, including clinical outcomes, pathology results and medications, were collected by clinicians directly from the complete medical record using the REDCap electronic data capture tools and did not rely upon coding data or an automated extract alone. 11 Continuous variables are summarised as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) and categorical variables as number (percentage). BG levels were measured using networked BG meters (StatStrip; Australasian Medical & Scientific, Ltd., Nova Biomedical), which transmit point‐of‐care capillary glucose and ketone results to the EMR in real time. BG measurements were excluded after day 14 of admission to avoid undue influence by prolonged hospital stays upon summary statistics. Analyses were conducted using both unrestricted and restricted data, where restricted data was limited to BG values from admission until the day prior to the diagnosis of an HAI, in those who developed an HAI. Restricted data was assessed to identify the effect of glucose on infection risk, given that infection can stimulate hyperglycaemia. Additionally, the method described by Weinberg and colleagues for excluding repeated measurements from episodes of hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia was applied. 12

Outcomes

HAIs were defined as a sterile‐site positive culture or clinician action antimicrobial therapy for suspected infection, with onset occurring at least 48 h after admission. 7 AKI was defined as per the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) 2012 criterion 1 as a creatinine increase of ≥0.3 mg/dL (≥26.5 μmol/L) in a 48‐h period, with other criteria omitted due to their lack of applicability to this population. 13 For patients with an elevated baseline creatinine, inpatient AKI was considered only if preceded by an interim return to baseline renal function. 4

Glycemic penalty index

GPI is a summary score ranging from 0 to 100, where higher values indicate poorer glycaemic control. We calculated the GPI for each individual glucose value in our patient cohort using a formula modified from that originally developed by Van Herpe et al. 10 While the original method involved summing GPI scores across a range of values, our approach calculated the GPI for each individual glucose value. The GPI thus represents a conversion of the glucose value to a linearised representation between 0 and 100 reflective of the risk to the patient contributed by glucose at this level. Glucose levels below 20 mg/dL (1.1 mmol/L) or above 250 mg/dL (13.9 mmol/L) are considered to contribute equal risk of adversity for a patient.

BG in the STOIC‐D Surgery Trial was collected in mmol/L, which was converted to mg/dL to calculate GPI. The original GPI formula was applied to the converted data to assign a GPI to each glucose value, allowing the calculation of the patient‐day mean GPI (PDMGPI), calculated by taking the mean of all GPI results for each individual patient day. A patient‐day weighted mean GPI (PDWMGPI) was calculated by taking the mean of all PDMGPI values for an individual patient over an entire admission.

Statistical analyses

PDWMGPI was entered into regression models as a continuous exposure variable. Odds ratios, therefore, represent the change in odds of outcome per one‐unit increase in PDWMGPI. Covariable selection for multivariable models was informed by directed acyclic graphs (Figures S1 and S2) and a priori clinical reasoning to identify measurable confounders of the relationship between glycaemic burden and clinical outcomes, while minimising over‐adjustment. Multivariable analysis included clinically relevant covariables: sex, age, preexisting diagnosis of diabetes, number of inpatient procedures, treatment with insulin prior to hospital admission, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), creatinine and haemoglobin. Events per variable (EPV) were considered during model specification to assess model stability. Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for continuous variables and the Pearson χ2 test for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For descriptive analyses, available case analysis was used, with each variable analysed based on non‐missing data. Missing data were present in 21 patients for one or more variables. For multivariable regression analyses, patients with missing data in any included variable were excluded (complete case analysis). All p values were two‐tailed, with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed.

Ethics

STOIC‐D Surgery and this analysis received ethics approval from the human research ethics committee of The Royal Melbourne Hospital (Melbourne, Australia; HREC/61095/mH‐2020). A waiver of individual consent was granted based on the study design and procedures conforming to section 2.3.10 of the National Health and Medical Research Council Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research – 2007 (Updated 2018). The trial was registered in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12620001303932).

RESULTS

There were 691 patients in the standard diabetes care (control) arm from the STOIC‐D Surgery trial who were included in this study. Baseline characteristics for the cohort are described in Table 1 and included a mean age of 65.4 ± 16.0 years, 433 (63%) were male and 480 (69%) had diabetes. Overall, 108 patients (15.7%) developed a HAI with infection onset occurring at a mean of 7.0 days following hospital admission. AKI occurred in 65 patients (9.4%) during the admission. Patients who developed HAI differed from those without HAI with respect to several baseline clinical characteristics (Table S1), including longer length of stay and a higher proportion undergoing inpatient procedures. Patients with HAI also had lower HbA1c and a lower prevalence of diabetes. On univariable analysis, patients without HAI had a mean total length of stay of 7.3 days, compared with a mean of 7.0 days from admission to infection onset among those who developed HAI, suggesting a similar observation period for glycaemic data prior to outcome or discharge. Mean PDWMGPI did not differ between patients with and without HAI (49.8 ± 17.7 vs. 52.4 ± 21.3, p = 0.20). This remained true both when analysis was conducted with all BG values and when restricted to values obtained prior to the occurrence of an HAI.

Multivariable analyses were performed in 670 patients with complete data. Increasing PDWMGPI was independently associated with HAI on multivariable logistic regression (odds ratio [OR] 1.02 per one‐unit increase, 95% CI 1.01–1.04, p = 0.008), as depicted in Figure 1. On univariable analysis, there was no association between GPI and hospital‐acquired AKI. Patients with an AKI had a mean PDWMGPI of 53.1 ± 21.1, compared with 51.2 ± 20.9 for patients without an AKI (p = 0.44). On multivariable analysis, there was no association between PDWMGPI and AKI (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.99–1.02, p = 0.56; Figure S4). No other covariates were independently associated with AKI, although inpatient procedures were associated with a numerical difference that was not statistically significant (OR 1.94, 95% CI 0.90–4.65, p = 0.11).

The multivariable models included 108 HAI events and 65 AKI events with 14 parameters, corresponding to EPV values of 7.7 and 4.6, respectively. These values were below conventional thresholds, particularly for the AKI model, and results should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

DISCUSSION

This nested cohort study demonstrated an association between GPI and HAI on multivariable analysis in the control group of a randomised controlled trial of proactive glycaemic intervention. After adjustment for multiple co‐factors, higher PDWMGPI remained significantly associated with HAI risk. The analysis was restricted to the standard diabetes care group to examine the relationship between glycaemia and HAI risk without the modifying effect of specialist‐led intervention. The absence of an association on univariable analysis likely reflects confounding by non‐glycaemic factors that are strongly associated with HAI risk; adjustment for these factors unmasked the independent association between glycaemic burden and infection risk. These findings suggest that a single‐value summary of overall glycaemic burden may be useful in assessing the glycaemic contribution to HAI risk.

The value of GPI lies in its ability to consolidate multiple glycaemic events into a single composite linearised score and accounts for the greater clinical impact of hypoglycaemia than hyperglycaemia for the same numeric degree of deviation from euglycaemia. The GPI thus allows clinicians to appreciate the contribution of overall glycaemic burden to risk of adversity. This addresses the challenge of interpreting numerous inpatient glucose measurements, particularly in settings of increasing clinician workload. Integrating GPI and similar summary measures into the EMR as both a real‐time alert for individual patients and a pooled metric for post‐discharge quality improvement could enhance glycaemic management pathways (Figure 2). Given the complexity of manual calculation and adjustment for other factors, GPI's optimal utility depends on automated computation and presentation within patient dashboards alongside other indicators of glucose‐associated risk. Unlike threshold‐based measures, GPI incorporates both hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia into a single representation of glycaemic burden. Institution‐specific clinical decision support incorporating GPI may assist in identifying patients at increased glycaemic risk; however, any downstream management recommendations would need to be informed by the underlying glycaemic pattern in addition to the composite GPI value.

Compared to other glucometrics, GPI uniquely integrates both hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia into a single actionable metric, similar to the adverse glycaemic day. 14 By comparison, time in range (TIR) derived from continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) is a validated patient‐centred metric of glycaemia, increasingly recognised as superior to the historical use of HbA1c as the primary assessment of glycaemia. 15 , 16 Where TIR offers additional insight into the quality of glucose control beyond that represented by HbA1c, GPI reflects the day‐to‐day fluctuations that are lost in the simple average that is the PDMG. Two patients with markedly different glucose variability may have equivalent HbA1c values yet spend substantially different times within the target range. Similarly, patients with comparable PDMG may have different GPI values due to penalties applied for both hypoglycaemia and hyperglycaemia (Figure 3). This makes GPI a valuable alternative to TIR, particularly in inpatient settings where CGM may not be readily available. By representing the risk of adversity associated with adverse glucose values and signalling the need for intervention, GPI has the potential to improve diabetes management and reduce diabetes‐related adversity.

In our cohort, GPI as originally described was not strongly predictive of HAI risk. It is known that those who develop a HAI as inpatients have a PDMG on average only 0.3 mmol/L above those who do not. 4 The observed difference in GPI of 2.6 points between the HAI and non‐HAI groups is thus comparable and plausibly accounts for this outcome difference. While the GPI discriminates between these two values, its wide dynamic range likely reduces its sensitivity in this context. These findings suggest that recalibrating GPI to reflect the ambient inpatient glucose levels and the specific glucose thresholds that differentiate adversity in a given population could enhance its predictive performance. Machine learning approaches offer a promising method for such dynamic recalibration, with emerging evidence supporting their value. 17 Additionally, the PDMGPI was generally higher in our cohort than in the original derivation cohort. 10 This likely reflects differences in patient selection, with the original cohort comprising an unselected surgical ICU population, whereas our cohort consisted of surgical ward patients specifically selected for having diabetes or hyperglycaemia.

Strengths of this study include the use of a large, prospectively collected and comprehensive dataset. Data was collected by clinicians directly from complete medical records using REDCap tools, avoiding reliance on automated extracts or coding data, enhancing data accuracy. HAI outcomes in STOIC‐D Surgery were determined through blind clinician adjudication, further reducing bias. Multiple glucose measurement modalities were included—point‐of‐care capillary, venous and laboratory venous glucose—facilitated by networked meters providing real‐time, accurate data directly transmitted to the EMR.

Limitations include potential residual confounding influence from unmeasured clinical factors, although those with the greatest effect size were likely accounted for. The GPI is intended to support clinical decision‐making as an adjunct, rather than a standalone measure, particularly concerning HAI risk. As a single‐centre study conducted in a quaternary teaching hospital, generalisability to other settings, including outpatients or patients with type 1 diabetes, may be limited. Furthermore, being the only Australian institution with both an EMR and hospital‐wide networked glucose meters at the time of trial commencement may limit applicability elsewhere. The prevalent COVID‐19 pandemic during the trial period likely influenced the enrolled population by limiting elective surgeries, resulting in a higher proportion of acute cases. Lastly, the focus on inpatient glucose and infection risk without follow‐up on long‐term or post‐discharge outcomes limits the scope of its findings.

Future research should evaluate this association in more diverse populations and institutional contexts to determine generalisability. Investigation of EMR‐based integration across varied clinical settings, including medical inpatients and patients with type 1 or gestational diabetes, may clarify broader applicability. If GPI is to function as a clinical risk stratification tool for HAI, further work is required to determine how GPI‐informed care can optimise management, including targeted infection prevention strategies or enhanced glucose monitoring for high‐risk patients. Interventional studies assessing whether GPI‐guided care or EMR‐based display improves inpatient glycaemia and whether this translates into reductions in healthcare‐associated infection and other glucose‐modifiable outcomes are needed. Longer‐term follow‐up will also be important to assess the sustained impact of inpatient glycaemic management on patient outcomes. Alternative modelling strategies treating GPI as a non‐linear input may yield additional insight into associations between GPI and outcomes of interest.

In conclusion, this nested cohort study demonstrates that, after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates, GPI is independently associated with HAI. By simplifying glycaemic burden into an actionable summary, GPI may facilitate timely clinical responses when interpreted alongside relevant clinical factors and offer an additional tool to support the management of adverse glycaemia, potentially improving outcomes for people with diabetes (Figure 4). Given the association between HAI and prolonged hospital stays, the use of GPI may also contribute to reducing long‐term healthcare resource utilisation.
