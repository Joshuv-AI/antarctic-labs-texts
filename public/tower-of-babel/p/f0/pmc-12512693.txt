The role of peripheral blood cell analysis-derived inflammatory parameters in predicting malignant duodenal stenosis
Authors: Palidanguli pata’er, Shanshan Liu, Jiawei Chen, Qi He, Guochen Shang, Yu Jin, Jun Liu, Chaoqun Han, Shi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe unique location and complex anatomical structure of the duodenum poses challenges for the application of endoscopic biopsy. Differentiating benign and malignant duodenal stenosis remains difficult for clinicians. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a new, reliable, and cost-effective biomarker to differentiate between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis.Methods140 patients diagnosed with duodenal stenosis during endoscopic examinations and subsequently underwent biopsies or surgery for pathological analysis were enrolled in the study. Based on the pathological results, patients were classified into benign duodenal stenosis group and malignant duodenal stenosis group. Clinical data and results of the initial peripheral blood analysis were collected retrospectively and analyzed.ResultsCompared to patients with benign duodenal stenosis, patients with malignant duodenal stenosis exhibited higher neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR, P < 0.001), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR, P < 0.001), and lower lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR, P < 0.001). Moreover, an increase in age, NLR, and AST were found to be independent risk factors for malignant duodenal stenosis. Besides, NLR > 3.42 was a more dependable predictor of malignant duodenal stenosis, with a sensitivity of 80.3% and a specificity of 91.1%.ConclusionsPeripheral blood cells analysis derived inflammatory biomarkers such as NLR holds significant predictive value for malignant duodenal stenosis.

Introduction

Duodenal stenosis is typically discussed in the context of newborns, with limited data available in adults. The cause of duodenal stenosis in adults can be attributed to a range of factors, including both neoplastic and non-neoplastic conditions. Malignant duodenal stenosis occurs due to the proliferation of duodenal cancer, the direct invasion of pancreatic or biliary malignant tumors into the duodenum Non-neoplastic disease-induced duodenal stenosis is typically caused by chronic inflammation, such as peptic ulcer, Crohn’s disease, and tuberculosis [1].

Despite the utilization of current identification methods such as pathology biopsy, endoscopy, and imaging examinations, differentiating between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis remains challenging for clinicians. This challenge is particularly pronounced when imaging examinations fail to identify a primary site and endoscopic biopsies do not reveal any cancer cells. The unique location and complex anatomical structure of the duodenum poses challenges for the application of endoscopy and biopsy. Moreover, the different treatments required for benign and malignant duodenal stenosis mean that an inaccurate diagnosis could lead to delays and a worsening of the condition. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a new, reliable, and cost-effective biomarker to differentiate between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis.

Many studies have discovered a close relationship between neoplastic conditions and inflammation, with changes in both local and systemic inflammatory parameters being observed in patients with cancer [2]. Emerging evidence suggests that analyzing peripheral blood cells and derived inflammatory parameters, such as the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), could be highly valuable in predicting malignancy in tumors [3–6]. To the best of our knowledge, there is currently no available evidence regarding the analysis of peripheral blood cells and their derived inflammatory parameters in duodenal stenosis. Therefore, this study was designed to investigate the association between the analysis of peripheral blood cells and their derived inflammatory parameters and duodenal stenosis, aiming to identify simple and practical inflammatory indexes that can be used to predict malignant duodenal stenosis.

Methods

Patient selection and group assignment

We conducted a retrospective review of the clinical data of 178 patients who were diagnosed with duodenal stenosis during endoscopic examinations and subsequently underwent biopsies or surgery for pathological analysis at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, from March 2019 to March 2024. The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) Patients with incomplete clinical data: no pathological results, no blood test was conducted; (2) patients with multiple malignant tumors or hematological malignancies; (3) patients who had received systemic intervention prior to endoscopic examination (e.g. particularly anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids, or immunosuppressants etc.). (4) Patients with clinically confirmed active infection (e.g., temperature > 38 °C, positive microbial culture, or C-reactive protein (CRP) > 10 mg/L). Thirty-eight patients were excluded, resulting in a total of 140 cases being enrolled in the present study.

Endoscopic biopsy of the stenotic area should yield at least four tissue specimens per patient, prioritizing regions with the most pronounced narrowing or abnormal appearance. For surgical resection specimens, perform full-thickness sampling and embedding according to standard protocols. Biopsy specimens must contain sufficient mucosal layer and include submucosal layer whenever possible. Specimens are considered adequate if they demonstrate evaluable duodenal epithelial architecture under microscopic examination or encompass the full-thickness structure of the duodenal wall. Specimens consisting solely of necrotic tissue, blood clots, or markedly compressed and deformed tissue are deemed inadequate and were excluded during data collection. All pathological results were performed by two experienced pathologists with a track record of more than 1000 cases per year. If there is a controversial result, it will be reviewed by a third pathologist. The initial independent diagnoses of all 140 cases by the two pathologists demonstrated good agreement. Cohen’s Kappa (κ) coefficient was 0.85 (95% CI: 0.77–0.92), indicating an excellent level of agreement. Among all cases, initial diagnoses differed in 10 cases (7.1%). These cases of disagreement were submitted to a third senior pathologist for blinded review, whose diagnosis was adopted as the final result for subsequent analysis. Based on the pathological results, all enrolled patients were classified into two groups: the malignant duodenal stenosis group, comprising patients with duodenal stenosis caused by malignant diseases and the benign duodenal stenosis group, comprising patients with duodenal stenosis resulting from chronic inflammation.

Pathological diagnostic criteria

Benign duodenal stricture: Defined by the presence of chronic inflammatory changes (e.g., ulcer healing with fibrosis, granulation tissue formation), specific inflammatory conditions (e.g., noncaseating granulomas in Crohn’s disease, caseating granulomas in tuberculosis), or nonneoplastic structural alterations (e.g., Brunner’s gland hyperplasia), without evidence of malignant cytology or histology.

Malignant duodenal stricture: Defined by histological evidence of adenocarcinoma originating in the duodenum or direct invasion of the duodenum by other malignant tumors (e.g., pancreatic cancer, cholangiocarcinoma). Diagnosis is based on the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of gastrointestinal tumors, primarily confirmed by cellular atypia, histological disorganization, invasive growth pattern, and necessary immunohistochemical staining results.

Clinicopathological data collection and inflammatory parameters definition

Retrospectively collected and analyzed clinical data of enrolled patients, including demographic information and results of the initial peripheral blood analysis at admission, such as neutrophil, lymphocyte, and platelet counts, as well as markers of liver function including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and total bilirubin (Fig. 1). Blood sample collection and processing: All patients had blood samples collected via venipuncture in the early morning (7:00–9:00 AM) after fasting for at least 8–12 h (water was allowed). EDTA-K₂ anticoagulant tubes (for plasma) or serum separation gel tubes (for serum) were used for collection. After blood collection, the tubes were gently inverted and mixed several times. Plasma samples were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 Min at 4 °C within 30 Min of collection to separate the upper layer of plasma; serum samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30–60 Min, then centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 min to separate the upper layer of serum. The separated plasma or serum should be immediately aliquoted into sterile cryogenic tubes, avoiding repeated freeze-thaw cycles, and stored at −80 °C in an ultra-low temperature freezer until further analysis. The patients received no any therapeutic intervention measures before. NLR is calculated by dividing the neutrophil count by the lymphocyte count. PLR is calculated by dividing the platelet count by the lymphocyte count. LMR is calculated by dividing the lymphocyte count by the monocyte count. Although nutritional indicators such as serum albumin levels were present in some patients’ admission records, retrospective data revealed these records were incomplete and non-standardized. Consequently, this study excluded them from the primary analysis.Fig. 1Flow diagram demonstrating study design

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (No: IORG0003571). All patients signed informed consent for the endoscopy, and the data had been anonymized and de-identified.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 was utilized for statistical analysis. Measurement data following a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (𝑥̅ ± 𝑠), and independent-sample T test was used for intergroup comparisons. Intergroup comparisons were analyzed using the non-parametric rank sum test. Count data were presented as frequency and percentage (n, %), with intergroup comparisons analyzed using the χ2 test. Binary Logistic regression model was employed to analyze the influencing factors of benign and malignant duodenal stenosis. ROC curve analysis and the Youden index were used to validate the predictive value of indicators for malignant duodenal stenosis. To address potential multicollinearity issues among inflammation-related indicators (NLR, PLR, and LMR) (all of which are calculated based on the same blood routine test variables), if the Pearson bivariate correlation coefficient between independent variables exceeds 0.7, multicollinearity may exist. According to Pearson correlation analysis, the correlation coefficients between NLR, PLR, and LMR are as follows: NLR and PLR: r = 0.67 (P < 0.001), NLR and LMR: r = −0.72 (P < 0.001), PLR and LMR: r = −0.63 (P < 0.001). All |r| values were < 0.7, indicating no strong multicollinearity. Subsequently, the condition index is calculated through factor analysis, and when its value exceeds 30, it indicates strong multicollinearity. VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) is used to measure the impact of multicollinearity on the variance of estimated coefficients. When the VIF value exceeds 10, it indicates significant multicollinearity, and the variable will be excluded. In the multiple logistic regression model, the VIF values for each inflammatory marker were: NLR: 2.1, PLR: 1.8, LMR: 1.9, confirming no significant multicollinearity interference in the model. The composite predictor was constructed using a binary logistic regression model: NLR, PLR, and LMR were incorporated as continuous variables to generate a predicted probability value (i.e., linear combination: Z = −3.72 + 0.89 × NLR + 0.006 × PLR − 0.43 × LMR). A ROC curve was plotted using this probability value. DeLong’s test revealed: AUC difference between the combined model and the single NLR model = 0.005 (95% CI: −0.02 to 0.03, P = 0.68), confirming no statistically significant difference in predictive performance between the two models.A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to evaluate the predictive performance of inflammatory markers. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated, and the optimal cutoff value was determined using the Youden index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1). To ensure robustness, the 95% confidence interval (CI) for AUC was generated using the bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) method with 2000 iterations of bootstrap resampling.

Sample size explanation

As this study employs a retrospective design based on historical medical records, it was not possible to perform a prospective sample size calculation prior to the study’s commencement.

Conduct power analysis

The calculation results indicate that for detecting a significant difference in NLR between benign and malignant groups (Cohen’s d = 1.61), the statistical power of this study is 99.8%, far exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of 80% for adequate power.

Results

Etiological distribution analysis

The etiological distribution analysis of the enrolled patients in the present study is detailed in Table 1. Thirty-eight patients were excluded because of no pathological results(n = 6), no blood tests(n = 12); patients with multiple malignant tumors or hematological malignancies(n = 9); patients who had received systemic intervention prior to endoscopic examination (11). A total of 140 patients with duodenal stenosis were included, with 79 patients (56.4%) diagnosed with benign duodenal stenosis and 61 patients (43.6%) with malignant stenosis. Peptic ulcer emerges as the primary cause of duodenal stenosis, also serving as the leading factor for benign duodenal stenosis. Additionally, pancreatic cancer stands out as the most prevalent cause of malignant duodenal stenosis. Univariate analysis of factors associated with benign and malignant duodenal stenosis

Compared to patients with benign duodenal stenosis, patients with malignant duodenal stenosis are usually older (P = 0.0003) and exhibit a higher frequency of abdominal pain (P = 0.003) and weight loss (P = 0.0001) during the course of disease. Additionally, patients with malignant duodenal stenosis presented significantly higher neutrophil counts (P = 0.002) and lower lymphocyte counts (P = 0.0005) than those with benign duodenal stenosis. In comparison to patients with benign duodenal stenosis, those with malignant duodenal stenosis demonstrate elevated levels of ALT (P = 0.0004), AST (P = 0.0006), and total bilirubin (P = 0.0008). Regarding to inflammatory parameters, patients with malignant duodenal stenosis exhibited higher NLR (P = 0.0001), PLR (P = 0.0003), and lower LMR (P = 0.0001) than those with benign duodenal stenosis (Table 2).

Multivariate logistic regression of factors associated with benign and malignant duodenal stenosis

The variables that exhibited statistically significant differences in the univariate analysis were incorporated into the binary logistic regression analysis. The results indicated significant differences in age (OR = 1.072, 95% CI: 1.013–1.134, P = 0.016), NLR (OR = 2.069, 95% CI: 1.377–3.108, P = 0.0001), and AST (OR = 1.060, 95% CI: 1.004–1.120, P = 0.034) between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis. The results indicate that an increase in age, NLR, and AST levels were independent risk factors for malignant duodenal stenosis (Table 3).

The results indicated that the Area Under the Curve (AUC) for NLR, PLR, and LMR in predicting malignant duodenal stenosis were 0.873, 0.753, and 0.779, respectively. These findings suggest that NLR is a more dependable predictor of malignant duodenal stenosis, with a sensitivity of 80.3% and a specificity of 91.1%. When combining NLR, PLR, and LMR to predict malignant duodenal stenosis, the AUC and specificity increased to 0.878 and 92.4%, respectively (Table 4 and Fig. 3). The AUC of the combined three parameters was significantly higher than that of PLR and LMR alone. However, there was no significant difference compared with NLR alone (results not shown). Discussion

Limited data is available on distinguishing between benign and malignant duodenal strictures in adults, posing a significant clinical challenge. The utilization of reliable, cost-effective, and feasible biomarkers to predict malignant duodenal stenosis may aid in mitigating the risks and financial burden associated with endoscopic biopsy and imaging for patients. Our study provides evidence that the analysis of peripheral blood cells and derived inflammatory parameters, such as NLR alone or in combination with PLR and LMR, holds significant predictive value for malignant duodenal stenosis.

An interesting discovery from the current study was that patients with malignant duodenal stenosis exhibited significantly elevated neutrophil counts and decreased lymphocyte counts compared to those with benign duodenal stenosis, aligning with previous research findings [7, 8]. Neutrophils and lymphocytes in peripheral blood are known to play critical roles in the immune response to various internal environmental changes such as trauma, infection, and neoplastic processes [9]. Previous studies have suggested that changes in neutrophil counts indicate active and persistent inflammatory responses, while variations in lymphocyte counts may highlight differences in regulatory pathways in various pathological conditions [10]. Therefore, the results mentioned above indicate that both neutrophil counts and lymphocyte counts could serve as sensitive markers for distinguishing between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis, especially when the biopsy result is negative or the imaging diagnosis is unclear.

NLR has emerged as a crucial parameter in predicting the severity and progression of current pathologies in various malignancies and chronic inflammatory diseases [3–6]. Especially in neoplastic conditions, NLR has been proven to be of great importance in predicting prognosis and therapeutic response [11, 12]. In the present study, an elevated NLR was identified as an independent risk factor for predicting malignant duodenal stenosis. Furthermore, an NLR exceeding 3.42 is considered a reliable indicator for predicting malignant duodenal issues, demonstrating acceptable levels of sensitivity and specificity. The aforementioned results suggest that the NLR alone may play a crucial role in distinguishing between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis. Further studies are required to validate these results and to gain a more profound understanding of its clinical implications.

Another notable discovery from the current study is that, while no significant differences were noted in the monocyte counts and platelet counts between the two patient groups, there were significant variations in the LMR and PLR. Specifically, individuals with malignant duodenal stenosis showed higher PLR and lower LMR levels compared to those with benign duodenal stenosis. Previous studies have indicated that an elevated LMR primarily signifies infection and inflammatory conditions [13]. In the current study, it is observed that patients with benign duodenal stenosis often experience this condition due to chronic inflammation. This finding elucidates the significant rise in LMR levels among patients with benign duodenal stenosis in the present study. Furthermore, the notable elevation in PLR among patients with malignant duodenum may be associated with elevated platelet levels and lymphopenia induced by fluctuations in peripheral blood cortisol levels in individuals with malignancies [14, 15]. Moreover, in cases of benign duodenal stenosis caused by chronic inflammatory diseases, excessive platelet destruction or consumption could also result in a decrease in PLR [16]. Further studies are needed to validate these hypotheses.

In the past decades, PLR and LMR had been proven to be a valuable inflammatory marker for predicting neoplastic diseases [17–19]. Previous studies have suggested that a combination of inflammatory parameters, including NLR, PLR, and LMR, may offer a more comprehensive understanding of neoplastic conditions [20]. Yang et al. included 16 studies and found that the NLR cutoff across centers ranged from 1.75 to 3.4 (median 2.5). They only confirmed its association with prognosis after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (HR = 1.96) and did not establish a diagnostic threshold. In contrast, this study proposed NLR > 3.42 (AUC = 0.873) for distinguishing benign from malignant duodenal strictures, suggesting potentially higher thresholds may be required for duodenal lesions [21]. Ortíz-López et al. confirmed in 788 patients after radical surgery that those with NLR > 3.3 at 1 year post-surgery had a 5-year survival rate of only 62.7%, significantly lower than the ≤ 3.3 group (85.7%), with a multivariable HR of 3.37 (95% CI 2.26–5.02). This threshold closely aligns with the 3.42 value used in this study for duodenal strictures, suggesting similar systemic inflammation thresholds may apply across different gastrointestinal segments. However, while CRC studies focus on postoperative recurrence monitoring, this research fills an evidence gap in distinguishing benign from malignant lesions at initial diagnosis [22]. Li et al. reported that NLR ≥ 2.4 correlates with larger pancreatic tumor diameters (54.05% ≥ 5 cm), but diagnostic performance remains limited (AUC = 0.711, sensitivity 61.7%). The NLR threshold for duodenal stricture in this study was significantly higher than that for pancreatic cancer stratification, suggesting anatomical site differences may cause inflammatory threshold drift [23]. Zhu et al.‘s latest meta-analysis of 17 studies involving 3,842 gastric cancer patients confirmed that pre-treatment NLR ≥ 3.0 significantly correlates with poor response to immunotherapy (pooled HR = 1.73, 95% CI 1.56–1.93, P < 0.00001), with low heterogeneity (I²=25%) [24]. Mei et al. conducted the first systematic review of LMR in metastatic colorectal cancer, demonstrating that high LMR was independently associated with improved OS (HR = 0.55, 95% CI 0.49–0.62) and PFS (HR = 0.68, 95% CI 0.57–0.81) [25]. Yu et al. pooled 24 studies involving 2,221 patients with esophagogastric junction/gastric cancer, finding that NLR > 2.5 and derived NLR significantly predicted reduced immunotherapy efficacy (OS HR = 1.86, P < 0.001; PFS HR = 1.68, P < 0.001) [26]. This emerging evidence not only validates site-specific thresholds for inflammatory markers but also supports NLR/LMR as a cost-effective, readily accessible prognostic indicator. It further highlights the critical research gap requiring independent validation of duodenal lesions. The discrepancy noted between univariate and multivariate analyses in the present study suggests that a composite model of inflammatory markers may offer greater predictive value in determining malignant duodenal stenosis. However, the current results indicate that incorporating NLR, PLR, and LMR in the prediction of malignant duodenal stenosis resulted in a slight increase in the AUC. Nevertheless, this combination was not deemed significantly better than utilizing NLR alone. These results imply that the combination of the three parameters may not constitute a superior model for predicting malignant duodenal conditions. Future research is imperative to ascertain the most effective combination of inflammatory markers for improved prediction of malignant duodenal stenosis.

This study confirms that an NLR > 3.42 is a reliable predictor of malignant duodenal stricture (sensitivity 80.3%, specificity 91.1%). The following clinical integration pathway is recommended: When biopsy results are inconclusive: Patients with NLR > 3.42 may require enhanced imaging even if biopsy is negative. In resource-limited settings: This can serve as a screening tool for high-risk patients (age > 60 years + weight loss), avoiding unnecessary contrast-enhanced CT scans. This predictive indicator can be used for initial triage at primary care facilities and to shorten the diagnostic cycle at tertiary hospitals. NLR is not only a robust independent predictor (OR = 2.069), but its exceptional discriminatory power (AUC = 0.873) demonstrates significant clinical application potential. When NLR > 3.31, its high specificity of 91.1% ensures that the vast majority of benign cases are not misclassified as malignant (low false-positive rate), helping avoid unnecessary invasive tests or surgical anxiety. Simultaneously, its sensitivity of 80.3% captures most malignant cases. This suggests that NLR can serve as an effective ‘screening’ or ‘risk stratification’ tool, particularly when endoscopic or imaging findings are inconclusive. It assists clinicians in prioritizing the possibility of malignant tumors and formulating more proactive diagnostic strategies.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, being retrospective, there is a potential for recall bias and there is also a lack of comparison with classic digestive tract tumor markers such as CA199 and CEA and limit the clinical applicability. Secondly, the study exhibits potential selection bias due to the exclusion of patients with incomplete data (n = 18): these patients were excluded because key data (such as pathology results or blood test reports) were missing. This exclusion may represent two distinct groups: (1) Patients too severely ill to complete all examinations; if malignant cases were prevalent among them, their exclusion could result in a relatively milder disease burden among the included malignant group, potentially underestimating the true inflammatory levels in this group; (2) Patients with mild or atypical symptoms for whom physicians did not order all tests (if benign cases were prevalent among these, their exclusion may have resulted in a heavier disease burden among the included study population, overestimating the inflammatory difference between benign and malignant groups). Exclusion of patients with multiple malignancies or hematologic malignancies (n = 9): These patients were excluded to focus on the inflammatory characteristics of duodenal stricture itself, rather than the effects of widespread systemic metastasis or hematologic diseases. This limits the applicability of findings to patients with duodenal strictures secondary to extensive metastatic cancer or hematologic malignancies, as their systemic inflammatory states may differ significantly. Exclusion of patients with prior interventions (n = 11): Excluding patients who used anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids, or immunosuppressants before examination is critical, as these medications directly affect core study indicators like NLR, PLR, and LMR. This exclusion may have omitted patients with milder initial symptoms who received medication or experienced delayed diagnosis, resulting in a final cohort biased toward patients with more urgent symptoms requiring earlier endoscopic or surgical intervention. The study was conducted at a single center with a limited sample size and lacked long-term follow-up. The retrospective, single-center design may limit the generalizability of findings, and the study lacks external validation. Thirdly, different tumor types may exhibit distinct inflammatory profiles; this heterogeneity could obscure important differences, further restricting clinical applicability. Fourthly, the retrospective nature prevents accurate and standardized assessment of symptom duration (time from symptom onset to presentation). Symptom duration may influence inflammatory biomarker levels. Fifthly, although our exclusion criteria excluded patients who received specific systemic treatments (e.g., anti-inflammatory drugs, steroids, immunosuppressants) prior to endoscopy, obtaining comprehensive medication histories retrospectively presents challenges. However, due to the limitations of the sample size, we are unable to conduct subgroup analysis. More prominently plans for prospective validation studies are needed to elucidate the role of peripheral blood cells analysis derived inflammatory biomarkers in predicting malignant duodenal stenosis in the further.

Conclusion

Our study provides evidence that peripheral blood cells analysis derived inflammatory biomarkers such as NLR holds significant predictive value for malignant duodenal stenosis. This suggests that peripheral blood cells and associated inflammatory parameters could potentially serve as indicators to distinguish between benign and malignant duodenal stenosis in clinical practice. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and determine the optimal models for predicting malignant duodenal stenosis.
