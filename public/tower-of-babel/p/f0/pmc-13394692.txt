Anthropogenic factors are correlated with wing color variation in Colias (Lepidoptera: Pieridae) butterflies across the Toronto urbanization gradient
Authors: Amanda Sabatino, Joshua P Jahner, S Eryn McFarlane
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHybridization, or interbreeding between 2 previously diverged populations, is increasing due to human influences on the environment through creating new niches and enhancing hybrid fitness. Rates of hybridization might be increasing particularly quickly among urban species, especially those that are known to be sensitive to environmental disturbances. Butterflies are one such taxa, as some species are commonly found in urban areas, despite being sensitive indicators of environmental variation. For example, Colias philodice Godart and C. eurytheme Boisduval (Lepidoptera: Pieridae) hybridize when their ranges are in contact or overlap and are commonly found in large cities throughout North America. However, it is an open question how variation in urbanization might affect variation in hybridization. Using wild Colias across a gradient of urbanization in Toronto, Ontario, we tested how the variation in Colias color, as an indicator of hybridization, relates to human disturbance and urbanization. We created an objective numeric classification for both C. eurytheme and C. philodice, against which we categorized Colias samples. We found that some of the metrics, including distance to road, ordinal date, and number of pedestrians affect the variation in Colias color, suggesting that urbanization affects Colias coloration, and potentially rates of hybridization, in Toronto. This means that human disturbance of the environment could be affecting Colias rates of hybridization.

Introduction

Hybridization is the interbreeding of individuals from 2 separate lineages, that are distinguishable based on 1 or more characteristics (Harrison 1990, Harrison and Larson 2014). Approximately 10% of animal species hybridize (Aubert et al. 1997, Mallet et al. 2007), which can result in introgression, or the integration of alleles from the gene pool of one species into another (Anderson and Hubricht 1938, Harrison and Larson 2014). While introgression can increase phenotypic variation within backcrossed populations (Rieseberg et al. 1999, Grant and Grant 2008), it can also decrease biodiversity through genetic mixing and drive population declines, making it a conservation concern (Rhymer and Simberloff 1996, Allendorf et al. 2001). Finally, human-induced environmental changes may alter both the rates, patterns, and outcomes of hybridization.

Habitat fragmentation and other human-induced changes to the environment, such as climate change, can generally increase hybridization rates (Todesco et al. 2016, Larson et al. 2019, Ålund et al. 2023). Human-driven environmental changes can create new niches that enhance hybrid fitness (Todesco et al. 2016), although examples of humans directly influencing hybridization rates remain rare. One exception is that Grabenstein et al. (2023) found that black-capped (Poecile atricapillus) and mountain chickadees (P. gambeli) hybridize at increased rates depending on levels of human disturbance. Similarly, historical rates of stocking have been found to affect rates of hybridization between Yellowstone cutthroat (Oncorhynchus clarkii bouvieri) and rainbow trout (O. mykiss) (Mandeville et al. 2019). Urbanization results in the modification, destruction, and creation of natural habitats (Sudha et al. 2012). Rapid urbanization in cities transforms the physical environment into a patchwork of fragmented habitats and alters the atmosphere and climate through the creation of urban heat islands (Sudha et al. 2012). Ultimately, given the rapid rate of urbanization in cities (Sudha et al. 2012), as a result of urban densification and its effect on the environment, including rapid environmental degradation (Sudha et al. 2012), there is good reason to suspect that urbanization could generally affect hybridization rates.

Urbanization increases pressures on ecosystems by fragmenting landscapes, and intensifying local heat (Grimm et al. 2008), and thus can affect both rates of hybridization and selection on hybrid individuals. Organisms in cities are faced with urbanization at varying degrees, with impacts on the functional structure of biological communities (Concepción et al. 2017). In ecosystems that are highly disturbed, like cities, human disturbance could change the fitness landscape to one that favors hybrids (Sartor et al. 2021, McFarlane and Mandeville 2023). The high spatial heterogeneity of urban environments helps promote some species, allowing them to be found in a greater abundance within urban habitats (Savard et al. 2000, McKinney 2008). Human disruptions within areas of high urbanization, result in spatial isolation, promote genetic diversity, and also can promote hybridization (Ålund et al. 2023). This process is often driven by habitat homogenization, which brings formerly isolated species into contact (Grabenstein and Taylor 2018). Within these disturbed environments, there is a breakdown of reproductive barriers as the efficacy of visual, chemical, and acoustic signals may be reduced (Grabenstein and Taylor 2018). Human changes to the environment can advance the establishment of new ecological niches or the removal of niches, causing varying selection (Ålund et al. 2023). For example, 2 species of red wood ants Formica polyctena and F. rufa, which typically do not interbreed with one another, were found to have an increased presence of intermediate individuals in areas that were highly disturbed and fragmented (Seifert et al. 2010). The extent to which habitat alterations impact population connectivity is linked to the species dispersal ability (Bohonak 1999). Dispersal can influence a wide range of ecological processes ranging from genetic structure and adaptation, to community dynamics (Sekar 2012), and can also drive the persistence or expansion of hybridization (Kovach et al. 2015). Recognizing the potential effects of human-induced landscape changes, particularly urbanization, on ecosystems and altered niches is important in understanding varying hybridization rates.

Butterflies are excellent models in urban settings as they are sensitive to urbanization (Forister et al. 2023), but are still persistent in city settings. Specifically, butterflies are suitable indicators of indirect measures of environmental variation: as they are highly responsive to local climate and light levels, and their distribution patterns are important in studying habitat structure (Blair and Launer 1997, Snell-Rood et al. 2014). The intensity and effect that urban environments have on butterflies vary between species (Hardy and Dennis 1999). For example, habitat and host plant generalist species tend to be more common than specialists in areas with greater urban pressures (Bergerot et al. 2011). In satyrine butterflies, anthropogenic effects, including anthropogenic fragmentation, have resulted in reduced diversity (Hill et al. 2006). Urban butterfly diversity is also influenced by park size, as larger parks with unmanaged areas can support more species overall (Sing et al. 2016). Butterflies are also excellent models, as hybridization is often visible in wing coloration (Mavárez et al. 2006). For example, in both Heliconius heurippa and Limenitis arthemis, butterflies have an intermediate morphology and a hybrid genome, along with intermediate wing patterns (Mavárez et al. 2006, Mullen et al. 2008). Taken together, butterflies are expected to be responsive to urbanization, and make a good model for understanding how the various, related human pressures in a city can affect rates of hybridization.

Colias (Lepidoptera; Pieridae) is a butterfly genus that is widely distributed around the world (Ae 1959). Colias eurytheme, the orange sulphur butterfly and C. philodice, the clouded sulphur butterfly are sympatric species that are widely distributed across Canada and the United States (Ferris and Brown 1981, Opler and Malikul 1992). Both species are known to be habitat generalist species (Vogel et al. 2007, Karowe 1994). Colias eurytheme and C. philodice hybridize where their ranges are in contact or overlap, producing F1 hybrids consisting of intermediate characteristics (Gerould 1943, Hovanitz 1949). These butterflies are known to hybridize widely over the United States (Silberglied and Taylor 1973). Furthermore, there is the production of backcross offspring and F2 hybrids (Hovanitz 1949). Hybridization that occurs between the 2 species does not eliminate either, rather C. eurytheme and C. philodice continue to persist regardless of gene exchange (Hovanitz 1949, Dwyer et al. 2015). The relative abundance of hybrids between C. eurytheme and C. eriphyle is driven by seasonal temperature that impacts hybrid offspring directly and indirectly affects the population dynamics of the parental species (Jahner et al. 2012). Abiotic conditions can predict the variation in the frequency of phenotypically identified hybrids (Jahner et al. 2012). Additionally, recent genetic work has shown that wing coloration in C. eurytheme and C. philodice is controlled by 2 major-effect loci and 1 minor-effect locus which explain over 70% of the heritable variation in color, suggesting an oligogenic architecture (Hanly et al. 2023). Hybridization between C. eurytheme and C. philodice can therefore be assessed through a phenotypic approach, as introgression occurs through visible traits, with hybridization being assumed as the presence of intermediates (Hovanitz 1949, Jahner et al. 2012), although recent work has demonstrated that while all hybrid individuals are phenotypically intermediate, not all phenotypically intermediate individuals can be assumed to be hybrids (Jahner et al. 2026). Thus, wing coloration can be used as an imperfect proxy of hybridization between C. eurytheme and C. philodice (Hanly et al. 2023).

How Colias respond to urbanization is an open question. Specifically, C. eurytheme and C. philodice have been classified as disturbance adaptable, with greatest abundance in areas that have moderate disturbance, making them a suitable model for understanding disturbance in urban areas (Hogsden and Hutchinson 2004). As far as we are aware, it is unknown how urbanization affects hybridization in Colias and the distribution of intermediate phenotypes. Using a hypothesis-generating research approach, our study incorporated a variety of data including pedestrian count, date, community density, the distance to the nearest tree, and the distance to the nearest road as measures of urbanization to determine relationships among urbanization and Colias color phenotypes. We analyzed color phenotype using the proportion of Colias individuals displaying intermediate phenotypes (defined as scores 4 to 7). If urbanization affects hybridization, then we predicted that the proportion of individuals displaying intermediate phenotypes would increase along an urbanization gradient, reflecting the elevated rates of hybridization of C. philodice and C. eurytheme.

Methods

Creating an Objective Classification

Hybridization between species can be detected through genomic tools and phenotypic measures, allowing for ancestry to be assessed without the need to directly observe mating (Grabenstein and Taylor 2018). In the study conducted by Hovanitz (1949), Colias individuals were classified based on their orange pigmentation ranging from 1 to 10. We also classified color phenotypes between 1 and 10, with C. philodice assumed to be 1 to 3, C. eurytheme ranging from 8 to 10 and intermediates represented by ranges 4 to 7 (Fig. 1) (butterfly scale). To standardize these categories and analyze the samples collected from fieldwork based on orange pigmentation, with intermediate Colias colors assumed to be hybrids, we developed a classification scale using museum samples from the Royal Ontario Museum (Toronto, Ontario). We examined approximately 2,500 samples of C. philodice and C. eurytheme and chose those samples to be representative of each stage in a color gradient, where the most pale yellow individuals were classified as “1” and the most orange were classified as “10.” Furthermore, we compared these representative butterflies to a wide range of paint chips ranging from light yellow to dark orange, so that each butterfly (1 to 10) could be represented as a paint color. For fieldwork, we created a reference sheet using these color swatches to ensure consistency among different observers and environments.

Survey Methods

We did all of our Colias sampling in Toronto, as urbanized areas in the city are expanding (Blais et al. 2000), there is substantial habitat variation among parks within the city limits, and Colias are widespread (https://www.inaturalist.org; iNaturalist 2024). To identify the city of Toronto parks that would best represent a wide range of urbanization, we examined all green spaces throughout the city (City of Toronto 2025a). The selected sites varied and included a wide range of urban habitats including urban parks, universities, forests, and meadows (Fig. 2). Using iNaturalist data (2014–2023), habitats were determined based on 2 factors; if C. philodice had been present within the previous years, or if C. eurytheme had been present within the previous years (https://www.inaturalist.org; iNaturalist 2024).

Objective numeric classification for C. eurytheme and C. philodice created using variable specimens from the Royal Ontario Museum. The color swatches serve as a reference for the color classification of all butterflies that were collected in the field. This standardized method allows for a framework to assess color variation in Colias. Each Colias was then assigned a number from 1 to 10 based on the coloration on its upperside.

The region of our study spans approximately 35.55 km north-south and 35.38 km east-west, demonstrating the large-scale coverage of the study area within the city. All of the sites included are located further then we expect Colias to disperse, as shown in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S2). This is given that Watt et al. (1979) reported for Colias philodice eriphyle, the minimum average dispersal is 112 m (recorded for females in 1977, Brood 1) and maximum dispersal is 428 m (recorded for males in 1977, Brood 2). Thus, both sexes have limited dispersal rates and overall mean ranges are less than 100 m (Tabashnik 1980).

To examine Colias in Toronto, we sampled between 7 May 2024 to 23 July 2024, haphazardly in City of Toronto parks and on university campuses. At each collection site, depending on the weather and visual inspection of the area, we sampled for between half an hour and 3 and a half hours. Sampling occurred during peak butterfly activity hours, ranging from 10.20 AM to 4.21 PM. We attempted to catch all Colias seen using hand nets. For each butterfly that we collected, we quantified phenotypic variation by comparing each collected specimen in the field to the standardized color scale created at the Royal Ontario Museum (Fig. 1). Using forceps, we held the wings open to expose the dorsal wing surface, allowing for a comparison with the interior wing color and the reference scale. Colias individuals were then assigned a numerical value (1 to 10) corresponding to the closest color match. This allowed us to observe butterflies and phenotypically assign a value to each butterfly collected.

We assessed pedestrian activity by counting the number of pedestrians within a radius of 3 m, at each capture site, to calculate the index of human disturbance (Thaweepworadej and Evans 2023). This allows us to account for heterogeneous use within parks and allows us to distinguish between the fine-scale anthropogenic pressure experienced by butterflies from broader, ward-level (municipal district) population metrics. For example, while the metrics are correlated, population density is demonstrably different from pedestrian activity (r118 = 0.47, P < 0.001). Furthermore, we considered pedestrian activity relevant to include as butterflies respond to fine-scale metrics due to their limited dispersal abilities (Bergerot et al. 2011, Han et al. 2025). To account for fine-scale habitat fragmentation, we estimated the distance to the nearest tree from the point where the butterflies were collected (meters). Ordinal date refers to the date of collection for each sample (1 to 366), representing the day of the year from 1 January to 31 December 2024. We used estimates of ward population density (km2) and total human population sizes (number of residents) from the City of Toronto (City of Toronto 2025b). Details of this are described below.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using R v4.4.1 (R Core Team 2024). To determine the density of each Toronto ward, the city of Toronto ward profiles were used (City of Toronto 2025b). To create maps, we used the R packages ggplot2, ggmap, and sf (Kahle and Wickham 2013, Wickham 2016, Pebesma 2018, R Core Team 2024). To determine the distances from each sampling point to the nearest road and quantify habitat fragmentation and spatial isolation, we calculated the distance between butterfly capture locations and nearby roads in meters using the R packages googleway, dplyr, and geosphere (Hijmans 2022, Cooley 2023, Wickham et al. 2023, R Core Team 2024).

To ask to what extent urbanization affects butterfly phenotypes, we used the R package glmmTMB to fit a beta-regression model (Brooks et al. 2017, R Core Team 2024). We used a beta-regression model as color is being used as a proxy for hybrid score, which must always sum to 1 (100% ancestry) (Bolker 2008). The response variables which were scaled for the beta regressions include a scale for 0 to 1 which represent the level of orange pigmentation based on the original scale (Fig. 1; Table 1), 1 being equal to 10 on the color scale. We also scaled all variables to be standardized using the scale function (R Core Team 2024), and included distance to the nearest road, ward population density (km2), date of collection, 2021 ward population, distance to the nearest tree, and pedestrians observed at the time of collection. Additionally, parks were included as a random effect in the model to account for the expected variability between the different parks. Data visualization was facilitated by the ggplot2 package (Wickham 2016, R Core Team 2024), which was used to generate scatter plots with regression lines for each predictor.

Furthermore, to additionally assess which species occurs closer to roads, we used data from iNaturalist (https://www.inaturalist.org; iNaturalist 2024). We included verifiable data from Toronto reported any time prior to December 2024 for both C. philodice and C. eurytheme. We then calculated the distance between the iNaturalist butterfly coordinates and nearby roads in meters using the R packages googleway, dplyr, and geosphere (Hijmans 2022, Cooley 2023, Wickham et al. 2023, R Core Team 2024). This allowed us to determine if the disturbances we observe match a larger observational data set.

Finally, using the ggpredict function in R from the ggeffects package (Lüdecke 2018, R Core Team 2024), we generated model-based predictions across the full range of each scaled predictor variable. To isolate predictions to correspond to intermediate response probabilities, we applied custom filtering using the dplyr function (Wickham et al. 2023, R Core Team 2024). This allowed us to filter predictions around intermediate values (4 to 7). This model extrapolates in which environments we would be most likely to find hybrids/intermediate phenotypes.

Results

In total, we collected 122 Colias butterflies from 12 sites Fig. 3). Any site in which we collected a butterfly, we attempted to revisit that location (Supplementary Table S1). However, at some parks (n = 6) we were only successful in catching Colias butterflies on a single date. We were not always successful in revisiting and collecting more from the same site. The average color across all sites was 5.45 ± 1.70 (mode = 6, least common were 1 and 10; Table 1).

Distance to road, ordinal date, and pedestrians all were associated with the color of butterflies in Toronto (Table 2). As distance to road increased, the proportion of orange butterflies observed also increased (Estimate = 0.280, z = 3.31, P < 0.001). Later in the season, the proportion of orange butterflies observed increased (Estimate = 0.294, z = 3.17, P = 0.002). Higher pedestrian activity resulted in a lower proportion of orange butterflies (Estimate = −0.359, z = −4.01, P < 0.001; Table 2). Other variables such as population density, 2021 ward population, and distance to nearest tree, did not show statistically significant effects (Table 2, Fig. 4). Results from the model indicate that the fixed effects accounted for 56.7% of the total variance (marginal R2 = 0.567) in the level of orange pigmentation, while the full model including random effects accounted for 60.1% of the variance (conditional R2 = 0.601). Distance to road, ordinal date, and number of pedestrians appear to influence the probability of detecting hybrids. Using these model results, we then extrapolated the model’s actual predicted values for hybrid occurrence across the observed ranges of the significant metrics. Specifically, this model predicts the occurrence of a “clouded-like” hybrid phenotype (color = 4, Fig. 1) in areas such as 62.73 m away from the road, with 6.48 pedestrians, and on an ordinal date of 173 (21 June of a leap year). We might see an “orange-like” hybrid (color = 6) 359.03 m away from the road, with 0 pedestrian activity, and on an ordinal date of 205 (23 July of a leap year). These values identify the predictive conditions under which each hybrid phenotype is most likely to be detected according to our model.

Using iNaturalist data, we found that C. philodice are found closer to roads than C. eurytheme. A Welch’s 2-sample t-test showed a significant difference in distance to roads of the iNaturalist data between C. philodice (mean = 125.35 m) and C. eurytheme (mean = 166.01 m) (t472.06 = −2.39, P = 0.017).

Discussion

We found that specific environmental and anthropogenic factors are correlated with the color of C. philodice and C. eurytheme. We also developed a standardized color scale for C. philodice and C. eurytheme identification. This new tool will allow for future studies to be consistent when determining Colias coloration, enabling more uniform comparisons of phenotypic classification and consistency across environmental contexts.

Our statistical predictions did not provide support for our initial expectation that intermediate individuals would be most abundant at the highest levels of urbanization. Rather, it seems that intermediate individuals are found at intermediate levels of urbanization, as would be expected if C. eurytheme and C. philodice inhabit different niche space across the urban gradient. We did find that some of the metrics, including distance to road, ordinal date, and number of pedestrians explain variation in Colias color, explaining 57% of the total variance (marginal R2 = 0.567; Table 2), suggesting a difference in habitat between C. philodice and C. eurytheme. The distance to the nearest road has a significant effect on the proportion of orange Colias observed (Table 2). As the distance to the nearest road increases, the proportion of orange phenotypes increases, suggesting that C. eurytheme and their backcrosses are more isolated and likely to be observed farther away from human disturbances such as roads. These patterns match our findings from iNaturalist where C. eurytheme is found further from roads than C. philodice, indicating that C. eurytheme prefers environments further from human disturbances. Additionally, this aligns with previous studies that have shown that C. eurytheme prefers patches that are slightly fragmented (Summerville and Crist 2001). Sampling date had a significant effect on the proportion of orange Colias butterflies (Table 2), which is not surprising, as previous studies have demonstrated that color deepens throughout the season (ie seasonal polyphenism; Gerould 1943). Pedestrian count at the collection sites showed a significant negative correlation with the proportion of orange butterflies (Table 2). This suggests that increased pedestrian activity may negatively impact the number of orange butterflies found in an area. Environmental and anthropogenic metrics might affect C. eurytheme more than C. philodice, although work understanding the specific underlying mechanisms is needed. For example, species occurrences may be shaped by physiological responses; however, it has yet to be determined if C. eurytheme and C. philodice react differently to sodium or lead, in the same way these elements have been shown to drive developmental responses in other butterflies (Snell-Rood et al. 2014, Kemmerling et al. 2025). If urban pollutants differentially impact C. eurytheme and C. philodice, and intermediate individuals occur between the distributions of each parental species, then future work could examine whether these intermediates are indeed hybrids, and if there are selective pressures that maintain hybridization (Barton and Hewitt 1985).

Our results show that Colias species are capable of inhabiting diverse urban environments; however, variation occurs with the level of urbanization, resulting in a nonuniform distribution across the city. Other studies have found that urban areas are important for species and that the way species react varies. There are a number of species that are at higher abundance in cities when compared to more rural settings, including European red squirrel (Sciurus vulgaris; Jokimäki et al. 2017), a variety of bird species (eg house sparrow, Passer domesticus; European starling, Sturnus vulgaris; and rock doves, Columba livia; Batten 1972, Savard et al. 2000). This is the result of multiple factors such as increased food resources in urban areas and a decrease in the number of natural predators (Adams 2009, Francis and Chadwick 2013, Jokimäki et al. 2017). Colias philodice might be such a species, as we were more likely to find C. philodice closer to roads and when there were more pedestrians. They might not be negatively affected by human disturbance, at least relative to C. eurytheme. A different pattern can be observed in birds, where there is a decrease in bird diversity as urbanization increases, as urbanization eliminates their varied habitats (Batten 1972, Savard et al. 2000). Pollinators are also faced with the increasing effects of urbanization, which tends to negatively affect the abundance of their populations (Liang et al. 2023). This demonstrates a need to study the impacts of urbanization on a variety of species as it is difficult to make general predictions about overall outcomes. Assessing how urbanization affects not only abundance but also how it might interact with hybridization to alter biodiversity patterns demonstrates how complicated these questions are. Looking at landscapes of urbanization are important study sites to view the outcomes of hybridization as they can inform us of how anthropogenic hybridization affects various species (Allendorf et al. 2001).

Possible Caveats and Future Directions

Future studies on Colias should use genomics to assess the reliability of color phenotypes as a proxy for hybridization. Other studies have found that phenotypic approaches can be useful to start examining hybridization; however, incorporating a genomic perspective is essential, as it allows for further details regarding the impacts of anthropogenic hybridization on species (McFarlane and Pemberton 2019). Similar studies that have been conducted in various regions in Northern California and Nevada have used 363 samples of C. eurytheme, C. eriphyle, and phenotypic intermediates for genetic analyses (Dwyer et al. 2015). Jahner et al. (2026) found that, while most genetically identified hybrids between C. eurytheme and C. eriphyle were phenotypically intermediate, there were many phenotypically intermediate individuals that were not hybrids, suggesting substantial color polymorphism within each species. To build on the results identified here, future research that incorporates genomic validation could further refine the precision on how urbanization affects hybridization and also evaluate the reliability of wing color as a proxy within this environmental context for C. eurytheme and C. philodice.

While our study took into account several environmental variables, there are other factors that could contribute to environmental variation. In particular, Colias nectar use is linked to the composition of the surrounding flora, highlighting the importance of vegetation surveys to understand resource selection (Watt et al. 1974). Urban parks in Toronto have varying vegetation which could affect microhabitats and Colias resource selection. It is yet unknown if or how hybrid Colias are different from parental type Colias in their resource use, but this would be an interesting avenue for future research.

Conclusion

Significant gaps remain in our understanding of the impact of urbanization on butterfly populations, including with regard to Colias. Urbanization is one of the major drivers of anthropogenic environmental change, and studies exploring its effects on biodiversity are essential (Seto et al. 2012, Liang et al. 2023). This study investigates how urbanization and human-induced disturbances influence hybridization using intermediate color as a phenotypic measure of C. philodice and C. eurytheme. “Clouded-like” intermediate individuals tend to live near roads early in the season, while “orange-like” intermediate individuals are more likely farther away from roads, later in the season and with fewer pedestrians. By examining the role of environmental heterogeneity and habitat fragmentation, we have begun to understand the changing dynamics of species interactions in an urbanized landscape.
