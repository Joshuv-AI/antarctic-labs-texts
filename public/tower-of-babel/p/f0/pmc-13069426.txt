Pulsed field ablation: Biophysical principles and practical considerations in atrial fibrillation treatment
Authors: Shivaraj Patil, Suraj Kapa, Abhishek J. Deshmukh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulsed field ablation (PFA) is an emerging nonthermal ablation modality for the treatment of atrial fibrillation. Unlike conventional thermal energy sources such as radiofrequency and cryoablation, PFA delivers high-voltage pulsed electric fields that induce irreversible electroporation, resulting in selective cardiomyocyte death while largely preserving surrounding extracellular structures. The biological response to pulsed electric fields depends on parameters such as field strength, pulse duration, waveform configuration, and number of pulses. Variations in electroporation thresholds among different cell types contribute to the relative myocardial selectivity observed with PFA. Lesion formation is also influenced by electrode configuration, catheter–tissue contact, and electric field distribution. Early clinical experience and large registries suggest that PFA enables efficient pulmonary vein isolation with a favorable safety profile and reduced risk of collateral injury compared with thermal ablation technologies. This review summarizes the fundamental biophysical mechanisms underlying pulsed electric field ablation and discusses practical considerations for its clinical application in atrial fibrillation ablation.

Introduction

Catheter ablation is an effective strategy in achieving long-term rhythm control. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and cryoablation have been used to treat atrial fibrillation (AF), with multiple randomized trials showing reductions in symptoms and improvements in quality of life compared with antiarrhythmic drugs. Beyond these conventional energy delivery systems, alternative energy sources such as lasers, microwaves, ultrasound, and heated air have also been investigated. Despite advances in catheter-based ablation technology to improve safety and efficacy, these thermal approaches inherently face challenges. Suboptimal control over the extent of lesion formation and tissue-indiscriminate effects of thermal ablation can potentially cause collateral injury to the esophagus, lung, vagus nerve, or phrenic nerve. In search of safer and more targeted therapies, pulsed field ablation (PFA) has emerged as a promising modality with rising clinical adoption globally [1,2].

PFA utilizes pulsed electric fields (PEF) to selectively induce cell death, primarily via irreversible electroporation (IRE), while preserving the structural integrity of the surrounding extracellular matrix [3]. A critical aspect of PEF technology's evolution has been the increasing ability to control the parameters of delivered electrical pulses, enabling greater precision and tailored tissue effects. Over the last decade, pre-clinical and clinical studies have shown that PFA in its modern form has the potential to create tissue-selective ablation lesions with short, high-energy electrical pulses that facilitate cell death while avoiding thermal energy-related side effects commonly associated with RFA and cryoablation. Subsequent real-world clinical experience with PFA has reinforced the impression of safety and procedural efficiency. Understanding the fundamental mechanisms governing PFA's interactions with biological tissues is crucial for its safe and effective clinical implementation. In this review, biophysical aspects of PFA relevant to the electrophysiologist are emphasized, along with procedural guidance for performing pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) using novel PFA systems.

Biologic effects of pulsed electric fields

Cellular responses to pulsed electric fields: electroporation and cell death

Applying pulsed electric fields (PEFs) to cells elicits distinct biophysical responses, predominantly affecting cell membrane integrity. Under resting conditions, eukaryotic cells maintain a transmembrane potential (TMP) ranging from approximately −40 mV to −90 mV, established by ionic concentration gradients and differential membrane permeability. Exposure to an external electric field induces charge accumulation at the membrane interfaces, altering the native TMP. This altered potential in the order of several 100 mV persists for the duration of the applied electric field pulse. If the magnitude of the induced TMP surpasses a critical threshold specific to the cell type, formation of aqueous, hydrophilic pores occurs within the lipid bilayer [3]. This phenomenon is termed electroporation (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Electric field-induced pore formation in the cell membrane and subsequent cell death.Fig. 1

Electric field-induced pore formation in the cell membrane and subsequent cell death.

Molecular dynamics simulations have shown a multi-stage process for pore formation: initially, water molecules penetrate the hydrophobic core of the lipid bilayer, often aligning in single-file structures. Subsequently, these initial water defects can expand into larger, stabilized aqueous pores, characterized by the reorientation of lipid head groups from the membrane-water interface towards the pore lumen. The kinetics of pore formation typically occur on nanosecond to microsecond timescales [4]. Additionally, the applied electric field can also impact membrane proteins, including voltage-gated ion channels, potentially inducing conformational changes or even creating pores within their voltage-sensor domains [5].

The cellular outcome following electroporation is contingent is several specific parameters of the applied PEF, such as electric field strength, pulse duration, and the number of pulses. If the induced membrane pores are transient, i.e., resealing after cessation of the electric field, membrane function and cellular homeostasis can be restored by repair mechanisms. This is called reversible electroporation (RE). The kinetics of this resealing process exhibit considerable variability, ranging from microseconds to minutes, dependent on pulse parameters and cellular characteristics. Conversely, exposure to PEFs of sufficient intensity and duration can cause irreversible electroporation (IRE). IRE is characterized by sustained membrane permeabilization, preventing the restoration of cellular homeostasis and culminating in cell death [3] (Fig. 2). While the outcome of cell death defines IRE, the underlying mechanisms are complex and not fully elucidated. IRE involves pleiotropic damaging events initiated by the electric pulses. Poration is not restricted to the plasma membrane; intracellular organelle membranes, including those of the nucleus, mitochondria, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER), can also be permeabilized, leading to organelle dysfunction.Fig. 2Cellular responses following exposure to pulsed electric fields. Depending on the pulse parameters, cells undergo either reversible or irreversible electroporation, with the latter causing permanent membrane damage and subsequent cell death.Fig. 2

The persistent loss of plasma membrane integrity during IRE precipitates immediate homeostatic disruption, notably osmotic imbalance leading to cell swelling and potentially rapid necrotic lysis. Furthermore, compromised membrane function permits unregulated transmembrane ion flux. A significant consequence is the elevation of cytosolic calcium concentration [Ca2+], driven by influx from the extracellular milieu and release from intracellular stores such as the ER. Elevated Ca2+ can activate secondary processes, including specific cell death signaling pathways and cytoskeletal disruption. Cellular ATP depletion occurs due to activation of Ca2+-ATPases from calcium influx, inhibition of mitochondrial ATP synthesis, and leakage of ATP through permeabilized plasma membrane. Mitochondrial poration and subsequent dysfunction further exacerbate ATP depletion and can trigger apoptosis by releasing signaling molecules, such as cytochrome c. Additional cellular insults associated with electroporation include inducing oxidative stress by the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid peroxidation, DNA fragmentation, leakage of DAMPs (Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns) that elicit local immune responses, and alterations in the local tissue microenvironment pH. Ultimately, IRE induces cell death through necrotic pathways and the activation of various programmed cell death cascades [6].

Tissue-specific response to pulsed electric fields

Different tissues exhibit distinct sensitivities to PEF, partly owing to variations in the biophysical properties of the cells that compose them, resulting in tissue-specific electroporation thresholds. This forms the basis of tissue selectivity. However, it is essential to recognize that the effects of PEF on a cell type depend heavily on the nature of the treatment protocol. Therefore, comparisons of tissue-specific IRE thresholds should be interpreted with caution and account for the PEF protocols used.

The interaction between the external electric field and a cell induces a transmembrane potential (TMP), a phenomenon mathematically described by the steady state Schwan equation for a spherical cell, which relates the induced TMP (ΔΦ) to the external field strength (E), cell radius (R), and the cell's orientation (θ) relative to the field [7].ΔΦ=1.5ERcosθ

According to the principles underlying the Schwan equation, a key factor governing a cell's susceptibility to electroporation is its size. A larger cell radius results in a higher induced TMP, making it relatively easier to reach the critical threshold for electroporation and subsequent cell death. Conversely, cells with smaller dimensions will alter their transmembrane potential more quickly, but to a degree below the IRE threshold (Fig. 3). Because atrial cardiomyocytes (10–15 μm) are larger than esophageal smooth muscle cells (3–10 μm) and neuronal axons (1–5 μm), they have a lower electroporation threshold and, thus, greater susceptibility to damage. This is consistent with the commonly cited lower IRE field threshold of approximately 400 V/cm for cardiomyocytes compared to nerve or smooth muscle cells [8].Fig. 3The effect of cell size on electroporation. The magnitude of induced transmembrane potential (TMP) across a cell membrane varies depending on its orientation (θ) within an electric field (Fig. 4). This relationship is complex and dependent on the pulse duration and cell shape. For a cardiomyocyte, the highest TMP and electroporation occur when field lines are parallel to the long axis of the cell (θ = 0 and θ = 180) for pulses greater than 10 μs at lower electric field strengths. However, as the strength of the electric field increases, perpendicularly oriented cells become more affected. Whereas, pulse durations that are in the order of ∼100 ns, electroporation occurs maximally for perpendicularly oriented cells, regardless of field strength [9]. During monophasic pulse delivery, the anode side experiences a greater effect and can electroporate at relatively lower field strengths than the cathode side [10]. This directional asymmetry is nullified when using biphasic electrical pulses. Moreover, other cellular factors such as membrane composition, ion channel distribution, and resting membrane potential also contribute to the observed differences in sensitivity between cell types.

The electric-field intensity is maximum near the electrode surfaces. This intensity decays rapidly and nonlinearly as the distance from the electrode increases. As current preferentially flows through pathways of higher conductivity, pore formation dynamically increases tissue conductivity. Thus, the electric field distribution itself evolves and increases during the application of treatment pulses. Tissue regions with low electrical conductivity exhibit substantial resistance to current flow, leading to significant voltage drops [11]. This attenuation of the electric field strength effectively shields tissues located downstream. Biological tissues, such as areas near pulmonary veins and the posterior left atrium, are inherently heterogeneous with varying tissue biophysical properties and multiple tissue interfaces and can result in a more pronounced spatial decay of the electric field intensity compared to what would be observed within an idealized, electrically homogeneous medium. Overall, these effects enhance cardiac selectivity and reduce the risk of collateral tissue damage.

To this end, several pre-clinical and clinical studies have demonstrated the excellent durability and safety of PFE lesions. Stewart et al. reported no perforation, aneurysm, stenosis, or injury to the phrenic nerve after multiple, high-dose, overlapping PFA applications near the right superior pulmonary vein, superior vena cava, and right atrial appendage with complete circumferential replacement fibrosis at 4‐week post-ablation in a porcine model. While the phrenic nerve was spared, injury to non-myelinated autonomic nerves was observed, suggesting potential implications for ganglionated plexus ablation strategies [12]. Koruth et al. delivered biphasic, bipolar PFA to the esophagus via thin-walled IVC and demonstrated no adverse histopathologic changes to the esophagus at 4 weeks [13]. Howard et al. showed a significantly reduced post-procedure risk of PV stenosis compared to RF in a canine model at 12 weeks [14]. This is attributable to PFA's largely non-thermal electroporation mechanism of injury, which induces less inflammation and fibrotic proliferation compared to thermal ablation. They also reported no evidence of vagus nerve injury and pulmonary vein injury with PFA. Kawamura et al., in porcine models, analyzed the effects of endocardial PFA application near annular sites and demonstrated angiographic evidence of coronary artery spasm with mild to moderate narrowing with recovery within 5 to 22 min, and angiograms at 4 weeks showed no narrowing. Subsequent histopathologic analyses demonstrated intimal hyperplasia and focal medial fibrosis [15]. Similar findings have been reported by Higuchi et al. after epicardial PFA application near coronary arteries with minimal to mild coronary artery stenosis on angiography at 4 to 8 weeks [16]. The underlying mechanism is thought to involve direct electrical stimulation of vascular smooth muscle cells, reversible electroporation leading to calcium influx, and stimulation of vascular nerve endings. Notably, this spasm effect appears to be mitigated by using nitrates before ablation, although specific dosage and administration protocols have not been systematically evaluated. Another unique safety concern with PFA is intravascular hemolysis. Erythrocytes, when exposed to high-voltage electric fields in blood-filled cardiac chambers, develop pores and undergo osmotic swelling and lysis. The extent of hemolysis directly correlates with the number of PFA deliveries and can cause clinically significant acute kidney injury [17].

Clinical data corroborate these preclinical observations. The ADVENT trial, a randomized study of 607 patients with drug-resistant paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF), compared PFA using a Pentaspline catheter to conventional thermal ablation, demonstrated a reduced incidence of pulmonary vein stenosis with pulsed field ablation relative to thermal ablation, and the absence of esophageal complications or permanent phrenic nerve injury. Transient phrenic nerve injury occurred in 1.3% of the PFA group [18]. Similarly in post approval studies real world population, the MANIFEST-17K registry encompassing 17,642 patients across 116 centers in Europe undergoing pulse field ablation (PFA) with a Pentaspline catheter reported no esophageal complications, pulmonary vein stenosis, or persistent phrenic palsy with pulsed field ablation, alongside a low incidence of transient phrenic palsy was observed in 0.06%, coronary arterial spasm in 0.14%, and hemolysis-related acute renal failure necessitating hemodialysis in 0.03% [19]. In the MANIFEST-US registry, 41,968 procedures were performed, and the overall major complication rate was 0.63%, with PF energy-specific complications coronary artery spasm (0.1%), hemolysis (0.16%) and kidney injury (0.02%), being very rare [20]. However, complications like aortoesophageal fistula are uncommon, even with thermal ablation procedures, occurring in ∼1 in 20,000 cases, with one reported case of esophageal injury with a pentaspline catheter [21]. Unique adverse events like malfunction of cardiac implantable electronic devices, inadvertent thermal injury due to significant joule heating from uncontrolled high current output, and unexplained sudden death have been reported with use of PF energy underscoring the need for continued vigilance [22].

Pulsed electric field parameters, electrode configuration, and tissue contact: implications for lesion formation

A pulse is the fundamental unit of PEF delivery. A pulse is characterized by its waveform, amplitude (voltage), duration (width), and frequency (Fig. 5). PEF systems employ various shapes of electrical waveforms, primarily grouped into sinusoidal and square waves, with square waveforms being most common in commercial ablation applications. Square waveforms can be categorized into monophasic (either positive or negative) and biphasic (both positive and negative) based on polarity. Monophasic pulses offer the strongest treatment effect due to maximal cell membrane charging, but often induce undesirable muscle contractions and other side effects like barotrauma, gaseous emboli, whereas biphasic pulses significantly reduce muscle stimulation but typically yield weaker treatment effects for equivalent parameters. Biphasic waveforms are considerably less arrhythmogenic than monophasic waveforms, and gated energy delivery using the R-wave as a trigger and avoiding delivery during the vulnerable cardiac repolarization period, which may trigger ventricular arrhythmia. The degree of asymmetry and frequency of the waveform can be adjusted to achieve the desired treatment effects.Fig. 5Parameters of pulsed electric field waveforms. Increasing the voltage increases the electric field intensity and treatment effect, but at the cost of increased tissue heating. As current flows through a resistive tissue load, Joule heating occurs. Heat generated is proportional to the square of the current delivered and directly proportional to the time that the current is applied (Heat = I2 × R × t). Also, higher voltages result in greater muscle contraction, gaseous microemboli, and an increased risk of electrical arcing, potentially causing char, coagulation, or barotrauma [23]. Increasing the pulse duration will produce a greater electroporation effect by increasing the cell's exposure to the electric field. An inverse, non-linear relationship exists between pulse duration and the required electric field strength to achieve a consistent level of cell electroporation, particularly for durations below 1 ms. Higher frequencies result in shorter pulse durations and reduce the electroporation effect, and require a greater amplitude of pulse to achieve IRE.

The delivery of multiple electrical pulses in close temporal proximity results in the aggregation of their individual effects, effectively forming a composite pulse structure, referred to as a packet. As the packet duration increases, the electroporation effect and lesion size increase, at the cost of side effects akin to those of increased applied voltage. An optimal packet delivery rate is also necessary. If there is insufficient time between the delivery of successive packets, the cooling effect of the surrounding blood flow will decrease, leading to significant heating at the electrode-tissue interface. A positive feedback loop can form, leading to further heating and collateral effects. Temperatures above 50 °C can cause cellular death, but higher temperatures can lead to extracellular protein denaturation, tissue carbonization, and gas formation. Gas production can occur due to the release of dissolved gas at elevated temperatures (degassing), water vaporization (boiling), or electrolysis. The mechanism of gas formation during the delivery of bipolar, biphasic pulses is more likely to be thermal than electrochemical [24].

The spatial distribution of the electric field during ablation is determined by the electrode configuration. In unipolar mode, an electric field is established between a single active electrode on the catheter and a large dispersive electrode on the skin. This configuration typically generates deeper-penetrating electric fields suitable for ablating thicker myocardial structures, such as ventricular walls. Bipolar mode uses adjacent electrodes on the catheter, creating more localized fields appropriate for thinner tissues such as the atria. The geometry, specifically electrode size and spacing, significantly impacts the resulting electric field's strength and distribution. Current density (J = I/A), representing current per electrode surface area, correlates with electroporation magnitude. Consequently, smaller electrode areas achieve higher current densities at a fixed current, enhancing electroporation. Minimizing the inter-electrode distance mitigates electric-field attenuation, maximizes penetration depth, and improves lesion continuity by reducing gaps, particularly in bipolar electrode configurations. Ablation in unipolar mode can cause significant muscle stimulation and pain, whereas in bipolar mode, there is a risk of arching if the electrodes are too close or distorted upon contact.

Proper catheter-tissue contact results in optimal lesion formation by efficiently delivering a lethal electric field. In monopolar ablation, a positive linear relationship between contact force and lesion depth/volume is seen [25]. Similarly, during bipolar ablation, good tissue-electrode approximation is critical for lesion formation. However, increasing contact force has minimal impact on lesion dimensions with bipolar ablation. Unlike traditional RF ablation, pulsed field ablation can also ablate tissue where catheter contact is suboptimal by leveraging the conductivity of blood surrounding the catheter to transmit the electric field to the nearby tissue, especially with strong electric fields. This is especially helpful in anatomically complex areas like trabeculations, ridges or recesses. Additionally, lesion size in PFA is uniquely influenced by the number of pulses delivered. Lesion size also grows cumulatively with repeated pulses applied to the same spot, a phenomenon referred to as repetition dependency (Fig. 6). This allows operators to potentially target deeper myocardial substrates by increasing the number of applied pulses. PFA also demonstrates the ability to treat areas of scar tissue, which are frequently implicated in arrhythmias. It can effectively penetrate scar tissue resulting from previous myocardial infarction or prior RFA procedures and homogenize it by killing any residual viable cardiomyocytes that might be embedded within these scar regions. Thermal preconditioning by heating the tissue prior to application of PF has also been shown to demonstrate larger lesion depth and diameter, possibly by lowering the electroporation threshold. Additionally, combined RF and PF applications at the same site, irrespective of order, significantly increases lesion size compared with the same dose of PF or RF alone, possibly by increasing the electrical conductance of tissue due to increased intercellular edema and decreased tissue impedance [26].Fig. 6The effect of application repetition on lesion size. Most clinical PFA technologies employ microsecond-scale pulses. Because these longer pulses tend to stimulate neurons and skeletal muscles, patients require general anesthesia or deep sedation to prevent pain and muscle contractions. However, shorter nanosecond pulse durations with higher-amplitude pulses preferentially electroporate the membranes of intracellular organelles rather than the cell membrane. This leads to greater cellular apoptosis than necrosis, potentially resulting in less inflammation. Although a higher voltage is used, the shorter duration of the pulse means the total energy delivered is often lower with nanosecond PF. Pores also form more uniformly across the cell with nanosecond pulses. Nanosecond pulses delivered at high repetition frequencies decrease the IRE threshold and diminish neuromuscular stimulation. This can facilitate percutaneous catheter ablation with local anesthesia and conscious sedation [27].

Pulsed field ablation workflows for pulmonary vein isolation

For the management of atrial fibrillation, several PFA systems using proprietary PEF parameters and electrode configurations have been designed and approved for clinical use, and many others are currently undergoing rigorous evaluation. In this review, we discuss a practical guide for performing pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) with a single-shot pentaspline PFA system and an integrated dual-energy focal ablation catheter and mapping system.

Considerations for performing pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) with the Farapulse™ pulsed field ablation (PFA) system

System Components: The Farapulse™ system utilizes ablation catheters (FARAWAVE™) available in 31- and 35 mm sizes. Each catheter features 5 splines and 4 electrodes per spline. Pulsed Field Ablation (PFA) energy is delivered in a bipolar configuration using all 20 electrodes simultaneously using a PF generator. The third electrode from the tip (pole 3) on each spline is designated for electrogram recording and pacing. The catheter's splines can be adjusted into configurations ranging from a spherical basket to a flower shape, which modifies the orientation of the electric field.

The accompanying sheath (FARADRIVE™) has a 13F inner diameter (16.8F outer diameter), an overall length of 91 cm, and is compatible with 0.035-inch guidewires. It features unidirectional deflection with a 35 mm reach. The sheath's transparency is a key feature, aiding in the visual detection of potential air bubbles.

Preprocedural planning and patient assessment

Before the procedure, several patient factors are considered. PVI can be safely performed in patients with Cardiac Implantable Electronic Devices (CIEDs); our protocol includes pre- and post-procedure device checks and deactivating ICD therapies during ablation. For patients with Left Atrial Appendage (LAA) closure devices, there is a potential risk of electric arcing with lobe and disc-type devices [28]. Patients with mechanical prosthetic valves can also undergo PFA, but careful catheter manipulation is crucial to prevent entrapment and electromagnetic interference. We routinely obtain a preprocedural cardiac CTA to assess pulmonary vein (PV) anatomy (including number, diameter, and variants like a common left ostium) and to exclude intracardiac thrombus.

Intraprocedural workflow

Anesthesia and Anticoagulation: PFA procedures can be performed under general anesthesia or deep sedation using the Farapulse system. However, careful consideration of factors related to the patient, procedure, and institutional capabilities must be given to determine a suitable anesthesia plan. At our institution, we routinely use general anesthesia with a neuromuscular blocking agent. Intravenous heparin is administered to maintain an activated clotting time (ACT) in the range 350-400 s.

Transseptal Access and Air Embolism Prevention: Transseptal puncture is performed using either a standard needle (e.g., BRK 98-cm) or a radiofrequency wire (e.g., VersaCross™) directly through the PFA sheath. The side port of the sheath must remain closed during the puncture. Vigilant aspiration and flushing through the side port are essential not only during transeptal access but also during each catheter exchange to prevent air embolism. Thorough inspection of the shaft and tubing must be performed before flushing.

Vagal Response: Vagally mediated bradycardia (sinus pause or AV block) can frequently occur during PFA and cause significant hemodynamic compromise (Fig. 7). This is mitigated by administering intravenous atropine or glycopyrrolate before the first PFA application, or by using backup ventricular pacing. Neuromuscular stimulation: Diaphragmatic contraction can occur during PFA, especially during procedures that are performed under deep sedation, either by direct muscle or phrenic nerve stimulation (Video 1). Besides patient discomfort, muscular contraction can cause significant body movement and shifts in the 3D mapping model and reduce the efficiency of PFA ablation.

Mapping and Visualization: Commercially available 3D Electroanatomic Mapping (EAM) systems are employed for PV identification, atrial substrate characterization, and real-time catheter visualization. This informs catheter orientation and allows the operator to make adjustments to the PFA catheter relative to the PV axis. Tracking PFA applications within the EAM system can help the operator plan optimal lesion sets and improve workflow. It also aids in post-ablation testing, target substrates beyond PVs, and reduces overall fluoroscopy time. However, non-mapping approaches have been described for PVI by primarily relying on fluoroscopy and/or intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) to establish adequate tissue-catheter contact.

Catheter Placement and Contact Assessment: PV cannulation is typically achieved using a 0.035-inch J-tip guidewire. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) provides real-time monitoring of the catheter's position relative to the PV ostium and helps assess tissue contact. Adequate catheter-tissue contact can also be assessed fluoroscopically and is suggested by spline indentation at the PV ostium in the basket configuration, or by backward flexing of the splines in the flower configuration (Fig. 8). Placement can be more challenging in the right-sided PVs due to constrained space and, at times, result in inverted splines (“cobra deployments”). This can be visualized on fluoroscopy and corrected by gentle retraction of the catheter into the sheath, rotation, and redeployment. Moreover, the PFA system automatically detects inverted splines and interrupts energy delivery immediately to prevent arcing. Ablation Protocol: A single PFA application typically lasts 2.5 s and consists of 5 pulses at 1.8 to 2 kV (Fig. 9). The bipolar, biphasic pulse sequence activates all 20 electrodes across all splines, generating an electric field at the PV muscle sleeves. Standard PVI involves delivering 4 PFA applications in the basket configuration and 4 applications in the flower configuration for each PV. After the initial two applications in each configuration, the catheter is rotated by approximately 36° (range 30-40°) to ensure circumferential lesion coverage. Posterior Wall Isolation (PWI): If indicated, PWI can be safely performed using the flower configuration. This typically involves 2 applications per site with approximately 50% overlap between lesions, ensuring the PWI lesion set is anchored to the bilateral PVI lesions.

Post-procedure management

Following procedures that involve a high number of applications (e.g., >60-70), we empirically administer 1-2 L of intravenous 0.9% sodium chloride or lactated ringer's solutions during and after ablation to mitigate the risk of acute kidney injury. We continue uninterrupted anticoagulation for at least 3 months after ablation.

PFA is delivered in a bipolar, biphasic manner through two active neighboring electrodes. The catheter's bidirectional, adjustable circular tip can be expanded (35 mm) or contracted (25 mm) to accommodate varying PV diameters. PVI typically involves a minimum of 12 applications per vein, delivered in salvos of 3, with each sequence spanning approximately 10 s and using all 10 electrodes. While all 10 poles are typically used for ablation, the most distal and proximal electrodes must be deactivated when electrode overlap occurs. The catheter is repositioned by rotating the loop by a quarter turn to cover inter-lesion gaps. Integration with the mapping system allows for real-time feedback of tissue-to-catheter proximity (TPI software, CARTO).

Considerations for performing pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) with dual energy focal Sphere-9™ catheter and Affera™ mapping and ablation system

System Components: The sphere-9™ catheter is an 8 Fr bidirectional deflectable catheter with an expandable 9 mm nitinol lattice tip electrode. The lattice forms a 9 mm sphere featuring 9 mini-electrodes, each with a thermocouple. It also houses a central electrode that contacts only blood, acting as a local indifferent reference, and two ring electrodes sit proximally on the shaft. The center of the lattice includes a central, protected micropore nozzle for uniform irrigation cooling. The tip houses an integrated magnetic sensor that enables 3D mapping and catheter tracking. Compared to a conventional 3.5-mm tip, the lattice tip has a 10-fold larger effective area and therefore delivers significantly higher currents at a lower current density with a wider footprint. The lattice tip has a 10-fold larger effective area than a conventional 3.5-mm tip, allowing it to deliver significantly higher currents at a lower current density and produce a larger lesion. The system additionally features dual energy generators, a peristaltic saline infusion pump, and an electroanatomic mapping system (Affera™ Prism-1 mapping system).

Mapping and Ablation: The integrated mapping and ablation system facilitates a streamlined workflow without requiring catheter exchange. Before transseptal access, intravenous heparin is administered to achieve and maintain a target activated clotting time (ACT) of ≥350 s. Access to the left atrium (LA) is obtained via a single transseptal puncture using an 8.5 Fr steerable sheath for better maneuverability.

After respiratory gating, a high-density electroanatomic mapping (EAM) of the left atrium is performed by systematically navigating the lattice tip throughout the LA cavity, encompassing the pulmonary veins (PVs) and the LA appendage. “Vein mode” helps to accurately delineate these anatomical structures and their spatial interrelationships. Voltage and activation maps are generated concurrently with LA anatomical data acquisition. Assessment of catheter-tissue contact is achieved by continuously monitoring impedance between each mini-electrode on the surface and the central electrode. Intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) can be used adjunctively to visually confirm optimal tissue contact (Video 2). High-fidelity bipolar electrograms are recorded between each mini-electrode and the central electrode (Close-Unipolar™ mapping) and are subjected to automated annotation.

We perform PVI using a standard wide-antral circumferential ablation (WACA) approach exclusively with PFA to minimize the potential risk of thermal injury to the esophagus and phrenic nerve, and to reduce the likelihood of PV stenosis. However, ablation along the anterior aspects of the PV antrum (anterior WACA) may be performed using RFA (anterior mode). Inadvertent phrenic nerve injury near the right superior pulmonary vein should be avoided by delineating its course with high-output pacing if RF ablation is being performed in the region.

Before energy delivery, the catheter is sequentially positioned in a point-by-point manner around the ipsilateral PV ostia. Each PFA application consists of unipolar, biphasic, microsecond pulses (PULSE3) delivered over a 4-s interval, accompanied by continuous saline irrigation at 15 mL/min. The mapping system provides real-time inter-lesion distance, aiding the operator in creating contiguous lesion sets and minimizing potential gaps (Fig. 11). Conclusion

The promise of PFA lies in its potential for enhanced safety through tissue-specific ablation. Accumulating evidence supports PFA's ability to effectively treat atrial fibrillation with a favorable safety profile, suggesting it could be more than just an alternative to conventional thermal ablation. Nevertheless, continued fundamental and clinical research of this technology is necessary to fully understand its potential and optimal use.
