Who complains? A nationwide register-based cross-sectional study of socioeconomic differences in patient complaints and complaint case outcomes in Denmark
Authors: Søren Bie Bogh, Sebrina Maj-Britt Hansen, Lars Morsø, Alex Gillespie, Tom Reader, Søren Fryd Birkeland
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine socioeconomic and demographic differences in who complains about healthcare and whether these factors are associated with the outcomes of complaint cases.DesignNationwide cross-sectional study in Denmark using linked national health, complaint and administrative registers.SettingThe Danish healthcare system provides universal coverage and has a centralised system for handling complaints with linked patient-level data to sociodemographic variables.ParticipantsAll healthcare contacts in 2022 in general practice (GP), non-psychiatric hospitals and psychiatric hospitals were included.Primary and secondary outcome measuresWe examined two outcomes: (1) submission of a complaint and (2) whether the authorities upheld the complaint. We then assessed how these outcomes varied by socioeconomic position (SEP), focusing on the contrast between patients with the lowest and highest SEP profiles. Relative risks (RRs) were estimated using modified Poisson regression with cluster-robust variance estimation at the patient level.ResultsThere were 31.9 million GP contacts, 4.8 million non-psychiatric hospital contacts and 345 000 psychiatric contacts, with 1381, 3545 and 439 complaints, respectively (0.43–12.7 per 10 000 contacts). Clear socioeconomic disparities emerged. Low-income patients were more likely to complain about GP and non-psychiatric hospitals, while higher education was associated with fewer complaints in these settings but more complaints about psychiatry. Patients outside the workforce were more likely to complain, whereas elderly patients were consistently underrepresented in the complaints.Low-SEP profiles had notably higher complaint probabilities than high-SEP profiles in GP (RR 2.60, 95% CI 2.22 to 3.70) and non-psychiatric hospitals (RR 1.69, 95% CI 1.40 to 1.98), but not in psychiatric hospitals (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.47 to 1.38). Although individual SEP indicators showed no consistent differences in complaint outcomes, the combined SEP profiles revealed lower probabilities for complaints being upheld in lower SEP patients in GP (RR 0.58, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.94) and non-psychiatric hospitals (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.96), while the estimate for psychiatric hospitals was inconclusive (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.24 to 1.66).ConclusionsIn this nationwide study, socioeconomic and demographic factors were linked to clear differences in both complaint submission and complaint outcomes. Except for psychiatric hospital services, patients with fewer socioeconomic resources were more likely to file a complaint yet had a lower probability of having it upheld. These findings point to disparities in who complains and in how complaints are assessed, and they underline the need to consider all complaints, regardless of outcome, when using complaint data to inform quality improvement efforts.

Introduction

Early warning scores, rapid response systems and reporting tools are widely used to identify quality breaches.1 However, some breaches are only recognised when patients or their families raise concerns,25 making their complaints a useful source of information.6 7 Correspondingly, patient complaints are increasingly used for quality improvement and as an early warning of patient safety risks.3 8 Yet, we have only limited knowledge about whether complaint data represent patients equally. A recent rapid review found limited and inconsistent evidence that patient characteristics predict complaints or medico-legal claims, with most characteristics examined in only a few studies and few replicated findings.9 Consequently, we still have a limited understanding of how complaint behaviour varies across socioeconomic and demographic factors. Who complains, and whose voices are heard through complaint systems, matters for both patients and organisations. Patients and families who speak up may sometimes influence staff behaviour and improve care in the moment.10 However, formal complaint procedures are typically slow, retrospective and rarely provide direct benefits to the individual patient who has complained. At the organisational level, complaints can support learning and highlight areas of risk.6 11 However, if some groups are less likely to complain, the problems they face may remain invisible, and complaint-guided quality improvement may overlook the areas where inequities are most pronounced. Consequently, complaint data may provide an incomplete or even skewed evidence base for organisational learning,31114 thereby reinforcing existing inequalities in healthcare experiences. This concern is also consistent with broader models of healthcare access and social determinants of health.15 16 These frameworks suggest that use of formal healthcare complaint mechanisms may be shaped not only by need but also by resources, knowledge, expectations, trust and the ability to navigate administrative systems. Applied to patient complaints, socioeconomic and demographic differences may therefore influence both whether patients submit a formal complaint and how their complaint progresses through the complaint system. This makes complaint systems relevant for health equity, patient safety and governance. From an equity perspective, variations in the number of complaints may skew visibility of issues within the healthcare system. From a patient safety and governance perspective, complaints are one of the formal mechanisms through which patients can signal problems, hold organisations accountable and contribute to organisational learning.

Research has tended to focus on complaints from single hospitals, with uncertainty about how the sample relates to the broader population, with little or no information about the socioeconomic backgrounds of those who complain. As a result, we still lack population-level evidence on complaint behaviour. In Denmark, patient complaints are centrally handled, allowing linkage to nationwide registers on education, income and labour-market affiliation. This level of data integration is rare in other healthcare systems, where complaints are often handled locally. It provides us with a valuable opportunity to study, at scale, who complains and how complaint outcomes differ across various groups. The primary focus of this study is socioeconomic and demographic representation within the formal complaint system, including both complaint submission and complaint case outcomes. Differences in access, complaint behaviour, expectations and institutional handling are considered possible explanations for these patterns but are not directly measured as separate outcomes. Specifically, we address two questions: (1) Are patient characteristics such as socioeconomic position and demographics associated with submission of a formal complaint? and (2) Are these characteristics associated with the outcome of complaint cases?

Based on previous evidence, we anticipated that complaint behaviour would differ by socioeconomic position (SEP).91721 Disadvantaged groups may be less likely to complain due to limited information about complaint procedures. Conversely, patients with greater resources (higher income, education or support from a partner) may be more willing to complain to authorities about perceived breaches in the quality of care or may receive higher-quality care, giving them less reason to complain. We did not expect significant socioeconomic differences in the outcomes of complaint cases, as all cases are managed by a central authority that aims to apply consistent rules and criteria across all patients and sectors.

Method

We conducted a nationwide register-based cross-sectional study using linked Danish register data on healthcare contacts, patient complaints and sociodemographic characteristics from 2022.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included all registered healthcare contacts in 2022 in general practice (GP), non-psychiatric hospitals and psychiatric hospitals in Denmark. These healthcare contacts constituted the denominator for the analyses. For GP, only one contact per person per day was included. For hospital care, outpatient visits and admissions were included, and contacts occurring within 4 hours were combined into a single episode. We excluded contacts outside the study period or outside the three included healthcare settings, contacts from Greenland and the Faroe Islands and records with missing or invalid personal identifiers, dates or other key linkage variables. Duplicate or erroneous records that could not be harmonised across registers were also excluded.

Setting

Denmark is a high-income country with approximately 6 million citizens. The Danish healthcare system is publicly funded and provides universal coverage for all residents. Hospitals are mainly public and include both non-psychiatric and psychiatric care. In the Danish healthcare system, non-psychiatric (‘somatic’) hospitals provide care for physical illnesses and injuries while psychiatric hospitals specialise in the treatment of mental health conditions. General practitioners act as gatekeepers, with most specialist and hospital services requiring referral.22

Under the Act on Complaints and Compensation, patients have a legal right to complain about healthcare and separately seek compensation for treatment-related injuries. This study only included formal complaints. Such complaints are submitted digitally and are handled by the Danish Patient Complaints Authority (DPCA). The DPCA handles complaints concerning hospital and primary care, collects the relevant case material and classifies decisions as either upheld or not upheld.23 Regional patient offices can advise patients about complaint options and help them prepare a complaint. These mechanisms align with broader international recommendations, as both the WHO and the European Union have highlighted access to complaint and recourse procedures as a key element of patients’ rights and healthcare system accountability.24 25

Data sources

Analyses were performed using data from various national registers. The DPCA provided information on all complaints, including details of the complainant, the type of service and the case outcome. Hospital contacts, both non-psychiatric and psychiatric, were identified in the Danish National Patient Register, and primary care contacts were obtained from the National Health Service Register for Primary Care. Both registers have been described in detail, including information on content, data quality and research use.26 27 Socioeconomic data came from the Income Statistics Register and the Danish Education Register, respectively.28 29 Demographic information was retrieved from the Danish Civil Registration System.30 All registers were linked at the individual level using the unique personal identification number assigned to all Danish residents. The included registers have nationwide coverage of the Danish resident population and capture contacts and complaints recorded in the respective national administrative systems. Thus, the study was based on all registered contacts and complaints meeting the inclusion criteria. Operational definitions, code lists and register-specific extraction algorithms are detailed in online supplemental table S5. Researchers accessed person-level data through the secure research environment at Statistics Denmark, with no possibility for exporting identifiable information.

Outcomes

We analysed two outcomes. The first outcome was complaint submission, defined as whether a patient had filed a formal written complaint to the DPCA in 2022 (yes/no). This was used as the empirical measure of complaint behaviour in the study. The second was the complaint case outcome, defined as whether the DPCA upheld the complaint case (yes/no) among submitted complaints. An upheld decision means that the DPCA found grounds for criticism of the healthcare provider, organisation or care process involved in the complaint.

Variables

Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, ethnicity, education, income and labour market affiliation. Age was categorised into five groups (<18, 18–25, 26–40, 41–65 and >65). Categorisation of ethnicity followed Statistics Denmark’s standard classification, distinguishing between Danish origin, Western immigrants or descendants and non-Western immigrants or descendants.31 32

Education and income were measured at the household level. Thus, education referred to the highest attained education in the household rather than only the individual patient’s education. Education was categorised into three levels: low (primary or lower secondary), medium (upper secondary or vocational) and high (post-secondary). Household income was categorised into low (the lowest quintile), medium (the middle three quintiles) and high (the highest quintile). Labour market affiliation was categorised into working, temporarily unemployed (on sick leave or parental leave), outside the workforce (on a disability pension or social welfare), retired and missing/other.33 For individuals younger than 18 years, SEP was assigned from the household reference person. Health-related variables included comorbidity, measured using the 10-year Charlson Comorbidity Index and grouped as 0, 1–2 and ≥3.34

During the construction of the study population, records with missing or erroneous values in key identifiers were excluded (<1%). Missing data in covariates were limited and reported where applicable in table 1. We did not assume that missing covariate data were missing at random, as missingness in sociodemographic register variables may be related to social or administrative factors. For sociodemographic variables, cases with missing information were therefore retained in the analyses as ‘unknown’ categories rather than excluded. In cases where equivalised disposable household income was missing, it was replaced by the corresponding income quintile based on the individual’s personal income. Data cleaning included checks for duplicate records, implausible values (eg, invalid ages or dates) and harmonisation of timestamps and identifiers across registers.

Statistical analyses

Throughout all analyses, data were stratified into three categories: GP contacts, non-psychiatric hospital contacts and psychiatric hospital contacts. We allowed multiple contacts per patient. For GP, only one contact per person per day was included. Hospital contacts included both outpatient visits and admissions, and any contacts within 4 hours were treated as a single episode.35 Healthcare contacts were included to define the underlying healthcare activity in each setting and to allow complaint submissions to be analysed relative to the number of visits. Because healthcare contacts and complaints were measured within the same calendar year, the analyses should be interpreted as associations between patient characteristics and formal complaint submission in 2022, not as causal effects of healthcare contacts on complaints.

We first described the distribution of variables among complainants compared with the reference population using frequency and percentages. We estimated the relative risks (RRs) of submitting a complaint using modified Poisson regressions with cluster-robust variance estimation at the patient level.36

We structured regression analyses in three models, estimated separately for each outcome and healthcare setting. The main model (M1) included SEP using three indicators: education, income and labour-market affiliation. Each indicator was entered in an individual model, adjusted for demographic variables and health-related variables described above. Running the SEP variables separately was chosen because they reflect related but distinct social mechanisms, and including them in the same model may lead to collinearity and attenuated estimates.33

As sensitivity analyses, we estimated two additional models. M0 consisted of unadjusted analyses, assessing the crude associations between each variable and the risk of complaint. M2 repeated M1 but included all three SEP indicators simultaneously. To illustrate the combined effect of SEP, we generated predicted probabilities for two contrasting SEP profiles (high vs low) using model M2. The high-SEP profile was defined as the highest household income, education and being employed, and the low-SEP profile as the lowest household income, education and being outside the workforce. Model robustness was assessed by comparing results across the three model specifications. All models converged, and cells with fewer than five observations were identified from descriptive cross-tabulations. All results are presented as RRs with 95% CIs. All data management and analyses were performed using Stata, V.18.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research, as the study relied solely on routinely collected data.

Results

The study population included 31.9 million GP contacts, 4.8 million non-psychiatric hospital contacts and 345 000 psychiatric hospital contacts. Among these, there were 1381 complaints in GPs, 3545 in non-psychiatric hospitals and 439 in psychiatric hospitals. This corresponded to 0.43 complaints per 10 000 GP contacts, 7.4 per 10 000 in non-psychiatric hospital care and 12.7 per 10 000 in psychiatric hospital care (table 1). The baseline characteristics of the complainants are shown in table 1.

Low-income groups were more likely to submit complaints in both GP and non-psychiatric hospitals. In GP, the medium- and high-income groups had RRs of 0.69 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.79) and 0.44 (95% CI 0.36 to 0.54), respectively. A similar gradient was observed in non-psychiatric hospitals (0.91, 95% CI 0.83 to 0.99, and 0.66, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.74). Associations in psychiatric care were weaker and partly non-significant (0.76, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.97 and 0.69, 95% CI 0.45 to 1.06) .

Education showed a different pattern across settings: Higher education was associated with fewer complaints in GP (medium: RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.89; long: RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.75) and non-psychiatric hospitals (medium: RR 0.98, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.09; long: RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.95). By contrast, in psychiatric hospitals, the association reversed, with higher complaint rates among educated patients (medium: RR 1.60, 95% CI 1.20 to 2.15; long: RR 1.46, 95% CI 1.00 to 2.15).

Labour market affiliation was strongly related to complaint risk. Compared with those in work, patients outside the workforce and retirees complained more frequently in GPs (RR 1.71, 95% CI 1.43 to 2.04; RR 1.78, 95% CI 1.46 to 2.18) and in non-psychiatric hospitals (RR 1.27, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.45; RR 1.31 (95% CI 1.14 to 1.51). In psychiatric hospitals, unemployment showed a particularly increased risk (RR 2.26, 95% CI 1.23 to 4.12).

Across other covariates, several patterns emerged. Men complained more often in GP (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.29) and psychiatric care (RR 1.31, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.64). Adults had higher complaint rates than children, while elderly patients were consistently underrepresented across settings. Western (RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.69) and non-Western immigrants (RR 1.56, 95% CI 1.34 to 1.83) complained more than Danish-born patients did for GP care but not for hospital care. Not being married was associated with more complaints across all settings. In non-psychiatric hospitals, higher comorbidity was associated with fewer complaints (medium 0.76, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.83; high 0.70, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.80) (figure 1).

Complaint case outcome

Among the complaints, 15.9% in GP, 19.2% in non-psychiatric hospitals and 9.6% in psychiatric hospitals were upheld (table 1). Overall, individual socioeconomic indicators showed no statistically significant associations with the probability of a complaint being upheld. In GP, the highest-income group had a higher likelihood of an upheld decision (RR 1.58, 95% CI 1.04 to 2.39), whereas in non-psychiatric hospitals, patients outside the workforce had a lower likelihood of an upheld decision (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.94). No clear patterns were observed for education or labour market affiliation in other settings (figure 2).

Younger patients (<18 years) were generally more likely to have their complaints upheld, while sex, marital status and comorbidity showed no statistically significant associations. In non-psychiatric hospitals, complaints from non-Western patients were less frequently upheld (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.91) (Figure 2).

When comparing the combined SEP profiles, the low-SEP profile complaints related to low-SEP patients had lower probabilities of an upheld decision in two of the three settings: GP (RR 0.58, 95% CI 0.22 to 0.94) and non-psychiatric hospitals (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.43 to 0.96). The estimate for psychiatric hospitals was inconclusive (RR 0.71, 95% CI 0.24 to 1.66).

Findings were similar across all model specifications (online supplemental tables M0 and M2).

Discussion

In this nationwide study of more than 37 million healthcare contacts and over 5000 formal complaints, we found considerable variance in who complains about healthcare. Low-income groups were more likely to submit complaints, while higher education levels generally predicted fewer complaints in GP and non-psychiatric hospitals but more in psychiatric hospitals. Patients outside the workforce and retirees complained more, while elderly in general and multimorbid non-psychiatric patients were significantly underrepresented. The overall patterns remained when comparing combined socioeconomic profiles, with a low-SEP profile showing substantially higher probabilities of submitting a complaint in GP and non-psychiatric hospital care.

For complaint outcomes, individual socioeconomic indicators showed no statistically significant differences, but the combined profiles indicated a general tendency for lower upheld probabilities among low-SEP profiles across settings. These patterns suggest that socioeconomic differences may be present at both stages of the complaint process, although the strength of the associations varies by setting. Upheld decisions depend on formal criteria, available documentation and how the concern is formulated within the complaint process. A complaint not being upheld should therefore not be interpreted as meaning that the patient’s concern was unimportant. Because upheld cases often trigger organisational reviews and improvement efforts,37 the lower upheld probabilities among low-SEP groups may influence which experiences become visible in formal organisational learning processes.

Comparison with existing literature

Complaint rates in our study were low when expressed per contact, ranging from 0.43 per 10 000 GP contacts to 12.7 per 10 000 psychiatric contacts. Previous studies have reported substantially higher rates, typically 1–9 complaints per 1000 contacts or admissions.63841 However, these figures are not directly comparable, as earlier studies often focused on single departments38 40 or specialties39 and commonly used admissions rather than all healthcare contacts (including outpatients) as the denominator.38 40 41 Using all healthcare contacts as the denominator provides a broader measure of the volume of care in which complaints may arise. However, a single complaint can relate to several encounters, meaning that using contacts as the denominator may yield a conservative estimate of complaint behaviour. These differences in denominators, study setting and complaint systems are therefore important when comparing complaint rates across studies. They may also partly explain why our findings differ from previous studies, which have often analysed selected complaint samples rather than complaint submission relative to the underlying healthcare contact population.

A recent rapid review found little consistent evidence for patient characteristics as predictors of complaints, emphasising the need for population-level studies with comprehensive socioeconomic data.9 Our study provides such evidence and suggests that socioeconomic and demographic predictors of complaints are more complex than previously assumed. Importantly, low-SEP healthcare users were not underrepresented among complainants.

Evidence from Denmark has documented inequalities in treatment quality. Parliamentary audits report poorer adherence to process indicators and worse outcomes among those with lower education, those without a partner or those outside the workforce,42 a pattern also confirmed in independent studies.4345 These broader inequities in treatment quality are compatible with our observation that disadvantaged patients complain more frequently. Yet they stand in contrast to the lack of socioeconomic differences in the outcomes of complaints.

Possible explanations and implications

Taken together, these findings suggest that disadvantaged groups may experience or perceive lower-quality care, although care quality was not directly measured in this study. Their higher complaint rates might reflect possible treatment deficiencies, different expectations, differences in healthcare use or a combination of these factors. Despite their increased risk of poor outcomes, elderly and multimorbid patients were notably underrepresented among complainants, despite evidence that these groups often receive less guideline-based treatment.4651 This creates a double disadvantage and risk of leaving areas with particular quality problems unattended in the quality improvement work.

Despite our results aligning with other Danish studies, they differ from earlier international studies. Several factors may contribute to this. First, our design departs from most earlier studies, which typically analyse only the complaints that are submitted. By linking all healthcare contacts in the population to the national complaint system, we can examine who does and who does not file complaints. Our socioeconomic indicators also originate from national registers assessed at the household level, which may capture social circumstances differently than survey-based measures. Second, behavioural and system-level biases may influence who reaches the formal complaint system. Patients with higher resources may resolve concerns informally, whereas low-SEP groups may rely more on formal pathways once problems escalate. Third, the structure of the Danish complaint system may also contribute. It is centralised, free of charge and embedded in a universal healthcare system, and patients can obtain procedural guidance, which may lower barriers relative to more fragmented or litigation-oriented systems. Finally, some patterns may be specific to Denmark. Two Danish studies found more complaints among patients in lower-income areas and among men with chronic illness, high healthcare use and rural residence,52 53 suggesting that health needs and exposure to care may shape complaint behaviour. Another Danish study showed that people with lower education or income, elderly and those in rural or minority communities were just as likely and in some cases more likely to want to complain as other groups.54 Together, these findings suggest that socioeconomic differences in complaints may reflect differences in illness burden and care pathways as much as differences in willingness to complain.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Only formal written complaints were included, while informally or verbally expressed concerns were not captured. The analyses were based on registered healthcare contacts and therefore do not address access to healthcare before such contacts occur. As with all register-based studies, the data were not collected for our specific research questions. This may introduce some misclassification, although socioeconomic and demographic variables were obtained from national administrative registers with consistent measurement across the study population. Missing data were minimal but may still add uncertainty, and complaint procedures can change over time, so patterns observed in 2022 may not fully reflect longer-term trends. These issues are unlikely to create spurious associations but may weaken some of the observed differences.

Some residual confounding may remain. The direction and magnitude of any residual confounding are uncertain, as unmeasured factors such as health literacy, trust, prior experiences with healthcare or severity of the underlying problem may be associated with both SEP and complaint behaviour.55 The centralised Danish complaint system also differs from complaint systems in other countries, particularly those that are more fragmented or litigation-oriented. Social and institutional norms around complaining may therefore vary between countries and limit the generalisability of the findings. Nevertheless, the results provide insight into how equity in access to mechanisms for voicing dissatisfaction can vary even within a universal healthcare system that actively seeks to promote equal access to complaint remedies.

Implications for practice and policy

Our findings have implications for how complaints are used in quality monitoring and equity initiatives. Higher complaint rates among low-income patients, combined with their lower approval rates, should be interpreted in light of existing research documenting socioeconomic inequalities in healthcare quality. This supports the importance of considering all complaints, not only upheld cases, when using complaints to guide improvement efforts.

Importantly, our results also speak to the concern raised in the introduction: that complaint-based quality improvement might disadvantage marginalised groups. In the Danish context, this concern appears less warranted. If all complaints are included, complaint-driven improvement is more likely to direct attention towards patients with fewer resources, though elderly patients remain underrepresented and may require separate strategies.

Finally, complaints should be assessed alongside other quality and safety indicators to support a more comprehensive and equitable system of learning.56

Conclusion

In this nationwide study, socioeconomic and demographic factors were linked to clear differences in who submits formal complaints. Although individual SEP indicators showed no consistent associations with upheld decisions, the combined profiles indicated lower approval probabilities for patients with lower SEP, except for psychiatric care, where estimates were inconclusive. Our findings also suggest that some patient groups with fewer socioeconomic resources appear more likely to raise concerns through the complaint system. It is essential to take complaints seriously, regardless of their outcome, as they point to areas where inequities in care may persist and where improvement efforts might be needed.
