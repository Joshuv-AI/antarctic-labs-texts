Enhancing anti-tumor immunity by targeting BATF and the STAT1/PD-L1 pathway in cervical carcinoma
Authors: Xing Peng, Yuanyuan Zhu, Yaqing Han, Chunyan Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCervical carcinoma remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women worldwide, with poor prognosis often linked to immune evasion mechanisms. The Basic Leucine Zipper Activating Transcription Factor (BATF) has emerged as a critical regulator of T-cell functionality, yet its role in cervical cancer progression and immune modulation remains poorly understood. This study investigates the role of BATF in cervical carcinoma, focusing on its effects on tumor progression, immune modulation, and immune checkpoint regulation, to identify BATF as a therapeutic target to enhance anti-tumor immunity.MethodsBATF expression was analyzed in cervical cancer tissues and cell lines. Functional assays, including cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, were performed following BATF knockdown. In vivo studies assessed tumor growth and metastasis in xenograft models. Immune cell populations, cytokine production, and immune checkpoint expression were analyzed using flow cytometry, quantitative reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), and immunohistochemistry. Mechanistic studies evaluated BATF regulation of programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) through the signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) signaling pathway. Finally, the therapeutic potential of BATF knockdown in combination with PD-L1 blockade was evaluated in vivo.ResultsAnalysis of patient-derived samples and cell lines revealed that BATF is overexpressed in cervical cancer and correlates with poor prognosis. Functional studies demonstrated that BATF promotes tumor proliferation, epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), migration, and invasion. In vivo, BATF knockdown significantly suppressed tumor growth and metastasis in xenograft models. Immune profiling revealed that BATF deficiency enhanced antitumor immunity by increasing CD8⁺ and CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration, increasing the production of CD8⁺ cytotoxic molecules such as granzyme B and IFN-γ while reducing tumor-associated macrophages and immune checkpoint expression, including Programmed death 1 (PD-1), T cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain-containing-3 (TIM-3), and Lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3.) Mechanistically, BATF regulated PD-L1 expression via the STAT1 signaling pathway, promoting immune evasion. Combination therapy with BATF knockdown and anti-PD-L1 antibodies significantly enhanced anti-tumor immunity and inhibited tumor growth compared to monotherapy.ConclusionsBATF plays a pivotal role in cervical cancer progression and immune evasion by regulating the STAT1/PD-L1 axis and modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Targeting BATF, alone or in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors (PD-L1), represents a promising therapeutic strategy to improve outcomes in cervical cancer patients. Further research is warranted to translate these findings into clinical applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05792-9.

Introduction

Cervical carcinoma, commonly referred to as cervical cancer, is a significant global health concern and a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide [1]. This malignancy originates in the cervix, the lower portion of the uterus that connects to the vagina and is primarily driven by persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV) [2, 3]. The pathogenesis of cervical cancer is typically preceded by a series of asymptomatic precancerous cellular changes, which progress slowly over time. These changes can be effectively identified through cervical screening methods such as Pap smears or HPV DNA testing [4]. Without timely detection and treatment, these precancerous lesions can evolve into invasive carcinoma.

The immune system plays a pivotal role in both the progression and prevention of cervical cancer [5]. Under normal circumstances, the immune system acts as a protective barrier, mounting responses against HPV infections to prevent malignant transformation [6]. Cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), a subset of T cells, are particularly important as they recognize and destroy HPV-infected cells, thereby reducing the risk of cancer development [7]. However, HPV has developed sophisticated immune evasion mechanisms, such as downregulating the expression of viral proteins on infected cell surfaces, which impairs CTL-mediated recognition and destruction [8]. This immune evasion contributes to persistent infection and tumorigenesis. Recent research has identified novel inflammation-associated immune biomarkers in cervical cancer, highlighting the dynamic interplay between the tumor microenvironment and immune responses. For example, a study has identified novel biomarkers of inflammation-associated immunity in cervical cancer [9]. Additionally, specific proteins such as ORMDL3, BRK1, and HMGN1 have been implicated to correlate with cervical cancer within specific immune cell populations, underscoring the complexity of immune regulation in this context [10]. These findings suggest that cervical cancer progression is closely associated with the modulation of immune responses, which could be targeted for therapeutic benefit.

Among the various factors influencing immune responses in cervical cancer, the Basic Leucine Zipper Activating Transcription Factor (BATF) has emerged as a critical transcription factor in T lymphocyte function [11]. BATF has been shown to regulate the differentiation and function of CD8+ T cells. Studies have shown that BATF is essential for the differentiation of effector CD8+ T cells and plays a key role in their activation and exhaustion following alloantigen exposure [12]. Notably, BATF expression is positively associated with the differentiation of effector CD8+ T cells, while BATF deficiency leads to the accumulation of exhausted CD8+ T cells, characterized by impaired cytotoxic activity [13]. While T cell exhaustion may be beneficial in conditions such as transplant tolerance, it poses a significant challenge in cancer, as exhausted CD8+ T cells are less effective at eliminating tumor cells [14]. This dual role of BATF underscores its importance in modulating immune responses within different pathological contexts.

Accumulating evidence suggests that signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins play central roles in modulating immune responses against cancer [15, 16]. STAT1 is a key regulator of cell proliferation, differentiation, and immune surveillance, though its role in tumorigenesis appears to be context-dependent and can exhibit both pro- and anti-tumorigenic functions [17–19]. Notably, STAT1 has been shown to promote the transcription of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), a critical immune checkpoint involved in tumor immune evasion. For instance, activation of the STAT1/PD-L1 axis facilitates immune escape in hepatocellular carcinoma through a CD8⁺ T cell-dependent mechanism [20]. In non-small cell lung cancer, targeting the IFN-γ-associated JAK-STAT1 pathway with Butein suppresses PD-L1 transcription and inhibits tumor growth [21]. Similarly, direct inhibition of STAT1 transcriptional activity has been reported to downregulate PD-L1 expression and potentiate anti-tumor immunity [22]. In the context of cervical cancer, elevated STAT1 expression has been correlated with increased PD-L1 levels in tumor cells, further highlighting the regulatory role of STAT1 in immune checkpoint modulation [23]. Despite these insights, the upstream regulatory mechanisms controlling STAT1 expression remain incompletely understood. In particular, the potential transcriptional or functional relationship between BATF and STAT1 has yet to be elucidated.

Despite significant advances in our understanding of cervical cancer pathogenesis, the role of BATF in modulating the immune response in cervical cancer remains poorly understood. This gap in knowledge limits our ability to design effective immunotherapeutic strategies targeting T-cell functionality. Given the dual role of BATF in promoting effector T cell differentiation and contributing to T cell exhaustion, investigating its impact on cervical cancer-specific immune responses could provide critical insights into disease progression and therapeutic opportunities. The present study aims to investigate the role of BATF in regulating the immune response to cervical carcinoma, with a specific focus on its impact on CD8+ T cell differentiation, function, and exhaustion. By elucidating the mechanisms through which BATF influences T cell-mediated tumor immunity, the study aims to identify potential pathways that can be targeted to enhance anti-tumor immune responses. This research seeks to bridge the gap between immune modulation and therapeutic interventions, ultimately contributing to the development of novel, BATF-focused immunotherapeutic strategies for cervical cancer.

Materials and methods

Patient sample collection

Cervical carcinoma tissue samples were collected from 20 patients undergoing surgical resection at our hospital. The diagnosis of cervical carcinoma was confirmed through histopathological evaluation by an expert pathologist. To ensure unbiased results, patients included in the study had not received any chemotherapy or radiotherapy prior to surgery. The control group also comprised 20 patients who underwent hysterectomy for benign gynecological conditions, such as uterine fibroids. For each patient, tissue samples were collected from both the tumor area and the surrounding non-tumor regions of the cervix. These non-tumor samples served as internal controls to distinguish tumor-specific changes.

Immediately following resection, all tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C to preserve RNA, protein, and cellular integrity for subsequent analyses. All patients provided written informed consent, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional Ethics Committee (Approval number: DW-P-2024-023-01) and adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell culture and transfection

Primary human cervical epithelial cells (HCerEpic, YS1527C, Yaji, China) and cervical cancer cell lines (Ms751: #YS529C, HPV18 and partial HPV-45 genome; SiHa: #YS269C, HPV-16; HeLa: #YS131C, HPV-18, Yaji, China) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) (11965-092, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10099-141, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15140-122, Gibco, USA). All cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂.

SiHa and HeLa cells were transfected with either short hairpin RNA targeting BATF (sh-BATF) or a non-targeting control (sh-NC), as well as with overexpression vectors (BATF or vector control), using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection efficiency was validated through quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) and Western blotting for BATF expression.

Cytokine stimulation and pharmacological inhibition

To investigate the effects of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) signaling, tumor cells were treated with 100 U/mL human recombinant IFN-γ (285-IF, R&D Systems, USA) for 24 h [24]. For experiments involving the inhibition of STAT1 signaling, cells were treated with 50 µM Fludarabine (S1491, Selleckchem, USA, dissolved in Dimethyl sulfoxide, DMSO), and cells in the control group were treated with 0.1% DMSO for 24 h [25]. All treatments were performed under standardized culture conditions, and untreated cells were used as controls.

Co-culture model for tumor cell and T-cell interaction

To simulate tumor-immune interactions and assess the impact of BATF modulation on the immune response, a co-culture model was established. SiHa and Hela cells stably transfected with either sh-BATF or sh-NC were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per well in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h.

Subsequently, CD3⁺ T cells were isolated from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) obtained from five healthy female donors (female, mean age 57.4 ± 4.16) using the Human T Cell Enrichment Kit (cat # 19051, STEMCELL Technologies, Canada). All procedures were approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval number: DW-P-2024-023-01). The isolated T cells were activated with recombinant human IL-2 (10 ng/mL, BioLegend), anti-CD3 antibody (OKT3, 2 µg/mL, BioLegend), and anti-CD28 antibody (5 µg/mL, BioLegend) for 3 days. Following activation, T cells were added to the tumor cell culture at a ratio of 10:1 (T cells: tumor cells) in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)−1640 medium (A1049101, Gibco, USA) containing 10% FBS and 100 IU/mL IL-2 (BioLegend). Co-cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ for 72 h. Subsequently, cells were harvested for further analysis.

Quantitative real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells and tissues using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74104, Qiagen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), with acceptable purity determined by an A260/A280 ratio of 1.8–2.0. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA using the SuperScript IV First-Strand Synthesis System (18091050, Invitrogen, USA). The reaction conditions were optimized according to the manufacturer’s protocol to ensure efficient and reliable reverse transcription. Quantitative PCR was performed using the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (A25742, Applied Biosystems, USA) on a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA). Each reaction was carried out in triplicate, with β-actin or glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) used as internal controls for normalization of gene expression data. The thermal cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used in this study are as follows: BATF: F 5’-AGGAGGAGGAAGGAGGAGGA-3’, R 5’-TGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTG-3’; STAT1: F 5’-TGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTG-3’, R 5’-AGGAGGAGGAAGGAGGAGGA-3’; PD-L1: F 5’-AGGAGGAGGAAGGAGGAGGA-3’, R 5’-TGGTGGTGGTGGTGGTG-3’; N-cadherin: F 5’-TGTGGGAATCCGACGAATGGATGA-3’, R 5’-TGGAGCCACTGCCTTCATAGTCAA-3’; E-cadherin: F 5’-AAGAAGCTGGCTGACATGTACGGA-3’, R 5’-CCACCAGCAACGTGATTTCTGCAT-3’.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared for immunohistochemistry by sequential deparaffinization in xylene, rehydration through graded ethanol, and antigen retrieval. Antigen retrieval was performed using a heat-induced epitope retrieval method in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 minutes, followed by cooling to room temperature. To block endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) for 15 minutes at room temperature. Positive and negative controls were included in all IHC experiments (Supplementary Fig. 1A-B). For negative controls, the primary antibody was replaced with non-immune mouse serum to confirm antibody specificity. To minimize non-specific binding, sections were incubated with a protein-blocking solution prior to antibody application. The slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against BATF (cat # PA5-104545, 1:200, Thermo Fisher, USA) and PD-L1 (cat # 13684, 1:200, Cell Signaling, USA) at optimized dilutions determined through preliminary titration experiments. After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. The signal was developed using the 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) Substrate Kit (SK-4100, Vector Laboratories, USA), followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin to visualize cell nuclei. Slides were examined under a light microscope by two independent pathologists blinded to experimental conditions. For each sample, five randomly selected high-power fields were analyzed. Immunoreactivity was scored semi-quantitatively based on the intensity and percentage of positively stained cells. The scoring system incorporated both staining intensity (0: no staining, 1: weak, 2: moderate, 3: strong) and the proportion of positive cells (0: 0%, 1: <25%, 2: 25–50%, 3: >50%). The final IHC score was obtained by summing the scores for staining intensity and the percentage of positive cells.

Western blot

Total protein was isolated from cells and tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and quantified using the Bicinchoninic Acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of total protein from each sample were loaded onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA) following separation by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBS-T) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies at optimized dilutions: BATF (cat # ab302568, 1:1000, Abcam, UK), N-cadherin (cat # 13116, 1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), E-cadherin (cat # 3195, 1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), STAT1 (cat # 14994, 1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), PD-L1 (cat # 13684, 1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA), and β-actin (cat # 4970, 1:1000, Cell Signaling, USA) as a loading control. After thorough washing with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (7074, 1:2000, Cell Signaling, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Lysates from cells known to express the target proteins were included as positive controls (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). Protein bands were visualized using the ECL Substrate (cat # 32106, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and imaged with the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ analysis software, and protein expression levels were normalized to β-actin.

EdU staining for cell proliferation assessment

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 488 Imaging Kit (cat # C10337, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were cultured in appropriate conditions and incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h. Cells not treated with EdU served as negative controls (Supplementary Fig. 1D). Following incubation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized using 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; D1306, Invitrogen, USA) for 5 min. Finally, imaging was performed using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at 40× magnification with an exposure time of 800 ms. For each sample, images were acquired from five randomly selected fields. The proliferation rate was quantified by calculating the ratio of EdU-positive nuclei (green fluorescence) to the total number of DAPI-stained nuclei (blue fluorescence) using ImageJ software.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay for cell viability

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (MA0218, Meilunbio, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well in 100 µL of complete growth medium and allowed to adhere overnight. The assay was performed at three time points: 24-, 48-, and 72-hours post-seeding. At each time point, 10 µL of the CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. Following incubation, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA). Each condition was tested in triplicate, and wells containing only medium and CCK-8 solution without cells served as blanks to correct for background absorbance. Cell viability was calculated as the mean absorbance of experimental wells after subtracting the blank absorbance.

Transwell assay for cell migration and invasion

Cell migration and invasion were evaluated using 8-µm pore size Transwell inserts (Corning, USA). For migration, 5 × 10⁴ cells in a serum-free medium were seeded in the upper chamber without Matrigel. For invasion, 1 × 10⁵ cells were seeded in Matrigel-coated upper chambers. The lower chamber contained a medium with 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. After 24 h of incubation at 37 °C, non-migrated or non-invaded cells were removed, and the membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Migrated or invaded cells on the lower surface were visualized and quantified under a microscope. Data were averaged from five random fields.

Animal model

BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment under controlled temperature and humidity. All animal experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of our institution.

In one set of experiments, the mice were randomly assigned to the sh-NC group (n = 5) and the sh-BATF1/2 groups (n = 5 per group). SiHa cells (1 × 10⁶) transfected with either sh-NC or sh-BATF were suspended in 100 µL of PBS and subcutaneously injected into the dorsal region of each mouse. Tumor growth was monitored over a 30-day period, after which the mice were euthanized. Tumors were carefully dissected, photographed, and weighed. Tumor volume (mm3) was measured using calipers and calculated using the formula: tumor volume = length × width2 × 0.5.

For the metastasis model, 1 × 10⁶ SiHa cells stably transfected with sh-NC or sh-BATF1/2 was injected into the tail vein of mice. After 30 days, the mice were euthanized. To visualize tumor burden, mice were anesthetized and intraperitoneally injected with D-luciferin (30 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) 10 min before imaging. Luminescent signals from tumor cells were detected using the IVIS Imaging System (PerkinElmer, USA). Metastatic tumor burden was assessed by manually counting individual lesions from metastatic tissue sections in different groups. Thereafter, the data was analyzed using the GraphPad Prism software.

In a separate set of experiments, 5 mice per group were intraperitoneally injected with either a control (PBS) or anti-PD-1 antibody (BE0273, 100 µg per mouse, dissolved in PBS; BioXCell) every three days for a total of six injections. Treatments began five days after the inoculation of mice with SiHa cells. On day thirty, tumor-bearing mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were photographed and collected for analysis. Tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T-cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell suspensions were prepared from tumor tissues by enzymatic digestion followed by mechanical dissociation. Cells were filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer to ensure a uniform suspension and washed with PBS supplemented with 2% FBS. Cells were stained with a panel of fluorescently labeled antibodies targeting specific surface and intracellular markers (all purchased from BioLegend, USA), including anti-MHCII (cat # 107605), anti-CD11c (cat # 117309), anti-CD45 (cat # 103105), anti-CD19 (cat # 115505), anti-NK1.1 (cat # 108705), anti-CD11b (cat # 101205), anti-F4/80 (cat # 123105), anti-CD3 (cat # 100205), anti-CD4 (cat # 100405), anti-CD8 (cat # 100705), anti-IFNγ (cat # 505805), anti-TNFα (cat # 506305), anti-granzyme B (cat # 515405), anti-PD-1 (cat # 135205), anti-LAG-3 (cat # 125205), and anti-TIM-3 (cat # 119705).

Surface staining was performed by incubating cells with antibodies in a staining buffer (PBS + 2% FBS) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. For intracellular markers (e.g., IFNγ, TNFα, and granzyme B), cells were fixed and permeabilized using the CytoFix/CytoPerm Kit (BD Biosciences, USA) prior to antibody incubation. After staining, cells were washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed immediately. Finally, cells were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

Luciferase reporter assay

The transcriptional activity of STAT1 in wild-type (WT) and mutant (Mut) forms was assessed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910, Promega, USA). Reporter plasmids containing either the WT or Mut STAT1 promoter sequences were co-transfected with a Renilla luciferase plasmid (internal control) into the target cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol.

After 48 h of transfection, cells were lysed using the lysis buffer, and luciferase activity was measured using a luminometer (Berthold Technologies, Germany). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to control for transfection efficiency. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and results were expressed as the relative luciferase activity (firefly/Renilla ratio).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

The SimpleChIP Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (9003, Cell Signaling Technology, USA) was used to assess STAT1 binding to target gene promoters. Cells were first cross-linked with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with glycine, and then chromatin was sheared to 200–500 bp fragments. Sheared chromatin was immunoprecipitated with anti-STAT1 antibody (14994, Cell Signaling, USA) or control IgG using protein G magnetic beads. Immunoprecipitated DNA was eluted, reverse cross-linked, purified, and analyzed by qRT-PCR using primers targeting STAT1 binding sites. Results were expressed as fold enrichment relative to the input DNA.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, USA). Group comparisons were performed using the student’s t-test for two-group analyses and one-way ANOVA for multi-group comparisons, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to account for pairwise differences. Correlations between gene expression levels were assessed using Pearson correlation analysis. Kaplan-Meier Plotter database (https://kmplot.com/analysis/) [26] was used for survival analysis of cervical cancer patients using the log-rank test, with P < 0.05 as the cutoff value. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and reliability.

Results

High BATF expression correlates with poor prognosis in cervical cancer

To investigate the clinical relevance of BATF in cervical cancer, we first analyzed its expression levels in patient-derived samples and cell lines. BATF mRNA levels were significantly higher in cervical cancer tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). Consistently, cervical cancer cell lines displayed markedly higher BATF mRNA levels than normal cervical epithelial cells (p < 0.01) (Figs. 1B-C). At the protein level, Western blot analysis demonstrated a significant upregulation of BATF in tumor tissues relative to adjacent normal tissues, and BATF was mainly distributed in the cytoplasm and nucleus (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1D). This finding was corroborated by IHC analysis, which revealed higher BATF staining intensity in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1E). To assess the prognostic significance of BATF expression, a Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted. Patients with high BATF expression had significantly worse overall survival compared to those with low BATF expression (Log-rank p = 0.012, Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that BATF may function as a marker for poor prognosis in cervical carcinoma.

BATF knockdown suppresses cervical cancer progression by inhibiting proliferation, migration, and invasiveness in vitro

Given the observed association between high BATF expression and poor prognosis, we next explored its functional role in cervical cancer using in vitro models. SiHa and HeLa cervical cancer cell lines were transfected with either BATF-targeting short hairpin RNA (sh-BATF) or a non-targeting control (sh-NC). Transfection efficiency was validated by Western blot analysis, confirming a significant reduction in BATF protein levels in the sh-BATF group compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2A). To evaluate the impact of BATF on cell proliferation, EdU incorporation assays revealed a significantly reduced percentage of EdU-positive cells in BATF-knockdown cells compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2B). This was further supported by results from cell viability assays (CCK-8), which showed a marked decrease in cell viability following BATF depletion (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2C). We then investigated whether BATF regulates epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a critical process in cancer metastasis. Western blot analysis showed that BATF knockdown led to increased expression of E-cadherin (epithelial marker) and decreased N-cadherin expression (mesenchymal marker), indicating that BATF promotes EMT (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2D). Additionally, Transwell assays demonstrated significantly reduced cell migration and invasion in BATF-knockdown cells compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Figs. 2E-F). These results suggest that BATF promotes cervical cancer progression by enhancing proliferation, EMT, migration, and invasion.

BATF knockdown suppresses tumor growth and metastasis in cervical cancer in vivo

To validate the in vitro findings, we investigated the effects of BATF knockdown on tumor growth and metastasis in vivo using a xenograft mouse model. SiHa cells transfected with either sh-BATF or sh-NC were injected into the recipient mice. Results showed that the tumor growth was significantly suppressed in the sh-BATF group, as indicated by reduced tumor volume and weight compared to the control group mice (p < 0.01) (Figs. 3A-C). In alignment with the in vitro results, analysis of EMT markers in xenograft tumors showed increased E-cadherin expression and decreased N-cadherin levels in the BATF knockdown group (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3D), supporting the notion that BATF facilitates EMT and metastatic progression. Moreover, the number of metastatic nodules was markedly reduced in mice injected with BATF-knockdown cells compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3E). These results confirm that BATF plays a pivotal role in promoting cervical cancer growth and metastasis.

BATF deficiency in tumor cells induces host antitumor immunity

Given the observed role of BATF in promoting tumor progression, we investigated whether BATF affects the tumor immune microenvironment. Flow cytometry analysis of immune cell populations in tumor tissues revealed that BATF knockdown did not significantly alter the proportions of dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, or B cells (p > 0.05) (Fig. 4A). However, macrophage levels (CD11b⁺F4/80⁺/CD45⁺) were significantly decreased in BATF-deficient tumors, while the populations of T lymphocytes such as CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells (CD3⁺/CD45⁺, CD4⁺/CD45⁺, and CD8⁺/CD45⁺) were markedly increased (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4B). Within the CD8⁺ T cell population, BATF knockdown enhanced cytotoxic activity, as evidenced by a significant increase in the production of granzyme B and IFN-γ, accompanied by a reduction in the population of TNF-α-producing CD8⁺ T cells (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4C). Similarly, in the CD4⁺ T cell population, we observed a substantial increase in IFN-γ production and a reduction in TNF-α-producing cells, indicating an enhanced antitumor immune response (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, BATF deficiency significantly downregulated the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, including LAG-3, TIM-3, and PD-1, on both CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, further promoting their antitumor activity (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4D and F). These findings suggest that BATF knockdown reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment to favor an antitumor immune response by modulating T cell activity and immune checkpoint expression.

To further evaluate tumor-immune interactions and the role of BATF in modulating the immune response, a co-culture model was established. SiHa and Hela cells stably transfected with either sh-BATF or sh-NC were co-cultured with human CD3⁺ T cells at a tumor cell-to-T cell ratio of 1:10 for 72 h. BATF is indispensable for PD-L1 gene expression

Given the role of BATF in modulating the immune response, we investigated its potential involvement in regulating the expression of PD-L1, a key immune checkpoint molecule that inhibits T cell function. A significant positive correlation between BATF and PD-L1 expression was observed in cervical tumor tissues (p = 0.00011; Fig. 5A). To further validate this correlation, we measured the gene expression level of PD-L1 in tumor infiltrating immune cells. qRT-PCR analysis revealed a significant reduction in PD-L1 gene expression in sh-BATF tumors compared to control (sh-NC) tumor cells (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5B). This finding was corroborated by flow cytometry analysis of single-cell tumor suspensions, which demonstrated a decrease in PD-L1 expression on CD45⁺ immune cells in sh-BATF tumors compared to sh-NC tumors (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, IHC analysis of mouse tumor tissues confirmed lower cytoplasmic and membranous PD-L1 expression in the sh-BATF group, further supporting the regulatory role of BATF (p < 0.01) (Fig. 5D).

IFN-γ is a cytokine produced by activated T cells and NK cells that plays a central role in antitumor immunity by enhancing immune cell activity. However, in the tumor microenvironment, IFN-γ also induces the expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells, which facilitates immune evasion by inhibiting T cell-mediated cytotoxicity [27]. To investigate whether BATF is involved in the regulation of PD-L1 in response to immune signaling, IFN-γ stimulation was employed as a physiological model to mimic this immune-driven pathway. For this purpose, tumor cells were stimulated with IFN-γ, and subsequent qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses showed a decrease in PD-L1 expression in sh-BATF cells compared to controls (p < 0.01) (Figs. 5E-F). Collectively, these results indicate that BATF is critical for the IFN-γ-induced expression of PD-L1, further supporting its role in immune evasion.

BATF regulates PD-L1 expression through the STAT1 signaling pathway

To elucidate the mechanism by which BATF regulates PD-L1 expression, we investigated the role of STAT1, a transcription factor known to regulate PD-L1 expression and a downstream effector of IFN-γ signaling. Data demonstrated that STAT1 expression was significantly upregulated in cervical cancer tissues BATF (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). Pearson correlation analysis demonstrated a strong positive correlation between BATF and STAT1 expression, supporting a regulatory relationship between these two factors (Fig. 6B). To explore whether BATF influences STAT1 expression, BATF knockdown was performed in cervical cancer cell lines. The results showed that BATF knockdown significantly reduced STAT1 expression, as measured by qRT-PCR and Western blot (p < 0.01) (Figs. 6C-D). These findings suggest that BATF may regulate PD-L1 expression via STAT1.

BATF knockdown enhances the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade immunotherapy

Given the role of BATF in regulating PD-L1 expression, we hypothesized that BATF knockdown could enhance the therapeutic efficacy of PD-L1 blockade immunotherapy. To test this hypothesis, tumor-bearing mice were treated with anti-PD-L1 antibodies in the presence or absence of BATF knockdown. Mice were divided into four groups: (1) control (untreated), (2) anti-PD-L1 monotherapy, (3) anti-PD-L1 + sh-NC monotherapy, and (4) anti-PD-L1 + sh-BATF combination therapy. Results revealed that the tumor volume and weight were significantly reduced in the anti-PD-L1 + sh-BATF group compared to all other groups, including the anti-PD-L1 monotherapy group (p < 0.01) (Figs. 7A-C). These findings indicate that BATF knockdown synergizes with PD-L1 blockade to inhibit tumor growth.

Discussion

This study underscores the critical role of BATF in promoting cervical carcinoma progression and modulating the tumor immune microenvironment. Elevated BATF expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis in patients with cervical cancer, as evidenced by its higher mRNA and protein levels in cancerous tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues. These findings align with previous studies that have linked BATF overexpression to the progression of various malignancies, including lymphoma and melanoma, where it promotes tumor proliferation, invasion, and immune evasion [28–31]. Our findings highlight the clinical significance of BATF as both a prognostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target in cervical cancer.

Functionally, we demonstrated that BATF contributes to cervical cancer progression by promoting tumor cell proliferation, EMT, migration, and invasion. In vitro experiments showed that BATF knockdown significantly reduced these malignant phenotypes, supporting its role as an oncogene. Consistent with the in vitro findings, in vivo studies using xenograft models confirmed that BATF depletion suppressed tumor growth and metastasis. EMT is a key process driving cancer metastasis [32], and our results indicate that BATF facilitates this process by downregulating E-cadherin (an epithelial marker) and upregulating N-cadherin (a mesenchymal marker). These findings are in line with previous studies [33, 34] and underscore BATF role as a critical regulator of EMT and metastatic potential in cervical carcinoma.

Importantly, this study highlights the immunomodulatory effects of BATF in the tumor microenvironment. BATF knockdown resulted in significant changes in immune cell populations, including a reduction in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and an increase in CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T lymphocytes. TAMs are known to promote tumor growth and immune suppression [35–38], and their reduction in BATF-deficient tumors likely diminishes their protumorigenic effects. Conversely, the increase in CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cells, both critical for orchestrating the immune response [39, 40], highlights the impact of BATF on T-cell recruitment and activation. CD4⁺ T cells, often referred to as helper T cells, primarily activate other immune system components [41]. On the other hand, CD8⁺ T cells, also known as CTLs, are crucial for defending against intracellular pathogens such as viruses and bacteria, as well as for monitoring and eliminating tumor cells [42]. Previous studies have shown that BATF regulates CD8⁺ T cell differentiation during chronic viral infections [12, 43, 44], highlighting the potential of BATF as a therapeutic target to restore CTL function and enhance antiviral immunity. In line with this, our study demonstrated that BATF expression was upregulated in HPV-positive cervical cancer cell lines, implicating its potential role in virus-associated oncogenesis. BATF deficiency amplified the cytotoxic efficiency of CD8⁺ T cells, as demonstrated by the heightened expression of IFN-γ and granzyme B. IFN-γ, a cytotoxic cytokine, induces apoptosis in tumor cells by promoting an inflammatory immune response and enhancing antigen presentation [45, 46]. Granzyme B, a serine protease secreted by CTLs, directly induces apoptosis in target cells by activating caspases and cleaving intracellular proteins [47, 48]. These findings indicate that BATF suppresses T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity, potentially by shaping the tumor immune microenvironment.

Additionally, BATF knockdown reduced the expression of immune checkpoint molecules, including PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3. These molecules are critical modulators of T-cell activity, functioning to either stimulate or suppress the immune response [49–52]. The diminished expression of these inhibitory molecules suggests that BATF deficiency may enhance T cell functionality by reducing inhibitory signals, thereby promoting an effective anti-tumor immune response.

One of the most significant findings of this study is the mechanistic link between BATF and PD-L1 expression, a key immune checkpoint molecule that facilitates immune evasion by inhibiting T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity [53, 54]. We observed a strong positive correlation between BATF and PD-L1 expression in cervical cancer tissues. Functional studies revealed that BATF knockdown significantly reduced PD-L1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels. Notably, BATF was found to regulate PD-L1 expression in response to IFN-γ stimulation, a physiological model that mimics immune-driven upregulation of PD-L1. This indicates that BATF is essential for IFN-γ-induced PD-L1 expression, contributing to immune suppression in the tumor microenvironment [55]. While PD-L1 blockade immunotherapy has transformed cancer treatment, its efficacy remains limited, with response rates rarely exceeding 40% in most cancers [56, 57]. Our findings suggest that targeting BATF could provide a novel approach to enhance the effectiveness of PD-L1 blockade therapies.

Our study also provides mechanistic insights into the regulation of PD-L1 by BATF. We identified a strong positive correlation between BATF and PD-L1 expression in cervical cancer tissues and demonstrated that BATF regulates PD-L1 expression through the STAT1 signaling pathway. STAT1, a transcription factor activated by IFN-γ signaling, is a well-established regulator of PD-L1 expression and plays a critical role in immune responses and apoptosis [58, 59]. While STAT1 is generally considered a pro-death and tumor-suppressive signaling molecule, its activation also drives the expression of immune-related genes, contributing to antiviral and antipathogen defenses, suppression of cell proliferation, and induction of apoptosis [60–62]. In our study, BATF knockdown reduced STAT1 expression, and inhibition of STAT1 expression abrogated BATF-mediated upregulation of PD-L1. Luciferase reporter and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays further confirmed that BATF interacts with STAT1 to promote transcriptional activation of the PD-L1 promoter. These findings establish BATF as a critical regulator of the STAT1/PD-L1 axis, underscoring its role in immune evasion and highlighting a potential therapeutic target.

Another significant finding of this study is the potential for BATF knockdown to enhance the efficacy of PD-L1 blockade immunotherapy. PD-L1 inhibitors have transformed cancer treatment but achieve clinical benefit in only a subset of patients, often due to insufficient anti-tumor immunity [63, 64]. In our study, the combination therapy with BATF knockdown and anti-PD-L1 antibodies significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to monotherapy, highlighting the potential of BATF as a therapeutic target to augment immune checkpoint blockade. Mechanistically, this enhanced therapeutic efficacy was associated with increased CD8⁺ T cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity in the tumor microenvironment. These results suggest that targeting BATF could overcome resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors and improve therapeutic outcomes.

Despite these promising findings, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The precise molecular mechanisms by which BATF modulates macrophage polarization and T-cell recruitment remain unclear. Additionally, while our results demonstrate that BATF interacts with STAT1 to regulate PD-L1 expression, the involvement of other signaling pathways or co-factors cannot be excluded. Furthermore, the interplay between BATF and other immune cells, such as regulatory T cells or myeloid-derived suppressor cells, warrants further investigation. Future studies should aim to address these questions and evaluate the safety and efficacy of BATF-targeting therapies in preclinical and clinical settings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides compelling evidence that BATF plays a multifaceted role in cervical cancer by promoting tumor progression, modulating the immune microenvironment, and regulating immune checkpoint expression. Targeting BATF, either alone or in combination with PD-L1 blockade, represents a promising therapeutic strategy to improve outcomes in cervical cancer. Further research is needed to fully elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying BATF-mediated immune regulation and to develop targeted interventions for clinical application.
