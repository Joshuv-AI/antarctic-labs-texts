Non-ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction Secondary to Embolic Prosthetic Valve Thrombus

ABSTRACT

Timeline of Events Summarizing the Key Cardiovascular Interventions and the Clinical Presentation in This Case

The patient underwent transcatheter aortic valve replacement in July 2023 and Watchman device placement in April 2024 for atrial fibrillation and fall risk. She subsequently presented in March 2025 with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction, ultimately found to be caused by embolic thrombus from prosthetic valve leaflet thrombosis.

On examination, her vital signs were stable, and the physical exam was noncontributory. She described her pain as pressure-like, located in the epigastric region, occasionally radiating down her abdomen. Her pain was nonexertional and was reproducible with palpation.

Past Medical History

Severe aortic stenosis treated with transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in July 2023, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation with elective Watchman device placement in April 2024 due to moderate fall risk, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type 2 diabetes mellitus, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (left ventricular ejection fraction 55%-60%), and chronic anemia.

Differential Diagnosis

Given the patient's symptoms of intermittent chest discomfort and palpitations, along with a history of TAVR, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular comorbidities, the differential diagnosis included atypical angina or myocardial ischemia, arrhythmia-related symptoms, and coronary embolism; gastrointestinal causes such as reflux or peptic ulcer disease; musculoskeletal chest wall pain given reproducibility with palpation; and less likely but critical considerations such as pulmonary embolism, aortic pathology, or progression of structural heart disease.

Investigations

Initial electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm/sinus tachycardia with nonspecific ST changes, and serial troponins were elevated.

Coronary angiography demonstrated a thrombus extending from the distal left main coronary artery into the proximal left circumflex artery in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease, suggestive of embolic etiology (Figure 2). Optical coherence tomography further confirmed embolic etiology rather than a plaque rupture. Management

Following identification of an embolic coronary event likely originating from the bioprosthetic aortic valve, the patient was started on anticoagulation therapy. She received intravenous unfractionated heparin during hospitalization and was transitioned to long-term oral anticoagulation with apixaban. She was discharged in stable condition with plans for close outpatient follow-up.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Since discharge, the patient has been followed up in outpatient cardiology clinic. She reported no recurrence of chest discomfort, palpitations, or other ischemic symptoms. She has been tolerating apixaban well, with no reported episodes of bleeding or adverse effects. At the time of follow-up, she remained clinically stable and functionally improved compared to her hospitalization. No follow-up imaging has yet been performed to reassess the status of the prosthetic valve thrombus, but outpatient cardiac CT is planned for further evaluation. Continued monitoring of anticoagulation and surveillance imaging is anticipated as part of her long-term management strategy.

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is typically caused by plaque disruption or erosion of an unstable coronary artery atherosclerotic plaque. Less commonly, ACS may result from coronary embolism, spasm, or dissection. Coronary embolism accounts for approximately 2.9% of de novo ACS cases, with atrial fibrillation being the most common cause. Embolic events originating from cardiac structures such as prosthetic valves or tumors are rare but clinically significant due to their recurrent potential.1,2

TAVR has increasingly supplanted surgical aortic valve replacement, particularly in older and high-risk patients. With its rising use, valve-related complications such as leaflet thrombosis are gaining clinical attention. These events may present silently or, as in rare cases, with systemic embolism and myocardial infarction.3,4

Current American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines recommend vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) as first-line therapy for valve thrombosis in patients with concurrent atrial fibrillation. However, emerging evidence suggests that direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) may offer similar efficacy, although this remains an area of clinical uncertainty.5,6

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but important complication of TAVR: prosthetic valve leaflet thrombosis leading to embolic ACS. While coronary embolism has been reported in up to 3% of ACS, the subset related specifically to valve thrombi remains rare and poorly characterized in large registries.3 As TAVR surpasses surgical aortic valve replacement in volume, especially among elderly and high-risk patients, the burden of such valve-related complications is expected to rise.7

Leaflet thrombosis post TAVR often presents silently and may only be detected through imaging as hypoattenuated leaflet thickening (HALT). In most instances, HALT is incidental; however, in rare cases such as this, the thrombus may embolize and cause significant end-organ ischemia, including myocardial infarction. The embolic territory in this case—the distal left main extending into the left circumflex—poses particularly high risk of hemodynamic compromise. Although initial angiography may appear nonobstructive, the embolic nature of the thrombus becomes clear with detailed imaging and clinical context.

Diagnostic imaging plays a pivotal role in these cases. While TEE is often used for assessing for intracardiac sources of embolization, it has limited ability to visualize prosthetic aortic valve leaflets due to acoustic shadowing and suboptimal angles. Routine transthoracic echocardiogram surveillance in this patient similarly failed to detect leaflet thrombus, further underscoring the limited sensitivity of echocardiograph. Cardiac CT, by contrast, provides high-resolution visualization of leaflet motion and morphology and has become the gold standard for detecting HALT and reduced leaflet motion.8,9

Therapeutic decision-making in TAVR-related thrombosis remains an evolving area. Although VKAs have long been favored, especially in patients with coexisting atrial fibrillation, data comparing VKAs to DOACs remain inconclusive. Observational studies and subgroup analyses have demonstrated both resolution and recurrence with DOAC use, suggesting the need for individualized decisions and further prospective trials.5,6,10 In this patient, apixaban was specifically chosen based on several considerations: her favorable prior experience and tolerability before the Watchman device implantation, moderate fall risk prompting the original choice of DOAC over VKA, and the presence of the Watchman device which significantly reduced stroke risk and partially mitigated the need for VKAs. Balancing thrombotic risk with her bleeding risk profile and prior clinical experience justified our decision. Nonetheless, anticoagulation management post TAVR thrombosis remains an area of uncertainty, underscoring the need for individualized therapy pending more definitive prospective trial data.

Conclusions

This case underscores the importance of considering embolic etiologies in patients presenting with ACS, particularly prosthetic heart valves or atrial appendage occlusion devices. Prosthetic valve leaflet thrombosis, although rare, can lead to coronary embolism and ACS. When TEE is inconclusive, cardiac CT should be pursued for accurate visualization of valve structures. Early recognition of this complication is critical, as it has significant therapeutic implications. While VKAs remain the standard for treatment, DOACs provide comparable efficacy, although this is an area of ongoing investigation.
