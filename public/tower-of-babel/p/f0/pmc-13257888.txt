Intranasal Delivery of Bacterial Extracellular Vesicles Enables RNA Cargo Entry Into the Brain
Authors: Jae Yeong Ha, So‐Mi Kim, Song‐Yi Choi, Chanyong Park, Sungsu Park, Su‐Hyung Hong, Il‐Sung Jang, Youngkyun Lee, Heon‐Jin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTExtracellular vesicles (EVs) released by bacteria are potent mediators of host–microbe interactions. They modulate immune responses, deliver functional molecules and influence disease progression. However, whether bacterial EVs can access the brain and functionally affect host cells remains unclear. In this study, we engineered Escherichia coli‐derived EVs by electroporating Cre recombinase mRNA (Ec EVCre) and assessed their transport and functional delivery following intranasal administration. Using mT/mG reporter mice, we observed EV uptake in the olfactory epithelium and recombination‐driven GFP expression in a subset of neurons in the olfactory bulb, providing proof‐of‐concept for the functional delivery of bacterial EV‐associated mRNA into the brain. Single‐cell RNA sequencing and imaging analyses of the olfactory regions revealed neuronal and immune cell subsets as key EV targets. Microfluidic biochip chamber assays with cultured sensory neurons demonstrated that EVs undergo retrograde axonal transport from neurite terminals to the soma via signalling endosomes. Pharmacological inhibition significantly impaired EV uptake, supporting the involvement of endocytic pathways. In addition to neuronal entry, we discovered that phagocytic cells, including neutrophils and macrophages, can engulf EVCre in the nasal mucosa and migrate into the brain, providing an alternative immune‐mediated route for vesicle delivery. Together, these findings indicate that bacterial EVs exploit both neuronal and phagocytic pathways to deliver functional RNA cargo into the brain, providing novel insights into microbial access to the central nervous system and its implications for neuroimmune interactions.

Introduction

Bacteria are increasingly recognized as influential modulators of neuronal activity and brain health; their effects are mediated through both direct and indirect interactions (Cryan et al. 2019). Recent studies have revealed that bacteria and their secreted products can modify neuronal excitability, regulate neuroplasticity‐related gene expression and influence central circuits underlying cognition, behaviour and inflammation (Al Noman et al. 2025; Park et al. 2025). Thus, the central nervous system (CNS), once considered immunologically privileged and physically protected from microbial invasion through specialized endothelial and epithelial barriers, is now recognized to exhibit robust immune activity along its borders (Smyth and Kipnis 2025). Most pathogens are effectively blocked, underscoring the formidable nature of these barriers in restricting neuroinvasion (Ampie and McGavern 2022). However, recent evidence suggests that host–bacterial interactions in the brain remain incompletely understood, with bacterial effectors being capable of modulating barrier properties or exploiting dedicated transmigration pathways (Al‐Obaidi and Desa 2018). These findings highlight the ability of microbial signals, transmitted via metabolites, proteins, or vesicles, to profoundly affect neuronal physiology. However, the precise mechanisms and outcomes of these host–microbe interactions, particularly within the CNS, remain unclear (Masuzzo et al. 2020; Yang and Chiu 2017). We recently reported that extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from bacteria can cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB) and deliver functional cargo into the host brain (Ha et al. 2020, Ha et al. 2023; Han et al. 2019). Bacterial EVs (bEVs) are nano‐sized (20–400 nm), membrane‐bound particles released by both gram‐negative and gram‐positive bacteria (Kim et al. 2015). These vesicles carry a wide range of biologically active cargo, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and small RNAs, which enhance bacterial survival, virulence and communication with host cells (Choi et al. 2017). These components can be delivered to host cells by bEVs through various mechanisms, including endocytosis, lipid rafts and membrane fusion (Mulcahy et al. 2014; O'Donoghue and Krachler 2016). In experimental models, bEVs have been found to access the brain and/or disrupt barrier integrity, providing a potential mechanism for microbiome–brain communication (Wei et al. 2020). Once within or adjacent to the CNS, bEVs act as potent neuroimmune modulators, triggering the activation of microglia, upregulation of proinflammatory cytokines and activation of inflammasome pathways. For instance, Porphyromonas gingivalis EVs have been reported to induce neuroinflammation, activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promote tau phosphorylation and impair memory function in mice (Gong et al. 2022). Similarly, Helicobacter pylori EVs have been reported to reach the brain, colocalize with amyloid‐β plaques, exacerbate amyloid pathology and worsen cognitive and synaptic deficits in APP/PS1 transgenic mice (Meng et al. 2024). Within the oral–brain axis, periodontitis facilitates the migration of Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans EVs to the brain and trigeminal ganglion, where they regulate neuroinflammatory responses (Ha et al. 2023). These data suggest that bEVs can deliver microbial signals to the neuroimmune interface, thereby contributing to brain pathophysiology (Lee 2020). In the intranasal setting, candidate nose‐to‐brain pathways are not limited to hematogenous BBB crossing. Instead, they may include direct neuronal transport and immune cell‐associated trafficking through the olfactory mucosa and cribriform/perineural regions (Asano et al. 2022; Dando et al. 2014; Laaker et al. 2025). Nevertheless, the precise routes, kinetics and cellular mechanisms by which bEVs are delivered to and distributed within the brain remain poorly understood.

In this study, we determined whether bEVs, together with their RNA cargo, can cross the olfactory epithelial barrier and reach the brain following intranasal administration. We further determined the cellular and molecular pathways involved in this process and explored their implications for interkingdom communication and potential CNS‐targeted biodelivery strategies.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Culture

An Escherichia coli K‐12‐derived laboratory cloning strain (DH5α; RBC Bioscience, New Taipei City, Taiwan) was used for EV production. To standardize the starting inoculum, approximately half of a well‐isolated colony from an agar plate incubated for 16 h was picked using a sterile 20‐µL pipette tip and inoculated into Luria–Bertani (LB) broth (BD Difco, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) for overnight culture. The overnight culture was then diluted 1:100 (1%, v/v) into fresh LB broth and incubated at 37°C with shaking at 200 rpm. Under these conditions, the culture reached an OD600 of 0.5 after approximately 5.5 h.

Isolation and Characterization of EVs

Isolation of EVs

E. coli‐derived EVs (Ec EVs) were isolated from the concentrated supernatant and characterized as described previously (Kim et al. 2022), according to the guidelines of the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2023; Welsh et al. 2024). In brief, the bacterial culture supernatant was clarified by centrifugation at 6,000 ×g for 15 min at 4°C. To ensure complete removal of residual bacterial cells, the collected supernatant was passed through a 0.22‐µm filter (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The filtrate was then concentrated using an ultrafiltration system equipped with a MasterFlex L/S pump (Cole‐Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) and a 100‐kDa PelliconXL cassette (Sigma‐Aldrich). Next, EVs were isolated from the concentrated medium using the ExoBacteria OMV Isolation Kit (SBI, Mountain View, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol. For fluorescent labelling, purified EVs were resuspended in 100 µL of phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS, calcium‐ and magnesium‐free; GE Healthcare BioSciences, Pittsburgh, PA, USA) and incubated with 5 µM Nile Red (N3013; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 1 h at 37°C, as described previously (Hornum et al. 2021). Unbound dye was removed by washing the samples with 1 mL PBS, followed by ultracentrifugation at 150,000 ×g for 1 h at 4°C. Control samples processed identically without EVs did not yield visible pellets, confirming the absence of dye aggregates.

Nanoparticle Tracking Analysis (NTA)

EV preparations were characterized by NTA using a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical, Malvern, UK) operated with NTA software (version 3.4, Build 3.4.4). Samples were diluted 1:100 in PBS to a final volume of 1 mL and analysed at 25°C, with the viscosity set to 0.89 cP. The instrument was equipped with an sCMOS camera and a green laser. Capture settings were as follows: camera level 14, gain 245, frame rate 25.0 frames/s and 1498 frames per recording. The analysis was conducted with the following settings: detection threshold 5, blur size auto and maximum jump distance 15–19 pixels. Particle size distributions and particle concentrations were calculated based on Brownian motion using the manufacturer's software. For each sample, three recordings were acquired under identical settings and averaged for analysis.

Electron Microscopy

For transmission electron microscopy (TEM), purified EVs were diluted 1:20 in calcium‐ and magnesium‐free PBS. A 10‐µL drop of the diluted sample was then placed on Parafilm, and a 200‐mesh Formvar/carbon‐coated grid (Ted Pella, Redding, CA, USA) was floated on the drop for 1 min. The grid was then blotted and transferred to a drop of UranyLess (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA, USA), prewarmed at 37°C for 5 min and stained for 1 min. Subsequently, the grid was air‐dried at room temperature for approximately 5 min and imaged using an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) operated at 100 kV.

For scanning electron microscopy (SEM), E. coli colonies grown on agar plates were collected by gently scraping the bacterial growth and resuspending it in PBS. The suspension was pelleted by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 2 min and fixed overnight at 4°C in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (GA) in PBS. After washing with PBS, the fixed cells were spotted onto silicon wafers and allowed to dry completely. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (30%, 50%, 70%, 80%, 90% and 100% [v/v] in distilled water), incubating them twice for 20 min at each concentration. The dehydrated samples were dried overnight and sputter‐coated with PtAu using the MC1000 Ion Sputter (Hitachi). SEM images were acquired using a SU8600 field‐emission scanning electron microscope (Hitachi).

Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) and Western Blotting

For protein profiling by silver staining, whole‐vesicle lysates and bacterial pellet‐derived whole‐cell lysates were prepared in 5× SDS–PAGE loading buffer, heated at 95°C for 15 min and separated by SDS–PAGE; 20 µg of total protein was loaded per lane. Silver staining was performed as described previously (Chevallet et al. 2006).

For GroEL western blotting, whole‐cell and whole‐vesicle lysates were prepared in 5× SDS–PAGE loading buffer, heated at 95°C for 15 min and subjected to SDS–PAGE; 10 and 20 µg of total proteins were loaded per lane for whole‐cell lysates and whole‐vesicle lysates, respectively. The proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane. The membrane was blocked for 30 min at room temperature in 5% (w/v) skim milk prepared in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20 (TBS‐T, pH 7.4) and then incubated with rabbit anti‐GroEL antibody (G6532; Sigma‐Aldrich) at a final dilution of 1:2000 for 1 h. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (#31460; 1:1000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Electroporation of RNA Into EVs

Purified Ec EVs suspended in PBS were used for electroporation. The concentration of the EV stock before electroporation was 1.4 × 1010 particles/mL, corresponding to 1.131 µg/µL total EV protein. For each master mix, 300 µL of purified Ec EV suspension (approximately 4.2 × 109 particles) was mixed with 1 µg of in vitro‐transcribed NLS–Cre mRNA (L‐7211; TriLink Biotechnologies, San Diego, CA, USA). To improve electrical field consistency and minimize arcing, 300 µL of each master mix was divided into two equal 150‐µL aliquots and loaded into sterile 0.2‐cm‐gap electroporation cuvettes. Electroporation was performed using the Gene Pulser Xcell System equipped with a CE module and ShockPod cuvette chamber (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) in exponential decay mode at 150 V and 100 µF. As this CE‐module configuration does not include a selectable parallel resistance, the instrument resistance was set to infinity (∞). Under these conditions, the observed time constant was 0.4 ms. In total, 10 pulses (150 µL) generated from five independent 300‐µL master mixes were pooled to yield 1.5 mL of electroporated EV suspension. The pooled suspension (1.5 mL) was immediately transferred onto ice, supplemented with MgCl2 to a final concentration of approximately 2 mM and incubated with 1 µL benzonase nuclease (≥250 U/µL; MilliporeSigma, Burlington, MA, USA) at 37°C for 25 min to remove extravesicular, nonincorporated mRNA, as described previously (Rodriguez and Kuehn 2020). In parallel, control Ec EVs without added Cre mRNA were subjected to the same benzonase treatment conditions and Cre‐electroporated EVs without benzonase treatment were included as an additional control. EV samples were filtered before use.

Olink Proteomics

Olink experiments were conducted by Macrogen (Seoul, Korea). Olfactory bulb (OB) tissues were homogenized using T‐PER Tissue Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with Halt Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer's protocol and Olink sample preparation guidelines (Olink Proteomics, Watertown, MA, USA). Approximately 100 µL of the reagent was added per 10 mg of tissue, and homogenization was performed using 3 mm tungsten carbide beads in a TissueLyser II device (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) at 25 Hz for 3 min. The lysates were clarified by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were then collected for analysis. Total protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and normalized to approximately 0.5 mg/mL across all samples using the same buffer. Protein expression profiling was performed using the Olink Target 96 panel (Olink Proteomics), according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, 92 antibody pairs and four internal controls with DNA tags were incubated overnight at 4°C with 1 µL of each tissue lysate, followed by proximity extension and preamplification reactions using DNA polymerase. DNA reporters corresponding to each protein were quantified by real‐time quantitative PCR (qPCR) using the Olink Signature Q100 System (Olink Proteomics). The thermocycling conditions were as follows: 50°C for 20 min; 95°C for 5 min; 17 cycles at 95°C for 30 s, 54°C for 1 min and 60°C for 1 min; and a final hold at 10°C. Raw Ct values were normalized using inter‐plate control (IPC) normalization to generate Normalized Protein eXpression (NPX) values via Olink NPX Signature software (Olink Proteomics). Quality control was performed by assessing deviations from internal control medians and protein assays were retained irrespective of their relation to the limit of detection (LOD). Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were identified using unpaired t‐tests with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. False discovery rate (FDR) was adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Data processing, statistical analyses and data visualization were performed in R (version 4.2.2) using the OlinkAnalyze package (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/OlinkAnalyze/vignettes/Vignett.html).

Animals

In this study, 7‐week‐old mT/mG transgenic mice [B6.129(Cg)‐Gt(ROSA)26Sortm4(ACTB‐TdTomato,‐EGFP)Luo/J] were obtained from the Jackson laboratory (#007676; Bar Harbor, ME, USA). The mice were housed in a specific pathogen‐free facility with regulated temperature/humidity and a 12‐h light/dark cycle. They had free access to normal rodent chow and tap water. Littermate wild‐type (WT) mice were used as controls.

In subsequent experiments, heterozygous mT/mG transgenic mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and intranasally administered 10 µL of PBS, Ec EVs, or EVs loaded with Cre recombinase mRNA (EVCre); 5 µL of each sample (PBS, Ec EVs or EVCre) was delivered into one nostril and the remaining 5 µL was delivered into the other nostril after 5 min. In the Ec EV and EVCre groups, each dose was normalized to 1.4 × 108 particles per mouse (i.e., 11.31 µg of total EV protein), equivalent to 7.0 × 107 particles per nostril. The 5‐min interval between administrations was used to minimize respiratory stress. For the repeated‐dose protocol, the mice were treated on days 1 and 3, and samples were collected 48 h after the second administration. The mice were either perfused with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) 48 h after administration for histological analysis or rapidly frozen for downstream experiments. The study was conducted in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines (Kilkenny et al. 2010). All animal experimental protocols were approved by the Committee on the Care and Use of Animals in Research at Kyungpook National University, Korea (approval number KNU 2024‐0330).

Culture of Dorsal Root Ganglion (DRG) Neurons

Mice (3–5 days old) were decapitated under ketamine anesthesia (50 mg/kg administered intraperitoneally). Their DRGs were dissected and treated with artificial cerebrospinal fluid (150 mM NaCl, 3 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM glucose and 10 mM HEPES [pH 7.4 with Tris base]) containing 0.3% collagenase (type I, C0130; Sigma‐Aldrich) and 0.3% trypsin (type I, T4799; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 15–20 min at 37°C. Thereafter, single DRG neurons were mechanically dissociated by trituration with fire‐polished Pasteur pipettes in an Eppendorf tube containing Dulbecco's modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 11995‐065; Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (16000‐044; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 0.1% penicillin–streptomycin (15140‐122; Thermo Fisher Scientific). They were then centrifuged at ∼400 ×g for 5 min and resuspended for plating. Culture substrates (35 mm dish, chamber slide, or 24‐well microplate) were coated with poly‐D‐lysine (PDL, 1 µg/cm2, P6407; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 2 h at 37°C, rinsed with sterile PBS and air‐dried in a biosafety cabinet. Isolated DRG neurons were plated as a central droplet and allowed to attach for 30–45 min at 37°C under 5% CO2. They were then flooded with prewarmed medium. Cultures were maintained in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 0.1% P/S. After 24 h, cytosine β‐D‐arabinofuranoside (AraC, C6645; Sigma‐Aldrich) was added at a low concentration (1–2 µM) for 24–48 h to suppress glial proliferation. The medium was changed every 2 to 3 days by slowly removing 50% of the volume and refilling with prewarmed fresh medium to avoid shear stress and cell detachment.

For inhibitor‐based experiments followed by RT–qPCR analysis, primary DRG neurons were additionally seeded in 6‐well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well and maintained in the same culture medium described above. Cultures were pretreated for 30 min at 37°C with Dynasore hydrate at 50 µM (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) or methyl‐β‐cyclodextrin (MβCD) at 80 µM (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The medium was then replaced with fresh culture medium, and Benzonase‐treated and filtered Ec EVs prepared as described in Section 2.3 were added at 50 µL per well, corresponding to approximately 7.0 × 108 particles and 56.55 µg total EV protein. Cells were harvested 24 h after EV treatment for total RNA extraction. Parallel chamber‐slide cultures prepared under the same treatment conditions were used for fluorescence imaging.

Immunohistochemistry

To visualize the olfactory epithelium and OB, entire mouse heads were processed without decalcification using a gelatin‐embedding cryosection protocol for craniofacial tissues (Yang et al. 2019). The heads were fixed in 4% PFA (P6148; Sigma‐Aldrich) in 1× PBS for 4 h at 4°C, cryoprotected overnight in 30% sucrose/PBS, embedded in 8% gelatin (G1890; Sigma‐Aldrich) in PBS and sagittally cryosections at −25°C into 10‐µm‐thick slices using a Leica CM3050 cryostat (Leica Biosystems, Nussloch, Germany). Sections were mounted on MAS‐GP Type‐A micro slides (Matsunami Glass Industries, Japan), air‐dried and stored at −80°C until staining. For immunostaining, slides and fixed DRG neuron cultures on glass microslide chambers were permeabilized using 0.2% Triton X‐100 (T9284; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 10 min and blocked for 1 h at room temperature in either 5% normal goat serum + 0.2% Triton X‐100 or 5% normal donkey serum + 0.2% Triton X‐100 in PBS, depending on the host species of the secondary antibody. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4°C in blocking buffer and washed three times with PBS (10 min each), followed by incubation with a fluorescent secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. After three final washes with PBS, the slides were mounted in VECTASHIELD Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (H‐1200; Vector Labs, Burlingame, CA, USA). The following antibodies and dyes were used: Cre recombinase rabbit mAb (#15036; 1:200, Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA, USA) with goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 350 (A‐11010; 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Iba1 monoclonal antibody (MA5‐27726; 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Goat anti‐mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (A28175; 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), anti‐mouse Ly‐6G/Ly‐6C monoclonal antibody, clone RB6‐8C5 (14‐5931‐82; 1:200, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with Goat anti‐rat IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (A‐11006; 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), anti‐mouse NeuN monoclonal antibody (MAB377; 1:100, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) with Goat anti‐mouse IgG (H + L) Alexa Fluor 488 (A28175; 1:500, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Rab7 (Alexa Fluor 488 conjugate, #47512; 1:100, Cell Signaling Technology) and Nile Red (5 µM; 1 h at 37°C, followed by centrifugation at 150,000 ×g for 1 h at 4°C to remove unbound dye). All tissue and cell samples were processed under identical conditions for fixation, staining and mounting to ensure reproducibility and comparability across experimental groups.

Microfluidic Biochip Device

The microfluidic biochip device was designed to contain three functional compartments: a soma compartment for neuron seeding, an EV compartment for targeted EV application and an intervening microgroove region for selective axonal growth. The soma and EV compartments were each 10 mm in length, 1 mm in width and 100 µm in height. The microgroove region contained 143 parallel microchannels, each 0.5 mm long, 10 µm wide and 4 µm high, with 60 µm spacing between adjacent grooves. Master molds were fabricated using multistep photolithography and deep reactive ion etching (DRIE). Microgroove patterns were initially created by spin‐coating photoresist (AZ GXR 601; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) onto a silicon wafer, followed by UV exposure through a chrome photomask, development and hard baking. DRIE was then performed using the Bosch process to etch grooves to a depth of 4 µm. Residual photoresist was removed by sequential immersion in stripper solution and oxygen plasma cleaning. For the compartmental features, SU‐8 2100 photoresist (Kayaku AM, Westborough, MA, USA) was spin‐coated to achieve a thickness of 100 µm, exposed through a transparency mask, postbaked and developed. Isopropanol rinsing and drying were then performed, followed by C4F8 plasma treatment to create a hydrophobic fluorocarbon layer on the mold surface, thereby reducing bubble adhesion during polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) casting. Devices were produced via standard soft lithography. PDMS (Sylgard 184; Dow Corning, Midland, MI, USA) was prepared by mixing the base and curing agent at a 10:1 ratio, degassed and poured over the prepared mold. After overnight curing at 80°C, PDMS slabs were peeled off and access ports (3.1 mm diameter) were punched. The PDMS layer and tissue culture‐treated dish were treated with air plasma for 60 s and bonded to form the final device. The design and fabrication of the microfluidic biochip are presented in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). Before neuron seeding was performed, the assembled chips were thoroughly rinsed with PBS.

Imaging Analysis

Live imaging was performed using a Zeiss LSM 800 laser‐scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) with environmental control (37°C, 5% CO2) in time‐lapse mode. DRG neurons cultured in two‐chamber glass slides or in the microfluidic biochip chamber were stained with 100 nM LysoTracker Blue DND‐22 (L7525; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 30 min at 37°C, washed with prewarmed FluoroBrite DMEM and exposed to Nile Red‐labelled EVs to record real‐time uptake, intracellular trafficking and colocalization with LysoTracker‐positive late endocytic compartments. Differential interference contrast (DIC) was additionally acquired for recordings in the microfluidic chamber to visualize channel architecture and DRG neuron morphology during live acquisition. Imaging was performed using fixed laser lines (405/488/561 nm) with constant laser power and detector gain across samples. Z‐stacks were acquired at defined intervals and processed as maximum‐intensity projections for analysis. Supplementary wide‐field fluorescence images were obtained using a BioTek Cytation 10 multimode imager (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The time‐lapse animations are presented in the Supporting Information Movies.

Confocal imaging of fixed brain tissue sections and DRG neuron cultures was performed using a Zeiss LSM 800 laser‐scanning confocal microscope. All samples were prepared as described in Section 2.7 and imaged under identical acquisition parameters. Fluorescent signals from DAPI, Alexa Fluor 350 and 488 and Nile Red channels were sequentially collected using the corresponding laser lines (405, 488 and 561 nm, respectively) to minimize spectral overlap. Images were acquired using a 20× or 40× objective for tissue sections and a 100× oil‐immersion objective for DRG neurons cultured in microplates; identical pinhole size, laser power, detector gain and exposure settings were used across all groups. For tissue imaging, z‐stacks were obtained at defined optical intervals and reconstructed as maximum‐intensity projections. Representative single‐plane and merged composite images were acquired using ZEN Blue 2.3 software (Carl Zeiss). All confocal data were analysed under consistent brightness, contrast and channel alignment conditions without postacquisition enhancement.

Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser‐scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 800; Carl Zeiss); the exposure and detector gain settings were identical across all samples. Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was quantified using ZEN 2.3 Lite software (Carl Zeiss). Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually defined over neuronal soma or axonal segments within the AF488 channel and the arithmetic mean intensity of each ROI was then measured. Background fluorescence was obtained from adjacent cell‐free areas within the same field of view and subtracted from the ROI signal to yield background‐corrected intensity; the following formula was used: MFI(corr) = Mean(ROI)—Mean(BG). Corrected values from at least three independent biological replicates per group were analysed using GraphPad Prism v8.4.3 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Reverse Transcription–qPCR (RT–qPCR)

Cre RT–qPCR

Cre RT–qPCR was performed to confirm the presence of Cre mRNA electroporated into Ec EVs. Total RNA extracted from EVs was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Omniscript RT Kit (Qiagen). PCR amplification was performed using specific primers for Cre (Table S1), and a known quantity of Cre cDNA was used as a positive control.

General qPCR for Genes Other Than the Cre Gene

Transcript‐level changes in primary DRG cultures following the inhibition of Ec EV endocytosis were assessed using RT–qPCR. Total RNA was extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA using the Omniscript RT Kit (Qiagen). The selected genes were identified from DEPs via Olink proteomic analysis. The primer sequences used for amplification are mentioned in the Supporting Information (Table S1).

QPCR for Ec‐1700 Small RNA

The inhibition of Ec EV uptake in primary DRG cultures was assessed by quantifying the expression of the E. coli small RNA Ec‐3p‐1700, which is one of the most abundantly expressed RNAs in Ec EVs and is readily detectable by RT–qPCR (Kang et al. 2013). For normalization, synthetic Caenorhabditis elegans miR‐39‐3p (cel‐miR‐39; Bioneer, Daejeon, Korea) was used as a spike‐in control (Kroh et al. 2010). During RNA extraction, 3 µL of cel‐miR‐39‐3p (330 fmol/µL stock) was added to each sample. Total RNA (25 ng) was reverse transcribed using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). From the 15 µL cDNA reaction mixture, 1.33 µL was used for qPCR, performed using TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix and Ec‐1700‐specific TaqMan probes (Applied Biosystems). Each qPCR reaction mixture was prepared in a 20‐µL volume and the reactions were run in 96‐well plates using the QuantStudio 3 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Six biological replicates were included per sample to ensure reproducibility.

Single‐cell RNA Sequencing (scRNA‐seq)

Olfactory epithelial and OB tissues from mT/mG mice treated with Ec EVs and EVCre (n = 2 each) were dissected and dissociated into single‐cell suspensions using the Multi Tissue Dissociation Kit 1 and a gentleMACS dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Red blood cells and dead cells were removed using Red Blood Cell Lysis Solution and the Dead Cell Removal Kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Cell concentration and viability were assessed using the LUNA‐FL Automated Fluorescence Cell Counter (Logos Biosystems, Seoul, Korea). Library preparation was performed using the 10× Genomics Single Cell Protocols Cell Preparation Guide and Guidelines for Optimal Sample Preparation (CG00053 and CG000126, respectively). Single‐cell 3′ gene expression libraries were constructed using a Chromium X instrument (10× Genomics, Pleasanton, CA, USA) with the GEM‐X Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v4 (CG000731); approximately 10,000 cells were targeted per channel. Following droplet barcoding and RT, pooled cDNA was subjected to end repair, A‐tailing, adapter ligation and PCR enrichment. Libraries were quantified using KAPA qPCR (Merck), and a quality check was performed using an Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent). Subsequently, sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the read configuration recommended in the user guide. Library construction and sequencing were performed by Macrogen.

Raw BCL files were demultiplexed using Cell Ranger's mkfastq module, and gene expression matrices were generated using the count module in Cell Ranger v9.0.1 (10× Genomics). A customized mouse reference (GRCm39‐2024‐A) including GFP and tdTomato sequences was used for alignment. Where applicable, sequencing runs from the same library were combined during the count process, and independent samples were integrated using the aggregation (aggr) module.

Downstream analysis was conducted using Seurat v5.3.1. Genes detected in fewer than three cells and cells with fewer than 200 detected genes were excluded. To remove putative multiplets and low‐quality/dying cells, cells with >8000 detected genes or >15% mitochondrial UMI fraction were filtered out. Thus, 55,445 cells across 28,658 genes were retained. Counts were normalized and scaled, and principal component analysis (PCA) was then conducted. Significant PCs were used for graph‐based clustering and UMAP visualization. Cluster marker genes (each cluster versus all others) were identified using Seurat's Wilcoxon rank‐sum test with min.pct = 0.25 and logfc.threshold = 0.25; only positive markers were retained. Functional enrichment of significant gene lists was performed using g: Profiler. Cell‐type labels were assigned using complementary approaches: SingleR (reference‐based, Spearman correlation), ScType (marker‐based, using CellMarker and PanglaoDB) and GPTCelltype (auxiliary; three runs at temperatures 0, 0.5 and 1.0 to assess consistency versus variability); final annotations reflect consensus across methods.

Bioinformatic Analysis of scRNA‐seq Data

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted in R (version 4.5.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) using RStudio (version 2025.09.2, Posit, Boston, MA, USA). Single‐cell gene–barcode matrices from the 10× Genomics Chromium platform were processed using Seurat (version 5.3.1) for quality control, normalization, dimensionality reduction, integration and clustering, according to standard workflows. Low‐quality cells were excluded based on library complexity, number of detected genes and mitochondrial transcript fraction. Normalized data were used to construct a shared nearest‐neighbour graph. Transcriptionally distinct populations were then identified using PCA‐, UMAP‐ and graph‐based clustering. Differential expression between groups or between cell types was assessed using Seurat's nonparametric tests, and p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Cell‐type annotation was performed using a combination of automated reference‐based labelling and manual curation. SingleR (version 2.8.0) was used to assign initial labels by comparison with published reference transcriptomes, and ScType was used to refine these assignments based on curated positive and negative marker sets. Predicted labels were inspected in Seurat feature plots and in the 10× Genomics Loupe Browser and corrected using canonical markers for neuronal, glial, immune, vascular and stromal cell types. Final curated labels were stored in the metadata (GPTCelltype_t0) and used for all downstream cell type‐resolved analyses. Gene‐level summaries (mean log‐normalized expression, proportion of expressing cells and composition of gene‐positive cells by cell type) and plots were generated in R using ggplot2 (version 4.0.1) and other tidyverse packages (dplyr version 1.1.4 and tidyr version 1.3.1).

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) or mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), where applicable. Statistical analyses and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.4.3 (GraphPad Software), unless otherwise stated. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐tests. For experiments involving a single independent variable with more than two groups, one‐way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison post hoc test. For experiments involving two independent variables, two‐way ANOVA was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. For Olink Target 96 proteomics, NPX values were analysed using two‐tailed unpaired Student's t‐tests. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure, where applicable. For animal experiments, n denotes the number of mice per group (biological replicates). For histology‐based quantification, three serial sections were prepared per mouse; however, fluorescence intensity was quantified using one representative anatomically matched section per mouse (selected to ensure intact morphology and minimal sectioning/staining artifacts). Statistical comparisons were performed using one value per mouse. For RT–qPCR, technical replicates were averaged to obtain a single value per biological sample before statistical testing. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05; exact n values and statistical tests are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

Intranasal Administration of bEVs Enables Translocation to the OB

To determine whether bEVs can enter the brain through the olfactory route, we first assessed whether fluorescently labelled Ec EVs can access CNS regions following non‐invasive intranasal delivery. The nose–brain pathway bypasses the BBB and may represent a physiologically relevant route of microbial communication with the brain, particularly through the olfactory neuroepithelium. We intranasally administered purified Nile Red‐labelled Ec EVs at the same normalized dose used throughout the animal studies and collected OB tissues 48 h after administration (n = 5 mice per group for imaging and proteomic analyses). Immunofluorescence staining revealed robust red fluorescent signals corresponding to EVs within the OB parenchyma of the treated mice; however, these signals were absent in the control animals (Figure 1b). These findings indicate that nasally delivered EVs can traverse the olfactory mucosa and reach brain tissue, confirming successful translocation via the olfactory pathway. To determine whether this entry induces molecular changes in the brain, we performed 96‐panel targeted proteomic profiling of OB tissues using the Olink Mouse Exploratory Panel. Comparative analysis revealed distinct proteomic alterations in the EV‐treated group. Several proteins related to immune regulation and neuronal signalling, including S100a4, Ccl2 and Ccl20, were significantly upregulated, while others were downregulated (Figure 1c,d). Hierarchical clustering and heatmap analysis revealed clear separation between the treatment groups, confirming reproducible expression patterns (Figure 1e). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified multiple significantly enriched pathways, including those related to cytokine response, myeloid cell activation, extracellular matrix organization and vesicle‐mediated transport, in the EV‐treated group (Figure 1f). These results suggest that EVs not only reach the OB but also modulate protein networks involved in neuroimmune communication. Together, these results provide initial evidence that bEVs can access the brain through intranasal administration and induce measurable changes in the OB microenvironment, laying the foundation for further mechanistic studies using this model.

Cre mRNA Can be Loaded Into bEVs via Electroporation

To generate RNA‐loaded bEVs, we electroporated Ec EVs with in vitro‐transcribed Cre recombinase mRNA (EVCre). To reduce extracellular, nonincorporated RNA, we treated the electroporated preparations with benzonase before downstream analyses (Figure 2b). NTA confirmed that untreated Ec EVs and EVCre exhibited comparable size distributions (50–200 nm) and similar particle concentrations (Figure 2c), indicating that electroporation and nuclease treatment did not compromise vesicle integrity. TEM images further revealed intact spherical vesicles without aggregation or structural deformation (Figure 2d), consistent with the vesicle morphology observed in the original SEM image of E. coli (Figure 2a). Silver‐stained SDS–PAGE also revealed similar protein banding patterns between the EV‐treated groups, indicating no detectable protein loss or degradation during loading (Figure 2e). We performed western blotting to further characterize the vesicle preparations using GroEL, a bacterial chaperonin that has been reported in some bEV preparations (Yu et al. 2024) but is not a universal bEV marker (De Langhe et al. 2024). GroEL was present in Ec EVs and EVCre, as well as in the corresponding bacterial cell pellet, supporting the bacterial origin of the vesicle‐associated material (Figure 2f). To determine the efficiency of RNA incorporation, we performed RT–qPCR using benzonase‐treated EV preparations and exclusively detected a strong Cre mRNA signal in EVCre; however, control EVs showed no amplification (Figure 2g). As we performed the RT–qPCR assay using benzonase‐treated EVCre preparations, the estimated RNA amount corresponded to approximately 4.21 × 10− 8 pg of Cre mRNA per EV particle after correcting for dilution (Figure S2). This value represents a particle‐normalized bulk estimate of EV‐associated Cre mRNA and does not define the proportion of individual Cre‐positive EVs. A dedicated benzonase protection assay further showed that benzonase removed a substantial extracellular, unincorporated Cre mRNA fraction while leaving a residual benzonase‐resistant EV‐associated RNA signal detectable (Figure S3). Together, these findings validate the generation of structurally intact, RNA‐loaded bEVs suitable for delivery studies. A broader comparison of the kit‐based workflow with ultracentrifugation‐ and density gradient‐based purification, including DNA, RNA, protein, endotoxin, GroEL and flagellin profiles, is provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S4).

Intranasally Delivered bEVs Mediate Functional mRNA Expression in the OB

To determine whether Ec EVs can access the CNS and deliver functional RNA cargo, we intranasally administered EVCre into mT/mG reporter mice according to the dosing scheme presented in Figure 3a. In this transgenic system, cells constitutively express membrane‐targeted tdTomato (mT, red; see Movie S5a); however, Cre‐mediated recombination irreversibly switches the expression to membrane‐targeted GFP (mG, green; see Movie S5b), providing a sensitive in vivo readout of Cre activity (Muzumdar et al. 2007). In our study, mice were intranasally administered Ec EVCre on days 1 and 3, and their tissues were harvested after 48 h for immunofluorescence analysis. In the control animals treated with control Ec EVs lacking Cre mRNA, no Cre signal or GFP expression was detected (Figure 3b). Moreover, mT fluorescence remained intact. In contrast, the mice treated with Ec EVCre exhibited detectable Cre immunoreactivity and mG expression in the olfactory epithelium and OB, indicating successful recombination (Figure 3c,d). Notably, a subset of neurons within the OB showed gain of mG fluorescence, indicating that bEVs can reach and functionally reprogram central neurons following intranasal delivery. These results provide proof‐of‐concept evidence that intranasally administered bEVs can deliver RNA cargo to the brain and induce Cre‐mediated genetic recombination in target neurons. A time‐lapse motion‐capture movie showing mG expression in primary DRG neurons isolated from mT/mG reporter mice after Ec EVCre treatment is provided in Supporting Information Movie 5 (Movie S5a–c). To further contextualize the delivery efficiency, we also performed an in vitro benchmarking comparison between Ec EVCre‐mediated and lipid‐mediated Cre mRNA delivery (Figure S5).

Single‐cell Transcriptomic Profiling of Olfactory Tissues Reveals Cellular Targets of bEV‐Delivered mRNA Cargo

To identify cellular populations that may receive bEV‐delivered RNA cargo in vivo, we performed scRNA‐seq on dissociated cells from the olfactory epithelium and OB 48 h after the intranasal administration of control Ec EVs or EVCre to mT/mG reporter mice (Figure 4a). Integrated analysis resolved multiple expected cell populations, including olfactory sensory neurons, mitral cells, interneurons, endothelial cells, macrophages, neutrophils and other supporting cell types (Figure 4b,c). Across both treatment groups, mT transcripts were broadly detected. In contrast, an mG (GFP) transcript signal was specifically detected in tissues from EVCre‐treated mice, consistent with Cre‐dependent reporter activation (Figure 4d,e). Violin plots further revealed increased mG transcript abundance in EVCre‐treated samples; however, mG transcripts were not detected in controls (Figure 4f). mG transcripts were detected in both neuronal and non‐neuronal clusters, including immune and vascular‐associated populations (Figure 4g). When mG‐positive (mG+) cells were summarized, immune cell clusters (including neutrophils and macrophages) and several non‐neuronal stromal populations accounted for a substantial fraction of mG+ events, with neuronal clusters (including mitral cells and olfactory sensory neurons) also containing mG+ cells. Although the fraction of mG+ olfactory sensory neurons was low, the presence of the mG signal within this projection neuron population is consistent with the delivery of EVCre cargo to neurons. Collectively, these findings indicate that intranasally administered bEVs can deliver functional mRNA to diverse cell types in the olfactory system, including neurons, immune cells and vascular components, supporting both neuronal and phagocyte‐mediated routes of EV uptake and gene delivery in vivo.

Phagocyte‐Mediated Uptake and Brain Infiltration of bEVs

To determine the role of immune cells in mediating the transport of bEVs into the brain, we performed immunofluorescence analysis of the olfactory epithelium and OB from WT mice intranasally administered Ec EVs. Confocal imaging revealed EV signals colocalized with Iba1‐positive (Iba1+) microglia/macrophages and Ly6G‐positive (Ly6G+) neutrophils in both olfactory epithelial and OB regions (Figure 5a–f), indicating that phagocytic cells can internalize EVs at the mucosal surface and traffic them into deeper brain structures. Quantitative analysis revealed significantly increased fluorescence intensity of Iba1 and Ly6G in EV‐treated mice, accompanied by substantial EV signals within these cell populations. This suggests that both resident microglia and infiltrating neutrophils facilitate EV uptake and serve as cellular vehicles for vesicle transport into the CNS. In parallel, EV signals were observed in NeuN‐positive (NeuN+) neurons in the olfactory epithelium and OB (Figure 5g,h), supporting direct uptake by neuronal populations. Together, these findings support a dual‐route model for EV entry into the brain: (1) direct uptake by neurons at the olfactory interface and (2) immune cell‐mediated trafficking of EVs across the mucosa and into the OB.

BEVs Undergo Retrograde Axonal Transport via Signalling Endosome Pathways

To directly visualize the intracellular trafficking of bEVs within neurons, we utilized a compartmentalized microfluidic biochip chamber system that recapitulates the polarized structure of neurons. In brief, we cultured primary DRG neurons with somata restricted to one chamber and neurites extending through microchannels into a separate distal compartment. We exclusively applied fluorescently labelled bEVs to the neurite compartment to evaluate retrograde transport toward the soma. As a control, bEVs injected into devices lacking neurite projections remained confined to the injection side, confirming that passive diffusion across microchannels does not occur in the absence of neuronal connectivity (Figure 6b). In contrast, DRG cultures with established neurite projections exhibited progressive accumulation of EV signals along the microchannels, directed toward the soma compartment (Figure 6c). Time‐lapse live imaging revealed dynamic retrograde movement of EVs from neurites to the soma, beginning within minutes of application and continuing for up to 30 min (Figure 6f). To determine the intracellular route of EV transport, we costained DRG neurons with LysoTracker for visualizing late endosomes. LysoTracker is a fluorescent dye that selectively marks acidic organelles, such as endosomes and lysosomes, in living cells without disrupting their function (Esner et al. 2014; Pal et al. 2018). We used this dye to visualize endosomal compartments and assess the dynamic transport of vesicular cargo along neurites. Confocal imaging revealed time‐dependent colocalization of EVs with LysoTracker‐positive vesicles in the soma, indicating active engagement of the endosomal trafficking system (Figure 6d). In time‐lapse images, Nile Red‐positive EV puncta moved retrogradely together with LysoTracker‐positive acidic compartments along neurites. We tracked three EV‐positive acidic compartments from independent time‐lapse clips frame‐by‐frame and calculated net velocity as net retrograde displacement divided by elapsed time. The individual net velocities were 0.55, 0.30 and 0.29 µm/s, yielding a mean velocity of approximately 0.4 µm/s (Figure 6e). These results indicate that bEVs can be internalized at distal axons and retrogradely trafficked to the soma via signalling endosomes, which play key roles in regulating neuronal survival, axonal growth and guidance, dendritic branching and cell migration (Cosker and Segal 2014).

Endocytosis Inhibitors Block Neuronal Uptake of bEVs

To determine the endocytic requirements for bEV uptake by sensory neurons, we treated primary DRG cultures with dynasore (50 µM), a dynamin‐dependent endocytosis inhibitor, or MβCD (80 µM), which disrupts lipid rafts (O'Donoghue and Krachler 2016; Zhang et al. 2024). Time‐lapse imaging of neurons exposed to fluorescently labelled bEVs revealed progressive accumulation of EV signals in the soma and neurites over 30 min, indicating active internalization and intracellular trafficking (Figure 7a). To determine the intracellular fate of internalized EVs, we assessed their colocalization with Rab7, a marker of late endosomes. Confocal imaging revealed robust colocalization of EVs with Rab7‐positive (Rab7+) endosomes in the cytoplasm, suggesting that bEVs engage the endosomal pathway following internalization (Figure 7b). Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher EV‐associated fluorescence in Rab7+ endosomes than in PBS‐treated controls (p < 0.01), supporting active routing through the late endosomal system. Next, we assessed the effects of endocytosis inhibition on bEV uptake. Confocal imaging revealed that both dynasore and MβCD significantly reduced intracellular EV signals in DRG neurons (Figure 7c). Quantification of EV fluorescence intensity confirmed strong suppression of EV internalization in both treatment groups (p < 0.001), supporting the involvement of dynamin‐dependent and membrane cholesterol‐sensitive uptake pathways.

To explore the downstream effects of EV uptake, we analysed mRNA expression profiles of DRG neurons treated with bEVs in the presence or absence of endocytosis inhibitors. As expected from our prior observations, the proinflammatory cytokine TNF‐α was strongly induced in ganglion neurons by bEV treatment (Ha et al. 2023); this response was significantly reduced by dynasore or MβCD (Figure 7d). In contrast, the neuronal genes Ntf3, Yes1, Cntn1 and Clstn2, which were identified in our Olink dataset (Figure 1) as EV‐responsive genes that were enriched in sensory neurons (Figure S6), exhibited modest suppression following bEV exposure; this decrease was partially restored when endocytosis was blocked (Figure 7e–h). The chemokine Ccl20, one of the most robustly upregulated proteins in our proteomic analysis (Figure 1), was also markedly elevated at the transcript level following EV treatment and was effectively rescued by either inhibitor (Figure 7i). Finally, we measured the abundance of Ec‐1700, a representative small bacterial RNA associated with Ec EVs (Kang et al. 2013). Ec‐1700 levels were markedly elevated in DRG neurons following EV exposure; however, they were significantly diminished in the presence of endocytosis inhibitors (Figure 7j), confirming that EV‐mediated RNA cargo delivery is endocytosis‐dependent. Together, these results indicate that sensory neuron uptake of bEVs is sensitive to the disruption of dynamin‐dependent and membrane cholesterol‐sensitive processes and that this uptake is required for the associated transcriptional responses and delivery of bacterial RNA cargo.

Discussion

In this study, we assessed whether bEVs, along with their active molecular cargo, can traverse the olfactory epithelial barrier and reach the brain. Furthermore, we elucidated the cellular mechanisms underlying bEV translocation and explored the potential biotechnological applications of this delivery route. The nasal cavity provides a unique portal to the CNS, where the olfactory epithelium establishes direct neuronal connections between the external environment and brain tissues (Illum 2004). In healthy individuals, the passage of bacterial or viral particles across the cribriform plate is usually very limited. This barrier is maintained by the olfactory epithelium, tight junctions, immune surveillance and the cribriform bone itself (Dando et al. 2014). However, under certain conditions, pathogens can circumvent these defences by hijacking nerves, exploiting epithelial injury, or being actively transported into the brain by host immune cells (Chacko et al. 2022; Dando et al. 2014; Laaker et al. 2025; St John et al. 2014). In contrast to these well‐characterized mechanisms, the extent to which EVs derived from nasal‐colonizing bacteria can enter the brain and their effects remain poorly understood.

We systematically traced the path of nasally administered bEVs and revealed their ability to cross the olfactory epithelium and accumulate in the OB, while carrying molecular cargo, such as mRNA.

Our results provide direct evidence that nasally delivered bEVs reach the OB and induce proteomic changes, particularly in cytokine signalling, extracellular matrix remodelling and vesicle trafficking pathways. Notably, the upregulation of various proteins, such as S100a4, Ccl2 and Ccl20 (Figure 1), reflects a biological response rather than passive diffusion. This study provides the first direct in vivo characterization of bEV trafficking from the nasal cavity to the brain and highlights how microbial products can modulate CNS homeostasis. Remarkably, sensory neurons express various chemokines, such as Ccl2 and Ccl20 (Ciechanowska and Mika 2024; Dawes et al. 2014; Zheng et al. 2023). Notably, Ccl2 overexpression in DRGs promoted sensory axon regeneration in a rat spinal cord injury model (Kwon et al. 2015), raising the possibility that neuron‐derived chemokines may contribute to the local response to bEV stimulation, potentially including immune cell recruitment.

A key finding of this study is that bEVs can deliver functional RNA cargo to the brain area. In our previous study, electroporation enabled the functional expression of nucleic acid‐loaded bEVs in host cells (Kim et al. 2022). In this study, we used the same approach to load Cre mRNA into E. coli EVs (EVCre). Intranasal delivery of EVCre to mT/mG reporter mice induced tdTomato‐to‐GFP recombination in the olfactory epithelium and OB (Figures 3 and 4), indicating successful RNA delivery and translation in neural tissues. These findings not only extend prior findings regarding RNA content in bEVs (Kim et al. 2022; Oh et al. 2025) but also establish the ability of bEVs to mediate functional gene transfer in the brain. In this context, we used GroEL as a supportive bEV‐associated protein, whereas flagellin served as a comparative indicator of potential flagellar carryover; neither was interpreted to be a universal bEV marker (De Langhe et al. 2024). Thus, bEVs can act as both immunomodulatory stimuli and vehicles of interkingdom molecular communication (Kaisanlahti et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2021). If RNA‐loading strategies can be expanded further, bEVs may serve as a platform for RNA vaccines against various pathogens, including viruses. This possibility is supported by prior efforts to use bEVs for human vaccine development (Chen et al. 2010; Peng et al. 2020). However, systemic biodistribution and pharmacokinetic analyses were not conducted in our study. Therefore, although our data clearly demonstrate that direct neuronal and immune cell‐associated nose–brain routes are functional, we cannot exclude an additional indirect contribution via systemic circulation. The quantitative contribution of these direct and potential indirect pathways to overall brain delivery remains an important question for future investigation.

At the nose–brain interface, scRNA‐seq revealed that not only neuronal populations but also phagocytic immune cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils, actively engulf bEVs within the nasal mucosa and later infiltrate the CNS (Figure 4). These findings reveal multiple routes of bEV entry into the brain: direct neuronal transport and immune cell‐mediated trafficking. The phagocyte‐associated route, particularly involving neutrophils at the olfactory epithelium–OB interface, appears to be a relatively underexplored aspect of intranasal bEV trafficking and may preferentially support initial access to the OB. Although the prominence of phagocytic populations in Figure 4g raises the possibility that immune cell‐mediated trafficking can substantially contribute under the present conditions, our study was not designed to determine which pathway is quantitatively dominant. We therefore interpret the present data as establishing the existence of both neuronal and phagocyte‐associated routes rather than defining their relative contribution to overall brain delivery. Future studies using route‐selective interventions, such as the depletion of specific immune cell populations, will be required to address this question more rigorously.

The ability of phagocytes to act as Trojan horses, carrying vesicles across the BBB or through perivascular routes, broadens our understanding of neuroimmune communication and raises questions about its role in inflammation and disease progression. Immunofluorescence analyses also confirmed that phagocytic cells, such as Iba1+ microglia/macrophages and Ly6G+ neutrophils, internalize bEVs within the olfactory epithelium and OB. These cells may facilitate bEV entry by migrating through the cribriform plate, thereby extending bEV delivery beyond neuronal pathways. Moreover, the infiltration of bEV‐carrying phagocytes may be driven by neuronal chemokine upregulation in response to bEV stimulation, as described earlier. This phenomenon is consistent with prior findings that peripheral immune cells can traverse neural barriers during olfactory mucosal inflammation (Asano et al. 2022; Hasegawa‐Ishii et al. 2017). However, overt short‐term neuronal loss was not apparent under the dosing conditions used in this study, as NeuN intensity remained unchanged in the olfactory epithelium and OB (Figure 5g,h). This is in contrast to prior findings that intranasal lipopolysaccharide administration can induce loss of olfactory neurons (Hasegawa‐Ishii et al. 2017), suggesting that short‐term bEV exposure does not induce comparable neurotoxic effects in this context. Serum cytokine profiling did not reveal a broad systemic inflammatory response after intranasal Ec EV administration. While CCL12, IL‐10 and IL‐4 exhibited nominal differences, the overall circulating cytokine pattern remained largely unchanged, supporting the absence of overt systemic inflammatory activation under our experimental conditions (Figure S7). The relatively limited toxicity observed in this study may reflect the short‐term exposure paradigm and the use of a nonpathogenic K‐12‐derived strain. However, future studies should assess long‐term toxicity, apoptotic markers and detailed histopathology, as well as test lower‐toxicity EV sources, including EVs derived from attenuated or probiotic bacterial strains.

Our microfluidic biochip experiments further revealed that bEVs undergo rapid retrograde transport along neurites toward the soma within minutes of distal application (Figure 6). Retrograde transport is essential for maintaining neuronal homeostasis by removing aged proteins and organelles from distal axons for degradation and recycling. It also plays a key role in relaying neurotrophic and injury‐related signals to the soma (Maday et al. 2014). These functions suggest that bEVs exploit similar mechanisms for their trafficking and signalling within neurons. Colocalization with LysoTracker‐labelled compartments supports endosome‐mediated transport (Figure 6e,f), a mechanism observed in neurotrophin and viral trafficking (Debaisieux et al. 2016; Villarroel‐Campos et al. 2018; Wang et al. 2016). The dynamics of signalling endosomes have previously been characterized using nerve growth factor (NGF)‐coated quantum dots, which exhibited directed retrograde movement toward the soma with average velocities ranging from 0.2 to 3 µm/s (Cui et al. 2007). In this milieu, the retrograde transport speed of Ec EVs observed in this study (∼0.4 µm/s; Figure 6e) falls well within the established range for active endosomal trafficking in neurons, supporting the idea that bEVs utilize similar intracellular transport mechanisms.

Importantly, the inhibitor data support the involvement of dynamin‐dependent and membrane cholesterol‐sensitive processes in bEV uptake by sensory neurons. Consistent with this interpretation, both dynasore and MβCD significantly reduced bEV internalization (Figure 7). However, these inhibitors can affect the membrane through other mechanisms; therefore, the data do not prove that bEVs use only one uptake pathway. Nevertheless, the results are consistent with prior findings that EV uptake commonly involves endocytic trafficking mechanisms (Gabaran et al. 2025).

Further research is warranted to identify naturally occurring bacterial RNAs and determine their potential effects on neuronal function, particularly their downstream effects on synaptic signalling, neural circuit activity and neural behaviour. Future studies employing in vivo imaging, spatial transcriptomics and disease models are also essential to determine the long‐term physiological relevance and persistence of these phenomena.

Finally, as early OB dysfunction is a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease and Parkinson's disease (Fullard et al. 2017; Ubeda‐Bañon et al. 2020), the ability of bEVs to reach and deliver cargo to this region via the nasal route offers a compelling strategy for early therapeutic intervention or localized modulation in these diseases.

Collectively, our data present bEVs as naturally occurring, nanoscale couriers that can overcome CNS barriers, modulate neuronal and immune cell physiology and potentially reshape the brain microenvironment. Understanding these mechanisms can not only expand our knowledge of host–microbe interactions in the CNS but also open novel avenues for exploiting bacterial vesicles in therapeutic delivery strategies targeting the brain.

Conclusions

In summary, intranasally administered Ec EVs traverse the olfactory epithelium, get internalized by both neurons and immune cells and deliver functional RNA to brain regions. The identification of neuronal retrograde and immune cell‐mediated transport provides mechanistic insights into physiological microbial access to the CNS (Figure 8). These findings expand our understanding of host–microbe communication and identify bEVs as active players in the microbiota–brain axis, with implications for neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration and the development of non‐invasive RNA delivery strategies for neurological disorders.

Schematic summary of bacterial extracellular vesicle (bEV)‐mediated delivery of RNA cargo to the brain via neuronal and phagocytic pathways. This figure presents a working model based on the experimental findings of this study.

Left panel: Anatomical context of EV uptake and trafficking routes. Intranasally administered EVs interact with sustentacular cells, olfactory neurons and immune cells in the olfactory epithelium. EVs are transported either via phagocytic cell migration across the lamina propria and cribriform plate or via retrograde axonal transport through olfactory neurons projecting into the glomerular layer of the olfactory bulb. Here, EV‐derived cargo was detected in mitral cells, astrocytes and microglia. Together, these findings support dual‐entry routes, namely neuronal and immune cell‐mediated, by which bEVs can deliver RNA cargo from the nasal cavity to the brain.

Right panel (top to bottom): A step‐by‐step summary of our experimental workflow and results. E. coli‐derived extracellular vesicles (Ec EVs) were electroporated with Cre mRNA (EVCre) and intranasally delivered to mT/mG reporter mice. EVCre were taken up at the olfactory mucosa by olfactory sensory neurons and infiltrating immune cells. Once internalized, the vesicles entered endosomal compartments, where Cre mRNA was released, translated in the cytoplasm, and translocated into the nucleus to drive Cre‐mediated recombination. This resulted in the reporter switch from membrane‐targeted tdTomato (mT, red) to GFP (mG, green), confirming functional RNA delivery to brain cells. The uptake and transport of EVs were inhibited by dynasore and MβCD, supporting the involvement of endocytic mechanisms. Images were created using BioRender.com.
