Novel_circ_002651 regulates the immune defense of eastern honeybee larvae against fungal invasion through sponging miR-6001-y
Authors: Kaiyao Zhang, Fangji Wang, Nian Fan, Xiaoxue Fan, Tao Wu, Yaqin Geng, Xinrui Chen, Jianfeng Qiu, Zhongmin Fu, Dafu Chen, Rui Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile high-throughput sequencing and bioinformatic predictions have identified numerous circRNAs in honeybees, research into their functional roles and molecular mechanisms remains limited. This study aims to characterize the regulatory functions of a previously identified circRNA (novel_circ_002651, ac2651) and its key target miRNA (ace-miR-6001-y), in eastern honeybee worker larvae responding to infection by A. apis, a causative fungal pathogen for chalkbrood disease. Here, RT-qPCR detection showed that the expression of ac2651 was significantly down-regulated in the 6-d-old larval gut, while the ace-miR-6001-y expression exhibited an opposite trend. RNAi-based interference of ac2651 significantly increased the expression of both host immune genes and fungal proliferation-associated genes. Additionally, silencing ac2651 resulted in a reduction of host survival rate and a significant elevation of chalkbrood incidence. Overexpression and knockdown experiments demonstrated that ace-miR-6001-y negatively regulated the expression of dorsal in the larval guts and affected the expression of mat1-2-1, Ste11-like, and Htf in A. apis. Dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed direct interactions between ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y as well as between ace-miR-6001-y and Ac14-3-3ζ. It was validated that simultaneous ac2651 silencing and ace-miR-6001-y knockdown reversed the upregulation of Ac14-3-3ζ induced by ace-miR-6001-y knockdown alone. These results delineate a novel regulatory axis wherein ac2651 sponges ace-miR-6001-y to alleviate its repression of Ac14-3-3ζ, thereby activating the host immune defense against the A. apis infection. Our findings not only offer novel insights into the interaction between honeybee larvae and fungal pathogen but also provide promising biomarkers and targets for the diagnosis and treatment of chalkbrood.

Introduction

Non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) are classified into various categories based on their length, structural features, and subcellular localization. Among these, microRNA (miRNA), long non-coding RNA (lncRNA), circular RNA (circRNA), and PIWI-interacting RNA (piRNA) represent the four major types, each playing distinct functional roles [1]. Several classes of ncRNAs regulate diverse cellular processes, including chromatin architecture, RNA/protein scaffolding, epigenetic modifications, enhancer function, alternative splicing, RNA stability and translation [2]. CircRNAs represent a distinct class of covalently closed ncRNA molecules characterized by the absence of free 5′ and 3′ ends [3]. CircRNAs in insects are involved in various aspects of biology, including development, reproduction, metamorphosis, insecticide resistance, aging, life-span, and host-pathogen interactions and immunity, among others biological processes [4]. MiRNAs are single-stranded noncoding RNAs approximately 22 nucleotides in length that act as powerful regulators of diverse cellular processes, including cell growth, differentiation, development, and apoptosis [5]. They function primarily through post-transcriptional regulation of gene expression. CircRNAs act as miRNA sponges by binding to miRNA response elements (MREs), thereby sequestering miRNAs and inhibiting their regulatory activity, in accordance with the competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) mechanism [6].

Research on ceRNA mechanisms has been conducted predominantly in a limited number of model organisms, especially humans, where numerous studies have elucidated the roles of ceRNAs in various pathological processes such as autoimmune diseases and cancers [7–9]. In contrast, investigations into insect systems remain relatively scarce. For instance, in Culex pipiens pallens, Lv et al. proposed that a specific circRNA (supercont3.352:252102|253283) may contribute to deltamethrin resistance via the supercont3.352:252102|253283/cpp-miR-1671/CYP4G15 axis [10]. Similarly, in the silkworm, Bombyx mori, circEgg was reported to modulate histone H3K9me3 modification both by sponging bmo-miR-3391-5p and by encoding a functional protein, circEgg-P122 [11]. Furthermore, Gao et al. demonstrated that in Aedes albopictus, aal-circRNA-407 facilitates ovarian development through the miR-9a-5p/Foxl pathway [12]. Complementing these findings, our previous research revealed that Ascosphaera apis infection in A. mellifera activates a circRNA- and lncRNA-mediated ceRNA network, which post-transcriptionally modulates key immune-related pathways, including lysosome, endocytosis, phagosome, autophagy, MAPK, Toll, and Imd signaling, by sequestering miRNAs [13,14]. Furthermore, a study by Ye et al. revealed that ame_circ_000115 may function as a ceRNA by sponging ame-miR-13b, thereby potentially regulating the immune response in the larval gut of A. mellifera workers under A. apis stress [15]. These results underscore the pivotal role of non-coding RNAs in regulating insect immune responses, highlighting the expanding significance of ceRNA mechanisms beyond vertebrate systems.

Honey bees are vital pollinators in both natural ecosystems and agricultural landscapes, playing a crucial role in maintaining flowering plant biodiversity and ensuring global food security [16]. Nearly three-quarters of the world’s major food crops depend on bee pollination, which significantly enhances agricultural productivity and supports economic stability [17]. Apis cerana, a significant indigenous pollinator widely distributed across Asia, exhibits distinctive biological traits that underpin its considerable ecological and economic value [18]. This species demonstrates high adaptability to environmental fluctuations and strong disease resistance, characteristics that have led to its extensive use in apiculture [19]. However, A. apis, a pathogenic fungus that specifically infects bee larvae and causes chalkbrood disease, poses a serious threat by impairing colony vitality, reducing productivity, and resulting in substantial economic losses in beekeeping [20]. In recent years, our team has systematically investigated circRNAs in the eastern honey bee, revealing their key regulatory roles in immune response and gut development. We identified numerous circRNAs in the midguts of worker bees and larval guts, which exhibit stage-specific expression patterns and are differentially expressed under pathogen challenge [21–23]. During Nosema ceranae infection, these circRNAs participate in immune-related pathways, such as endocytosis, lysosome function, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis, via the ceRNA mechanism [21]. Furthermore, during larval gut development, circRNAs are involved in regulating signaling pathways including Hippo, Wnt, and mTOR, thereby influencing functional maturation of the gut. Gao et al. [23] identified and validated a critical ceRNA regulatory axis, novel_circ_001627/ace-miR-6001-y/apterous, which provides direct molecular evidence for the role of circRNAs in eastern honey bee gut development. Despite these advances, research on the functional roles of circRNAs in A. cerana, particularly those mediated through the ceRNA mechanism, remains in an exploratory stage and warrants further investigation.

In a previous study, based on deep sequencing and bioinformatics, we analyzed the differential expression profile of circRNAs in the guts of A. cerana worker larvae infected by the A. apis [24]. Due to high abundance and significant differential expression [24], novel_circ_002651 (ac2651) was selected for further investigation in this study. It is speculated that ac2651 acts as a potential “master regulator” through a ceRNA network targeting multiple immune-related pathways, thereby coordinating the host’s broad-spectrum defense against A. apis infection. The back-splicing (BS) site in ac2651 was verified using molecular method, and the expression profiles of ac2651 and its key target miRNA (ace-miR-6001-y) were then investigated. Additionally, RNAi-based silencing of ac2651 was performed, followed by detection of the expression of fungal proliferation-relevant genes as well as host immune genes and measurement of host survival rate and chalkbrood incidence. Overexpression and knockdown of ace-miR-6001-y were conducted to decipher its function in the larval A. apis-response. Furthermore, simultaneous silencing of ac2651 and knockdown of ace-miR-6001-y were conducted to explore the mechanism underlying the host response to fungal invasion. Findings from this current work could not only offer new insights into the interaction between A. cerana worker larvae and A. apis but also provide candidate biomarkers and targets for the diagnosis and treatment of chalkbrood disease.

Materials and methods

Fungi and bee larvae

A. apis was previously isolated from chalkbrood mummies and conserved at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (NO. 40,895). A. cerana worker larvae were derived from three colonies reared in the teaching apiary of the College of Bee Science and Biomedicine at Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou, China.

Target prediction and analysis

By using the TargetFind software and a combination of mireap (http://sourceforge.net/projects/mireap/), miRanda (http://www.microrna.org/microrna/home.do), and TargetScan (http://www.targetscan.org/) software, the potential targeting relationships between ac2651 and miRNAs as well as those between ace-miR-6001-y and mRNAs were predicted. On the basis of the predicted targeting relationships, ac2651/miRNA and ace-miR-6001-y/mRNAs regulatory networks were constructed followed by visualization utilizing the Cytoscape v.3.2.1 software, with default parameters.

PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from the 6-d-old larval guts using the SteadyPure RNA Extraction Kit (Accurate, Hunan, China). Following treatment with RNase R (Beyotime, China) to remove linear RNAs, cDNA synthesis was performed with the Hifair® III 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen, China) using Random Primers N6. The resultant cDNA was used as templates to conduct PCR amplification of the BS site in ac2651, using divergent primers (Table S1). Meanwhile, the reverse transcription was performed to gain cDNA templates with stem-loop primers (Table S1) for PCR amplification of ace-miR-6001-y. The amplified products were cloned into a pClone007 vector (Tsingke, China) for Sanger sequencing by Sangon Biotech (China) Co., Ltd.

Detection of expression profiles of ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y

To characterize ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y expression profiles, total RNA were extracted from the 4-, 5-, and 6-d-old larval guts inoculated with A. apis spores (AcT1, AcT2, and AcT3 groups) and the uninoculated 4-, 5-, and 6-d-old larval guts (AcCK1, AcCK2, and AcCK3 groups). The cDNAs of circRNA and miRNA were obtained, respectively, using the same method as previously described. And the expression of ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y, relative to the internal reference actin (NCBI reference number: XM_017059068.2)and U6 (NCBI reference number: XM_017055661.2) gene (Table S1), was determined qRT-PCR. All experiments were repeated three times.

Dual-reporter gene assay

PCR amplification primers were designed to target the binding site between ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y (Table S1). The complementary sequence of the binding site was synthesized with appropriate sticky ends and cloned into the pmirGLO vector (Promega, USA) using SacI and XhoI restriction enzymes, resulting in the recombinant plasmid pmirGLO-ac2651-miR-6001-wt. Using the same strategy, additional recombinant plasmids were constructed: pmirGLO-miR-6001-14-3-3ζ-wt, which contains the binding site, as well as pmirGLO-ac2651-miR-6001-mut and pmirGLO-miR-6001-14-3-3ζ-mut, which carry mutated versions of the binding site.

Based on the nucleotide sequence of ace-miR-6001-y, miRNA mimics (Mimic-ace-miR-6001-y, abbreviated as M-miR-6001) and corresponding negative controls (Mimic negative control, M-NC) were synthesized by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd (Table S1). For luciferase reporter assays, HEK293T cells were co-transfected with 100 ng of plasmid and 10 pmol of mimic using 0.5 μL of Hieff TransTM Liposomal Nucleic Acid Transfection Reagent (Yeasen, China). After 24 h of culture, firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured with the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

siRNA of ac2651

To control for nonspecific physiological stress and exogenous RNA interference, a non-targeting scramble siRNA (si-scramble) was used as the negative control, serving as the baseline for natural disease progression to ensure that observed phenotypic changes were specifically attributable to ac2651 silencing. The siRNAs for ac2651 (si-ac2651) and si-scramble were designed by our group and then synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd (Table S1). The 3-d-old larvae (n = 3) in the treatment group were initially administered 50 µL of diet containing A. apis spores (1 × 106 spores/larva) along with si-ac2651 (2 µg/larva). After 24 h, the diet was replaced with one containing only si-ac2651, which was continued until the larvae reached 6-d-old. In the control group, 3-d-old larvae (n = 3) were fed with 50 µL of diet supplemented with an equivalent concentration of spores (1 × 106 spores/larva) and a scrambled siRNA control (si-scramble, 2 µg/larva). The gut tissues were dissected from larvae in both groups the gut tissue of 4-, 5-, and 6-d-old larvae (n = 3 per time point). There were three biological replicates per group. The collected tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent analysis.

Overexpression and knockdown of ace-miR-6001-y

The inhibitors for ace-miR-6001-y (I-miR-6001) and corresponding negative control (I-NC) were designed by our team followed by synthesis by Beijing Tsingke Biotech Co., Ltd (Table S1). The 3-d-old larvae in the treatment group were administered 50 μL of a spore-supplemented diet along with either M-miR-6001 or I-miR-6001 (at a dosage of 40 pmol/g). After 24 h, the diet was replaced with one containing only the corresponding miRNA mimic or inhibitor, which was continued until the larvae reached 6-d-old. Control larvae were fed an equivalent amount of diet containing either M-NC or I-NC. Gut tissues were collected from 4-, 5-, and 6-d-old larvae (n = 3 per time point), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at − 80°C for subsequent analysis. Three biological replicates were included in each group.

Simutaneous ac2651 silencing and ace-miR-6001-y knockdown

The larvae were divided into two groups: (1) 5 μL spores + 10 μL si-scramble + 35 μL feed containing I-NC; (2) 5 μL spores + 10 μL si-ac2651 + 35 μL feed containing I-6001-y. Spore-free feed was replaced every 24 h, and the larvae were fed continuously for 3 d. Gut samples of the larvae were collected on 4-, 5-, and 6-d-old (n = 3 per time point) for gene expression analysis via qRT-PCR. Meanwhile, statistics on the survival rate and incidence rate of the larvae were collected. The presence of white hyphae on the larval exterior was utilized as the definitive marker for disease incidence in this study. There were three biological replicates per group.

RT-qPCR detection of host immune genes and pathogen proliferation genes

Three immune genes from the host, such as hymenoptaecin (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_017049926.1), toll (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_017053307.2), and dorsal (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_017053960.2), were selected for qRT-PCR analysis, with actin (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_017059068.2) serving as the reference gene. In parallel, three pathogenicity-related genes associated with fungal proliferation and infection, namely mat1-2-1 (GenBank accession number: EF156413), Ste11-like (GenBank accession number: EF156415), and Htf (GenBank accession number: EF156416), were also included in the qRT-PCR assay, using the 5.8S rRNA gene (GenBank accession number: U68313.1) as an internal control. Total RNA was isolated from the gut tissue of 6-d-old larvae (n = 3), and then reverse transcribed with a 1:1 mixture of Oligo dT and Random 6-mer primers, and the resulting cDNA was employed as template for quantitative real-time PCR amplification. Three biological replicates were included in each group.

Evaluation of host survival rate and chalkbrood incidence

Based on the aforementioned methods, 3-d-old larvae (3 biological replicates per group, each biological replicate included 24 individual larvae) were inoculated with A. apis spores and subsequently fed daily with si-ac2651, si-scramble, M-miR-6001, I-miR-6001, M-NC, or I-NC until they reached 10-d-old. Beginning at 0 d post-inoculation (dpi), daily observations were conducted to monitor and statistically analyze larval survival rates across treatment and control groups, as well as the occurrence of chalkbrood disease in the host. Data are presented as the mean values derived from three independent biological replicates. The presence of white hyphae on the larval exterior was regarded as the definitive marker for disease incidence in this study.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). All experimental data are expressed as the Mean±SD. Sudent’s t-test was used for statistical analysis: ns: p > 0.05, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.

Result

Molecular validation and expression pattern of ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y in the guts of A. cerana worker larvae infected by the A. apis

ac2651 was predicted to target a total of 100 miRNAs, forming a regulatory network (Figure 1(A)). Using the divergent primers, the PCR amplification generated a fragment corresponding to ac2651 with the expected size (about 109 bp) (Figure 1(B)). The Sanger sequencing suggested that the sequence of amplified fragment was consistent with that predicted based on transcriptome data (Figure 1(C)). Additionally, the sequence included the BS site of ac2651. The expected fragment (approximately 87 bp in size) was amplified from ace- miR-6001-y, and as shown in Figure 1(D), the sequence of target fragment was in accordance with the predicted sequence using transcriptome data (Figure 1(E)). Among them, it was found in the sequencing data obtained in the early stage that the expression trend of ace-miR-6001-y and ac2651 in the larval gut after infection with A. apis showed a negative correlation. Post-inoculation with A. apis, qRT-PCR analysis indicated that the expression level of ac2651 was significantly down-regulated in 4- (t ratio = 13.81, df = 4.00), 5- (t ratio = 5.17, df = 4.00), and 6-d-old (t ratio = 12.69, df = 4.00) (p < 0.01) (Figure 1(F)). While ace-miR-6001-y was significantly down-regulated in 4- (t ratio = 6.74, df = 4.00) and 5- (t ratio = 5.79, df = 4.00) d-old (p < 0.01), and significantly up-regulated (p < 0.05, t ratio = 4.47, df = 4.00) in 6-d-old (Figure 1(G)). Silencing ac2651 suppressed the host immune response to the A. apis infection

qRT-PCR assays suggested that the expression level of ac2651 was significantly decreased (p < 0.05) by 53.6% and 48.3% in the 5- (t ratio = 4.69, df = 4.00) and 6-d-old (t ratio = 3.00, df = 4.00) larval guts in the si-ac2651 group, as compared with that in the si-scramble group (Figure 3(A)). As shown in Figure 3(B), silencing ac2651 significantly enhanced the expression of host immunes genes toll (t ratio = 4.50, df = 4.00) and dorsal (t ratio = 3.23, df = 4.00) (p < 0.05). Additionally, the expression of three fungal proliferation-relevant genes, such as mat1-2-1 (t ratio = 4.97, df = 4.00), Ste11-like (t ratio = 6.35, df = 4.00), and Htf (t ratio = 22.15, df = 4.00) expression, were increased significantly after silencing ac2651 (p < 0.01) (Figure 3(C)). The role of ace-miR-6001-y in the immune response during A. apis infection in A. cerana

In comparison with that in the M-NC group, the expression level of ace-miR-6001-y was increased in the 4-d-old (t ratio = 1.61, df = 4.00) larval gut and significantly upregulated (p < 0.01) in the 5- (t ratio = 9.34, df = 4.00) and 6-d-old (t ratio = 7.38, df = 4.00) larval guts in the M-miR-6001 group (Figure 4(A)). In addition, the ace-miR-6001-y knockdown caused inverse expression patterns of host immune genes and fungal proliferation-relative genes mentioned above (Figure 4(G, H)). Discussions

In this study, ac2651 was observed to be down-regulated in the guts of A. cerana worker larvae infected by the A. apis (Figure 1(F)), indicative of its involvement in the larval response to fungal invasion. On basis of our previous gained transcriptome data, ac2651 was predicted to originate from the genomic coordinates 2 337 070 to 2 347 657 of the kinectin gene (NCBI gene symbol: LOC108001331), with a total spliced length of 9 063 bp. kinectin, a crucial gene encoding a major kinesin-binding protein localized to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). It mediates interactions with microtubules and modulates the dynamics and functionality of the cytoskeleton [25]. In addition, PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing confirmed the BS site in ac2651 as well as the expression and sequence of ace-miR-6001-y, as shown in Figure 1. The results validated the authenticity of ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y and their expression in the larval guts.

Accumulating evidence have suggested that ncRNAs, including circRNAs and miRNAs, are vital modulators in responses of insects to microbial attacks [26]. In B. mori, the overexpression of circRNA_1193 resulted in a significant reduction in BmCPV replication, whereas its knockdown had the opposite effect, indicating that circRNA_1193 may serve as a negative regulator of importance for viral replication in silkworms [27]. In Aedes aegypti infected with Beauveria bassiana, the expression of novel-circ-930 was significantly increased, suggestive of the activation of novel-circ-930 by fungal infection [28]. Here, RT-qPCR assays showed that the expression level of ac2651 was significantly reduced in the larval guts following the A. apis infection (Figure 1(F)), indicating the suppression of ac2651 in the host guts by the fungal challenge. This implied that ac2651 was engaged in the larval A. apis- response. Conversely, we observed that ace-miR-6001-y was significantly down-regulated in the guts of 4- and 5-d-old larvae, while up-regulated significantly in the 6-d-old larval gut, as shown in Figure 1(G). This biphasic response may be attributed to the metabolic and regulatory shifts during infection: at the early stage, the rapid germination of A. apis spores not only consumes substantial host energy but may also involve fungal miRNAs that interfere with the biogenesis of host ace-miR-6001-y. In contrast, at the later stage of infection, ace-miR-6001-y may be recruited as a key regulatory element to mobilize the host’s immune defense system. These data demonstrated that during the fungal infection there was a negative correlation of the expression levels between ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y.

A circRNA could target multiple miRNAs, and vica versa. As a kind of endogenous regulatory modulators, circRNAs exert biological functions by acting as transcriptional regulators, miRNA sponges or protein templates [29]. In this study, regulatory network analysis demonstrated that ac2651 could target a total of 100 miRNAs, including ace-miR-6001-y (Figure 1(A)). Previously, Fan et al. found that miR-6001-y might be activated in the development of A. cerana worker larval gut, potentially regulating several developmental signaling pathways of great importance such as Wnt, Hippo, and Jak-STAT [30]. Chen et al. discovered that an array of DEcircRNAs may affect the development of the A. cerana worker’s midgut by competitively absorbing miR-6001-y, further controlling the expression of downstream development-related genes [22]. Dual-luciferase reporter gene assays validated the binding relationships between ac2651 and ace-miR-6001-y (Figure 5(E)). Moreover, RNAi-based silencing of ac2651 resulted in the up-regulation of ace-miR-6001-y. Together, these results indicated that ac2651 targeted and negatively regulated ace-miR-6001-y in the larval guts infected by the A. apis.

The seven highly conserved 14-3-3 protein isoforms (β, ε, γ, η, σ, τ/θ, and ζ) play an essential part in diverse cellular processes, such as the DNA repair, development, tissue injury response, as well as cell cycle, proliferation, apoptosis, adhesion, and motility, across eukaryotes [31]. The 14-3-3ζ has recently been reported to be involved in the innate immune response of Drosophila [32] and Haliotis discus hannai [33]. Notably, the downregulation of Ac14-3-3ζ observed in A. apis-infected larval guts [34] showed a negative correlation with the expression of ace-miR-6001-y, as identified in this study (Figure 1(G)). Here, the authentic binding between Ac14-3-3ζ and ace-miR-6001-y was verified, as shown in Figure 5(J). Subsequent functional assays demonstrated that ace-miR-6001-y acts as a negative regulator of Ac14-3-3ζ during fungal infection (Figure 6(C)). In summary, these results were indicative of the ceRNA network among ac2651, ace-miR-6001-y, and Ac14-3-3ζ.

CeRNAs compete with mRNAs for binding to shared miRNA response elements (MREs), thus acting as natural miRNA sponges that modulate miRNA activity by limiting their availability to authentic targets [35]. ciRS-7 promotes Cryptosporidium parvum propagation in HCT-8 cells through sponging miR-1270 to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway [36]. In Tetranychus cinnabarinus, circ1-3p enhances cyflumetofen resistance by sponging miR-1-3p to upregulate TcGSTm04 expression [37]. Here, simultaneous ac2651 silencing and ace-miR-6001-y knockdown reversed the upregulation of Ac14-3-3ζ induced by ace-miR-6001-y knockdown alone (Figure 6). In addition, ac2651 silencing in combination with ace-miR-6001-y knockdown reduced the host chalkbrood incidence from 79.19% to 58.33% (Figure 7(D)). These data demonstrate that ac2651 enhances the Ac14-3-3ζ expression in A. cerana worker larval gut during A. apis infection by targeting and suppressing ace-miR-6001-y.

Dorsal and toll are two key immune genes associated with the Toll signaling pathway. In insects, the Toll signaling pathway is initiated when pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) activate the extracellular cytokine Spätzle, which binds to the Toll receptor and further triggers intracellular signaling cascades involving Myd88, Pelle, and Tube, ultimately leading to NF-κB transcription factors (e.g. Dif/Dorsal and Rel1) translocating into the nucleus to induce the expression of antimicrobial peptides and other immune effector genes [38]. Hymenoptaecin, one of critical antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) mainly expressed in bees and other hymenopteran insects, is engaged in innate immune defense. As small bioactive molecules with broad-spectrum activity against bacteria, fungi, and viruses, AMPs serve as evolutionarily conserved effectors of innate immunity in prokaryotes and eukaryotes, participating in diverse physiological and pathological processes [39,40]. In this current work, the A. apis infection significantly upregulated the expression of dorsal in the larval guts, indicating its crucial role in antifungal defense (Figure 7A). Notably, simultaneous ac2651 silencing and ace-miR-6001-y knockdown abolished the suppressive effect of ace-miR-6001-y knockdown on the toll expression. This suggests that ac2651 modulates the Toll signaling pathway by targeting ace-miR-6001-y in host response to the A. apis challenge.

The genome of A. apis encodes three putative high mobility group (HMG-box) transcription factors, including the mat1-2-1, Ste11-like, and Htf [41]. HMGB, a subfamily of HMG proteins, exerts functions via modulation of DNA repair processes, facilitation of damage repair, and induction of apoptosis [42]. HMGB deficiency exhibits pleiotropic effects on hyphal growth, morphology, conidial metabolism, and heterochromatin-associated secondary metabolite production [43]. In the present study, it was observed that the expression of Htf in the 6-d-old larval gut infected by the A. apis was significantly activated (Figure 7C). This phenomenon likely results from rapid fungal spore germination under aerobic conditions and extensive hyphal proliferation during the late stage of the A. apis infection [44]. Additionally, following ac2651 silencing and ace-miR-6001-y knockdown, the significant downregulation of mat1-2-1 and Ste11-like in the A. apis infecting the 6-d-old larval guts (Figure 7C), suggestive of the activation of the Toll signaling pathway in the A. apis-infected larval guts to enhance antimicrobial peptide production, thereby inhibiting the fungal proliferation [45].

In this current work, the identified ac2651/ace-miR-6001-y/Ac14-3-3ζ provides a foundation for developing targeted RNAi formulations (e.g. as feed additives) to boost larval immunity during high-risk seasons. More importantly, based on these candidate RNA molecules, new control strategies, such as RNAi, are expected to be developed in the future, which is beneficial for biocontrol of chalkbrood disease.

Conclusion

ac2651 targets ace-miR-6001-y as a “molecular sponge,” directly controlling the Ac14-3-3ζ expression and indirectly regulating the expression of host immune genes (hymenoptaecin, toll, and dorsal) as well as fungal proliferation-associated genes (mat1-2-1, Ste11-like, and Htf), which further modulates the immune defense of the A. cerana worker larvae against the A. apis invasion (Figure 8).
