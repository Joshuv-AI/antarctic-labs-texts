Origin of crack propagation in lithium cobalt oxide positive electrode for lithium-ion batteries
Authors: Wenguang Zhao, Zijian Li, Mingyang Li, Hengyu Ren, Cong Lin, Haocong Yi, Shengyu Wu, Xiaohu Wang, Jun Wang, Bin Fei, Muyu Zhang, Mingwei Wang, Ye Sun, Guojie Chen, Zhefeng Chen, Weixin Chen, Xiaoxu Zhao, Shunning Li, Dong Zhou, Qinghe Zhao, Feng Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough the anionic activity in layered oxide positive electrodes is partially understood, the mechanism by which it induces internal crack propagation under high-voltage conditions remains unclear, severely limiting stable cycle life and available capacity. In this study, the mechanism underlying internal crack propagation is elucidated in LiCoO2 under harsh float-charge conditions at 60 °C and a potential of 4.6 V vs. Li+/Li. We reveal that sequential phase transitions from O3 to O1 and then to a cation-mixed phase during deep Li+ extraction generate significant lattice-mismatch stresses that initiate internal cracks. Crucially, the accompanying anionic activity drives the migration of the oxidized lattice On− (0 < n < 2) towards these cracks, where it even accumulates as molecular O2 that can be released upon crack propagation to the surface. It is identified that the sustained O1tocation-mixed phase transition and the bulk lattice On− migration synergistically drive the interior crack propagation, ultimately leading to LiCoO2 disintegration and electrochemical degradation. This work not only deepens our understanding of the anionic activity in layered positive electrodes but also provides mechanistic insights for the future design of stable LiCoO2 positive electrodes.Subject terms: Batteries, Batteries, Batteries

Introduction

As a cornerstone positive electrode in commercial lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), layered LiCoO₂ (LCO) is extensively applied and dominates portable electronics1,2. However, to meet the escalating demand for higher energy density, operating LCO at elevated charge potential above 4.55 V (vs. Li+/Li) is crucial for accessing its full capacity3. This high-voltage operation, however, inherently triggers severe structural instability and causes rapid electrochemical deterioration3. Our previous investigations have illuminated that the high-voltage instability is intricately associated with the surface lattice curvature4, irreversible phase transitions (e.g., the well-established O3 → H1-3 → O1 pathway)4,5, and the activation and subsequent release of lattice On− upon deep delithiation6,7, which are coupled with cation disordering8. These factors collectively contribute to the intrinsic degradation mechanisms of LCO.

Historically, research into LCO degradation at high voltages has predominantly focused on surface structural evolution, often describing a surface-to-bulk degradation pathway9–11. This surface-centric view highlights two primary aspects. First, the gradual activation of surface lattice On− (0 < n < 2) leads to the migration and loss of O and Co12, which subsequently accelerates cation mixing while triggering irreversible phase transitions in the surface regions13. As a result, this progressively transforms the LCO surface to spinel and then rock-salt (RS) phases, critically impeding interfacial Li⁺ transport, causing heterogeneous charge distribution14, and ultimately leading to rapid capacity decay15,16. Second, electrolyte decomposition, particularly the formation of corrosive species such as HF, further exacerbates surface degradation and destabilizes surface-protective cathode/electrolyte interphase (CEI)9,17–19. However, these surface-dominated mechanisms do not adequately explain the bulk structural degradation observed through crack formation. Despite their frequent observation and wide recognition, the precise mechanisms of crack propagation and their localized microscopic origins within LCO bulk remain significantly underexplored.

Upon deep delithiation, layered-oxide positive electrodes are known to undergo complex phase transitions, accompanied by significant anisotropic lattice distortions and accumulation of lattice-mismatch stress. This ultimately leads to the mechanical degradation and microcrack formation within individual particles2,8,20–22. Generally, the high-voltage-induced O3-to-O1 phase transition, often proceeding through the H1-3 intermediate, is particularly pronounced, causing localized lattice dislocations and Co-O slab bending. These structural changes further induce drastic lattice variations with Co-O slab slippage, local structure collapse, and even oxygen reorganization15,23. As a result, substantial residual strain is accumulated with concentrated tensile and compressive stresses, initiating subsequent internal cracks and further particle fragmentation24. Recent studies have also highlighted a strong correlation between lattice strain-induced oxygen vacancies OV) and reduced barriers for lattice oxygen release, which can intensify crack propagation along the (003) basal planes25. However, the precise mechanism by which the anionic activity contributes to the internal crack propagation in LCO upon deep delithiation is largely elusive.

Given the practical significance of developing high-voltage and high-energy-density LIBs, it is imperative to elucidate the mechanisms and critical factors governing the propagation of internal cracks in LCO, a pervasive phenomenon and a major contributor to electrochemical degradation. To this end, we employed float tests26, a robust accelerated aging technique, to assess the long-term stability related to interior cracks. Here, with various experimental techniques and theoretical calculations, we present the mechanistic study of the interior crack propagation within LCO. By carefully minimizing the influence of external factors (particularly the surface effects and electrolyte degradation), our results reveal a synergistic phase transition and lattice On− migration-induced crack propagation mechanism. Specifically, upon nearly complete delithiation, the bulk lattice On− in O1 phase tends to migrate towards interior cracks and accumulates there, forming molecular O2 trapped inside LCO bulk. This process is concurrently accompanied by the emergence of a cation-mixed (CM) phase. Crucially, the subsequent float charge further exacerbates the internal stress along the O1/CM phase boundaries, significantly promoting the interior crack propagation. Our findings demonstrate that the sustained O1-to-CM phase transitions and the migration of oxidized lattice On− species, coupled with molecular O2 accumulation, synergistically drive the continuous propagation of interior cracks that eventually penetrate throughout the particle to the surface, leading to gaseous O2 release and severe LCO disintegration. With this fundamental mechanism underlying LCO crack propagation, our study may not only deepen understanding of LCO degradation but also provide valuable guidance for the design of advanced layered-oxide positive electrodes.

Results

Phase transition of LCO upon float charge

A commercial LCO, characterized by a large D50 value of about 15 μm and containing trace Al (4500 ppm), Mg (1500 ppm), and Ti (500 ppm), was selected for this investigation into the degradation under float conditions. To assess the float-charge performance and induced degradation, coin-type Li||LCO cells were assembled and used. Float charge tests were performed by initially charging the cells to 4.6 V with a constant current (CC) of 100 mA g−1 (CC stage), followed by constant voltage (CV) charging at 4.6 V (CV stage) (Fig. 1a). Fundamental characterizations, including scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Supplementary Fig. 1), X-ray diffraction (XRD) coupled with Rietveld refinement (Supplementary Fig. 2), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Supplementary Fig. 3), confirmed a uniformly smooth surface and a pure α-NaFeO2-type layered structure with an R-3m space group. However, the enlarged image in Supplementary Fig. 3b reveals the presence of unavoidable inherent defects, such as curves in partial Co-O layers within the commercial LCO, which is difficult to eliminate in actual production.

Under the high temperature float charge conditions, progressive crack formation was distinctly observed during float charge from 0 to 40 h (Supplementary Fig. 4). Further investigation into the temperature dependence of this degradation revealed a pronounced increase in leakage current above 50 °C, underscoring the accelerated degradation kinetics at elevated temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 5). Consistent with this, the extent of crack formation varied with temperature: at 25 and 45 °C, only slight cracks appeared, whereas at 60 °C, severe particle disintegration was evident (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). To further elucidate the propagation of interior cracks, cross-sectional TEM analyses were conducted on the samples after float charge at 25, 45, and 60 °C for an extended duration of 120 h (Supplementary Fig. 8). These analyses demonstrated that while only minor interior cracks were observed at 25 °C, extensive interior cracking became prevalent with increased temperature.

XRD analysis revealed that, after float charge for 120 h, O1 phase became dominant, a notable deviation from the H1-3 phase typically expected at such a high voltage (Fig. 1b). High-resolution scanning TEM (STEM) combined with geometric phase analysis (GPA) revealed heterogeneous O1 domains embedded within the charged LCO bulk before float charge (Supplementary Fig. 9). As a drastically compressed structure, O1 stacking imposes pronounced stresses on the adjacent O3 matrix. This stress evidently intensifies the curvature of the Co-O layers. To elucidate the correlation between stress evolution and crack propagation under different float charging durations, systematic analyses using 4D STEM were conducted (Supplementary Figs. 10–13). Specifically, the stress distribution mapping and line-scan profiles reveal a pronounced spatial correlation between stress-concentration regions and crack locations, suggesting that localized stresses induced by phase transitions and other factors are a critical driving force for crack propagation. To quantitatively assess the relationship between stress concentration severity and crack geometry, a stress concentration factor K, defined as K = 1 + 2×(L/W), where L and W denote crack length and width, respectively, was introduced (Supplementary Fig. 14). The results demonstrate a monotonic increase in K with prolonged float charging time, rising from 3.37 at 5 h to 7.80 at 20 h, 9.50 at 40 h, and ultimately reaching 20.26 at 120 h, indicating progressively intensified stress concentration. More critically, a previously unreported phase characterized by a layer spacing (d = 2.087 Å) approximately half that of the O1 phase (d = 4.191 Å) was identified after float charge for 5 h (Supplementary Fig. 15). This newly formed phase, which we term the cation-mixed (CM) phase, originates from Co migration into the Li layer of the O1 phase under certain stress disturbances (such as more curved Co-O layers and more dislocations). Subsequently, driven by the internal stress, lattice oxygen undergoes oxidation and migration. The oxygen vacancies generated during this process, along with the induced local distortions and coordinatively unsaturated sites, significantly lower the energy barrier for Co ions to hop into adjacent vacancies in the Li layer. Ultimately, under the synergistic driving forces of internal stress, lattice oxygen migration, and vacancies in the Li layer, a portion of Co ions irreversibly migrate from the Co-O layer into the Li layer, thereby forming the structurally disordered CM phase. Aberration-corrected STEM (AC-STEM) confirmed the atomic structure of the CM phase, suggesting that it forms a semi-coherent interface with the O1 phase (Fig. 1c). The occurrence of this CM phase in LCO indicates the increased cation disorder and significant lattice Co and O loss under severe float conditions, a key indicator of bulk degradation27.

The emergence of the CM phase is not merely a consequence of excessive Li⁺ extraction; rather, our findings suggest that the float charge actively facilitates the migration of bulk lattice On−, which is considered the primary driver for the CM phase formation. This crucial mechanism was further confirmed by electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) performed across regions transitioning from the layered to CM phases (Fig. 1d). In the EELS spectra, the pre-edge feature around 530 eV corresponds to the O 1s → O 2p–Co 3d hybridized states, while the main edge at around 541.5 eV is attributed to the core-level O 1s → O 2p transitions28. The intensity ratio of the pre-edge (Ip) to the main edge (Im), denoted as Ip/Im, directly correlates with the concentration of OV29. Notably, as shown in Fig. 1e, the OV concentration was found to decrease upon transition from the layered to the CM phase. This indicates that the substantial oxygen loss from the layered phase drives the generation of under-coordinated Co ions, which then migrate into the Li layers to form the structurally rearranged and potentially more stable CM phase. Concurrently, the Co L3-edge peak shifts to lower energy in the CM phase, further corroborating a reduction in Co oxidation state (Fig. 1f), a direct consequence of O loss and structural rearrangement within the LCO lattice.

Formation of molecular O2 trapped in interior cracks

As discussed previously, the formation of the CM phase within LCO induces significant internal stresses at its boundary with the layered phase due to their distinct lattice configurations, which serve as the critical initiation sites for interior cracks. To investigate the origin and propagation of these cracks, particularly those formed after float charge for 5 h, high-resolution STEM coupled with GPA was performed (Fig. 2a–c). Analysis reveals concentrated regions of intense tensile (shown by red) and compressive (shown by green) stress localized at the crack tips, indicating these stresses as the primary driving force for crack propagation (Supplementary Fig. 9). Further structural analysis of the crack-tip region shows the coexistence of layered O1 and CM phases (Supplementary Fig. 15), where the inherent lattice mismatch at their interface acts as the starting point for these internal cracks. Continued float charge under high-voltage conditions further promotes this crack propagation, ultimately leading to severe particle disintegration.

Beyond stress-induced crack initiation, our comprehensive analyses, including EELS, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), and in situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS), also elucidate the crucial role of bulk lattice On− migration in the crack propagation mechanism. Specifically, the O K-edge EELS spectra reveal a significant increase in OV concentration from LCO bulk towards the crack tips (Fig. 2e, f), indicating the substantial loss of bulk lattice On− at these defect sites. This observation supports our hypothesis that the interior cracks, serving as the in situ-formed defects, provide preferential and shorter diffusion pathways for the On− migration and, critically, the confined spaces for molecular O2 accumulation. EPR measurements provide evidence for the presence of molecular O2 within these interior cracks (Fig. 2g): characteristic resonance signals between 5500 and 9000 G were detected in the LCO after float charge for 10 h30. However, the LCO that undergoes float charging at 25 °C does not produce a significant O2 signal (Supplementary Fig. 16). The release of this trapped molecular O2 was further probed by in situ DEMS (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Fig. 17). Supplementary Fig. 18 displays the resonant inelastic X-ray scattering (RIXS) spectra comparing pristine LCO with the LCO after float charging at 60 °C and 4.6 V for 10 h and 20 h. The absence of detectable signals in pristine LCO, in stark contrast to the clear RIXS signatures observed for the LCO after 10 h and 20 h of charging, substantiates the formation of O2 in the LCO bulk. Our findings suggest that the accumulated molecular O2 within the interior cracks induces additional internal stresses that drive crack propagation, in addition to the lattice-mismatch-induced internal stresses. In addition, DEMS reveals pronounced O2 evolution between 8 and 20 h of float charge, which coincides with an increase in the float charge capacity (Cfloat) from 10 to 20 h and indicates a close correlation between gaseous O2 release and Cfloat increase. This underlying correlation can be elucidated as follows. First, as the float charge proceeds, the concentration of oxidized lattice On− increases, and these On− ions migrate into the interior cracks and form molecular O2 there. When these interior cracks propagate sufficiently to reach the particle surface, the trapped O2 is released, producing the observed surge in O2 signal. Second, the subsequent propagation of these cracks to the surface exposes fresh and highly active LCO interfaces (with active species like Co4+/On−) to the electrolyte, which triggers intense interfacial side reactions. These reactions are directly responsible for the observed increase in Cfloat. This precise correlation between O2 evolution and current response supports the proposed mechanism of high-temperature (HT) floating-induced failure, which originates from bulk oxygen oxidation and crack propagation.

Propagation of interior cracks

To gain a deeper understanding of the dynamic interior crack propagation, transmission X-ray microscopy (TXM) coupled with X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) was further employed (Fig. 3a, b). In TXM images, lighter regions correspond to the lower-valence Co, whereas darker regions indicate the higher-valence Co. After float charge for 5 h, LCO bulk exhibits a uniform distribution of Co valence, suggesting that the overall bulk structure remains largely intact at this early stage. However, a stark contrast emerges after float charge for 40 h, i.e., the Co valence distribution becomes highly heterogeneous, indicating extensive bulk degradation that is attributed to the large-scale migration of lattice On− and the propagation of interior cracks. Complementary TEM images further corroborate these findings, revealing a pronounced CM phase and intensified crack growth between 5 and 40 h of float charge (Supplementary Fig. 19). This highlights the close correlation between the O1-to-CM phase transition and the propagation of interior cracks. Cross-sectional TEM analyses of the LCOs after float charge for 5, 20, and 40 h further show that the interior cracks preferentially initiate along the (003) planes and subsequently coalesce into larger and more destructive fissures during prolonged float charge (Supplementary Fig. 20). Furthermore, TXM tomography provides a powerful three-dimensional visualization of the internal crack networks (Fig. 3c). Its quantitative analysis shows that the volume fraction of interior cracks dramatically increases from 2.06% to 5.51% between 5 and 40 h of float charge, clearly illustrating the rapid and extensive propagation of these interior cracks.

XANES was also employed to monitor the dynamic changes in lattice On− migration and local structural evolution (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 21a). XANES analysis of the Co absorption edge reveals a shift to higher energy after 20 h of float charge, consistent with an increase in Co oxidation state associated with the O3 → O1 phase transition31. However, it moves to the opposite direction after further float charge for 120 h, reflecting a decrease in Co valence due to the substantial lattice On− release, a key event in the degradation process. Further insights into the local structural evolution were enabled by EXAFS analysis (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 21b). The nearest Co–O coordination shell first shifts to a reduced radial distance after float charge for 20 h, signifying shorter Co–O bond lengths characteristic of the O1 phase32. By contrast, it shifts back after 120 h, indicating bond elongation, consistent with the formation of the CM phase. Unlike Co–O, the first Co–Co coordination shell exhibits a sustained decrease in radial distance throughout the float charge process, directly attributed to the transition of O1 to CM phase. Moreover, the diminished peak intensities and contracted distances at higher R-values (4–10 Å) for other Co–O and Co–Co coordination shells after float charge for 120 h reveal the widespread existence of structural disorder within LCO.

Theoretical calculations on the migration of bulk lattice On−

These experimental observations above, including EELS, in situ DEMS, XANES, and EXAFS, clearly illustrate the migration of bulk lattice On− to the interior cracks in LCO, which is at the core of the proposed mechanism linking molecular O2 accumulation and crack propagation. To further elucidate the precise underlying atomic-scale mechanisms, theoretical investigations were conducted. Here, we employed Monte Carlo (MC) simulations and percolation theory to evaluate the capability of On− migration within LCO33. Our theoretical model was predicated on the understanding that during the O1-to-CM phase transition, Co migrates from its original CoO6 octahedron into the interlayer region, leaving behind several types of under-coordinated O ions (i.e., connected with fewer than three Co ions) that tend to be oxidized to On−. Crucially, these redox-active On− species can form a continuous migration pathway within the anion sublattice, similar to the case of Li-rich layered-oxide positive electrodes. For computational efficiency, we simplified the anion sublattice into a two-dimensional cellular network representing On− migration from the bulk to the boundary (Fig. 4a). In these MC simulations, the redox-active lattice On− species are defined as the under-coordinated O ions induced by Co migration. A migration pathway for lattice On− is established when two adjacent redox-active On− sites are connected to the same Co ion, and once the concentration of these redox-active lattice On− sites reaches a critical threshold, a continuous network for On− migration emerges.

As the O1-to-CM phase transition progresses, the proportion (denoted as α) of Co migrating to the interlayer region increases. The simulations demonstrate that at the early stages of Co migration (α < 0.2), the connection probability (p) of redox-active On− sites—representing the likelihood of forming a continuous pathway from one boundary to another—remains less than 0.05 (Fig. 4b). However, as α surpasses 0.5, p increases sharply to over 0.9, indicating that nearly the entire network of redox-active On− sites becomes fully connected to the boundary. This is known as the percolation of redox-active On− in the anion sublattice, upon which the oxidized lattice On− can migrate over long distances to the LCO surface or cracks, ultimately leading to the release of gaseous O2. Significantly, our analysis shows that the distribution of Co ions within the CM phase exactly aligns with the case of α = 0.5; that is, half of the Co ions are located in their original layer of the corresponding O1 phase, while the other half occupy the interlayer region. This suggests that before the complete formation of the CM phase, a considerable fraction of redox-active lattice On− can diffuse to the boundary during the Co migration, thus generating a substantial amount of OV in the lattice. When α approaches 0.5, the accumulated OV triggers an atomic rearrangement of the anion sublattice, culminating in the formation of the CM phase, which, as observed experimentally, exhibits a much lower OV concentration. These results suggest that the formation of CM phase and the long-range migration of lattice On− are mutually synchronized, and this theoretical insight explains our experimental observations that the accumulation of molecular O2 at the interior cracks is consistently accompanied by the CM phase emergence near the crack tips.

Beyond the above migration pathways, we further studied the atomic-level mechanisms facilitating these transformations using density functional theory calculations within the Hubbard U-corrected generalized gradient approximation (GGA+U). PBE-based methods are known to have limitations in accurately describing correlated electronic states and the relative energies of transition-metal and oxygen states in transition-metal oxides34,35. In this context, more advanced approaches, such as dynamical mean-field theory (DMFT) and related beyond density functional theory (DFT) treatments, have been reported in the literature for correlated oxides including LixCoO234,36,37. However, the high computational cost of these methods prevents their application to the large simulation cells considered in this work. Indeed, we have compared the results between GGA + U and the Heyd-Scuseria-Ernzerhof (HSE06) hybrid functional for LCO (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23), which supports the reliability of GGA + U in capturing the qualitative trends in the electronic structure (e.g., enhanced oxygen activity). Therefore, all the calculations provided below are based on the GGA + U scheme. The formation of O dimers within LCO bulk is considered a prerequisite for generating molecular O2, yet its mechanistic role in the O1-to-CM phase transition remained unclear. As shown in Fig. 4c, we investigated the scenario where one Co ion migrates near an O dimer formed within the corresponding CoO6 octahedron in the initial configuration. Both the intralayer and interlayer migrations of Co ions were evaluated. Our calculations demonstrate that the presence of an O dimer significantly reduces the energy barriers by 0.65 and 0.68 eV for intralayer and interlayer Co migration, respectively (Fig. 4d). Even in the presence of some residual Li (with SoC = 90.9%, Supplementary Fig. 24), the formation of an O dimer can still accelerate the Co migration. This finding indicates that the formation of O dimers actively accelerates the Co migration, thereby facilitating the O1-to-CM phase transition. Given the low kinetic barriers in the delithiated O1 phase, the Co migration likely precedes both the long-range migration of lattice On− and the propagation of interior cracks, which is consistent with the experimentally observed rapid formation of the CM phase within LCO bulk.

Degradation mechanism

In addition to bulk degradation, we also observed severe surface degradation of LCO under float charge conditions. This pronounced degradation is primarily attributed to two factors. First, an elevated state of charge (SOC) at the LCO surface promotes the generation of highly oxidative Co4+ and On− species. Second, this environment accelerates the electrolyte decomposition, leading to the formation of corrosive species such as HF and HPO2F2. Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analysis of the electrolytes cycled at 25, 45, and 60 °C demonstrates the temperature-dependent increase in the characteristic peaks of PO2F2−, a byproduct of HF, at chemical shifts of −83.0 and 85.5 ppm (Supplementary Fig. 25). This indicates enhanced formation of HF and HPO2F2 and severe SiO2-based glass corrosion at elevated temperatures. Correspondingly, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) signals of Co on Li negative electrodes from the Li||LCO cells after float charge for 120 h intensify markedly with increased temperature, directly reflecting the accelerated HF-induced Co dissolution from the LCO surface (Supplementary Fig. 26).

At 60 °C, the LCO surface undergoes significant morphological and structural changes. A highly porous surface layer, exceeding 100 nm in thickness, is observed (Supplementary Fig. 27), predominantly as a consequence of the severe Co and O loss. Selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) from this region confirms the coexistence of O3, O1, spinel, and RS phases, alongside a distorted lattice, indicative of numerous vacancies and crystal-plane slippage. Furthermore, cross-sectional STEM further reveals a ~ 5 nm thick amorphous surface film, underlain by a 5–10 nm RS subsurface layer and a mixed O1/CM/spinel bulk phase (Supplementary Fig. 28). Meanwhile, the CEI exhibits a thick and porous morphology (Supplementary Fig. 29), reflecting aggressive interfacial side reactions. Collectively, these integrated observations demonstrate that float charge at 60 °C induces pronounced surface degradation, driven by intensified interfacial reactions, ultimately leading to extensive Co/O depletion and the formation of a porous layer.

Figure 5 comprehensively illustrates the proposed mechanism of interior crack propagation in LCO, highlighting the synergistic interplay between sustained O3 → O1 → CM phase transitions and the collective lattice On− migration towards the interior cracks. During float charge, continuous Li⁺ extraction from the LCO bulk is accompanied by the emergence of uneven O1 domains, which evolve into the CM phase under exacerbated disturbance, such as stresses from Co-O lattice curves. The CM phase transition is also promoted by oxygen oxidation and migration. Oxidized lattice On− species migrate into Li-layer sites, thereby significantly lowering the energy barrier for Co migration and driving the irreversible formation of the CM phase. These transitions generate more pronounced internal stresses due to lattice mismatch, contributing to the generation and propagation of the interior cracks along the O1/CM boundaries. Simultaneously, as elucidated in previous sections, lattice On− migrates towards and accumulates within these interior cracks as molecular O2. The trapped O2 generates additional localized gas pressure at the crack tip, which synergizes with the lattice-mismatch-induced stress, creating a combined driving force that effectively wedges open the crack and accelerates its propagation. Upon propagation to the particle surface, the accumulated O2 is released, and the newly exposed and highly active interfaces undergo aggressive side reactions with the electrolytes (Supplementary Fig. 30). Thus, the synergy between sustained phase transitions and On− migration continuously drives crack propagation from the bulk to the surface, culminating in severe LCO disintegration and electrochemical degradation.

Proposed mechanism of interior crack propagation in LCO under float-charge conditions.

Starting from LCO containing inherent lattice defects, deep Li+ extraction triggers the O3-to-O1 phase transition and induces unevenly distributed O1 domains within the charged particles. Stress disturbances around these distorted O1 regions then promote the Co migration into the Li layer, producing a CM phase that serves as the crack origin (Co and O atoms are depicted as orange and red spheres, respectively). The continuous O1-to-CM phase transition further imposes the lattice-mismatch stress at the layered/CM phase boundaries, leading to the interior crack nucleation. Meanwhile, the oxidized lattice On− migrates along the defect-rich regions toward these nascent cracks and accumulates there as molecular O2. Such trapped O2 exerts additional local pressure at the crack tips that, together with the lattice-mismatch stress, expands the cracks and accelerates their propagation. Once the cracks extend to the particle surface, the accumulated O2 is released, and the newly exposed interfaces undergo intensified side reactions with the electrolyte, ultimately resulting in the severe particle disintegration and electrochemical degradation.

As the desire for increased energy density of LCO positive electrodes becomes increasingly urgent in industry, there is a pressing requirement to develop LCO with high floating durability at 4.6 V and 60 °C. Our above results have comprehensively demonstrated the interior crack propagation mechanism of LCO, including the sustained O1-to-CM phase transition, accumulation of molecular O2 in interior cracks, the propagation of cracks in the bulk region of LCO, and severe surface Co/O loss induced by side reactions. Based on these findings, we propose the following strategies to effectively enhance floating durability. Firstly, suppressing the bulk O1-to-CM phase transition is essential, as it serves as the origin for long-range migration of lattice On− and subsequent propagation of interior cracks. Secondly, it is crucial to develop advanced electrolytes that remain stable at high temperatures (such as 60 °C), thereby reducing electrolyte decomposition and the formation of corrosive acidic species (e.g., HF), which in turn mitigates surface Co/O loss and interfacial side reactions.

From the perspective of bulk durability, structural modifications, especially doping by heteroatoms like Al, have been validated as feasible approaches to enhance the stability of LCO lattice, while their effects in alleviating both the extensive migration of lattice On− and the propagation of the interior cracks remain insufficiently explored. To this end, the modified LCO was synthesized by incorporating 8000 ppm Al into the LCO bulk (Al-reinforced LCO), with the detailed synthesis provided in the Supplementary Information. Specifically, due to the strong covalent Al-O bonds, the bulk lattice On− is more effectively stabilized within the crystal structure, thereby significantly enhancing the bulk stability during float testing. Supplementary Fig. 31 compares the XRD patterns of the Al-reinforced LCO after floating for 120 h. At 25 °C, the H1-3 phase predominates the bulk structure of Al-reinforced LCO, with some O3 phase retained. Even at 60 °C, the bulk structure is still dominated by the H1-3 phase, with only a small amount of O1 phase detected, which contrasts with the bare LCO (Fig. 1b). This indicates that the heavy Al doping can effectively suppress the O1-to-CM phase transition, thereby fundamentally reducing the bulk internal stresses and resulting in the more integrated LCO with fewer cracks (Supplementary Fig. 32). Consequently, compared with commercial LCO, the Al-reinforced LCO exhibits a much lower leakage current without obvious gaseous O2 release upon floating (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34).

Discussion

In this work, we provide a comprehensive elucidation of the intricate mechanism governing interior crack propagation in LCO during float charge at 4.6 V with elevated temperatures. Upon float charge, the substantial Li+ extraction triggers a sequential O3 → O1 → CM phase transition. The critical irreversible O1-to-CM phase transition plays a dual role in LCO degradation: it not only generates significant internal stresses that concentrate near the interior crack tips, but also actively promotes the long-range migration of bulk lattice On−, which in turn leads to the accumulation of molecular O2 within these interior cracks. The synergistic interplay among these factors, including intensified internal stresses from lattice mismatch at the O1/CM interface and accumulated molecular O2 within interior cracks, is the primary driving force for crack propagation. As these cracks propagate to the surface, the trapped molecular O2 is released as gas. Such cascading processes ultimately lead to severe LCO disintegration with pronounced Co and O depletion, further exacerbated by aggressive surface side reactions occurring along the newly exposed crack surfaces. Overall, our study highlights the critical and interconnected roles of the O1-to-CM phase transition and the long-range lattice On− migration in the interior crack propagation of LCO, providing mechanistic insights into the rational design for advanced layered positive electrodes like LCO with enhanced bulk stability and long-term durability.

Methods

Material characterization

The commercial LCO used in this work was purchased from Pulead Technology Industry Co., Ltd. The XRD was performed on a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm), and Rietveld refinements of XRD patterns were carried out using the FULLPROF software. Sample morphology and elemental distribution were examined using SEM (Zeiss SUPRA-55) with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Oxford X-Max 80). The CEI was characterized by cryogenic TEM (cryo-TEM): charged electrode powders were harvested by gently scraping the cycled electrode surface with a micro-spatula inside an Ar-filled glovebox. These powders were then dry-dispersed onto the copper grids via direct contact and gentle manual agitation, utilizing physical adhesion to load the sample. This solvent-free approach was chosen to avoid potential dissolution or structural alteration of the CEI caused by organic solvents. The copper grid was then mounted on a Fischione 2550 cryo-transfer holder, and transferred into a field-emission TEM (JEOL JEM-3200FS). The TEM operated at 300 kV, with an emission current of 112 μA. The sample was not exposed to the ambient atmosphere throughout the entire transfer process. High-resolution STEM images and EELS analysis were performed by the double aberration-corrected FEI Titan Themis G2 equipped with a Gatan GIF Quantum ERS spectrometer. The STEM operated at 300 kV, with a beam current of 40 pA. The intensities of the EELS spectra were normalized to ensure consistency across measurements. Specifically, the O K-edge spectra were normalized relative to the maximum intensity of the main peak, while the Co L-edge spectra were normalized to the maximum intensity of the L3 peak. Lattice strain fields were analyzed using GPA based on high-resolution STEM images. TEM samples were prepared using the focused ion beam microscope (FIB, Thermo Fisher SCIOS). In situ DEMS measurements employed an EL-CELL EC-DEMS cell, utilizing a perforated coin cell case for the positive electrode to facilitate gas transport. A glass fiber separator was used to ensure high electrolyte wettability and gas permeability. The cell was connected to an online mass spectrometer (Hiden Analytical, HPR-20 EGA). High-purity Ar was used as the carrier gas with a precise flow rate of 0.75 standard cubic centimeters per minute (sccm). After assembly, the cell was allowed to rest for 30 min to reach equilibrium before testing. The electrochemical protocol consisted of a galvanostatic charge/discharge at 100 mA g−1, corresponding to 0.5 C based on 1 C = 200 mA g−1, followed by a float charge (constant voltage) step. To maintain a stable testing environment, the cell mold was heated using a custom-made heating jacket. XPS was carried out on a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi using a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. The binding energy scale was calibrated by the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. XANES and EXAFS spectra were collected on an easyXAFS300+ spectrometer. The Co K-edge XANES spectra were processed using standard procedures. The background was removed by subtracting a linear pre-edge function, and the spectra were subsequently normalized to the average absorption intensity of the post-edge region (edge-jump normalization). TXM tomography data were acquired at the 3D nano imaging beamline (BL18B) of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). To ensure reproducibility, a specialized micro-loading procedure was employed. Individual electrode particles were harvested and delivered onto the tip of a micron-tapered glass capillary using an industrial dispenser and a micro-manipulator within an Ar-filled glovebox. To maintain the sample's pristine state and prevent air exposure, the glass capillary was subsequently encapsulated within a secondary glass tube and sealed. For the TXM-XANES analysis, a pixel-wise normalization procedure was automatically applied to the XANES spectrum of each individual pixel. The normalization was performed by conducting linear regressions on both the pre-edge and post-edge regions. 19F NMR spectra were recorded on a Quantum-Plus 400 MHz spectrometer at 298 K. EPR was performed on a Bruker EMX plus 10/12 instrument equipped with a dual-mode cavity and cooled by an Oxford ESR910 liquid-helium cryostat, and the spectra were recorded in the perpendicular mode at 60 K, with the microwave frequency, power, and modulation amplitude set to 9.645 GHz, 2 mW, and 2 G, respectively38. The 4D STEM experiments were conducted on a JEOL JEM-ARM300F2 transmission electron microscope operated at 300 kV, with a beam current of 31 pA and a probe-forming aperture with a 1.3 mrad convergence semi-angle. A Medipix3RX MerlinEM direct electron pixelated detector was employed to capture the 4D STEM dataset. Strain mapping was performed using Python code with reference to the py4DSTEM project34. O K-edge RIXS measurements were carried out on the U41-PEAXIS beamline of the BESSY II at Helmholtz-Zentrum Berlin (HZB). To avoid air exposure, the samples were sealed in a vacuum suitcase for transfer from an Ar-filled glovebox directly into the measurement chamber. With the scattering geometry fixed at a 60° specular configuration, the energy resolution was calibrated to 90 meV against a carbon-tape reference. Each spectrum was then recorded at an incident photon energy of 531.0 eV over an acquisition window of 30 min35.

Electrode preparation and electrochemical methods

The positive electrodes were fabricated by mixing LCO (90 wt%), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, 5 wt%) (Aladdin, K30), and acetylene black (5 wt%) (C45 conductive carbon black, TIMCAL) in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) (Aladdin, 99.5%) solvent, followed by casting the slurry onto a 40-μm-thick aluminum foil via coating machine from MTI, Shenzhen Kejing Company (single-side coated) and dried at 120 °C in vacuum for 12 h. The dried electrode sheet was cut into circular pieces of diameter 12 mm via a cutting machine from MTI, Shenzhen Kejing Company. The mass loadings of active materials in the positive electrode films were ~6 mg cm−2. The electrochemical performance was assessed by coin-type (CR2032, i.e., 20 mm in diameter, 3.2 mm in thickness, with the battery casing and the spring made of stainless steel) half cells using Li as the negative electrode (China Energy Lithium Co., Ltd., with 16 mm in diameter, 0.6 mm in thickness, and 99.9% in purity, which was stored in the Ar-filled glovebox for no more than 3 months), Celgard 2400 polypropylene (PP, 25 μm in thickness, 19 mm in dimension, 41% in porosity, 100 ~ 200 nm in pore size) membrane as the separator, 1.0 M LiPF6 in ethylene carbonate (EC) / ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) / fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) with a volume ratio of 1:1:1 as the electrolyte (Guangzhou Tinci Materials Technology Co., Ltd.). The volume of the electrolyte for each coin-cell was 60 μL. The float charge measurements were carried out using a NEWARE battery test system at 25, 45, 50, 55, and 60 °C in NEWARE incubator (all batteries were left to rest at 25 °C for 8 hours before cycling). In the float charge procedure, the Li||LCO cells were firstly charged to 4.6 V upon a constant current (0.5 C, 1 C = 200 mA g−1), and then the cells were continuously held at a constant 4.6 V voltage for 120 h. After the float charge, the coin cells were disassembled inside an Ar-filled glovebox (H2O < 0.01 ppm, O2 < 0.01 ppm) using a battery disassembling tool (MTI, Shenzhen Kejing Company). The harvested positive electrodes were rinsed twice with anhydrous EMC via manual agitation for 30 seconds each time to remove residual electrolyte salts and then left to dry naturally in the glovebox environment. For SEM, FIB, and XRD measurements, the dried electrodes were mounted onto the holders within the glovebox and experienced only brief exposure to air during the loading process into the equipment chambers. In contrast, for TEM, STEM, XPS, EPR, TXM, CT, and NMR, the samples were loaded into specialized air-tight transfer vessels or sealed tubes inside the glovebox. These containers were only opened within the vacuum chambers or inert environments of the respective instruments to strictly prevent direct contact with atmospheric moisture and oxygen.

Theoretical calculation methods

First-principles calculations were performed within the density functional theory (DFT) framework using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 5.4.4). Core–valence interactions were treated using the projector-augmented-wave (PAW) method, while exchange and correlation interactions were described using the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA). A kinetic energy cutoff of 500 eV was adopted. Electronic self-consistent convergence criterion was set to 10−6 eV, and structural relaxations were carried out until the residual forces on all ions fell below 10−4 eV Å−1. The Brillouin zone was sampled using Γ-centered Monkhorst–Pack grids with a minimum k-point density of 0.2 Å−1. To accurately account for the strong on-site Coulomb repulsion of the localized Co 3 d electrons, a Hubbard correction was applied with an effective Ueff of 5.0 eV for Co36. Long-range dispersion was incorporated through the semi-empirical DFT-D3 scheme during relaxation. Ionic migration barriers were calculated via the climbing-image nudged elastic band (CI-NEB) method. Detailed calculation parameters and optimized structures are provided in Supplementary Data 1–3. The connectivity of the redox-active lattice On− was assessed using a Monte Carlo (MC) sampling-based percolation analysis33. Given the kinetically prohibitive interlayer transport of On− between adjacent Co–O slabs, the analysis was confined to a single slab mapped onto a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice, where each hexagon corresponds to one site. Lattice dimensions of 100 × 100, 200 × 200, and 300 × 300 were tested to ensure finite-size convergence. An oxygen site was defined as redox-active if fewer than three of its nearest-neighbor cation sites were occupied by Co. Two redox-active oxygen sites were considered connected only if they shared a common Co neighbor, allowing the nearest-neighbor segments bridging vacancy-adjacent oxygen to form a continuous pathway. A configuration was defined as percolating when a contiguous network of these redox-active sites spanned the periodic boundaries of the simulation cell. Each MC realization was initialized with a fully Co-occupied slab, followed by the stochastic introduction of Co vacancies until a target concentration (ranging from 0.1 to 0.9) was reached, which mimics the cross-layer Co migration during the phase transition. For each configuration, the percolation probability p was calculated as the fraction of redox-active oxygen atoms belonging to a successfully percolating network relative to the total number of redox-active oxygen atoms. To guarantee statistical convergence, p was averaged over 10,000 independent random Co vacancy distributions for each degree of cross-layer Co migration.
