Grief, depression and psychotic-like experiences among bereaved college students: the predictive role of attitudes towards death
Authors: Po Hu, Wenli Qian, Chang Xi, Nannan Liu, Jun Wen, Xin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Bereavement may lead to heterogeneous psychological responses, including prolonged grief disorder (PGD), depression, and psychotic-like experiences (PLEs). Individuals’ attitudes toward death may play a significant role in bereavement adjustment.Objective: This study aimed to identify latent subgroups of bereaved Chinese college students based on their symptom endorsement of PGD, depression, and PLEs, and to examine the predictive role of five specific death attitudes – fear of death, death avoidance, neutral acceptance, approach acceptance, and escape acceptance – on class membership over time.Method: At baseline, 366 bereaved college students completed measures of PGD, depression, PLEs, and death attitudes. Of these, 159 (43.4%) completed the six-month follow-up survey. Latent class analysis was conducted to identify subgroups, and multinomial logistic regression was used to explore predictors of class membership at follow-up.Results: A three-class solution was identified as optimal among the retained participants: a Resilient class, a Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class and a High Symptom Class. Compared to those in the High Symptom Class, students in the Resilient Class were more likely to report better physical health and to have experienced a non-violent loss. Furthermore, higher levels of fear of death and escape acceptance at baseline significantly predicted membership in High Symptom Class six months later.Conclusions: Bereaved college students exhibited distinct symptom profiles, with a small subgroup experiencing severe comorbid symptoms of grief, depression, and PLEs. Death attitudes were important predictors of class membership. Although the high attrition rate and differences between retained participants and dropouts warrant caution in generalising the findings, the results underscore the importance of early identification and tailored interventions for bereaved college students. Interventions targeting maladaptive death attitudes, particularly fear of death and escape acceptance, may represent an effective strategy to promote adaptive bereavement adjustment.

Introduction

Death is a natural part of life, making bereavement an almost universal and largely unavoidable experience. Although there is no universal consensus on whether young adulthood spans from ages 18–25 or extends to 30, it is widely recognised as a distinct developmental period characterised by multifaceted life transitions (Arnett, 2013; Guassi Moreira et al., 2018). This stage is marked by identity formation, self-exploration, and increasing autonomy, alongside continued emotional reliance on parents, reflecting a complex nature of this developmental stage (Arnett et al., 2014). When such developmental tasks intersect with the experience of bereavement, the impact can be both unique and profound (Cupit et al., 2016). Undergraduate and graduate students typically fall within this age range and represent a salient subgroup of young adults navigating these developmental challenges. Previous studies indicated that about 40% of college students experience a loss within 24 months (Balk et al., 2010; Cousins et al., 2017; Cupit et al., 2022). In China, most college students, especially those enrolled in universities outside their home province, are geographically separated from their families. This separation may limit access to immediate familial support during the grieving process (Liew & Servaty-Seib, 2018). In addition, psychosocial stressors such as uncertainty about career paths and future prospects may further compound bereaved students’ difficulties in coping with loss (Plocha et al., 2023).

Previous studies found that bereavement could negatively impact college students in various aspects, such as academic performance, social engagement, and peer relationships (Cupit et al., 2022; Spiccia et al., 2023). Furthermore, bereavement has been associated with an increased risk of health problems, with prolonged grief disorder (PGD) being one of the most prominent outcomes (Buur et al., 2023; Stroebe et al., 2017). Although grief is a common and natural response to the loss, individuals who experience grief that is intense, persistent, and significantly exceeds cultural expectations may develop PGD (Killikelly et al., 2025; Simon & Shear, 2024). Over the past few decades, considerable research has established that PGD is a distinct bereavement-related distress (Lenferink et al., 2021; Malgaroli et al., 2018; Shear, 2015). It has been officially included in the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11; World Health Organization, 2018) and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, fifth edition, text revision (DSM-5-TR; American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Both diagnostic systems identify its core symptoms as persistent yearning or longing for the deceased, accompanied by intense emotional pain. Meta-analysis studies have suggested that approximately 10% of bereaved adults develop PGD following a non-violent loss (Lundorff et al., 2017), while the rate may reach 50% among those who have experienced a violent loss (Djelantik et al., 2020). In China, the prevalence of PGD was found to be 8.9% based on a meta-analysis of 16 studies (Yuan et al., 2024). In a recent empirical study of bereaved university students, 14.2% met the DSM-5-TR criteria for PGD according to self-reported responses to the Prolonged Grief Disorder-13-Revised (PG-13-R) scale (Jiang et al., 2025). However, emerging evidence suggests that these prevalence estimates may be overestimated due to methodological limitations, such the use of non-probability sampling methods (Comtesse et al., 2024), or reliance on self-report scales rather than clinical interviews (Eisma, 2025). These findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation of PGD prevalence rates and underscore the importance of employing diverse methodological approaches in bereavement research.

PGD symptoms frequently co-occur with other psychological disturbance, and depression is one of the most common comorbidities (Boelen et al., 2016; Eisma & Buyukcan-Tetik, 2024; Malgaroli et al., 2018; Wen et al., 2022). A recent meta-analysis indicated that over two-thirds of bereaved adults with PGD symptoms also experienced at least one additional mental health disorder. Specifically, based on the 23 included studies, the co-occurrence of PGD symptoms with depression, anxiety and posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) was estimated at 63%, 54%, and 49%, respectively (Komischke-Konnerup et al., 2021). In a cross-sectional study conducted among Saudi Arabian college students, those with the highest PGD scores tended to report the highest levels of depression (Al-Gamal et al., 2019). These findings indicated that depression is also a major concern within the bereaved population. While less frequently discussed are psychotic-like experiences (PLEs) following bereavement. PLEs typically include hallucinations and delusional thoughts that are clinically relevant but fall below the diagnostic threshold for a psychotic disorder (Linscott & van Os, 2012). For instance, hallucinations about the deceased or transient sensations of their presence are common across cultures (American Psychiatric Association, 2022). Such hallucinations during the acute grief period are often considered as benign and the diagnosis of psychotic disorder among bereaved individuals typically requires additional presence of delusions (Shear, 2015). However, large-scale population studies revealed that children and adolescents who have experienced bereavement exhibited a heightened risk of developing psychotic disorders compared to those who had not encountered such a loss (Burrell et al., 2022; Liang et al., 2016). Furthermore, a systematic review provided evidence that the loss of a family member may generate psychotic symptoms, and conversely, the onset of psychosis may trigger grief responses (Del Pozo-Herce et al., 2024). One reason to investigate children and young people with psychotic-like symptoms is because these symptoms are associated with an elevated risk of subsequent psychotic disorders and poorer functioning (Dominguez et al., 2011; Staines et al., 2022). Therefore, PLEs may represent an important but often overlooked psychological concern among bereaved college students. More fine-grained investigation of the co-occurrence of PGD symptoms, depression and PLEs in the bereaved people are needed.

Both the Cognitive Attachment Model and the Cognitive-Behavioral Conceptualisation of prolonged grief highlighted the significant role of cognitive factors in influencing bereavement outcomes (Boelen et al., 2006; Maccallum & Bryant, 2013). Numerous studies have demonstrated that grief-related cognitions were closely associated with symptoms of grief, depression, and post-traumatic stress, as well as functional impairment (Smith & Ehlers, 2021; Xu et al., 2023). However, bereaved individuals’ death attitudes may also influence their adaptation, which lacks sufficient empirical research. Death attitudes refer to individuals’ perceptions and emotions regarding their own mortality or the death of others, including fear of death (i.e. feelings of fear or anxiety towards death), death avoidance (i.e. a tendency to avoid thinking or discussing death-related topics), escape acceptance (view of death as escape from a painful existence), approach acceptance (i.e. death is a gateway to a happy afterlife) and neutral acceptance (i.e. death is considered as an integral part of life) (Wong et al., 1994). These varying attitudes toward death have been linked to different mental health outcomes (Neimeyer et al., 2004; Oker et al., 2022). For example, a neutral acceptance attitude may help bereaved individuals maintain a sense of meaning and purpose in life (Boyraz et al., 2015), which is crucial for adaptive bereavement adjustment (Neimeyer, 2019). Consequently, widowed individuals who perceived death as a natural part of life were more likely to experience integrated grief (Bonanno et al., 2002). In this state, the reality of the loss is accepted, emotional pain becomes less intense, and the bereaved can reengage with life while sustaining an ongoing bond with the deceased (Iglewicz et al., 2020). In contrast, studies among bereaved adults and widowed individuals whose partners died from AIDS found that fear of death – but not neutral acceptance – was the only attitude dimension significantly associated with grief symptom severity (Gegieckaite & Kazlauskas, 2022; Yu et al., 2016). Similarly, research on bereaved mothers indicated that fear of death was significantly related to maternal grief (Barr & Cacciatore, 2008). In the context of Chinese college students, those who regarded death as a natural process reported the highest levels of subjective well-being, whereas those with ambivalent attitudes toward death were at greater risk for mental health problems (Ma et al., 2024). While excessive reliance on avoidance strategies can complicate acute grief and increase the risk of PGD (Shear, 2010), the specific contribution of death avoidance attitude in bereavement adjustment remains understudied. Furthermore, although escape acceptance has been identified as potentially the most maladaptive death attitude during COVID-19 (Oker et al., 2022), its role in post-loss adaptation is yet to be clarified.

Taken together, the existing literature suggests that different death attitudes may influence whether bereavement progresses toward integrated grief or remains in a disturbed state. It is worth noting that Chinese collectivist culture differs substantially from Western individualist culture, placing strong emphasis on filial piety toward parents and ancestors, discouraging the open expression of intense emotions after bereavement, and treating death-related topics as taboo (X. Xu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). Moreover, college students are at a different developmental stage from adults and face unique developmental tasks. For many, losing a loved one may be their first experience of bereavement, and both their death attitudes and the influence of these attitudes may differ from those of middle-aged and older adults. Therefore, further empirical research is needed to clarify how these attitudes shape post-loss distress among Chinese bereaved students.

Individuals’ responses to bereavement may vary considerably. Latent class analysis (LCA) is widely used to identify distinct patterns of symptoms based on binary indicators. Research adopting this approach has revealed heterogeneous clinical trajectories following bereavement, mostly involving symptoms of grief, depression, and PTSD (Heeke et al., 2023). For example, among individuals confronted with unnatural loss, distinct classes such as a resilient class, a predominantly PGD class, and a combined PGD/Depression class have been identified (Boelen et al., 2016). Similarly, in a sample of recently bereaved individuals, LCA revealed a low symptom class, a predominantly PGD class, and a high symptom class characterised by elevated PGD, PTSD, and depression symptoms (Boelen & Lenferink, 2020). However, the comorbid symptom patterns involving grief, depression, and PLEs remain underexplored. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify subgroups of bereaved students based on their endorsement of PGD, depression symptoms and PLEs. Additionally, we adopted a longitudinal design to explore the impacts of five types of death attitudes on these latent classes. We hypothesised that students with more death-related fear, death avoidance and escape acceptance would be more likely to belong to the high-symptom class.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The present study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Soochow University (No. SUDA20231213H03). Participants were recruited via online platforms (e.g. WeChat, Weibo, QQ) and accessed the questionnaires through a link provided in the advertisement. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals were currently enrolled in higher education (i.e. undergraduate or graduate programmes) and aged 18 years or older; and (2) experiencing the loss of a loved one within the past three years. Data were collected via the Qualtrics platform, and electronical informed consent was obtained before starting the survey. Participants completed questionnaires at baseline (T1) and again six months later (T2). Compensation of 30 yuan was provided at T1, with an additional 40 yuan provided at T2.

A total of 402 students participated in the survey at T1. Data from 30 participants were excluded due to potential invalidity, such as excessively short response times or patterned answering. Additionally, 6 participants were excluded because they lost their loved one more than three years ago. Among them (N = 366), 159 participants completed the follow-up questionnaires six months later. Therefore, the final sample comprised 159 participants, including 72 males (45.3%) and 87 females (54.7%), with a mean age of 21.70 years (SD = 1.99). The mean score of participants’ self-rated physical health was 3.95 on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = very good). The average time since loss for retained participants was 11.98 months (SD = 8.01), which was significantly longer than that of the dropped group (M = 9.98, p = .02). Most participants experienced a non-violent death (n = 141, 88.7%), and about two-thirds (n = 101, 65.4%) reported that the death was unexpected. Participants reported a high level of emotional closeness with the deceased (M = 6.28, SD = 0.96), as measured on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = not at all close, 7 = very close). Additionally, only about one third of them (n = 52, 32.7%) had the opportunity to say goodbye. Regarding the relationship with the deceased, most participants had lost a grandparent (n = 102, 64.2%), followed by other relatives (n = 35, 22.0%), parents (n = 15, 9.4%), and friends (n = 7, 4.4%).

Measurements

Demographic information questionnaire

A self-developed questionnaire was used to collect demographic information (e.g. gender, age, physical health status) and details related to the death (e.g. relationship to the deceased, perceived closeness to the deceased, the cause of death, time since loss, the opportunity to say goodbye). Participants rated their overall physical health on a single item using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very poor, 5 = very good), with higher scores indicating better health. Participants also rated their perceived emotional closeness with the deceased using a single-item measure on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (‘not at all close’) to 7 (‘extremely close’), with higher scores reflecting greater perceived emotional closeness. Both two items were analysed as continuous variables in subsequent analyses.

Prolonged grief scale-revised (PG-13-R)

The current study used an adapted version of PG-13 scale, the PG-13-Revised, to assess participants’ grief symptoms (Prigerson et al., 2021). Ten items correspond to the DSM-5-TR criteria for PGD (e.g. yearning, preoccupation, identity disruption, avoidance). Participants rated each item on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = not at all, 5 = overwhelmingly), and a total score of 30 or higher on the PG-13-R is considered indicative of meeting the DSM-5-TR criteria for PGD. Following previous LCA studies (Boelen & Lenferink, 2020; Xu et al., 2025), responses of 4 or 5 were considered indicative of symptom presence and were recoded as ‘1’, while 1–3 as absence and were recoded as ‘0’. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the PG-13-R in this study were 0.91 at T1 and 0.89 at T2, respectively.

The patient health questionnaire-9 depression module (PHQ-9)

The PHQ-9 is a 9 items self-report measure that reflect core depression symptoms (Kroenke et al., 2001). Participants are asked to rate the frequency of these symptoms over the past two weeks on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = not at all to 3 = nearly every day). A total scores of 5, 10, 15, and 20 represented mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression, respectively. To create binary indicators for the LCA, scores of ‘2 (more than half of the days)’ or ‘3 (nearly every day)’ were recoded as ‘1 (symptom present)’, while responses of ‘0 (not at all)’ or ‘1(several days)’ were recoded as ‘0 (symptom absent)’ (Boelen & Lenferink, 2020). The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 is a sound tool for screening depression among college students (Zhang et al., 2013). Its Cronbach's α coefficients were 0.90 at both assessment time points in the current study.

Community assessment of psychic experiences-positive subscale (CAPE-P8)

The Community Assessment of Psychic Experiences is a reliable instrument for evaluating a range of PLEs in the general population (Konings et al., 2006). The current study used the Chinese version of CAPE-P8, which has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties (D. Wang et al., 2020). The scale comprises 8 items, which are categorised into two dimensions: delusional experiences and hallucinatory experiences. Each item is rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 4 (nearly always). In accordance with previous research identifying a mean score of 1.47 as a screening threshold for ultra-high risk of psychosis in adolescents (Bukenaite et al., 2017), responses of ‘2 (sometimes)’, ‘3 (often)’, or ‘4 (nearly always)’ were recoded as indicative of symptom presence. The Cronbach's α coefficients of the CAPE-P8 in the current study were 0.90 at T1 and 0.89 at T2, respectively.

Death attitude profile-revised (DAP-R)

The DAP-R is a 32-item scale that assesses five dimensions of death attitudes: fear of death, death avoidance, neutral acceptance, approach acceptance and escape acceptance (Wong et al., 1994). Each statement was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Subscale scores are computed by summing the relevant items, with higher scores indicating greater endorsement of each respective attitude toward death. The current study used the Chinese version of the DAP-R, which has been proven to have sound psychometric properties among college students (Q. Wang et al., 2020). The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the five subscales were 0.90, 0.88, 0.62, 0.87 and 0.87 at Time 1.

Data analysis

There were no missing data of questionnaire items at both time points. Consistent with the LCA framework, binary indicators of PGD, depression, and PLEs (as described above) were used as class indicators, while scores for each death attitude dimension were treated as continuous covariates in the multinomial logistic regression analyses.

LCA was conducted by initially fitting a one-class model to the data, followed by models increasing by one class each time. The optimal number of latent classes was determined based on multiple model fit indices, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-size adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (SA-BIC), the Lo-Mendell-Rubin likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT). A model with lower AIC, BIC and SA-BIC values, higher Entropy values (>0.80), along with significant p values of LRT and BLRT, was considered to provide a better fit (Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). In selecting the optimal model, parsimony, interpretability and clinical meaningfulness were also key considerations, alongside the model fit indices. Generally, a conditional probability greater than 0.60 for an indicator is considered to represent a high probability of symptom endorsement by members of that class, value between 0.15 and 0.59 indicates a moderate probability, and below 0.15 suggests a low probability (Maccallum et al., 2024).

After determining the optimal class solution, a three-step approach was applied to examine predictors of class membership at six-month follow-up, while accounting for measurement error in the latent class assignment (Asparouhov & Muthén, 2014). Specifically, demographic variables, loss-related characteristics, and five dimensions of death attitudes assessed at T1 were entered as covariates to predict participants’ membership in the latent classes identified at T2. An odds ratio (OR) greater than 1 indicates that the predictor is associated with a higher likelihood of belonging to a given class compared to the reference class, while an OR less than 1 suggests a lower likelihood. Statistical significance was evaluated at the p < .05 level.

At T1, the three-class model showed a relatively lower AIC and the lowest BIC among all tested models. Although the five-class model had the lowest AIC and SA-BIC, the LRT was not significant for either the four- or five-class model. In contrast, both the LRT and BLRT were both significant for the three-class model, supporting its selection. Additionally, the three-class model had high entropy, indicating good classification accuracy. At T2, the three-class model again showed a favourable balance of fit and parsimony. It had a low AIC and the lowest BIC, along with significant LRT and BLRT results. Taken together, the three-class model was selected as the optimal solution at both T1 and T2, based on statistical fit indices, parsimony, classification quality, and interpretability.

According to the conditional probabilities of class members endorsing symptoms of grief, depression, and PLEs (see Table 3), class labels were assigned. Class 1, referred to as the Resilient Class, included 61 participants (38.4%) at T1 and 83 participants (52.2%) at T2. This class was characterised by low probabilities across nearly all symptom indicators. Class 2, Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class, comprised 70 participants (44.0%) at T1 and 52 participants (32.7%) at T2. This class was marked by moderate levels of grief and depressive symptoms, alongside consistently high probabilities of delusional symptoms. Class 3, the smallest subgroup at both time points, comprised 28 participants (17.6%) at T1 and 24 participants (15.1%) at T2. This High Symptom Class was distinguished by high endorsement probabilities across all symptom domains. Compared to individuals in the High Symptom Class, those in the Resilient Class were more likely to report better physical health status at T1 (B = 1.43, p < .001, OR = 4.19). Additionally, experiencing a non-violent death (versus a violent death) was also associated with increased odds of membership in the Resilient Class (B = 1.77, p = .01, OR = 5.86). Regarding death attitudes, lower levels of fear of death (B = −0.32, p < .001, OR = 0.73) and escape acceptance (B = −0.19, p = .02, OR = 0.83) significantly predicted membership in the Resilient Class six months later.

Individuals in the Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class did not differ significantly from those in the High Symptom Class in terms of demographic or loss-related characteristics. However, fear of death and escape acceptance remained significant predictors. Bereaved college students with higher levels of death fear (B = −0.19, p = .001, OR = 0.83) and escape acceptance (B = −0.15, p = .046, OR = 0.86) were more likely to belong to the High Symptom Class six months later.

Discussion

The present study used a person-centered, longitudinal approach to explore heterogeneity in bereavement outcomes among Chinese university students. Three distinct subgroups were identified at two time points based on symptoms of grief, depression, and psychotic-like experiences. Additionally, the current study also revealed that fear of death and escape acceptance were significant predictors of class membership six months later.

In line with previous LCA studies among bereaved adults, of which the majority (76%) identified three latent classes (Heeke et al., 2023), our study also revealed a three-class model in bereaved college students: a Resilient Class, a Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class, and a High Symptom Class. Notably, the Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class was the largest group at Time 1, whereas the Resilient Class became the most prevalent at Time 2. The results provide additional evidence that most bereaved people would recover from their grief gradually (Smith & Ehlers, 2020; Wen et al., 2022). The identification of both a low symptom class and a high symptom class is also consistent with prior research on bereaved adults (Boelen & Lenferink, 2020; Kokou-Kpolou et al., 2021).

The Resilient Class exhibited consistently low probabilities across nearly all symptom indicators, reflecting minimal psychological distress. This finding aligns with the notion that most bereaved individuals are able to come to terms with the reality of loss and gradually reintegrate into daily life, leading to a natural decline in symptom severity over time (Simon & Shear, 2024). In contrast, students in the High Symptom Class endorsed high levels of PGD, depression symptoms, and PLEs, indicating a complex and multifaceted pattern of psychological distress. Consistent with previous findings that 13.4% to 14.2% of university students were at risk of PGD (Glickman, 2020; Jiang et al., 2025), the proportion of individuals in the High Symptom Class was similarly high, accounting for 17.6% at Time 1 and 15.1% at Time 2. Several developmental challenges faced by emerging adults – such as establishing independence, setting personal and academic goals, and forming intimate relationships – may interfere with the process of bereavement adaptation. Additionally, cultural expectations, particularly those rooted in filial piety, may exacerbate emotional distress and restrict the expression of positive emotions (Zhou et al., 2023). Although the High Symptom Class constituted the smallest subgroup, it warrants particular clinical attention due to the severity and complexity of the psychological symptoms observed.

One of the most striking findings from the LCA was the emergence of a distinct subgroup of bereaved Chinese college students who exhibited moderate levels of grief and depression symptoms alongside elevated PLEs, particularly delusional beliefs. This pattern may be partially attributable to differences in the symptom coding thresholds across measures. Specifically, for PLEs, responses of ‘2 (sometimes)’, ‘3 (often)’, or ‘4 (nearly always)’ were recoded as indicative of symptom presence. However, for grief and depression symptoms, only responses of ‘3 (often)’ and ‘4 (nearly always)’ were considered symptomatic. This discrepancy in recoding criteria likely contributed to the generally higher probabilities of PLEs across all latent classes, including this one. Additionally, cultural and developmental characteristics may also help explain the prominent delusions and hallucinations symptoms in this subgroup. In traditional Chinese culture, maintaining continuing bonds with the deceased – such as through funerary rituals, spiritual offerings, and commemorative practices – is both normative and valued (Lalande & Bonanno, 2006). Continuing bonds include engaging with memories, illusions, as well as hallucinations that evoke an inner relationship with the deceased (Hewson et al., 2024). Chinese funerary and grieving rituals also established a system that allowed for a continuing reciprocity and connection between the living and the dead (Lalande & Bonanno, 2006). However, Chinese college students frequently reside in urbanised academic settings that are removed from family-based mourning rituals. In such contexts, the continuing bonds may manifest in forms of hallucinations – such as sensing the presence of the deceased or hearing their voice. Prior research has shown that grief-related hallucinations are particularly common in Chinese samples (Li & Prigerson, 2016). The current findings indicate the importance of culturally sensitive clinical interpretation of bereavement responses. At the same time, such experiences may reflect genuine emotional distress that is under-recognised in this population. Previous studies have shown that more than one in five Chinese adolescents reported frequent PLEs (Sun et al., 2017; Xi et al., 2025), and 40.3% of Chinese college students reported at least one PLE in the past month, with depression being a significant correlate of frequent PLEs (B. Xu et al., 2024). Furthermore, exposure to traumatic events has been associated with a higher likelihood of delusional experiences (Scott et al., 2007). Therefore, it is reasonable to expect that students who have lost a close loved one may face an elevated risk for PLEs, particularly when depression symptoms are also present. The emergence of the Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class underscores the need for clinical attention not only to PGD and depression, but also to PLEs among bereaved college students.

Individuals with poorer physical health and those who experienced a violent death of a loved one were more likely to be classified into the High Symptom Class rather than the Resilient Class. This result is consistent with a recent study reporting that the risk of PGD among bereaved Chinese college students was associated with physical health status and the causes of death (Jiang et al., 2025). Although meta-analyses predominantly involving middle-aged and older adults have indicated that females are generally more vulnerable to PGD (Buur et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024), gender was not a significant predictor of class membership in the present study. This result is consistent with prior research conducted among bereaved college students (Glickman, 2020; Jiang et al., 2025), and provides partial support for the gender similarities hypothesis, which posits that males and females are largely similar across many psychological dimensions (Stelzer et al., 2019). One possible interpretation is that gender differences in PGD may vary across developmental stages, and may be less pronounced during emerging adulthood (Jiang et al., 2025). Another possible explanation is that male and female students may employ different coping strategies. Female students, despite exhibiting greater emotional sensitivity to loss (Little et al., 2009), may be more inclined to seek emotional support, which is generally associated with better psychological adjustment. In contrast, male students may be more likely to engage in maladaptive behaviours such as excessive internet use (Lin et al., 2021) or smoking (Li et al., 2022), which might offer temporary relief but could hinder long-term grief resolution. Therefore, the absence of gender differences in symptom profiles in the currents study may reflect a short-term equilibrium. Future longitudinal research is needed to examine whether these coping patterns differentially affect bereavement trajectories across genders over time.

Participants with less death-related fear were more likely to be Resilient Class and Moderate Symptoms with Elevated Delusions Class six months later, compared to the High Symptom Class. Terror Management Theory posits that humans possess an inherent fear of death (Mikulincer et al., 2003), and death of a loved one can exacerbate death anxiety (Honey & Dark-Freudeman, 2024). Some adolescents may become increasingly preoccupied with the potential loss of others, particularly following unexpected bereavement experiences (Andriessen et al., 2021). Moreover, even today, death is often regarded as misfortune or inauspiciousness among some Chinese people, and open discussions about death within families remain uncommon (Shen & Yan, 2015). Such cultural norms may limit bereaved college students’ opportunities to process and understand death adaptively, thereby heightening death anxiety and hindering bereavement adjustment. Prior research has identified fear of death as a transdiagnostic construct, which is associated with various forms of psychological distress, including depression, anxiety, and stress (Menzies et al., 2019; Neimeyer et al., 2004). The current findings extend the literature by suggesting that heightened death fear may impede adaptive bereavement adjustment in college students, increasing their risk for grief, depression, and PLEs.

Additionally, participants who exhibited a stronger desire to escape life’s suffering through death were more likely to be classified into the High Symptom Class. Escape acceptance shares conceptual overlap with suicidality, as both involve perceiving death as a means to end unbearable distress. Escape acceptance refers to a cognitive attitude in which death is perceived as a preferable alternative to an intolerable life situation (Wong et al., 1994). Suicidality encompasses a broader continuum of thoughts and behaviours, ranging from passive death wishes to active suicide planning or attempts (Turecki & Brent, 2016). While escape acceptance does not necessarily entail active suicidal intent, elevated levels may signal overwhelming emotional distress and limited psychological resources for coping with adversity (Ma et al., 2024; Wong et al., 1994). In this regard, the endorsement of death as a means of escape may serve as a cognitive precursor to suicidality, especially when accompanied by hopelessness and intense psychological pain (Abramson et al., 2002). Accordingly, high levels of escape acceptance should be regarded as a potential clinical warning sign. Moreover, it is also important to consider that suicidality in bereaved individuals may not solely arise from the desire to escape suffering, but may also be driven by a longing to reunite with the deceased (Shear et al., 2011). Although reunion-oriented suicidality differs from escape-based suicidality in its underlying intent, both forms carry substantial clinical risk and warrant careful assessment in bereavement contexts. Taken together, our findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that escape acceptance might be the most maladaptive form of death attitude, as it was related to poorer mental health (Oker et al., 2022).

Contrary to our hypothesis, death avoidance did not significantly predict membership in the High Symptom Class. This diverges from previous research suggesting that avoidance of death-related thoughts and emotions is linked to maladaptive bereavement adjustment in university populations (Hay et al., 2022). A study of undergraduate students found that avoidance attitudes may not always be a maladaptive coping strategy. Among individuals experiencing moderate grief, such attitudes can function as a resilience mechanism that is mobilised during bereavement to minimise its unpleasant effects (Gibbons et al., 2018). Another plausible explanation may lie in the cultural context. In Chinese society, death remains a highly sensitive and often taboo subject. Moreover, Chinese culture places a strong emphasis on collectivism. Individuals tend to prioritise their familial and group responsibilities over personal needs (Xu et al., 2024). For bereaved Chinese adolescents and young adults, avoiding death-related topics and suppressing intense emotional expressions may not be regarded as maladaptive, but rather as culturally sanctioned protective behaviours – either to shield family members from distress (Li et al., 2024) or to maintain interpersonal harmony (Zhou et al., 2023). Within this sociocultural framework, a death-avoidant attitude may function less as a risk factor for psychological maladjustment and more as a socially appropriate coping mechanism.

Implications

Revealing three distinct latent subgroups among bereaved Chinese college students and identifying significant influencing factors can contribute to recognising at-risk individuals and informing targeted psychological interventions. These findings underscore the need for culturally grounded, tiered psychological support systems in university settings. Notably, the study indicates that it is essential to attend not only to symptoms of PGD and depression, but also to PLEs, which may represent a less commonly recognised but clinically relevant outcome of bereavement in this group. In addition, individuals with poor physical health or who have experienced the violent death of a loved one appear to be at higher risk and should be the focus of regular mental health screenings and targeted support.

The findings also emphasize the unique predictive value of death attitudes in shaping bereavement outcomes. Students with more adaptive understandings of death and grief may be able to better cope with the pain of the loss (Goveas et al., 2024). Importantly, prior research has demonstrated that death education can significantly reduce death fear and escape acceptance among college students (Han et al., 2023). Therefore, integrating death education into school curriculum may be beneficial for college students to navigate bereavement. Psychoeducational interventions should aim to normalise bereavement reactions, reduce death fear and help bereaved students find meaning in both their life and their bereavement.

Limitations

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling, combined with exclusive online recruitment and self-administered surveys, may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the high attrition rate between T1 (N = 366) and T2 (N = 159) raises concerns about selective dropout. The retained participants had, on average, a longer time since loss, were more likely to have lost parents or relatives rather than friends, and predominantly experienced non-violent bereavements. Caution is advised when generalising to broader bereavement populations, particularly those with underrepresented loss characteristics (e.g. short-term bereavement, friend loss, violent deaths). Moreover, although simulation studies suggest that LCA can be conducted with smaller samples under conditions of clear class separation, larger sample sizes (e.g. N > 500) are generally recommended (Nylund-Gibson & Choi, 2018). Therefore, the relatively small sample size at T2 may have limited the statistical power to detect small but clinically meaningful classes. Future research with larger, more heterogeneous samples recruited by diverse channels is encouraged to replicate and extend the current results. In addition, incorporating qualitative methods and experimental designs are recommended to further investigate the influence of death attitudes in bereavement adjustment.
