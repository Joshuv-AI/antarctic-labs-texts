Analysing Resilience Indicator Traits in Austrian Fleckvieh, Brown Swiss and Holstein‐Friesian Cattle
Authors: M Agyiri, R Onzima, A Costa, B Fuerst‐Waltl, F Steininger, C Egger‐Danner, G Mészáros, J Sölkner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent studies suggest that variations in milk yield during lactation can serve as a metric for assessing individual resilience, with minimum fluctuations reflecting greater resilience in cows. With automatic milking systems, multiple records can be obtained from many cows on a daily basis. Given that animals are continually exposed to a range of stressors throughout their productive life, identifying resilience traits is crucial for preserving both their productive and reproductive capacities. We studied two resilience indicators: natural log‐transformed variance (ln(σ 2)) and lag‐1 autocorrelation (r lag) of the daily deviations in milk yield recorded from the automatic milking system, with average daily milk yield incorporated as an additional trait to assess the correlations between the resilience indicators and milk yield. The resilience indicators were derived from three fitted lactation curves: the Wilmink curve, the penalised quadratic spline and the penalised cubic spline. The data consisted of 18,549,535 daily milk records of 37,118 Fleckvieh, 7322 Holstein‐Friesian and 2784 Brown Swiss cows in 1070 Austrian dairy herds. The data were collected for lactation periods of up to 350 days across multiple parities. Additionally, the resilience indicators were calculated based on days 11–50 of lactation, as the transition period and early lactation are crucial moments for the cows. Genetic analyses were conducted using univariate and bivariate analyses to estimate heritability (h 2 ) and genetic correlations between the traits. Estimated breeding values of the indicator traits obtained from the genetic analysis were correlated with routinely estimated breeding values and official indexes of the cows, including dairy, health, functional and conformation traits. The resilience indicators were scaled to negatives (−ln(σ 2) and −r lag) to compute these correlations, so that positive values indicate better resilience. The h 2 ranged between 0.09 and 0.20 for ln(σ 2) and between 0.04 and 0.06 for r lag, with the highest estimates observed when the penalised cubic spline was used. Generally, the h 2 decreased when only the early lactation period was considered. Genetic correlations of the resilience indicators with existing traits were low to moderate, with stronger correlations observed with −ln(σ 2) than in −r lag, particularly when estimated with the dairy traits. The negative correlations with milk yield, ranging from −0.13 to −0.45 for −ln(σ 2) and −0.03 to −0.23 for −r lag suggest that selecting for the resilience traits may result in a reduction in milk yield. This study demonstrated that deviations derived from fluctuations in lactation curves are heritable. Among the two resilience indicators evaluated, −ln(σ 2) emerged as the more suitable trait for selection, considering its h 2 and positive correlation to health and functional traits. These findings underscore the potential for incorporating resilience traits into breeding programs while emphasising the need to balance improving resilience with milk production, considering the probable risk of reduced milk production in cows.Keywords: genetic parameters, lactation curves, resilience indicators

Introduction

Throughout their lifespan, livestock are subjected to a variety of intrinsic and extrinsic stressors. In dairy cows, these include management practices and negative energy balance during early lactation (Mekuriaw 2023), problems arising from climate change, such as fluctuations in feed and water availability (Vaiknoras et al. 2024), emerging diseases and heat stress (Bett et al. 2017). The dairy sector is particularly vulnerable, as this leads to reduced reproductive and productive performance and impaired health (Das et al. 2016; Rhoads et al. 2009; Sejian et al. 2018). An animal's susceptibility to stressors is influenced by factors such as its genetic potential, past selective pressure, species or breed, nutrition, farm management and production system (Das et al. 2016). The stress experienced can be acute or chronic, depending on its duration and intensity. Hence, a cow that can adapt to diverse situational stressors, irrespective of its genetic makeup or environmental conditions, is generally more desirable.

Resilience is the term that defines a cow's ability to cope with or recover swiftly from disturbances while maintaining production and reproductive capabilities (Colditz and Hine 2016; Keßler et al. 2024; Poppe, Bonekamp, et al. 2021). Studies of daily milk yield recorded on farm suggest that deviations from expected performance capture disturbances during lactation (Elgersma et al. 2018; Keßler et al. 2024; Poppe, Mulder, et al. 2022; Poppe, Veerkamp, et al. 2022; Putz et al. 2019). From these studies, the deviations of natural log‐transformed variance (ln(σ 2)) and lag‐1 autocorrelation (r lag) have been identified as promising indicators for resilience and have also been shown to be heritable (Keßler et al. 2024; Poppe, Bonekamp, et al. 2021). The variance indicator ln(σ 2) measures the fluctuations in the performance of a cow, with a more resilient cow expected to exhibit fewer fluctuations in the milk pattern across a lactation when disturbances occur (Keßler et al. 2024). The indicator standardises the residuals to a normal distribution, enabling descriptive comparison between populations (Iung et al. 2020). The r lag, on the other hand, indicates the duration until the recovery of a cow following a disturbance (Keßler et al. 2024; Poppe, Bonekamp, et al. 2021). r lag close to zero is preferable as it is indicative of a greater potential for resilience. Given the uncertainty surrounding the start, end or even the presence of a disturbance, analysis of resilience indicators using longitudinal data, such as daily milk records, which focuses on changes over a specific period, rather than their direct reaction to a specific disturbance, is advantageous (Keßler et al. 2025).

While ln(σ 2) has been shown to be a better indicator than r lag, further research across diverse populations from different management systems is essential to validate these findings and support their broader applicability. This study aims to analyse the resilience indicators and estimate their genetic parameters across multiple lactations in Austrian Fleckvieh (FL), Austrian Holstein‐Friesian (HF) and Austrian Brown Swiss (BS) cattle using deviations in daily milk yields retrieved from farms equipped with automatic milking systems (AMS). Since understanding the relationship between the resilience indicator traits and other regularly recorded traits is crucial, the genetic correlations between the resilience indicators and milk production, health and functional traits were estimated.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Data consisted of 18,549,535 daily milk records from 47,224 cows. These included 14,890,461 daily milk records from 37,118 FL, 2,674,800 from 7322 HF and 984,274 from 2784 BS cows. The data were obtained from ZuchtData EDV‐Dienstleistungen GmbH (Vienna, Austria) upon agreement and encompassed milk records collected from farms (n = 1070) with AMS, including records of the amount of milk yield for each milking from 2012 to 2021. The AMS data consisted of individual cow milkings and other information, including milk volume in kg, milk volume in kg per hour, milking time and number of visits to AMS. Methods for obtaining and preparing the lactation data adhered to the approach detailed in Hogeveen et al. (2001). Individual milkings were obtained and validated. Interrupted milkings were not included in the dataset. Daily milk production time was computed by apportioning each interval between successive milkings across the periods they spanned. For intervals between milkings that spanned 2 days, the appropriate fraction of the interval was assigned to each day. The daily total production was obtained by summing all intervals assigned for that day. Based on validated milking events, daily 24‐h milk production averages were derived.

Prior to fitting lactation curves, quality checks and editing of data were implemented to ensure accurate phenotypes and robustness of the analyses. Daily milk yields from lactations 1 to 6 were retained. For each lactation, only milk records between 10 and 350 days in milk (DIM) were considered to avoid variabilities in milk yield in early and late days in lactation that may not necessarily reflect a cow's typical lactation curve. Additionally, cows that commenced lactation later than day 15 were excluded from the study. To include only cows with full lactation, those with a minimum of 210 days of lactation were selected according to the guidelines of the International Committee for Animal Recording (ICAR 2022). Cows lacking at least 50% of the expected DIM for a full lactation were also excluded. Following data filtering, the cows retained for analysis are as listed in Table 1.

Methods

Modelling and Fitting Lactation Curves

Given that different lactation curves may capture different aspects of the data, this study will compare three models, namely the Wilmink curve (WC) and two penalised spline methods—a quadratic spline with degrees of freedom set to 5 (QS) and a cubic spline with degrees of freedom set to 8 (CS). WC was fitted using the stats package in R (R Core Team 2024) while the QS and CS models were implemented using the pspline package (Ramsay et al. 1997). These curves were fitted to reflect the typical trajectory of milk yield.

Computation of Resilience Indicators

Based on previous studies by Keßler et al. (2024) and Poppe, Bonekamp, et al. (2021), we defined two resilience indicators, the ln(σ 2) and r lag. A total of six resilience indicators were calculated for each cow, as the two resilience indicators each were computed for all three types of lactation curves. Average daily milk yield (ADMY) was included as an additional trait to determine the correlations between the resilience indicators and productivity level.

Considering that cows may experience postpartum metabolic disorders and diseases during the early days of lactation, we also calculated resilience indicators only for DIM 11–50 of lactation. Analyses were conducted using the estimates from the full‐lactation milk yield records, restricted to DIM 11–50, as DIM 1–10 had been excluded during prior filtering. The data set comprised 1329 BS, 20,758 FL and 3987 HF cows.

The vector of fixed effects b consisted of age at first calving class in days combined with parity class (12 levels in total; eight levels for cows in first lactation and four levels for cows in lactations 2, 3, 4 and ≥ 5), completeness of data class (5 levels, categorised according to the number of data points observed for each lactation between 10 and 350 DIM thus < 150, ≥ 150 < 200, ≥ 200 < 250, ≥ 250 < 305 and ≥ 305), year‐season (17 levels in BS, 15 levels in FL and 13 levels in HF, with year, categorised into four distinct seasons: spring (March–May), summer (June–August), autumn (September–November) and winter (December–February)) and herd (552 levels in FL, 200 levels in HF and 74 levels in BS). For each breed, herds with less than five cows per herd were excluded. The traits to be analysed were assumed to be continuous and normally distributed with a mean of zero, thus, a ~ N (0, Aσa2), pe ~ N (0, Ipe σpe2) and e ~ N (0, Ie σe2), where A is the additive genetic relationship matrix, Ipe and Ie the identity matrices of the permanent environmental effects and residual effects, respectively, and σa2, σpe2 and σe2 the variances of a, pe and e, respectively.

The narrow‐sense h 2 was calculated from the variance components as: h2=σa2/σa2+σpe2+σe2. The model was extended to a bivariate model to estimate the genetic correlations between the resilience indicators. For each breed, pedigree data used for all genetic analyses consisted of 4 ancestral generations.

We fitted a univariate animal model, similar to the full‐lactation analysis, to estimate genetic parameters in the early lactation period for all three breeds. The vector of fixed effects b included: age at first calving class in days combined with parity class (12 levels; eight levels for cows in first lactation and four levels for cows in lactations 2, 3, 4 and ≥ 5), completeness of data class (2 levels, ≥ 50% and ≥ 75), year‐season (16 levels in BS, 14 levels in FL) and 11 levels in HF, with year, categorised into four seasons as in the full‐lactation model and herd (529 levels in FL, 160 levels in HF and 59 levels in BS). Inclusion criteria required that cows have 50% of expected lactation records and that herds contain at least 5 individuals for genetic analysis. The resulting cows following these additional filtering were 1179 BS, 20,072 FL and 3603 HF.

Correlations Between Resilience Indicators and Selection Index Traits

The estimated breeding values (EBV) of the indicator traits obtained from the univariate models were correlated with the EBV of the cows for the traits in the selection index from routine genetic evaluation, using Pearson's correlation. The resilience indicators were redefined as −ln(σ 2) and −r lag. Taking the negative values of ln(σ 2) and r lag was done to scale the traits such that positive values indicate greater resilience. The routinely evaluated traits considered included the total merit index, dairy traits (dairy index, milk, fat and protein and fat and protein content), health traits (somatic cell count, mastitis, udder health index, early fertility problems, cystic ovaries, milk fever, claw health), functional traits (longevity, persistency, fertility values, calving ease, milk speed, milk temperament) and conformation traits (body frame, muscularity, feet and legs and udder conformation).

Detailed descriptions of the Austrian official indexes are regularly published and updated by ZuchtData EDV‐Dienstleistungen (Rinderzucht Austria 2025a). Generally, relative weights for the three breeds, scaled by genetic standard deviations, are 36%–50% for milk, 44%–49% for functional and health traits and 0%–18% for meat traits (Rinderzucht Austria 2025b). Conformation traits do not have weights of their own in the Austrian total merit index system, but some are used as auxiliary traits in the genetic evaluation of functional traits.

Discussion

Descriptive Analyses and Genetic Parameters

Descriptive analyses revealed that, on average, the HF had the highest ln(σ 2), as well as the highest ADMY. This indicates lower resilience compared to the BS, which exhibited lower estimates, considering the definitions for the indicator traits. The elevated means of HF may explain the comparatively higher estimates observed for ln(σ 2) in this breed (mean–variance relationship) compared to the other breeds. However, this does not necessarily imply lower resilience, as previous studies suggest that cows with higher milk production tend to exhibit fluctuations in milk production within the lactation, that is, higher milk variability (Elgersma et al. 2018).

The h 2 estimates for the resilience indicators, though moderate, demonstrate that resilience, as captured by these indicators, has a genetic basis and could be considered as useful proxies in breeding programs. Among the indicators, r lag exhibited lower h 2 estimates compared to ln(σ 2). These findings are consistent with results reported in other studies (Elgersma et al. 2018; Keßler et al. 2024; Poppe, Bonekamp, et al. 2021; Poppe et al. 2020). When analysed by breed, FL showed higher h 2 compared to BS and HF, indicating that, compared to the other breeds, targeted genetic strategies may result in a larger response to selection and reshape of phenotypic and genetic variability of resilience. Additionally, the significant deviation from zero in the SE of FL for r lag suggests σa2 for the trait in the population. In contrast, the lack of significance in other breeds may indicate lower genetic variance or insufficient data precision, potentially due to smaller sample sizes, limited pedigree depth or greater environmental noise obscuring genetic effects.

However, when h 2 was estimated using data from DIM 11–50, the values decreased consistently across all lactation curves. A similar trend was observed by Poppe, Bonekamp, et al. (2021) who found h 2 equal to 0.13 for ln(σ 2) between 11 and 110 DIM. This finding is further corroborated by Kašná et al. (2025), who observed that h 2 estimates for the traits they investigated, including ln(σ 2), r lag and skewness, were generally higher when calculated using full lactation data compared to estimates derived from specific lactation periods. These trends suggest that at the start of lactation, dairy cows are more susceptible to environmental influences, resulting in smaller additive genetic estimates and stronger environmental effects. In general, the h 2 for resilience indicators, though low‐to‐moderate, align with findings from previous studies on health and fertility traits such as somatic cell count (SCC), ketosis and calving interval. These traits are typically characterised by low‐to‐moderate h 2 due to their complex genetic architecture, polygenic nature and the inherent challenges associated with accurately measuring them. For example, h 2 estimates for SCC as studied in HF and FL among other dairy breeds can range from 0.05 to 0.15 (Jattawa et al. 2012; Koeck et al. 2010; Pelmuș et al. 2022; Wei et al. 2021) and calving interval h 2 ranged from 0.01 to 0.10 (Atashi et al. 2020; Cassell 2001; Jayawardana et al. 2023).

Types of Lactation Curves

In this study, we limited the dataset to cows with a minimum of 210 DIM to ensure sufficient data for reliable lactation curve fitting. Short or incomplete lactations often lead to unstable parameter estimates and hinder comparability across animals. While we recognise that this criterion may exclude some of the most problematic cows—those potentially exhibiting poor resilience and culled before 210 DIM—our primary objective was to develop indicators that capture within‐lactation dynamics, rather than being influenced by survival bias due to premature culling or management interventions. Therefore, the resilience indicators presented here should be interpreted as representative of cows with relatively complete lactations, while survival‐related aspects of resilience may require additional, complementary analyses.

The WC is a widely used classical method for modelling lactation curves for its ability to capture the typical biological trajectory of milk production, as well as its simplicity and interpretability (Poppe et al. 2020). The model excels at capturing the overall shape of lactation curves, including the initial rise in milk yield, the peak, the plateau and the subsequent decline. However, its reliance on a fixed functional form may limit its capacity to accommodate individual differences in lactation patterns. In contrast, the penalised splines offer greater flexibility due to the inclusion of a penalty term (degrees of freedom) that regulates the smoothness of the curves. Specifically, the QS employs a second‐degree polynomial, while the CS uses a third‐degree polynomial. Unlike the WC, which applies a single function across the entire lactation period, penalised splines fit piecewise polynomials to different segments of the lactation curve, ensuring smooth transitions at predefined knots. This approach is particularly advantageous for capturing variations in milk yield across lactation, as different phases may carry varying biological significance (Keßler et al. 2024). According to some studies, the selection of an appropriate modelling approach is critical for improving the accuracy of predictions, as models that better represent the underlying biological processes are more likely to yield estimates that closely reflect reality (da Cunha et al. 2010; Trejo‐Díaz et al. 2024). A potential disadvantage of closely fitting curves is that they may also reflect responses to stressors; however, based on the definition of resilience, such curves are useful for capturing swift changes across lactations, which may be relevant for incidents like mastitis or heat stress. For example, a sudden drop in milk yield due to mastitis or heat stress might be reflected in the curve as a sharp change or deviation. These deviations may serve as indicators of resilience, providing insights into how quickly and effectively an animal recovers following such incidents.

In our study, results for ln(σ 2) indicated that h 2 increased when the lactation curve followed daily milk yield patterns more closely. In fact, this trend was particularly evident for the CS. However, these differences between the three lactation curve models were not statistically significant, as the observed differences were within the range of their SE. Similarly, h 2 estimates for r lag showed minimal variation across the three modelling approaches, suggesting that the choice of curve‐fitting method has a limited impact on h 2. In fact, Poppe et al. (2020) investigated the WC alongside other lactation curves and reported that the choice of the modelling method has minimal impact on the estimated h 2 of ln(σ 2).

Correlation Between Indicators and Indicators With ADMY

Due to the small population size, correlations between the resilience indicators were unreliable for BS and HF. In contrast, positive genetic correlations were observed in FL, consistent with findings from previous studies (Chen et al. 2023; Poppe et al. 2020). These positive correlations align with expectations, indicating that as the variance in milk yield increases along the lactation curve, the r lag also increases, leading to slower recovery following a perturbation.

The positive genetic correlations of resilience indicators with ADMY also agreed with earlier studies, although in most cases, our values were somewhat stronger (Keßler et al. 2024; Poppe, Mulder, and Veerkamp 2021). Until about three decades ago, selection indices predominantly emphasised increasing milk yield, resulting in the development of high‐producing cows but often at the expense of reproductive and functional traits (Lucy 2019). The addition of other traits other than milk production traits, in recent times, has mitigated these adverse effects. Therefore, the positive correlation between the indicators and ADMY may not be a disadvantage but rather highlights the inherent relationship between fitness traits and milk yield. Results should be interpreted with caution because of the mean–variance relationship. How much weight to accord to our indicators in the selection index to ensure a balance between milk loss and resilience must nonetheless be considered. The phenotypic correlation had the same direction but a lower magnitude (≤ 0.35).

Correlation Between Indicators and Traits in the Total Merit Index

Stronger Pearson's correlations were observed between −ln(σ 2) and the routinely measured traits. Meanwhile, −r lag had negligible correlations with them, suggesting that −ln(σ 2) can be considered as a better predictor of resilience than −r lag (Figure 2). This is similar to prior studies (Keßler et al. 2025) which indicate that −ln(σ 2) tends to be a better predictor of resilience than −r lag when using daily milk records. The negative correlation between the resilience indicators and most dairy traits further emphasises that selection for better resilience, if pursued without control on milk productivity, would result in a decreased milk yield in the medium‐ to long‐term. The positive correlations, however, with health traits suggest that incorporating the indicator traits into the selection index would genetically lead to a reduction in disease occurrence among cows, resulting in positive outcomes for fitness/health traits, which aligns with expectations. It is expected that selecting for resilience indicators will lead to improved health, with resilient animals better able to cope with stressors, recover from disturbances, maintain stable physiological and production states and reduce the likelihood of diseases.

Resilience in dairy cattle is increasingly recognised as a valuable trait with both economic and non‐economic implications. Cows with greater resilience, indicated by higher −ln(σ 2), are linked to higher lifetime gross margins stemming primarily from improved longevity and decreased risk of culling (Poppe, Mulder, et al. 2022; Poppe, Veerkamp, et al. 2022). While breeding for resilient cows is generally considered more profitable, the precise effects on modern features such as feed efficiency, methane emissions and other cost‐related factors remain unclear (Brito et al. 2021) and must be estimated and validated to ensure optimal breeding goals (Bengtsson et al. 2022; Poppe, Mulder, et al. 2022; Poppe, Veerkamp, et al. 2022). Further research is required to fully assess their overall impact on various dairy sector aspects.

As highlighted by Elgersma et al. (2018), resilience also extends beyond the economic benefits of reduced disease incidence and increased longevity, encompassing traits associated with easy‐to‐manage or trouble‐free production. Assuming that management is optimal and the σp2 constant, breeding for resilience, that is, selecting cows with lower milk variance, is expected to result in animals that generate fewer alerts on farms in subsequent generations, thereby reducing labour demands, associated costs and greater farmers' satisfaction due to reduced management challenges (Elgersma et al. 2018). This assumption may hold if the population in question has no inherent health problems. Defining resilient cows solely by milk variability could mask subtle or chronic health issues that have little to no substantial impact on milk yield or only do so minimally and for a short duration. Comprehensive herd surveillance, including clinical, reproductive and metabolic monitoring, should continue to serve as complementary measures.

The integration of resilience indicators into dairy cattle breeding programs presents both opportunities and challenges. While resilient cows offer indirect benefits such as fewer health events and reduced labour, these advantages may be less visible or immediately valued by farmers compared to traditional production traits like milk yield. This underscores the need for education and clear communication to highlight the long‐term economic and management benefits.

Conclusion

Inclusion of resilience traits in the selection index of the Austrian dairy cattle population shows a potential for improved herd health, as animals with minimum fluctuations in daily milk yield are more resilient and recover quickly. However, findings from this study also highlight the inherent challenges in obtaining very high h 2 estimates for resilience indicators, which are subject to the complex genomic architecture and nature of their measurement. Nevertheless, the consistency of our estimates of −ln(σ 2) with prior research, coupled with its greater h 2 and positive correlation with health and functional traits compared to −r lag, supports its viability as a candidate resilience trait. Through its incorporation, breeding programs can enhance the resilience, supporting long‐term sustainability and profitability. Ultimately, the successful adoption of resilience‐focused breeding strategies will depend on refining their economic valuation, effectively communicating their benefits to farmers and ensuring their integration complements existing breeding goals.
