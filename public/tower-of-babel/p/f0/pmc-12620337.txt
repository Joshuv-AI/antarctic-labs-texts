Modelling of the Systemic Inflammatory Host Response in Relation to the Microbiome of the Endodontic Infection
Authors: A. C. Georgiou, G. R. van der Ploeg, J. A. Westerhuis, S. V. van der Waal, A. Heintz‐Buschart, A. K. Smilde, B. W. Brandt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThis study aimed to investigate systemic inflammatory responses in individuals with apical periodontitis (AP) compared to age‐matched healthy controls, using advanced multiway modelling techniques. We examined associations between inflammatory mediators, age, gender, symptomatic status, and the microbial composition and function to better understand host–microbe interactions in AP.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal study with 53 participants (27 with AP, 26 controls) aged 18 to 80. Blood inflammatory mediators were measured at multiple time points. Root canal microbiome and potential functions were analysed using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Data were analysed using Parallel Factor Analysis (PARAFAC) and Advanced Coupled Matrix and Tensor Factorization (ACMTF) to capture time‐resolved variation.ResultsPARAFAC revealed that IL‐4 levels were significantly elevated in AP cases, suggesting a role for adaptive immune activation. Age strongly influenced markers such as CRP, TNF‐α, and VEGF. Symptomatic AP cases showed higher CRP and lower OPG levels, indicating more active inflammation and altered bone metabolism. ACMTF identified associations between specific taxa (e.g., Parvimonas micra , Streptococcus mutans ) and inflammatory mediators, with functional analysis highlighting enriched pathways like sphingolipid signalling in asymptomatic cases.ConclusionThis study provides novel insights into the systemic immune profile of individuals with AP. IL‐4 and bone metabolism markers may serve as potential biomarkers for distinguishing AP status. Our findings support the relevance of systemic inflammation in endodontic infections and underscore the value of multi‐marker, multivariate approaches to better characterise disease progression and host–microbiome interactions. Such insights could contribute to improved risk stratification and personalised management in dental and systemic health contexts.

Introduction

Apical periodontitis (AP) is an inflammatory reaction of the periapical tissues of a tooth after pulp necrosis and subsequent infection of the root canal system (Kakehashi et al. 1965). Pulp necrosis can occur after invasion of microorganisms into the tooth and is usually the result of an irreversible inflammatory response to microorganisms in the pulp (Nair 2006). After pulp necrosis, the microorganisms invade the root‐canal system until they eventually reach the root apex (Moller et al. 1981). To confine the infection, a complex inflammatory response starts in the area where the root canal meets the periapical tissues. Locally, the mandibular bone is resorbed in order to make space for inflamed tissue.

Bone remodelling, in response to the microbiome and their metabolic byproducts, is one of the characteristics of AP. It is the result of the induction and differentiation of osteoclasts that are modulated by mediators activated during the inflammatory process (Troen 2003). Some of the pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory mediators that are involved in this activation are interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐6, interferon (IFN)‐γ, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)‐α, transforming growth factor (TGF)‐β and IL‐4. The extent of the bone damage—that is, the AP lesion—depends on the virulence of the involved microorganisms (Neto et al. 2018). During a chronic inflammatory state, such as AP, there is a constant fluctuation in the balance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory mediators of the host response to the antigen stimulation (Neto et al. 2018).

In recent years, a popular topic in endodontics research has been the examination of the systemic consequences of these inflammatory mediators. Many studies have been published about circulating inflammatory mediators in the blood of people with AP (Abdolsamadi et al. 2008; Harjunmaa et al. 2018; Garrido et al. 2019; Sebring et al. 2024). Some factors that influence the levels of inflammatory mediators in the blood—such as aging—have not been adequately considered in earlier studies. However, as more research emerges and our understanding of the immune system's complexity deepens, it has become increasingly clear that the analytical methods previously employed were often insufficient. In particular, these analyses rarely account for the inherently multivariate and longitudinal structure of immunological data. Such data often exhibit complex patterns of variation over time, influenced by multiple interacting factors, including treatment effects, temporal dynamics, and subject‐specific responses (Georgiou, van der Waal, et al. 2023). With the growing availability of data and improved domain knowledge, more advanced analytical approaches can now be employed to capture this complexity more effectively.

Additionally, there are differences in the microbiome of the endodontic infections that are associated with the manifestation of symptoms (Hou et al. 2022). This is due to an interplay between the immune system and the microbiome, where different inflammatory responses trigger the symptoms. AP is diagnosed as symptomatic when there are pain complaints and asymptomatic in their absence (Duncan et al. 2023). The relationship of the microbiome to the host response is not yet explored in the context of apical periodontitis.

Multi‐way modelling methods are increasingly being applied to the analysis of longitudinal omics data, including microbiome (van der Ploeg et al. 2024, 2025) and metabolomics datasets (Acar et al. 2015). Techniques such as Parallel Factor Analysis (PARAFAC) can reveal dominant sources of time‐resolved variation (Bro 1997). More recently, Advanced Coupled Matrix and Tensor Factorization (ACMTF) has emerged as a powerful data integration framework capable of identifying shared patterns of (time‐resolved) variation across multiple datasets (Acar et al. 2015). Compared to traditional approaches—such as Generalised Additive Models (Wood 2017) or Linear Mixed Effects Models (Pinheiro and Bates 2000; Molenberghs 2006)—these methods offer a key advantage: they model time categorically rather than parametrically. This feature makes them particularly well‐suited for small‐scale cohort studies, where limited time points and high dimensionality are common challenges (van der Ploeg et al. 2025).

In this article, we present a proof of concept demonstrating how PARAFAC and ACMTF can be applied to longitudinal inflammatory mediator data—both independently and in combination with microbiome data—to better understand immune dynamics. This work is motivated by the clinical challenge of interpreting complex, time‐resolved immunological signals in conditions characterised by chronic or dysregulated inflammation, such as autoimmune diseases or post‐infectious syndromes. Using PARAFAC, we aim to identify the dominant sources of longitudinal variation in blood cytokine levels, while ACMTF will enable us to uncover patterns of variation that are shared between cytokine and microbiome data. These methods offer a powerful framework for understanding complex biological processes and may help pinpoint key time‐dependent interactions between the host immune system and the microbiome.

Materials and Methods

This study was a multi‐centre prospective case–control intervention study conducted at several private dental practices in the Netherlands and at the Academic Centre of Dentistry in Amsterdam (ACTA). The study was reported in compliance with the Reporting Items for study Designs in Endodontology (PRIDE) (http://pride‐endodonticguidelines.org/) and registered prospectively with the Clinical Trial Registry in the Netherlands (http://www.trialregister.nl) with registration number NL6081. The detailed protocol has been published (Georgiou et al. 2021). All procedures were performed in accordance with good clinical practice guidelines and declarations of Helsinki and have received the approval of the Medical Ethical Committee of the VU medical centre, Amsterdam, the Netherlands (registration number 2016.187).

Selection of Patients

Participants were recruited from various dental practices in the Netherlands between January 2017 and September 2019. The study included individuals aged 18–80 with asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic apical periodontitis (AP), confirmed by intra‐oral radiographs. Apical periodontitis (AP) was diagnosed based on radiographic evidence of a widened periodontal ligament space and other clinical signs. Patients preferred extraction of the affected tooth over root‐canal treatment due to poor prognosis or no restorability. The rest of the dentition had to be free of AP and marginal periodontitis, confirmed through clinical examination and radiographs. The control group consisted of healthy individuals without marginal or apical periodontitis and did not receive any intervention other than drawing blood. Detailed criteria are described in the published protocol article (Georgiou et al. 2021). Participants consented to six blood draws and had their medical history reviewed at each visit.

Clinical Procedures for Collection of Blood Samples

Participants visited the clinic six times for blood collection and health updates (Figure 1A). The first two visits were planned within three to 6 weeks before the AP‐affected tooth was extracted, the third on the day of extraction, and 1, 6, and 13 weeks post‐extraction. The first three visits established baseline inflammatory mediator levels, while the subsequent visits monitored post‐infection changes. Appointments were consistently timed to account for circadian rhythm variations.

(A) Flowchart of the included subjects during the different visits. (B) PARAFAC can identify the largest source of time‐resolved variation in a three‐way array of longitudinal inflammatory mediator data. PARAFAC decomposes the three‐way array into triplets of vectors describing the subject scores, the feature loadings and time, respectively. (D) ACMTF can also be performed on the functionally annotated tooth microbiome data. This yields loadings describing the influence of microbial functions on the differences observed in the subjects, which can be further investigated using pathway enrichment analysis.

Blood was collected via venipuncture using the Vacutainer system in 9 mL EDTA tubes, centrifuged on‐site, and plasma was collected, cooled, and transported to ACTA for aliquoting and storage at −80°C within 24 h. Extracted teeth were also stored at −80°C until further use.

Quantitative Protein Assessment

Details regarding the blood analysis have been reported previously (Georgiou, Twisk, et al. 2023). Briefly, inflammatory mediators were selected based on their roles in the immune response and previous studies on apical periodontitis. GM‐CSF, IL‐1α, IL‐17A, and VEGF were measured using a custom multi‐analyte immunoassay kit (V–plex cytokine panel 1). IFN‐γ, IL‐1β, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12p70, and TNF‐α were measured using another custom kit (V–plex Proinflammatory panel 1). CRP and MIP‐1α were measured using a V–plex Vascular Injury panel 2 and a Chemokine panel 1 single‐analyte kit, respectively. OPG was measured using the MSD R‐Plex human OPG antibody set with MSD GOLD Small Spot Streptavidin plates on 2‐fold diluted plasma samples. All measurements were performed in duplicate using the Quickplex SQ120 (MSD), and protein concentrations were recorded in pg/mL. Data analysis was done using MSD Discovery Workbench 4.0 software. RANKL was measured in duplicate using a magnetic bead‐based singleplex assay (LXSAHM, R&D Systems, UK) on 2‐fold diluted samples, run on a Bio‐Plex Multiplex System (Bio‐Rad, USA). Protein concentrations were recorded in pg/mL.

DNA Extraction and Amplicon Sequencing

After extraction, the tooth was stored at −80°C until all samples were collected. DNA extraction from the teeth was carried out as previously described (Persoon et al. 2017; Georgiou, van der Waal, et al. 2023). In brief, the tooth surface was vigorously scrubbed with gauzes soaked in 5% sodium hypochlorite. This was inactivated by wiping off the tooth with a gauze soaked in 5% sodium thiosulfate. Thereafter, the tooth was decoronated using an end‐cutting plier and a hammer. The tooth was placed in a sterile gauze and the coronal part was engaged in the plier, which was hit with force with the hammer. The coronal part was discarded. The root segments were cryopulverized under aseptic conditions using a freezer mill (Spex Certiprep, Metuchen, NJ, USA) and stored at −80°C.

The powdered root segments were thawed and centrifuged for 5 min at 4500 × g, suspended in 300 μL lysis buffer (Mag Mini DNA Isolation Kit, LGC, Hoddesdon, UK), and transferred to 2 mL screw‐capped tubes (Sarstedt, Nümbrecht, Germany) with 350 μL zirconium beads (0.1 mm; BioSpec products, Bartlesville, OK, USA), 350 μL phenol saturated with Tris–HCl (pH 8.0; Carl Roth, Germany) and 120 μL 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) to reverse the binding of DNA to dentin (Brundin et al. 2014). Samples were placed in a Mini‐BeadBeater‐96 (BioSpec products, Bartlesville, OK, USA) for 4 × 2 min at 2100 oscillations/min. Root‐canal microbial DNA was extracted using the Mag Mini DNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fischer Scientific, The Netherlands).

A quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was used to determine the amount of bacterial DNA. Primers were specific to the bacterial 16S rRNA gene, forward: TCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAGT, reverse: GGACTACCAGGGTATCTAATCCTGTT; probe: 6FAM‐CGTATTACCGCGGCTGCTGGCAC‐BHQ1 according to (Ciric et al. 2010).

Amplicons of the V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene were generated using barcoded forward and reverse primers (Kozich et al. 2013) containing the 16S rRNA gene‐specific sequences V4F/515F: GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA and V4R/806R: GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT (Caporaso et al. 2011). From the equimolar mix, 8 pmol including 30% PhiX, was sequenced using the Illumina MiSeq platform with the MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (2x251 nt) at the Core Facility Genomics, Amsterdam UMC, The Netherlands.

To confirm aseptic processing of the teeth, the qPCR and sequencing were also applied to several control samples, such as extracted ‘sound’ teeth with a vital pulp, swab samples from the surface of the roots after cleaning and before pulverisation, the freezer‐mill vials, blank isolation and, finally, negative PCR controls. The 16S rRNA gene concentrations of these samples were similar to the blank isolations, and therefore, the sample processing was considered adequate.

16S rRNA Gene Data Processing

The sequencing reads were merged, quality‐filtered, and denoised, after which the merged reads were clustered to zero‐radius operational taxonomic units (zOTUs) as described previously (Kahharova et al. 2020). The sequencing data are available in the NCBI BioProject database under accession number PRJNA849825.

Statistical Analysis—Inflammatory Mediator Data Processing

The inflammatory mediator data were processed in R (v4.3.2; R Core Team 2023). All inflammatory mediator measurements were manually inspected for differences between the replicate samples. Overall, the distributions of replicates 1 and 2 were highly similar for all variables. However, for IL‐1β, GM_CSF, and IL17A, we observed that outliers were present. Here we define outliers as observations that fall below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR, where Q1 is the first quartile, Q3 is the third quartile, and IQR is the interquartile range. This is identical to the statistical definition and most plotting software such as ggplot2 (Wickham 2016). This was the case for IL‐1β, where replicate 2 contained outliers, while replicate 1 did not. Hence, only replicate 1 data was used for this variable. Conversely, for GM‐CSF and IL17A, replicate 1 data contained outliers, while replicate 2 did not. Hence, only replicate 2 data was used for these variables. All other variables were free from outliers, and the average of both replicates could be used safely. Subsequently, the inflammatory mediator data was natural log transformed after the addition of a pseudo count of 5e‐3 to all values below the detection limit.

The dataset was then converted to a three‐way array. Missing samples—a subject‐time combination—(participant did not attend a visit, due to the given reasons) were kept in the array as a row of missing values. This is possible because our multi‐way approaches interpolate the missing data automatically. This maximises the amount of information for the modelling procedure as the other samples of the subject do not have to be removed entirely. Additional arrays were created corresponding to sub‐cohorts consisting of only the case subjects or only the control subjects. Subsequently, centering across the subject mode and scaling within the inflammatory mediator mode—ignoring missing values—was performed for every tensor to make the samples comparable at every timepoint and the inflammatory mediators comparable for all time points (Bro and Smilde 2003; van der Ploeg et al. 2025). For the full cohort, one asymptomatic AP subject, and two control subjects were removed due to being outliers. This was not needed for the sub‐cohort data and yielded a tensor of size 49×16×6 for the full cohort, size 26×16×6 for the healthy sub‐cohort and size 24×16×6 for the AP‐only sub‐cohort.

Statistical Analysis—Microbiome Data

The zOTU data and taxonomic information were processed in R (v4.3.2; R Core Team 2023). Only samples corresponding to case subjects were available, as the tooth was extracted only for these subjects. Samples were removed from the data if the total sum of all counts was lower than 6500. This removed two samples from the data (Figure S1). In three subjects, two teeth were removed and subsequently sequenced. As a limitation of the ACMTF algorithm, only one microbiome sample can be kept per subject. As such, the extra samples were removed, while the sample corresponding to the other tooth was kept. All steps together resulted in 24 out of 29 samples being retained. Subsequently, zOTUs were kept if the sparsity was < 50% in either the symptomatic or asymptomatic group. All other zOTUs were removed from the data. This step selected 160 out of the 471 zOTUs. After the addition of a pseudo‐count of 1 to all values, a centered‐log ratio transformation was performed to correct for compositionality (Gloor et al. 2017; Aitchison 2018). Finally, the zOTUs were mean centered and scaled to a standard deviation of 1.

Statistical Analysis—Functional Profile Prediction of Microbiome Data

A functional profile prediction based on the zOTU count data was performed using the Tax4Fun2 package in R (Tax4Fun2 version 1.1.5, R version 4.0.3; Aßhauer et al. 2015; Wemheuer et al. 2020). Samples were rarefied to 6500 reads to make them comparable (Figure S1). Samples with fewer total reads were removed. This step removed samples A11‐18 and A11‐3. zOTUs without counts after rarefying were removed. All steps together removed 4 out of 471 zOTUs. Tax4Fun2 was run with default settings against the included Ref99NR database (Wemheuer et al. 2020) and a custom database based on the HOMD genomes (v9.14, accessed 2020‐11‐09; Chen et al. 2010). Molecular Functions (KEGG orthologous groups) of the genomes were assigned using Tax4Fun2's DIAMOND wrapper (Buchfink et al. 2015). HOMD‐annotated 16S rRNA gene sequences were extracted from the genomes, cut to the 515F‐806R fragment used in this study using cutadapt v1.18 (Martin 2011). Exact duplication within genomes was removed, and 16S rRNA gene sequence fragments were combined with the functional assignments using Tax4Fun's generateUserDataByClustering function. The resulting functional prediction profiles—which contain values between 0 and 1 for each function—were used for subsequent analysis. The fraction of unused taxonomic units and the fraction of unused sequences are reported (Figures S2 and S3).

Statistical Analysis—Processing of Functional Profile Predictions

The functionally predicted microbiome data were processed in MATLAB (version 2024a). The predicted KEGG orthologous groups (KOs) were purposefully not filtered as the data integration aspect of ACMTF protects from slow convergence and component redundancy due to high dimensionality. Subsequently, a centred‐log ratio transformation was performed to correct for compositionality using the lowest observed non‐zero value (1.8e‐09) as pseudo‐count (Gloor et al. 2017; Aitchison 2018). Finally, the KOs were mean‐centred and scaled to a standard deviation of 1.

Statistical Analysis—PARAFAC

Details on the creation of PARAFAC models have been described elsewhere (Bro and Smilde 2003; Martino et al. 2021; van der Ploeg et al. 2025), (Figure 1B). The PARAFAC implementation from the N‐way toolbox (version 1.8.0.0) in MATLAB (version 2024a) was used to create PARAFAC models of the inflammatory mediator data for all sub‐cohorts (Bro and Kiers 2003). Similar to Principal Component Analysis (PCA), the correct number of components of the PARAFAC model needs to be determined to create an optimal model (Bro and Kiers 2003; van der Ploeg et al. 2024). This was done by inspecting the number of iterations needed to converge (Bro 1997), the core consistency diagnostic (CORCONDIA) (Bro and Kiers 2003), the variation explained (Bro 1997), and the Tucker congruence coefficient per mode (Tucker 1951; Lorenzo‐Seva and ten Berge 2006). Additionally, a jack‐knife approach was used to determine the stability of the PARAFAC modelling procedure. These metrics were inspected per component for every generated model (Figures S4–S15). The selected models are shown in Figures 2 and 3.

Statistical Analysis—ACMTF

Details on the creation of ACMTF models have been described elsewhere (Acar et al. 2011; Acar et al. 2015). The acmtf‐opt implementation from the CMTF toolbox (v3.1; Acar et al. 2011) was used in combination with the Tensor toolbox (v3.1; Bader and Kolda 2008) and the Poblano toolbox (v1.2; Dunlavy et al. 2010) in MATLAB (version 2024a).

We combined the processed inflammatory mediator three‐way array of the case‐only sub‐cohort with the processed microbiome data table for modelling with ACMTF using a shared subject mode (Figure 1C,D). To correctly match the subject mode between the datasets, sample A11‐18 and A11‐3—removed from the microbiome data due to low library size—had to be removed from the inflammatory mediator data as well prior to modelling. As a result, the inflammatory mediator tensor was size 24×16×6 and the microbiome data matrix was size 24×160. The correct number of components for this model was chosen based on inspecting the Factor Match Score as defined in Figure S16 (Acar et al. 2011; Li 2015). Additionally, a jack‐knife approach was used to determine the stability of the ACMTF modelling procedure (Figures S17 and S18). Both approaches showed that a stable ACMTF model was only possible using one component (Figure 4).

Statistical Analysis—Functional Enrichment Analysis

The feature loadings corresponding to the functionally predicted microbiome data in the ACMTF model, where it was integrated with the inflammatory mediator data, were used to perform a pathway enrichment test. A ranked list was created by calculating the variance explained per KO, with the best modelled KOs being at the top. The SetRank R package (version 1.1.0) was used to obtain pathways of interest (Simillion et al. 2017). SetRank is a gene set enrichment algorithm that obtains a high sensitivity by correcting for multiple pathway membership. A database of pathway elements was created through the KEGG API (Supporting Information). Pathways were removed from our database if they could not be performed by prokaryotes. Generic, large pathways with more than 750 elements were removed to ensure that the enrichment results were specific enough for interpretation. Subsequently, the SetRank analysis was performed with standard settings. The resulting pathway list were filtered using the following settings: pSetRank ≤ 0.05 and adjusted p‐value ≤ 0.05 and corrected p‐value ≤ 0.05. The resulting filtered pathway list was sorted by corrected p‐value and is reported (Table S1). The full SetRank output pathway list is available in Supporting Information. Pathway elements were visualised with enrichment in asymptomatic (green) or symptomatic (red) participants using the Pathview R package (v1.44.0; Luo and Brouwer 2013; Figures S23–S25).

Results

A total of 53 subjects were included in the study, 27 with apical periodontitis (AP) and 26 without (Figure 1A). The average age of the AP group was 52.7 years (SD: 14.5) and of the control group 51.5 (SD: 13.17) with no statistically significant difference between the groups (t‐test, p > 0.05). Fifteen females and 12 males were included in the AP group, and 14 females and 12 males in the control group. Two individuals dropped out of the AP group; one on visit four and one on the second visit, for personal reason, unrelated to this study. In the control group, three subjects dropped out in the fourth, fifth, and sixth visit because they had to use antibiotics for a nondental reason. All available data were used.

Parallel Factor Analysis Identifies Time‐Resolved Differences in Inflammatory Mediators Between Case and Control Subjects as Well as Individual Variation Within the Healthy Sub‐Cohort

To describe the systemic immune dynamics associated with AP, we applied PARAFAC to the processed longitudinal blood inflammatory mediator data (Figure 1B). Based on a combined random initialization and cross‐validation procedure, the appropriate number of components was determined for the full cohort and for a sub‐cohort containing only the healthy control subjects (Figures S4–S15; Table S2). This approach yielded a three‐component PARAFAC model that captured 35.8% of the variation in the full cohort (Figure 2) and a three‐component PARAFAC model that captured 47.6% of the variation in the control‐only sub‐cohort of the processed inflammatory mediator dataset (Figure 3).

To interpret the variation that was captured in the PARAFAC models, we performed correlation tests between the time‐resolved subject loadings of each component with various clinical parameters, including gender, age, symptom status, and case–control classification (Table 1). For the PARAFAC model of the full cohort, the first component was significantly associated with subject age (p = 0.018), separating younger from older subjects across all time points. The second component was tentatively associated with differences between symptomatic and asymptomatic AP cases (p = 0.09), while the third component was tentatively associated with case–control differences (p = 0.09). For the PARAFAC model of the healthy sub‐cohort, the first component likely captured individual variation and was excluded from further interpretation, as no correlations with metadata were significant (p > 0.05). The second component was tentatively associated with subject age (p = 0.08), and the third component was associated with gender‐related differences (p = 0.015).

Next, we examined the magnitude and sign of the inflammatory mediator loadings to determine their role in the captured subject differences. For the first component in the PARAFAC model of the full cohort, higher levels of CRP, IL‐6, OPG, TNF‐α and VEGF were associated with older age, while a higher level of RANKL was associated with young age (Figure 2B). In the second component, higher levels of CR, IL‐6,6 and MIP‐1α were tentatively associated with symptomatic status, while higher levels of IL‐10 and IL‐12p70 were tentatively associated with asymptomatic status (Figure 2E). In the third component, higher IL‐12p70, IL‐1β and IL‐4 levels were tentatively associated with control status, while higher RANKL and IL‐1 α levels were tentatively associated with case status.

For the second component in the PARAFAC model of the healthy sub‐cohort, higher levels of OPG, TNF‐α, and CRP were tentatively associated with older age, while higher levels of RANKL were tentatively associated with younger age (Figure 3). In the third component, higher levels of IL‐12p70, IL‐14, IL‐4, and VEGF were associated with a subject being female, whereas higher GM‐CSF levels were associated with a subject being male.

Due to the way in which the inflammatory mediator data was processed (Methods), the largest absolute time loading per component corresponds to the time point where the inter‐subject differences are the largest. Hence, the first component in the PARAFAC model of the full cohort states that age‐related subject differences were maximal at visit 2 and minimal at visit 5 (Figure 2C). Similarly, inter‐subject differences due to symptom status in the second component were largest at visit 6 and minimal at visit 3 when the tooth extraction took place. In the third component, case–control differences were smallest at visit 1 and increased over time. For the PARAFAC model of the healthy sub‐cohort, the second component found that age‐related differences were largest at visit 6, and the third component found that gender‐related differences were maximal at visit 4.

Data Integration Using Advanced Coupled Matrix and Tensor Factorization Reveals Connections Between Inflammatory Mediators in Time and the Extracted Tooth Microbiome at the zOTU and Functional Pathway Level

We investigated host‐microbiome interactions resulting in asymptomatic and symptomatic AP by analysing the microbiome of the extracted tooth together with the inflammatory mediator data using ACTMF (Figure 1C) (Acar et al. 2011). After choosing the most appropriate number of ACMTF components using Factor Match Score for the combined processed extracted tooth microbiome data and processed longitudinal inflammatory mediator data (Figure S16), a one‐component model was found describing 6.1% of the inflammatory mediator data and 19.8% of the extracted tooth microbiome data (Figure 4A,B).

Similar to the PARAFAC models, we tested the correlation between the time‐resolved subject loadings of this component with various clinical parameters (Table 2). This approach revealed that the shared subject mode of the ACMTF model described differences between asymptomatic and symptomatic AP cases (p = 0.042). Inspection of the inflammatory mediator loadings revealed that higher levels of OPG, IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐1β, and IL‐10 were associated with asymptomatic status, while higher levels of RANKL and MIP‐1α were associated with symptomatic status (Figure 4A). Likewise, some members of the microbiome could be associated with asymptomatic and symptomatic AP based on the extracted tooth using the loadings of the model. ZOTUs, with assigned taxonomic names, like Porphyromonas micra, Peptostreptococcaceae, and Parvimonas sp. were associated with symptomatic cases while zOTUs like Streptococcus mutans and Leptotrichia wadei were associated with asymptomatic cases and caused a different inflammatory reaction in time (Figure 4B). The time mode loadings of the inflammatory mediator data showed that the inter‐subject differences were largest at visit 3, when the tooth extraction took place, and subsequently decreased sharply (Figure 4A).

With the established connection between asymptomatic and symptomatic AP and variation in the extracted tooth microbiome data and inflammatory mediators, we also investigated host‐microbiome interactions at the pathway level. We obtained functional predictions of the microbiome using Tax4Fun2 (Aßhauer et al. 2015; Wemheuer et al. 2020). After processing, the functionally predicted microbiome data was integrated with the processed inflammatory mediator data for ACMTF modelling. A one‐component ACMTF model was obtained (Figure S22) describing 6.2% of the variation in the inflammatory mediator data and 30.5% of the variation in the functionally predicted microbiome data describing differences between asymptomatic and symptomatic AP cases (p = 0.024; Table 2).

Enriched KEGG‐pathways were identified using SetRank, which is a functional enrichment algorithm that corrects for multiple pathway membership (Simillion et al. 2017). This revealed that the (members of) three metabolic pathways were associated with asymptomatic cases (Table S1). The Degradation of Aromatic Compounds pathway, comprising 215 components, was the most significantly enriched, with a corrected p‐value of 2.67e‐28 (Table S1). This suggests that aromatic compound metabolism is encoded by bacteria markedly more present in asymptomatic cases. Similarly, the Benzoate Degradation pathway, with a pathway size of 106, exhibited significant enrichment in asymptomatic cases, with a corrected p‐value of 1.51e‐8. These findings highlight the potential protective role of aromatic compound degradation in preventing symptomatic progression. Furthermore, the Sphingolipid Signalling pathway, encompassing 82 components, was significantly enriched in asymptomatic cases (corrected p‐value 2.22e‐2).

Discussion

With this study, we applied multi‐way modelling to compare the inflammatory response in subjects with and without apical periodontitis (AP). We took underlying patterns and relationships that might not have been captured by previous linear analysis into account (Georgiou, van der Waal, et al. 2023), like the incorporation of age‐related factors. We also tried to identify patterns in the healthy individual groups and in the AP subjects separately. In addition, we modelled this response in combination with the microbiome and the functional potential of the extracted teeth with AP. By applying advanced modelling techniques such as PARAFAC and ACMTF, we were able to capture complex, time‐resolved interactions between inflammatory mediators and microbial composition—patterns that would not have been visible through traditional univariate analyses. These approaches helped us identify not only the dominant sources of variation but also shared patterns between microbial and host immune factors, providing a more integrated view of disease processes in apical periodontitis.

We believe that this work contributes biologically by advancing our understanding of how systemic immune responses are shaped by local endodontic infections, and clinically by supporting the development of biomarker‐informed diagnostics and improved risk assessment strategies.

When modelling the inflammatory response in the cases and control subjects using PARAFAC, the cases were separated from controls, based mainly on IL‐4, which was higher in the circulating blood of apical periodontitis subjects (Figure 2H). IL‐4 acts directly on T‐cells, which are one of the important types of white blood cells of the immune system and play a central role in the adaptive immune system (Hahn and Ghoreschi 2017). IL‐4 induces a wide‐spectrum intracellular signalling cascade in CD8+ T‐cells and is critical for the development of protective memory responses against antigens that are constantly presented to the immune system at the apex (Freire et al. 2021). Since it is one of the initiators, quite high in the cascade of the immune response, it is logical that it differentiates the case and control groups. IL‐4, according to an animal study, plays an important role in containing the bone destruction, and its activity contributes to host protection due to its anti‐osteoclastogenic action (Freire et al. 2021). However, IL‐4 is increasingly recognised as a pleiotropic cytokine with both pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory functions, depending on the cellular context and disease state.

In the context of chronic inflammation and wound repair, IL‐4 plays a complex role by modulating immune responses, promoting alternative macrophage activation, and influencing tissue remodelling (Pan et al. 2025).

In the full‐cohort PARAFAC model (Figure 2), the first component was strongly associated with age and included a notable interplay between several immune markers: CRP, TNF‐α, and VEGF. These markers form a cascade of immune responses that evolve as individuals age, with CRP and RANKL levels increasing with age, while TNF‐α and VEGF also show significant age‐related variation. The second component distinguishes between symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. Symptomatic individuals exhibited higher levels of CRP, suggesting an elevated inflammatory response, alongside lower levels of OPG and higher levels of RANKL, indicating a potential imbalance in bone metabolism and inflammatory processes. In the PARAFAC model of the control sub‐cohort (Figure 3), the third component reveals gender‐specific differences in immune response markers such as IL‐12p70, but also to a lesser degree IL‐14, IL‐4, and VEGF, suggesting that male and female immune systems may respond differently to stimuli, potentially influencing susceptibility and progression of inflammatory conditions.

The combination of microbial composition with immune markers in the ACMTF model (Figure 4), such as OPG and RANKL, highlights the primary differences between the groups. Asymptomatic individuals tended to have higher OPG levels and lower RANKL levels, whereas symptomatic individuals exhibit the opposite pattern. The correlated behaviour of inflammatory mediators IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐8, and IL‐10 indicates what is already known: they operate in a coordinated manner during immune responses (Kleiner et al. 2013). These findings underscore the complexity of the immune response and its modulation by age, symptomatic status, and gender in the AP cases. The interplay between various inflammatory mediators and markers, such as CRP, RANKL, OPG, and VEGF, provides a deeper understanding of the underlying mechanisms, and may be paving the way to understanding the systemic consequences of oral diseases.

From the different models in the different groups, we noticed that age was an important factor describing the immunologic profile of both cases and healthy controls. The markers CRP, RANKL, and TNF‐α were mainly affected by age. Aging plays a crucial role in influencing the onset and progression of various diseases (Farooqui and Farooqui 2009; Cham et al. 2021). TNF‐α is an early mediator of the acute‐phase response and is involved in the production of chemokines, IL‐6, and CRP as well as the recruitment of leucocytes during inflammatory reactions (Bruunsgaard et al. 2000). Just like in cancer and degenerative disorders, age is a recognised factor in inflammatory responses to bacteria as in apical periodontitis. This increased susceptibility with age is attributed to physiological changes known as immunosenescence, wherein there is a decline in immune competence (Franceschi et al. 2018). Immunosenescence manifests as alterations in cellular immune function and inflammatory responses to infections (Nikolich‐Zugich 2018). These responses can have both protective effects, by mediating immunity, and detrimental effects, by contributing to immunopathology, due to dysregulation and inappropriate production of inflammatory mediators and chemokines (Kleiner et al. 2013). Additionally, the complex interactions between age‐related alterations affecting various aspects of the immune system are not fully understood. It is unclear which changes are primary, resulting directly from aging, and which are secondary, adaptive, or compensatory responses to primary changes. These results should be taken into consideration when conducting research on the effect of apical periodontitis on blood inflammatory mediators.

When including the presence of symptoms in the ACMTF models, a difference was found between symptomatic and asymptomatic cases, mainly in the higher levels of CRP and OPG in symptomatic cases. Higher levels of CRP in the blood have been associated with higher pain sensitivity ratings at pain threshold and tolerance in a study with twins (Afari et al. 2011). In another study, the levels of CRP were higher in subjects with symptomatic AP compared to asymptomatic AP (Haug and Marthinussen 2019). Additionally, the levels of OPG have been related to the existence of pain in diseases related to bone destruction, like bone cancer (Clohisy and Mantyh 2004). It is unclear, however, how osteoclastic bone resorption causes pain. Osteoclasts themselves may be responsible for nociceptive stimulation, which could reflect osteoclast‐mediated direct injury to nociceptive neurons within bone or, alternatively, could reflect exposure of acid‐sensing neuronal channels to the acidic pH of the osteoclast resorption bay (Clohisy and Mantyh 2004). A second possibility is that the osteoclast‐mediated bone loss decreases the mechanical integrity at the root tip, resulting in mechanical nociceptor stimulation.

There was a difference in the levels of mainly OPG and RANKL depending on the abundance of different bacteria described in the ACMTF model (Figure 4). ZOTUs, assigned taxonomic names such as Parvimonas micra , Bacteroidales, Solobacterium moorei , Mogibacterium, and Fretibacterium have been found in higher abundance in symptomatic AP cases and at the same time at higher levels of OPG. This relationship has been observed before in histological studies done in lesions (Takahama Jr. et al. 2018). It is worth stating that species related commonly with marginal periodontitis are associated with higher levels of osteoclastic activity (Iniesta et al. 2023). However, species like Streptococcus mutans and Bifidobacterium dentium that are less proteolytic and metabolise more sugars or carbohydrates (Huang et al. 2022) are related to asymptomatic apical periodontitis and lower levels of OPG. These results are in agreement with the results of our previous analysis (Georgiou, van der Waal, et al. 2023), but are in this article enriched with information about the effect of specific bacteria in AP in the levels of bone metabolism cytokines.

The functional analysis revealed that three pathways were enriched in asymptomatic cases; namely degradation of aromatic compounds, benzoate degradation, and sphingolipid signalling (Figure S23–S25). The degradation of benzoate was found to be upregulated in asymptomatic cases. This seems counterintuitive as we would expect that asymptomatic cases would be the result of immunosuppression. It has been reported that immunologically stimulated THP‐1 cells in the presence of sodium benzoate secreted reduced levels of IL‐6 and IL‐1β and higher levels of TNF, while other inflammatory mediators, such as IP‐10 and IL‐8 remained unaffected (Ribeiro 2018). The inhibitory effect on IL‐6 and IL‐1β secretion was a consequence of a free radical scavenging characteristic of sodium benzoate, which neutralises the reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated downstream of Toll‐like receptor activation (Ribeiro 2018). It has been shown that sphingolipids and their metabolic products contribute to inflammation induced by endoplasmic reticulum stress, independent of the well‐known vitamin D receptor‐dependent mechanism (Mohammed et al. 2023). The sphingolipid signalling pathway was also enriched in the asymptomatic infection (Figure S24). Sphingolipids drive neurodegeneration and could be the reason that there are no symptoms in this case (Hannun and Obeid 2018). In the future, more research is needed in order to understand the functional capacity and functions of the microorganisms in the different types of AP by performing metagenome shotgun and metatranscriptome sequencing (Georgiou, Brandt, and van der Waal 2023).

The different (PARAFAC) models revealed a difference in the immune system between men and women in both the case and the controls group, specifically for IL‐12p70, GM‐CSF, IL‐10, and VEGF. These results further support the effect of the sex hormones on the immune system which were unexplored in our simpler previous analysis (Georgiou, Twisk, et al. 2023; Georgiou, van der Waal, et al. 2023). Oestrogen, progesterone, and testosterone significantly influence immune responses (El‐Etr et al. 2011). Oestrogen, for instance, tends to enhance the production of anti‐inflammatory mediators, like IL‐10, while testosterone has more complex and variable effects on inflammatory mediator production (Kumar and Goyal 2021). These hormones modulate the activity of various immune cells, leading to differences in inflammatory mediator levels (Kumar and Goyal 2021). Additionally, sex hormones modulate neutrophil development and function, with young adult women exhibiting an activated/mature neutrophil profile characterised by enhanced type‐I IFN pathway activity, proinflammatory responses, and distinct immunometabolism compared to young men (Schröder et al. 1998). Furthermore, the distribution and activity of different immune cells, such as T cells, B cells, and macrophages, vary between men and women (Brown and Su 2019). These differences in cell populations can influence the levels of inflammatory mediators produced (Kumar and Goyal 2021). For example, women tend to have higher numbers of certain T‐cell subtypes, which can affect inflammatory mediator production profiles (Klein and Flanagan 2016). Differences in metabolism and nutritional status between men and women can also affect immune function and inflammatory mediator levels. For instance, adipose tissue, which is generally higher in women, can produce pro‐inflammatory mediators and influence overall inflammatory mediator levels. Finally, in women, the menstrual cycle causes fluctuations in hormone levels, particularly oestrogen and progesterone, which can modulate immune function and inflammatory mediator production (Khosravisamani et al. 2014).

We used PARAFAC and ACMTF models to describe the time‐resolved variation in our datasets. PARAFAC is well‐suited to identify the largest amount of time‐resolved variation in a multi‐way dataset like our longitudinal blood inflammatory mediator data. However, this method fails to significantly describe differences between asymptomatic and symptomatic subjects as this type of variation is smaller than other biological differences like age and gender. Based on earlier work (Georgiou, van der Waal, et al. 2023), it was clear that the differences between asymptomatic and symptomatic subjects was much clearer in the microbiome of the extracted tooth. Using ACMTF, we could expand the modelled differences to the longitudinal blood inflammatory mediator data and indeed found that these differences only account for 7% of the variation in that dataset. However, more than one stable component could not be found and differences related to age and gender were not modelled with this approach. We report both modelling approaches in this work to give a full overview of the variation that exists, both shared and distinct, between the longitudinal blood inflammatory mediator dataset and the extracted tooth microbiome dataset.

Conclusions

Key inflammatory mediators, particularly those involved in bone metabolism, emerged as distinguishing factors between AP patients and healthy controls, underscoring their role in regulating immune responses. Age was a major determinant of systemic inflammation, influencing markers such as CRP, TNF‐α, and VEGF, and highlighting the evolving nature of immune function across the lifespan. This suggests that systemic immune profiles vary across age groups, which may be relevant for understanding disease progression and tailoring patient care in the future. Gender‐related differences further suggest differential susceptibility to inflammatory dysregulation. Importantly, we observed robust associations between specific inflammatory profiles and distinct microbial compositions, along with enrichment of metabolic pathways such as sphingolipid signalling. Together, these findings provide a more integrated view of the immune and microbial interactions in AP, offering insights into the systemic impact of oral infections and potential targets for improved diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.
