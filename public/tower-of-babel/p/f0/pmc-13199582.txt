Post-translational modification signature shapes the tumor immune microenvironment and predicts clinical outcomes in melanoma
Authors: Tianmei Lin, Biao Fu, Xia Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-translational modifications (PTMs) play a pivotal role in the initiation and progression of melanoma. To systematically evaluate their clinical significance, we integrated 10 widely used machine learning algorithms and 101 combinations to construct a PTM-related risk score model (PTMRS) based on 54 prognostic PTM genes. The model demonstrated robust prognostic predictive performance and stability across multiple melanoma cohorts (TCGA, GSE19234, GSE22153, GSE54467, and GSE65904). Immune profiling revealed that patients in the low-PTMRS group exhibited a more active immune microenvironment, characterized by increased infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells and M1 macrophages, along with reduced immune exclusion scores. Single-cell analysis further indicated that melanoma cells had the highest PTMRS scores, and distinct cell–cell communication patterns were observed between high and low PTMRS groups. ALG3, identified as a key gene positively correlated with PTMRS, was associated with poor prognosis and an immunosuppressive state. Immunohistochemistry and RT-qPCR results supported its potential oncogenic role in melanoma. Moreover, PTMRS was correlated with drug sensitivity across multiple compounds, suggesting its utility in guiding personalized therapeutic strategies. Collectively, the PTMRS model serves not only as an independent prognostic indicator for melanoma patients but also as a valuable tool for immune landscape assessment and targeted treatment decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04477-4.Keywords: Melanoma, Post-translational modifications, ALG3, Machine learning, Single cell sequencing

Introduction

Melanoma is a highly aggressive malignancy derived from melanocytes, which originate from the neural crest and are responsible for pigment production [1, 2]. Although melanoma accounts for only about 1% of all skin cancers, it leads to more than 80% of skin cancer-related mortality and is the most lethal form of skin cancer [3]. According to histological features, melanoma can be divided into multiple subtypes, including superficial spreading melanoma (SSM), acral melanoma, mucosal melanoma, and uveal melanoma, and each subtype varies significantly in population distribution, clinical presentation, and molecular characteristics [4, 5]. For early melanoma, prompt resection is usually curative and has a better prognosis; however, once distant metastases occur, the prognosis of patients deteriorates significantly, with historical data showing 5-year survival rates of only 10% to 30% [6–8]. Over the past decade, the treatment landscape for advanced melanoma has revolutionized with the use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting CTLA-4 and PD-1 and targeted therapies against BRAF/MEK mutations, significantly prolonging overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in some patients [9, 10]. However, long-term survival remains available for only a subset of patients and durable remission has not been universally achieved. Therefore, the exploration of curative strategies urgently needs to further understand the biological characteristics of melanoma, the interaction between tumor and host, and the mechanism of therapeutic resistance, which also prompts people to shift their research focus to a variety of novel molecular mechanisms including protein post-translational modifications to reveal their potential role in tumor progression and immune escape.

Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) refer to covalent modifications of specific amino acid residues that can significantly change the conformation, stability, subcellular localization of proteins and their interaction with other molecules after the completion of protein synthesis, thereby regulating protein function [11]. Studies have shown that more than 450 PTMs have been identified and play a key role in regulating cellular signal transduction, metabolic status and immune response [12]. In particular, in cancer, PTMs not only participate in multiple pathways regulating tumor development, progression and metastasis, but also play an important role in the remodeling process of the tumor immune microenvironment (TME). For example, phosphorylation affects tumor cell proliferation and survival by activating MAPK/ERK, PI3K/AKT and other signaling pathways; while ubiquitination and deubiquitination are involved in regulating protein degradation and affect tumor cell cycle and immune escape [13–15]. In recent years, novel acylation modifications (such as succinylation, lactate acylation, β-hydroxybutyrylation, etc.) have gradually become a research hotspot and have received attention because of their special roles in metabolic reprogramming and immune regulation. It has been confirmed that lactylation can promote tumor immune escape by stabilizing PD-L1 expression, and succinylation can also affect anti-tumor immune responses by regulating T cell function [16–18]. Although the role of PTMs in solid tumors such as non-small cell lung cancer and breast cancer has been well reported, their systematic studies in melanoma are still lacking. Melanoma is a highly heterogeneous and aggressive skin tumor, and its complex immune microenvironment and variable response to immunotherapy make it particularly important to identify key immune regulators. PTM may play an important role in regulating the immune escape mechanism of melanoma as a key bridge between the metabolic status of tumor cells and immune function. A study on lung adenocarcinoma found that learning profiles constructed based on multiple PTM patterns identify key immune rejection factors, suggesting that PTMs may be novel targets for regulating immune cell infiltration and response to therapy [19].

Therefore, systematic dissection of the distribution characteristics of PTMs in melanoma and their impact on the tumor microenvironment is expected to provide theoretical basis and practical support for predicting immunotherapy efficacy, identifying potential biomarkers, and developing novel targeting strategies.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and processing of transcriptomic data

In this study, RNA expression profile data and corresponding clinical information of melanoma patients (n = 455) were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database for model construction and preliminary validation [20]. All RNA sequencing data were uniformly converted to TPM format and further log2-transformed for subsequent analyses. To validate the stability and accuracy of the model, four additional melanoma microarray datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database were included as external validation cohorts, comprising GSE19234 (n = 44) [21], GSE22153 (n = 54) [22], GSE54467 (n = 79) [23], and GSE65904 (n = 210) [24], totaling 842 samples including the TCGA data. Microarray data were normalized prior to analysis using the normalizeBetweenArrays function from the limma package.

Moreover, seven melanoma datasets related to immunotherapy were incorporated for further validation, including Melanoma_GSE100797 (n = 25) [25], Melanoma_GSE93157 (n = 25) [26], Melanoma_GSE91061 (n = 109) [27], Melanoma_phs000452 (n = 153), Melanoma_Nathanson_2017 (n = 24), Melanoma_PRJEB23709 (n = 91), and IMvigor210 (n = 348). To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between melanoma and normal tissues, we integrated TCGA melanoma data (1 normal, 472 tumor samples) with 812 normal skin samples from the GTEx database. Batch effects arising from integrating data from different sources were corrected using the Combat function in the sva package to ensure data consistency and comparability [28].

Acquisition and processing of single-cell transcriptomic data

The single-cell RNA sequencing data used in this study were obtained from two GEO datasets: GSE215120 (containing 4 melanoma samples) and GSE217707 (containing 7 melanoma samples) [29–31]. All data analyses were performed using R, primarily with the Seurat package. Quality control was applied using the following filters: mitochondrial gene content < 20% (25% for GSE217707), hematopoietic gene content < 3%, UMI counts per cell between 200 and 30,000, and gene counts between 200 and 7,000 (200–6000 for GSE217707). Data normalization was carried out using the NormalizeData function, and highly variable genes were identified with FindVariableFeatures (nfeatures = 2000). The ScaleData function was used to regress out cell cycle effects (vars.to.regress = c(“S.Score”, “G2M.Score”)). Batch effects were corrected using the Harmony algorithm. For downstream analysis, dimensionality reduction was performed via UMAP and clustering via the Louvain algorithm, both implemented in Seurat. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using FindAllMarkers with thresholds of p < 0.05, log2 fold change > 0.25, and expression in > 10% of cells within the cluster.

Cell annotation analysis

To annotate cell types, this study referenced well-established marker genes to identify major cell subpopulations. Specifically, the markers used included: melanocytes (DCT, PAX3); fibroblasts (DCN, THY1, COL1A1, COL1A2); endothelial cells (PECAM1, CLDN5, FLT1, RAMP2); T cells (CD3D, CD3E, CD3G, TRAC); NK cells (NKG7, GNLY, NCAM1, KLRD1); B cells (CD79A, IGHM, IGHG3, IGHA2); plasma cells (JCHAIN); myeloid cells (LYZ, MARCO, CD68, FCGR3A); and mast cells (KIT, MS4A2, GATA2).

Based on the expression profiles of these marker genes, clusters were annotated accordingly. The distribution and characteristic expression patterns of each cell subpopulation were further visualized using UMAP dimensionality reduction plots, bar plots, and heatmaps.

Acquisition of prognosis-related genes

First, differential expression analysis between tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues was performed using the limma package, with screening criteria of |log2 Fold Change (logFC)| > 0.5 and p-value < 0.05 [32, 33]. Subsequently, genes related to post-translational modifications (PTMs) were extracted from the differentially expressed genes. The candidate genes were further subjected to univariate Cox regression analysis to identify those significantly associated with patient prognosis (p < 0.05). Ultimately, 54 PTM-related genes significantly correlated with prognosis were obtained and used to construct the post-translational modification related signature.

Construction of tumor-related risk signature

In this study, a prognostic risk scoring model was developed using an ensemble of 101 machine learning algorithms to generate individualized risk scores. The optimal cutoff value for risk stratification was determined using the surv_cutpoint function, allowing patients in the TCGA and external validation cohorts to be classified into high- and low-risk groups. The model’s prognostic performance and robustness were subsequently evaluated across multiple datasets.

Construction of risk signature based on integrated machine learning methods

To develop a prognostic model with high accuracy and stability, termed PTMRS, we integrated 10 commonly used machine learning algorithms, forming 101 algorithm combinations. The integrated algorithms included: Random Survival Forest (RSF) [34], Elastic Net (Enet) [35], Lasso regression [36], Ridge regression [37], Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, Partial Least Squares for Cox (plsRcox) [38], Supervised Principal Components (SuperPC) [39], Gradient Boosting Model (GBM) [40], and Survival Support Vector Machine (Survival-SVM) [41]. Step 1: Determine the parameters of the foundation model by using hyperparameter grid search. Step 2: The prediction results of the Cox model, random forest, and algorithms are used as meta-features, and logistic regression is input to generate the final score. Step 3: Calculate the stability score of each base model through cross-validation, and dynamically adjust the integration weight.

Cell–cell communication analysis

To investigate potential intercellular communication networks, we analyzed single-cell transcriptomic data using the CellChat package [42]. Normalized gene expression matrices were imported to create CellChat objects. Data were then preprocessed using default parameters with the functions identifyOverExpressedGenes, identifyOverExpressedInteractions, and projectData to identify significantly expressed ligand–receptor pairs. Communication probabilities between cell types were computed using computeCommunProb, and low-probability or non-significant interactions were filtered with filterCommunication. Activated signaling pathways and key communication routes were identified using computeCommunProbPathway. Finally, aggregateNet was used to integrate ligand–receptor interactions and construct a comprehensive intercellular communication network, highlighting signaling patterns and their potential roles in the tumor microenvironment.

Mutation analysis

In this study, GISTIC 2.0 was used to analyze copy number variations (CNVs) and identify significant genomic amplifications or deletions [43]. Mutation data were processed with the R package maftools to calculate tumor mutation burden (TMB) for each patient, enabling assessment of mutation levels and their association with model risk scores [44].

Correlation analysis between the prognostic model and tumor immune microenvironment

To explore the association between the prognostic model and the tumor microenvironment (TME), immune cell infiltration in the TCGA cohort was assessed using the IOBR R package [45]. IOBR integrates seven established algorithms—xCell, MCP-counter, quanTIseq, CIBERSORT, CIBERSORT-ABS, TIMER, and EPIC—to estimate the relative abundance of immune cell types within the TME. Heatmaps based on these results were generated to visualize immune infiltration patterns, enabling direct comparison of immune landscapes between high- and low-risk groups and providing immunological insights supporting the prognostic model.

Drug sensitivity analysis

To assess the association between risk scores and drug sensitivity, the oncoPredict R package was used to estimate the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) and area under the curve (AUC) for common anticancer drugs [46]. Drug response data were sourced from the GDSCv2 and CTRP databases. Predicted sensitivities were compared between high- and low-risk groups using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, aiming to identify candidate drugs with selective efficacy and support personalized treatment strategies.

Immunotherapy response prediction

The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) algorithm was used to predict immunotherapy response in TCGA patients. TIDE scores were calculated to evaluate immune evasion and exclusion levels, enabling comparison of predicted immunotherapy efficacy between high- and low-risk groups. This analysis highlights the potential association between the prognostic model and immunotherapy response.

Cell culture

In this study, normal human epidermal keratinocytes NHEK and human melanoma cell line A375 were used. Both cell types were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Takara, Japan), followed by reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene to quantify the relative expression level of ALG3 by real-time quantitative PCR. Primer sequences are detailed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

All data processing, statistical analyses, and figure generation were conducted using R software (version 4.1.3). Pearson correlation was used for continuous variable associations, and chi-square tests for categorical comparisons. Group differences in continuous variables were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Optimal cutoff values were determined via the survminer package. Survival analyses, including univariate and multivariate Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier curves, were performed using the survival package. All tests were two-sided, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Result

Screening of PTM-associated genes and prognostic association analysis

To investigate the potential prognostic value of PTM-associated genes in melanoma, we first integrated and normalized the TCGA versus GTEx datasets, correcting the data for batch effects to ensure comparability between data from different sources (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Following completion of data preprocessing, we identified differentially expressed genes between tumor tissue and adjacent non-cancerous tissue, from which genes associated with PTM were selected for subsequent analysis (Fig. 1A). Next, we assessed the prognostic value of these PTM-associated genes using univariate Cox regression analysis (with p < 0.05 as a significant criterion), and finally selected 54 PTM genes that were significantly associated with overall survival (Fig. 1B; Supplementary Fig. 1B). To further understand the potential role of these genes in biological function, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on them. The results showed that these genes were mainly enriched in key pathways such as protein polyubiquitination, I -kappaB kinase/NF-kappaB signaling, ubiquitin-like protein activity transferase, and ubiquitin—protein activity transferase (Fig. 1C, D), suggesting that they may play an important role in tumor immune escape, inflammatory signaling, and regulation of cell homeostasis. In addition, we showed the distribution characteristics of these prognostically relevant PTM genes on chromosomes by Circos plots, revealing their localization and distribution trends at the genomic level (Fig. 1E). After ensuring that all datasets were complete with batch effect removal, we further performed principal component analysis (PCA), which showed good discrimination in dimensionality reduction space for different groups of samples, further validating the rationality of data processing and the reliability of subsequent analysis (Fig. 1F). Finally, we evaluated CNV at the genomic level for these 54 genes. By constructing visual waterfall plots, the CNV gain and deletion distribution patterns of each gene in patients are visually presented, which provides important genomics support for the subsequent construction of robust prognostic models (Fig. 1G).

To construct and validate the predictive value of PTMRS model in melanoma prognosis and immunotherapy

In order to develop a prognostic scoring model with high accuracy and stability, Post-Translational Modification-Related Signature (PTMRS), we comprehensively used 10 mainstream machine learning algorithms and constructed a total of 101 algorithm combination models. Based on the 54 PTM genes that were significantly associated with prognosis, we comparatively assessed the performance of different models in each dataset and finally determined that the model constructed with the SuperPC algorithm performed best in the C-index score and was thus selected as the final model (Fig. 2A). The survival analysis results in multiple melanoma datasets indicated that patients with lower PTMRS scores had significantly better overall survival (OS) in the TCGA, GSE19234, GSE22153, GSE54467, and GSE65904 datasets, verifying the good robustness and broad applicability of model (Fig. 2B–F). Further, we applied the model to seven publicly available immunotherapy datasets to demonstrate that patients with lower PTMRS scores also had a more significant survival advantage, who may be more sensitive to immunotherapy (Fig. 2G–M). We introduced an immunogenicity score index (IPS) to analyze the differences between PTMRS groups in terms of immune response potential. It showed that the low PTMRS group scored higher across multiple IPS dimensions, indicating a greater likelihood of benefiting from immunotherapy (Fig. 2N–Q). These results suggest that PTMRS can not only be used as an effective predictor of prognosis in melanoma patients, but also has the potential to predict the response to immunotherapy and has important clinical application prospects in the development of precise treatment strategies [47].

To verify the independence and comprehensive predictive ability of PTMRS model

We systematically analyzed PTMRS performance in terms of C-index with traditional clinical features (age, gender, stage, pN, and pT) in GSE19234, GSE22153, GSE54467, GSE65904, and TCGA cohorts (Fig. 3A). Among these, PTMRS showed better or no worse predictive ability than traditional clinical features in all datasets with strong independent prognostic value. Further, PCA and ROC curves showed that the high and low risk groups were clearly distributed in the PCA dimension, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) reported the good discriminatory ability of the model (Fig. 3B, C), further verifying the utility of the PTMRS model in melanoma prognosis prediction. MSigDB gene set functional enrichment analysis was performed to assess the close association of PTMRS with innate immune processes and that immune-related pathways such as NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and Leishmania infection were significantly activated in low PTMRS group (Supplementary Fig. 2A–G). Finally, a comparative analysis of C-index values with 23 prognostic score models reported in the literature showed that PTMRS was stable in most datasets, and the overall ranking was at a moderate and upper level, taking into account the prediction efficacy and model simplicity, which had good potential for clinical promotion (Fig. 3D).

Association analysis of PTMRS with tumor immune microenvironment, genomic variation and immune response

To investigate the relationship between PTMRS and the tumor immune microenvironment of melanoma in depth, we first assessed immune cell infiltration in tumor samples using CIBERSORT and TIDE algorithms and compared the differences between high and low PTMRS groups (Fig. 4A–F). The results showed that there were significant differences in immune cell composition between the two groups. Samples from the low PTMRS group were enriched for more immune activating cell types, such as M1 macrophages, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and IFNG expression levels; while the high PTMRS group showed higher immune rejection scores (Exclusion Score) and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) levels, suggesting that it may have a stronger immune escape ability. Further analysis of the relationship between PTMRS and tumor immunity and immunotherapy-related pathways revealed that PTMRS was significantly positively correlated with DNA damage repair pathways such as homologous recombination repair, mismatch repair, and nucleotide excision repair based on the TIP database (Supplementary Fig. 2H), indicating that it may be closely related to tumor immunotherapy responses. Subsequently, differences in the expression of immune-related genes (including MHC I, MHC II, other MHC, corepressors, and costimulators) and their correlation with PTMRS were compared between the high and low PTMRS groups, and the results showed that these key immunoregulatory genes were generally highly expressed in the low PTMRS group, and most of them were negatively correlated with PTMRS, further supporting a stronger immune activation status in the low PTMRS group (Fig. 4G, H). In order to verify the accuracy of immune cell infiltration analysis, we used seven mainstream immune infiltration assessment methods, and the results showed that each method had a high consistency of immune cell discrimination in the high and low PTMRS groups, demonstrating the reliability of immune infiltration results (Fig. 4I). In addition, representative pathological sections showed a tighter distribution of histiocytes in the low PTMRS group and also showed stronger immunoreactive features morphologically (Fig. 4J).

At the genomic level, we further compared CNV status in the high and low PTMRS groups. Following analysis of CNV segment data from TCGA using GISTIC2.0 software, visual waterfall plots were drawn to demonstrate CNV gain versus deletion distribution patterns in patients in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). In the mutation analysis, we evaluated the TCGA mutation data using maftools software and found that TTN versus MUC16 was the gene with the highest mutation frequency in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 3C). Further CNV burden analysis revealed that patients in the high PTMRS group scored higher on Focal Gain Load and Focal Loss Load, while patients in the low PTMRS group had more advantage on Broad Gain Load and Broad Loss Load, revealing significant differences in genomic structural variants between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Finally, we calculated TMB values for each patient and combined PTMRS risk groupings to divide samples into four subtypes for survival analysis. The findings revealed that patients exhibiting both high TMB and low PTMRS showed the most favorable survival prognosis, implying that combining PTMRS with TMB can more effectively predict responses to immunotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 3E).

Single-cell analysis reveals the heterogeneity and intercellular communication features of PTMRS across different cell types

To further investigate the cellular heterogeneity and potential biological mechanisms of PTMRS within the melanoma microenvironment, we performed an in-depth analysis using two high-quality single-cell RNA sequencing datasets. In the GSE215120 dataset, six major cell types were annotated: Endothelial cells, T/NK cells, Melanoma cells, B cells, Fibroblasts, and Myeloid cells (Fig. 5A). By projecting the PTMRS scores onto the single-cell level, we found that Melanoma cells exhibited significantly higher PTMRS scores compared to other cell types, suggesting that they may be the main driving population of the high PTMRS state (Fig. 5B). Further analysis revealed that high PTMRS cells were predominantly clustered within the Melanoma cell population, whereas low PTMRS cells were mainly composed of T/NK cells (Fig. 5C), indicating that immune cells reside in a relatively low PTM-related risk state.

In the GSE217707 dataset, we identified nine cell populations, including Melanoma, T/NK, MAST, Myeloid, Neutrophil, Endothelial, B, Fibroblast, and Plasma cells (Fig. 5D). Consistent with the previous dataset, Melanoma cells again displayed the highest PTMRS scores in this dataset (Fig. 5E), while T/NK cells showed significantly lower PTMRS scores, reflecting a similar trend (Fig. 5F). The distribution of PTMRS scores across different cell populations is illustrated in Fig. 5G and H, further confirming the heterogeneity of PTMRS among various cell types.

Subsequently, we performed cell-cell communication analysis on the two single-cell datasets to explore the intercellular signaling patterns under different PTMRS levels (Fig. 5I). In the high PTMRS group, Melanoma cells exhibited the strongest communication activity, followed by Fibroblasts and Endothelial cells, suggesting highly active interactions between tumor cells and their stromal counterparts (Fig. 5J and L). In contrast, within the low PTMRS group, Fibroblasts and Endothelial cells became the most communicative, while Melanoma cells showed relatively diminished communication activity (Fig. 5K and M), which may reflect a scenario where immune and non-tumor cells play a dominant role in maintaining microenvironmental homeostasis under low-risk conditions.

Notably, we also observed marked differences in the outgoing signaling patterns of Melanoma cells under high and low PTMRS states. Specifically, in the low PTMRS group, Melanoma cells, in addition to conventional signaling pathways, communicated with other cells via the ANNEXIN signaling pathway—an additional route not observed in the high PTMRS group (Supplementary Fig. 4). This finding suggests that the PTMRS score not only reflects the transcriptional features of tumor cells themselves but may also influence their communication patterns with surrounding immune cells, thereby potentially impacting immune responses or immune evasion.

Role of ALG3 in melanoma and its association with the PTMRS model

To further explore potential molecular regulators underlying the PTMRS model, we performed correlation analysis between the PTMRS score and the expression levels of key genes. The results showed a significant positive correlation between PTMRS scores and ALG3 expression (Fig. 6A), suggesting that ALG3 may be involved in PTMRS-driven biological processes. Further prognostic analysis in the TCGA melanoma cohort revealed that patients with high ALG3 expression had significantly worse survival outcomes (Fig. 6B), consistent with the enrichment of ALG3 in the high-PTMRS group. Immunohistochemistry data obtained from the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database demonstrated positive expression of ALG3 in melanoma tumor tissues, whereas its expression was minimal in normal tissues (Figs. 6C, D), indicating that ALG3 may serve as a tumor-specific biomarker involved in melanoma progression.

In immune-related analyses, we systematically evaluated the correlation between ALG3 expression and multiple immune-related gene categories, including immunostimulators, immunoinhibitors, MHC family genes, receptors, and chemokines. The results indicated that higher ALG3 expression was generally associated with lower expression of immune-related genes (Fig. 6E), implying that ALG3 may promote tumor immune evasion by suppressing anti-tumor immune responses. Additionally, RT-qPCR experiments detected ALG3 expression in normal human epidermal keratinocytes NHEK and human melanoma cell line A375. The results showed significantly upregulated ALG3 expression in A375 cells compared to NHEK cells (Fig. 6F), further supporting the role of ALG3 in melanoma pathogenesis.

Association analysis between PTMRS and protein abundance, functional dependency, and drug sensitivity

To further elucidate the relationship between PTMRS scores and functional proteins, we integrated proteomics and gene functional screening data. The analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between PTMRS scores and the expression levels of proteins such as AFG3L2, DDX39B, FOXA1, IGF1R, PMSB5, PMSB6, and TIMM44 (Supplementary Figs. 5 A–H), suggesting that these proteins may act as downstream effectors or co-regulatory factors of PTMRS. Further CERES score analysis demonstrated that knockout of the corresponding genes for these proteins showed a negative correlation with PTMRS scores across various cancer cell lines (Supplementary Fig. 5I–P), indicating that high PTMRS states might depend on the functional maintenance of these essential genes. This implies a potential “addiction” phenotype, offering novel insights into possible targeted therapeutic strategies. Finally, we assessed the association between PTMRS scores and drug sensitivity using two independent drug response databases. Eight compounds, including darinaparsin, UNC0321, and BRD-K51831558, were identified to be significantly negatively correlated with PTMRS scores (Supplementary Fig. 5Q–R), suggesting that these drugs may exhibit enhanced efficacy in tumors with high PTMRS. Additionally, three inhibitors targeting the ERK signaling pathway, such as ERK_6604_1714 and SB505124_1194, also showed a negative correlation with PTMRS (Supplementary Fig. 5Q–R), indicating that ERK signaling may constitute a component of the mechanisms driving high PTMRS.

Collectively, these findings not only highlight ALG3 as a potential key regulator driving PTMRS-mediated immune evasion in melanoma but also uncover multiple protein dependencies and candidate drugs associated with PTMRS scores, providing a theoretical basis and target resources for future precision therapy.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analyzed the prognostic value of genes associated with post-translational modifications (PTM) in melanoma. By integrating TCGA and GTEx data and removing batch effects, we selected 54 PTM genes that were significantly associated with overall survival, and enrichment analysis showed that these genes were mainly involved in key pathways such as protein ubiquitination and NF-kappaB signaling, suggesting that they may play an important role in tumor immune regulation. Protein ubiquitination plays a key role in the development of melanoma by regulating a variety of signaling pathways, especially by affecting processes such as cell proliferation, apoptosis and immune escape, providing a potential new strategy for targeted therapy [48, 49]. Studies have shown that ubiquitin ligases (E3 ligases) and deubiquitinases (DUBs) play a dual role in the growth and progression of melanoma and other skin cancers, and their dysregulation may lead to abnormal survival and metastasis of tumor cells [50, 51]. In addition, the ubiquitin-proteasome system is also directly involved in the regulation of melanin metabolism, affecting melanin synthesis and degradation, and then has important significance in metabolic reprogramming of melanoma [52]. Based on these 54 genes, we applied multiple machine learning algorithms to construct 101 model combinations and finally identified the SuperPC model as optimal. This PTMRS scoring model effectively discriminated patient outcomes across multiple independent cohorts and predicted better survival performance in immunotherapeutic populations, showing good robustness and potential for clinical application.

This study systematically assessed the relationship between PTMRS scores and the immune microenvironment of melanoma. It revealed the molecular stratification of melanoma response to immunotherapy. Existing staging relies on anatomical features but does not reflect molecular heterogeneity. Liquid biopsy combined with PTMs scores may detect recurrence months earlier than imaging. Immuno-infiltration analysis showed that the low PTMRS group was significantly enriched for M1 macrophages, CD8 and CD4 T cells and IFNG expression, suggesting that it had stronger anti-tumor immune activity; while the high PTMRS group showed higher immune rejection scores and CAF levels, which may have more significant immune escape characteristics. Immune-related pathway analysis further showed that PTMRS was positively correlated with DNA repair pathways such as homologous recombination and mismatch repair, suggesting that it may affect immunotherapy response. At the level of immune gene expression, MHC class molecules and costimulatory/inhibitory factors were generally highly expressed in the low PTMRS group, and most of them were negatively correlated with PTMRS, further strengthening its immune activation advantage. Genome-level analysis revealed that although TTN and MUC16 were the main mutated genes in both groups, CNV patterns were significantly different, with the high PTMRS group more biased towards focal amplification versus deletion, while the low PTMRS group showed more extensive chromosomal structural variation. In addition, combined TMB analysis showed that patients with high TMB and low PTMRS had the best prognosis, suggesting that the combination of the two is expected to be an effective predictor of immunotherapy response. At the single cell analysis level, PTMRS scored highest in melanoma cells and lowest in T/NK cells, reflecting significant cell-type heterogeneity. In the high PTMRS state, the communication between tumor cells, fibroblasts and endothelial cells was active, reflecting a strong tumor-matrix interaction; while in the low PTMRS state, the communication center of gravity turned to immune and non-tumor cells, suggesting that the tumor was in a state more susceptible to immune system control. Notably, melanoma cells in the low PTMRS group communicated with other cells via the ANNEXIN pathway, suggesting that there may be unique mechanisms in their immune regulation.

In the key regulator screen, we found that ALG3 was highly positively correlated with PTMRS, and its high expression predicted poor prognosis. Both immunohistochemical and qPCR results verified the specific high expression of ALG3 in melanoma, while it was negatively correlated with multiple classes of immune-related genes, suggesting that it may be involved in tumor immune escape by inhibiting immune pathways. ALG3 is a gene encoding α-1,3-mannosyltransferase involved in the early steps of N-linked glycosylation of proteins [53]. ALG3 has been found to be up-regulated in a variety of cancers and promotes stemness maintenance, radiation tolerance, and malignant progression of tumors by regulating glycosylation modifications of key signaling pathways [54, 55]. In triple-negative breast cancer, ALG3 enhances tumor immune escape by modulating N-linked glycosylation of PD-L1, thereby weakening anti-PD-1 treatment efficacy [56, 57]. While ALG3 promotes malignant phenotype primarily through the JAK2/STAT3 pathway in breast cancer, in melanoma, STAT3 activation may be more dependent on glycosylation-modified cytokine IL-6, forming a positive feedback loop of ALG3 and LAG-3 [58]. The co-expression rate of ALG3 and LAG-3 in melanoma was significantly higher than that in breast cancer [59]. In addition, high ALG3 expression was demonstrated to be strongly associated with poor prognosis in lung cancer patients, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker [60]. In lung cancer, ALG3 has weak regulation of the immune microenvironment and plays a more important role by directly promoting the proliferation of cancer cells. ALG3 plays a tumor-promoting role in multiple cancer species, suggesting that it may also be a potential therapeutic target with important functions in melanoma.

Finally, integrome and function-dependent data revealed that PTMRS high expression status depends on functional maintenance of a range of key proteins IGF1R, FOXA1, etc., suggesting a potential “tumorigenic-dependent” mechanism [61]. Drug sensitivity analysis identifies multiple PTMRS-negative drugs darinaparsin, ERK pathway inhibitors, etc., providing potential precision treatment strategies for high-risk patients. Although the model performed well in prognostic assessment, the biological mechanism linking ALG3 to PTM regulation and immune escape is still speculative, and the causal relationship needs to be further verified by functional studies such as knockdown experiments. Immune cell infiltration analysis relies on computational deconvolution methods, and the use of multiple immunohistochemistry or spatial transcriptomics techniques for verification in the future will enhance the robustness of the conclusions. In addition, while TIDE analysis predicts immunotherapy response, direct validation using checkpoint inhibitor cohorts with known treatment response status would be more convincing. Expanding normal tissue representation could improve the accuracy of PTM gene identification.

In summary, PTMRS not only reflects the state and heterogeneity of melanoma immune microenvironment, but also associates genomic variants, immune escape mechanisms and potential therapeutic targets, which has important clinical predictive value and therapeutic guiding significance.

Conclusion

In summary, PTMRS can not only effectively distinguish the immune microenvironment status and genomic characteristics of melanoma patients, but also is closely related to the immune escape mechanism, the expression of key regulators ALG3 and drug sensitivity. It has important potential in immune response prediction and individualized treatment strategy development, providing theoretical basis and targeted support for precise treatment of melanoma.
