Exploring the Oral Microbiome in the Dental Biofilm of Children with Caries from Vulnerable Communities in Cartagena, Colombia: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Luis Fang, Alejandra Herrera-Herrera, Jonathan Harris-Ricardo, Doris Olier-Castillo, Natalia Fortich-Mesa, Vivi Hoyos-Hoyos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundTo explore the oral microbiome in the cariogenic dental biofilm in children from vulnerable communities in Cartagena, Colombia.Materials and methodsWe conducted a prevalence study that included 75 children aged 5–8 from three vulnerable communities in Cartagena, Colombia. Taking into account the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS), calibrated examiners diagnosed all participants with dental caries. Samples of dental biofilm were obtained from carious tooth surfaces; extraction of bacterial DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) was performed, and sequenced V3–V4 region of 16S rRNA (ribosomal ribonucleic acid) gene. Statistical analyses were conducted at the genus and species levels. Microbial diversity was analyzed using the Shannon diversity index.ResultsThe most prevalent bacterial genera identified were Streptococcus (probe 4), Lactobacillus (probe 5), Streptococcus (probe 1), and Fusobacterium (probe 4). The most abundant species identified were Lactobacillus salivarius, Streptococcus mutans, Streptococcus sanguinis, and Leptotrichia sp. HOT 417. A comparison of relative species abundance showed a more significant presence of Parascardovia denticolens, S. mutans, and Streptococcus sobrinus in extensive cavitated carious lesions (p < 0.001). Introduction

Dental caries is defined as an ecological change in oral biofilm and is classified as a multifactorial disease whose pathogenesis involves genetic, environmental, and behavioral factors.1 Oral biofilm may be established over a long period on the surfaces of the teeth, constituting the Sine qua non factor for dental caries.2 The slightest shift in the microbial balance of this biofilm results in a mineral loss that, in time, may lead to the development of cavities on those same surfaces.3 The worldwide prevalence of early childhood caries has been estimated at 48% (95% CI: 45–53), with a similar prevalence observed on the American continent.4 In Colombia, South America, a national survey [Estudio Nacional de Salud Bucal IV (ENSAB IV)]5 reported a national prevalence of dental caries in mixed dentition (ages 5–12) of 47.11%. However, upon implementation of the International Caries Detection and Assessment System (ICDAS) criteria6 for the diagnosis of incipient lesions, national prevalence increased to 86.24%.5 The study suggests that caries prevalence across the country remains high.

Characterizing the oral microbiome will allow us to determine commensal bacteria under different conditions in the oral cavity and their behavior in each individual. Previous studies have reported that Streptococcus mutans is not the only bacterium involved in dental caries, and it implicates a rather complex relationship with other bacterial species.7 In children aged 2–8 from Ohio State [United States (EE.UU.)] Streptococcus sanguinis has been associated with healthy individuals, while Bifidobacterium, S. mutans, Streptococcus parasanguinis, Streptococcus constellatus, Actinomyces gerencseriae, Streptococcus salivarius, Veillonella, and Lactobacillus fermentum have been involved in dental caries.8 Moreover, children aged 6–7 from London (England) showed differences between microorganisms isolated from noncavitated carious lesions; in partially erupted molars with white spot lesions, Streptococcus oralis and S. salivarius were isolated in a more significant proportion, while S. mutans was commonly isolated from fully erupted molars affected by white spot lesions.9 In children aged 3–6 from China, the most common microbes present in carious lesions are Lactobacillus, Veillonella, and Prevotella. At the same time, Actinomyces, Bifidobacterium, and Abiotrophia are found in the healthy teeth of children with caries. Neisseria, Leptotrichia, Porphyromonas, and Gemella are present in children free of dental caries.10 These population differences may be explained by social determinants such as economic status, nutritional patterns, and hygiene habits. For example, caries is associated with low socioeconomic status, families with low economic income, and little education.11

Therefore, the objective of this study was to explore the oral microbiome in the dental biofilm of children with dental caries from vulnerable communities in Cartagena, Colombia.

Materials and Methods

Design

The present research was conducted with a prevalence design with 75 participating children. Sample size was computed using the Epidat v4.0 statistical software, with an expected carious lesion prevalence of 90%,5 a 95% confidence interval, and a 5% error. All subjects were recruited from three public educational institutions in Cartagena (Colombia), located in vulnerable communities in the city. School-aged children (5–8 years) were recruited into the study after agreeing to participate, and the parents gave their written consent. Participants were excluded due to systemic disease, endocrine disorder, diabetes, hematologic disease, autoimmune disease, use of orthodontic appliances, or any antibiotic treatment during the previous 3 months. This study has institutional ethical approval.

Caries Diagnosis

A calibrated examiner detected and diagnosed carious lesions using the visual criteria of the ICDAS6 in conjunction with the lesion activity assessment (LAA) scoring system.12 Teeth were clinically assessed pre and postbiofilm removal. For data analysis, lesions with scores of 1 and 2 were grouped as initial lesions, 3 and 4 as moderate lesions, and 5 and 6 as extensive lesions. The DMFT index, encompassing decayed, missing, and filled teeth, was used to quantify caries experience.

Sample Collection

To standardize sampling, children were instructed not to perform oral hygiene measures during the 8 hours preceding biofilm collection. To determine the participants' oral health, a modified visible plaque index was performed, as described by Silness and Löe.13 Biofilm samples were collected directly from carious lesions using a sterile applicator, transferred to TE [Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid or Tris EDTA] buffer, and preserved at −20°C.

Microbial DNA (Deoxyribonucleic Acid) Extraction

Biofilm samples were resuspended in 150 µL TE buffer supplemented with 1 µL Ready-Lyse Lysozyme solution (Epicentre, R1802M, United States), followed by incubation at 37°C overnight. Each tube was subsequently supplemented with 150 µL of 2× TyC lysis buffer and 1 µL of proteinase K and incubated at 65°C for 30 minutes under constant mixing. After incubation on ice for 3–5 minutes, 175 µL of mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) protein precipitation reagent was added to 300 µL of the lysate and thoroughly vortexed for 10 seconds. After centrifugation at 10,000 × gm for 10 minutes at 4°C, the supernatant was collected in sterile 1.5 mL tubes, combined with 500 µL of isopropanol, thoroughly mixed 30–40 times, maintained on ice for 10 minutes, and subsequently centrifuged at 10,000 × gm at 4°C. Isopropanol was carefully discarded, and the pellet was washed with 500 µL of 75% ethanol by briefly mixing and centrifuging. Ethanol was discarded, and the pellet was washed once again. DNA was reconstituted in 25 µL of TE buffer, quantified spectrophotometrically at 260/280 nm with a MULTISKAN GO® (ThermoFisher Scientific, United States), and preserved at −20°C until processing.

Microbiological Characterization

The microbial composition derived from purified DNA was assessed via next-generation sequencing (Illumina MiSeq System, Illumina, Inc., San Diego, California, United States), using primers directed to the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA (ribosomal ribonucleic acid) gene as previously reported.14 Bad or low (<5,000) reads were removed from analyses using BLAST (basic local alignment search tool), allowing the identification of about 600 different oral bacterial taxa at the same time. For the analysis, 538 oligonucleotide probes ranging from 17 to 40 bases were designed for species-level identification, together with 94 probes specific for bacterial genera. The data are shown in the target taxa's % frequencies (i.e., relative abundance).

Statistical Analysis

Qualitative data were reported as frequency tables, and quantitative data were summarized using mean and standard deviation. Differences among clinical variables, such as caries severity, caries activity, dental surface, and arcade, were evaluated by applying Pearson's χ² test. In addition, the Shannon alpha diversity index was calculated based on the number of readings obtained per sample. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied to assess group differences, considering p values <0.05 as statistically significant.

The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare the presence and relative abundance of bacterial genera and species between the two caries severity groups. The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled by applying Benjamini–Hochberg corrections for multiple testing. An adjusted p value < 0.001 was considered statistically significant for these analyses. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v25 and MeV 4_8_1 software.

Results

Sociodemographic and Clinical Characteristics

This study included 75 children from vulnerable communities, of whom the majority (54.7%) were male, and the average age was 6.84 ± 1.04 years. Participants were distributed in school grades from kindergarten through third grade, exhibited poor oral hygiene according to the Silness and Löe modified plaque index, and an increased prevalence of initial caries was observed (Table 1). The experience of dental caries, measured as the number of DMFT, exhibited a statistically significant difference between initial, moderate, and extensive caries (p = 0.000) (Table 2). Most caries lesions were active on the occlusal surface in the posterior lower arch (Supplementary Table 1).

Oral Microbiome in the Dental Biofilm

Sixty-four genera-specific probes yielded amplified products, which contain information on 47 bacterial genera present in the analyzed oral biofilm (Supplementary Table 2). As shown in the table, the genera Fusobacterium, Gemella, Streptococcus, Veillonella, Actinomyces, and Leptotrichia were identified in 90% of the samples. Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, Fusobacterium, Leptotrichia, Veillonella, and Neisseria were identified as the most prevalent genera in the oral biofilm (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). A total of 369 bacterial species were identified in our samples. Granulicatella adiacens, Rothia mucilaginosa, S. sanguinis, Veillonella dispar, and Corynebacterium matruchotii species were identified in over 90% of the samples (Supplementary Table 5). S. sanguinis, S. mutans, Lactobacillus salivarius, Leptotrichia sp. HOT 417, and Lautropia mirabilis were identified as the most prevalent species in the oral biofilm (Supplementary Table 6).

Oral Microbiome in the Dental Biofilm According to Caries Severity

Statistically significant differences between the relative abundance of bacterial genera and caries severity could not be identified (p > 0.001) (Supplementary Table 7). However, we observed differences mainly in the relative proportion of the Streptococcus probe 4 (Fig. 1A). Also, we observed that Lactobacillus probes 1, 3, and 5 are present only in extensive lesions (Fig. 1B). When analyzing bacterial species with greater relative abundance by carious lesion severity, we found S. mutans, Parascardovia denticolens, and Streptococcus sobrinus species to be significantly associated with severe carious lesions (p < 0.001), and their presence ranged from 25% to 85% (Table 3).

The analyzed oral biofilm samples exhibited a richness of bacterial species, as indicated by the α-Shannon index of 3.02 (SD 0.36). This microbial richness was preserved among study groups and did not show statistically significant differences (p = 0.44) (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to explore the oral microbiome in the dental biofilm of children with dental caries from vulnerable communities in Cartagena, Colombia. 64 genera and 369 bacterial species were identified. S. mutans, P. denticolens, and S. sobrinus species are significantly associated with severe carious lesions.

Regarding oral health, the Silness and Löe modified index showed that most of the study population had poor oral hygiene and caries experience, consistent with other studies conducted in poor or low-income communities with a high prevalence of dental caries.15–17

In this study, we explored and characterized the oral microbiome in dental biofilm samples isolated from carious lesions of varying severity. A total of 64 genera-specific probes containing information on 47 bacterial genera yielded amplified products in our samples. The Fusobacterium, Gemella, Streptococcus, Veillonella, Actinomyces, and Leptotrichia genera were found in over 90% of the analyzed samples. These results are comparable to those reported by Mantzourani et al.18 in which biofilm samples isolated from carious surfaces, in that study, an increase in Fusobacterium, Streptococcus, Actinomyces, Leptotrichia, Prevotella, and Porphyromonas genera was identified. Our study observed Prevotella and Porphyromonas genera in 80% and 81.3%, respectively, suggesting that age may be a relevant factor affecting the oral microbiome profiles of children vs adults. However, further studies are required to confirm these findings.

Changes in oral microbial diversity were evaluated in children with early childhood caries and compared to caries-free children from China. Dental biofilm samples of 12 children aged 8, 14, 20, 26, and 32 months were analyzed using polymerase chain reaction–denaturing gradient gel electrophoresis (PCR-DGGE). The analysis revealed the presence of 21 genera, among which were those more frequently found in both groups (healthy and with caries), including Streptococcus, Capnocytophaga, Leptotrichia, Lautropia, Neisseria, and Porphyromonas, although intergroup differences did not reach statistical significance at the genus level.19 In our study, while we did not include a caries-free control group, we considered the different stages of carious lesions and identified 47 bacterial genera, thus providing a more complete bacterial profile. In a longitudinal study, samples obtained for each participant were analyzed during infancy (3 months) and early childhood (3 years). Streptococcus, Veillonella, and Gemella genera were the most prevalent in those samples.20 Similarly, in addition to identifying those genera, our results also show the presence of Fusobacterium, Actinomyces, and Leptotrichia genera, which may be related to primary microbial colonization during the first months of life.

Regarding bacterial genera relative abundance according to caries severity, we observed a greater abundance of Streptococcus (probe 4), Streptococcus (probe 1), Fusobacterium (probe 4), Veillonella (probe 2), Neisseria (probe 2), and Leptotrichia (probe 3) in biofilm samples isolated from surfaces with initial caries. On the other hand, moderate carious lesions exhibited greater abundance of Streptococcus (probe 4), Streptococcus (probe 1), Neisseria (probe 2), Leptotrichia (probe 3), Actinomyces (probe 3), and Veillonella (probe 2). In severe carious lesions, the following genera were observed: Streptococcus (probe 4), Streptococcus (probe 1), Leptotrichia (probe 3), Lactobacillus (probe 5), Veillonella (probe 2), and Fusobacterium (probe 4). It is worth pointing out that Streptococcus probe 4 collects data on the following species: australis, cristatus, infantis, mitis, mitis bv.2, oligofermentans, oralis, parasanguinis, parasanguinis II, peroris, pneumoniae, pseudopneumoniae, salivarius, sinensis, and vestibularis, among other taxa. A number of the identified genera were earlier reported as indicators of caries-free dentition in microbiota studies.21 Our results show abundant Streptococcus in initial, moderate, and severe caries. On the other hand, in our study, Lactobacillus was only identified in extensive caries, potentially due to the diagnostic criteria employed for dental caries in the current research.

Regarding bacterial species with greater relative abundance according to caries severity, we observed a statistically significant difference for P. denticolens and S. sobrinus, as they were identified only in extensive caries. In addition, a statistically significant difference was also observed for S. mutans, whose presence increased with increased lesion severity, being found in 14.3% of initial caries, 40% of moderate lesions, and 85% of extensive caries. These results are similar to those reported22 in which the most frequent bacterial species found in active dentinal caries were—almost exclusively—S. mutans, P. denticolens, and L. salivarius. These results may be attributed to this group of bacteria's aciduric and acidogenic potential. In our study, L. salivarius was only present in extensive caries. Similarly, other authors18 have evaluated bifidobacteria in occlusal carious lesions in permanent and deciduous teeth, in which they observed 424 species, and similar to our study, P. denticolens was one of the species presenting greater presence.

In this study, enriched bacterial diversity was observed, as determined by the α-Shannon index of 3.02, which was preserved among study groups and did not show statistically significant differences among them. These results are similar to those reported by Richards et al.,22 in which statistically significant differences in microbial diversity were not found among their groups. Our data shows bacterial diversity in biofilm samples isolated from dental surfaces with caries of varying severity, where a greater relative abundance of P. denticolens, S. mutans, and S. sobrinus was found in extensive caries. Statistical analyses revealed no significant intergroup differences in the distribution of genera.

Conclusion

This study highlights the diversity and richness of the oral microbiome in children from vulnerable communities in Cartagena, Colombia, affected by dental caries. Bacterial genera such as Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Fusobacterium were identified with high relative abundance in the oral biofilm. Species including S. mutans, S. sobrinus, and P. denticolens emerged as being significantly associated with the occurrence of extensive cavitated caries.
