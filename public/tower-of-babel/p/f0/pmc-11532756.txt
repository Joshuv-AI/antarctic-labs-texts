Yeast-based assay to identify inhibitors of the malaria parasite sodium phosphate uptake transporter as potential novel antimalarial drugs
Authors: Joseph M Sweeney, Ian M Willis, Myles H Akabas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMalaria affects almost 250 million people annually and continues to be a significant threat to global public health. Infection with protozoan parasites from the genus Plasmodium causes malaria. The primary treatment for malaria is artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs). The spread of ACT-resistant parasites has undermined efforts to control and eradicate malaria. Thus, it is crucial to identify new targets for the development of novel antimalarial drugs. Phosphate is an essential nutrient for all cells. The Plasmodium falciparum genome encodes a single sodium-coupled inorganic phosphate transporter named PfPiT that is essential for parasite proliferation in the asexual blood stage. Thus, PfPiT inhibitors may be promising antimalarial drugs. Like Plasmodium, yeast requires phosphate to grow. We developed a Saccharomyces cerevisiae based growth assay to identify inhibitors of PfPiT. Genome editing was used to create a yeast strain where PfPiT was the only phosphate transporter. Using a radioactive [32P]phosphate uptake assay, the measured phosphate Km for PfPiT in yeast was 56 ± 7 μM in 1 mM NaCl at pH 7.4. The Km decreased to 24 ± 3 μM in 25 mM NaCl consistent with it being a Na+ coupled cotransporter. Conditions under which yeast growth was dependent on phosphate uptake mediated by PfPiT were identified and a 22-h growth assay was developed to screen for PfPiT inhibitors. In a screen of 21 compounds, two compounds were identified that inhibited the growth of the PfPiT strain but not that of the parental strain expressing Pho84, one of the five endogenous yeast phosphate transporters. Radioactive phosphate uptake experiments confirmed inhibition of phosphate uptake by the two compounds. The growth inhibition assay provides a simple and inexpensive approach to screen a large compound library for PfPiT inhibitors that may serve as starting points for the development of novel antimalarial drugs.Keywords: Drug discovery, Sodium phosphate cotransporters, Malaria, Plasmodium falciparum, PfPiT, Assay development

Introduction

Despite efforts to control and eradicate malaria, it remains a significant threat to global public health particularly in the tropical and subtropical regions of Africa, Southeast Asia, and South and Central America. In 2022, almost 250 million people were infected and more than 600,000 died of malaria. Those who died were mostly children under the age of five and pregnant women in sub-Saharan Africa (World Health Organization, 2023). In addition, the morbidity caused by malaria has an impact on family income while the breadwinner(s) are ill. Artemisinin-based combination therapies (ACTs) remain first line treatments for malaria. However, the spread of ACT-resistant parasites throughout Southeast Asia and now Africa is a major threat to efforts to control the disease (Rasmussen et al., 2017; Rosenthal, 2018; Balikagala et al., 2021; Harris, 2023). Therefore, it is imperative to identify new potential drug targets for the development of novel antimalarial drugs.

Malaria is caused by infection with Plasmodium genus protozoan parasites. Infection with Plasmodium falciparum parasites is the most lethal, but infection with any of the other four Plasmodium species can cause serious symptoms (World Health Organization, 2023). These obligate intracellular parasites have evolved to proliferate in the highly regulated cytoplasmic environment of hepatocytes and red blood cells (RBC). Because host cells maintain the composition of the cytoplasmic environment homeostatically, Plasmodium parasites have limited need to respond to environmental stresses. Plasmodium parasites use membrane transport proteins to import essential metabolic substrates from the host cells, export metabolic wastes, and for regulation of ion gradients (Saliba and Kirk, 2001; Downie et al., 2006; Kirk, 2015; Meier et al., 2018; Wunderlich, 2022). These transport proteins are potential targets for the development of novel antimalarial drugs. For example, an antimalarial drug targeting the malaria PfATP4 transporter is being developed (Rottmann et al., 2010; Spillman et al., 2013; Qiu et al., 2022; Schmitt et al., 2022). Also, a number of drugs approved to treat human diseases target membrane transport proteins including diuretics, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, proton pump inhibitors, and sodium-glucose cotransporter inhibitors (Brunton and Knollmann, 2023). Thus, membrane transport proteins are viable drug targets, but robust assays that can be used to screen large chemical libraries must be developed to identify inhibitors of membrane transport proteins.

We previously utilized a yeast-based expression system to develop a high throughput screening (HTS) assay to identify inhibitors of the P. falciparum Equilibrative Nucleoside Transporter Type 1 (PfENT1) (Frame et al., 2015). In collaboration with GlaxoSmithKline, we screened their 1.8 million compound library and extensively characterized six hits that inhibited PfENT1 in a variety of yeast and parasite-based assays (Sosa et al., 2019). PfENT1 and the human homologue hENT1 are 19% sequence identical but PfENT1 specific inhibitors were identified (Sosa et al., 2019). A high resolution cryo-EM structure of PfENT1 was recently published with one of the inhibitors, GSK-4, bound in the central cavity at the orthosteric site (Wang et al., 2023). This demonstrates that yeast-based expression systems can be used to screen large chemical libraries to identify inhibitors of membrane transport proteins that can serve as starting points for medicinal chemistry.

Inorganic phosphate is an essential nutrient that all cells require for synthesis of nucleotides, nucleic acids, phospholipids, and sugar phosphates. Humans and yeast have five phosphate transporters whose expression and functional activity are regulated by environmental and internal conditions. In contrast, P. falciparum parasites have a single sodium-coupled phosphate transporter, known as PfPiT (PF3D7_1340900) (Saliba et al., 2006). PfPiT is homologous to two human phosphate transporters, PiT-1 and PiT-2, and is a member of the SLC20 transporter family (Virkki et al., 2007; Forster et al., 2013; Tsai et al., 2020). PiT-1 and PiT-2 are 28% and 26% sequence identical to PfPiT, respectively. Both transcriptomic and proteomic data indicate that PfPiT is expressed in asexual blood stage parasites and gametocytes. Random transposon mutagenesis categorized PfPiT as essential for optimal growth of asexual blood stage parasites (Zhang et al., 2018). Additionally, inorganic phosphate is required in culture media for the in-vitro proliferation of intraerythrocytic P. falciparum (Saliba et al., 2006; Pillai et al., 2013). This suggests that PfPiT might be a good therapeutic target for the development of novel antimalarial drugs.

To identify inhibitors of PfPiT, a Saccharomyces cerevisiae based HTS assay was developed. S. cerevisiae is not viable if all five endogenous phosphate transporters are knocked out (Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001). However, strains expressing any one of the five transporters are viable, although they have altered growth characteristics compared to wild type yeast (Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001). In the EY918 strain, four of the five yeast phosphate transporters are knocked out and viability is maintained by PHO84 which encodes a high affinity proton-coupled phosphate transporter (Bun-Ya et al., 1991; Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001). In the present work, the genome of strain EY918 was modified to replace PHO84 by a yeast codon optimized version of the gene encoding PfPiT. The resultant strain expressed PfPiT as the sole phosphate transporter. The growth of PfPiT-expressing yeast was dependent on phosphate import via PfPiT and the ion and pH dependence of PfPiT in yeast was characterized using radioactive phosphate uptake and growth assays. We developed a robust 22-h growth assay and screened a small set of compounds for inhibitors of PfPiT. We demonstrate that our yeast-based assay can be used to screen small molecule chemical libraries to identify PfPiT inhibitors which can potentially be used to develop novel antimalarial drugs.

Materials and methods

Please see supporting information for additional experimental procedures.

S. cerevisiae strains

The parent S. cerevisiae strain used to construct the PfPiT strain was EY918. It was obtained from Dr. Dennis Wykoff, Villanova University (Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001) (Table S1). EY918 was derived from K699 (ade2-1 trp1-1 can1-100 leu2-3112 his3-11,15 ura3) (Schwob and Nasmyth, 1993). The PfPiT strain used in this work was developed by targeted replacement of the PHO84 coding sequence (Fig. S1). Experimental procedures, plasmids, and primers used to develop the PfPiT strain are in supporting information experimental procedures, and Tables S2 and S3.

PfPiT gene synthesis

A S. cerevisiae codon-optimized gene for PfPiT (PF3D7_1340900) was generated using Genscript's GenSmart™ Codon Optimization Tool (Fig. S2). A hemagglutinin epitope-tag was added to the sequence before the stop codon. The PfPiT gene was synthesized by Genscript and delivered in a pUC57 plasmid (Table S2).

Yeast culture media

Synthetic complete liquid media, with all amino acids and 25 mM phosphate (SC+25Pi) was used to culture yeast prior to growth assays. SC+25Pi contained 2 g/L Drop-out mix complete w/o yeast nitrogen base (US Biologicals, #D9515), 1.5 g/L Yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, without ammonium sulfate and without phosphate, supplemented with KCl (Formedium # CYN6803), 20 g/L glucose (Fisher D16-3), 1.9 g/L L-glutamine (Sigma G3126) and 3 g/L NaH2PO4 (Sigma S8282).

2x Synthetic complete media with 24 mM phosphate (2xSC+24Pi) was prepared for the growth inhibition assay. 2xSC+24Pi media contains 4 g/L Drop-out mix complete w/o yeast nitrogen base (US Biologicals, #D9515), 3.0 g/L Yeast nitrogen base without amino acids, without ammonium sulfate and without phosphate, supplemented with KCl (Formedium # CYN6803), 40 g/L glucose (Fisher D16-3), 3.8 g/L L-glutamine (Sigma G3126) and 2.88 g/L NaH2PO4 (Sigma S8282). The pH of this media was 4.3.

2xSC+24Pi media was modified for Bioscreen C growth assays depending on the variable being tested. 2xSC+24Pi with no phosphate was used for the phosphate-dependent assays. 2xSC+24Pi media with Na2HPO4 (3.4 g/L, Fisher S374) instead of NaH2PO4 was used for the pH-dependent assays and was pH 6.8.

Yeast Extract–Peptone–Dextrose (YPD) media contained 10 g/L yeast extract (Fisher BP1422), 20 g/L Bacto peptone (Gibco 211677), and 20 g/L glucose (Fisher D16-3). YPD culture plates contained 20 g/L agar (Fisher BP1423). All culture plates in this work used 100 mm petri dishes (Fisher FB0875713).

32Pi radioactive uptake assay

Tris-succinate buffer, pH 7.4 was used for radioactive uptake assays and consists of an equimolar combination of succinic acid (Fisher BP336) and Tris base (Fisher BP152) using the volumes described in Table S4. Concentration-dependent growth assay showed that 10 mM Tris-succinate buffer, pH 4.2 did not inhibit PfPiT strain or EY918 strain growth (Fig. S3).

50 μL of the inhibitor was added to each well of a 96-well polystyrene, nonsterile, untreated plate (Fisher 12-565-511), and serially diluted in water. 50 μL of 4x uptake solution (40 mM tris-succinate buffer, pH 7.4, 20 μM KPO4 (Fisher P285), pH 7.4, 12% glucose (Fisher D16-3) and 100 mM NaCl (Fisher BP358)) was added to each well. Then, 50 μL of 32Pi solution containing 40 μCi/mL [32P]-phosphate (32Pi) (PerkinElmer NEX053005MC, specific activity 285.6 Ci/mg) was added to each well. Plates were placed in an incubator at 36 °C to pre-warm the uptake solution. Yeast were grown to mid log phase in SC+25Pi media, harvested by centrifugation for 5 min at 3000×g, 4 °C, and washed twice with cold wash buffer (10 mM Tris-succinate buffer, pH 7.4 and 3% glucose). 50 μL of yeast resuspended in water at OD600 = 1.6 was added to the prewarmed assay plate, and incubated for 10 min at 36 °C. The final volume of each well was 200 μL, with 10 mM Tris-succinate buffer, pH 7.4, 5 μM KPO4, 3% glucose, 25 mM NaCl, 2 μCi 32Pi, and yeast OD600 = 0.4.32Pi uptake was performed as described elsewhere (Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001). At 10 min, the magnitude of the counts per minute (CPM) signal was ∼150 times the background with no yeast present, providing a large signal window between maximum uptake and background. After the 10 min incubation, yeast were harvested onto glass fiber filtermats (Filtermat A, GF/C; 1.2 μM pore, PerkinElmer 1450–421) using the TomTec 96-well cell harvester system (#96-3-469). The filtermats were dried, placed into plastic sample bags (PerkinElmer 1450–432), loaded into plastic adapter cassettes (PerkinElmer 1450–104), and 32P Cherenkov radiation was counted with a microplate scintillation counter (1450 MicroBeta TriLux, PerkinElmer). Two technical replicates of each condition were completed for each biological replicate. Biological replicates were completed on different days. CPM values were plotted on GraphPad Prism 9.3.1. Values were normalized to the maximum uptake counts of the negative control, which did not have added inhibitors.

Yeast strain growth characterization (Bioscreen C)

A Bioscreen C machine (Growth Curves USA) was used to characterize the phosphate/pH/NaCl/DMSO-dependent yeast growth. Yeast growth was assessed by measurement of the culture optical density at 600 nm (OD600). 2xSC+24Pi media was diluted 1:1 with sterile water containing 2% Nonidet P-40 Substitute Solution (NP-40) (Sigma 98379) and then added to each well in a 100-well honeycomb plate (Fisher NC9976780). Each well ultimately contained 150 μL of 1x SC media, pH 4.3, with 12 mM NaH2PO4, 13 mM glutamine, 1% NP-40 and yeast at an OD600 of 0.05. The media was adjusted accordingly to account for the variable being tested. Prior to the start of a growth assay yeast were grown to saturation in SC+25Pi media and washed twice in water prior to the assay. Each condition had at least two technical replicates for each biological replicate. Biological replicates were completed on different days. The Bioscreen C machine (Growth Curves USA) operated with the following settings: continuous high amplitude linear shaking, OD600 reading every 15 min, at 30 °C. Values were plotted on GraphPad Prism 9.3.1.

Growth inhibition assay

100 μL of compound dissolved in water were added to each well of a Corning Costar Clear, Sterile 96-Well, Cell Culture-Treated, Flat-Bottom Microplate (Fisher 07-200-90). Serial dilutions were made directly on the plate. Yeast strains were grown to saturation in SC+25Pi media prior to the assay, harvested by centrifugation, washed twice in water, then resuspended in 2xSC+24Pi media. The OD600 of each strain was adjusted to twice the “starting OD600”. The starting OD600 of the EY918 strain is 0.011 and the starting OD600 of the PfPiT strain is 0.04. These starting OD600 values were calculated so that at the completion of the assay, each strain would reach a similar OD600 of ∼0.9 in the absence of an inhibitor. Finally, 100 μL of the yeast in 2xSC+24Pi was added to 100 μL of the inhibitor in each well. Each plate had background controls consisting of the inhibitor and media without yeast, and maximum growth controls consisting of yeast and media without the inhibitors. Plates were covered and placed into a stationary humidified incubator at 30 °C. The OD600 of the EY918 strain was measured after 16 h (due to its significantly faster growth) and the OD600 of the PfPiT strain was measured after 22 h. At least two technical replicates were completed for each biological replicate. Biological replicates were completed on different days. SpectraMax iD3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices) using SoftMax Pro 7.1 software was used to measure OD600 values. Values were plotted on GraphPad Prism 9.3.1. The maximum growth control was used to normalize the data.

Inhibitor concentrations used in 21-compound growth inhibition assay

The 21 inhibitors tested are shown in Table S5. They were tested at different concentrations due to solubility issues encountered in making the stock solutions. JS5 was tested at 0.25 mM; JS7 at 0.5 mM; JS6 at 2 mM; JS11 at 5 mM; JS2, JS3, and JS18 at 10 mM; JS16 at 12.5 mM; JS1, JS4, JS8, JS14, and JS20 at 25 mM; and JS9, JS10, JS12, JS13, JS15, JS17, JS19, and JS21 at 50 mM.

Results

Development and characterization of a yeast strain expressing PfPiT

To construct a yeast strain expressing the malaria phosphate transporter PfPiT as the sole phosphate transporter, the genome of strain EY918 was edited using CRISPR/Cas9. The EY918 strain only expresses one of the five endogenous yeast phosphate transporters, Pho84 (Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001). A yeast codon optimized PfPiT gene was used to replace the PHO84 coding sequence in EY918 strain, placing the PfPiT gene under the control of the PHO84 promoter and 3′ untranslated sequences (see supporting information experimental procedures, Fig. S1). Throughout this work, this strain will be referred to as the “PfPiT strain”. PCR confirmation and DNA sequencing confirmed correct insertion of PfPiT into the yeast genome.

To characterize the properties of PfPiT expressed in yeast, the affinity for phosphate and the dependence of phosphate uptake on NaCl and pH was determined using a32Pi uptake assay. Measurement of 32Pi uptake provides a direct approach to assess PfPiT's functional transport parameters. 32Pi uptake into yeast was linear over at least a 15 min uptake period. To remain in the linear uptake range, in subsequent experiments, uptake was measured after a 10 min incubation. PfPiT-mediated phosphate-dependent 32Pi uptake yielded an apparent phosphate uptake Km of 24 ± 3 μM at pH 7.4 with 25 mM NaCl (Fig. 1A, Table 1). Reducing the NaCl concentration to 1 mM increased the PfPiT apparent phosphate uptake Km to 56 ± 7 μM, significantly higher than that with 25 mM NaCl (p < 0.03; Fig. 1A–Table 1). This is consistent with its characterization as a sodium-dependent phosphate transporter (Saliba et al., 2006). In contrast, Pho84-mediated phosphate-dependent 32Pi uptake in the EY918 strain had an apparent Pi Km of 110 ± 10 μM at 25 mM NaCl and an apparent Km of 66 ± 30 μM with 1 mM NaCl, a difference that was not significant (p = 0.07, Fig. 1A–Table 1). The insensitivity of Pho84 to differing sodium concentrations is consistent with its characterization as a proton-coupled phosphate transporter (Bun-Ya et al., 1991).

pH-dependent 32Pi uptake was characterized with 25 mM NaCl and 1 mM KPO4 within a range of pH 4 to pH 7 (Fig. 1B). It is important to note that changing the media pH changes both the free proton concentration and the predominant ionization state of the phosphate. If a transporter prefers either the mono- or di-valent phosphate ion, a change in pH will change the concentration of the transported ion. The maximum phosphate uptake of the PfPiT strain occurred at ∼ pH 5. The phosphate uptake of the PfPiT strain significantly decreased as the pH increased from pH 5 to pH 7 (p < 0.05). At pH 7, the phosphate uptake of the PfPiT strain was reduced to 64 ± 7% (n = 4) of the maximal uptake at pH 5. This is similar to what was reported for PfPiT expressed in Xenopus oocytes (Saliba et al., 2006). In contrast to EY918, the phosphate uptake of the PfPiT strain significantly decreased as the pH decreased from pH 4.5 to pH 4 (p < 0.01), with the phosphate uptake reduced to 36 ± 5.4% (n = 4). The Pho84-mediated phosphate uptake in the EY918 strain did not change significantly within the tested pH range from 4 to 7 (p = 0.1, Fig. 1B). These results demonstrate that PfPiT expression altered the functional phosphate uptake characteristics compared to yeast expressing Pho84.

PfPiT strain growth characterization and growth inhibition assay development

The concentration-dependent effect of phosphate, pH, NaCl, and DMSO on PfPiT strain growth was characterized to optimize these parameters for a robust and sensitive growth inhibition assay. These characterization assays were carried out in synthetic complete media, to allow precise determination of phosphate and ion concentrations that is not feasible with rich media formulations, such as YPD. There are several significant differences between the conditions used to characterize [32P]Pi uptake and yeast growth. The Pi uptake experiments are done over 10 min at room temperature and are largely independent of the metabolic state of the yeast. The amount of Pi uptake does not have to meet the metabolic demands for yeast growth. In contrast, yeast growth is dependent on numerous factors, many independent of the rate of phosphate uptake. The replacement of Pho84 with PfPiT may cause significant changes in the metabolic state of the yeast because the yeast phosphate uptake system is integrated into the yeast metabolic control and growth systems (Tamai et al., 1985; Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001; Pinson et al., 2004). This issue is addressed in greater detail in the Discussion. Furthermore, as yeast grow, they acidify the media, so over the 16–22 h of a growth assay the media pH is ∼4. Thus, it is not unexpected that the optimal growth conditions might vary from those determined in radioactive substrate uptake experiments. The goal of these experiments was to identify starting media conditions where a reduction in phosphate uptake would limit the yeast growth rate and make yeast growth rate sensitive to the presence of PfPiT inhibitors in the media.

In the absence of phosphate, the PfPiT strain did not grow over a 72-h incubation (Figs. S4B and 2A) whereas the EY918 strain exhibited minimal growth (Figs. S4B and 2B). PfPiT strain growth was sensitive to media phosphate concentrations within the tested range of 4 mM–93 mM NaH2PO4 (Fig. 2A and C). The phosphate-dependent growth EC50 of the PfPiT strain was 17 ± 1 mM (Table 1). Furthermore, the growth of the PfPiT strain was roughly linearly proportional to the phosphate concentration between 4 mM and 18 mM phosphate (Fig. 2C). 12 mM phosphate was used in the media for the growth inhibition assay because this phosphate concentration was within the phosphate-dependent linear range of growth. EY918 strain growth was not sensitive to changes of phosphate concentration within the tested range (Fig. 2B and C).

The dependence of PfPiT strain growth on the starting media pH was characterized because the media pH determines the phosphate ionization state. Previous studies showed that PfPiT preferentially transports the monovalent phosphate anion (H2PO4−) over the divalent ion (Saliba et al., 2006). Yeast acidify the media to ∼pH 4 as they grow unless high buffer concentrations are added to the media (Peña et al., 2015). PfPiT strain growth was sensitive to starting media pH in the tested range of pH 4.3 to pH 6.8 (Fig. 3A). The PfPiT strain OD600 was the highest after 22-h when the starting pH was 4.3, but growth decreased as the starting pH was increased to pH 6 (Fig. 3A). If the starting pH was greater than pH 6, then the OD600 increased minimally after 22-h (Fig. 3A). The EY918 strain was not sensitive to changes in the starting media pH within the tested range (Fig. 3A). A starting media pH of 4.3 was used for the growth inhibition assay, as this reduces pH changes throughout the assay (Peña et al., 2015).

Since PfPiT is a sodium-coupled phosphate transporter (Saliba et al., 2006), the effect of NaCl concentration on the growth of the PfPiT strain was characterized. Synthetic complete media contains 1 mM NaCl. With 12 mM NaH2PO4 used in the assay, the media contains ∼13 mM sodium at baseline. The basal concentration of sodium was increased by adding 5.5 mM–140 mM NaCl to the media. The addition of NaCl to the media decreased the growth of the PfPiT strain, yet the EY918 strain was not sensitive to the increased NaCl concentrations (Fig. 3B). Additional NaCl was not added to the growth inhibition assay media as the ∼13 mM basal sodium concentration was suitable for transport function.

Ammonium sulfate is commonly used as a nitrogen source in synthetic complete media, but sulfate may inhibit phosphate transport (Dallaire and Béliveau, 1992; Hortells et al., 2020). For this reason, ammonium sulfate was replaced with 13 mM glutamine, which is known to be a suitable alternative nitrogen source (Ljungdahl and Daignan-Fornier, 2012; Fairbairn et al., 2017). Based on this characterization, the optimal yeast media for the PfPiT inhibitor growth assay consisted of 12 mM NaH2PO4, no added NaCl, 13 mM glutamine, with a starting pH of 4.3. The initial yeast inoculum for each 96 plate well was optimized so that the assay could be completed in 16-h with the EY918 strain and 22-h with the PfPiT strain with high reproducibility of the OD600 at that time point. The parental EY918 strain had a shorter delay before entering log growth phase than the PfPiT strain (Fig. S4A). As a result, after 22 h in media with 12 mM phosphate the OD600 was significantly higher for EY918 than the PfPiT strain (p < 0.001) (Fig. S4). For this reason, growth of the EY918 strain was quantified by measuring the culture OD600 after a 16-h incubation, whereas for PfPiT strain, the culture OD600 was quantified at 22-h.

Most small molecule chemical libraries are dissolved in DMSO. Therefore, the effect of 1% DMSO on the growth of the PfPiT strain was assessed. The growth of both the PfPiT strain and EY918 strain was reduced ∼11% compared to growth without DMSO (Fig. S5). Thus, compounds dissolved in DMSO can be used in a growth inhibition assay with the PfPiT strain.

Validation of the yeast growth inhibition assay

PFA inhibition

Phosphonoformic acid (PFA) is a competitive inhibitor of phosphate transporters (Szczepanska-Konkel et al., 1986; Ravera et al., 2007; Yoshiko et al., 2007). A concentration-dependent growth inhibition assay and a32Pi uptake inhibition assay were conducted with PFA to validate the conditions for assessing growth inhibition. PFA inhibited PfPiT strain growth with an IC50 of 0.14 ± 0.003 mM, and inhibited PfPiT-mediated phosphate uptake with an IC50 of 1.2 ± 0.2 mM (Fig. 4, Table 2).

PFA inhibited EY918 strain growth with an IC50 of 34 ± 0.4 mM, but inhibited Pho84-mediated phosphate uptake with an IC50 of 0.77 ± 0.02 mM (Table 2). The difference in IC50 values in the two assays is likely a result of multiple factors including, 1) the different phosphate concentrations, 12 mM in the growth assay, and 5 μM in the 32Pi uptake assay, 2) the differences in pH, 4 and 7, respectively, that impacts the ionization state of PFA and phosphate, and 3) the specificity of the two transporters for mono-vs di-basic phosphate anion.

To assess the robustness and reproducibility of the PfPiT screening assay, plate uniformity, intra-day, and inter-day variability tests were performed with PFA according to NIH assay validation guidelines (Iversen et al., 2004). Assay robustness was characterized by Z′-scores >0.93 (Fig. S6). A Z′-score of 0.4 is considered the minimum value for a HTS assay (Iversen et al., 2004). The Z′-score, coefficient of variation (CV) and signal window (SW) conformed with NIH guidelines, indicating that the chosen conditions create a robust and reproducible assay.

Twenty-one compound growth inhibition assay

Several studies have demonstrated that sulfate can inhibit phosphate transporters (Dallaire and Béliveau, 1992; Hortells et al., 2020). In preliminary experiments, high concentrations of sulfonate-containing buffers such as PIPES, MOPS and ACES inhibited growth of the PfPiT strain. As proof of principle that the optimized 22-h growth assay could be used to screen for PfPiT inhibitors, 21 compounds that contained either sulfonate or carboxylate groups were screened. Because both PfPiT inhibitors and yeast cytotoxic compounds will inhibit growth, we screened compounds against both the PfPiT and EY918 strains. Compounds that inhibit growth of both strains are assumed to be cytotoxic. The concentrations and characteristics of the tested compounds are listed in Table S5.

JS14, 3-(N-ethyl-3-methylanilino)Propanesulfonic Acid (3-NPA), and JS21, potassium acetate, inhibited PfPiT strain growth but not growth of the EY918 control strain (Fig. 5). This implies that these compounds inhibit PfPiT phosphate transport. In contrast, JS5 inhibited the growth of both the PfPiT strain and the EY918 control strain. Therefore, it is either cytotoxic to yeast independent of phosphate transport or may inhibit both transporters (Fig. 5).

The concentration dependence of growth inhibition by 3-NPA and potassium acetate was determined. 3-NPA more potently inhibited growth of the PfPiT strain compared to potassium acetate (Fig. 6A and C). 3-NPA inhibited PfPiT strain growth with an IC50 of 3.7 ± 0.2 mM, and potassium acetate inhibited growth with an IC50 of 13 ± 0.7 mM (Table 2). At higher concentrations than those used in the initial screen, the compounds inhibited growth of the EY918 strain as well (Fig. 6A and C).

Radioactive 32Pi uptake provides a more direct assessment of the ability to inhibit PfPiT. To validate the screen results, the ability of 3-NPA and potassium acetate to inhibit 32Pi uptake in the 10-min uptake assay was assessed (Fig. 6B and D). Based upon the 32Pi uptake assay, 3-NPA inhibited PfPiT-mediated phosphate uptake with an IC50 of 8.1 ± 0.7 mM, and potassium acetate inhibited phosphate uptake with an IC50 of 78 ± 0.4 mM (Table 2). Both compounds also inhibited 32Pi uptake into the EY918 strain with potencies similar to the inhibition of 32Pi uptake into the PfPiT strain (Fig. 6B and D). 3-NPA inhibited Pho84-mediated phosphate uptake with an IC50 of 4.0 ± 0.3 mM, and potassium acetate inhibited uptake with an IC50 of 55 ± 3 mM (Table 2). The differences between the results of the growth and the 32Pi uptake assays is likely a result of the different experimental conditions. Yeast acidify the media as they grow. Thus, in the yeast growth assay the media pH falls to pH ∼4, but pH remains at 7.4 during the 32Pi uptake assay. This difference in media pH will affect not only the protonation state of residues within the phosphate transporters, but also the ionization state of the inhibitor compounds. This may have significant effects on the potency of the compounds in the two assays. Another potentially significant difference in the two assays is that in the yeast growth assay the phosphate concentration is 12 mM, but in the 32Pi uptake assay it is 5 μM. Thus, the relative concentration of phosphate to inhibitor is significantly different in the two assays.

Discussion

Heterologous expression of the malaria phosphate uptake transporter, PfPiT, in yeast allowed the development of a robust, reproducible, and HTS-compatible growth assay that can be used to identify PfPiT inhibitors. In addition, our study shows that the lethality yeast cells lacking the five endogenous yeast phosphate transporters experience can be rescued upon provision of an alternative phosphate import mechanism. Thus, it is phosphate import that is critical not the specific yeast phosphate transport proteins.

PfPiT transport dependence on phosphate, NaCl and pH in 32Pi uptake assays were consistent with the previously published characterization of PfPiT (Saliba et al., 2006). The apparent Km of PfPiT expressed in Xenopus oocytes was 20 ± 2 μM at pH 7.4 and 96 mM NaCl, similar to that observed in isolated parasites (Saliba et al., 2006). This is similar to the apparent Km of PfPiT in this work, 24 ± 3 μM at pH 7.4 and 25 mM NaCl (Fig. 1A–Table 1). The apparent Km of PfPiT expressed in isolated P. falciparum decreased ∼2 fold when the NaCl concentration was reduced from 15 mM to 5 mM (Saliba et al., 2006). This is consistent with the decreased apparent Km observed when the NaCl concentration of the uptake solution was reduced from 25 mM to 1 mM in this work (Fig. 1A–Table 1). PfPiT was also shown to prefer the monovalent phosphate anion (H2PO4−) over the divalent phosphate anion (HPO42−) (Saliba et al., 2006). This is consistent with the significant increase of the PfPiT strain phosphate uptake as the pH decreased below pH 7 (Fig. 1B). The consistency of the 32Pi uptake results for the PfPiT strain with previously published properties of PfPiT in other systems implies that expression of PfPiT in the yeast plasma membrane does not significantly alter PfPiT functional properties.

Unexpectedly, growth of the PfPiT strain was reduced by increasing media NaCl concentration and initial media pH > 6 (Fig. 3). In contrast, growth of the EY918 parental strain was unaffected by changes in either NaCl concentration or initial media pH (Fig. 3). The growth assay is sensitive to the metabolic state of the yeast, which may affect the duration of lag phase before the yeast enter log growth phase. The sensitivity of PfPiT strain growth to elevated media NaCl concentration and increasing media pH may be related to the disruption of the S. cerevisiae phosphate homeostasis system that regulates the metabolic state of the yeast. Yeast have a complex system to maintain intracellular phosphate concentration (Tamai et al., 1985; Wykoff and O'Shea, 2001; Pinson et al., 2004; Ghillebert et al., 2011). In addition to regulating the expression of phosphate transporters, this system also regulates the expression of enzymes and proteins such as acid phosphatases and vacuole transporter chaperones (Ogawa et al., 2000). Endogenous phosphate transporters may also contribute to regulation of this yeast phosphate system and signaling pathways (Auesukaree et al., 2003; Pinson et al., 2004). For example, Pho84 is involved in the activation of the protein kinase A signaling pathway (Giots et al., 2003; Samyn et al., 2012). Deletion of the endogenous Pho84 transport protein in the PfPiT strain may alter the metabolic state of the PfPiT yeast relative to EY918. This may account for the slower growth of the PfPiT strain (Fig. 2). Thus, deletion of all endogenous phosphate transporters may lead to dysregulation of proteins involved in phosphate homeostasis or these signaling pathways, potentially causing yeast to be more sensitive to variations in media composition (Huang et al., 2002).

There are also differences in the results of the growth assays and the 32Pi uptake assays (Fig. 4, Fig. 6). As noted in the Results section 3.3.2, these differences may arise from the different media composition in the two assays and the impact of yeast metabolic state on the growth assay. In contrast, during the 10 min 32Pi uptake assay, the amount of 32Pi imported may be largely independent of the metabolic state of the yeast. Despite the discrepancy between the properties of the PfPiT strain in growth and 32Pi uptake assays, the PfPiT strain can reliably be used in a growth inhibition assay to identify PfPiT inhibitors. Growth of the PfPiT strain is dependent on PfPiT-mediated phosphate transport because it lacks other phosphate uptake pathways. Consistent with this, PFA and 3-NPA inhibition of PfPiT was confirmed in both growth inhibition and 32Pi uptake assays. The growth assay offers a simple assay for screening a small molecule library for PfPiT inhibitors that might serve as the basis for development of novel antimalarial drugs. In contrast, the 32Pi uptake assay is more difficult to perform for a large-scale compound screen. However, it will be useful to confirm the target of compounds identified in the growth assay because it is a more direct test of the PfPiT phosphate transport activity.

The growth inhibition assay has several drawbacks which may result in false positive or false negative hits. The intracellular erythrocyte pH range is ∼7.07–7.28 (Waddell and Bates, 1969), yet the growth assay is performed at pH 4.3. The efficacy of some compounds identified by the growth assay may differ when administered under physiological conditions relevant to mammalian cells due to differences in ionization state. The ionization state of amino acid residues on PfPiT itself may be altered, also potentially affecting compound interactions. The other disadvantage of the growth assay is that it indirectly quantifies PfPiT phosphate transport inhibition. Compounds could affect yeast growth via interactions with other cellular processes. Although the EY918 strain provides a control for toxic substances, it may be less sensitive to some toxic compounds compared to the PfPiT strain. The efficacy of growth assay hits can be confirmed with the more direct 32Pi uptake assay, which is performed at pH 7.4. Despite these drawbacks, the growth assay provides a simple and robust approach to screen a large compound library to identify PfPiT inhibitors that could be developed into novel antimalarial drugs.

Conclusions

In summary, we have created a yeast strain expressing PfPiT as its sole phosphate transporter. We used this strain to develop a robust HTS assay to identify inhibitors of PfPiT. This simple and inexpensive assay can be scaled to screen a large compound library to identify PfPiT inhibitors. Hopefully, this will lead to the discovery and development of novel drugs to combat malaria infection.
