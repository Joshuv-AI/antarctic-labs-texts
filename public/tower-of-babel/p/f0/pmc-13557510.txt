Tracheostomal myiasis in an elderly patient: A rare case report
Authors: H Sharma, V Khanna, H Basista, SK Awasthi
Published: 2026

The term myiasis refers to infestation of human or vertebrate tissues by dipterous larvae (maggots), which feed on necrotic or living tissue and body fluids. The predisposing factors include immunocompromised individuals, poor hygiene, neglected wounds, and low socioeconomic status of the patient. In tracheostomized patients, additional factors include inadequate stoma care and foul-smelling discharge.[1]

We report the case of an 80-year-old male who presented with complaints of foul-smelling discharge and larval extrusion from the tracheostomy site for 3 days. He had undergone a percutaneous tracheostomy 2 months prior at an outside institution for prolonged ventilatory support following intraventricular hemorrhage associated with impaired consciousness. The patient was a known case of hypertension, with no history of diabetes mellitus, immunosuppression, or other significant comorbidities.

On examination, the patient had a 7.5 cuffed tracheostomy tube in situ. The peristomal skin showed induration, blackish discoloration suggestive of necrosis, and foul-smelling mucopurulent discharge [Figure 1]. Endoscopic evaluation using a 0-degr degree endoscope was performed to assess the extent of infestation and to exclude intratracheal extension of larvae. Multiple motile larvae were visualized at the stoma site, with no evidence of infestation within the trachea. No evidence of airway obstruction was noted. A diagnosis of tracheostomal myiasis was made.

Tracheostomy stoma showing peristomal necrosis with visible larvae (black arrow)

The patient was admitted in a sterile isolated environment in a dark room. All routine blood investigations were done. Turpentine oil was instilled locally to facilitate larval migration and extraction. Approximately 14 larvae (10 live and 4 dead) were extracted manually using forceps [Figure 2]. The larvae were disposed of using the burning method. The wound was thoroughly debrided and cleaned well with antiseptic solution. Following larval removal and wound debridement, the existing tracheostomy tube was replaced with a new 7.5 cuffed tracheostomy tube. Meticulous care was taken to prevent aspiration of larvae into the lower airway. The patient’s vital parameters remained stable throughout the procedure. Broad-spectrum antibiotics were prescribed to prevent secondary bacterial infection. The patient showed good clinical improvement and was discharged with a healthy stoma. On follow-up, he remains asymptomatic.

Extracted larvae following manual removal. Yellow arrows indicate larval specimens, including dark-colored dead larvae and whitish live larvae

Although myiasis is relatively common in tropical and subtropical regions, tracheostomal myiasis remains rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature from India.[23] Tracheostomal myiasis is an extremely rare condition, and its exact incidence is unknown as the literature is primarily isolated to case reports and small case series.

Myiasis can be classified into obligate and facultative types. Obligate parasites require living tissue for larval development, whereas facultative species infest necrotic tissue and viable tissue under favorable conditions.[1] The head and neck region is most affected, including the oral cavity, nasal passages, ears, and tracheostomy wounds.[4] Clinical manifestations vary according to the involved site and may include pain, foul-smelling discharge, visible larvae, ulceration, bleeding, and secondary bacterial infection. Secondary bacterial infection is commonly caused by organisms such as Escherichia coli, Serratia marcescens, and Enterococcus faecalis.[1]

Although rare, tracheostomal myiasis is clinically significant due to risk of airway compromise. Larvae within or near the tracheostomy site may act as live foreign bodies with the possibility of migration into the tracheobronchial tree, leading to obstruction or aspiration-related complications.[5]

In the present case, the absence of immunosuppression suggests that local factors such as inadequate stoma hygiene, necrotic tissue, and possibly socioeconomic conditions played a more significant role in predisposing to infestation. Early recognition and prompt management, including mechanical removal of larvae, wound care, and antibiotic therapy, are essential to prevent complications. Patient and caregiver education regarding tracheostomy tube care, regular stoma cleaning, and early recognition of warning signs is crucial for preventing recurrence and associated complications.
