Burning Mouth Syndrome: Understanding the Biopsychosocial Dimensions of Invisible Pain
Authors: Ana Garcia‐Martinez, Antonio Vidal‐Sanchez, Pia Lopez‐Jornet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo analyse the biopsychosocial impact of burning mouth syndrome (BMS) on quality of life related to oral health, and to study the influence of perceived invalidation, stigma, and catastrophising on the experience of pain.Materials and MethodsObservational study of cases and controls with a sample of 226 participants (BMS group n = 156 and Control group n = 70), recruited at the University Dental Clinic of the Morales Meseguer Hospital. The following tests were administered: BPI‐SF (pain and functioning interference), SSCI‐8 (global stigma), ISC (internalised stigma), 3*I (perceived invalidation), HADS (anxiety and depression), PCS‐6 (catastrophising scale), OHIP‐14 (quality of life related to oral health) and the diagnostic legitimacy perception scale.ResultsThe BMS patients presented significantly worse scores in all the variables analysed with respect to the healthy controls (p < 0.001), especially in oral health quality of life and catastrophising. In the regression models, the perceived invalidation showed an independent and consistent association with a worse oral health quality of life (p < 0.001), higher intensity of pain (p < 0.001) and higher perceived stigma (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBMS had a strong impact on the quality of life and emotional well‐being of the patients. The perceived invalidation emerged as a factor that is closely related to pain, stigma, and deterioration of quality of life, which supports the need for a comprehensive approach that includes not only clinical management but also the recognition and validation of the experience of the patient. These findings reinforce the need for a multidisciplinary approach that includes clinical, psychological, and social dimensions in the management of BMS.Keywords: burning mouth syndrome, catastrophising, chronic pain, quality of life, stigma

Introduction

According to the definition by the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD‐3), Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) is defined as a chronic orofacial pain disorder, characterised by an intraoral burning sensation without objectively detectable lesions, with the burning symptoms present for at least 3 months and for more than 2 h per day [1]. Its worldwide prevalence is estimated to be 1.73%, with a woman‐to‐man ratio of 3:1, predominantly affecting women of medium‐advanced age [2].

The underlying etiopathogenesis is unclear, although a multifactorial aetiology that involves local, systemic, and psychological factors has been suggested [3]. One of the etiological hypotheses refers to the hormonal state, as BMS is more frequently found in women with ages close to menopause or currently undergoing it [4]. On the other hand, neurophysiological studies suggest that BMS is neuropathogenic, involving alterations of the peripheral and central nervous pathways [5]. In addition, psychological factors must be mentioned, which modulate the perception of patients with respect to burning and pain symptoms, as an association between anxiety and depression has been found in BMS patients, and it has been observed that psychological alterations intensify the symptoms in the context of chronic pain [6].

For its diagnosis, it is important to exclude other local or systemic conditions that could explain the sensation of burning in the mouth, and for this, exclusion diagnoses are performed, which can be a great challenge for the professional [7], as they can lead to delays in the diagnosis, unnecessary diagnostic tests, and very often inefficient treatments [8]. Although different treatments have been proposed, both topical and systemic, to improve the burning, pain, and psychological distress, knowledge about the disease and the existence of an efficient treatment remains limited [9].

BMS is a paradigmatic example of invisible chronic pain, in which the absence of evident clinical findings can promote the minimisation of the patient's suffering. People who live with chronic pain must not only cope with the physical and emotional pain it entails, but on some occasions, with feelings of guilt and a frequent feeling of a lack of understanding from their social environment [3, 7, 10]. The scarce knowledge about BMS outside of specialised settings may justify the diagnostic delay observed in many patients, as well as the frequent consultation with many professionals before obtaining a definitive diagnosis [10, 11]. This diagnostic delay can have an individual and social impact, and can be a burden for health systems, which leads to higher socioeconomic costs. In the meantime, the patients tend to become lost in a never‐ending circuit of health professionals, being referred from one specialist to another [12].

On the other hand, research with other populations has shown that the perception of misunderstanding and invalidation associated with chronic pain and social stigma (racial, gender‐based, or age‐based discrimination, etc.) is associated with greater clinical pain and an unequal management of pain [13, 14]. The stigma refers to the convergence of cultural tags, stereotypes, or social discrimination, which leads to an unjust distribution of experiences and opportunities, and is recognised as a social determinant of health disparities [15]. BMS patients perhaps experience cumulative effects of the intersecting stigmas of women's pain, age, chronic orofacial pain, aside from other stigmatisation processes such as social status markers [16, 17].

The aim of the present study was to assess the biopsychosocial impact of BMS on oral health‐related quality of life, as well as to evaluate pain, perceived invalidation, stigma, and pain catastrophising in patients with BMS compared with healthy controls.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Patient Selection

An observational, cross‐sectional, and retrospective study was conducted through structured telephone surveys aimed at patients diagnosed with BMS and a control group. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received a favourable report from the Research Ethics Committee of the University of Murcia (ACTA28/2026/CEI). The protocol followed the recommendations from the STROBE statement to ensure transparency and quality in the presentation of cross‐sectional observational studies [18]. All the participants were informed about the aims of the study and gave their consent. The BMS patients were selected consecutively based on the clinical records of BMS patients who were under follow‐up at the Department of Oral Medicine of the Dental Clinic of the University of Murcia.

As this is an exploratory retrospective study, the sample size of the BMS patients was determined by the availability of the complete clinical data in the clinical histories. From an initial set of 186 patients, the ones that were excluded were those who: Did not pick up the phone (n = 4), refused to participate (n = 6), did not have complete records in the databases (n = 11), and could not answer independently due to cognitive impairment (n = 9), with a final sample of 156 finally obtained (91.7% women, 8.3% men), who completed the questionnaire (Figure 1).

Final patient collection flowchart.

Patients were included if they were older than 18 years old with a clinical diagnosis of Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS) according to the criteria from the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd edition (ICHD‐3). Specifically, participants reported intraoral burning sensations recurring daily for more than 2 h per day and persisting for more than 3 months, in the absence of identifiable local or systemic causes. Patients were excluded if they had clinical or histological evidence of conditions that could explain the symptoms, including oral lichen planus, denture stomatitis, oral candidiasis, or other oral mucosal diseases. Medical history, clinical records, and laboratory data were reviewed to rule out systemic conditions associated with oral burning. Laboratory assessment included complete blood count, fasting blood glucose, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c), serum iron, ferritin, and transferrin levels. Patients with systemic diseases, metabolic or neurological disorders, haematological abnormalities, active cancer treatment, severe uncontrolled psychiatric disorders, alcohol or substance abuse, or taking medications associated with oral burning symptoms or xerostomia were excluded.

The control group included individuals of similar age and sex to the BMS group who had not experienced symptoms of burning, stinging, or oral paresthesia during the previous 3 months and who had the cognitive ability to understand and complete the questionnaires. Individuals with recurrent or chronic pain conditions, visible oral mucosal lesions, active dental pathology, psychiatric disorders, or neurodegenerative diseases were excluded.

Data Collection

The participants answered the questions that were asked over the phone by a single qualified and trained professional (AVS), who was not part of the habitual team, to avoid biases associated with the professional‐patient relationship. The evaluator compiled the clinical history of each patient, including sociodemographic and clinical data (age, sex, educational level, diseases, and medication), symptom duration, the number of professionals consulted before the BMS diagnosis, and the number of treatments for BMS before the definitive diagnosis. Educational level was categorised as primary, secondary, or university education. The length of time (months) that passed between the start of the oral symptoms and the definite diagnosis was also recorded. The patients were also given the validated questionnaires to assess the perception of oral health, anxiety, depression, symptom intensity, the degree to which the patient perceives the professional's views on their diagnosis, their perception of stigma, invalidation, and catastrophising. On the other hand, the control group was surveyed about their quality of life, anxiety, depression, the stigma perceived by them, and catastrophising. The procedure lasted about 40–45 min.

Oral health quality of life was assessed through the Oral Health Impact Profile‐14 (OHIP‐14), which consists of questions structured in seven domains that encompass functional limitations up to social impact. It is calculated by adding the score of all of them (0–56 points), with a higher score indicating a worse quality of oral health of these patients [19].

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed with the IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 for Windows. A descriptive analysis of the qualitative variables was performed through frequencies and percentages, and the quantitative ones through means, standard deviations, and minimum and maximum values. The comparisons between the qualitative variables were performed through the Chi‐square test, and for the quantitative ones, an ANOVA was performed to compare the normality and homogeneity of the variances through the Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Levene's test. In addition, multiple linear regression models were performed to assess the association between variables. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The final sample size was composed of 226 patients. In agreement with the inclusion and exclusion groups, the sample was divided into two groups: The BMS group (n = 156), composed of patients diagnosed with BMS, and a control group (n = 70), composed of healthy individuals. In the BMS group, the mean age was 65.1 years, and in the control group, it was 63.4 years. Discussion

The present study analyses the experience of BMS patients in a multidimensional manner, integrating clinical, psychological, and social variables. The patient's pain cannot be solely understood through its intensity, but is strongly modulated by emotional and contextual factors [28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34]. In this study, significant differences were found between the BMS group and the group of healthy patients, with the former showing higher levels of anxiety and depression (HADS), a worse oral health quality of life (OHIP‐14), and a feeling of social stigma (SSCI‐8) and catastrophising (PCS‐6).

The most disruptive result from the multivariate regression analysis was to find social invalidation (3*I Inventory) as one of the factors that most affects BMS patients. This factor is directly related to a worse quality of life, a higher pain intensity, and an increase in the global stigma perceived by the patient. This means that the suffering of patients is multiplied when both medical and family environments question the veracity of their symptoms. When their pain is doubted, suffering arises that goes beyond the body: The pain of not being believed [33]. Just as with other chronic pain disorders, people with BMS not only suffer the impact of physical pain, but also emotional pain when they are not understood [34]. In addition, for patients who remain professionally active, a lack of understanding in the workplace may further contribute to feelings of invalidation and social misunderstanding. BMS is a prototype of how the lack of observable clinical findings can favour the minimisation of the suffering of patients, making them feel guilty or insecure. This can affect the therapeutic relationship, in which patients feel frustrated and lose their trust in the professionals and the healthcare system.

The association between higher educational level and lower stigma should be interpreted within the BMS group. Although patients with BMS showed higher stigma scores than healthy controls, the regression analysis indicated that among patients with BMS, those with higher educational attainment tended to report lower levels of stigma. Therefore, educational level may act as a protective factor against stigma within the patient group, without eliminating the overall impact of BMS on perceived stigma.

Another aspect in these patients is the diagnostic delay, which continues to be a relevant problem [35]. In our series, the mean time that passed until the definitive diagnosis was 14.02 ± 3.41 months, similar to that described previously in the literature, in which intervals between 12 and 20 months were reported from the start of the symptoms until a diagnostic confirmation [36, 37]. Nevertheless, some studies have shown even longer delays. Adamo et al. [35]., e.g., described lengths of time up to 29.71 ± 47.19 months. Altogether, these results demonstrate the difficulties that still exist for the early identification of the disease.

Within the context of chronic pain that frequently goes unnoticed – and which has also been explored in the present study through the different scales utilised, we find the stigma associated with the disease. Many people who live with chronic pain must not only deal with the physical and emotional suffering derived from the symptoms, but also with feelings of guilt, shame, or lack of understanding by those who surround them [31, 34].

BMS is a complex entity of chronic pain, in which no visible clinical findings in proportion to the intensity of these symptoms exist [3, 24]. This lack of objective signs can favour the underestimation of the condition, generate misunderstanding, and negatively affect the relationship between patient and professional. Although in some cases it can coexist with emotional factors such as anxiety, depression, or stress, these must not be used to invalidate the experience of pain nor reduce its origin to an exclusively psychological cause. It is fundamental to avoid diagnostic overshadowing, as it cannot be assumed that every symptom is solely explained by psychological factors (38). The relationship between BMS and the anxiety‐depression symptoms must not be understood as pain that is not real, but as part of how this chronic pain and mental health mutually influence each other, in which the affective alteration occurs bi‐directionally, intensifying the perception of pain and perpetuating the chronicity of BMS. Feeling misunderstood is the main obstacle to adaptation to pain, an idea that is also shared by Nicola et al. (39).

In agreement with Canfora (40), BMS requires a multifactorial understanding, in which neuropathic mechanisms, emotional factors, and the social context interact. For this, a biopsychosocial approach is fundamental, based on active listening, the validation of suffering, and individualised therapeutic strategies aimed at improving the symptoms and quality of life. Our model found that the level of education was a significant protector against stigma (p = 0.036), which suggests that education provides patients with better resources to seek legitimate information and to defend the veracity of their process as compared to a stigmatising environment. Giving legitimacy to the patients' pain, even when observing apparently healthy mucosa, is a priority therapeutic intervention. Actively listening, validating their experience of pain, and providing clear explanations can be just as therapeutic as any pharmacological intervention. The current evidence suggests that therapeutic success in BMS depends not only on the management of symptoms, but also on restoring trust and the recognition of the patient. Rather than “finding nothing”, the clinical challenge in these patients lies in knowing how to look where one doesn't always see [33].

Among the strengths of the study, the sample size stands out, firstly, as it provides consistency and solidity to the results obtained. Likewise, the use of validated tools to assess the psychosocial dimension allows addressing the problem from a broader perspective, overcoming the exclusively organic approach. The use of multivariate regression models is also relevant, as they allowed controlling for possible confounding variables and estimating with greater precision the role of invalidation as an independent predictor. In addition, we find the diagnosticrigour, supported by the application of the international criteria found in the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3 rd edition (ICHD‐3).

On the other hand, some limitations should be acknowledged. The cross‐sectional design of the study does not allow causal relationships to be established. Although associations were observed between invalidation, pain intensity, stigma, and quality of life, it is not possible to determine the direction of these relationships. Furthermore, bidirectional relationships cannot be ruled out, as higher levels of invalidation may be associated with greater pain perception, stigma, and poorer quality of life, while patients with a higher symptom burden may also perceive greater levels of invalidation and stigma.

Second, the source of the sample may limit the generalisability of the findings, as all patients were recruited from a single healthcare centre.

Although clinical records, medical history, and available laboratory data were reviewed to exclude secondary causes of oral burning, additional investigations for certain underlying conditions were not systematically performed in all patients. Therefore, the possibility of residual diagnostic misclassification cannot be completely excluded.

Furthermore, information regarding early retirement due to pain was not collected, preventing assessment of its potential impact on perceived social invalidation. Finally, collecting information through telephone interviews may have introduced social desirability bias, as some patients may have been reluctant to report sensitive issues such as depressive symptoms or feelings ofstigmatisation. In addition, although participants were recruited consecutively, only those who could be contacted and agreed to complete the interview were included, which may have limited the representativeness of the sample.

Likewise, experiences related to ageism, age‐based stigma, or age‐related discrimination were not evaluated, although they may be common in this population and should be addressed in future studies. Based on these findings, future studies with longitudinal follow‐up are warranted.

Conclusion

BMS suggests that pain cannot be understood outside its psychosocial context. Perceived invalidation was closely associated with pain, stigma, and quality of life. These findings emphasise the importance of recognising patients' experiences when assessing BMS. A multidisciplinary approach that considers clinical, psychological, and social dimensions may be appropriate in the management of this condition.
