Management of Impacted and Inverted Mesiodens - A Report of Two Cases
Authors: Vabitha Shetty, Rajmohan Y. Shetty, Deepshikha Mehrotra, Amina Usman, Nagashree Prabhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSupernumerary teeth represent a significant dental anomaly that has a notable impact on the primary and early mixed dentition, potentially leading to pathological disturbances in the development of permanent dentition. Mesiodens specifically refers to an additional tooth (erupted/unerupted) present in the midline between the two central incisors. This article describes two cases involving the surgical extraction of inverted and impacted mesiodens by using a minimally invasive surgical approach in children. Early extraction of supernumeraries can offer the advantage of minimizing loss of eruptive potential, space loss, and midline displacement. The decision on the treatment plan should be primarily influenced by the position of the mesiodens and its proximity to the permanent incisors.

INTRODUCTION

Mesiodens, the most common type (80%) of supernumerary teeth, is an unerupted extra tooth located between the two central incisors in the premaxilla. It can occur in either jaw, unilaterally or bilaterally, erupted or unerupted, and in single or multiple forms (mesiodentes). Prevalence ranges from 0.3% to 0.8% in primary dentition and from 0.15% to 3% in permanent dentition, affecting males more often than females at a 2:1 ratio.[1]

Only 25% of mesiodentes naturally emerge into the oral cavity. Most remain impacted and symptomless, often detected during routine radiographic examination. Supernumerary teeth can cause delayed eruption, midline diastema, ectopic eruption, and crowding and affect tooth alignment and facial aesthetics. Adjacent roots may undergo dilaceration or resorption due to pressure, and cyst formation with bone destruction is another reported complication. Timely removal of supernumerary teeth can prevent these issues.[2] This case report details the surgical treatment of two cases of inverted, impacted mesiodens.

CASE 1

An 8-year-old female patient reported to our department with a complaint of spacing between her upper front teeth, compromising her esthetics. Intra-oral examination revealed a midline diastema in the maxillary arch. An intra-oral periapical radiograph revealed the presence of inverted and impacted mesiodens with a fully formed root [Figure 1a]. The maxillary right (11) and left (21) permanent incisors were fully erupted and had half of their root formation completed. Using the same lingual opposite buccal rule, the position of the tooth was seen to be placed labially. A treatment plan involving the surgical extraction of the mesiodens was planned. The procedure and expected outcomes were explained to the patient and her mother, and written consent was obtained. Nasopalatine and infraorbital nerve block were given using 2% lignocaine hydrochloride with 1:200,000 epinephrine. A full-thickness flap was raised from the distal aspect of 11 to 12 by using a mucoperiosteal elevator. Bone guttering was performed to expose the apex of the mesiodens. To avoid tooth sectioning, excess bone removal, and damage to the developing permanent incisors, an attempt was made to luxate the mesiodens by using an 80-number K-file engaged into the apical foramen [Figure 1b]. Once the mesiodens was sufficiently luxated, the tooth was extracted using a Coupland elevator [Figure 1c]. The flap was placed back, and interrupted sutures (3-0 black silk) were placed and maintained for 7 days. The recall appointments were planned for suture removal and to assess the healing process. Following this, orthodontic closure was carried out.

CASE 2

A 7.5-year-old male patient reported to the department with a complaint of malpositioned maxillary anterior teeth, affecting his esthetics. Medical history was non-contributory. The patient had undergone an extraction of mesiodens 3 years ago. Intra-oral examination revealed rotated and palatally inclined maxillary central incisors (11 and 21). An inverted and impacted mesiodens, lying close to the roots of the maxillary central incisors, was observed on a maxillary occlusal radiograph [Figure 1d]. The permanent incisors were observed to have up to half of their root completed. To further evaluate the proximity of the mesiodens to the root and anatomic landmarks, a cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) of the desired region was performed. The CBCT revealed 1.62 mm from the impacted tooth to the nasopalatine nerve canal. The presence of bone covering was observed to be much less on the labial aspect of the impacted tooth than on the palatal [Figure 1e and 1f]. Hence, the surgical extraction was planned using a labial approach like that in the previous case.

DISCUSSION

Mesiodens may be impacted vertically (55.2%), horizontally (7%), or inverted (37.6%). Research suggests that inverted mesiodens have a low likelihood of erupting into the oral cavity.[1] Accurate diagnosis of the impacted mesiodens position and assessment of its relationship with neighboring permanent incisors are crucial factors in determining the optimal timing for extraction.[3] Other factors determining the treatment of impacted mesiodens include the child’s age, clinical findings, ability of the child to tolerate the surgical procedure, and root development stage of the adjacent permanent teeth.

Two contrasting perspectives exist regarding the extraction of supernumerary teeth. The first advocates for prompt removal upon diagnosis,[34] while the second proposes leaving the supernumerary tooth in place until adjacent teeth complete root development to prevent damage to their root apices.[56] Inverted mesiodens, located higher and deeper in the alveolar bone, are more likely to impede the eruption of permanent maxillary incisors and are considered candidates for prompt removal to prevent malocclusion.[7] In our first case, the mesiodens, was situated at an angle that interfered with the growth of the permanent maxillary right central incisor’s root. In the second case, removal of the mesiodens was deemed essential to facilitate orthodontic treatment aimed at addressing the prevailing malocclusion. Hence, in both cases, extraction was carried out promptly upon the diagnosis of the mesiodens.

The frequently utilized surgical method entails the palatal approach, utilizing full-thickness mucoperiosteal flaps. Alternative techniques, including the labial approach, apically reposition flaps with releasing incisions, and the modified vestibular approach are also employed.[8] In our first case, the mesiodens’s proximity to the labial cortical bone necessitated a labial approach for surgical removal. In the second case, despite the mesiodens being closer to the palatal bone, a labial approach was chosen to avoid inadvertent damage to the nasopalatine vessels and nerves. Both cases were monitored regularly until continued root development of the permanent incisors was observed.

In particular, the early diagnosis of the inverted orientation of mesiodens is crucial, and extraction should be carefully planned, considering various factors. The treatment plan should be primarily influenced by the position of the mesiodens and its proximity to the permanent incisors. However, the treatment planning should be customized for each case.
