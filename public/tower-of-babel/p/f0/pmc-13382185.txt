Menin inhibitors as a treatment for acute leukemia: from bench to the clinic
Authors: Justin Watts, Ellen Madarang, Tomasz Cierpicki, Jolanta Grembecka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMenin inhibitors exploit the key biological mechanism behind KMT2A-rearranged (KMT2A-r) and nucleophosmin 1–mutated (NPM1-mut) leukemias by targeting the menin-KMT2A interaction and disrupting KMT2A/KMT2A fusion complexes binding to chromatin, thereby preventing transcriptional activation of fundamental leukemogenic programs (HOXA9/MEIS1). A series of remarkable discoveries, from the cloning and identification of the MLL1 (KMT2A) gene in 1991, to the first discovery of menin’s role in leukemia in 2005, to the development of the first small-molecule menin inhibitor in 2012, and to the initiation of the first-in-human clinical trials in 2019, led to the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of the first menin inhibitor (revumenib) in 2024 for in the treatment of relapsed or refractory (R/R) KMT2A-r acute leukemia, representing a significant scientific achievement for the rapid clinical translation of molecularly targeted therapy. The menin inhibitors revumenib and ziftomenib have subsequently been FDA approved for R/R NPM1-mut acute myeloid leukemia (AML) in 2025, and at least 5 other menin inhibitors are in clinical development for leukemia. Here, we review the discovery, preclinical and clinical development, and safety and efficacy of menin inhibitors through 2025. Menin inhibitors hold promise as one of the most powerful single-agent targeted therapies in acute leukemia, with response rates of ∼50% or higher in R/R AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut disease. Despite this notable clinical activity, response duration to menin inhibitors remains short-lived for most patients, which has resulted in an explosion of combination therapy trials in both R/R and newly diagnosed AML, including intensive chemotherapy and nonintensive (venetoclax-based) approaches, as well as combinations with targeted therapies and novel differentiation therapies.

Introduction

Small-molecule inhibitors targeting the menin-KMT2A (MLL1) interaction, hereafter termed menin inhibitors (MENi), have demonstrated success in clinical studies in patients with acute leukemia, generating substantial interest and enthusiasm as a novel and effective therapy for patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL).1 After the discovery and preclinical evaluation of the first-in-class MENi by Grembecka and Cierpicki’s laboratories,2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 2 MENi were introduced to clinical studies in 2019: ziftomenib (codeveloped by Kura Oncology and Grembecka’s and Cierpicki’s groups)10, 11, 12 and revumenib (developed by Syndax Pharmaceuticals), for treatment of refractory or relapsed (R/R) AML with KMT2A rearrangements (KMT2A-r) or nucleophosmin 1-mutations (NPM1-mut).13, 14, 15 In 2024, revumenib was granted US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for the treatment of KMT2A-r leukemia, whereas in 2025, both revumenib and ziftomenib were approved for NPM1-mut leukemias.16 Several other MENi derived from either revumenib or ziftomenib scaffolds have been introduced to clinical studies in recent years, but they are at earlier stages of clinical evaluation.16 Here, we review the discovery and development of MENi and their clinical advancement as monotherapy and in combinations with other drugs, known resistance mechanisms, and present our perspective on their therapeutic potential in patients with leukemia.

Menin-KMT2A interaction as a therapeutic target in leukemia

The first studies revealing the importance of menin in leukemia came from 2 decades ago, when Yokoyama et al17,18 and Hess19 discovered that menin is a critical oncogenic cofactor of KMT2A fusion proteins in KMT2A-r leukemia. Chromosomal translocations of KMT2A, which result in the expression of over 100 distinct KMT2A fusion proteins,20 are found in both patients with AML and ALL, accounting for ∼5% to 10% of adult and ∼70% of infant acute leukemias,21,22 leading to poor clinical outcome (∼35% event-free survival at 5 years).23, 24, 25 Menin is a scaffold protein that binds to the N terminus of KMT2A and KMT2A fusion proteins and recruits KMT2A/KMT2A fusion complexes to target genes (Figure 1A).26 The menin-KMT2A interaction drives activation of the key target genes required for leukemogenesis, including HOXA9 and MEIS1, both of which are highly upregulated in KMT2A-r leukemias.17, 18, 19 Genetic disruption of the menin-KMT2A interaction leads to downregulation of Hoxa and Meis1 genes, induced growth arrest and differentiation of KMT2A-r leukemia cells, and abrogated development of leukemia in mouse models (Figure 1E).17 These findings have provided compelling validation of menin as a therapeutic target in KMT2A-r leukemia.

Nearly a decade ago, studies from the Armstrong group revealed that menin also plays a crucial role in leukemias with NPM1 mutations (Figure 1B), representing another high HOX subtype of leukemia,27 which account for ∼30% of adult AML.28, 29, 30 In NPM1-mut leukemia, the menin-KMT2A interaction is essential in upregulating HOXA/B and MEIS1 genes, and disruption of this interaction results in downregulation of these genes and blocks leukemogenesis.9,31 Mechanistically, an NPM1 mutant colocalizes the menin-KMT2A complex on chromatin to coregulate the expression of menin-KMT2A target genes (MEIS1, HOXA/B; Figure 1B,E).31,32 However, the menin-KMT2A dependency in NPM1-mut leukemia appears to be more complex because NPM1 mutations have been reported to result in cytoplasmic translocation33 and the formation of nuclear condensates that regulate gene expression34 and/or can lead to a potential dependency on the nuclear export protein XPO1,35 as recently reviewed.36

Furthermore, acute leukemias with chromosomal translocations of the nucleoporin 98 (NUP98) gene, which is found in ∼5% of pediatric AML cases and leads to very poor prognosis,37,38 also rely on the menin-KMT2A interaction for upregulation of HOXA/B and MEIS1 genes (Figure 1C).39,40 In addition, acute leukemias with UBTF tandem duplication, another high-risk pediatric AML subtype characterized by elevated HOXA/B expression, have demonstrated sensitivity to menin inhibition (Figure 1D).41 Overall, the interaction of menin with either wild-type KMT2A or KMT2A fusion proteins plays a critical role across several subtypes of AML with upregulated HOX genes, comprising >50% of all AML cases, representing, therefore, a great opportunity for therapeutic intervention.

The molecular basis of MENi development

Menin interacts with a ∼46 amino acid fragments from the N terminus of KMT2A with low nanomolar binding affinity (dissociation constant, 6nM).26 Structural studies have revealed that KMT2A binds to the large central cavity on menin, with key residues of KMT2A (ie, F9KMT2A, P10KMT2A, and P13KMT2A) occupying well-defined hydrophobic pockets on menin (Figure 2).3,42,43 These 3 amino acids are key contributors to KMT2A’s binding to menin because their mutations to alanine substantially reduce binding affinity.26 Altogether, the molecular architecture of the menin-KMT2A interface, with well-defined and closely located hydrophobic pockets, has established menin as a “druggable” target, rationalizing the development of small-molecule inhibitors blocking this protein-protein interaction.

Discovery of first-in-class MENi

Grembecka’s and Cierpicki’s laboratories pioneered the development of MENi, and in 2012, they reported the thienopyrimidine class of compounds representing the first small-molecule inhibitors of the menin-KMT2A interaction.2,3 This work included the discovery of MI-2-2 MENi (50% inhibitory concentration [IC50], 520nM; Figure 3A) and reported the crystal structure demonstrating that MI-2-2 directly binds to menin at the KMT2A binding site, mimicking the critical interactions of residues F9KMT2A and P13KMT2A with menin (Figure 4A).3 Further optimization efforts led to the development of MI-503 (IC50, 15nM; Figure 3A), with substantially improved activity and drug-like properties, including high oral bioavailability in mice.5,6 MI-503 strongly inhibited cell growth and induced differentiation of KMT2A-r leukemia cells, accompanied by downregulation of the KMT2A fusion protein target genes involved in leukemogenesis (eg, HOXA9, MEIS1, PBX3, and MEF2C), supporting an on-target mechanism of action.5 Importantly, MI-503 was the first MENi to demonstrate in vivo activity, manifested by reduced leukemia burden and prolonged survival in mouse models of KMT2A-r leukemia.5 Notably, MI-503 did not impair normal hematopoiesis in mice. These promising preclinical results provided in vivo proof of principle that MENi could represent an effective therapeutic strategy for acute leukemia, attracting industry attention to initiate MENi programs.

Development of advanced MENi and their activity in preclinical AML models

Although several classes of MENi have been reported, this review highlights only those that entered clinical testing and closely related analogs essential to their development. To date, 2 chemically distinct classes of MENi have advanced into clinical evaluation: the thienopyrimidine compounds (ziftomenib and DS-1594) and the pyrimidine/triazine class of compounds (revumenib, bleximenib, enzomenib, and zefamenib).

Thienopyrimidine class of MENi: MI-3454 and ziftomenib

Medicinal chemistry optimization of the thienopyrimidine MENi MI-503 by the Grembecka and Cierpicki laboratories led to the development of MI-3454 with subnanomolar potency (IC50, 0.51nM; Figure 3A), which interacts with F9KMT2A and P13KMT2A binding pockets on menin (Figure 4B).9 MI-3454 blocked proliferation (50% growth inhibitory concentration [GI50], 7nM-28nM) and induced differentiation in KMT2A-r and NPM1-mut leukemia cells, accompanied by strong downregulation of the menin-KMT2A target genes, including HOXA9 and MEIS1, at low nanomolar concentrations.9 In vivo, MI-3454 induced complete, long-lasting remission or regression of leukemia in multiple KMT2A-r and NPM1-mut xenograft and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, associated with strong downregulation of menin-KMT2A–dependent genes, including MEIS1, and with no impact on normal hematopoiesis.9 More recently, MI-3454 was also shown to slow down leukemia progression in NUP98-rearranged (NUP98-r) leukemia PDX models.40

The collaboration of the Grembecka and Cierpicki laboratories with Kura Oncology led to the discovery of ziftomenib (KO-539; Figure 3B), a structural analog of MI-3454, which also occupies F9KMT2A and P13KMT2A binding pockets on menin.10 Ziftomenib demonstrates pronounced antileukemic activity, both in vitro (IC50, 4nM in biochemical assays; GI50, 4.5nM-28nM in leukemia cells) and in vivo, resulting in durable survival benefit or long-lasting remission in AML xenograft and PDX mouse models with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut.10,44 When tested in leukemia cells and primary patient samples with menin-KMT2A dependency, ziftomenib profoundly reduced clonogenic potential and strongly induced differentiation, accompanied by pronounced downregulation of key target genes (MEIS1, HOXA9, HOXB2).10 Importantly, ziftomenib’s improved pharmacokinetic profile over MI-3454 enables once-daily dosing,10 representing an important therapeutic advantage. The strong preclinical efficacy and favorable drug-like properties led to the clinical translation of ziftomenib to patients with R/R AML in 2019 (NCT04067336), as described in the ‘Clinical activity of MENi montherapy’ section (Table 1).

The thienopyrimidine scaffold was also adopted by Daiichi Sankyo, resulting in DS-1594 (Figure 3B), a potent (IC50, 1.4nM) and orally bioavailable MENi with promising antileukemic activity in leukemia cells and mouse models of AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut.49 Although clinical trials with DS-1594 were initiated in 2021 in patients with AML (NCT04752163), this compound was recently withdrawn from further clinical studies likely because of lack of efficacy at studied dose levels and portfolio realignment by the company.16,50

Pyrimidine/triazine-based MENi: revumenib, bleximenib, enzomenib, zefamenib

In 2019, Krivtsov et al51 reported a chemically distinct MENi, VTP50469 (Figure 3A), which represents the pyrimidine-based class of compounds. Similarly to the thienopyrimidine MENi, VTP50469 occupies the KMT2A binding site on menin and interacts with the 3 subpockets used by KMT2A: F9KMT2A, P10KMT2A, and P13KMT2A (Figure 4B). In leukemia cells with menin-KMT2A dependency, VTP50469 effectively suppressed the expression of leukemia-relevant genes (MEIS1, PBX3, MEF2C), blocked proliferation (GI50, 13nM-37nM), and induced differentiation.51 In vivo, this compound induced complete leukemia remission, reduced leukemia burden, or provided substantial survival benefit in KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut xenograft and PDX models, accompanied by downregulation of MEIS1 and PBX3.31,51 Subsequently, a very close structural analog of VTP50469 with similar preclinical activity, namely, SNDX-5613 (revumenib; Figure 3C), developed by Syndax Pharmaceuticals, was introduced to phase 1/2 clinical trials in patients with R/R AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut.14,52 Revumenib is a potent (IC50, 1.2nM),51 selective, and orally bioavailable MENi that reduces leukemia burden and increases survival in PDX models of KMT2A-r leukemia.52 The promising preclinical activity of revumenib supported its clinical translation to patients with leukemia with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut (AUGMENT-101, NCT0406539913).

More recently, Janssen reported another MENi, JNJ-75276617 (bleximenib),53 a structural analog of revumenib in which the pyrimidine ring is replaced by triazine and a different moiety is introduced to interact with the P13 pocket on menin (Figure 3C). Bleximenib showed potent antiproliferative activity (GI50, 5nM-45nM) and induced differentiation in KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut leukemia cells, accompanied by downregulation of MEIS1 and FMS-like tyrosine kinase 3 (FLT3).53 In xenograft models of AML and ALL that rely on the menin-KMT2A interaction, bleximenib reduced leukemia burden and induced significant survival benefit or complete remission (CR). These findings supported the clinical translation of this compound to patients with R/R acute leukemia (NCT04811560).53 Two other MENi with structural similarity to revumenib, namely, BN-104 (zefamenib developed by BioNova) and DSP-4336 (enzomenib, developed by Sumitomo Pharma; Figure 3C), were advanced into phase 1/2 clinical studies in AML in 2024,16 although their preclinical activity has not been reported to date. Few other MENi with undisclosed chemical structures have also recently entered clinical evaluation in AML, but their preclinical activity has not been described (see the study by Wenge and Armstrong for details).16

Clinical activity of MENi as monotherapy

There are now 2 FDA-approved MENi for R/R AML, revumenib and ziftomenib, based on contemporaneous, single-arm phase 1/2 trials demonstrating meaningful single-agent efficacy in targeted populations of high unmet need (Table 1). Other key compounds under clinical investigation with encouraging early results include enzomenib and bleximenib.

Revumenib (Figure 3C), the first FDA-approved MENi in November 2024, is commercially available for R/R AML with either KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut. Early phase 1 results from AUGMENT-101 showed CR/CR with partial hematologic recovery (CRh) rate of 30% in R/R AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut (n = 60), and phase 2 expansion in KMT2A-r (efficacy evaluable, n = 57) showed an overall response rate (ORR) of 63%, CR/CRh rate of 23%, and minimal residual disease (MRD) negativity in 70% of CR/CRh responders, leading to its initial approval in KMT2A-r (Table 1).14 Median duration of response (DOR) was limited (4.3 months), with median CR/CRh duration of 6.4 months. However, updated follow-up showed improved CR/CRh duration of 13 months.47

Subsequent phase 2 expansion of revumenib in R/R AML with NPM1-mut (efficacy evaluable, n = 64) showed ORR of 47% and CR/CRh rate of 23%, comparable to clinical activity in KMT2A-r. Median DOR was short in NPM1-mut (4.4 months), with median CR/CRh duration of 4.7 months (Table 1).15 Length of follow-up and patient censoring may be confounding for response duration. Median overall survival (OS) in CR/CRh responders was 23 months. Revumenib was granted additional regulatory approval for NPM1-mut in October 2025.

Rates of differentiation syndrome (DS), an important class effect seen with revumenib, were 28% in KMT2A-r (DS, 16% grade ≥3) and 19% in NPM1-mut (DS, 13% grade ≥3; Table 1). Corrected QT interval (QTc) prolongation was noted, with 14% grades 3 to 4 in safety population (n = 94).14 Revumenib requires QTc monitoring and dose adjustment for strong inhibitor azoles (160 mg twice daily with azole vs 270 mg twice daily without azole) due to drug-drug interactions (DDI).

Ziftomenib (600 mg daily; Figure 3B) is the most recently approved (in November 2025) MENi for R/R AML with NPM1-mut, representing the only once-daily MENi that is FDA approved or in advanced clinical development. In phase 1b (n = 20), ziftomenib demonstrated an ORR of 45% and a CR/CRh rate of 35%,11 whereas phase 2 results (n = 92) showed corresponding rates of 33% and 22%, meeting its primary end point for agency approval (Table 1).12 MRD negativity was observed in 61% of CR/CRh responders, with a median DOR of 4.6 months and OS of 18.4 months among responders (KOMET-001).12 Enrollment in KOMET-001 was halted for KMT2A-r given lower response rates and likely higher levels of DS (Table 1).11 Ziftomenib was better tolerated in patients with NPM1-mut, with DS manifested in 25% of patients (DS, 15% grade 3; no grade 4-5) and QTc prolongation observed at a relatively low rate.12 The risk of DS may be ameliorated by early mitigation measures, preemptive corticosteroids, and chemotherapy combinations that may blunt the inflammatory response provoked by potent differentiating agents, which applies to all MENi, but perhaps more strongly to ziftomenib, and in the setting of KMT2A-r.

Enzomenib (Figure 3C) appears somewhat distinct from other MENi given its unique physicochemical properties (amide bond, favorable absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion profile), lack of significant QTc prolongation, and lower rate of severe DS (Table 1), which may be because of the kinetics of intracellular uptake and low lipophilicity/accumulation, among other factors.45,54 As presented by Daver et al, updated results from the phase 1 trial of enzomenib monotherapy demonstrated notably high response rates in R/R AML with both KMT2A-r (ORR, 73%; CR/CRh rate, 40% at recommended phase 2 dose of 300 mg twice daily) and NPM1-mut (ORR, 52%; CR/CRh rate, 44% at doses ≥200 mg twice daily), with emerging evidence of response durability in KMT2A-r (median CR/CRh duration, 12.5 months; Table 1).45 The registration-enabling phase 2 trial (HORIZEN-1) of enzomenib in KMT2A-r (300 mg twice daily) and NPM1-mut (dose optimization, 200 mg vs 300 mg twice daily) is ongoing (Table 1). Enzomenib is also being explored in R/R myelodysplastic syndrome (mutation agnostic), R/R AML with TP53 mutation, and R/R multiple myeloma as part of a multiarm phase 1/2 trial.55 The mechanism of action in mutations and disease subtypes not associated with canonical HOX gene activation remains to be elucidated, and these cohorts should be considered exploratory.

Bleximenib (Figure 3C) is also being evaluated as a single agent in R/R AML (cAMeLot-1 study), now at the recommended phase 2 dose of 100 mg twice daily with step-up dosing during the first 2 weeks (50 mg twice daily) to mitigate severe DS. Response rates with bleximenib were similar to revumenib in both KMT2A-r and NPM1-mut R/R AML (Table 1). QT prolongation was not reported. The cAMeLot-1 study also included NUP98 and NUP214 gene rearrangements.56

All 4 of the aforementioned studies included mixed populations of R/R acute leukemia with KMT2A-r or with NUP98-r, and although most patients had AML, a minority had ALL or mixed-phenotype acute leukemia. All patients with NPM1-mut had AML. New data from the American Society of Hematology (ASH) 2025 showed similar efficacy across KMT2A-r leukemia subtypes treated with revumenib.57 AUGMENT-101 was the only study to enroll pediatric ALL.

In addition to revumenib, ziftomenib, enzomenib, and bleximenib, other MENi, namely, zefamenib (Figure 3C), HMPL-506, and balomenib, are under clinical exploration in first-in-human trials (Table 1), for a total of 7 MENi being developed in leukemia. Phase 1/2 trial of DS-1594 (Figure 3B) was terminated early because of lack of efficacy, and the program is closed.50 Finally, a ninth MENi (icovamenib, which binds covalently) is no longer being clinically developed in hematologic malignancies but under active investigation for diabetes and weight loss, showing synergy with GLP-1 antagonists and restoration of pancreatic β-cell function in preclinical models.58,59

Biomarkers of treatment response and MRD

Based on preclinical studies,9,10 MEIS1 has been proposed as a potential biomarker of treatment response to MENi in patients with leukemia. In clinical settings, MEIS1 expression was measured as a pharmacodynamic biomarker of ziftomenib activity in patients with AML included in the trial,11 revealing >85% stronger reduction at the end of cycle 1 (28 days of treatment) for ziftomenib at 600 mg dose vs 200 mg. Other MENi have shown similar pharmacodynamic findings relating to MEIS1 as a fundamental biomarker of response, as well as increased expression of myeloid differentiation markers such as CD11b (integrin).60

As encouraging as treatment response is with MENi monotherapy in R/R AML, primary and secondary (acquired) resistance is common. The average ORR of MENi is ∼50%, trending modestly higher in KMT2A-r compared to NPM1-mut. Thus, half of patients have no response at all, and most other relapse. When considering outcomes, it bears to mention that all MENi monotherapy trials enrolled extremely high-risk patients with R/R AML, with ∼25% to 50% having received prior allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT; particularly KMT2A-r). CR/CRh is associated with longer response duration than CR with incomplete hematologic recovery or morphologic leukemia-free state, as shown with both revumenib and enzomenib in KMT2A-r. It follows that depth of response, including MRD negativity, may be an important biomarker for survival, particularly in phase 3 trials of MENi combination therapy. MRD by multiparameter flow cytometry is limited because of lack of sensitivity (0.1%-0.01%), particularly in AML with monocytic differentiation. Molecular MRD for NPM1-mut (polymerase chain reaction [PCR]/next-generation sequencing [NGS]) has emerged as a powerful predictive tool, with commercial assay sensitivity of 0.005% using bidirectional paired-end NGS (Invivoscribe, San Diego, CA). Molecular MRD for KMT2A-r by PCR techniques (Pupil Bio; Pairidex) are also being investigated but not yet in routine use.

It is unknown if DS is associated with response, and although it may be, severe DS-causing treatment interruption may delay or even prevent response. MENi-DS may be related to disease factors, such as extramedullary disease, total leukemia burden, increased sensitivity of KMT2A-r (more direct interaction), and drug-specific pharmacologic properties, such as tissue penetration and drug accumulation. Macrophage activation syndrome may be related to DS or disease progression and has also been described with MENi, in some cases leading to overt hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis, which can be catastrophic if unchecked.

Resistance to MENi

Despite the promising activity of MENi as monotherapy in patients with leukemia, clinical studies with revumenib revealed somatic point mutations in the MEN1 gene encoding menin in ∼40% of patients with AML on the drug.61 Specifically, the most prevalent mutations included M327I/V, T349M, G331R, and G331D,61 all of which are located in the revumenib binding site on menin (Figure 4C-D).62 These mutations emerge relatively rapidly, often within the 2 treatment cycles (∼60 days), and lead to relapse in a subset of patients with AML who initially respond to revumenib. Biochemical studies using the menin mutants identified in patients with AML demonstrated a significant reduction (5-fold to >1000-fold) in revumenib’s ability to inhibit the menin-KMT2A interaction.61,62 Interestingly, structural and biochemical studies revealed that these mutations do not impair KMT2A binding to menin (Figure 4C).62 Instead, these mutations, which extend beyond the KMT2A binding interface on menin, create steric clashes with revumenib,61,62 affecting drug binding mode and activity (Figure 4D). Notably, the same mutations were detected in preclinical base-editor screens and in PDX models of AML upon revumenib treatment, confirming that these mutations can arise under therapeutic pressure and represent a key mechanism of acquired resistance.61

Preclinical and clinical studies on resistance to other MENi remain limited. Ziftomenib was shown to retain biochemical, cellular, and in vivo activity with T349M and G331R menin mutants, but not with M327I mutant.10,11 In the clinical setting, out of 29 patients screened for known MEN1 mutations, only 1 patient receiving ziftomenib was detected to harbor M327I MEN1 mutation, although the sensitivity of the quantitative reverse transcription PCR assay used in this study was limited.11 In vitro and cellular studies with bleximenib demonstrate moderately reduced sensitivity in M327I and T349M mutants compared to wild-type menin.53 However, systematic assessment of MEN1 mutations in patients undergoing treatment with bleximenib or other clinical MENi, besides revumenib, is currently lacking, but it would be important to understand the frequency and clinical relevance of MEN1 mutations induced by these drugs. In addition, mutations in other genes apart from MEN1 (eg, NRAS G12D mutation was identified upon ziftomenib treatment)11 could represent another mechanism of genetic resistance, which requires further investigation. Preclinical studies indicate other possible resistance mechanisms to menin inhibition, including repression of noncanonical KMT2C/D-driven myeloid differentiation program63 or loss of subunits of the PRC1.1 complex,64, 65, 66 but these mechanisms require clinical validation.

Another source of MENi resistance may be FLT3 internal tandem duplication (FLT3-ITD) persistence or emergence and subclonal outgrowth, resulting in clonal dominance independent of primary driver inhibition. FLT3-associated progression has been demonstrated in preclinical leukemia models67 and anecdotally described in patients with R/R AML with NPM1-mut or KMT2A-r treated with either MENi monotherapy or MENi plus venetoclax (VEN) hypomethylating agent (HMA), but this requires confirmation and further study. FLT3 is comutated more commonly with NPM1-mut, which may, in part, explain the numerically shorter DOR in these patients. FLT3-ITD persistence, even by molecular MRD, is strongly associated with AML relapse and can be successfully targeted with FLT3 inhibitors (FLT3i), which have shown preclinical synergy with MENi in comutated models.67, 68, 69 Therefore, trials of revumenib, ziftomenib, and enzomenib plus the type 1 FLT3i gilteritinib, ziftomenib plus quizartinib, and revumenib plus midostaurin are underway (Table 2).70, 71, 72 Many other targeted agents and differentiation partners are being explored preclinically in combination with MENi, including E3 ligase modulators, XPO1 inhibitors, IDH inhibitors, LSD1 inhibitors, BET inhibitors, and CBP/P300 inhibitors.16,66,73,74

Combinatorial treatments with MENi: preclinical studies

Combination therapies may provide an effective way to prevent or overcome resistance to menin inhibition. Indeed, several combinatorial studies with MENi and other targeted agents were reported even before the discovery of clinical resistance to revumenib. Combinations with other targeted agents already approved for AML are of particular interest because they can be rapidly translated to patients. Among these, cotreatment of KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut AML cells with VTP-50469 (a revumenib analog) and the BCL-2 inhibitor VEN (FDA approved for AML) resulted in synergistic cell lethality52 and reduced leukemia burden in vivo, leading to improved survival of mice bearing KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut leukemia.52 Similarly, synergistic effects were reported upon combination of VEN with other MENi, including ziftomenib88 and bleximenib.53 Furthermore, strong synergy was observed upon combination of MI-3454 (a ziftomenib analog) with FLT3i gilteritinib in leukemia cells with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut harboring, simultaneously, FLT3 mutations or FLT3-ITD.89 Mechanistically, the combination of menin and FLT3i resulted in more profound downregulation of MEIS1 and HOXA cluster genes and had stronger effects on MYC, stemness, and differentiation pathways over single agents, resulting in complete, long-lasting remission in PDX models of AML.89 Strong synergistic effects were also observed upon combination of MENi with another FLT3i, quizartinib, in KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut leukemia models.67,89 These promising preclinical results strongly support the clinical evaluation of MENi combinations with VEN or FLT3i, which are currently underway (see below the ‘Clinical studies for MENi combinations’ section for details).

Several other MENi-based combination therapies have also shown synergistic activity in preclinical studies, as reviewed recently.16 For example, the combination of MI-3454 with the CDK6 kinase inhibitor palbociclib synergistically impaired cell growth, colony formation, and differentiation in patient samples with NUP98 translocations and markedly enhanced the antileukemic activity of MENi in PDX models of NUP98-r leukemia.40 Furthermore, mezigdomide, a small-molecule degrader of the transcription factor Ikaros, synergized with revumenib in preclinical models of KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut leukemia and was able to overcome resistance in MEN1-mutant leukemia models.90,91 In addition, cotargeting of menin and histone acetyltransferases KAT6/KAT7 had synergistic effects in KMT2A-r, NPM1-mut, and NUP98-r leukemia models.92,93 Finally, synergistic effects have also been observed for MENi combined with various epigenetic agents that have been previously shown to demonstrate activity in AML cells with menin-KMT2A dependency, including inhibitors of DOT1,94,95 BET proteins,74 LSD1,96 or CBP/p300.16,74 Overall, these examples highlight the potential for using MENi as a therapeutic backbone for combination strategies to achieve more effective AML treatments.

Clinical studies for MENi combinations

The future of MENi therapy is shaping into frontline intensive or nonintensive multiagent combination regimens, that is, MENi with 7+3 or VEN-HMA. Optimal combinations of agents by age group and genotype are yet to be determined. Combination therapy may help overcome intrinsic causes of MENi resistance (KMT2A fusion partner, cryptic fusions/insertions, RAS pathway activation) and subvert selective pressure for acquired resistance (MEN1 mutations, FLT3-ITD, epigenetic remodeling of noncanonical pathways [MYC and TP53]).61,66,97,98 Data on menin resistance remain largely preclinical, clinically anecdotal, and extrapolated from other targeted therapies and an increasing awareness of MRD. Clinically, MENi resistance remains a largely uncharted territory except for several MEN1 mutations described with revumenib (∼40%), and data remain limited with other MENi regarding the rate of MEN1 mutations and if resistance can be overcome, apart from a single case report with bleximenib showing clinical response in a patient with multiply relapsed KMT2A-r MEN1 M327I mutant treated with prior revumenib and allogeneic HCT.99

Combination therapy (R/R AML)

All 4 of the most clinically advanced MENi are being explored in triplet combinations with VEN-HMA in R/R AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut (or with NUP98-r; Table 2). Revumenib was evaluated on the single-center SAVE-AML trial (with VEN and oral decitabine or cedazuridine), showing encouragingly high response rates (ORR, 82%; CR/CRh rate, 48%; n = 33) and early evidence of response durability.100

New data for VEN plus azacitidine (AZA) plus ziftomenib in R/R NPM1-mut AML were presented at ASH 2025 (KOMET-007), with similar findings of encouraging triplet activity (Table 2).101 Phase 1 data were also presented at ASH 2025 for VEN plus AZA plus enzomenib triplet therapy in R/R AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut, with high response rates in preliminary analysis, including in patients with prior VEN and MENi exposure (Table 2).102 Bleximenib is also under evaluation with VEN (with or without AZA) in R/R AML as part of the ALE1002 phase 1b study (Table 2).103

The degree of benefit that VEN plus HMA adds to MENi in R/R AML is unclear and may depend on driver mutation, comutations, and prior VEN and/or MENi exposure. Optimal VEN treatment duration with triplet therapy (R/R or first-line) also remains under question, and 14 days of VEN in cycle 1 is likely sufficient. There was increased treatment-related myelosuppression using 28 days of VEN on BEAT AML in newly diagnosed (ND) AML.77 Rates and severity of DS appear reduced with triplet therapy across trials, but the risk is not eliminated.

Trials of MENi plus salvage chemotherapy are also described in Table 2, as are MENi plus FLT3i combinations in R/R AML with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut and co-occurring FLT3-ITD or tyrosine kinase domain mutations. FLT3-ITD is emerging as a possible key resistance mechanism to MENi, and its presence has strong implications for frontline therapy as well. Although FLT3 mutations co-occur with KMT2A-r, they are more commonly seen with NPM1-mut, whereas RAS mutations are more common with KMT2A-r. Whether activated signaling pathway resistance to MENi is HOXA9/MEIS1 axis dependent is unknown.

Preclinical combinations of novel agents with MENi are also under evaluation as terminal differentiation therapy without chemotherapy or with minimal chemotherapy for cytoreduction, particularly in KMT2A-r AML.16,73,74,90 Trials of MENi plus IDH inhibitor in NPM1/IDH comutant R/R AML are also planned.

In terms of selection of targeted therapy if >1 targetable mutation is present, this is an area for further study and may depend on multiple competing factors, such as line of therapy, combination therapy vs single agent, clonal dynamics, prior therapy exposure, and levels of MRD. In younger patients with FLT3-ITD, the standard of care would be 7+3 plus quizartinib (or midostaurin) regardless of comutations.104 Beyond that specific setting, in our experience and as conceptual guideline, KMT2A-r is always clonally dominant and should generally be targeted first, with FLT3-ITD targeted second and/or as maintenance for MRD positivity. NPM1/FLT3-ITD–comutated AML is highly dependent on FLT3 signaling,105 and priority to FLT3 targeting (over menin) should be considered depending on the clonal and therapeutic context. If NPM1 and IDH1/2 are comutated without FLT3, then targeting priority likely depends on therapy line and if IDH is clonally dominant or equivalent to NPM1 vs branching subclone.106 The possibility of therapy sequencing or targeted combinations in response to changing clonal dynamics is intriguing for the future and may apply to all of the previously discussed cases. NPM1 is particularly comutation dependent, and distinct NPM1 variants (A/B/D vs non-ABD) and genotypes (NPM1/DNMT3A, NPM1/IDH, NPM1/TET2, NPM1/other, each with or without FLT3-ITD) may have differential sensitivity to menin inhibition and combination approaches.107

Lastly, the use of any single agent or targeted therapy combination should be used with caution in patients with rapidly proliferative or bulky disease, and cytoreduction with hydroxyurea and, perhaps more effectively, with flat-dose cytarabine or cyclophosphamide may be required to mitigate DS and allow time for response.

Combination therapy (untreated AML)

Revumenib was successfully combined with VEN plus AZA in the intensive chemotherapy (IC)–ineligible KMT2A-r and NPM1-mut frontline AML population as part of the multicenter BEAT AML trial (phase 1b), as reported by Zeidner et al.77 Response rates were high (ORR, 100% in KMT2A-r vs 85% in NPM1-mut) and particularly notable in KMT2A-r, which trends toward primary VEN resistance. Early mortality or drop out was seen in NPM1-mut (15%) and was perhaps related to prolonged VEN duration and excess myelosuppression. An international randomized trial (VEN plus AZA plus revumenib vs VEN plus AZA plus placebo; EVOLVE-2) is ongoing. Revumenib was successfully combined with VEN and oral decitabine/cedazuridine in ND-AML on the SAVE-AML trial (VEN duration reduced to 14 days in cycle 1), with similar reported outcomes on a single-center study (Table 2).108

Promising first-line data with VEN plus AZA plus ziftomenib for IC-ineligible NPM1-mut AML were presented at ASH 2025 (KOMET-007),109 and a large multiarm phase 3 trial is now underway in ND-AML (KOMET-017), with coprimary end point of CR and OS in NPM1-mut (KMT2A-r excluded from nonintensive arm; Table 2). Frontline studies of enzomenib (phase 1 expansion) and bleximenib (ALE1002 and cAMeLot-2) with VEN plus AZA are also underway in KMT2A-r and NPM1-mut ND-AML, with high response rates seen on ALE1002 (bleximenib starts C1D4) in a small cohort (Table 2).103 The phase 3 cAMeLot-2 study excludes FLT3 comutants, whereas other frontline VEN plus HMA triplets do not.

Monocytic LSC–driven resistance to VEN may be overcome by triplet therapy synergy, including in KMT2A-r or high-risk NPM1-mut, such as TET2 and/or FLT3 comutated.110 KMT2A fusion partner may also be important for MENi triplet therapy resistance, and more data are needed to determine if t(9;11)/KMT2A-MLLT3, for example, has better outcomes than variable translocations, as with conventional chemotherapy.111

Multiple frontline studies are assessing 7+3 plus MENi, the largest KOMET-017 (ziftomenib; phase 3) in untreated IC-eligible patients with KMT2A-r or NPM1-mut AML, with coprimary end point of event-free survival and CR with MRD negativity (NGS assay; NPM1 only), a first in AML for potential surrogate survival end point (Table 3). FLT3 comutation is exclusionary on KOMET-017 (intensive arm), and quadruplet (QUAD) therapy or FLT3i sequencing for MRD positivity may be considered. Bleximenib is also being evaluated with 7+3 on HOVON181 (phase 3; Table 3). Earlier phase combinations of enzomenib and revumenib with IC are listed in Table 2. MENi maintenance after allogeneic HCT is being explored on multiple arms of myriad studies. Patients who may benefit from MENi maintenance, based on MRD level/method, acute leukemia subtype/genotype, and therapy intensity/line, remain to be elucidated.

Choice of MENi for leukemia treatment

Menin is one of the most promising targets to be discovered in AML, along with RARA, FLT3-ITD, BCL2, and IDH1/2. Two MENi are approved, with more likely to come, including second- and third-generation inhibitors, analogous to tyrosine kinase inhibitors in chronic myeloid leukemia. It is not known if some MENi overcome resistance to others in the clinic, via second-site mutation inhibition or other mechanism, and work here to date remains in vitro or in animal models.53,113,114 MENi may expand into other AML genotypes, myelodysplastic syndrome or myeloproliferative neoplasms, inflammation, and clonal hematopoiesis and already have at early stages.16,55,115 The ideal choice of MENi requires balance between agent-specific toxicity profile (QTc, DDI, DS, myelosuppression), efficacy, comorbidities, disease characteristics (disease burden, genotype, extramedullary disease, comutations), combination therapy (and type), monotherapy or maintenance approach, MENi resistance, prior treatment exposure, and ease of administration/compliance (eg, once-daily [ziftomenib] vs twice-daily [revumenib, enzomenib, bleximenib] dosing and significant DDI [revumenib]). Choice of agent will likely need to be individualized for a patient-centered approach based on available data. All clinically available MENi are active; there is no single best in class, and the optimal agent for each patient will depend on the therapeutic context. Both approved MENi are currently labeled for single-agent use in R/R acute leukemia, and equipoise is needed for MENi addition to backbone salvage regimens or targeted therapy combinations outside of a clinical trial. The choice and use of MENi in the posttransplant relapse setting needs to be further assessed, and early outcomes have been poor across trials; posttransplant maintenance is also being explored and may have greater success for high-risk subtypes and in targeting MRD persistence.11, 12, 13, 14, 15

Conclusions and future perspectives

Two decades after the discovery of menin’s role in leukemia,17 7 small-molecule MENi are being extensively used in clinical studies, demonstrating promising effects in patients with R/R AML and ALL, with revumenib and ziftomenib already granted FDA approval. Importantly, in KMT2A-r leukemia, menin binds to the N-terminal portion of KMT2A retained in all fusion proteins; accordingly, based on current evidence, fusion partner diversity does not affect sensitivity to MENi. The ongoing clinical trials of MENi in combination with IC, VEN-based therapy, and targeted agents seem to provide substantial improvement of their effectiveness in patients with AML, although more comprehensive efficacy and safety data are needed to fully assess the benefits of combination treatment across clinical settings. Frontline triplet combinations are particularly appealing for KMT2A-r and high-risk NPM1-mut (eg, ziftomenib with 7+3 [FLT3 wild-type] or VEN plus AZA on KOMET-017). Furthermore, clinical activity in patients with leukemia beyond KMT2A-r and NPM1-mut, such as those with NUP98-r or other leukemias with high HOX/MEIS1 expression, which constitute known biomarkers for sensitivity to MENi, or those with activation of noncanonical menin-dependent transcriptional programs, still needs to be assessed.

To date, the resistance mutations arising from MENi treatment were only comprehensively studied for revumenib,61 but such mutations are likely to arise for other MENi under clinical evaluation, raising the question of how to address resistance in patients with leukemia. One potential strategy is to develop a new generation of MENi with enhanced potency that solely occupies the KMT2A-binding site on menin.62 Another approach is to interchange between structurally distinct MENi, as some agents might retain activity against specific MEN1 mutations. For example, if resistance to revumenib emerges because of MEN1 point mutations, switching to ziftomenib (eg, for T349M or G331R mutants) or bleximenib (eg, for M327I mutant) could be considered.10,53 Furthermore, combination therapies might help prevent resistance in a more general way (eg, regardless of the resistance mechanism) due to faster and more effective eradication of leukemic cells vs monotherapy. Since MENi entered clinical trials only 6 years ago, their long-term toxicity, both on-target and off-target, are still not completely recognized. Importantly, the application of MENi might extend beyond leukemia because their antitumor activity was reported in preclinical models of solid tumors (prostate cancer, hepatocellular carcinoma, breast cancer),116, 117, 118, 119, 120 and clinical trials are currently underway for the MENi revumenib in colorectal cancer (NCT05731947) or ziftomenib in combination with imatinib in gastrointestinal stromal tumor (NCT06655246). Besides that, the covalent MENi icovamenib is currently being explored in clinical trials in patients with type 2 diabetes (NCT07502508). Overall, MENi have shown high efficacy in patients with leukemia, and ongoing clinical studies seem promising to substantially improve outcomes for patients with AML, supporting that MENi might become an essential therapy for these patients.
