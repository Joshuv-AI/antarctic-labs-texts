Spatial instruction of tissue immunity
Authors: Miguel Reina-Campos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTissue immunity must meet the architectural and physiological demands of each organ, from viral entry in the respiratory tract to immune surveillance in the gastrointestinal mucosa. Recent advances in spatial technologies and computational biology now allow us to map entire immune communities in situ, capturing not only their composition but their positional logic, connectivity patterns, and local transcriptional landscapes. These tools are revealing that immune function is not evenly distributed but is patterned along regionalized cytokine gradients, anatomical landmarks, and physical niches that confer specialized capabilities. Understanding the principles driving this spatiotemporal logic is essential to decipher how immune networks are built, maintained, and subverted in disease. To this end, network topology analyses, immune allocation plots, and spatial reference frameworks are beginning to define the “wiring diagrams” of immunity, while emerging perturbation-coupled spatial approaches enable causal dissection of the signals that program location-specific phenotypes. These insights have broad implications, from explaining why certain organs resist tumor initiation or metastasis, to revealing metabolic constraints on immune cells in solid tumors, to understanding clonal lymphocyte dynamics in health and disease. Here, we synthesize recent conceptual and technological advances that are transforming how we study tissue immunity; highlight exemplar findings from infection, cancer, and autoimmunity; and outline the experimental and computational innovations needed to bridge key knowledge gaps. We propose that the next phase of immunology will require integrating multiomic, high-resolution spatial data with predictive models of immune behavior to forecast disease risk, design personalized therapies, and ultimately deploy immune protection at the right place and time.Keywords: tissue immunity, spatial transcriptomics, T cells, cancer, infection

Introduction: Tissue immunity meets form meets function

The shape, size, and composition of our organs are intrinsically tied to their function, from nutrient absorption in the small intestine to mechanical force generation in muscle. Form must meet function, which is accomplished by diverse cellular communities distinctly organized in various shapes and sizes, working toward a common goal. This diversity presents a remarkable logistical challenge for the immune system, as it must deploy effective solutions that meet the unique demands and potential liabilities at each site, from tiny airborne viruses in the respiratory mucosa to worms in the gastrointestinal tract. Recent high-resolution in situ technologies have provided a breakthrough in our ability to map these communities at scale.1 Emerging studies support the nascent notion that immune cells form interactive and spatially organized networks shaped by local anatomical constraints, are uniquely adapted to distinct organ architectures and environments, and are optimized by evolutionary pressures to meet distinct functional demands.1,2 We posit that over evolutionary time, each tissue has arrived at a characteristic composition, organization, and immune function optimized to meet its unique physiology. We argue that elucidating the core principles of these archetypal immune ensembles when they operate at their peak, what we term here the tissue immune blueprint, holds tremendous promise for advancing human health.

Here, we discuss new studies and emerging ideas that offer a different perspective on understanding how tissue immunity is achieved. For instance, we discuss spatial instruction as the process by which cues derived from tissues signal immune cells to influence their movement, location, differentiation, and function. We also describe how the emerging use of innovative technologies that generate super-resolved, comprehensive maps of cells within tissues, combined with recent advances in computational biology, provides a breakthrough to address major knowledge gaps. We then outline key challenges and future advances that we expect will help reveal the organizing principles, connectivity patterns, and signaling dynamics that underpin effective immune function and tissue protection. The ideas presented here rely on 2 foundational premises. First, we view tissue immune blueprints through the lens of pathogenic infections, which we argue have imposed the strongest evolutionary pressures at most anatomical sites. Studying immunity in this context provides a unifying reference point, from which insights can be extended across diverse disease settings, from cardiovascular disease to cancer, where immune mechanisms play a central role. Second, we posit that the principles underlying tissue immune organization at a given anatomical location are intrinsically tied to the physical characteristics and spatial distributions of its cellular components. In other words, immune systems must adapt to the shapes of tissues, which must first meet the defined functions of organs. Fully decoding these blueprints will require building upon previous reductionist approaches by integrating them with frameworks that capture the collective behaviors, interactions, and emergent properties of immune communities in their native tissue contexts.

Toward a more connected understanding: Pieces, board, and rules

Decades of immunology research have laid the groundwork for understanding the key components of the immune system. Borrowing from chess, we might say that the pieces—the various immune cell types and their core functions—are largely known. While novel players and processes continue to emerge, few entirely new pieces remain to be discovered. What remains far more elusive are the rules of the game and the nature of the board itself. Unlike chess, the immune system operates across multiple playing fields, which are dynamic, ever-changing environments that grow, contract, shed, age, and regenerate. Deciphering this 4-dimensional chess game, in which timing and spatial positioning are critical for protective immunity, could help solve important immunological phenomena that currently remain unexplained,3 and offers an exciting and essential area of ongoing discovery.

The immense diversity of a fully functioning mature immune system arises from a limited pool of common progenitors.4,5 This complexity is built progressively through a series of differentiation events influenced by both intrinsic genetic programs and extrinsic environmental cues, occurring across distinct anatomical sites and developmental stages.6 Rather than fitting into a rigid dichotomy, intrinsic and extrinsic signals act in concert to shape immune cell identity and function. Understanding the spatiotemporal interactions that guide these differentiation trajectories is central to decoding immune cell ontogeny and understanding how immune cell identities are maintained, adapted, or dysregulated across health and disease. What are the inflection points and environmental inputs that steer a cell toward one fate over another? Insights from T cells and macrophages7 support the notion that immune cell differentiation follows a modular programming, in which cells acquire distinct gene modules that confer fate (e.g. memory CD8+ T cell), tissue adaptation (e.g. persistence in the small intestine), and specialized functions (e.g. residency with effector potential) (Fig. 1A).8–10 These gene sets may partially overlap and are integrated across space and time through cues such as chemokines, cytokines, nutrient sensing, and interactions with immune and nonimmune cells. For CD8 T cells, this integration likely begins during priming in the lymph node11 and continues as cells migrate, extravasate, and start to inhabit different organs (Fig. 1B).9,12–14 However, the precise sequence, source, and location of these instructive signals remain largely unresolved.

Cytokines are context-dependent, pleiotropic mediators of immune cell behavior, often with seemingly overlapping functions. While a comprehensive understanding of their signaling across diverse immune populations is beginning to emerge,15,16 the spatial determinants that shape cytokine activity in vivo remain poorly defined. Recent studies have uncovered striking regionalization of cytokine expression within the intestinal villus, for instance, IL-15 and IL-18 are enriched at the villus tip and base, respectively.2 Because homeostatic cytokines like IL-7 and IL-15 play tissue-specific, partially redundant roles in maintaining populations of lymphocytes in tissues,17,18 differential access to cytokines within the niche might play a major role in immune cell diversity and tissue occupancy. Beyond cytokine production, the spatial distribution of cells that consume, inactivate,19 or relay cytokine signals critically influences the extent and dynamics of signal propagation.20 A classic example, lymphodepletion prior to adoptive cellular transfer removes homeostatic cytokine sinks and allows adoptively transferred CD8 T cells access to a larger cytokine pool.21 In addition to limiting immune occupancy, these mechanisms of spatial regulation contribute to nongenetic heterogeneity, for instance by imprinting specific phenotypes on immune cells,22 and may represent a major force instructing immune patterning and the formation of discrete functional niches, such as tertiary lymphoid structures.23

In sum, new tools that capture multiple layers of biology within integrated immune cell communities are poised to reveal how the pieces fit together—the tissue immune blueprint—to help researchers derive first principles.

Emerging spatial technologies offer a new perspective

The rapid development of more sensitive chemistries and multiplexing strategies to detect both RNA and proteins within tissue sections is opening the immunology field to previously inaccessible biological questions.1 Unified under the term “spatial omics,” spatial transcriptomics (ST) and spatial proteomics have been named Nature Methods Method of the Year in 2020 and 2024, respectively.24,25 ST approaches refer to technologies that can profile RNA molecules in space while retaining a measure of cellular and tissue context.26 Both the increase in multiplexity (hundreds to thousands of genes) and resolution (∼50 µm to ∼100 nm) are enabling an unprecedented new look at immune cells within tissues. Two key ST modalities currently play a key role.1, 26 The first one is based on next-generation sequencing of RNA, typically polyadenylated messenger RNA, captured using spatially barcoded messenger RNA capture oligos on a solid phase.27,28 The second approach uses fluorescent probes and sequential cycles of hybridization to deconvolve the identity of the RNA molecule in situ, often referred to as imaging-based ST.29 The higher resolution of the latter enables a true single-cell approach but is limited in multiplexity by targeted probe-based approaches. Despite the limitations, immunologists are now equipped with tools that capture single-cell transcriptome profiles within large areas of tissue. Additional readouts, such as protein markers and histological stains, further aid in cell type identification, cellular segmentation, and pathology assessment. Importantly, most ST modalities capture rare and or sensitive immune cells, such as granulocytes, that would otherwise be lost during the preparation of single-cell suspensions in conventional single-cell approaches. Ultimately, these new ST technologies yield comprehensive cellular maps with detailed biological information that can be used to derive important principles in immunology (Fig. 2).

A long-standing question that can now be addressed is why some organs, despite similar cellular composition, show vastly different susceptibility to cancer. An outstanding example of this occurs along the gastrointestinal tract. The small intestine is rarely the primary site of tumor incidence, whereas the colon and rectum, although physically connected and harboring a similar number of stem cells capable of malignant transformation, experience ∼20-fold higher cancer incidence.30 Remarkably, the small intestine is also an uncommon site for metastatic colonization, despite its large size and rich vascularization.31 Genetic mouse models confirm that small intestine stem cells can indeed give rise to tumors, yet chemical carcinogenesis models bias tumor formation toward the colon and rectum.32 These patterns suggest that the cellular immune networks that surveil the small and large intestines are inherently distinct. Such tissue-specific immune diversity could be leveraged to identify the principles that underlie resilient immune networks capable of robust tumor control. For example, small intestine tissue-resident memory CD8 T (TRM) cells live in a heightened state of basal activation,33,34 contain abundant cytotoxic molecules,35,36 and are strategically positioned to offer potent and fast immune responses.36,37 Small intestine TRM cells are often used as the archetypical TRM population and a useful blueprint to derive advantageous adaptations that endow tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes with enhanced capabilitites.12,13, 38–40 Incidentally, TRM cells of the small intestine and colon are phenotypically and functionally different, with a higher density of archetypical CD103+ TRM in the small intestine compared with the colon.41 While the intrinsic programming directing CD8 T cells toward an intestinal TRM fate has been thoroughly addressed,9, 41–43 the spatiotemporal signals that program this phenotype are less understood. New data suggest specific cellular interactions within the small intestine program CD8αβ T cells to a particular location and fate.2, 44 These spatiotemporal cues coordinate the formation of 2 phenotypically distinct populations of TRM cells, progenitor-like TRM and effector-like TRM cells, which are found to be distinctly segregated along the crypt and villus top, respectively.2 These data suggest that a functional design exists to coordinate the spatial separation of TRM populations consistent with having effector-like T cells in the more exposed half of the barrier tissue, while safeguarding the progenitors in deeper tissue sections. Mechanistically, CD8αβ T cells entering the small intestine in response to an infection maintain high expression of XCL1 and XCL2, which are ligands for XCR1, a chemokine receptor expressed on conventional dendritic cells type 1 (cDC1)45 favoring CD8-cDC1 contacts.44 While the signals provided by the cDC1 are not entirely known, these interactions favor the acquisition of a TGFβ-dependent program that promotes the archetypical intraepithelial TRM phenotype.44 Given the superior functions of CD8 T cells primed by cDC1, these mechanisms can be exploited to bolster antitumor immunity44, 46,47 and may underlie the exacerbation of autoimmunity, such as diabetes.48 As the cellular organization of the small intestine in health49,50 and disease2,51 continues to emerge, these studies are revealing a sophisticated patterning that supports two foundational ideas. First, an evolved functional design, guided by regionalized cytokine signaling and other spatiotemporal cues, controls immune cell deployment. Second, the differentiation, function, and location of immune cells in tissues are tightly linked.

Extending these insights to other disease contexts and organs is an active area of exploration requiring new paradigms and visualizations. To capture cellular communities within a tissue at a glance, researchers increasingly turn to network topology analysis, in which elements of the community (each cell or cell type) are linked to their neighbors either by a defined metric of interaction, such as physical proximity or the coexpression of ligand-receptor pairs.52–54 These approaches enable an inclusive and intuitive global layout of the community (Fig. 3A). An alternative approach leverages tissue-specific reference coordinate systems, previously termed immune allocation plots (IMAPs),2 which provide a useful approach to map local immune cell communities. IMAPs are spatial frameworks representing simplified coordinate systems that enable the visualization and analysis of cells within a frame of reference based on known anatomical features. Repetitive functional tissue units, such as the renal glomerulus, liver lobule, germinal center, intestinal villus, or lung alveolus, are especially amenable to this approach. For example, in the small intestine, the position of every cell can be mapped onto a 2-dimensional space by calculating the distance to the nearest epithelial cell (x) and the distance to the base of the muscularis (y) (Fig. 3B). IMAPs are easy to interpret, enabling the visualization of overall shifts in cell location at a glance. They are created unbiasedly through reproducible code and increase analytical power by pseudo-bulking all cells within the same 2-dimensional space, which facilitates the visualization of sparse gene expression as a function of spatial positioning. Those with clearly delineated structures, like the epidermis and dermis of the skin, or ventricles in the heart, might still benefit from this strategy. However, not all tissues contain functional repetitive structures or clear anatomical waypoints. Others, like the bone marrow, might appear to contain immune cells in no apparent order, yet have recently been shown to also follow a degree of spatial patterning.55 Alternatively, cellular networks, constructed on measures of cellular connectivity, such as spatial proximity or ligand-receptor coexpression, can reveal cliques or niches that favor specific biological functions (Fig. 3C). Thus, while some tissues are heavily structured and might be modelled by reference to functional axes (small intestine), others might require deriving probabilistic models (bone marrow), and unbiased neighborhood-based approaches.56,57 Combined, these computational methods might be able to turn hundreds of millions of data points into a simplified abstraction that summarizes archetypical tissue immune blueprints across anatomical locations. These abstractions will facilitate comparisons between external perturbations and address questions about system resilience, adaptability or conservation across species.

Beyond bird watching, these new spatial technologies enable causative mechanistic insights into immune function. Several approaches have been devised to co-profile intrinsic CRISPR-based perturbations in vivo with targeted or whole-transcriptomics profiling in situ. Most of these are based on recovering the nature of the perturbation via image-based readout of the single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequence or other genetic barcodes within perturbed cells, such as via RCA (rolling circle amplification),2, 58 RNA polymerase-dependent amplification followed by fluorescent in situ hybridization,59,60 direct capture or polyadenylated capture followed by DBiT (deterministic barcoding in tissue)61 or RCA-MERFISH.62 Other approaches employ combinatorial expression of protein barcodes (pro-codes) to map tumor clones using protein stains overlaid with next-generation sequencing–based ST to measure the effect of dozens of genetic perturbations on the immune tumor environment.63 Ultimately, these exciting new approaches enable perturbation of key nodes within parenchymal or immune cells to understand spatial instructions. For instance, 2 sources of CXCR3 ligands, CXCL9 and CXCL10, exist in the small intestine to steer TRM cell maturation.2,64 The first one is a constitutive source in the upper half of the lamina propria of the villus, and the second one is induced upon viral infection and arises from stromal cells within the muscularis.2 To test the functional relevance of these signals, we genetically targeted antigen-specific CD8 T cells as they initially populated the small intestine in response to an acute viral infection, with sgRNAs targeting Cxcr3 or Cd19 and Thy1 (2 negative controls).2 Synthetic barcodes added to the UTR of the fluorescent reporters of the sgRNA-containing constructs enabled co-profiling of perturbation identities and cellular transcriptomes using Xenium (10x Genomics).65 Cxcr3-targeted cells showed a significant shift in their position toward the upper intraepithelial niche within the villus, and acquired a more differentiated effector-like TRM phenotype.2 These data suggested that Cxcl9/10 signals from the muscularis attract pre-TRM and condition their ability to reach upper regions of the villus diverting them from a more abundant source of key homeostatic cytokines, such as IL-15.2 Devising new approaches to neutralize cellular sources of specific signals, and developing new strategies of genetic barcoding to increase plex, sensitivity, and ease of use, is an area that will likely see tremendous progress over the next few years. In sum, causal data is crucial to move beyond atlasing and pattern recognition to establish working mechanisms.

Major knowledge gaps and the technologies to bridge them

A large fraction of advanced solid tumors remains unresponsive to immunotherapies, long-term protective immunity after vaccination remains a challenge for certain pathogens, and sex-based differences in the immune system remain mysterious. Advances in technologies to probe tissues might narrow these gaps by providing a better understanding of the spatial instructions of tissue immunity.

Metabolism is a major force controlling immune cell function.66–68 How do tissues ensure that all immune cells are properly nurtured? Tissues exhibit markedly distinct metabolic activities,69 and tissue-specific metabolic adaptations enable TRM formation.12,70,71 Thus, it seems that rather than ample access to nutrients, immune cells within tissues might be heavily limited by access to resources. A better understanding of the limiting factors for each cell type and context might help us define nutritional interventions to combat disease. For example, excess dietary lipids or acute sustained fasting impact enteric populations of plasma cells72–74 and TRM cells,12 and extended intermittent fasting has a cell type–selective effect on the survival of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyes,75 ketogenic versus vegan diets differentially impact peripheral immune cells,76 and certain tissues like the bone marrow offer an energetic sanctuary where memory B and T cells retreat in the advent of persistent dietary restriction.74, 77 These data support the idea that tissue immunity dynamically responds to organismal nutrition and could be controlled via dietary interventions,78 especially in organs with a two-front supply system like the intestine.79 However, probing immune cell metabolism in situ has so far relied on inference via genetics12, 80–83 or transcriptomics,84 while direct evidence is limited to dissociated cells taken from tissues, thus missing the spatial context. Emerging spatial metabolomics approaches using highly resolved spatial mass spectrometry85 or optical-based stimulated Raman scattering microscopy86 offer new avenues to capture metabolites within single cells in tissues. The all-optics nondestructive nature of stimulated Raman scattering offers a unique advantage to be multiplexed with existing ST platforms.87 These new technologies could be applied to better understand the nutritional deficiencies observed in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes that curtail responses to immunotherapy.88 For instance, prostate cancer remains unresponsive to current immune checkpoint blockade.89,90 The local high concentration of steroid hormones, zinc and citrate91–93 might participate in restraining local protective immunity,94 paradoxically leading to persistent infections, chronic inflammation, age-induced atrophy, and cancer. The function, location, frequency, and metabolic activities of immune cells in the healthy and diseased prostate remain largely unexplored.

Beyond the transcriptome, metabolome and proteome, additional layers of meaningful spatial biological information, such as the virome, microbiome, glycome, genome, epigenome, previous cellular interactions, and biophysical properties of cells and the extracellular matrix, among others, hold the potential to further transform our understanding of tissue immunity (Fig. 4). These additional layers of information not only will provide a more detailed tissue immune blueprint, but also may help define the forces that mold them. For instance, a spatial overlay of oxygen tension with transcriptomics and metabolomics might reveal fundamental principles of immune adaptation to extravascular tissue dwelling, which has lower access to oxygenated arterial blood. This comprehensive approach will need to be accompanied by interdisciplinary work and the clever development of profiling and analysis tools.

The breadth, selectivity, and specificity of antigen recognition by antigen receptors expressed by adaptive immune cells are foundational to mediating host protection. Each T cell clone recognizes a dominant epitope via unique T cell receptor (TCR) pairs, such as TCRα and TCRβ in conventional T cells. How does the immune system ensure the distribution of T cell specificities matches their odds of antigen encounter? At the organismal scale, naive T cells are most abundant in lymphoid organs, where they are presented with cognate peptides to mount an effector response, whereas memory CD8 T cells are most abundant in nonlymphoid tissues as TRM cells.95–99 Additional approaches that retain spatial context have provided more granular measurements of TCR and BCR clonotype distributions within tissues, revealing their clonal dynamics in homeostasis and within tumors.27, 100–102 Newer approaches leveraging parabiosis studies in mice,103,104 and retrospective C14 tracing in humans105 can model the probabilistic migration events that account for tissue immune composition, dwelling times, and immune cell longevity in tissues. Further, studies in mice and humans have shown asymmetries between circulatory and tissue-resident clonotype distributions.98 These tissue- and cell type–specific patterns suggest that these are not stochastic processes. Thus, what signals control lymphocyte distributions? One could argue that antigens likely shape the spatial dynamics of clonotypes. A major roadblock to test this hypothesis is the lack of tools to empirically pair endogenous TCR sequences to their cognate epitopes in space and at scale. While profiling TCR sequences within tissue is doable,27,98, 100–102 none of these approaches provides single-cell resolution. The use of tetramers to define reactive clonotypes, ex vivo stimulation assays with peptide arrays106–108 or viral delivery of antigen libraries (T-Scan)109 enables the systematic identification of T cell epitopes but does not readily reveal the specific TCR pairing. Because TCRs recognize not the linear peptide but a 3-dimensional conformation of a peptide-major histocompatibility complex (MHC), the choice of MHC molecule for antigen presentation (known as MHC restriction) plays a key role in TCR recognition and adds a major hurdle to establishing these TCR-epitope pairings. Computational modeling approaches might bridge this important gap.110,111 Achieving a TCR-epitope pairing would unlock crucial insights of immediate clinical use. For example, enabling the identification of tumor-reactive T cell clones and immunogenic tumor antigens, readily distinguishing antigens driving autoimmune responses, or selecting conserved T cell epitopes to design vaccines with broad protection.107

Future directions: On the horizon

The ideal spatial technology would unbiasedly capture multiple layers of biological information at single-molecule resolution, in situ, and within tissues that retain their native architecture (Fig. 4). This platform would enable accurate cell segmentation, high-throughput and rapid processing, and remain affordable and accessible. Four key metrics, resolution, plexity, throughput, and access, are essential for benchmarking progress and identifying areas for improvement. Among these, access stands out as a force multiplier: lowering entry barriers and fostering open-access platforms, such as OpenST,112 will likely accelerate advances across the board by fostering community-driven innovation. While the commercialization of end-to-end solutions has fueled the rapid expansion of spatial omics, no single technology can do it all. Extensive methodological development is still required to address important challenges, including RNA degradation and diffusion, robust cell segmentation, multiomic integration, and imaging of 3-dimensional tissues,113 and overall reductions in processing time and cost. In the near term, overcoming these limitations will depend on the strategic integration and clever combination of complementary technologies.

Live ST poses not only a massive technical challenge, but also an extraordinary opportunity to uncover fundamental biological principles. Based on the rapid progress in nanomanipulation tools for single-cell biopsies114 and single-molecule live-cell RNA imaging,115 we might see the emergence of real-time, highly multiplexed in vivo ST in the near future. Additional approaches to measure the history of cellular interactions in vivo, such as those employing viral barcodes,116,117 surface enzymatic labeling,118,119 or traceable secreted proteins,120 will be key to providing a chronological record. These could synergize with deep learning methods to infer topological velocities121 and in vivo fate-mapping approaches.122 A combination of these could be used to bring a temporal dimension to current ST platforms.

Beyond local tissue immunity, how immune events in one organ influence immune networks across the body remains an important and largely unresolved question. These include the niche-priming effects of metastatic spread, long-range neuroimmune interactions, or the systemic impact of hormones and other signals in organ-to-organ communication, among others. For example, the loss of tryptophan absorption in the small intestine after viral infection reduces circulating serotonin, which leads to loss of vagal nerve stimulation and diminished cognition in patients with postacute sequalae.123 These distal immunological connections are increasingly recognized as key modulators of both homeostasis and disease progression. However, spatial omics technologies applied at the scale of the whole organism remain rare,124 marking a compelling frontier for discovery. Emerging efforts aiming to bridge this gap include ImmGenMaps, a consortium-led initiative aiming to generate a ST atlas of the mouse immune system in healthy and disease contexts,125 the Spatial Atlas of Human Anatomy,126 or the Spatiotemporal Omics Consortium.

Developing alternative ways to think about these problems will be crucial to exploring these new areas of tissue immunology. Social network analyses have successfully predicted population behaviors and community resilience by mapping the topologies of human interactions.127 These social networks are created by mapping human connections within a community, and insights are derived by contrasting different communities or by introducing perturbations, much like comparing immune cells from 2 different organs after an infection challenge. In the case of human communities, the aim is often to understand factors that contribute to stable, thriving human groups.128 Importantly, these analyses have predictive power. For example, given the network topology of a human community, it is possible to predict who will rise through the social ranks.129 If we consider cells as elements of the community, could we derive similar predictions applied to tissue health? What if we could predict disease before symptom onset? Can we identify tissue states that are conducive to malignant transformation? Certainly, these tools would bolster personalized therapies and enhance our diagnostic capacity.

Additionally, the construction of in silico models of cells and cellular communities, much like digital twins of immune circuits, would add a layer of fast, reproducible, and perhaps more affordable experimentation. The standardization of data outputs in transparent, publicly available, and organized databases is crucial to facilitate these ambitious goals. With the rise of complex and highly advanced technologies comes an urgent need to develop gold standards and resources that ensure rigorous experimental designs, reproducible results, and democratize access to the technology, data, and analysis methods. Community alliances, such as GESTALT,130 and emerging academic associations like the European Society for Spatial Biology, among others, are paving the way in this emerging field.

The rapid expansion of spatial biology is opening transformative opportunities for immunology, a field poised to highly benefit from technologies that resolve cellular context, organization, and communication in tissues. Ultimately, a more integrated view of the spatial instructions that shape tissue immunity will offer critical insights to achieve a life without disease.
