Risk factors of respiratory distress among newborns admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit at public hospitals of addis ababa, Ethiopia. unmatched case-control study
Authors: Tenaw Belay Shitie, Daniel Mengistu Bekele, Feven Mulugeta Ashagrie, Tirusew Nigussie Kebede
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory distress (RD) is a common cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality globally, particularly in resource-limited settings. Although standard management exists in the neonatal intensive care unit, the determinants are not well understood. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the risk factors for respiratory distress among newborns admitted to NICUs in selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, in 2024.MethodsAn institution-based unmatched retrospective case-control study was conducted at selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa from March 1 to April 1, 2024. Systematic random sampling was used to select the cases and controls. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured, pre-tested questionnaire for mothers (Informed consent was obtained from each mother through written and oral explanations), and a data extraction checklist for mothers and neonatal medical records. Data were collected using the Kobo Toolbox (Harvard Humanitarian Initiative, Cambridge, MA, USA), and the collected data were exported to the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 26 for analysis. Binary logistic regression was used for both bi-variable and multivariable analyses, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsIn this study, 489 neonates and their mothers were included, yielding a 100% response rate. Emergency cesarean section (AOR = 3.074, 95% CI: 1.016–9.302), meconium-stained amniotic fluid (AOR = 5.103, 95% CI: 1.739–14.974), low birth weight (< 2500 g) (AOR = 3.592, 95% CI: 1.293–9.978), and low first-minute Apgar score < 7 (AOR = 9.693, 95% CI: 3.945–23.817) were identified as predictors of respiratory distress.ConclusionEmergency cesarean section, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, low birth weight, and low first-minute Apgar score were identified as determinants of respiratory distress. Monitoring during labor and delivery and prompt and effective neonatal resuscitation may reduce respiratory distress.Keywords: Respiratory distress, Newborns, Risk factors, Meconium-stained amniotic fluid, Emergency cesarean section, Ethiopia

Introduction

Neonatal respiratory distress is a breathing problem that affects both preterm and full-term newborns [1, 2]. 15% of term infants and 29% of late preterm infants in the newborn critical care unit suffer from respiratory morbidity; the prevalence is much higher for infants delivered before 34 weeks of pregnancy [3].

Worldwide, respiratory distress (RD) in neonates is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality and a common condition requiring admission to the NICU [4]. Most neonatal deaths worldwide occur in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Neonatal respiratory distress is a frequent neonatal emergency worldwide, and the highest neonatal death rates are seen in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, with reported incidence rates in India being 19.2% [5], in Rwanda 60% [6], in Cameroon 47.5% [7], and in Nigeria 58.4% [8]. In Ethiopia, different regional research varies between 11.5% and 42.9% [4, 9]. Globally, neonatal mortality has remained relatively unchanged, particularly in developing countries [10]. According to the World Health Organization, approximately one million infants die in the first 24 h of Life, of which 75% of neonatal deaths occur in the first week of life, and these deaths are due to respiratory distress [11, 12]. Most causes of neonatal respiratory distress are preventable; regardless of the reason, if not recognized and managed quickly, it can progress to cardiac arrest, respiratory failure, and death. Ethiopia aimed to reduce the Neonatal Mortality Rate (NMR) from 29 per 1000 Live births in 2016 to 11 per 1000 Live births in 2019/2020. Despite these efforts, paradoxically, it had increased to 33per1000 Live births in 2019 [13]. Among these, the mortality of neonates in respiratory distress is a major public health problem [14, 15]. Additionally, different policies, strategies, and programs work on or advocate for the prevention and care of preterm neonates and their birth outcomes, including respiratory distress, as the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG 3) focuses on reducing neonatal mortality to below 12 per 1000 Live births by 2030 [17]. Besides, there is also a strategy called basic emergency obstetric and newborn care (BEmONC) [18]. Furthermore, Ethiopia planned to reduce neonatal mortality from 33 to 21 per 1000 Live births by 2024/2025 [19].

However, neonatal RD remains the leading cause of NICU admission, morbidity, and mortality among newborns in this country despite the efforts [20]. To address this, early identification of contributing factors and starting treatments on time can dramatically lower newborn respiratory distress and related impacts. Although these efforts are made to improve neonatal health, including the establishment of NICUs in public hospitals, respiratory distress remains a significant challenge. Even though studies have identified risk factors for respiratory distress in newborns, the incidence and mortality of newborns with RD are considerably rising [14]. Furthermore, the risk variables are not verified, particularly in this study area. In addition, this study adds more predictors, such as indication for cesarean section and birth intervals, which were not analyzed in previous studies. Therefore, this study aimed to identify the Risk factors of respiratory distress among newborns admitted to the NICU in Addis Ababa public hospitals, Ethiopia,2024.

Methods

Study design, period, and setting

An institution-based unmatched retrospective case-control study was conducted in the NICUs of selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, from March 1 to April 1, 2024. Systematic random sampling was used to select the cases and controls. Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia, serves as the seat of the African Union and the United Nations Economic Commission for Africa. Covering an area of 527 square kilometers, the city is divided into 11 sub-cities and has an estimated population of 5.46 million in 2023 [21]. The city has 13 public hospitals, including Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (the only university hospital), six federal hospitals, and six hospitals managed by the Addis Ababa City Administration Health Bureau. Among these, 11 hospitals have NICUs. For this study, four public hospitals were selected through a lottery method. This method involved assigning numbers to all eligible hospitals and using a random number generator to ensure a fair and unbiased selection process. This multi-center study design was implemented to enhance the generalizability of the findings.

Population

All neonates admitted to the NICU at public hospitals in Addis Ababa were the source of the population.

Study population

For cases

All Neonates admitted to the NICU with documented respiratory distress signs and symptoms at selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa during the study period. For controls: All Neonates admitted to the NICUs, other than signs and symptoms of respiratory distress, at selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa during the study period.

Eligibility criteria

For cases

Neonates admitted to the NICU with documented signs and symptoms of respiratory distress in the neonate’s chart.

For controls

Neonates admitted to the NICU at selected public hospitals in Addis Ababa, without signs and symptoms of respiratory distress.

The study excluded participants with incomplete documentation, newborns with congenital malformations and signs of respiratory distress, and in the case of twin or triplet newborns, one was selected by lottery method for both cases and controls.

Sample size determination, sampling procedure, and selection of eligible population

The sample size was determined using EpiInfo-7 Stat Calc (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), Atlanta, GA, USA), with the double population proportion exposure difference formula. The assumption was that the percentage of cases with exposure was 57.3% (main exposure variable: meconium-stained amniotic fluid (MSAF), from an unmatched case-control study [9], with 95% CI, 80% power of the study, and a 1:2 ratio of cases to controls. A 10% non-response rate was added, resulting in a final sample size of 489 (163 cases and 326 controls). Four out of 11 Public hospitals were selected using simple random sampling, and the sample size was proportionally allocated to each hospital based on the previous month’s admission reports. Two controls were selected for each case from the same hospital to ensure case and control homogeneity.

Operational definitions

Respiratory distress (RD)

A neonate, if they exhibit one or more signs of with or without cyanosis, retraction (intercostal, suprasternal, subcostal), fast breathing or objective measurement RR > 60 breath/min, apnea, grunting, nasal flaring [3, 4].

Case

Neonates admitted to the NICU with documented respiratory distress signs and symptoms in the neonate’s chart by physicians.

Control

Neonates admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit, other than signs and symptoms of respiratory distress.

Study variables

Dependent variable

Respiratory distress (RD).

Independent variables

Socio-demographic characteristics- Maternal age, educational status of the mother, marital status, and occupational status of the mother.

Obstetrics-related factors- ANC follow-up, Gravidity, parity, prolonged labor, obstructed labor, mode of delivery, oligohydramnios, meconium-stained amniotic fluid (MSAF), indication for CS (maternal and fetal), multiple pregnancy, Prolonged rupture of membrane (PROM), chorioamnionitis, preeclampsia, antepartum hemorrhage, birth interval.

Maternal health condition-related factors- Chronic hypertension, Diabetes mellitus, and anemia.

Neonatal-related factors- Sex of the newborn, Birth weight, Gestational age, fetal distress.

Data collection tool and procedure

Data collection tools were adapted from relevant literature to address the study objectives [3, 4, 7, 9, 22]. Both primary and secondary data (chart review) were used. Data was collected through face-to-face interviews with mothers and data from neonatal medical records. Respiratory distress was confirmed by reviewing the neonate’s medical chart. The data collection tool includes socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric-related factors, maternal health conditions, and neonatal-related factors.

Data quality control

The questionnaire was prepared in English, translated to Amharic, and back-translated to English for consistency. The tool was pre-tested, and modifications were made based on feedback. Validity and reliability were checked, tool was reviewed by two neonatologists and one senior neonatal nurse. And data collectors were trained. Close supervision was maintained during data collection to ensure quality.

Data processing and analysis

Data was checked for completeness and consistency, and it was exported to SPSS software version 26 for analysis from the online Kobo Toolbox. Cross-tabulation and.

Logistic regression was used for analysis. The model’s fitness was checked by using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p = 0.78), confirming its goodness of fit, and variables with p-values < 0.25 in the bi-variable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. Statistical significance was declared at p < 0.05, and associations were summarized using adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from Addis Ababa University, the College of Health Science, Institutional Review Board (IRB) with protocol Number SNM/19/2024. The supporting letter was obtained from the Addis Ababa Health Bureau, and permission was obtained from the hospital’s clinical director. The study was conducted per the Declaration of Helsinki. (Informed consent was obtained from each mother through both written and oral explanations), And waived for data extractions. Confidentiality of the information was secured throughout the study process by using code instead of any personal identifier & is meant only for the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 489 participants (163 cases and 326 controls) were recruited for this study, with a 100% response rate. The average age of mothers in the study was 26.94 years. The 25–29 age range was the most common, representing 45.0% of all participants. Marital status was consistent across both groups, with most mothers being married (95.1% of cases and 95.7% of controls). Regarding education, the proportion of women with no formal schooling was slightly higher among cases (39.3%) than controls (37.7%). (Table 1).

Maternal health Condition-related factors

Maternal chronic hypertension was present in 8 (4.9%) of cases and 12 (3.7%) of controls, and six (3.7%) of the cases and fifteen (4.6%) of the controls had gestational diabetes. Anemia during pregnancy was observed in 1.2% of cases and 0.9% of controls, respectively. Pregnancy-induced hypertension was detected in 2.5% of cases and 4.3% of controls.

Risk factors of respiratory distress

Using bivariate logistic regression analysis, each independent variable was assessed for its association with respiratory distress. Variables identified as predictors of respiratory distress in the bivariable analysis (p-value < 0.25) included maternal age, maternal residence, birth interval, maternal chorioamnionitis, duration of labor, type of cesarean section, failed induction of labor, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, birth weight, fetal distress, and neonates with low APGAR scores in the first and fifth minutes. These variables were collectively entered into a multivariable logistic regression model. After adjusting for potential confounding variables, multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that emergency cesarean section, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, low birth weight, and low first-minute APGAR score were significantly associated with respiratory distress in newborns (P ≤ 0.05). The multivariable analysis indicated that mothers whose newborns were delivered via emergency cesarean section were three times more likely to experience respiratory distress compared to those delivered through elective cesarean section (AOR = 3.074, 95% CI: 1.016, 9.302). The study results showed that neonates delivered with meconium-stained amniotic fluid were more than five times (AOR = 5.103, 95% CI: 1.739, 14.974) more likely to develop respiratory distress than those without meconium staining. Additionally, newborns with low birth weight were more than three times more likely to experience respiratory distress than those with normal birth weight (AOR = 3.592, 95% CI: 1.293, 9.978). Furthermore, newborns with Apgar scores below 7 in the first minute were nearly ten times more likely to have respiratory distress than their counterparts (AOR = 9.693, 95% CI: 3.945, 23.817). Discussion

The current study aimed to identify factors associated with respiratory distress among newborns admitted to NICUs in Addis Ababa public hospitals. The findings suggest that emergency cesarean section, meconium-stained amniotic fluid (MSAF), low birth weight, and a low first-minute Apgar score were significant predictors.

According to the study’s findings, newborns delivered in the presence of meconium-stained amniotic fluid (MSAF) were found to have five-fold increased odds of developing respiratory distress compared to those with clear amniotic fluid. This finding is consistent with previous studies from Thailand [23], India [24], and Southern Ethiopia [9], which showed a significant association between respiratory distress (RD) and meconium-stained amniotic fluid (MSAF). This is biologically plausible because meconium can be aspirated into the lower airways, leading to mechanical obstruction, chemical pneumonitis, and surfactant inactivation. These changes impair alveolar ventilation and gas exchange, increasing the risk of hypoxia and respiratory distress [25]. This suggests that neonates exposed to MSAF are at a higher risk of developing respiratory complications, emphasizing the need for close monitoring and intervention in such cases.

The odds of respiratory distress (RD) were increased 3 times in newborns born to mothers with emergency CS than in newborns born to mothers by elective CS. This finding is supported by a study conducted in China [26]. Emergency CS often results from intrapartum complications like fetal distress, obstructed labor, or prolonged labor. These conditions may reduce placental perfusion and oxygen delivery, predisposing neonates to respiratory distress [27]. Additionally, emergency procedures are associated with inadequate perinatal preparation and a lack of physiologic fluid clearance from the lungs, particularly when labor is not well-established [28].

Low birth weight (< 2500 g) was another neonatal characteristic that was found to be significantly associated with respiratory distress. Compared to newborns with normal birth weight, newborns who had low birth weights were 3.6 times more likely to develop respiratory distress. This finding is consistent with the studies conducted in Korea [29], Afghanistan [30], and China [22]. A likely explanation is that a significant number of low birth-weight newborns are born prematurely. Their lungs are not yet fully developed; they cannot produce enough surfactant, a substance that prevents the air sacs in the lungs from collapsing [31, 32]. Compared to newborns with an Apgar score of equal to or more than 7, those who had a score of less than 7 in the first minute were 9.7 times more likely to experience respiratory distress. This finding was compatible with studies conducted in Cameroon, Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH), and South Ethiopia [4, 7, 33]. This low score often reflects poor respiratory effort, muscle tone, and oxygenation, suggesting underlying perinatal asphyxia or birth-related complications. Such conditions can impair surfactant production and lung compliance, hinder the clearance of fetal lung fluid, and lead to hypoxemia and acidosis, all of which contribute to respiratory distress. Another possible explanation could be that small babies might suffer difficulty breathing and might develop difficulty in cardiopulmonary transition and respiratory distress, which predisposes the newborn to different complications, including a low fifth-minute Apgar score [31]. This emphasizes the need for immediate and effective resuscitation efforts for these infants to improve their outcomes.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Since this study was conducted in a multi-center setting, which enhances the generalizability of its findings across the public hospital system in Addis Ababa. However, limitations must be considered. The retrospective case-control design is inherently susceptible to information bias from incomplete medical records and recall bias from maternal interviews. Also, include the selection of controls from the NICU, who may not be representative of the general newborn population.

Conclusion

This study identified significant associations between respiratory distress in newborns and the factors of emergency cesarean section, meconium-stained amniotic fluid, low birth weight, and a low first-minute Apgar score in the context of NICUs in Addis Ababa. These findings highlight critical areas for clinical attention. Enhancing the quality of intrapartum care to reduce the need for emergency CS, vigilant monitoring for signs of fetal distress like MSAF, and ensuring the capacity for immediate and effective neonatal resuscitation for at-risk infants are crucial that help reduce the incidence of respiratory distress.
