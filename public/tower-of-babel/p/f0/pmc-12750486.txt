Can Real‐World Data From Hospital Information Systems Be Directly Utilized for Efficacy Studies: An Analysis of Data Time Points
Authors: Jiaying Luo, Aomeng Zhang, Wulin Gao, Xiangwei Bu, Runming Li, Zehui Ye, Xinyi Zhang, Lili Ren, Yin Jiang, Zhiyue Guan, Hui Guan, Liyuan Tao, Guohua Dai, Chen Zhao, Hongcai Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundData from Hospital Information Systems are crucial components of real‐world data, but concerns arise regarding the transparency of measurement time‐points when directly utilizing for efficacy studies. The objective of the study was to analyze the distribution characteristics of time‐points for laboratory test records from HIS.MethodMedical records before December 31, 2019 from Affiliated Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, for patients primarily diagnosed with coronary heart disease (CHD), or heart failure combined with secondary diagnosis of CHD were retrieved from HIS. Fifteen test groups were extracted. The number of records, average of test times, and distribution characteristics of time points were analyzed.ResultsThe renal function tests have the most records, and the blood glucose have the most times. Tests time‐points distribution showed concentration in the early stage of hospitalization, with the majority occurring within the first 0–10%. For those measured ≥2 times, their first tests are also centrally distributed in the early stage, while the lasts in all period of hospitalization. Besides, substantial difference is showed in the time span of the first and last test. Abnormal value may be a trigger that promotes more intensive examination, and normal or abnormal status of the first examination is significantly weak to moderate correlated with the number of examinations and time span.DiscussionThe disclosure of time‐points of clinical studies based on HIS should be encouraged and the pre‐survey of data measuring time point is essential to the design of trial.

Introduction

Real‐world study (RWS) is the process of conducting relevant research based on real‐world data (RWD) to generate real‐world evidence (RWE), which can serve as the complement or proof for randomized controlled trial (RCT) [1, 2, 3], to some extent can help save the timing and economic cost [4]. In cases involving rare diseases, critical conditions or other ethical constraints that limit the feasibility of RCTs, RWS alternatively gathers clinical data, offering evidence‐based support for medical decision‐making [5, 6, 7].

Data from Hospital Information Systems (HIS), as the essential component of RWD, is rich in volume and diverse in types. With laboratory data generated by medical instruments providing objective and structured information, it is getting gradually valued in relevant medical decision‐making [8, 9]. Therefore, many studies would like to choose the kind of data to analyze [10, 11, 12, 13]. The comparison of a patient's current state of examination with the baseline status prior to the intervention can reflect a disease or medical condition over a period of time [14]. A strong correlation had been shown between laboratory biomarkers assessed at early or late time points and multiple clinically important outcomes [15], suggesting that the location of the examination time point throughout the intervention may reflect key information about diseases or treatments [16].

However, unlike RCTs [17], the timing of the tests is determined by the clinician based on the patient's status at the time of treatment, which is uncertain and difficult to pre‐design. Even with a pre‐design, the opacity of the measurement time points of the indicators can affect the quality of clinical trial outcome reporting, especially when the schedule of the protocol measurement time point is inconsistent with the clinician's execution habits, resulting in a mismatch between the real measurement time point of the laboratory and the designed intervention time window. Currently, the transparency of data time points in real‐world clinical practice has been raising concerns. It is indicated in some systematic reviews that there are issues with incomplete reporting of time points in clinical trial data, which hinders the translation of trial results into real‐world evidence [18, 19]. Prior to conducting research, gaining an understanding of the distribution of laboratory test time points in real‐world data may facilitate more targeted optimization of trial design [20].

Therefore, through a cross‐sectional survey of real‐world clinical data, we focus on the specific phenomenon of laboratory test timing distribution. By objectively presenting the temporal characteristics of different tests, we aim to gain a transparent understanding of the actual status of test data over time. This study seeks to encourage subsequent research to prioritize transparency in testing timing when utilizing real‐world medical data for analysis, thereby providing foundational references for more rigorous real‐world data studies. In this analysis, we assume that treatment efficacy throughout the entire hospitalization period needs to be evaluated.

Methods

Data Source

The study data were obtained from medical records before December 31, 2019, at Affiliated Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, for patients primarily diagnosed at discharge with coronary heart disease (CHD), or heart failure (HF) combined with a secondary diagnosis of CHD. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, with the ethics number of (2024)No.(62)‐KY.

Data Processing

Data Retrieval

Using the search query “(*angina*)or(*myocardial infarction*)or(*coronary artery*)or(*myocardial ischemia*)or (*ischemic heart disease*)or(*X syndrome*)or(*ischemic heart disease*)[Record Home—Discharge Main Diagnosis. Diagnosis type]and[Home of the case—diagnosis. Date of diagnosis of disease]<20191231“, ”(*heart failure*)or(*cardiac insufficiency*)[medical record Home‐discharge main diagnosis. Diagnosis type]and[Home of the case—diagnosis. Date of disease diagnosis] <20191231,” initial targeted medical record data were retrieved from the information system, including general hospitalization information, inpatient progress notes, and laboratory records.

Data Selection

Hospitalization records with a primary diagnosis of CHD were included. For the records with the main diagnosis of HF, the discharge diagnosis was further screened, and the hospitalization records containing the diagnosis related to CHD were finally included (note: the records of the same patient in different hospitalizations were analyzed separately in this paper).

Hospitalization records are excluded if the hospitalization information including ID number, gender, age, admission time, discharge time, and progress notes (verified by reviewing all medical records), or the test information including test sub‐item names and test timestamps are missing or incorrect (for example, the test was earlier than the time of admission or later than the time of discharge), or the length of stay is ≤ 2 days or the length of stay is ≥ 100 days. Data were screened by two individuals (Jiaying Luo and Runming Li), with disagreements resolved by a third arbitrator (Xinyi Zhang). After attempting data supplementation and verification, the final data were obtained.

Data Extraction and Analysis

Examination Grouping

All tests sub‐item data were grouped according to the hospital laboratory classifications, merging the clinically associated into corresponding categories. Some tests recommended under the same classification by the laboratory but individually conducted in hospital were considered for separate grouping. Finally, based on clinical experience, 15 types of laboratory test groups were selected for analysis, including complete blood count (CBC), urine routine (UA), fecal routine test (FRT), lipid profile (LP), blood glucose (BG), electrolytes (E‐lytes), homocysteine (Hcy), renal function tests (RFT), liver function tests (LFT), cardiac enzyme panel (CEP), cardiac troponin (cTn), N‐terminal Pro B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP), coagulation function tests (CFT), D‐dimer, and high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein (hs‐CRP) (Table S1 for more grouping details).

Data Analysis

The number of all records and the number with 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, or more times tests under the same record are separately calculated. The total and average number of test times is also counted. To describe the distribution of laboratory tests time points during hospitalization, the length of stay for each record was equally divided into ten parts, and the number of records over different parts were calculated. For tests that were examined more than twice in one record, the distribution of their first and last tests were also analyzed separately. In addition, the interval between the first or last tests and the time of admission, and the interval between the first and last examinations were analyzed to understand the coverage of test of any type in the entire stage of hospitalization.

To further explore the distribution characteristics of in‐hospital examinations, we selected core indicators for coronary heart disease (CHD)—specifically troponin I (cTnI) and creatine kinase‐MB (CK‐MB)—as examples. We calculated the number of subsequent examinations, examination coverage rate, and distribution of time points for abnormal examinations after the first abnormal values were detected for these indicators. As supplementary reference, 200 patients were randomly sampled from the population with abnormal examination values, and the changes in their test values over the course of hospitalization were described in the form of strip diagram. This entire analysis aimed to investigate whether the occurrence of abnormal examination values might influence in‐hospital examination decision‐making. Subsequently, we expanded the scope to include several other clinically routine indicators for CHD patients, such as D‐dimer, N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide (NT‐Pro BNP), and homocysteine. Together with troponin I and creatine kinase‐MB, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficients between the “normal/abnormal status of the first admission examination” for these indicators and their “subsequent number of examinations” as well as “examination time span.” This step was intended to gain a deeper understanding of the nature of such influences. Additionally, using core indicators for CHD as examples, we attempted to construct weighting models based on different examination time intervals across multiple post‐admission examinations. This effort sought to explore how to quantify the impact of such temporal heterogeneity in future studies. Given that the focus of this study is a cross‐sectional investigation of the temporal distribution of in‐hospital examinations, the results of this analysis are presented in Table S2 for reference only.

Statistical Methods

The number of records are described in terms of frequency and percentage. The number of test times are described as frequency and average. The distribution of tests over the 10 periods of hospitalization are described as frequency and represented by line charts. Spearman correlation analysis was employed to assess the relationships among the “normal/abnormal status of the first admission test” for core indicators, the subsequent number of examinations and the examination time span. A result was deemed statistically significant when p < 0.01. In a strip diagram of the test values over time, the percentage values on the x‐axis are calculated by dividing the interval between the test time point and admission time point by the total length of hospitalization. The gray shaded area represents the 25th–75th percentile (P25–P75) range of the test values, and the solid line denotes the median. In addition, violin plots and box plots are drawn to represent the distribution characteristics of the first and last test time points and their spans. The violin diagram combines the characteristics of the kernel density map, the width of the violin reflects the density of the data at that location, and contains its corresponding box plot in the center of the violin, which is used to help understand the concentrated and discrete trends of the data (in order for better showing, In the graph, we swap the x‐axis (test types) with the y‐axis position (time points), and at the end of the graph, we supplement the mean and standard deviation values of the corresponding tests. The above data management and analysis is carried out through Microsoft Excel 2016, Python 3.12.0, SPSS27.1 and R 4.4.1 software packages.

Distribution of Time Points

As shown in Figures 2, 3, 4, each kind of time points is concentrated in the early stages of hospitalization. In Figure 5, it can be observed that the first tests are primarily concentrated in the early period, while the last are distributed throughout the entire hospitalization, and it is found that the last distribution of cTn, CEP, RFT, E‐lytes is also relatively concentrated in the early stage after admission. As for the time intervals between the first and last tests in Figure 6, data points closer to the position of 1 suggest a higher temporal coverage during the entire hospitalization. However, Figure 6 shows that the data are not predominantly concentrated near 1, indicating significant variability in coverage. In fact, for the UA, FRT, CEP, and cTn examinations, half or more of the data exhibit coverage of less than 50% of their hospitalization.

Discussion

This study found that examination records during hospitalization are predominantly concentrated in the early stages after admission. Given the abundance of data during this phase and the fact that patients have not yet undergone inpatient treatment, baseline characteristics can be effectively assessed. Therefore, it is recommended to consider conducting relevant cross‐sectional studies [21, 22]. Moreover, it is observed that the last examinations are distributed throughout the entire hospitalization period, rather than ideally concentrated near the discharge time point and that even some examinations are partially concentrated in the early stages after admission. This distribution may be related to the clinical practice of repeating examinations for the sake of accuracy. Consequently, the data may lack adequate follow‐up on outcomes during the hospitalization period.

It is also founded that the distribution of the time span between the first and last tests is of significant difference. Further enhancement should be required to the timing coverage of those repeated measured data in order to honestly reflect the interventions’ effectiveness. The pre‐survey is suggested to conduct when utilizing these lab data. Only indicators that are continuously and repeatedly measured over an appropriate duration can be potentially reported as primary outcomes in clinical trials [23], and the closer the timing of the obtained measurement data is to the start and end of the intervention trial, the more accurately the effects of the intervention can be interpreted. Investigating real‐world testing data in advance, especially for the timing of tests with abnormal value, can not only further quantify the result bias caused by this kind of time heterogeneity, but also help to understand the timing of clinician prescribing tests or patient visits, develop more acceptable study protocols or follow‐up plans and optimize trial design [24], thereby improving the feasibility of clinical trials and study compliance of subjects [25].

Furthermore, it is proposed to make the raw data transparent by disclosing its authentic time points of conducting examinations in researches. Disclosure of the measurement points of outcome indicators is also one of the essential requirements of transparent clinical trial. Both the SPIRIT 2013 Statement and the CONSORT‐Outcomes 2022 Extension have mentioned recommendations for describing time of primary, secondary, and other outcomes in clinical trial protocols [26, 27], which are considered to help to supplement key indicator information and improve the current incomplete reporting of clinical trial outcomes so that other stakeholders can reproduce the results and reduce selectivity bias. Transparency to study design and procedures can also help regulators and other researchers evaluate the reliability and validity to better support complementary healthcare‐related decisions [28, 29].

This study is merely a preliminary temporal investigation based on partial examination data retrieved from Affiliated Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine. Due to the replacement of the hospital's information system, only test data before 2019 were selected for analysis. As a cross‐sectional study, its focus lies on temporal‐level phenomena of the data, aiming to reveal the temporal distribution characteristics of real‐world data. This is intended to prompt researchers to pay greater attention to the temporal transparency of data when using real‐world data for efficacy analysis. Currently, the adopted dataset lacks information such as treatment medications, making it difficult to construct multi‐variable models (e.g., treatment‐time‐efficacy models). Therefore, the methodology primarily relies on simple statistical analyses, serving only as a demonstration of preliminary investigations into real‐world data. Methodological research on addressing efficacy evaluation biases caused by factors like temporal heterogeneity awaits further exploration in subsequent studies. In addition to the measurement time points focused on in this study, other data information that should be transparent—such as data completeness, authenticity, and traceability—also deserves attention.
