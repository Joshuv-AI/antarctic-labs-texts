Increased Arrhythmic Risk in Obesity Is Transduced by Adipose Tissue–Derived Extracellular Vesicles
Authors: Worawan B Limpitikul, Marta Garcia-Contreras, Paul Spangler, Michael J Betti, Quanhu Sheng, Steffen Pabel, Samuel Sossalla, Emeli Chatterjee, Ling Xiao, Patrick T Ellinor, Eric R Gamazon, Ravi Shah, Saumya Das
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Visual AbstractKey Words: arrhythmias, atrial fibrillation, extracellular vesicles, long QT, obesity

Obesity impacts nearly 2.5 billion worldwide,1 exacting a series of high morbidity cardiometabolic complications. Cardiac arrhythmias are prime among the metabolic complications of obesity, with obesity driving development and progression of atrial fibrillation (AF)2, 3, 4, 5, 6 and sudden cardiac death.7 Each 5-kg/m2 increase in body mass index (BMI) is associated with a 16% increased risk of sudden cardiac death,7 and individuals with obesity exhibit increased ventricular arrhythmia susceptibility including prolonged QT interval.8,9 Moreover, obesity-related AF is more resistant to conventional intervention (catheter ablation,10,11 antiarrhythmic therapy12), and weight loss improves AF.13, 14, 15 Although the traditional view ascribes increased arrhythmia susceptibility to obesity-related hemodynamic or global inflammatory state,16, 17, 18 recent genetic studies suggest that a shared causal predisposition exists between obesity and AF.19 Recent studies at the local fat-heart interface suggest that trans-tissue metabolic signals may contribute to cardiovascular disease susceptibility,20 although what this signal entails remains unclear.

Although extracellular vesicles (EVs)—membrane-bound vesicles that can carry molecular signals (eg, RNAs, proteins) across tissues—have very recently been implicated in the pathogenesis of obesity-related complications,21 whether fat-derived EVs may serve as a trans-tissue endocrine signal in obesity-related cardiovascular disease remains unclear. Here, we hypothesize that direct communication between visceral adipose tissue (VAT) and myocardium causally contributes to obesity-related cardiac arrhythmogenesis. For the first time to our knowledge, we demonstrate that atrial myocytes from human atrial tissue exhibit a prolonged action potential duration (APD) in individuals with obesity, in contrast to widely used animal AF models with shortened APD.22 We subsequently show that EVs isolated from human VAT from individuals with obesity produced 3 key pathophysiological changes in cardiac cells: 1) significant APD prolongation in both atrial and ventricular induced pluripotent stem cell–derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CMs); 2) a profibrotic phenotype in cardiac fibroblasts (CFs), with enhanced myofibroblast differentiation and increased collagen production; and 3) a shift toward proinflammatory activation states in cardiac macrophages linked to arrhythmogenesis.23, 24, 25 Using a genetic model of EV tracking (Exomap1)26 to trace adipose-derived EVs, we demonstrate in vivo evidence of adipose-derived EV delivery to the myocardium, specifically in obese mice. Next, to identify potential causal mechanisms of visceral adipose tissue–derived extracellular vesicles (VAT EVs) on arrhythmogenesis, we performed transcriptomics in VAT EV–treated iPSC-CMs, demonstrating: 1) enrichment for pathways putatively involved in arrhythmogenesis; and 2) concordant causal mapping to large human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of AF and QT prolongation. We experimentally validated a prioritized target—transient receptor protein channel 3 (TRPC3), a nonselective cation channel—targeted by VAT EV signaling, with TRPC3 inhibition abrogating VAT EV effects on APD prolongation in iPSC-CMs. Collectively, these findings establish adipose-derived EV transmission as a novel avenue of interorgan crosstalk in obesity-related arrhythmias that implicates both previously known and novel genes and pathways in arrhythmia pathogenesis. This study expands the current mechanistic paradigm linking excess adiposity to cardiovascular risk in obesity.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male and female patients and composite findings are reported. Our study was not powered to detect sex-specific differences in the phenotypes.

Human myocardial tissue

All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the ethics committees of the University of Regensburg and the University of Göttingen (approval numbers 14/9/11 and 21/10/00). Written informed consent was obtained from every participant. Samples of human atrial myocardium were collected during atrial resections performed during open-heart surgery (see Supplemental Table 1 for patient details). Following removal, the tissue was immediately transferred into cardioplegic solution cooled to 4 °C for transport.

Human cardiomyocyte isolation

Atrial myocardium from patients was used for cellular experiments. Cardiomyocyte (CM) isolation was preformed based on an established method by Pabel et al.27 Before isolation, atrial tissue was carefully cleaned of adipose tissue and blood vessels, minced into fine fragments, and rinsed thoroughly. The samples were washed 3 times in a Ca2+-free buffer composed of: 100 mM NaCl, 20 mM glucose, 10 mM KCl, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM MgCl2, 50 mM taurine, 10 mM 2,3-butanedione monoxine, and 5 mM 3-(N-morpholino)propanesulfonic acid (pH 7.2). Tissue fragments were then incubated at 37 °C in a spinner flask with continuous oxygenation, containing Ca2+-free buffer supplemented with 0.77 mg/mL collagenase (Worthington type 1, 370 U/mg) and 0.4 mg/mL proteinase (Sigma type XXIV, 7–14 U/mg). After 45 minutes, the digestion medium was replaced with fresh Ca2+-free buffer containing collagenase only, and the suspension was gently triturated with a Pasteur pipette for 5 to 20 minutes. Enzymatic activity was stopped by adding 2% bovine calf serum. The cell-containing supernatant was collected and centrifuged (58 g, 5 minutes).

For further processing, the residual tissue was resuspended in storage solution containing: 20 mM taurine, 70 mM glutamic acid, 30 mM KCl, 10 mM KH2PO4, 1 mM MgCl2, 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 10 mM butanedione monoxine, 11 mM glucose, and 2% bovine calf serum (pH 7.4, adjusted with KOH, 37 °C). Additional CMs were released by gentle pipetting with a serological pipette. The suspension was centrifuged again, and cell pellets were resuspended in storage solution. Only preparations enriched in elongated CMs with visible cross-striations were used. These cells were plated onto laminin-coated recording chambers and allowed to settle for 30 minutes before experiments, which were performed at room temperature.

Action potential recordings

Action potentials were measured using the whole-cell patch-clamp method in current-clamp mode. Recording pipettes (3-5 MΩ) were filled with an internal solution containing: 92 mM K-aspartate, 48 mM KCl, 1 mM Mg-ATP, 10 mM HEPES, 0.02 mM ethylene glycol-bis(β-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid, 0.1 mM guanosine 5′triphosphate, tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane salt, and 4 mM adenosine 5′-triphosphate disodium salt (pH adjusted to 7.2 with KOH). The external bath solution consisted of: 140 mM NaCl, 4 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 2 mM CaCl2, 10 mM glucose, and 10 mM HEPES (pH adjusted to 7.4 with NaOH). To trigger action potentials, square current pulses (1-2 nA, 1-5 ms duration) were applied at a frequency of 1 Hz. After achieving whole-cell access, series resistance was typically 5 to 15 MΩ. Capacitance transients were compensated in the cell-attached state, and both membrane capacitance and series resistance were corrected following rupture. Signals were acquired using an EPC10 amplifier (HEKA Elektronik) and filtered with Bessel filters at cutoff frequencies of 2.9 and 10 kHz.

Differentiation and maintenance of iPSC-CMs and iPSC–CFs

Human induced pluripotent stem cell-derived atrial cardiomyocytes (iPSC-aCMs) were purchased from Axol Bioscience (cat. no. ax2518). Cells were thawed and seeded on fibronectin-coated culture vessels and were maintained in CM maintenance basal medium (Axol Bioscience, cat. no. ax2530) as per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Human induced pluripotent stem cell–derived ventricular cardiomyocytes (iPSC-vCMs) were differentiated from a human iPSC line BJ RiPS-D, which was derived from fibroblasts of a healthy Caucasian male donor,28 using a modified protocol from Limpitikul et al.29 Briefly, iPSCs were cultured in Geltrex matrix (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. A1413302)–coated tissue culture plates and fed daily with Essential 8 medium (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. A1517001). When ∼95% confluent (day 0), medium was changed to RPMI-1640 GlutaMAX medium (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 61870127) supplemented with B-27 (minus insulin) (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. A1895601) and 6 μM CHIR99021 (Stemgent, cat. no. 04-0004). Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 GlutaMAX medium supplemented with B-27 (minus insulin) for the first 7 days, with medium exchange every 2 days. On day 3, 5 μM IWP-4 (Stemgent, cat. no. 04-0036) was added. On day 8, the medium was changed to RPMI-1640 GlutaMAX with B-27 supplement (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 17504044), with medium exchange every 2 days. Spontaneous contraction of iPSC-CMs was observed by days 8-9. IPSC-vCMs were then replated at half density on day 11 for metabolic selection30 on days 15-20. On day 25, cells were replated again on Geltrex matrix–coated optically clear imaging 96-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/well. Optically clear imaging plates were used to create monolayers for imaging. IPSC-vCMs were used for functional studies on days 32-35. Detailed characterization of iPSC-aCMs and iPSC-vCMs is shown in Supplemental Figure 1.

Human iPSC-CFs were differentiated from the BJ RiPS-D cell line using a previously published protocol.31 Briefly, when iPSCs reach ∼95% confluence (day 0), the medium was exchanged with RPMI-1640 GlutaMAX supplemented with B-27 (minus insulin) and 6 μM CHIR99021. Cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 GlutaMAX medium supplemented with B-27 (minus insulin) for the first 7 days, with medium exchange every 2 days. On day 3, 5 μM IWR-1 (Selleckchem, cat. no. S7086) was added. On day 6, cells were replated at 2 × 105 cells/cm2 density in Advanced DMEM/GlutaMAX medium (Life Technologies, cat. no. 12634-028) containing 5 μM of CHIR99021, 2 μM of retinoic acid (Sigma, cat. no. R2625), 5 μM of Y27632, and 1% fetal bovine serum. Cells were maintained in Advanced DMEM/GlutaMAX from days 6-13 with medium change every 2 days. On days 7-8, 5 μM of CHIR99021 and 2 μM of retinoic acid were added. On day 11, cells were replated at the 2 × 105 cells/cm2 density in Advanced DMEM/GlutaMAX supplemented with 2 μM SB431542 (Tocris Bioscience, cat. no. 1614). At this step, a confluent sheet of proepicardial cells was achieved. On days 14-19, proepicardial cells were cultured in fibroblast growth medium 3 (PromoCell, cat. no. C23025) containing 20 ng/mL of FGF2 (R&D Systems, cat. no. 233-FB) and 10 μM of SB431542. On day 20 and every other day thereafter, CFs were fed with fibroblast growth medium 3, supplemented with 10 μM of SB431542. IPSC-CFs were seeded at 1 × 105 cells/cm2 for usage. Detailed characterization of iPSC-CFs and their epicardial precursors is shown in Supplemental Figure 2.

Differentiation of human macrophages

Human monocyte cell line, THP-1, was differentiated into macrophages based on a modified protocol from Phu et al.32 Briefly, THP-1 cells were cultured in suspension in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 11875093), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% GlutaMAX (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 35050061), and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol. When confluent, cells were then seeded on tissue culture–treated plates at a density of 2.8 × 105 cells/cm2 and differentiated into macrophages by culturing in 25 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. P1585) for 48 hours. Differentiated macrophages were then switched to AIM V serum-free medium (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 12055083) for at least 48 hours before usage.

Isolation of and treatment with VAT EVs

VAT biopsy samples were obtained from donors as part of the EVOC (EVs in Obesity and Cardiometabolic Disease; NCT06408961) study. Routinely discarded visceral fat was collected during sleeve gastrectomy surgery, and in gastric bypass, where no visceral fat was routinely removed, a small segment of visceral fat was removed from the greater omentum. VAT was minced and cultured in RPMI-1640 media (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 11875093) with B27 supplement (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 17504044). Conditioned media, containing EVs released from VAT, were collected after 48 hours and 96 hours, centrifuged and filtered (0.8 μm) to remove tissue debris, and stored at −80 °C until usage.

Small EVs were isolated from VAT-conditioned media by size exclusion chromatography (SEC)–based method using qEVoriginal 35 nm Gen 2 columns (Izon Science, cat. no. ICO-35). The void volume was set at 2.5 mL, and 3 mL of EV-enriched fractions in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 10010049) were collected. Concentration and size distribution of EVs were measured by microfluidic resistive pulse sensing (Spectradyne’s nCS1, Signal Hill). Cells were treated with 7.5 × 1010 EVs/mL for 24 hours for iPSC-CMs, 96 hours for iPSC-CFs (re-dosed at the same concentration after 48 hours), and 48 hours for macrophages. As controls, iPSC-CMs, iPSC-CFs, and macrophages were treated with an equal number of EVs extracted from conditioned medium from respective cell types for the same duration.

Measurement of electrical properties and calcium transients of iPSC-CMs

For action potential measurement, iPSC-CMs were stained with voltage-sensitive fluorescence dye, FluoVolt (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. F10488), for 20 minutes at 37 °C, following the manufacturer’s instructions, washed with Tyrode’s solution 3 times, and imaged in Tyrode’s solution. For calcium transient measurement, iPSC-CMs were stained with calcium-sensitive fluorescence dye, Fluo-4 NW (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. F36206), for 30 minutes at 37 °C, following the manufacturer’s instructions, washed 3 times, and imaged in Tyrode’s solution. Both action potential and calcium transient measurements were performed using an automated video imaging system, Kinetic Imaging Cytometry (Vala Sciences), at 95 frames per second. Image intensity was measured using ImageJ.33 Custom software written in MATLAB (Mathworks) was used to calculate APD, calcium transient magnitude, time to peak, and decay constant.

For the normalized amplitude of action potential and Ca2+ transient, “bleach” signal was first fitted with an exponential function to estimate fluorophore bleaching. Next, the normalized signal was calculated by subtracting the bleach signal from the measured signal and dividing this value by bleach signal. Ca2+ transient decay constant (τ) was measured by fitting an exponential function to the decay limb of the normalized Ca2+ transient tracing.

Assessment of gene expression with quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated using miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 217084). RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific). An equal amount of total RNA (250-1,000 ng) was used to create complementary deoxyribonucleic acid (cDNA) libraries using a high-capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 4368813). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed using Ssoadvanced Universal SYBR (Bio-Rad, cat. no. 1725274) in the QuantStudio 6 Flex real-time PCR system (Thermo Scientific). Primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 1.

Quantification of collagen secretion by iPSC-CFs

Medium from EV-treated iPSC-CFs was collected at 48 and 96 hours and combined. Secreted collagen in medium was then measured using Sirius Red Collagen Detection Kit (Amsbio, cat. no. 9062), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Animal studies

All animal studies were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Exomap mice were a kind gift from Dr Stephen Gould at Johns Hopkins University.26 Adipoq-Cre BAC transgenic mice, where Cre recombinase is expressed under the control of adiponectin promoter/enhancer region, were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (strain no. 028020). Transgenic mice were crossed with wild-type (C57BL/6J, The Jackson Laboratory) to produce litters containing both transgenic and wild-type offspring. Genotyping was performed by PCR on DNA extracted from ear tissue, using the following primer set: forward 5′-GGTGATAGGTGGCAAGTGGTATTCCGTAAG-3′, reverse 5′-CATA-TATGGGCTATGAACTAATGACCCCGT-3′ for Exomap and forward 5′-GGATGTGCCATGTGAGTCTG-3′, reverse 5′-ACGGACAGAAGCATTTTCCA-3′ for Adipoq-Cre. Starting at 8 weeks of age, the double-positive Adipoq-Cre+/Exomap+ male mice were fed with either high-fat diet (Research Diets, cat. no. D12492, 60% fat by calories) for obese mice or regular chow for lean mice. After 16 weeks of diet, the mice were sacrificed, and their hearts were collected.

Flow cytometry of cardiac macrophages

Macrophages were isolated from left ventricular (LV) tissue using a protocol described in Hulsmans et al.34 Briefly, mice hearts were perfused with cold PBS. LVs were cut out, minced, and incubated in enzyme cocktails at 37 °C for 40 minutes while on a shaker at 200 rpm. Enzyme cocktails include 450 U/mL of collagenase I (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. C0130), 125 U/mL of collagenase XI (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. C7657), 60 U/mL of DNase I (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. D5319), and 60 U/mL of hyaluronidase (Sigma-Aldrich, cat. no. H3506) and 20 mM HEPES. Cells were strained through a 40-μm cell strainer, centrifuged at 340 ×g for 5 minutes, and resuspended in PBS.

Cells were stained with the following antibodies at 4 °C for 30 minutes (company, catalog no, dilution): APC-Cy7–conjugated anti-mouse CD45 (Biolegend, 103115, 1:50), BV650-conjugated anti-mouse/human CD11b (Biolegend, 101239, 1:25), and BV785-conjugated anti-mouse CD206 (Biolegend, 141729, 1:50). 7-aminoactinomycin viability staining solution (Biolegend, 420403, 1:16) was used as viability marker. Then, stained cells were washed with MACS buffer (0.5% bovine serum albumin and 2 mM EDTA in Dulbecco's PBS), centrifuged at 340 ×g for 5 minutes, and resuspend in MACS buffer for flow cytometry. Data were acquired on an FACSAriaIIu (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with FlowJo software (FlowJo, LLC). Macrophages were identified as CD45+/CD11b+. Proinflammatory macrophages were CD206– and reparative macrophages were CD206+.

Imaging of adipose tissue

After the hearts were harvested, inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT) was dissected from the mice. A small 3 × 3 × 3 mm piece of iWAT was placed on a microscope slide and a coverslip was pressed on top of the iWAT to flatten the tissue for imaging. Prepared slides of flattened iWAT were imaged under fluorescence microscope using standard green and red fluorescence cubes.

Immunoblotting

Western blot analysis was performed on snap-frozen left atrial (LA) and LV tissue. Tissue samples were lysed in Pierce RIPA lysis and extraction buffer (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 89900) supplemented with Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fischer Scientific, cat. no. 78441). Protein concentration was measured using Pierce BCA protein assay kits (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 23227). Equal amounts of protein lysate (23 μg) were treated with Laemmli buffer with β-mercaptoethanol and incubated at 95 °C for 7 minutes. The lysate was then electrophoresed on a 4% to 20% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide resolving gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride membrane. Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, washed, and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature with secondary antibodies. Primary antibodies used included (company, catalog no, dilution): mNeonGreen (Chromotek, 32f6-100, 1:1000) and vinculin (Sigma-Aldrich, V9264, 1:10,000). Secondary antibodies included (company, catalog no, dilution): horseradish peroxidase–conjugated goat anti-rabbit immunoglobulins (Agilent Technologies, P044801-2, 1:2500) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse immunoglobulins (Agilent Technologies, P044701-2, 1:5000). Lastly, antibody signal was detected using Supersignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. 34095). The intensity of proteins of interest was quantified using iBright Analysis Software (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Nanoflow cytometry analysis of circulating EVs

Mouse whole blood was collected by buccal puncture into EDTA-containing tubes and centrifuged at 2,000 ×g for 10 minutes at 4 °C. Supernatant (plasma) was then collected. Small EVs were isolated from plasma by SEC-based method using qEVoriginal 35 nm Gen 2 columns. The void volume was set at 2.5 mL, and 3 mL of EV-enriched in PBS were collected. Isolated EVs were concentrated back down to 0.5 mL using Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter, 10 kDa MWCO (Millipore, cat. no. UCF8010).

Concentration of isolated plasma EVs was measured using nanoflow cytometry with a flow nanoanalyzer (NanoFCM). An aliquot of 1-2 × 1011 EVs/mL was diluted 10-fold with PBS and fixed with permeabilization buffer (NanoFCM, cat. no. P2320) at a 1:5 ratio for 30 minutes at room temperature. Next, fixed sample was diluted 1:10 fold with PBS and incubated at room temperature with mNeonGreen primary antibody (Chromotek, 32f6-100) at 1:20 concentration for 30 minutes. Next, secondary antibody, Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed, Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, cat. no. A-11001) was added to the mixture at 1:1,000 concentration and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. Samples were diluted at 1:100 to 1:200 with PBS-H (10 mM HEPES in PBS, pH 7.4) and analyzed with a flow nanoanalyzer using a standard green fluorescence filter.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice hearts were perfused with cold PBS, dissected, and fixed overnight at 4 °C with 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed hearts were transferred to 70% ethyl alcohol and were paraffin embedded. Thin sections of tissue were cut, underwent deparaffinization and antigen retrieval, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-10 (Thermo Scientific, cat. no. PI85111) in PBS, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin in PBS. Thinly sliced tissue sections were then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed, and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Tissue sections were finally counterstained with 4′6-diamidino-2-phenylindole. Primary antibodies used included (company, catalog no, dilution): mNeoGreen (Chromotek, 32F6, 1:50), cardiac troponin I (Abcam, ab47003, 1:100), vimentin (Abcam, ab92547, 1:100), and CD68 (Cell Signaling, 97778T, 1:200). Secondary antibodies included (company, catalog no, dilution): Goat anti-Mouse IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Cyanine3 (Invitrogen, A10521, 1:500) and Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (H+L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, Cyanine5 (Invitrogen, A10523, 1:500).

Bulk RNA sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was isolated using miRNeasy Micro Kit (Qiagen, cat. no. 217084). Samples were treated with DNase I and cleaned up using RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 (Zymo Research, cat. no. R1014). Stranded cDNA libraries were created using SMARTer Stranded Total RNA-Seq Kit v2 – Pico Input Mammalian (Takara Bio USA, Inc, cat. no. 634413) and sequenced at paired-end 150 bp on the Illumina NovaSeq Xp platform (Illumina).

Adapter sequences were trimmed from sequencing reads using Cutadapt (v4.8).35 Next, quality assessment of both raw and adaptor-trimmed reads was performed with FastQC (v0.12.1) (www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Subsequently, the trimmed reads were aligned to the Gencode GRCh38.p13 reference genome using STAR (v2.7.11a).36 STAR was supplied with Gencode v38 gene annotations to enhance mapping precision, and gene-level read counts were obtained using featureCounts (v2.0.6).37 ComplexHeatmap38 was used for cluster analysis and visualization. Significantly differentially expressed genes with absolute fold change ≥1.2 and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) P ≤ 0.10 were detected by DESeq2 (v1.42.1).39 For differential expression analyses, genes with a median read count below 5 in both conditions were excluded. To account for the batch effects arising from the sequencing of active fibroblast and macrophage samples across 2 distinct batches, 2 batch correction methods were used for differential expression analysis. First, DESeq2 was used with a batch included as a covariate in the model. Second, DESeq2 was applied to data preprocessed using ComBatSeq40 to remove batch effects. Only genes identified as differentially expressed by both approaches were reported to ensure robust and reliable results. Genome Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes pathway over-representation analysis was performed on differentially expressed genes using the WebGestaltR package (v1.0.0).41 Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA package (v4.3.2)42 on the database (v2022.1.Hs). For both over-representation analysis and GSEA, differential expression results derived from ComBat-Seq preprocessed data were used for the active fibroblast and macrophage datasets.

Transcriptome-wide genetic association studies and localization of cell types of interest via epigenetics and chromatin-based studies

Using summary statistics from GWAS of QT interval (n = 117,532)43 and AF (n = 1,650,345 with 181,446 cases),44 we performed transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS)45 of the corresponding phenotypes. We used MR-JTI46 TWAS models trained on 2 heart tissues (left ventricle and atrial appendage) and subset the associations to retain only genes that were differentially expressed (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) upon EV treatment in atrium, ventricle, fibroblast, or monocyte. Because each subset of TWAS genes consisted of ≈900 genes, TWAS associations with P < 5.56 × 10-5 (Bonferroni adjusted for 900 comparisons) were considered significant.

Because TWAS was performed in atrial and ventricular tissue, inference on whether specific genes in a specific cell type (eg, aCMs and vCMs, fibroblasts, macrophages) are related to our phenotypes is inaccessible to standard TWAS methodology. Therefore, we used cell type–specific epigenomic, chromatin accessibility, and chromatin conformation assays to provide cell type–specific genetic evidence linking QT interval and AF with the VAT EV–induced gene expression changes in the 4 cell types tested. Our methodology builds on previous work,21 as appropriately referenced and attributed. We used a regulatory annotation framework that integrates 18 epigenomic features, including proximity to a transcription start site, assays of chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq and DNase-seq), histone ChIP-seq (for chromatin marks H3K27ac, H3K27me3, H3K4me1, H3K4me2, H3K4me3, H3K36me3, H3K79me2, H3K9ac, H3K9me3, and H4K20me1), and transcription factor ChIP-seq (for RNA polymerase II subunit POL2RA and cohesion complex subunits RAD21 and SMC3).21,47 This framework was used to identify cell type–specific regulatory elements in 4 atrium biosamples, 8 ventricle biosamples, 41 fibroblast biosamples, and 4 macrophage-analog biosamples.48 We used Hi-C data from the 4D Nucleome Project49 to identify chromatin contacts between regulatory elements and differentially expressed genes. Hi-C in primary CMs from the LA (4DNFIBNXTSV4) was used to represent chromatin contacts in atrium, Hi-C in primary CMs of the left ventricle (4DNFISWHXA16) was used to represent chromatin contacts in the ventricle, Hi-C in the cell line IMR-90 (4DNFIJTOIGOI) was used to identify chromatin contacts in fibroblasts, and Hi-C in monocytes (4DNFI4HZHGLC) was used for chromatin contact analysis in macrophages. For each Hi-C dataset, the contact matrix had been processed using the gold standard 4D Nucleome pipeline (https://data.4dnucleome.org/resources/data-analysis/hi_c-processing-pipeline) and normalization had been performed using the iterative correction and eigenvalue decomposition algorithm.50 Cooler (v0.8.2)51 was downloaded in mcool format. Contact pairs between a regulatory element and one of the differentially expressed genes were then exported in bedpe format. Using BEDTools (v2.30.0),52 we identified genome-wide significant (P < 5 × 10–8) GWAS SNPs associated with QT interval43 and AF44 that overlapped one of the cell-type-specific regulatory elements in contact (via Hi-C) with a differentially expressed gene.

TRPC3 suppression

IPSC-aCMs and iPSC-vCMs were treated with pyrazole 3 (Pyr3) (Tocris, cat. no. 3751), a small molecule TRPC3 inhibitor, at 7.5 μM for 24 hours at the same time as VAT EV treatment. Myocytes were also treated with the same concentration of Pyr3 during action potential recording.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean ± SEM or fold change ± SEM, unless otherwise indicated. They were compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were presented as percentages and were compared using the Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. For multiple hypothesis testing, the Bonferroni correction was applied. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 5 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Altered electrical properties of myocytes in obese individuals

To explore the effect of obesity on electrophysiological properties of human atrial myocytes, we first examined the relationship between obesity and cardiac electrophysiology in human subjects. APD at 90% repolarization (APD90) was measured at 1 Hz pacing rate in cardiac tissue samples from patients undergoing open heart surgeries with BMI <25 kg/m2 (lean) and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 (overweight/obese). Figure 1A shows action potential tracing from myocytes isolated from the right atria of individuals with BMI <25 and ≥25 kg/m2. Patients with higher BMI exhibit significantly prolonged APD compared to lean individuals (Figure 1B, 157.5 ± 71.4 ms vs 77.9 ± 28.4 ms, P = 0.003), providing clinical evidence that obesity is associated with altered cardiac electrophysiology that could predispose to arrhythmias. Detailed characteristics of right atrial tissue donors do not show significant differences in baseline comorbidities, medications, or LA size (Supplemental Table 2).

Atrial myocytes from lean patients (body mass index [BMI] <25 kg/m2; n = 6) showed significantly shorter APD90 compared to atrial myocytes from overweight/obese patients (BMI ≥25 kg/m2; n = 9) measured at 1-Hz pacing rate. (C) Resting membrane potential (RMP) of atrial myocytes. No statistically significant difference was found between the RMP of lean and overweight/obese groups. VAT EVs promote pro-arrhythmic electrical and Ca2+ activity in human iPSC-CMs

To determine the direct signaling effects of VAT EVs on human CMs, we next treated human iPSC-aCMs and human iPSC-vCMs with EVs derived from VAT to assess changes in electrical properties. EVs were extracted from conditioned medium of cultured minced VAT (VAT EVs) from donors who underwent bariatric surgeries (EVOC study) by SEC. Detailed characteristics of VAT donors are displayed in Supplemental Table 3. VAT EVs as well as EVs derived from 0.5 mL of plasma from obese (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) or lean (BMI <25 kg/m2) patients were characterized following Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles guidelines, including size distribution and concentration via microfluidic resistive pulse sensing and canonical surface and cargo protein markers with some variability observed in the yield of VAT EVs obtained from the adipose tissue samples (Supplemental Figure 3). Additional VAT EV characterization can be found in Chatterjee et al.21 Both iPSC-aCMs and iPSC-vCMs were treated for 24 hours with VAT EVs at a concentration of 7.5 × 1010 EVs/mL. To account for potential unintended effects of EV treatment, the control group was treated with the same number of EVs extracted from conditioned media of iPSC-aCMs or iPSC-vCMs. Dose-response studies showed that treatment with of 7.5 × 1010EVs/mL (but not lower doses) of VAT EVs for 24 hours rendered phenotypic changes without overt toxicity in iPSC-CMs (Supplemental Figure 4)

Figure 2A shows fluorescence signal representing membrane voltage of iPSC-aCMs during 1-Hz pacing. After 24 hours of VAT EV treatment, pronounced action potential prolongation was observed in iPSC-aCMs treated with VAT EVs compared to control. APD measured at 80% repolarization (APD80) at 1-Hz pacing rate was significantly longer in VAT EV–treated iPSC-aCMs compared to control (Figure 2B) (394 ± 28 ms vs 345 ± 29 ms, P < 0.001). Similarly, prolonged action potential was observed in iPSC-vCMs treated with VAT EVs (Figure 2C) with significantly longer APD80 compared to control (Figure 2D) (602 ± 56 ms vs 435 ± 42 ms, P < 0.001). Although VAT EV treatment led to prolongation of APD in both atrial and ventricular myocytes, an argument can be made that in this setting, CMs are directly exposed to adipose tissue–derived EVs and may not represent the in vivo state in which CMs are exposed to circulating EVs in which only a fraction of total EVs originate from adipose tissue, thus potentially diluting out the phenotype observed. To simulate this in vivo condition, we treated CMs with plasma-derived EVs from lean and obese donors (see Supplemental Table 4 for detailed characteristics of plasma donors). After 24-hour treatment of plasma-derived EVs, the action potential was similarly prolonged in iPSC-aCMs treated with plasma-derived EVs from obese donors compared to those from lean donors (Supplemental Figures 5A and 5B). Similarly, iPSC-vCMs treated with plasma-derived EV from obese donors showed APD prolongation compared to those treated with EVs from lean donors (Supplemental Figures 5C and 5D). In both cell types, the extent of APD prolongation after plasma EV treatment was less than after VAT EV treatment, likely reflecting exposure to relatively lower dose of adipose EVs in the plasma (compared to EVs directly derived from adipose explants).

Alteration of action potential shape and/or duration is a known risk factor for arrhythmia. In fact, we observed (pro)arrhythmic behaviors in iPSC-aCMs treated with VAT EVs, including early after depolarization and oscillation of membrane voltage independent of pacing stimulus (Figure 2E). These (pro)arrhythmic events were observed more frequently in CMs treated with VAT EVs compared to control (Figure 2F) which likely corresponds to the observed increased risk of arrhythmia in obese individuals.

In addition to membrane voltage disturbances, dysfunction of intracellular Ca2+ cycling may also drive arrhythmogenesis. Therefore, we examined the effect of VAT EV treatment on intracellular Ca2+ transients in cardiomyocytes. Figure 2G shows representative Ca2+ transient of iPSC-aCMs at 0.5-Hz pacing rate. IPSC-aCMs treated with VAT EVs demonstrate slower upstroke (time to peak) and slower decay time (t) compared to control (Figure 2H) (time to peak 410 ± 51 ms vs 342 ± 23 ms, P < 0.001; t 362 ± 41 ms vs 292 ± 33 ms, P < 0.001) although without significant difference in Ca2+ transient magnitude (normalized fluorescence intensity 0.92 ± 0.17 vs 0.86 ± 0.14, P = 0.37). Similar alteration in Ca2+ transient dynamics was observed in iPSC-vCMs treated with VAT EVs (Figures 2I and 2J) where VAT EV treatment led to prolongation of upstroke (565 ± 41 ms vs 499 ± 26 ms, P = 0.022), slower decay time (403 ± 87 ms vs 279 ± 47 ms, P = 0.001), and no significant change in Ca2+ transient magnitude (0.70 ± 0.14 vs 0.71 ± 0.17, P = 0.766).

In addition to systolic Ca2+ level, diastolic Ca2+ and the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) Ca2+ content could also affect proarrhythmic behavior of cellular substrate. Therefore, we measured diastolic Ca2+ level and SR content in CMs in treatment and control groups. Although there was impaired systolic Ca2+ cycling, there was no statistically significant difference in diastolic Ca2+ level or SR Ca2+ content in iPSC-aCMs and iPSC-vCMs treated with VAT EVs compared to control (Supplemental Figure 6). Disruption in overall Ca2+ transient dynamic can impact the well-orchestrated interaction between membrane voltage and intracellular Ca2+ during action potential generation leading to an increased risk of arrhythmogenesis.

VAT EVs induce a profibrotic and inflammatory state in non-CM cell types central to arrhythmogenesis in vitro

Apart from CMs, cardiac-resident fibroblasts and macrophages are critical in the pathogenesis of arrhythmia through effects on inflammation and fibrosis. We first investigated whether VAT EVs influence human iPSC-CFs trans-differentiation into myofibroblasts—cells characterized by enhanced contractility and increased extracellular matrix production53 and a characteristic response of fibroblasts to injury or activation of the transforming growth factor beta 1 signaling pathway. Real-time quantitative PCR analysis revealed a significant upregulation of myofibroblast markers after treatment of iPSC-CFs with VAT EVs, including α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, 2.3 ± 0.7 fold, P = 0.003) and periostin (7.3 ± 3.8 fold, P = 0.005) in VAT EV–treated cells compared to controls (iPSC-CFs treated with the same concentration of iPSC-CF–derived EVs) and upregulation of collagen type 1 (COL1A1, 2.3 ± 0.8 fold, P = 0.002), a major component of extracellular matrix (Figure 3A). Additionally, iPSC-CFs treated with VAT EVs also secreted more collagen into culture media compared to control (Figure 3B) (1.7 ± 0.4 fold, P = 0.020). Overall, treatment with VAT EVs increased trans-differentiation of iPSC-CFs to myofibroblasts and increased collagen production, both of which are hallmarks of increased cardiac tissue fibrosis, a major risk factor for arrhythmogenesis.

Next, we investigated the effect of VAT EVs on macrophage polarization toward proinflammatory phenotypes. Figure 3C shows that VAT EV treatment induced a shift along the macrophage activation spectrum, with upregulation of proinflammatory activation markers including tumor necrosis factor α (1.3 ± 0.2 fold, P = 0.004), interleukin 1β (1.5 ± 0.3 fold, P = 0.008), and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (5.7 ± 1.8 fold, P < 0.001), and concurrent downregulation of markers associated with anti-inflammatory or reparative activation states, including arginase 1 (Arg1) (0.8 ± 0.1 fold, P = 0.007) and cluster of differentiation 206 (CD206) (0.3 ± 0.2 fold, P < 0.001). Taken together, these gene expression changes indicate that VAT EVs shift macrophages away from reparative activation states and toward a proinflammatory activation phenotype. To extend these findings in vivo, we performed flow cytometry on cardiac macrophages from lean and obese mouse hearts using CD206 expression as a marker of reparative activation state (Figure 3D). Consistent with our in vitro findings, obese mouse hearts showed a significant shift in the macrophage activation landscape, with a higher proportion of CD206-negative (proinflammatory) relative to CD206-positive (reparative) macrophages compared to lean hearts (Figure 3E) (0.4 ± 0.2 vs 0.2 ± 0.4, P = 0.038). This VAT EV–driven shift in macrophage activation states—observed both in vitro and in vivo—likely reflects a continuous remodeling of the cardiac immune microenvironment and could create a vulnerable cardiac substrate conducive to arrhythmogenesis.34

In vivo evidence of adipose to myocardial communication by adipose-derived EVs

Given the in vitro effects of VAT EVs on CMs, we next studied whether adipose-derived EVs reach the heart in vivo in obesity. We used a unique transgenic mouse model, Exomap1, where tissue-specific EVs are tagged with green fluorescent protein and human cluster of differentiation 81 (CD81) surface marker protein in response to Cre expression,26 which allows tracking of their biodistribution. Crossing this Exomap1 transgenic line with Adiopoq-Cre transgenic mice, where a Cre recombinase is expressed under control of adiponectin promoter/enhancer region, allows tracking of tagged adipose tissue–specific green EVs. Only double-positive Adipoq-Cre+/Exomap+ mice expressed a fusion protein hsCD81-mNeonGreen, as evident by green fluorescence signal from iWAT and immunoblot of iWAT lysate against mNeonGreen protein (Figures 4A to 4C and 5A).

After 16 weeks, the body weight of double-positive Adipoq-Cre+/Exomap+ male mice fed with high-fat diet (“obese”) was significantly higher than those fed with regular diet (“lean”) (Supplemental Figures 7A and 8A). However, although we noted a trend towards increased heart-weight and markers of pathologic hypertrophy, the weight of the ventricles normalized by tibial length (Supplemental Figures 7B and 8B), markers of molecular hypertrophy (Supplemental Figure 8C), the degree of histologic cardiac interstitial and perivascular fibrosis (Supplemental Figures 7C to 7F), or markers of molecular fibrosis (Supplemental Figure 8D) did not reach statistical significance between the 2 groups. This is consistent with the modest effects of diet-induced obesity on pathologic cardiac hypertrophy or fibrosis despite enhanced atrial arrhythmogenicity.54

To investigate inter-organ communication between adipose and cardiac tissue, we first demonstrated that adipose tissue–specific EVs, tagged with hsCD81-mNeonGreen protein, are secreted into plasma (Figure 4D) and consistent with many prior studies,21,55 obesity was associated with a higher proportion of adipose-derived circulating EVs (Figure 4E). Next, to establish that circulating adipose-derived EVs target the heart, we performed immunoblot of cardiac tissue lysate which demonstrated presence of hsCD81-mNeonGreen protein in both LA and LV, indicating that adipose tissue–specific EVs were trafficked to the heart (Figures 5B to 5C). Interestingly, both LA and LV from obese mice had significantly higher targeting of adipose tissue–specific EVs compared to lean mice, as evident by higher intensity of normalized hsCD81-mNeonGreen bands to a housekeeping protein, vinculin, in immunoblot (1.6 ± 0.6 fold, P = 0.039 for LA and 3.43 ± 2.8 fold, P = 0.004 for LV).

Additionally, immunohistochemistry of LV tissue showed that adipose tissue–specific EVs are deposited primarily in CMs, with more modest targeting of CFs, and macrophages, as evident by double positive staining of mNeonGreen and cardiac troponin I, vimentin, and CD68, respectively (Figures 5D to 5G). In vivo demonstration that the 3 pillars of arrhythmogenic substate are targets of adipose-derived EVs validates the physiological relevance of our in vitro findings.

VAT EVs induce broad alterations in transcriptional profile of CMs, CFs, and macrophages in vitro

To further explore potential mechanisms of VAT EV–associated arrhythmogenesis, we next performed RNA sequencing of VAT EV–treated CMs, CFs, and macrophages (Figures 6A to 6H). Genes involved in electrical and structural remodeling, inflammation, and cholesterol and lipid metabolism were prominently affected in both aCMs and vCMs (Supplemental Figure 9). In iPSC-aCMs (Figure 6B, Supplemental Table 5), we observed alteration in gene expression of ion channels (CACNA1G, HCN4, SCN9A, TRPC3), calcium handling proteins (RYR3, TRPC3), structural elements (CNTN5, ACTA2, various collagen types), natriuretic peptides indicative of hemodynamic stress (NPPA, NPPB), and cholesterol/lipid metabolism (HMGCR, LDLR). IPCS-vCMs (Figure 6D, Supplemental Table 6) displayed upregulation of genes involved in excitation-contraction coupling and metabolic remodeling (93 overlapping differentially expressed genes with aCMs) (Supplemental Table 7).

Transcriptome-wide genetic association studies implicate genes induced by VAT EVs in arrhythmogenesis

We next employed transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) in left ventricle and atrial appendage to perform genetic inference on key arrhythmic phenotypes in the heart (QT interval and AF) for the genes dysregulated by VAT-EVs. TWAS revealed significant association (P < 5.56 × 10-5) of 32 unique differentially expressed genes with QT interval and 24 associated with AF (Supplemental Table 10). The QT interval TWAS (Figure 7A) identified several genes with established roles in cardiac repolarization that were significantly altered by VAT EV treatment. For example, STRN, associated with cardiac hypertrophy and structural remodeling, showed significant upregulation in both aCMs and vCMs. TMEM176, which plays a role in inflammasome activation and has been linked to arrhythmia susceptibility, was prominently upregulated across both cell types. FADS1, involved in lipid metabolism and known to influence membrane composition and ion channel function, showed differential expression patterns. SYNPO2L, important for sarcomere development and contractile function, was also significantly affected.

TRPC3 mediates VAT EV–induced action potential prolongation

As proof-of-principle for the potential for adipose-heart communication via EVs to identify modifiable targets in arrhythmias, we focused on modulating TRPC3 (a cardiac ion channel upregulated after VAT EV exposure in CMs). TRPC3 channels conduct nonselective cation currents that are upregulated during stress and can prolong APD when overexpressed61 but have not previously been known to be involved in the pathogenesis of obesity-related AF. Moreover, TRPC3 was significantly upregulated in atrial myocytes (2.7-fold, FDR-adjusted P < 2.8 × 10–6) upon VAT EV treatment and had an available selective small molecule inhibitor. We co-treated CMs with VAT EVs and Pyr3, a small molecule selective TRPC3 inhibitor.62 In aCMs (Figures 8A and 8B), Pyr3 treatment abrogated VAT EV–induced APD prolongation, with APD80 values returning to near baseline (301 ± 12 ms with Pyr3 vs 396 ± 8 ms without Pyr3, vs 345 ± 6 ms in untreated controls; P <0.001). Treatment of control CMs with Pyr3 alone (without VAT EVs) produced modest APD shortening (Figure 8B) (294 ± 14 ms vs 346 ± 10 ms in untreated controls, P = 0.010), indicating some basal TRPC3 contribution to repolarization. However, the magnitude of APD reduction with Pyr3 treatment was significantly greater in VAT EV–treated cells (95 ± 14 ms reduction) compared to control cells (52 ± 17 ms reduction, P = 0.040), supporting a specific role for TRPC3 upregulation in mediating VAT EV–induced APD prolongation. In vCMs (Figures 8C and 8D), we observed a similar rescue, with Pyr3 treatment reducing APD80 from 602 ± 13 ms to 540 ± 7 ms (P = 0.025), whereas Pyr3 treatment of control vCMs produced insignificant shortening (495 ± 8 ms to 462 ± 13 ms, P = 0.058). The magnitude of APD reduction with Pyr3 treatment was again significantly greater in VAT EV–treated ventricular cells (62 ± 26 ms reduction) compared to control cells (33 ± 15 ms reduction, P = 0.009). These data provided a proof-of-concept for identification of putative therapeutic targets for obesity-associated AF, leveraging our approach of VAT EV–mediated perturbation of CM transcriptional programs.

Discussion

The traditional view on how obesity impacts cardiovascular risk points to effects on systemic inflammation and metabolic stress,63 although whether obesity-cardiovascular disease links result from direct fat-heart communication or a more general process remains occult. Recent work has broached the potential for local adipose mediators to mediate key obesity-related cardiac complications.20,64 Here, we present evidence linking VAT EVs from obese individuals to cardiac arrhythmia pathogenesis via integrated model system, human cellular, and human genetic approaches. We found that treatment of human iPSC-CMs with VAT EVs phenocopied APD prolongation observed in human atrial tissue from obese individuals and produces broad shifts in the transcriptional landscape of human iPSC-aCMs and iPSC-vCMs. These differentially expressed genes displayed causal association with AF and ventricular arrhythmia susceptibility via functional genomic methods in large GWAS of AF and QT interval. Furthermore, the effects of VAT EVs extended to proinflammatory macrophage polarization and profibrotic fibroblast activation, key steps in lowering arrhythmic thresholds in the heart. Importantly, we observed adipose-myocardial crosstalk in vivo through genetically engineered mouse models of EV tracking, specifically in obese rodents. Finally, focusing on TRPC3 (a pharmacologically targetable ion channel gene differentially expressed on VAT EV exposure), we demonstrated abrogation of the effect of the VAT EVs on APD with a selective small molecule TRPC3 inhibitor, providing additional evidence linking adipose-heart EV communication and obesity-induced AF. Through comprehensive molecular genetics, human, and experimental evidence, these results present an emerging paradigm involving direct adipose-myocardial communication as a targetable mechanism of arrhythmogenesis in obesity.

Individuals with obesity have a heightened risk for both atrial and ventricular arrhythmia, thought to be mediated by a panoply of systemic inflammatory factors,18,65 adverse cardiac remodeling,66,67 and cardiometabolic comorbidity.68,69 Recent data across more than 50,000 individuals worldwide suggests shared genetic links between obesity and AF,19 although how obesity itself (not only via its comorbidities) impacts arrhythmia is less clear. In this space, EVs have emerged as endocrine transducers of molecular signals across tissues in obesity-related complications. Adipose tissue serves an important source of circulating EVs,55 with weight loss causing a reduction in the number of overall and adipose-derived EVs in circulation as well as a shift in their cargo.70, 71, 72 In addition, adipose-derived EVs impact broad inflammatory states relevant to metabolic health,73, 74, 75 including macrophage polarization, hepatic fibrosis, muscle metabolism, and cardiovascular states. Most data in human arrhythmogenesis arise from explanted tissue from patients with established disease, demonstrating greater EV release and more proinflammatory/fibrotic contents from adipose tissue in patients with AF (relative to without AF),76 with the ability of these EVs to transfer these arrhythmic effects across cells. Specifically, transcriptional cargo (microRNAs) from these EVs can impact the membrane potential and potassium channel expression in rodent CMs.77

The current report adds substantially to these findings. First, in contrast to APD shortening observed in most animal systems78,79 of arrhythmia susceptibility used in discovery efforts and in recent epicardial fat-derived EV studies,76 we directly observed APD prolongation in electrophysiologic studies from human right atrial tissue in individuals with obesity (relative to nonobese), a phenotype reproduced upon treatment of iPSC-CMs with VAT EVs from individuals with obesity. We acknowledge that our findings of APD prolongation in human right atrial appendage myocytes appear to contrast with several prior studies demonstrating shortened effective refractory period (ERP) in obesity models. However, we hypothesize that these observations may be complementary rather than contradictory, potentially reflecting differences in anatomical location and measurement approaches. Specifically, we speculate that many prior studies measuring ERP in pulmonary veins—the primary ectopic trigger sites in AF—may reflect a distinct regional substrate where shortening facilitates rapid focal firing and arrhythmia initiation. In contrast, our measurements of APD in right atrial appendage working myocardium may reflect a different regional process, where prolongation contributes to dispersion of refractoriness relevant to re-entrant circuit maintenance. We acknowledge this anatomical framework remains speculative, as we did not directly measure ERP in pulmonary veins in this study, and direct comparisons across studies are limited by differences in methodology, species, and disease context. Similarly, although APD and ERP are related measurements that can diverge due to post-repolarization refractoriness—particularly when sodium channel recovery kinetics are altered80—whether such mechanisms account for discrepancies between our findings and prior reports remains to be formally tested. Importantly, our findings align with clinical observations demonstrating QT interval prolongation in obesity,9 which fundamentally requires prolonged ventricular APD and supports that obesity can cause prolongation rather than exclusive shortening of cardiac repolarization. The validation of APD prolongation in healthy iPSC-CMs treated with VAT EVs from obese donors—independent of confounding comorbidities present in surgical patients—provides strong evidence that adipose-derived signals directly cause this electrophysiological phenotype. Finally, many prior preclinical models reporting on APD shortening are derived from nonobese models with either rapid pacing, heart failure, or valvular disease, where the mechanism of atrial remodeling and AF may be different. We therefore propose that obesity-related electrical remodeling is heterogeneous and location-specific, with distinct mechanisms operating in different cardiac regions to collectively enhance arrhythmia susceptibility. This mechanism represents a departure from traditional models of AF (typically focus on APD shortening) and therapeutics (focus on causing APD prolongation). In addition, the impaired calcium handling we observed—specifically the prolonged time-to-peak and decay kinetics—further supports arrhythmogenic potential of VAT EVs: although peak calcium amplitude remained unchanged, the altered kinetics suggest disrupted excitation-contraction coupling that could contribute to both contractile dysfunction and electrical instability. Our data are a key contribution to the growing literature on differences in obesity-associated AF compared to AF associated with other conditions.81,82

In addition, our results extend previous proteomic results in adipose-derived EVs,76 demonstrating a broad adverse impact of VAT EVs on CMs, macrophages, and fibroblasts—key cells involved in preparing myocardial substrate for arrhythmia. The profibrotic response we observed in cardiac fibroblasts—characterized by upregulation of myofibroblast markers and increased collagen secretion—creates a substrate conducive to arrhythmogenesis through several mechanisms. Increased tissue fibrosis leads to conduction heterogeneity, slowed propagation velocity, and enhanced susceptibility to reentrant arrhythmias. The proinflammatory activation of macrophages further compounds this proarrhythmic environment by promoting tissue remodeling and potentially affecting CM function through paracrine signaling.25

Furthermore, the overlap of genes differentially expressed upon VAT EV treatment in these target cells with large-scale GWAS of AF and QT interval emphasized the causal role of adipose-derived EVs on arrhythmia pathogenesis. Our approach improves upon standard TWAS methodology by incorporating epigenetic and chromatin-based studies in the target cell types to enable cell type–specific genetic inference linking the genes differentially expressed upon VAT EV exposure to arrhythmia susceptibility. Key genes identified include STRN (associated with cardiac hypertrophy), TMEM176 (involved in inflammasome activation), FADS1 (lipid metabolism), and SYNPO2L (sarcomere development). The enrichment of transcription factors like PRRX1, which regulates cardiac development and fibrosis, suggests that VAT EVs may induce long-term epigenetic changes in cardiac cells. Finally, we observed for the first time in vivo direct communication between adipose tissue and the myocardium using a novel genetically engineered tracking system that labeled only adipose-derived EVs. The collection of our data—evidence of trans-organ communication, transfer of phenotype, and change in molecular state with reversibility (with TRPC3 modification)—provides compelling evidence to expand the current framework of obesity-induced arrhythmia to include trans-organ EV communication.

The identification of TRPC3 upregulation in VAT EV–treated CMs through RNA sequencing exemplifies how comprehensive transcriptomic analysis can guide rational drug repurposing strategies. The fact that TRPC3 emerged as a significantly differentially expressed gene provided mechanistic insight into the potential role of TRPC3 in observed action potential changes induced by VAT EVs and provided an example of novel target discovery. This led us to test Pyr3, an existing TRPC3 inhibitor originally developed for other applications. This approach represents a paradigm shift from traditional empirical drug screening to hypothesis-driven therapeutic targeting based on pathway-specific perturbations. The successful rescue of VAT EV–induced APD prolongation by Pyr3 treatment validates this transcriptomics-to-therapeutics pipeline and highlights the potential for systematically mining EV-induced gene expression changes to prioritize existing drug libraries for repurposing. This strategy could be particularly valuable for complex diseases such as obesity-associated cardiovascular complications, where multiple pathways are simultaneously perturbed. Future studies should systematically interrogate other targets identified in our transcriptomic screen.

Study limitations

Although baseline characteristics and medications are not significantly different (apart from BMI) between the obese and lean group, given the limited sample size, heterogeneity in baseline characteristics may nonetheless confound the electrophysiologic data in human atrial tissue (Figure 1). Our primary studies were on iPSC-CMs which are relatively less mature compared to myocytes of fully grown adults. Some of the ionic current composition and kinetics of each type of ion channels are different between iPSC-derived myocytes and adult human cells,83 so the contribution of phase 2 vs phase 3 prolongation might not align perfectly between the iPSC-derived myocytes and adult human myocytes. Additionally, the electrophysiological alterations observed in obese individuals likely result from a complex interplay of metabolic dysregulation, endocrine factors, cardiac structural changes, and diverse circulating mediators. Although VAT EVs represent an important mechanism in this process, they constitute one component within a multifactorial framework of obesity-related electrical remodeling. Moreover, adults with obesity exhibit chronic exposure to EVs whereas our acute in vitro exposures may not fully recapitulate this context. Chronic exposure patterns and reversibility of EV-induced changes will need to be characterized to inform therapeutic strategies. Although iPSC-CMs may not fully recapitulate in vivo human states, the concordance between human right atrial tissue electrophysiologic studies and iPSC-CMs in APD is reassuring. Additionally, this system allows for assessment of other molecular changes (such as transcripts) induced by VAT EV treatments that converge on a signal for AF.

Importantly, although our study shows that VAT EVs induce pro-arrhythmic cellular and molecular changes, including APD prolongation, membrane oscillation, calcium handling abnormalities, and proinflammatory remodeling, we have not directly documented arrhythmia generation in intact hearts or animal models following VAT EV exposure in this study. Although our previous work has shown that diet-induced obesity increases AF inducibility in mouse models,54 establishing a definitive causal link between VAT EVs and arrhythmias in vivo would require future studies incorporating continuous electrocardiographic monitoring in appropriate animal models or ex vivo perfused heart preparations following VAT EV exposure. Furthermore, we cultured all cell types separately with VAT EVs, given the complexity inherent in studying multicellular interactions that are likely at play in vivo. Future studies using co-culture systems, “heart on a chip” technologies,84 or other organoids will likely more closely recapitulate how adipose-derived EVs impact the multicellular environment of the human heart. Other areas of study within adipose-tissue EVs (proteomics, noncoding RNA) beyond transcripts are an additional future area of investigation. Additionally, although our transcriptomic analysis identified key pathways and targets such as TRPC3, the precise mediators within VAT EVs responsible for these effects remain unknown. Future studies incorporating comprehensive proteomic and RNA profiling of VAT EV cargo combined with functional validation will be necessary to identify the specific molecular mediators driving these pro-arrhythmic changes.

Conclusions

Our findings support a direct communication pathway between adipose tissue and the heart that promotes pro-arrhythmic remodeling. In vivo genetic tracking models support transfer of EVs from adipose tissue to myocardium, and studies in human iPSCs support broad transcriptional and functional effects of adipose-derived EVs on pathophenotypes relevant to arrhythmia. Both large-scale human genetic and functional studies (TRPC3 modulation) support a causal role for VAT EVs on arrhythmia susceptibility. This work implicates direct EV-based trans-tissue communication as a new mechanism linking adipose tissue in obesity to adverse cardiovascular consequences, offering a new pathway to interrupt cardiac arrhythmia in an increasingly obesogenic environment.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Obesity affects nearly 2.5 billion individuals worldwide and confers substantial risk for AF and ventricular arrhythmias, yet the mechanisms by which excess adiposity directly promote arrhythmogenesis remain incompletely understood. This study demonstrates for the first time that human atrial myocytes from obese individuals exhibit prolonged APD compared to lean counterparts—and that this phenotype may be mediated by the direct effects of adipose-tissue derived EVs. Beyond cardiomyocytes, VAT EVs promote profibrotic activation of cardiac fibroblasts and proinflammatory polarization of cardiac macrophages, collectively establishing a multicellular arrhythmogenic substrate. In vivo tracking using a transgenic mouse model confirmed preferential trafficking of adipose-derived EVs to the myocardium in obese compared to lean animals. These findings establish a direct fat-heart communication axis mediated by EVs as a novel mechanism underlying obesity-associated arrhythmogenesis, distinct from the traditionally described pathways of systemic inflammation and hemodynamic stress.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: These findings carry important clinical implications. The demonstration that obesity-associated AF involves a mechanistically distinct pathway—direct EV-mediated adipose-to-myocardial signaling—may explain the well-recognized resistance of obesity-related AF to conventional therapies such as catheter ablation and antiarrhythmic drugs. Pharmacologic inhibition of TRPC3, an ion channel upregulated in cardiomyocytes upon VAT EV exposure, rescued the observed APD prolongation, identifying a novel and potentially druggable target. Furthermore, the transcriptomics-to-therapeutics pipeline demonstrated here—linking EV-induced gene expression changes in relevant cardiac cell types to large-scale GWAS of QT interval and AF—provides a broadly applicable framework for identifying and repurposing therapeutic targets in complex cardiometabolic disease. Future studies should evaluate whether circulating VAT EV burden or cargo can serve as biomarkers of arrhythmia risk in individuals with obesity, and whether interventions that reduce adipose EV secretion (such as bariatric surgery or weight loss) attenuate arrhythmic susceptibility through this pathway.
