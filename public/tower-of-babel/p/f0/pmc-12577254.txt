Nurse-Directed coordination of cancer survivorship care in a tribal primary care clinic
Authors: Amber S Anderson-Buettner, Kathleen A Dwyer, Zsolt J Nagykaldi, LaRessa Perrin, Janis E Campbell, Stephanie F Pharr, Mark P Doescher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo explore feasibility of using a Nurse Cancer Care Coordinator (CCC) to synthesize cancer survivorship care data, assess degree of cancer-related distress, and coordinate care for American Indian (AI) patients seen in a tribal primary care clinic.MethodsA CCC from one of Choctaw Nation’s primary care facility collected data on American Indian adults who had completed cancer treatment within the preceding five-years. Measures included demographics, cancer type, treatment and potential adverse effects, schedule of follow-up visits, behavioral risk assessment, global distress and checklist of distress sources, and length of time and frequency of attempts to complete data collection.ResultsNone of the participants had ever received a survivorship care plan (SCP). A total of 15 oncology providers were contacted to gather SCP and Distress Thermometer (DT) information on the 23 participants. Time to complete the SCP ranged from 30 to 120 min, with an average of 62 min per participant. The baseline DT score was 4.4 out of 10 and the average number of problems reported was 5.0. The top five challenges experienced by participants were fatigue (57%), pain (57%), worry (48%), tingling in hands/feet (33%), and sleep (33%).ConclusionsA Nurse CCC embedded within a tribal clinic organized clinically relevant survivorship care information, but this was a labor-intensive undertaking. Further work is needed to determine if risk stratification could be used to identify subsets of patients who would most benefit from this intervention.Implications for cancer survivors: Enhancement of Primary Care and Oncology communication may improve survivor care and health outcomes.Keywords: Cancer, Cancer survivorship, Delivery of health care, Oklahoma, Primary health care, Quality improvement

Introduction

Oklahoma is home to 38 federally recognized American Indian (AI) Tribal Nations. (Note: American Indian and Alaska Native is the racial term used in federal data reporting. Other terms that may be used to refer to the pre-colonial inhabitants of the US include “Native American” and “Indigenous”. Few Alaska Native persons live in Oklahoma.) In 2021, the state’s AI population was nearly 634,000 (16.0%) [1]. Many AI persons live in rural, economically disadvantaged, and medically underserved areas [2]. The overall age-adjusted cancer incidence rate among AI is 39% higher and the cancer mortality rate is 49% higher than the corresponding Oklahoma all races rates [3]. The large burden of cancer within the AI population of Oklahoma has led to an increasing number of cancer survivors who require improved healthcare delivery.

Cancer survivorship care, which typically focuses on the period of time after the completion of active cancer treatment, is critical for identifying and addressing recurrence [4], addressing late and adverse effects of cancer treatment [4, 5], and treating a range of physical, social, and psychological factors that cause patient distress [6–8]. Despite this growing need, survivorship care is often inadequately delivered. Inadequate survivorship care leads to increased costs, and potentially avoidable morbidity and mortality [4, 9, 10].

Although the frequency of visits to oncology providers (ONCs) is associated positively with receipt of recommended follow-up testing for cancer survivors [9], the majority of cancer survivors do not see oncologists regularly [10], and less than one third of cancer survivors see oncologists by five years after diagnosis [11]. While best practice guidelines for follow up care consist of routine patient monitoring through frequent appointments, optimal care is largely dependent on type of cancer, treatment received, and other patient-specific factors [12]. Thus, the long-term quality of cancer survivorship care may depend on how well oncology and primary care practices communicate with each other and with their mutual patients, and how well they coordinate clinical services by employing a bi-directional flow of information between practices [13, 14]. Moreover, our survey of primary care providers and oncology providers in Oklahoma revealed that primary care providers would prefer to have a model of cancer survivorship care that involves shared care between oncology and primary care providers [15].

Many AI persons in Oklahoma reside in rural locations, and rural residence location introduces additional complexities into the delivery of cancer survivorship care [16–18]. Travel time to oncology practices [19–21], lack of specialized pathology and radiologic services needed to detect recurrence [22], are among the barriers faced by rural populations. The decline in the frequency of visits to oncology providers for survivorship care is especially steep for patients living in rural areas [11]. Receipt of care in Indian Health Service direct care, Tribal, or Urban Indian healthcare (ITU) facilities that receive core funding through the Indian Health Service (IHS) can complicate survivorship care further. AI patients who need specialty care services, such as cancer-related care, often rely on the Purchased and Referred Care (PRC) program of the Indian Health Service (IHS) to subsidize referrals to non-IHS facilities [23]. The annual budget for the PRC program is often depleted prior to the end of the fiscal year, so the IHS must ration care by prioritizing services for urgent issues, and other requests are denied frequently [24]. When specialty services are inaccessible through the IHS, AI patients may seek to cover costs through health insurance coverage. However, many AI patients remain uninsured, so rely on the PRC program. Given the challenges to the receipt of high-quality cancer survivorship care faced by rural-dwelling AI persons who receive care in ITU clinic facilities, interventions that enhance oncology-primary care communication and care coordination may be required [25].

The pilot intervention described here capitalizes on the fact that most cancer survivors continue to be seen in primary care even as they gravitate away from oncology. The study featured a primary care-based nurse, termed the “Nurse Cancer Care Coordinator” (Nurse CCC), who was trained in cancer survivorship care navigation. The Nurse CCC was tasked with collecting and organizing cancer survivorship care data for AI patients attending a rural, tribal clinic. The Nurse CCC reached out to oncology practices and patients to complete a survivorship care plan (SCP) that was specifically modified for use in the primary care setting. The Nurse CCC also had patients complete the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Distress Thermometer [26] to augment the information gathered in the SCP. Findings from this initial work are reported in this manuscript and are intended to help inform future development of more efficacious approaches to addressing cancer survivorship care in the primary care setting.

Methods

Setting

The Choctaw Nation of Oklahoma is the third largest federally recognized tribe by population in the United States and spans nearly 11,000 square miles in predominately rural, southeastern Oklahoma [27]. While the McGirt v. Oklahoma Supreme Court Decision has strengthened the ability of the Choctaw Nation to revert to its original reservation boundaries [27], tribal program and service eligibility during the timeframe of the present study was determined by a longstanding 10 and a half county tribal jurisdictional area. Five of these counties are federally defined persistent poverty ones [28]. Self-governance tribes, including the Choctaw Nation, are allocated annual federal funding through the IHS to operate and control all health programs, services, functions, and activities [29]. Choctaw Nation was the first federally recognized tribe in the US to independently purchase and build a hospital, and it provides healthcare to over 100,000 AI individuals through eight primary care (PC) facilities of the Choctaw Nation Health Services Authority (CNHSA). Most oncology services are not available directly through CNHSA and require a referral through Choctaw PRC to community-based oncology facilities. The University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center research team has a long history of collaborating with CNHSA. This existing relationship and partnership promoted successful collaboration throughout the study.

Screening and recruitment

Potential pilot study participants were identified by CNHSA’s health information technology department, which is part of the electronic health record system’s team. Participants were identified through a retrospective medical record review in the family medicine department at two CNHSA health facilities. Participants were deemed eligible if they were AI adults over the age of 18 years who had been diagnosed with cancer (ICD-10 C00-C97), excluding non-melanoma skin cancer[C44]), and had completed the acute phase of cancer treatment within the preceding five-year period. A total of 34 potential participants met these criteria. Of these, a total of 28 participants were recruited by the CNHSA “Nurse Cancer Care Coordinator”, who is a registered nurse with experience in the provision of case management. A total of 23 participants were consented and enrolled in this pilot study.Prior to to data collection, participants signed an informed consent describing all study components.

Training, data collection, and intervention procedures

Prior to recruitment, the CNHSA Nurse CCC completed the Collaborative Institution Training Initiative (CITI) course for Human Subjects Research, consisting of 16 modules related to research guidelines, ethics, and federal regulations for persons involved in Human Research Programs. In addition, this individual completed the free, online courses, Cancer Survivorship Training for Healthcare Professionals and George Washington Cancer Center’s Cancer Survivorship E-Learning Series for Primary Care Providers, modules 1–10 [30]. These required approximately ten hours of time to complete.

The CNHSA Nurse CCC maintained a list of consented participants and relied upon a number of sources to develop a primary care-oriented survivorship care plan (SCP) for each of the participants derived from the existing American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) SCP [31]. Sources to complete the primary care-oriented SCP included the CNHSA electronic health record, health records sent from the participating patient’s oncology practices, direct discussion with staff at oncology facilities, and information from participating patients themselves. To augment the information provided in the SCP, the Nurse CCC contacted participating patients to review the SCP and complete a baseline NCCN Distress Thermometer, which includes an overall self-reported distress score and a checklist of concerns or problems the patient is facing [26].

Based on the SCP and patient-reported challenges identified using the Distress Thermometer, the Nurse CCC communicated patient needs to their provider prior to the appointment. After receiving the provider’s permission, the Nurse CCC initiated internal consultations and referrals to outside specialists, and orders needed for PCP visits. Also, the Nurse CCC occasionally assisted patients in finding additional resources related to identified needs, such as referring participants to programs that assist with transportation costs for appointments and connecting them with services that help individuals obtain health insurance coverage through Medicaid. The Nurse CCC created notes in the EHR documenting the patient’s needs that were identified.

Measures

Participant demographics

The CNHS EHR was accessed to extract participant age in years, gender (male/female), and health insurance type (coverage through private insurance, Medicaid, Medicare, any combination of private, Medicaid or Medicare insurance, or no coverage).

Survivorship care plan

The SCP used was based on the ASCO Survivorship Care Plan Templates (updated 09/27/13) [31], but for the purposes of this pilot study modified for use in the primary care setting (Appendix 1). Modifications placed a greater emphasis on monitoring for adverse effects of cancer treatment and assessing the need for follow-up care. Measures related to cancer treatment and survivorship were collected systematically by the Nurse CCC and entered into the SCP. This information, primarily collected from the patient’s referred oncology practice, was categorized as General Information, Treatment Summary, and Follow-Up Care Plan. General Information included patient-related information, such as health care provider team members and patient demographics. The Treatment Summary consisted of information related to cancer diagnosis, treatment(s), and familial cancer risk assessment. The Follow-up Care Plan included the schedule of planned follow-up visits with both the patient’s oncology and primary care teams, ongoing cancer surveillance strategies, and a brief lifestyle/behavior risk assessment. To help characterize completion challenges, the length of time and frequency of attempts to complete each section were collected. The Nurse CCC documented frequency of attempts each time contact was made in an effort to collect information.

Distress thermometer and problem list

The Nurse CCC met with participants to complete the NCCN Distress Thermometer and Problem List for Patients Version 2.2020 [26], which measures the degree of distress experienced within the past week on a scale from 0 (no distress) to 10 (extreme distress). Additionally, this tool is used to inventory common concerns and sources of distress commonly faced by patients with cancer. This problems list provides context for the reported Distress Thermometer score and includes five categories of concerns: physical, emotional, social, practical (e.g., including financial concerns), and spiritual/religious. Participants reported a Distress Thermometer score and identified problems at baseline and repeated at a follow-up visit, targeted for 30 days from baseline.

Process measures

Documentation of the sources of information needed to complete the SCP, Distress Thermometer content, number of days between baseline and follow-up, and length of time required to compile and organize this information was tracked using a standardized form. This information was then transmitted securely to the analytic team upon completion.

Analyses

Measures characterizing categorical data are summarized by tabulating frequency counts. For continuous data, data are reported as means and ranges. This small feasibility study was not powered to perform inferential statistics.

Discussion

This pilot study was designed as an initial step in developing a proactive, primary care-based intervention led by a Nurse CCC to improve the delivery of cancer survivorship care for rural-dwelling AI patients seen in Tribal Health Services (THS) settings. Several major components were explored. First, a Nurse CCC was trained to serve as the focal point of organizing survivorship care for AI patients seen in THS settings. Second, the Nurse CCC was tasked with collecting and organizing information on the cancer survivorship needs of patients by completing a primary care modified SCP. Third, the Nurse CCC met with patients, predominately by telehealth visits, to review and finalize the SCPs and to complete the NCCN Distress Thermometer. When warranted, these meetings also focused on scheduling tests and visits and connecting participants with case management/financial services at CNHSA. The Nurse CCC was also tasked with helping schedule tests and visits and outside referrals to address needs identified through the SCP, the Distress Thermometer or both. The reduction in Distress Thermometer scores from baseline to follow-up may be attributed to a number of factors, including the identification of cancer-related challenges and subsequent referrals to alleviate patient burden.

At baseline, we found that very little clinical information from oncology groups had been transmitted to the Tribal clinic. None of the 23 participants had received SCPs from their oncologists. Moreover, the tribal clinic electronic health record contained limited information about the survivorship care needs of participants. Sometimes a scanned oncology clinic note was present, but in many cases no information from the oncology practice was found.

The rationale for training and inserting a Nurse CCC into primary care was based, in part, on the growing evidence base for navigation in cancer survivorship indicating improved quality of life [32, 33], and higher rates of attendance to medical appointments [34, 35]. We focused on nurse-led CCC as opposed to lay navigation because collecting and organizing complex cancer treatment and surveillance data and communicating this information to primary care providers and to patients requires clinical expertise. Also, the THS system currently supports nurse-based case management services for diabetes care, so piloting Nurse CCC was viewed as being feasible by the participating tribal clinic.

Completing the SCP was a collaborative and complex process. The growing use of EHRs offers an opportunity for primary care teams and oncology providers to access each other’s clinical notes directly. This access could support primary care teams in addressing patients’ needs related to survivorship care and distress. However, the effectiveness of EHRs for this purpose depends on the consistent completion of SCPs and distress assessments by oncologists, which was a practice that was not observed routinely in our study. Additionally, secure messaging within the EHR could reduce reliance on phone calls for communication between primary care and specialty teams. Nonetheless, the potential of EHRs to enhance communication and care coordination across different healthcare systems remains limited, particularly when incompatible EHR platforms are involved. As is the case in many primary care settings, patients with cancer receive treatment in a variety of community oncology settings. In our case, 15 different oncologists needed to be contacted. Challenges included difficulty in identifying and connecting with contact persons at each of the oncology sites, adequately obtaining information from oncology sites to complete survivorship care plans even when a contact person was identified, and locating information within the electronic health record that could be used to inform the SCP. Information collected in the general information section of the SCP was frequently accessible through the electronic health record, whereas the information needed to complete the treatment and follow-up care plan sections required collaboration with each patient’s oncology practice. Patients often lacked awareness of their follow-up care plans, and records from oncology practices, when available, frequently did not include this information. Attempts to clarify plans by phone were difficult, and even when someone at the oncology practice was reached, they were not always familiar with the follow-up details. These findings suggest that, to reduce confusion among patients and primary care teams, oncology practices should clearly communicate follow-up care plans, even if a full SCP is not completed. These findings illustrate the struggle primary care practices face in getting basic information describing the ongoing care needs of their patients who have been treated for cancer.

While completion of the SCP added critical information to the primary care record about potential adverse effects of cancer treatment and need for ongoing surveillance, several studies show that SCPs alone do not improve clinical outcomes or quality of life [36–38]. The completion of the NCCN Distress Thermometer added a proactive aspect to the Nurse CCC role. Roughly half of the participants reported fatigue, pain, or worry, and about one third reported tingling in the hands or feet and difficulty with sleep. These types of symptoms and concerns, which may at least in part reflect long-term effects of cancer treatment, can be managed effectively in the primary care setting. Our findings suggest that further research is needed to examine the role of the Nurse CCC in identifying and helping cancer survivors proactively address symptoms and related issues, such as financial toxicity and quality of life, in the context of primary care.

One approach might be to risk-stratify patients into survivorship care pathways based on the complexity of their ongoing needs and the types of providers their personalized pathway requires [39–42], including Nurse CCCs when warranted. Also, research is needed to determine which specific components of proactive survivorship care improve outcomes. Patients in one recent study were satisfied with multicomponent intervention that included a personalized SCP, face-to-face survivorship education seminars and personalized consultation for supportive care referrals (“Transition Day”), and a mobile app delivering personalized education and self-management advice [43]. However, outcomes other than patient satisfaction were not reported and uptake of each component was variable. Also, research is needed to help determine whether patients who are closer to the active phase of cancer treatment might benefit more from Nurse CCC interventions than those who are farther out from treatment, as many experts are calling for the initiation of survivorship care closer to the time of cancer diagnosis [44–46].This could facilitate the early recognition of treatment-related adverse effects and distress, and lead to interventions to address them.

Limitations

This pilot intervention was designed to begin defining the role of the Nurse CCC embedded within a rural, tribally operate primary care practice serving AI patients. The small sample size limited the ability to conduct statistical analyses but did allow us to gain an appreciation of the challenges primary care clinics face in collecting and organizing data generated by oncologists. Also, this study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic. Staffing limitations and scheduling restrictions imposed by the pandemic caused us to jettison the notion of a planned primary care visit once the Nurse CCC had synthesized information on participants’ survivorship care needs. Instead, the Nurse CCC herself worked with clinic staff to place referrals related to SCP monitoring recommendations and Distress Thermometer findings. Upon reflection, this might have been a more efficient process than scheduling a planned care visit. Although we preferentially recruited participants who had recently completed active cancer treatment, many participants were several years out from active treatment. These longer-term cancer survivors may experience fewer or different challenges than those who have recently completed treatment. As noted above, future studies could employ risk stratification to determine which cancer survivors might benefit most from active Nurse CCC involvement.

Conclusions

Both oncologists and primary care providers have vital roles in providing proactive health care for patients who have been treated for cancer. We reported previously that PCPs indicate an interest in a shared-care approach to survivorship care. This is important as many patients stop seeing oncologists after active cancer treatment has been completed. The current report shows that a Nurse CCC embedded within a primary care setting can collect and organize clinically relevant information on patients with cancer, but this is a labor-intensive undertaking. Future work should concentrate on refining the role of the Nurse CCC, determining if risk stratification could be used to identify patients in which Nurse CCC is most needed, determining if Nurse CCC involvement should occur closer to the time of diagnosis, and working with oncology groups to improve the flow of clinically meaningful information into primary care. Poor patient outcomes that could be reversed through improved survivorship care are likely to persist unless improvements in PCP-ONC care coordination are made.
