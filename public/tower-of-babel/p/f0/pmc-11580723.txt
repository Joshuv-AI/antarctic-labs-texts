Motor, mood, and memory impairments persist during remission periods in chronic colitis and are influenced by neuroinflammation and sex
Authors: Gema Sotelo‐Parrilla, Alejandro Ruiz‐Calero, Pablo García‐Miranda, María L. Calonge, María D. Vázquez‐Carretero, María J. Peral
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractUlcerative colitis is a chronic pathology characterized by relapsing–remitting phases of intestinal inflammation. Additionally, some patients develop neuropsychiatric disorders, such as depression and anxiety, or cognitive deficits. We aimed to investigate whether the development of chronic colitis elicits memory, locomotion, and mood impairments. It further examined whether these impairments are influenced by the relapsing–remitting phases of the colitis or by sex. Here, we used a chronic colitis model in male and female rats, induced with sodium dextran sulfate, mirroring the phases of human ulcerative colitis. Our results revealed that the severity of colitis was slightly higher in males than females. Chronic colitis triggered motor and short‐term memory deficits and induced anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors that remained throughout the development of the disease. There are also sex differences under control or inflammatory conditions. Therefore, in both situations, females compared to males displayed: (i) slightly lower locomotion, (ii) increased anxiety‐like behaviors, (iii) similar depression‐like behaviors, and (iv) similar short‐term memory deficit. Additionally, under control conditions, the mRNA levels of IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α were higher in the female hippocampus. In both sexes, when chronic colitis was established, the neuroinflammation was evidenced by increased mRNA levels of these three cytokines in the hippocampus and in the motor and prefrontal cortices. Interestingly, this neuroinflammation was slightly greater in males. In summary, we show that the development of chronic colitis caused persistent behavioral abnormalities, highlighting sex differences, and that could be a consequence, at least in part, of the increase in IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α in the brain.

INTRODUCTION

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is one of two inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) characterized by chronic inflammation of the intestine with relapsing–remitting phases, and severe symptoms including diarrhea, rectal bleeding, and weight loss. 1 Its pathogenesis is complex and unclear, and factors such as age, sex, genetics, and immunity affect its evolution. 1 , 2 , 3 In addition, neuropsychiatric disorders, such as depression and anxiety, are more prevalent in UC patients than in the general population. 3 , 4 These patients declare reduced emotional well‐being, cognitive dysfunctions, and mood disturbances. 5 These symptoms are associated with poor quality of life of UC patients, especially during acute episodes. 4 , 6

Mood alterations, such as depression or anxiety‐like behaviors, and memory deficits, were observed in UC animal models. 7 However, in these models, results on the effects of colitis on locomotion are controversial. Several authors reported a reduced motor activity, 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 whereas others did not find effects. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Most studies on behaviors used an acute model of colitis and performed the tests at a time point of the treatment despite being human UC a chronic progressive condition with differences between the active and resolution phases. 4 , 20

Sex differences have also been found in the frequency and clinical course of mood disorders in UC patients, with women with UC being more likely than men to develop anxiety and depression. 3 , 4 However, the results of sex differences were not clear when an animal model of experimental colitis was used. In these models, for example, Painsipp et al. 13 found that the female mice suffer more depression‐like behaviors and the males more anxiety, other authors, even though using female and male rodents in their studies, did not make a sex differentiation on behavior, 21 , 22 and others did not find differences between sexes. 9 , 16 , 18 Regarding the motor behaviors, there are scarcely any reports examining whether, in the colitis models, the sex affects these behaviors. Consequently, these limited studies indicated a comparable reduction in locomotion across both sexes, with no discernible effect in either sex. 9 , 13 , 16 , 18 , 22

Gut–brain axis could be the route that leads to these impairments in behaviors as has been suggested. 7 , 20 , 23 The peripheral inflammation caused by chronic colitis can elicit a sustained neuroinflammation over time, which is considered an important inducer for the development of neurodegenerative diseases. 24 This inflammatory environment in the brain is possibly produced by the activated microglia and astrocytes that release pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin‐1 beta (IL‐1β), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α). 25 These proinflammatory cytokines may modify neuronal functions resulting in behavioral abnormalities. Although neuroinflammation has been observed in various brain regions of animals with colitis and is considered a plausible responsible for behavioral impairments, 7 , 26 there is limited direct evidence supporting this link. Previous studies, including one of ours, have indirectly associated increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines with behavioral alterations. 16 , 22 , 27

We previously reported that acute colitis in male rats triggered neuroinflammation and increased excitability of the motor cortex and impaired motor activity and coordination. 27 In this work, we used a DSS‐based model of chronic colitis in rats, considered a well‐established animal model for studying UC. Moreover, we can take advantage of investigating the relapsing–remitting phases of UC and more specifically the changes with the activity of the disease, since in humans it would require an exhaustive follow‐up of the patient who goes through these periods. 28 We asked whether, in male and female rats, in these phases throughout the development of chronic colitis: (i) motor activity, mood, and memory are altered, (ii) neuroinflammation occurs in the motor cortex, hippocampus, and/or prefrontal cortex, and, (iii) these effects are different depending on the sex of the animal.

So far, no studies have dealt with the evolution of such motor activity, mood, and memory impairments that occur throughout the development of chronic colitis considering relapsing–remitting phases and sex differences.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male and female Wistar rats (45 day‐old) were used. This study included 16–30 adult rats per experimental group, evenly divided into 8–15 males and 8–15 females. They were humanely handled, and efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and their suffering. The procedures concerning the protection of experimental animals were following the guidelines of the European Union Council (Directive 2010/63/UE) and the Spanish Royal Decree (BOE 34/11370, 2013). The research protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Seville and Junta de Andalucía (Number of approval project: CEEA‐US2021‐14, 21/03/2022/051). The rats were obtained from the “Centro de experimentación animal de la Universidad de Sevilla”. Animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (50 mg/kg) plus xylazine (10 mg/kg) and sacrificed.

Biological preparations

Once the rats were sacrificed, the colons were removed and washed with ice‐cold saline solution and their weights and lengths measured. Segments of the distal colon were either fixed by overnight incubation with phosphate buffer saline (PBS; in mM, 137 NaCl, 2.7 KCl,10 Na2HPO4, and 1.8 KH2PO4; pH 7.4) containing 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for histological analysis or frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until its use in RNA extraction to perform PCR‐measurements. The hippocampus, motor, and prefrontal cortices were also removed, frozen, and stored at −80°C until their use for PCR measurements.

Induction and assessment of experimental chronic colitis

Rats with similar body weight values were randomized into untreated or water groups (control) and sodium dextran sulfate (DSS)‐treated groups. The control rats' groups drank normal water. Chronic colitis was induced by administering three cycles of 4% DSS (MW 40 kDa; PanReac AppliChem, Spain) for 7 days in drinking water. 28 Between DSS cycles, rats received normal tap water for 7 days, except for the third DSS cycle where they only received water for 2 days (Figure 1A). This treatment mimics the relapsing–remitting periods of inflammation as those of ulcerative colitis in humans. Throughout the DSS treatment, von Frey filaments were used to assess nociceptive responses by applying the stimulus in the abdominal area in both DSS‐treated and untreated female and male rats.

Induction and progression of the colon inflammation were evaluated by determining clinical manifestations using the disease activity index (DAI) and colon parameters, such as weight/length ratio, histological score, and mRNA relative expression of colonic proinflammatory cytokines, as previously described. 29 For DAI evaluation, every 2–3 days, throughout the DSS treatment, the animals were monitored for body weight gain, stool consistency, and the presence of blood in feces. For histological scoring, at the end of each DSS or water period and in parallel with control groups, rats were sacrificed, and paraffin‐embedded sections of the removed distal colon were stained with hematoxylin/eosin. Colon injury was graded by evaluating the destruction of epithelium, the dilatation of crypts, the loss of goblet cells, the inflammatory cell infiltrate, edema, and crypt abscesses. For DAI or histological assessment, each of the items was scored on a scale of 0 to 3 and added together to obtain a cumulative score.

Relative quantification of real‐time PCR

Real‐time PCR was carried out as previously described. 29 Briefly, total RNA was extracted from the distal colon, hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, and primary motor cortex of control and DSS‐treated rats using RNeasy® kit (Qiagen). cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using QuantiTect® reverse transcription kit (Qiagen) as described by the manufacturer. The primers used are given in Table S1. Real‐time PCR was performed with NZYSupreme qPCR Green Master Mix® (MB41903; Nzytec). β‐Actin served as a reference gene and was used for sample normalization. The comparative Ct method was applied to determine the mRNA relative expression and the lowest value measured of each gene was set at 1. Results are also presented as fold change for each cytokine. Fold changes are calculated by dividing the relative expression of each gene in rats with DSS treatment by that in rats with normal water (control). All these determinations were performed in samples taken on day 37 of DSS treatment.

Behavioral tests

The behavioral tests conducted in this study were the open field test and the novel object recognition test. They were first administered on day 0 (pretest) to all animals before randomizing them in water (control) and DSS groups. Then, as indicated in Figure 1A, the tests were performed on days: 9, 16, 23, 30, and 37 of DSS treatment, which is 1–2 days after each period, in the case of DSS to avoid coinciding with the sudden changes in the peaks of inflammation. Control groups are evaluated in parallel.

Open field test

This test was used to assess the motor activity and the state of mind of rats, either untreated or DSS treated. It was conducted in a normally illuminated room, where the animals were acclimated for 30 min. The open field was a white plastic opaque cage measuring 50 cm long × 35 cm wide × 35 cm high. The cage floor was divided into 15 squares and placed in a sound‐free room. A video camera was mounted above the open field to record the movements of each animal. The rat was placed in the center of the cage and left to explore freely for 5 min, and all its movements were recorded. The cage was cleaned with 70% ethanol and dried after each rat session to remove olfactory cues.

The following parameters were manually counted: percentage of time spent in the center area, percentage of immobility time, number of crossings, number of rearings, and number of groomings. Furthermore, DeepLabCut software was used to measure automatically: the mean speed and distance traveled in the whole and in the center of the cage, the number of transitions from the periphery to the center of the cage, total time spent in the corners, and immobility time spent in the corners, following the methodology previously described 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 with some modifications.

Novel object recognition test

This test was used to assess short‐term memory and is based on the rat's attraction to novelty and the unknown. Before experimentation, rats were acclimated to the room for 30 min in the home cage. The task was conducted in the same opaque white plastic cage used in the open‐field test after allowing the rats to explore freely for 5 min. The task consisted of three phases: training, resting, and testing. The movements performed by the rats during the training and the testing phases were recorded on video. During the training phase, the rat was placed in the center of the cage and allowed to freely explore for 10 min two identical objects placed on the opposite sides of the cage. The total time that the animal explores the two identical objects in the training phase (expressed as a percentage of the total time) was also measured. Subsequently, during the resting phase, the rat was placed in the home cage for 1 h. For the testing phase, one of the objects was replaced with a novel one, and then the rat was placed back in the cage and allowed to freely explore for 10 min. The total time that each rat investigated each object (nose facing the object within a maximum distance of 2 cm) was analyzed and used to calculate the discrimination index. This index assesses the preference for the novel object and was calculated by dividing the total time exploring the new object by the total time exploring both objects and multiplying by 100. A value of 50% or more indicates that the rat prefers the novel object which, in turn, points out that the rat remembers and recognizes the familiar one. All objects were appropriately cleaned using 70% ethanol to remove odor cues between trials. Objects designated as familiar or novel were randomized among behavioral trials. To ensure that all the objects used were equally attractive to the rats, a pretest for saliency was performed with a different group of rats with no previous experience with the objects. The saliency test was conducted using the same objects that would be used later for the novel object recognition test and the value obtained for the discrimination index was 50 ± 2 (mean ± SEM), n = 30, indicating that the rats did not prefer any of the objects over another.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed separately for male rats (n = 8–15) and female rats (n = 8–15) to investigate sex‐specific effects. In addition, combined analyses were performed with data pooled from both sexes (n = 16–30). The n was reported in the figure legends. Comparisons between the experimental groups were evaluated with the ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test using the GraphPad Prism software version 8.0. The differences were set to be significant at p < .05. The Pearson correlations were performed using the R 4.3.2 version (RStudio Team, 2023, Boston, MA, USA), and the graphics were built using the ggplot2 package version 3. 5.0. 34

RESULTS

The development of DSS‐induced colitis presents sex differences once the chronic colitis is established

We start the study by evaluating clinical manifestations and histology of the colon to assess DSS‐induced colitis severity along with the development of the disease in male and female rats. For that we use the disease activity index (DAI) and score the colon injury using the parameters previously described in Methods section. The DSS model used here (Figure 1A) resembles the development of ulcerative colitis, since it provides an alternation between relapsing–remitting phases similar to those currently occurring in humans. All rats exposed to the DSS cycles developed these characteristic phases of chronic colitis. Figure 1B shows increased DAI values in both male and female rats compared to control rats, peaking on days 7, 21 and 35, whereas remission values are observed on days 16 and 30. The DAI was higher in males than in females only at the end of DSS‐treatment when chronic colitis is established. Figure 1C reveals that the colon weight/length ratio on day 37 was higher in DSS‐treated rats indicating colonic inflammation in those animals. The results also showed that this relationship was greater in males compared to females, again indicating higher severity in males at this point of treatment. Histological analysis performed along DSS treatment (Figure 2A–C) shows that colon injury, modified with the pace of the development of colitis, was significantly higher on day 9 followed by days 23 and 37 and lower on days 16 and 30. The score indicating the loss of crypt architecture and the infiltration of inflammatory cells in the mucosa and submucosa (Figure 2C) throughout the development of colitis was similar in females and males, and although not statistically significant the damage was slightly greater in males on day 37.

Moreover, we examined the mRNA relative abundance of colon proinflammatory cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α on day 37 of DSS treatment, when chronic colitis is established. Figure 3A shows that the mRNA abundance of the three cytokines increased in the colon of DSS‐treated rats with data from females and males combined. Beside these changes, we found sex differences since IL‐1β and TNF‐α increased in the colon of both sexes but IL‐6 only in DSS‐treated males, compared to untreated rats (Figure 3B). In addition, the observed increase in IL‐1β and IL‐6, due to DSS treatment, was higher in males as compared to females, but that of TNF‐α was similar in both sexes. We did not find sex differences in the mRNA abundances of the colonic proinflammatory cytokines under control conditions (Figure 3B).

These results indicate that while chronic colitis develops similarly in both sexes males experienced slightly more severe chronic colitis.

Motor activity is reduced throughout the development of chronic colitis and partially affected by sex

Motor activity analysis of the animals was carried out using the open field test. The results are summarized in Figure 4. First, we observed that under control conditions, none of the parameters evaluated in either females or males showed differences when comparing day zero with other time points, indicating that motor behavior is not affected, whether analyzing data from both sexes combined or separated by sex (Figure 4A,B). The development of chronic colitis in DSS‐treated rats (combined data) led to a general diminution of locomotion, displaying a lower number of rearings and crossings, indicating a reduction in vertical and horizontal motor activity respectively, and a decreased traveled distance and average speed (Figure 4A). These motor deficits were evident from day 9 and continued throughout the development of chronic colitis, there was no correspondence with the remitting–relapsing phases of the pathology observed in the DAI (Figure 1B) and in the colon histology (Figure 2). Furthermore, the differences between each time point and day zero were significant in all parameters indicating that DSS‐treated rats do not return to normal locomotion at any time during the development of colitis. These colitis‐induced motor deficits were also observed in both males and females when data were analyzed separately by sex (Figure 4B).

Sex differences were found in the average speed on day 9 of DSS treatment showing that females moved slower than males. In addition, there was a sex effect on the traveled distance in both the water and the DSS groups. Female rats traveled shorter distances compared to males under control conditions (water group) at all the time points measured, and under inflammatory conditions on days 9, 23, 30 and 37 of DSS treatment. We also calculated the differences in traveled distance between males and females under both control and DSS conditions (Table 1). Comparison of these values indicated that the effect of colitis on that parameter was similar in both sexes, and therefore the observed differences between females and males were inherent to sex. Representative images of the movement tracks from the rats on day 37 (Figure 4C) show this sex effect on the traveled distance.

Anxiety‐like behaviors are induced throughout the development of chronic colitis and differ by sex

Next, we wondered whether the rats' mood‐related behaviors throughout the development of chronic colitis were modified and whether these alterations, if any, were different due to sex. For this purpose, we used the open‐field test and analyzed the parameters linked to anxiety. As in motor activity, in none of the evaluated parameters there were differences between day zero compared to other time points of water (control) groups. We found anxiety‐like behaviors induced by the development of chronic colitis at all the time points measured (combined data). DSS‐induced colitis led to a reduction in the following parameters: (i) the time spent in the center of the cage, (ii) the number of transitions to the central area, and (iii) the locomotion in the center (traveled distance and average speed); and an increase in both, the total time spent and the time immobile in the corners (Figure 5A,C). These modifications were maintained along DSS treatment, without changes associated with the remitting–relapsing phases of the pathology and persisted when the DAI almost returned to normal in the remission periods. Comparisons of each time point with day zero were significant in all parameters revealing that the rats with colitis do not return to normal behavior at any time during the development of chronic colitis.

These colitis‐induced anxiety‐like behaviors were also observed in both males and females when data were analyzed separately by sex (Figure 5B,C). Sex differences related to the parameters measured at the center of the cage were also found, such as the time spent, the number of transitions, and the traveled distance (Figure 5B). Therefore, females exhibited lower values, including those from day zero, than those of males under control and inflammatory conditions in most of the time points measured. Differences between sexes in the parameters related to corners were also observed. However, in this case, most sex‐related effects were observed in the still time in the corners instead of in the total time spent over there (Figure 5C). Thereby both control and DSS‐treated females remained more time immobile at the corners than males at all the time points measured. We then calculated the differences between males and females under both control and DSS conditions for those parameters where the sexes differed in the water group (Table 1). Comparison of these values indicated that the effect of colitis on those parameters was similar in both sexes, and therefore, the observed differences between females and males were inherent to sex. Representative activity tracking from the rats on day 37 shows these effects described so far (Figure 4C).

To sum up this section, the development of chronic colitis triggered anxiety‐like behaviors, with females exhibiting a more anxious profile than males under both control and inflammatory conditions.

Depression‐related behaviors are induced throughout the development of chronic colitis but without sex differences

Next, we evaluated the effect of DSS‐induced chronic colitis on two parameters related to depressive‐like behaviors using again the open‐field test: the number of groomings and the immobility time, understood as freezing time or the time that rats are completely quiet. First, the results showed that in the control groups none of the measured parameters exhibited differences between day zero and the other time points (Figure 6A,B). DSS treatment increased immobility time and affected self‐care behavior, as reflected in a reduced number of groomings, whether the data were combined or analyzed separately by sex (Figure 6A,B). These behavioral abnormalities began on day 9 of DSS treatment and were maintained throughout the development of chronic colitis. Again, compared to each time point with day zero, differences were significant in both parameters, revealing that at any time the rats with colitis return to normal behavior. No differences between sexes have been found under control or inflammatory conditions.

Short‐term memory is impaired throughout the development of chronic colitis but without sex differences

To also evaluate whether chronic colitis alters cognitive functions, we assessed short‐term memory using the novel object recognition test in DSS‐treated and untreated rats, and in both females and males. First, we analyzed the total time that rats spent exploring the two identical objects and then, after changing one of the objects for a new one, we determined the discrimination index. Data are shown combined (Figure 7A) and separated by sex (Figure 7B). The results revealed that the chronic colitis did not modify the total time of exploration, indicating that the rats did not lose their curiosity for exploration, an instinctive activity of animals in the face of novelty. In addition, we observed that the discrimination index did not change in rats that drank water compared to day zero with the other time points, indicating that the short‐term memory does not change under control conditions. A different effect was observed in DSS‐treated rats displaying a decrease in the discrimination index throughout the entire treatment (Figure 7A), indicating that the impairment in short‐term memory stayed at the same level without recovery in the remission periods of the colitis. Significant differences in this index as compared each time point with the day zero confirmed this effect. On the other hand, we did not find sex differences; however, the observed changes in both sexes have different tendencies (Figure 7B). The reduction in the discrimination index in the females was more pronounced on days 9 (relapsing phase) and 16 (remitting phase) and then increased slightly along the development of colitis indicating an improvement in the memory, whereas in the males the values decreased from day 9 and remained that way throughout the treatment. Representative images of the heat‐map plots from the rats on day 37 are shown in Figure 7C.

Proinflammatory cytokines are increased in the brain by chronic colitis establishment and differ by sex

Next, we wondered whether mood, motor activity, and short‐term memory alterations observed in female and male rats with chronic colitis were associated with a neuroinflammation induced by the colitis in the brain regions involved in those tasks. For this purpose, we determined the mRNA abundance of the proinflammatory cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, by real‐time PCR, on day 37 in both sexes, in water (control) and DSS groups, when the chronic colitis is established, in hippocampus, motor cortex, and prefrontal cortex. The results revealed that chronic colitis increased the mRNA expression levels of the three cytokines in the three brain regions explored indicating neuroinflammation (Figure 8). In addition, we found some sex differences in the relative amount of the three cytokines. In control rats, mRNA abundance of the three cytokines, in the hippocampus and those of TNF‐α and IL‐1β in the prefrontal cortex, was higher in females than in males. However, in the motor cortex, we did not find differences between the sexes in any of the three cytokines analyzed (Figure 9A). If we pay attention to the cytokine relative abundances in DSS‐treated rats, we observe the same‐sex differences as previously mentioned under control conditions (Figure 9A). We did not observe sex differences in the motor cortex. It is worth mentioning that of the three brain regions analyzed, the hippocampus showed the highest levels of cytokine expression in females in both control and inflammatory conditions. Nevertheless, to find out the effect of chronic colitis according to sex, we calculated the expression levels of the three cytokines, for each sex, as a fold change between the DSS group and their own water group, and the results were different. A significantly greater increase of IL‐1β in the hippocampus and of IL‐6 in prefrontal and motor cortices were found in the males compared to the females (Figure 9B). Only TNF‐α was significantly higher in females than in males in the motor cortex.

The results indicated that chronic colitis triggered neuroinflammation with differing profiles between sexes. Females had higher baseline cytokine levels, but males reached greater neuroinflammation.

Finally, we wanted to know whether the behavior parameters correlate with the cytokine expression levels measured in the brain (Figure S1). We showed some graphics that represent the behavior parameters that are more affected, and the cytokines measured in the brain region associated with such behaviors. We only found two significant correlations in female DSS‐treated rats, a negative correlation between the traveled distance and the mRNA abundance of IL‐6 and a positive correlation between the immobility time and the expression levels of the same cytokine. These significant correlations indicate that in females the different IL‐6 expression levels in either prefrontal or motor cortices could be important to modulate the behaviors.

DISCUSSION

Many patients with ulcerative colitis (UC) exhibit psychiatric disorders, such as depression and anxiety, with a higher rate in women. 3 , 4 Some studies reported that these symptoms increase during active disease periods, 4 , 20 whereas others that persist during remission periods. 20 This report is the first to present mood, cognitive and motor impairments examined in the relapsing–remitting phases of chronic colitis, observing sex differences, and changes in the cytokines proinflammatory profile in the brain with the establishment of the chronic colitis.

Previous studies conducted by our research group and by others reported that rodents with DSS‐induced colitis exhibit anxiety‐ and depressive‐like behaviors, and cognitive and motor impairments that could be associated with neuroinflammation in different brain regions. 7 , 26 , 27 To our knowledge, none of them investigated the behavioral changes throughout the development of chronic colitis paying attention to the relapsing–remitting phases of the disease and, also, considering an effect of the sex. Furthermore, they did not examine whether the colitis‐induced neuroinflammation is modified by sex. We found that, throughout the development of DSS‐induced chronic colitis and regardless of changes in disease activity, female and male rats exhibit reduced motor activity, short‐term memory impairment and anxiety‐ and depression‐like behaviors. These alterations, at least in part, might be due to a neuroinflammation in the hippocampus and in motor and prefrontal cortices induced by chronic colitis. We also showed sex differences in behavior, such as the females displaying more anxiety than the males, and in colon inflammation and neuroinflammation profiles. During behavioral tests, DSS‐treated rats did not show signs of pain, such as abnormal gait or posture, or nociceptive responses indicating that the changes in the behaviors were not due to illness.

We used a DSS chronic colitis model in rats to study the behavioral comorbidities associated with UC because it is considered reliable and reproducible, and results in clinical manifestations, histological features of the colon and inflammation recurrent periods similar to those of UC patients. 28 First, we confirmed the development of chronic colitis with the DSS treatment in male and female rats exhibiting relapsing–remitting periods that mimic the phases of UC in humans. We found sex differences when chronic colitis is established. Therefore, our results indicated that the severity of chronic colitis was slightly higher in males, as we observed a higher DAI, colon weight/length ratio and colonic abundance of the proinflammatory cytokines IL‐1β and IL‐6 in males than in females. However, there were no sex differences in the colon histology or in the TNF‐α abundance. Some of these results agree with those shown by Bábíčková et al. 35 in acute colitis in which male mice had more diarrhea and greater colon shortening than the females. In human studies, the incidence of UC is equal in males and females until age 45 years, thereafter men have a higher incidence than women. 36

UC patients, especially women, are more likely to experience psychiatric disorders. 3 , 4 , 20 However, it is not clear whether these alterations continue during the remission periods. Therefore, we wondered whether, in a rat chronic colitis model, the behavioral alterations differ between sexes and persist throughout the pace of the disease. For this, behavior tests were conducted on the rats, either females or males, exposed to DSS treatment during the development of the disease.

The open field test revealed that the parameters associated with the locomotion decreased since the first week of DSS treatment and remained low throughout treatment, independently of the disease phase, either in male or female rats. These results indicate that chronic colitis triggers motor deficits, as we previously showed in male rats with acute colitis. 27 Here we also show the effect of sex, finding that females exhibited a shorter traveled distance than males, indicating a diminution in locomotion, surprisingly not only under inflammatory conditions but also under control conditions. In addition, we found equal differences in traveled distance between males and females under both conditions, thus providing that the effect of colitis was similar in both sexes, and therefore the lower values in this parameter observed in females were inherent to sex.

Our results contrasted with those of authors who did not find changes in locomotion in murine colitis models. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 Consistent with our results, in males, motor deficits in acute 8 , 11 or in chronic colitis 10 , 12 were reported. The literature on the effects of sex on motor activity is scarce. In agreement with our results, the female mice compared to males exhibited decreased locomotion under control conditions, 37 , 38 however, Scholl et al. 39 found that female rats exhibited greater motor activity than males. In models of acute colitis mice, both sexes, either displayed similar reduced locomotion, 9 or no effect was found. 13 , 16 , 18 , 22 In patients with UC, a reduction in muscle mass and strength, which might affect locomotion, has been shown to be associated with disease activity. 40 Furthermore, in women with UC, impaired muscle function was also observed. 41

We also analyzed the mood alterations of the rats, such as depression and anxiety‐like behaviors, with and without DSS treatment. Numerous works showed the effect of acute and/or chronic colitis in male rodents and some of them also in females, however, none showed the evolution of these behaviors in both sexes and along the development of chronic colitis. 7 In this study, we showed that as chronic colitis developed, both male and female rats were displaying anxiety‐like behaviors. These behaviors were evident at the beginning of DSS treatment and maintained throughout the development of the colitis during the remission periods, when there were hardly any symptoms, and the rats exhibited low levels of inflammation and practically returned to normal condition.

Our results agreed with previous studies conducted in male rodents showing anxiety‐like behaviors in colitis models, either acute 8 , 9 , 13 , 14 , 18 , 42 , 43 or chronic. 10 , 21 , 44 These anxiety‐like behaviors were also observed in the resolution phase of acute experimental colitis; 18 , 42 , 43 however, Burgueño et al. 45 did not find them in this phase. We also showed that females are more attracted than males to stay in the corners rather than the center exhibiting a more anxious profile than males under both, inflammation and control conditions, which was especially evident even from day zero and without normalizing. As with the traveled distance, we concluded that the effect of colitis on several anxiety‐related parameters was similar in both sexes, as the differences between males and females under both conditions were equal. Therefore, the lower values observed in these parameters in females appear to be inherent to sex. Other authors, under baseline conditions, found less anxiety‐like behaviors in females than in males 39 , 46 or no differences between sexes. 38 , 47 Under inflammatory conditions, the authors who considered the sex showed that the males developed more anxiety than females in an acute colitis model, 13 or similar effects in both. 9 , 18 Consistent with our findings, anxiety disorder is more common in women than in men 48 and also in women patients with UC. 3

Here we also showed that chronic colitis elicits a depression‐like behavior throughout its development in both sexes. Again, as in the locomotion and anxiety, the parameters that indicate depression‐related behaviors were observed at the beginning of the DSS treatment on day 9 and persisted as colitis progressed. The changes were in a similar magnitude in both, females and males, indicating an equal effect induced by the colitis in both sexes. Consistent with our findings, in rodent models of acute colitis in males, this effect continued during remission, 14 , 43 and differences between sexes were not found. 16 However, we disagreed with Painsip et al. 13 who found that females, but not males, developed an increased depression‐like behavior in an acute colitis model. Depression is observed in patients with UC 4 , 20 and, as anxiety, most studies concluded that women were more likely than men to develop this mood. 3

In the present work, we evaluated short‐term memory throughout the development of chronic colitis, in females and males, and found memory deficits since day 9 of DSS treatment that continued even in the remission periods. The degree of short‐term memory impairment in the DSS groups was similar in both sexes, although the profile was not statistically significant throughout the development of colitis, the way these alterations occur tended to be different depending on the sex. According to our results, Salvo et al. 18 did not observe sex differences in acute colitis. Other authors found memory impairments in both, male and female mice, although they did not notice sex differences, either in acute 42 or in chronic colitis. 21 Others reported these alterations only in males in both, acute 17 , 49 and chronic colitis. 50 In addition, our data also agree with the human studies that reported cognitive deficits, such as memory problems, in patients with IBD. 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55

As has been previously suggested, either in patients with IBD or in animal models of colitis, 7 , 26 , 56 we postulated that all these mood, motor activity and memory alterations could be a consequence of the neuroinflammation, triggered by the chronic colitis, in areas of the brain that are considered the main responsible for those behaviors. Our results revealed that chronic colitis triggered neuroinflammation with different profiles between sexes. Females exhibited higher baseline cytokine levels, but males were more vulnerable leading to greater neuroinflammation. We found, in both sexes, increased mRNA abundance of the proinflammatory cytokines IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α in the hippocampus, motor cortex and prefrontal cortex when chronic colitis was established, indicating neuroinflammation in those areas. Several studies conducted in murine colitis models reported similar results to ours; 7 however, none of them considered sex differences in the neuroinflammation induced by colitis, the present study being the first to address this topic. Under control conditions, we observed that the females exhibited higher hippocampal levels of all three cytokines compared to the males. Under inflammatory conditions (chronic colitis), to better show the sex effect, we considered the fold change (regarding their controls) of the cytokine expression, revealing higher increases of IL‐1β in the hippocampus and of IL‐6 in prefrontal and motor cortices from males, whereas TNF‐α was greater in the female motor cortex. Our analysis of correlations between behavior parameters and the expression of these three cytokines revealed only two correlations for IL‐6 in DSS‐treated females suggesting that a threshold, as a minimum increase in the cytokines, would be sufficient to trigger changes in behaviors. Previous studies indirectly linked increased brain proinflammatory cytokines induced by experimental colitis to behavior alterations. In acute colitis, Cluny et al. 22 observed elevated cortical IL‐1β in females, which was associated with anxiety. Similarly, Gadotti et al., 16 observed high IL‐1β in the male hippocampus which was related to depression. In addition, Mitchell et al. 50 observed memory decline and higher IL‐1β production induced by chronic colitis in male mice. Previous studies in our laboratory revealed a link between increases of IL‐1β and IL‐6 in the male motor cortex with motor impairments. 27 According to this postulate, Sohrabi et al. 19 after inducing moderate colitis in male mice, did not find changes in motor or anxiety‐like behaviors nor an increase in proinflammatory cytokines in the parietal cortices.

Notably, we observed that females exhibited higher levels of these three cytokines in the hippocampus compared to males, under both control and inflammatory conditions. Mitchell et al. 50 found, in male mice with chronic colitis, higher inflammatory response in the hippocampus than in other brain regions, suggesting that the hippocampus is more sensitive to inflammation. As the hippocampus is also involved in emotional regulation, such cytokine increase in females might explain the differences in anxiety‐like behaviors compared to males. Numerous studies confirmed that mood disorders are associated with neuroinflammation in regions from the limbic system, such as the hippocampus. 57 On the other hand, microglia implicated and responsible for secreting the proinflammatory cytokines might cause such sex differences in neuroinflammation, since male murine microglia were associated with a more proinflammatory phenotype compared to that of female microglia. 58

CONCLUSION

To our knowledge, this is the first report to show that chronic colitis throughout its development causes motor activity, mood, and cognitive impairments that are maintained in both the relapsing and remitting phases of the disease, which could be mediated by elevated proinflammatory cytokines in the brain, and that differ by sex.
