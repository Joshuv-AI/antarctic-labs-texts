Introduction

Cardiac metastases are more common than primary cardiac malignancy and are more frequently related to primary lung cancer, followed by breast cancer and haematologic malignancies. Typical presentation includes arrhythmias or conduction disturbances. The imaging findings of cardiac metastases are non-specific, but mostly infiltrative, heterogeneous, and multiple masses may be present. Echocardiography is the initial imaging test for the detection of cardiac metastasis, although cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR), computed tomography (CT), and positron-emitting tomography (PET)/CT may be helpful.

Herein, we described a case of cardiac metastasis with electrocardiogram (EKG) mimicking acute coronary syndrome.

Case presentation

A 78-year-old man with a recent diagnosis of lung squamous cell carcinoma was referred to our cardiology department for marked EKG abnormalities (ST-segment elevation in V2–V4 and T wave inversion in the antero-lateral leads; Figure 1) in the absence of ischaemic symptoms and for the detection of an intramyocardial mass at the apical segments on the CT scan. He had no significant previous cardiovascular history. A transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) was performed and showed an isoechoic mass at the left ventricular (LV) apical segments measuring 3 × 4 cm apparently incorporating the myocardial wall without a clear cleavage plane (Figure 2, yellow star). The ejection fraction (EF) was normal. No pericardial effusion was present. The contrast echocardiography with SonoVue revealed a late uptake of the contrast (Figure 3, yellow star).

Contrast echocardiography with ultrasonic enhancing agent revealed a late uptake of the contrast by the mass (star).

Cardiac magnetic resonance showed an ovular image in the para-apical area of ​​40 × 23 mm consistent with a secondary lesion (Figure 4). In particular, CMR showed the metastasis located near the anterior-apical left ventricle wall, which appears as an oval solid lesion isointense on T1w-black blood sequence four-chamber view (Figure 5A, arrow), isointense on steady-state free precession (SSFP) sequence long-axis view (Figure 5B, arrow) and in SSFP sequence short-axis view (Figure 5C, arrow), and mildly hyperintense on late gadolinium enhancement sequence in the SA view (Figure 5D, arrow).

Due to the infiltrative appearance of the mass and the oncology history, this formation was highly suspicious for metastasis. No complicated ventricular arrhythmias were detected on EKG monitoring. No symptoms of heart failure were reported.

Due to the marked EKG repolarization abnormalities, the patient underwent coronary angiography, which excluded significant stenosis.

Fludeoxyglucose (FDG) F18 PET/CT showed areas of increased metabolism at the level of cardiac mass and also at the liver and adrenal left gland. Finally, the patient was transferred to the oncology department for specific management.

Discussion

Cardiac metastases are more common than primary cardiac malignancy and are more frequently related to primary lung cancer. Primary tumour can metastasize to the heart through four pathways, including haematogenous, lymphatic, or transvenous spread, or direct invasion. Each metastatic pathway has different target tissues.1

Pericardial involvement is the most common (69.4%), which may result in pericardial effusion and tamponade, followed by epicardial or myocardial metastases, at 34.2% and 31.8%, respectively.2 Only 5% of cardiac metastasis involves the endocardium.

Typical presentation includes arrhythmias or conduction disturbances.3 The clinical manifestations of myocardial metastasis depend not only on the degree of metastatic infiltration but also on the location.1,4 The imaging findings of cardiac metastases are non-specific but mostly infiltrative and heterogeneous, and multiple masses may be present.

The diagnosis of cardiac metastasis is often incidental or delayed due to the non-specific nature of the symptoms. While the diagnostic value of EKG is limited, tissue histopathology remains the gold standard. Transthoracic echocardiography is the most common non-invasive, cost-effective diagnostic method, showing high sensitivity (90%) and specificity (95%) due to the clinical inaccessibility of tissue samples for histopathology.2 However, TTE alone may not provide sufficient detailed information, especially regarding the tumours’ relationships with adjacent tissues, as tumours usually infiltrate adjacent tissues. Transoesophageal echocardiography provides clearer and more precise information about cardiovascular morphology and anatomy and is an effective complement to TTE.5 Three-dimensional (3D) TEE has been suggested to produce high-quality images of cardiac tumours, offering real-time images and their relationships with adjacent organs.

Numerous echocardiographic criteria have been developed to identify cardiac masses, but errors in diagnosis and classification can lead to unnecessary surgery or inappropriate anticoagulation. By judiciously using ultrasonic enhancing agents (UEAs) to characterize cardiac masses and integrating all available information to establish aetiologies, these diagnostic errors can potentially be avoided. Suspicious cardiac masses not clearly visible on baseline images can be confirmed or ruled out by injecting intravenous UEAs to better delineate structures. Echocardiographic perfusion imaging, using a very low mechanical index (VLMI) with intermittent flash (high-MI) technique, has been shown to characterize the vascularity of cardiac masses and aid in distinguishing malignant, highly vascular tumours from benign tumours or thrombi. Complete enhancement or hyperenhancement of the tumour, compared to the surrounding myocardium, indicates the presence of a highly vascular tumour, which is often malignant. Stromal tumours, such as myxomas, have poor blood supply and appear partially enhanced, whereas thrombi or papillary fibroelastoma is generally avascular and shows no enhancement.6–8

Cardiac magnetic resonance is considered the gold standard for defining the nature of intracardiac masses and their relationship to para-cardiac structures. Cardiac magnetic resonance allows optimal tumour characterization with superior depiction of soft tissues and provides accurate evaluation of cardiac structure and function to complement echocardiography, enabling early detection and management.9 The benefits of CMR over echocardiography in studying cardiac tumours have been recognized.10 Previous studies suggested that cardiac metastases can be mistaken for thrombi, endocarditis, or primary tumours in echocardiograms, and supplemental imaging with CMR is often necessary for further characterization.11 A prospective CMR study has demonstrated that tumour size (>3.25 cm), invasion, and first-pass perfusion are useful imaging characteristics in differentiating benign from malignant tumours. Another prospective CMR study has revealed that invasiveness, irregular border, and late heterogeneous gadolinium enhancement are better variables for differentiating benign and malignant tumors.12

Although several studies have confirmed the value of 18F-FDG PET/CT, it has not yet become a routine examination method for evaluating cardiac metastasis, perhaps due to the high FDG uptake by the myocardium. It is important to understand the normal patterns of cardiac FDG activity that can be seen in oncologic FDG PET/CT studies. These include focal and regional patterns of increased FDG myocardial activity. Focal activity can be observed in papillary muscles, the atria, the base, and the distal anteroapical region of the left ventricle. Regional increased cardiac FDG activity may be diffuse or localized in the posterolateral wall or the base of the left ventricle. Abnormal patterns of cardiac FDG activity not related to malignancy include those associated with lipomatous hypertrophy of the interatrial septum, epicardial and pericardial fat, increased atrial activity associated with atrial fibrillation or a prominent crista terminalis, cardiac sarcoidosis, endocarditis, myocarditis, and pericarditis. Knowledge of these patterns of cardiac FDG activity is important to be able to recognize malignant diseases involving the paracardiac spaces, myocardium, and pericardium. With a better understanding of the range of normal and abnormal patterns of cardiac FDG activity, important benign and malignant diseases involving the heart and pericardium can be recognized and diagnosed.12

Herein, we described a case of cardiac metastasis with EKG mimicking acute coronary syndrome. This finding is not uncommon among these patients. Regarding EKG changes in cardiac metastasis, the most common abnormalities are ST-T changes (nonspecific ST-T changes, T wave inversion, ST-segment depression, ST elevation).3 Although ST-elevation (STE) related to myocardial metastasis is rare, it has been published in several case reports.2,4,13–15 Of note, evolutionary changes occurring in ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) are not seen in patients with cardiac metastases mimicking STEMI.4,16 These EKG features seem to be a sign of myocardial metastases. In patients with intracardiac, pericardial, or para-cardiac neoplastic masses, 86% of patients with STE suffered from myocardial infiltration detected by two-dimensional echocardiography.2 In addition, the localization of STE reflects the location of the cardiac infiltration.

Therefore, EKG findings of myocardial ischaemia or injury, particularly localized and prolonged ST elevation, in the absence of ischaemic symptoms have been reported in previous studies as high specificity for cardiac metastasis in patients with malignancy.

Finally, we want to highlight the importance of using all cardiac imaging techniques in the differential diagnosis of cardiac masses; among others, CMR certainly represents the gold standard in these situations, but the contribution of simple TTE with ultrasonic enhancing agent is definitely helpful.
