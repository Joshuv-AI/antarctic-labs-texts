Zinc methionine mitigates intestinal mucosal injury of broilers challenged with Salmonella by modulating mucus secretion, tight junction and microbial composition
Authors: Yuxin Shao, Yanrui Suo, Zheng Wang, Huiling Sun, Yifang Cui, Fangfang Guo, Dongmei Lin, Tenghe Ma, Fuzhou Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The investigation aimed to reveal potential mechanisms of zinc methionine on the intestinal mucosal function of Salmonella-challenged broilers. Totally, 320 Ross 308 male chicks were divided into 4 treatments, with 8 repetition per group and 10 chicks each. A 2 × 2 factor in a randomized block design was employed to explore the impacts of zinc addition, Salmonella challenge, and their interactions. The experiment lasted for 21 days. Results indicated that Salmonella infection decreased (P < 0.05) average daily gain (ADG) and the ratio of villus height to crypt depth (V/C) in both jejunum and ileum. The infection also reduced (P < 0.05) MUC2 and KLF4 mRNA levels, while elevating (P < 0.05) feed to gain (F:G), caudal-related homeobox transcriptional regulator 2 (CDX2) mRNA level, and altering (P < 0.05) bacterial composition. In contrast, zinc addition increased (P < 0.05) ADG, average daily feed intake, jejunal villus height, V/C, and tight junction mRNA expressions, but decreased (P < 0.05) F:G and CDX2 mRNA level. Moreover, zinc enhanced (P < 0.05) jejunal goblet cell number and ileal mucus thickness, and restored (P < 0.05) bacterial abundance. Correlation analysis found that zinc-enriched bacterial genera, including g_Butyricicoccus, g_Oscillibacter and g_Monoglobus were significantly positively correlated with broilers’ growth performance and intestinal morphology and tight junction Claudin-1 mRNA. CUT&Tag-seq analysis further demonstrated KLF4 binds to the exon region of MUC2 gene, but CDX2 did not bind to MUC2 promoter region. These suggest zinc mitigates Salmonella induced intestinal mucosal damage by promoting mucus secretion and tight junction mRNA expression, and microbial community reshaping, but not by improving KLF4-mediated MUC2 gene expression.

Introduction

With the rapid development of large-scale and intensive poultry farming models, pathogen infections are considered the main cause of intestinal diseases in broiler chickens. Among them, Salmonella is the most common foodborne pathogen in poultry production (Shaji et al., 2023), which can be transmitted horizontally and vertically, leading to widespread carriage in flocks. Although Salmonella levels in poultry feed always be zero according to feed production standards, the number of Salmonella in newly hatched broilers was as high as 25 % (Liao et al., 2020). It causes significant harm to broiler chickens in the early growth stage, with main clinical symptoms including damaged intestinal structure, diarrhea, accompanied by decreased growth performance or even death, causing substantial economic losses (Scharff, 2020; Acevedo-Villanueva et al., 2021). Additionally, it can be transmitted to humans through meat, eggs, and other foods, posing a serious threat to human health (Hugas and Beloeil, 2014).

Antibiotics have been the primary means to prevent and treat Salmonella infections. However, their extensive use has resulted in issues of drug-induced bacterial resistance and residual drug effects. Consequently, both European Union and China have prohibited the utilization of growth-promoting antibiotics in feed production and initiated the reduction of veterinary antimicrobial use in large-scale farms. Nevertheless, the prohibition of feed antibiotics has made broiler chicken intestines more vulnerable to pathogens such as Salmonella (Hofacre et al., 2018). Thus, how to prevent and control Salmonella infection has emerged as one of the urgent challenges in the current broiler industry.

The intestinal microbiota is the first-line barrier for the homeostasis of the entire intestinal environment, and it is of crucial importance for resisting the colonization of pathogenic microorganisms (Ducarmon et al., 2019). The intestinal pathogen Salmonella first breaks through the colonization resistance of the intestinal microbiota and then invades the intestinal mucosal epithelial cells, causing intestinal mucosal damage and inflammatory diarrhea (Narm et al., 2020). The intestinal microbiota is the first contact point for pathogenic Salmonella and also the first important defense line of the host intestine. The diversity of the microbial community protects the host from pathogen invasion through the colonization resistance formed by nutritional competition (Spragge et al., 2023). Therefore, preserving the intestinal microbiota homeostasis is crucial for reducing Salmonella colonization and invasion in the gut of chickens.

Adjacent to the microbial layer, the intestinal mucosal barrier, primarily composed of the mucus layer on the mucosal coating and the gastrointestinal epithelial cells of the submucosa, can also isolate pathogenic microorganisms and various foreign antigens, preventing their invasion and damage to the intestinal epithelium (Stolfi et al., 2022). Intestinal epithelial cells are interconnected through adherens and tight junctions, orchestrating the formation of an organized, selectively permeable monolayer. Across their apical surfaces, all gastrointestinal epithelium exhibit the production of transmembrane mucins with extensive glycosylation, which are critical components of the epithelial barrier (McGuckin et al., 2011). Moreover, goblet cells within the epithelium are specialized for the biosynthesis and secretion of gel - forming mucins, which undergo self - assemble to generate a viscous mucus layer that dynamically coats the epithelial surface. The intestinal pathogen Salmonella first breaches the intestinal surface mucus layer, then adheres, colonizes, and invades intestinal mucosal epithelial cells, causing intestinal mucosal damage and inflammatory diarrhea (Narm et al., 2020). The mucus layer represents the first interface between the intestinal microbiota and the host, functioning as an important line of defense for the intestinal tract. Intestinal mucus is primarily composed of the gel-like mucin 2 (MUC2) synthesized by goblet cells (Johansson, 2014). Therefore, maintaining intestinal mucus layer MUC2 is crucial for reducing Salmonella colonization and invasion in broiler chicken intestines. In recent years, studies have shown that caudal - related homeobox transcriptional regulator 2 (CDX2) is considered a critical DNA-binding protein in chickens’ gut and a key binding site for the MUC2 promoter sequence (Woodfint et al., 2017). CDX2 promoter activity is regulated by a zinc - containing protein named the zinc finger transcriptional regulator Kruppel - like factor 4 (KLF4).

Dietary zinc nutrition is one of the effective measures to enhance mucosal barrier functions and promote intestinal health in livestock or poultry (Shao et al., 2014; Jang et al., 2023). Zinc-loaded zeolite powder can effectively reduce the number of Salmonella pullorum in the cecum and improve intestinal health (Wang et al., 2012). Broiler chickens counter Salmonella typhimurium infection by regulating systemic zinc homeostasis (Wu et al., 2020). However, under the condition of Salmonella infection, whether zinc could alleviate intestinal damage by regulating mucosa secretion, goblet cell differentiation and MUC2 gene expression in the gut of chickens and related mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, we investigated the impact and mechanism of zinc methionine on mucus production, epithelial barrier properties and microbial compositions during Salmonella-host interactions.

Materials and methods

The research was conducted in compliance with the ethical standards established by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Institute of Animal Husbandry and Veterinary Medicine, Beijing Academy of Agriculture and Forestry Sciences (IAHVM-BAAFS), Beijing, China.

Chicken experiment

Totally, 320 Ross 308 male broilers at 1 day of age were randomly divided to 4 treatment groups, with 8 replicated cage per group and 10 chicks each. The experimental process was shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. A 2 × 2 factor in a randomized block design was employed to explore the effects of zinc addition (basal diet or basal diet supplemental 80 mg/kg zinc), Salmonella challenge (not infected or infected), and their interactions. The zinc level in basal diet was 24.78 mg/kg. To establish an enteritis model induced by Salmonella infection, both the control group and the infected group in this study were subjected to zinc-deficient conditions (Liu et al., 2012; Shao et al., 2014; Hou et al., 2021). Zinc source was organic methionine hydroxy analog chelated zinc (Zn-Met, Novus International, Saint Charles, USA). Our previous study compared the effects of different levels of zinc sulfate (0, 40, 80, 120 mg/kg of diet) on intestinal health in broilers and found that 80 or 120 mg/kg zinc improved intestinal mucosal barrier function by promoting the mRNA expression of tight junctions (Zhang et al., 2012). Additionally, we compared the effects of different zinc sources (zinc sulfate, zinc methionine zinc, zinc glycine and nano-zinc oxide) at the same supplementation level (80 mg zinc/kg diet and demonstrated that zinc methionine showed better growth performance and a lower inflammation response than other zinc sources (Xie et al., 2025). Therefore, dietary supplementation with Zn-Met at a dose of 80 mg/kg was used in the present study. The entire study lasted 21 days. Birds were reared in a light and temperature-manipulated house, and the feeding conditions were set as a previous study (Shao et al., 2019). The groups without zinc addition were supplemented with equal amounts of methionine hydroxy analog, and the methionine content was consistent across all experimental groups.

Salmonella challenge

Salmonella typhimurium was ordered from the China Institute of Veterinary Drug Control (Beijing, China). The bacterium was shaking cultured overnight in lactose broth at 37°C. To enumerate colony-forming units (CFUs), the bacterial culture underwent serially dilution followed by plating on XLD agar (Qingdao Haibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) for 24-48 h at 37°C. From day 5 to 7, all chicks in the challenged group received oral gavage of Salmonella (1.0 × 109 CFU per bird) once a day. In contrast, the control birds received oral gavage of equal amounts of sterile broth.

Growth performance

Chicks and feed on days 1 and 21 were weighed in a replicate. Subsequently, the growth performance including average daily gain (ADG) and average daily feed intake (ADFI) were recorded, and the ratio of feed to gain (F:G) was subsequently calculated for the 21-day study phase. Daily visual monitoring and recording of health status and mortality were performed throughout the study.

Sample collection

At 14 days, a random of eight birds per treatment group were selected and euthanized via cervical dislocation. Jejunal and ileal samples (approximately 1 cm in length) were cut, immediately immersed in a fixative solution for histological analysis. The mucosal scrapings of jejunum and ileum were sampled using a pre-chilled glass, and rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen for DNA and RNA extraction. Additionally, the cacal contents were sampled aseptically and frozen in liquid nitrogen for 16 s rRNA gene sequence analysis.

Intestinal histomorphology and goblet cell histochemistry

Consecutive 5-μm-thick sections were subjected to hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining for histomorphology examination and observed using an Olympus optical microscope (Jena, Germany). Villus height and corresponding crypt depth were determined in a random of 10 selected villi from each chicken at a magnification of 40 × . The calculation of villus height to crypt depth ratio was performed by dividing the villus height by crypt depth. Staining of sections was carried out with the periodic acid-Schiff and Alcian blue at pH 2.5 to measure the mucin. Goblet cells containing mucin along the villi were enumerated, and the corresponding surface area of the full-length villus was measured. Goblet cell density was presented as the goblet cell number per unit surface area. The mucus layer thickness on the villus surface was detected in 10 randomly selected villi per chicken at 100 × magnification.

RNA extraction and its concentration was determined, and subsequently, RNA was converted into cDNA via reverse transcription (RT), followed by real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR). The main protocols was performed as our published study (Shao et al., 2017). The gene-specific primers for MUC2, CDX2, KLF4, Claudin-1, Occludin, ZO-1 and GAPDH were list in Table 2. CUT&Tag for DNA library of the intestinal mucosa

Intestinal mucosal cells were collected, quantified, and pelleted via centrifugation at 600 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Chromatin cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) was carried out using the Assay Kit for Illumina Pro (TD904, Vazyme, China). Briefly, about 100,000 cells per sample were washed twice with 0.5 mL of Wash Buffer by pipetting gently. Samples were then incubated in 10 µL activated conA-coated magnetic beads for 15 min at room temperature to enhance binding efficiency. Following magnetic separation, bead-bound cells were redissolved in 50 µL Dig-wash Buffer supplemented with primary antibodies against CDX2 or KLF4 (ABclonal, China) at a 1:50 dilution and rotated overnight at 4°C. Unbound antibodies was removed using a magnetic separator, and samples were subjected to incubate with a dilution of 1:50 secondary IgG antibody in Dig-Wash buffer for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were treated with 3 washes with 0.2 mL Dig-Wash buffer, and 100 µL of the pA/G-Tnp pro was added to the cells, gently vortexed, and kept for 1 h at room temperature. After 3 washes with Dig-med Buffer, cells were pipetted with 50 µL of Tagmentation buffer and kept for 1 h at 37°C. Tagmentation was halted by introducing 2 µL of 10 % SDS and DNA spike-in to each sample, and then a incubation for 10 minat 55°C. DNA extraction and library amplification were then carried out for sequencing.

Next-generation sequencing libraries were prepared as follows briefly. DNA sample underwent terminal repair, 5′ phosphorylation, and dA-tailing and adaptor linking sequentially in a single reaction using the End Prep Enzyme Mix. Adaptor-ligated DNA was purified using magnetic beads and amplified via PCR with P5/P7 primers to enable bridge PCR and multiplexing. Indexed libraries were pooled and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform in a 2 × 150 paired-end configuration. Raw data were processed using Cutadapt to filler out low-quality sequencing. Clean data were mapped to the reference genome via Bowtie2, and peak calling and annotation were performed using MACS2.

DNA extraction, 16S rRNA gene cloning and sequencing of Cecal microbiota

Cecal content lysis and genomic DNA extraction were performed via the TIANGEN Stool DNA Kit. DNA concentration and purity were determined on a Quawell spectrophotometer (San Jose, CA). The hypervariable regions V3-V4 from bacteria 16S rDNA were PCR with 338F/806R primers on a thermocycler (ABI 9700, CA). High-fidelity PCR were performed as follows: denaturation at 95°C for 3 min; 29 cycles (95°Cfor 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, 72°Cfor 45 s); extension at 72°C for 10 min. Amplicons were resolved via electrophoresis, purified via the DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, CA), and quantified with QuantiFluor-ST (Promega, WI). Equimolar pooled libraries were sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (2 × 300 bp paired-end) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. Raw FASTQ data were processed via Trimmomatic for quality-filtering and FLASH for read merging with the parameters: (i) the truncated reads at site have an average quality score < 20 over a 50-bp sliding window; (ii) overlapping sequences (> 10 bp) merged with ≤ 2 mismatches; (iii) samples demultiplexed by exact barcode matching and primer sequences allowing 2 mismatches. The sequence were clustered into OTUs using a 97 % similarity threshold via v7.1 UPARSE with an integrated pipeline that concurrently performs chimera filtering and OTU clustering. Taxonomic classification was subsequently performed via the RDP classifier algorithm against the Silva (SSU123) 16S rDNA database, with assignments requiring a confidence score of ≥70 %.

Statistical analysis

Data were statistically analyzed via two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in SPSS 12.0, and results are present as means ± SEM. If there is a significant interaction between main effects, individual treatment means were compared via Duncan’s multiple range test. Statistical significance was considered as P < 0.05.

CDX2 did not bind to the promoter region of MUC2, but KLF4 binds to the exon region of MUC2 in the jejunal mucosa

To determine whether the transcription factor CDX2 and KLF4 regulates MUC2 gene expression, the binding sites were predicted firstly. We used the website ‘http://jaspar.genereg.net/’ to predict that CDX2 or KLF4 has multiple binding loci across the promoter sequence of MUC2 gene. Then we used the CUT&Tag technique and DNA sequencing to determine the actual binding sites of CDX2 or KLF4. The results revealed that CDX2 exhibited no binding to the promoter region of MUC2. Correlation analysis showed that most genera were positively associated with ADG and V/C, but negatively correlated with F:G and CDX2 mRNA level. In details, the bacterial genera enriched by zinc, including g_Butyricicoccus, and g_GCA-900066575 exhibited strong positive correlations with ADG, and negative correlations with the F:G. These indicate that zinc could improve growth performance by regulating these microbiota taxa. Besides, the genera g_Oscillibacter and g_Monoglobus enriched by zinc have a strongly positive correlation with mRNA level of tight junctional protein Claudin-1, and g_Monoglobus positively correlated with the jejunal VH and V/C, implying that zinc might promote the formation of intestinal morphological development and enhance integrity by increasing the abundance of these bacterial genera. Faecalibacterium, whose abundance was decreased due to Salmonella infection, showed a significant positive correlation with MUC2 mRNA expression. Both g_Butyricicoccus and g_Colidextribacter were negatively correlated with the mRNA level of the transcription factor CDX2.

Discussion

Salmonella infection leads to a significant adverse impact on poultry’s growth performance (Huang et al., 2022). It is possible that Salmonella can promote its own growth and survival by competing with the host for the nutrients zinc (Liu et al., 2012) and iron (Roche et al., 2025), leading to the loss of trace element nutrients in the body and subsequent impaired growth. Dietary zinc methionine supplementation alleviated the reduced body weight of broilers under S. typhimurium infection or zinc-deficient condition. Our previous study demonstrated that diet supplemental zinc sulfate was very effective in improving growth performance of broilers infected with S. typhimurium (Shao et al., 2014). To consistent with this study, the growth performance of yellow feather broiler was significantly improved by supplementing 60 mg/kg zinc glycinate than those fed with basal diet without zinc addition (Zhu et al., 2022). Additionally, dietary supplemental 80 mg/kg zinc methionine showed better growth performance than other zinc sources including zinc sulfate, zinc glycinate and nano-zinc oxide at the same level (Xie et al., 2025). However, the overall growth performance seemed lower than the expected performance of Ross 308 broilers. Firstly, a relatively low nutritional level was used to establish an infection model, leading to low production performance of broilers. Secondly, infected and non-infected broilers were not raised separately, resulting in reduced production performance of the entire flock.

The small intestine serves as the primary site where nutrients undergo digestion and absorption, and the intestinal morphological structure including villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio is the most widely established approach for assessing intestinal mucosal integrity and absorption capability (Krebs, 2000). The intact intestinal morphology could reduce Salmonella invasion of the intestinal epithelium and the body weight recovery (Shao et al., 2014). Our current study showed that zinc methionine produced an improvement of the intestinal morphology structure by increasing the villus height and villus height/crypt depth ratio in the jejunum of broilers infected with Salmonella. These results are in agreement with others showing that zinc methionine supplementation dramatically enhanced jejunal intestinal morphology of laying hens (Li et al., 2019) and ducks (Chang et al., 2022).

The mucus on the intestinal mucosal surface primarily consists of gel-like MUC2, which is mainly produced by secretory intestinal goblet cells and maintains intestinal homeostasis while restricting bacterial contact with epithelial cells (Birchenough et al., 2015; Nystrom et al., 2021). Zarepour et al. (2013) reported that MUC2 knockout mice showed significant vulnerability to Salmonella attack, harboring a greater cecal pathogen burden, experiencing more severe barrier breakdown, and rising mortality rates compared to wild-type mice. In line with prior research findings, we demonstrated that Salmonella infection significantly reduces MUC2 gene expression in the jejunal mucosa of broilers. However, the addition of zinc methionine only elevated the jejunal goblet cell number in non-infected broilers, but did not enhance MUC2 gene expression regardless of infection status in the current study. In contrast to our findings, previous research has shown that maternal dietary supplementation with 300 mg/kg zinc methionine can promote jenunal MUC2 mRNA expression in offspring broilers (Li et al., 2015). Dietary supplementation with 120 or 150 mg/kg zinc methionine significantly increased jenunal MUC2 mRNA expression in meat duck (Chang et al., 2022). A possible reason is that the dosage of zinc methionine supplemented in our study (80 mg/kg) is relatively low. In future studies, we will investigate different dosages of zinc methionine to explore their impacts on mucin gene expression. In vitro experiments indicate that zinc deficiency inhibits the mRNA expression of goblet cell MUC2 and MUC5AC, as well as the O-glycosylation level of MUC2 (Maares et al., 2020). Therefore, in future studies, we could try to determine the levels of MUC5AC or MUC5L gene expression or the O-glycosylation level of MUC2.

The gene expression of MUC2 in intestinal mucosal goblet cells is typically regulated by promoters and enhancers (Yamamoto et al., 2003). Triple staining of human intestinal mucosal epithelial cells with CDX2/MUC2/acid mucin revealed that CDX2 may antedate MUC2 transcription and ensuing acid mucin production (Omori et al., 2020). Our study found that Salmonella infection inhibited MUC2 gene expression while upregulating CDX2 transcription, whereas post-infection zinc supplementation inhibited CDX2 gene expression. A closely related study by Hu et al. (2024) demonstrated that treatment with metformin effectively improved gastrointestinal function by increasing the MUC5AC expression while inhibiting CDX2 expression. To further investigate whether the transcription factor CDX2 regulates intestinal mucosal MUC2 expression by binding to its promoter, we employed CUT&Tag and high-throughput sequencing technologies. Strikingly, sequencing results revealed no interaction with CDX2 to promoter sequences of mucin-associated genes (MUC2 or MUC5AC). Instead, CDX2 binding peaks were predominantly detected at LOC112529953, J6367_mgr01 encoding cytochrome b, and MicroRNA loci. It was reported that CDX2-induced miR-744 up-regulation leads to a marked increase the motility and invasive capabilities of NSCLC cells (Sha et al., 2021). The possible explanation is that as a DNA-binding transcriptional regulator, the CDX2 protein interacts with thousands of chromatin sites, broadly regulating gut function in stem cells and terminally differentiated cells (Nakagawa et al., 2015).

Additionally, the zinc-finger transcription factor KLF4 is a zinc-binding molecule. Studies have shown that nano-zinc oxide can promote KLF4 expression through sustained release of Zn²⁺ to maintain stem cell stability (Kong et al., 2019). Katz et al. (2002) reported that KLF4 is crucial for intestinal goblet cell differentiation. In KLF4 gene-deficient mice, the expression of intestinal mucosal MUC2 protein is reduced. Similarly, in vitro experiments have confirmed that organoids differentiated from eYFP⁺ cells with KLF4 gene deficiency have a decreased goblet cells number secreting MUC2 (Ghaleb et al., 2011; Katano et al., 2020). Our study found that Salmonella infection obviously inhibited the transcription of KLF4, and KLF4 gene expression patterns was paralleled to MUC2 mRNA levels. Notably, CUT&Tag sequence further confirmed that KLF4 protein could bind to the exon region of MUC2 and MUC5L gene, suggesting a potential regulatory role of the transcription factor KLF4 on MUC2 and MUC5L gene expressions. These results implied that Salmonella infection reduces the targeted binding of KLF4 to the MUC2 promoter by inhibiting the expression of the transcription factor KLF4, thereby suppressing the expression of the mucin MUC2. However, zinc did not promote the expression of either KLF4 or MUC2 in the jejunum of broilers, indicating the improvement of intestinal barrier function by zinc was not related to the KLF4/MUC2 signaling pathway. Small intestinal KLF4 produced in high abundance in the intestine, is expressed at elevated levels in mice fed a low-zinc diet (Liuzzi et al., 2009). At present, there is no relevant literature on the regulatory effect of zinc on KLF4 in poultry.

Adjacent to the mucosa is the epithelial cell cytoskeletal proteins and tight junctional proteins (Claudin-1, Occludin and ZO-1), which were also important for Salmonella attachment and invasion into the cell interior (Zhang et al., 2012). Once these sites are damaged, causing inflammatory diarrhea and even death. Our previous experiments showed that zinc sulfate promotes the proliferation of Caco-2 epithelial cells and tight junction protein expression via PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways, thereby improving intestinal mucosal barrier function (Shao et al., 2017). The present research investigated that intestinal morphology and gene expressions of jejunal Claudin-1, Occludin and ZO-1 were upregulated by zinc methionine supplementation for broilers’ diet. Relevant results indicate that zinc can effectively form an organized monolayer with tight junctional proteins and prevent pathogenic microbial colonization and invasion.

It is well known that gut microbiota plays an important role in maintaining the structural and functional integrity of the mucosa, modulating the immune system, and preventing pathogen colonization in chickens (Sommer and Backhed, 2013). In our study, Salmonella challenge dramatically depleted the population of Faecalibacterium and Colidextribacter. Similarly, after E. maxima infection, the abundance of Faecalibacterium in the cecum of broilers decreases significantly by 30-40 %, accompanied by elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α (Graham et al., 2025). However, dietary zinc methionine did not increase Faecalibacterium abundance, whereas it enriched the genera Butyricicoccus, Colidextribacter, Oscillibacter, and Monoglobus. Additionally, we found that Butyricicoccus and Oscillibacter have positive associations with broilers’ growth performance. These findings align with those reported by Eeckhaut et al. (2016), who revealed that Butyricicoccus pullicaecorum supplementation can boost nutrient utilization, decrease the quantity of potentially pathogenic bacteria colonizing the distal intestinal segment, and help prevent necrotic enteritis in broilers. Additionally, Oscillibacter enriched by zinc methionine had a strong positive correlation with tight junction Claudin-1 gene expression. Among the target microbiota taxa, Oscillibacter has emerged as a notable genus with implications for dietary interventions and inflammatory conditions (Potrykus et al., 2021). Recognized for its ability to produce SCFAs such as butyrate, this genus produces compounds that exert anti-inflammatory effects and enhance gut barrier function (Latorre-Pérez et al., 2021). Importantly, in current study, there is a positive association between Monoglobus and intestinal morphology, as well as Claudin-1 gene expression. It has been reported that the genus Monoglobus in human possesses a specialized glycobiome for pectin degradation, resulting in polysaccharide degradation products (including SCFAs) involved in intestinal immune regulation (Kim et al., 2019; Elshahed et al., 2021). Research on the application of Oscillibacter and Monoglobus in livestock and poultry is extremely limited, and their roles and functions require further verification. In any case, our research preliminarily revealed that zinc may improve intestinal mucosal barrier function and promote production performance by enriching these probiotic genera in the caecum of broilers.

In conclusion, dietary supplementation of zinc methionine alleviates intestinal mucosal damage induced by Salmonella infection by promoting mucus secretion, tight junction gene expression, and microbial community composition, but not by improving KLF4-mediated MUC2 gene expression, thereby leading to the improvement of the growth performance of broilers.
