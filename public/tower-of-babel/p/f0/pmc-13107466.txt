Enantioselective Iridium-Catalyzed C–H Bond Alkylation for the Synthesis of N–N Atropisomeric Phosphines
Authors: Si-Hao Fu, Rui-Jing Pang, Yi Yue, Long-Long Xi, Jia Feng, Ren-Rong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Over the past few decades, atropisomeric biaryl phosphines have emerged as privileged scaffolds in the design of chiral ligands, bioactive molecules, and drug candidates. However, despite their considerable potential, the enantioselective synthesis of N–N atropisomeric phosphines remains underdeveloped. Herein, we report an iridium-catalyzed, enantioselective C–H alkylation for constructing N–N atropisomeric monophosphines, employing a phosphine­(III) group as a directing motif. This method enables ready access to a diverse range of N–N-linked indole–pyrrole and indole–indole frameworks with up to 98% ee. Furthermore, the utility of this approach is demonstrated through a series of synthetic transformations and catalytic applications.

Introduction

A central challenge in transition-metal-catalyzed enantioselective synthesis lies in the design of optimal ligands that simultaneously deliver high catalytic efficiency and excellent stereocontrol. Within this pursuit, monophosphines have proven to be among the most effective ligand classes. The past decade has witnessed a surge in the development of structurally diverse monophosphines, including examples featuring central (e.g., AntPhos, I, and II), planar (e.g., PPFOMe, III), spiro- (e.g., IV), helical (e.g., V), and axial (e.g., MOP, VI) chirality (Scheme a). Among these, atropisomeric monophosphinescharacterized by their adaptable structural frameworks and tunable steric environmentshave emerged as a significant subclass of phosphorus ligands. Despite their demonstrated potential, the enantioselective synthesis of atropisomeric monophosphines remains a highly desirable, yet formidable undertaking.

Transition-Metal-Catalyzed Enantioselective Synthesis of Atropisomeric Biaryl Monophosphines

Atropisomeric biaryl monophosphines constitute a paramount class of chiral phosphine ligands, and tremendous achievements have been made in their enantioselective synthesis. Direct cross-coupling of phosphorus-substituted aryl halides with arylboronic acid forges chiral C–C axes, delivering target biaryl monophosphines either directly or following reduction (Scheme b left). Cramer’s enantioselective carbene insertion into C–H bonds provides a remarkably efficient method for atropisomeric biaryl monophosphines (Scheme b left). Recently, Li and Yu demonstrated an enantioselective ring-opening strategy of cyclic phosphonium salts for atropisomeric biaryls synthesis, enabling the incorporation of diverse functional groups into biaryl monophosphines (Scheme b right). Additionally, using pyridine as a directing group, transition metal-catalyzed enantioselective C–P bond formation enables efficient access to atropisomeric heterobiaryl monophosphines through dynamic kinetic resolution or kinetic resolution.

Notably, using phosphines­(III) as directing groups, transition-metal-mediated C–H functionalization has emerged as a transformative approach for synthesizing phosphine ligands and architecting phosphorus-containing molecules. Shi pioneered transition-metal-catalyzed C–H functionalization for axial chiral and planar chiral phosphine synthesis, establishing an efficient method for phosphine ligand construction via late-stage C–H activation (Scheme c). This methodology successfully achieved enantioselective C–H arylation, alkenylation, and alkylation to forge C–C atropisomeric biaryl monophosphines with excellent enantioselectivity.

The unique structural and electronic properties of N–N atropisomers have recently spurred a renaissance in their study. Since the first independent reports of enantioselective catalytic syntheses of N–N atropisomers by the groups of Lu/Houk and Liu in 2021, this field has undergone rapid growth. Early groundwork was laid in 1997 when Sannicolò reported the synthesis of N–N atropisomeric diphosphine BIMIP using preparative chiral HPLC and conducted detailed structural investigations. More recently, Liu and Feng achieved the preparation of C2-symmetric N–N atropisomeric phosphines via resolution from readily available starting materials, enabling the incorporation of diverse steric groups. Despite these advances, a general catalytic enantioselective route to N–N atropisomeric phosphines remains, to our knowledge, undeveloped (Scheme d). Herein, we report the synthesis of such phosphines via an iridium-catalyzed, phosphine­(III)-directed enantioselective C–H alkylation of indole/pyrrole derivatives (Scheme e). A key challenge in this reaction arose from a dual, and ultimately problematic, consequence of the substrate’s electron-rich architecture. On one hand, the indole or pyrrole framework enhances the donor strength of the phosphorus center to the point of impeding catalysis. On the other hand, beyond this electronic interference, C–H activation of indoles presents its own inherent difficulties. To address this, the development of a suitable catalytic system was crucial. By employing a tailored iridium catalyst system, we successfully managed these competing electronic effects. Consequently, reactions using N–N indole/pyrrole substrates proceeded efficiently to afford target products in up to 98% ee. For the more challenging indole/indole scaffolds, a dynamic kinetic resolution strategy was implemented, delivering the corresponding phosphines with high enantioselectivity (up to 94% ee).

Results and Discussion

Optimization of Reaction Conditions

Enantioselective C–H alkylation was performed using N–N indole–pyrrole 1a and ethyl acrylate 2a as starting materials (Table ). Under catalysis by [Ir­(cod)­Cl]2 and an axially chiral BINOL-derived phosphinamide ligand at 150 °C, C–H functionalization afforded the monoalkylated product 3a in 33% yield with 63% ee after ready oxidation (entry 1). Employing the phosphinamide ligand L2 or L3, bearing a cyclic amine moiety, led to a slight improvement in both the yield and enantioselectivity (entries 2–3). Increasing the steric bulk at the ortho position suppressed the reaction (entry 4). Furthermore, modifying the ligand scaffold to H8–BINOL or TADDOL did not yield satisfactory outcomes (entries 5–6). Notably, the use of a spiro phosphinamide significantly enhanced the enantioselectivity to 91%, albeit with a lower yield of 21% (entry 7). A detailed evaluation of spiro phosphinamide ligands failed to yield satisfactory results in terms of both efficiency and enantioselectivity (entries 8–10). Subsequent solvent screening revealed that alternatives such as xylene, 1,4-dioxane, and DMF were unsuitable for the reaction (entries 11–13). Replacing the catalyst with [Ir­(cod)­OMe]2 did not improve the reaction efficiency or selectivity, while [Ir­(cod)2]­BF4 afforded the desired product in only trace amounts (entries 14–15). Notably, the use of [Ir­(coe)2Cl]2 significantly enhanced the reaction outcome, delivering the product in 60% yield with 85% ee (entry 16). Further refinement by increasing the loading of 2a from 1.2 to 3.0 equiv led to a more efficient process, furnishing the alkylated product in 65% yield and 91% ee along with the dialkylated product 3a’’ in 20% yield within a shortened reaction time of 4.5 h (entry 17). The addition of alkenes (e.g., cyclohexene) was ineffective in achieving a comparable yield (entry 18). Furthermore, employing ligand L1 with 3.0 equiv of 2a resulted in a slight decrease in both yield and enantioselectivity relative to the conditions employing L7 (entry 19). A lower reaction temperature with 3.0 equiv of 2a led to a decreased yield while maintaining an ee of 91% (entry 20).

Investigation of the Substrate Scope

With the optimal conditions established, we next evaluated the generality of the protocol (Scheme ). First, a series of alkyl acrylates were subjected to enantioselective C–H functionalization. These reactions proceeded with excellent enantioselectivity in moderate to good yields. Acrylates bearing methyl, ethyl, n-butyl, n-dodecyl (–C12H25), or branched alkyl groups all proved to be suitable substrates, affording N–N atropisomers with up to 97% ee (3a–3e). Upon observation of partial oxidation of the electro-rich product during column chromatography, an in situ oxidation procedure was implemented to ensure its full conversion to the phosphorus oxides 3′, thereby simplifying the isolation process. An ester-functionalized acrylate was also tested and gave satisfactory results (3f′). Benzyl acrylate was successfully applied, yielding product 3g′ with 93% ee. Next, various aryl-substituted acrylates were examined under the optimized conditions to demonstrate the broad substrate scope. Phenyl acrylate underwent smooth C–H alkylation, affording 3h′ in 57% yield with 97% ee. Ortho-substituted phenyl acrylates bearing bromo or tert-butyl groups provided high enantioselectivities (>93% ee, 3i–3j). The 2,4-di­(tert-butyl)­phenyl acrylate derivative gave axially chiral phosphine 3k with improved yield and enantioselectivity (75%, 93% ee). The chirality of 3k was found to be stable, as validated by heating in xylene at 150 °C for 24 h, which caused no erosion of enantioselectivity. The corresponding racemization barrier was calculated to be 155.2 kJ/mol at 170 °C. Reactions with 3-substituted and 3,5-disubstituted aryl acrylates proceeded in comparable yields and with excellent enantioselectivities (up to 98% ee, 3l’–3p′). Acrylates with 4-methyl, 4-tert-butyl, or 4-methoxy-substituted benzyl groups also afforded high enantioselectivities (up to 97% ee, 3q’–3s′). Para-halogen substituents (F or Br) were well-tolerated, yielding axially chiral phosphine oxides with excellent enantioselectivity (3t’-3u′). The 4-phenyl acrylate substrate provided product 3v′ with 97% ee; its structure and absolute configuration were unambiguously confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (CCDC 2494509). A 4-(4-bromophenyl) substituted acrylate was also compatible with this methodology (3w′). A polyfluoroalkyl acrylate afforded the corresponding product in 45% yield and 91% ee (3x′). Finally, several acrylates derived from natural productsincluding testosterone, ergosterol, and D-ribofuranosidewere successfully employed, yielding the desired N–N atropisomers in 43–62% yields (3y’–3aa′).

The substrate scope was then evaluated with respect to fused indole scaffolds. Employing a methoxy group at the C6 position afforded the desired phosphine atropisomer 3ab with 93% ee. Introduction of a five-membered ring fused across the C5 and C6 positions proceeded smoothly, delivering product 3ac′ with excellent enantioselectivity. Satisfactory results were also achieved with a C6 electron-withdrawing fluoro group, providing 3ad’. In the case of C5-substituted indoles, both electron-donating and electron-withdrawing groups were found to be compatible with the protocol, yielding products 3ae’–3ag′ with excellent enantioselectivities. The investigation was extended to phosphorus substrates bearing different aryl groups. Under the standard conditions, substrates with 4-tolyl or 3,5-xylyl groups on the phosphorus atom gave products 3ah′ and 3ai′ in moderate yields and with excellent enantioselectivities. Vinyl ketone was also applied to the protocol, and 3aj′ was obtained with excellent enantioselectivity. However, the reaction with styrene and a disubstituted olefin could not give the desired products (3ak–3al).

Next, we evaluated the generality of the protocol using N–N indole–indole substrates (Scheme ). First, the racemization barrier of substrate 4a at 423 K was calculated to be 143 kJ/mol. The half-life of 4a at 423 K is 7.1 h, indicating that the C–H alkylation process would preclude effective kinetic resolution. Then, enantioenriched 4a (99% ee) was subjected to the standard C–H alkylation conditions for 4.5 h. The process afforded product 5a′ with excellent enantioselectivity (93% ee), while the recovered 4a’ (26% yield) showed a significantly reduced ee value of 18%. Notably, the racemization rate of 4a under standard conditions exceeded that observed in toluene at 150 °C, which can likely be attributed to the formation of an iridacycle intermediate. To improve the ee of the recovered 4a′, two modified reaction conditions were tested: shortening the reaction time and lowering the reaction temperature.

Attempts to achieve full conversion of 4a by extending the reaction time to 72 h proved unsatisfactory, yielding 5a′ with lower yield and enantioselectivity (32%, 84% ee). Finally, employing rac-orthotrisubstituted N–N indole-pyrrole 4b as the substrate, the C–H alkylated products were formed in 49–71% yield with excellent enantioselectivities, and 4b was recovered in up to 32% yield (5f-5h).

Mechanism Research

To elucidate the mechanism, a series of control experiments were conducted (Scheme ). Treatment of 1a with CD3OD instead of acrylate under standard conditions afforded 1a’-[D] in 80% yield, with deuteration observed at both the C2’/C5′, while no deuterium incorporation occurred at the C3 position (Scheme , eq 1). Additionally, deuteration at the C3′/C4′ position was observed, likely resulting from isomerization of the iridium complex. In a related experiment, treating 3k with D2O under the same conditions produced 3k’-[D] without any deuterium incorporation at C1″ or C2’’ (Scheme , eq 1). These results indicate that the phosphine directing group may facilitate C–H activation at the C2′ and C5′ positions of the pyrrole ring, potentially through a six-membered iridacycle intermediate. Addition of CD3OD to the standard enantioselective C–H alkylation reaction yielded both 1a’-[D] and 3h’-[D] in 45% and 35% yield, respectively. 1 H NMR analysis of 3h’-[D] revealed partial deuteration at the C3′, C4′, C5′, C1″, and C2″ positions (Scheme , eq 2). A kinetic isotope effect (KIE) of 1.0 was determined, which implies that C–H activation is probably not the rate-determining step (Scheme , eq 3). Subjecting deuterated acrylate 2e-[D] to the standard reaction conditions furnished the partially deuterated product 3g’-[D], with 68% deuterium retention observed in the recovered acrylate. This result indicates the regeneration of an Ir–H species during the reaction (Scheme , eq 4). Furthermore, the natural abundance 12C/13C KIE for the reaction of 1a with 2a was determined by quantitative 13C NMR spectroscopy. The measured KIE values of 1.032 at C1″ and 1.042 at C2′ are significant, supporting that reductive elimination (C–C bond formation) is the rate-determining step (Scheme , eq 5). A series of control experiments were also performed (Scheme ). Replacing ligand L7 with 3k under standard conditions afforded 3m′ with only 7% ee, which precluded the possibility of self-catalysis by the newly formed phosphine-containing product. Furthermore, the enantioselective C–H alkylation of enantioenriched 4a furnished racemic products 4a′ and 5a′, providing further evidence against a self-catalyzed process. Subjecting 1a to 2b and 2a subsequently under standard conditions enabled an iterative process, furnishing compound 6 in 41% yield with 82% ee. In the above process, the second C–H alkylation proceeds via a kinetic resolution to afford the enantioenriched product 6.

Control Experiments

Based on the control experiments and mechanistic studies described above, a plausible catalytic cycle is proposed (Scheme ). First, the active cationic IrI-complex is formed by the coordination of [Ir­(coe)­Cl]2 with 2a and L7. Subsequent oxidative addition of the C–H bond to this IrI species affords the IrIII-intermediate INT-1. Subsequent insertion of 2a into the Ir–H bond generates INT-2, which can revert to INT-1 via regeneration of the Ir–H species. Final reductive elimination then affords the desired alkylated product, completing the catalytic cycle. Based on the above mechanism research, the C–C bond-forming step is rate-determining, while the stereochemistry is established in the transformation of INT-2. In the proposed stereocontrol model, INT-2 is more stable than INT-2′, which demonstrates the steric repulsion between the aryl rings on L7 and the pyrrole rings.

Synthetic Application

To demonstrate the utility of the protocol, synthetic and catalytic applications were explored. A gram-scale experiment was performed, affording the desired product in a comparable yield and enantioselectivity (Scheme a). Treatment of the resulting phosphine oxide 3h′ with NBS readily provided bromide 7 while retaining enantioselectivity (95% ee). Subsequent sulfur treatment of the alkylated product furnished thiophosphine oxide 8 in 69% yield and 92% ee. Hydrolysis of the ester under basic conditions in a mixed solvent system liberated the free acid group, yielding compound 9, which serves as a bifunctional catalyst in asymmetric catalysis. With the newly synthesized N–N atropisomeric monophosphines in hand, their application in asymmetric allylic alkylation was investigated (Scheme b). A preliminary screening of both N–N indole–pyrrole and indole–indole atropisomers revealed that catalyst 3e exhibited excellent stereochemical induction, affording the alkylated product 11 with 87% ee. Moreover, employing ligand 3e in the asymmetric allylic alkylation of indole afforded product 12 in 95% yield and 80% ee.

Synthetic and Catalytic Applications

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have realized an iridium-catalyzed, enantioselective C–H alkylation using a phosphine­(III) directing group. This method enables the construction of atropisomeric monophosphines, including N–N indole–pyrrole and N–N indole–indole scaffolds, through either desymmetrization or dynamic kinetic resolution with excellent enantiocontrol. Furthermore, the utility and robustness of this approach are highlighted by its success in diverse synthetic transformations and catalytic applications.
