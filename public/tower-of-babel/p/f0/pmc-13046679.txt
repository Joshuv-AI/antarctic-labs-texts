Multiple laser spot sizes treatment of childhood spider nevi: a retrospective study of 452 patients
Authors: Jianping Wu, Pingdong Li, Chengjun Cui, Hongsong Ge, Ling Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpider nevus (SN) is a common cutaneous vasoproliferative disorder in children. Data on the use of 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes for treating SN in children remain scarce.ObjectiveTo summarize the clinical characteristics and evaluate the effectiveness and safety of 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes in treating SN in children.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 452 patients treated for SN using 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes.ResultsAmong the 452 children with a total of 508 lesions, complete resolution was achieved. Lesions on the head and face required a greater proportion of multiple treatments compared to other locations, whereas lesions on the neck and other areas were cured with a single treatment (χ2 = 7.774E-8, p = 0.001). There was a significant association between lesion diameter and the number of treatments, with larger lesions requiring a greater proportion of multiple treatments (χ2 = 4.725, p = 0.001). Regarding treatment season, more treatments were needed in summer (χ2 = 13.233, p = 0.001). A total of 36 children (7.08%) experienced localized adverse reactions.ConclusionsOur findings confirmed the efficacy and safety of 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes for treating SN in children.Keywords: Spider nevus, 595 nm pulsed dye laser, Children, Treatment, Retrospective study

Introduction

Spider nevus (SN), also known as spidery capillary dilation, often occurs in the distribution area of the superior vena cava, such as the face, neck, and chest wall. It consists of small central arteries and superficial radial branches that resemble spiders in appearance [1, 2]. Possible mechanisms of SN formation include vasodilation of small arteries, promotion of neovascularization by angiogenic factors, direct alcohol action, and excess estrogen due to impaired hepatic metabolism [3]. Although SN is often considered a somatic feature of liver disease, it is primarily idiopathic in children and is usually not associated with systemic conditions [3, 4]. In children, SN lesions frequently appear on the face and can be persistent, causing aesthetic concerns. This leads to parental anxiety during the watchful waiting period. Additionally, children are often too young to cooperate with treatment, and inadequate laser treatment can result in significant pain and psychological burden. This creates a conflict between the need for treatment and the challenges associated with it [5].

Numerous treatment methods for SN have been reported, including electrocoagulation [6], diode lasers, argon lasers [7], PDL lasers [8], KTP lasers [6, 9], and 1064 nm Nd: YAG lasers [10]. While these methods have proven effective, side effects such as pain, scarring, atrophy, pigmentary changes, and local recurrence are not uncommon. Treatment challenges arise due to patient age, lesion location, and the inherent invasiveness of some techniques. Pulsed dye lasers (PDL), which operate based on selective photothermolysis, use wavelengths of 585 nm and 595 nm and are widely employed in the treatment of vascular abnormalities [11]. The efficacy and safety of 585 nm PDL for treating SN have been well established. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical efficacy, safety, and recurrence of 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes in treating SN.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

A total of 452 children (225 males and 227 females) admitted to the dermatology department of Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital from June 2016 to June 2025 were included in this study. All participants received treatment with multiple laser spot sizes therapy with informed consent from their families. Lesion locations were categorized into scalp, face, neck, and other body parts. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Chinese Han population; (2) healthy children with no history of liver or other systemic diseases; (3) no history of prior treatment; and (4) age between 0 and 15 years. The Anhui Provincial Children’s Hospital’s Ethical Committee examined and approved the study protocol (reference number: ETLL-2021-022). Prior to treatment, all the patient’s guardians gave written, informed consent after being briefed on the procedure’s advantages and hazards.

Laser and technique

Instrument

The thermal relaxation time (TRT) of blood vessels is calculated using the formula TRT ≈ d²/24ɑ, where d represents the vessel diameter and α denotes the thermal diffusivity. SN exhibit vessel diameters of approximately 0.1–0.5 mm, with pediatric patients typically having smaller vessel diameters. Calculations based on this formula indicate a corresponding thermal relaxation time of approximately 4.2ms. Therefore, we selected a more reasonable and safer 3 ms pulse duration. The treatment device was a Candela PDL (V-beam Perfecta, Candela Corporation, Wayland, MA, USA), with the following parameters: 595 nm wavelength, 3 ms pulse duration, spot sizes of 3, 7, and 10 mm, and energy fluence at 3 mm spot size: 21–23 J/cm², energy fluence at 7 mm and 10 mm spot sizes: 7–10 J/cm². No anesthesia or sedation was required. The dynamic cooling device was set to a spray duration of 30 ms and a delay of 20 ms.

Treatment procedure

Before treatment, parents were informed of the potential clinical efficacy, precautions, adverse effects, and the possibility of recurrence, and they provided informed consent. A digital camera (EOS750D, Canon, Japan) with consistent angle, illumination, and parameters was used to take pictures for archiving and evaluating treatment efficacy at each follow-up. The treatment was performed by the same doctor, with the child wearing a special eye mask and the operator using a filter. Step 1: A 3-mm spot was applied vertically at the center of the spider nevus (central artery) with an energy fluence of 21–23 J/cm² and a pulse duration of 3 ms. The treatment was stopped once the surrounding radial branching vessels disappeared, even in the absence of the clinical endpoint. Step 2: For remaining radial branching vessels, for diameters <7 mm, select 7 mm spot size; for diameters ≥7 mm, select 10 mm spot size, with an energy fluence of 7–10 J/cm² and a pulse duration of 3ms. The clinical endpoint was a purplish-red or greyish-white appearance of the lesion. Immediately after the procedure, ice packs were applied intermittently for 10 min, and erythromycin ointment was applied twice daily for 5–7 days to prevent local infection. Patients were instructed to avoid prolonged sun exposure to prevent hyperpigmentation and to refrain from scratching the affected area. Patients whose lesions had not completely disappeared after 6 weeks were treated again until complete resolution. Follow-up occurred at 6 months.

Assessment of efficacy and adverse reactions

Efficacy and adverse effects were assessed by two consultant dermatologists who were unaware of the study. Pre- and post-treatment photographs were compared to evaluate outcomes. Efficacy was classified as follows: Cure: complete disappearance of dilated blood vessels (Fig. 1); Improvement: partial disappearance of dilated blood vessels; Ineffective: no change in dilated blood vessels. Adverse effects were monitored and included local edematous erythema, hyperpigmentation, hypopigmentation, crusting, blistering, infection, and scarring.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27 software. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s) if normally distributed. Count data were expressed as rates (%). Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact probability method. Multifactor logistic regression was used for multiple independent variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 452 patients were included in this study, with the following baseline characteristics (Table 1): The total number of lesions was 508. Among the patients, 225 (44.29%) were male and 227 (44.69%) were female. The age distribution was as follows: 0–4 years: 87 (17.13%); 5–9 years: 338 (66.54%); 10–15 years: 83 (16.34%). The age of onset was: 0–4 years: 188 (37.01%); 5–9 years: 278 (54.72%); 10–15 years: 42 (8.27%). The duration of lesions was: 0–12 months: 306 (60.24%); 13–24 months: 116 (22.83%); 24–86 months: 86 (16.93%). The number of lesions per patient ranged from 1 to 4, with 408 (90.27%) having a single lesion, 33 (7.30%) having two lesions, 10 (2.21%) having three lesions, and 1 (0.22%) having four lesions. The distribution of lesions by location was as follows: 15 (2.95%) on the scalp, 462 (90.94%) on the face, 4 (0.79%) on the neck, and 27 (5.31%) on other areas of the body. We used the maximum length of radial branching vessels to describe the size of lesion. Regarding lesion size, 237 (46.65%) lesions were ≤ 9 mm in diameter, 220 (43.31%) were between 10 mm and 19 mm, and 51 (10.04%) were ≥ 20 mm.

Curative effect and correlative factors

All 452 patients, comprising a total of 508 lesions, were cured with 595 nm PDL treatment (Fig. 1). Four lesions recurred after treatment, but re-treatment was effective. The head and face required a higher proportion of multiple treatments compared to other sites, while lesions on the neck and other areas were cured with one treatment (χ² = 7.774E-8, p = 0.001). There was a significant correlation between lesion diameter and the number of treatments; larger lesions required a greater proportion of multiple treatments (χ² = 4.725, p = 0.001). Additionally, more treatments were required during the summer (χ² = 13.233, p = 0.001).

Factors associated with adverse reactions

We analyzed the factors affecting adverse reactions to treatment. A total of 36 (7.08%) patients experienced adverse reactions, including hyperpigmentation in 28 (5.51%), hypopigmentation in 5 (0.98%), and mild depressed scarring in 3 (0.59%). No significant differences were observed between adverse reactions and other associated factors, such as gender, age of onset, lesion duration, location, and season of last treatment. However, lesion size (p = 0.008) and the number of treatments (p < 0.001) were significantly associated with adverse reactions (Table 2).

Discussion

Spider nevus (SN) is an idiopathic capillary dilatation formed by the expansion of the terminal segments of small cutaneous arterial branches. Children are often too young to cooperate with laser treatment, and poorly planned laser treatment can cause significant pain and psychological burden. Therefore, selecting the appropriate timing for treatment is crucial. This study represents the first large-scale investigation and analysis of the characteristics, clinical data, treatment timing, treatment modalities, and prognosis of pediatric spider nevi, aiming to reduce the number of appropriate laser treatments.

In this study, we summarized the basic clinical characteristics of children aged 0–15 years with non-hepatic SN, aiming to gain a deeper understanding of the epidemiological features of spider nevi in children. We found that the female-to-male ratio of SN incidence was close to 1:1, which aligns with previous reports on pediatric cases [4]. The highest prevalence, 54.72%, occurred in the 5–9 years age group. A total of 90.27% of the cases involved single lesions. Most lesions were small, with 46.65% having a diameter of ≤ 9 mm. The majority of lesions were located on the face (90.94%), with the highest proportion on the cheeks (45.47%).

We analyzed the effects and adverse reactions of the 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes for the treatment of SN in children. Additionally, we examined factors influencing lesion size, the number of treatments, and adverse reactions, providing a reference for selecting appropriate treatment plans and timing for pediatric spider nevi. The theory of selective photothermal action serves as the theoretical basis for using 595 nm PDL in treating vascular diseases. The laser energy is selectively absorbed by blood in the vessels, heating the blood until the vessel walls are destroyed, ultimately blocking the affected vessels and treating vasculoproliferative lesions [12]. The 595 nm PDL causes minimal damage to surrounding dermal structures and the epidermis, making it an ideal method for treating superficial vasculoproliferative diseases [13]. The target vessel depth for SN is approximately 300 μm [14]. Compared to the 585 nm PDL, the 595 nm PDL has the advantage of a longer wavelength, allowing for therapeutic penetration to a depth of up to 1.5 mm [15]. Since SN consists of a small central artery and radially arranged fine vessels, with dilated vessels around the central artery transiently disappearing on physical examination, laser treatment targeting the central artery alone should theoretically be equally effective. Haywood reported using 2 mm and 7 mm spot sizes of the 585 nm PDL to treat multiple spider nevi on a patient’s body. 107 lesions on the left side of the body were treated with the 2 mm spot size, while 104 lesions on the right side were treated with the 7 mm spot size, showing no statistical difference between the two treatments [16]. This opens the possibility of combining small and large light spots for spider nevus treatment in practice. Previously, when PDL was used to treat spider nevi, either a single large or small spot size was chosen based on the lesion’s diameter. In the present study, we used a combination of 3-mm small spot sizes with high energy to target small central arteries and 7-mm and 10-mm large spot sizes to treat the radial fine blood vessels. This combination made it easier for operators to select parameters and perform the procedure. The small spot size with high energy takes advantage of the Gaussian distribution of the laser beam [17], concentrating the energy at the center of the spot and optimizing the selective photothermal effect. After coagulating the central small arteries with heat, blood flow in the branch arteries slows down, which facilitates the subsequent treatment with larger spot sizes. The intravascular hemoglobin absorbs the laser energy and is uniformly heated to close the blood vessels, ensuring the repeatability of the treatment effect and the operation.

A total of 452 children aged 0–15 years, with the number of lesions ranging from 1 to 4, totaling 508 lesions, were included in this study. All patients were cured after combined treatment with small and large photofacials. Lesions on the head and face were larger in diameter compared to those on the trunk and limbs, requiring more treatments (χ² = 7.774E-8, p = 0.001). This may be attributed to the denser distribution of blood vessels in the head and face, with larger vessel calibers and faster blood flow rates, affecting energy absorption. The larger the lesion diameter, the higher the proportion requiring multiple treatments (χ² = 4.725, p = 0.001). Larger diameters result in greater blood flow in the small central arteries, faster circulation, and wider radiation. As the laser energy is absorbed, more heat is carried away by the rapid blood flow, impacting the heating of intravascular hemoglobin and the conduction of heat to the vessel wall, thus affecting treatment efficacy. Children treated during the summer months required more treatments (χ² = 13.233, p = 0.001), likely due to the high temperatures and vasodilatation associated with summer. Therefore, we recommend treatment during the autumn and winter for a higher one-time cure rate, as well as for postoperative care. Interestingly, our study found that lesion duration had no effect on lesion size, providing a valuable reference for selecting the optimal treatment timing. Furthermore, younger children often do not cooperate well, so it is beneficial to treat them when they are more cooperative, as explained to parents.

The SN clearance rate in children treated with 595 nm PDL and multiple laser spot sizes was 100%. Patients with lesions on the face, larger lesion diameters, and those treated in the summer typically required multiple treatments. In conclusion, 595 nm PDL with multiple laser spot sizes is an effective treatment for SN in children, offering high clearance rates, easy operation, mild pain, short treatment times, low recurrence rates, and no scar formation, making it worthy of clinical promotion and application.
