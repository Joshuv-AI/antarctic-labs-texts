Digital diagnostics, biomarkers and therapeutics in an evolving healthcare system: From promise to practice
Authors: Mees H. P. Stoop, Gertjan J. A. Driessen, Adam F. Cohen, Matthijs D. Kruizinga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHealth care is shifting towards a digital‐guided system, integrating digital diagnostics, biomarkers and therapeutics in many care pathways. However, despite rapid technological advancement and preliminary adoption accelerated by the COVID‐19 pandemic, a significant implementation gap persists. This narrative review explores the causes of this gap, highlighting several examples from early development to final implementation. These show that technical validation alone is insufficient. Success depends on alignment with clinical need, robust external validation, patient empowerment and sustainable funding models. Furthermore, other systemic barriers include data privacy concerns and lack of transparency by commercial companies. To improve adoption and translate promise to practice, more international alignment of regulations and international collaboration is needed. Lessons must be learned from promising initiatives that did not reach clinical care, as much as from their successful counterparts. Ultimately, a comprehensive redesign of healthcare will be undertaken, with digital diagnostics and therapeutics both embedded as critical components rather than add‐ons. This demands a multi‐stakeholder effort involving governments, regulatory bodies, insurance providers, industry, research funders, hospital leadership, clinicians and, most importantly, patients.

INTRODUCTION

Clinical medicine tends to be conservative in adopting new diagnostic and therapeutic strategies due to the inherent complexity and risks of healthcare. However, in recent years, there has been a growing recognition of the need for a shift towards a more digital‐guided healthcare system. The integration of digital diagnostics, biomarkers and therapeutics has the potential to significantly enhance the precision, efficiency, personalization and accessibility of care. This transition is not only inevitable but also essential in addressing the evolving needs of patients and healthcare systems and the general scarcity of (human) resources. However, the development and implementation of digital tools remain challenging and time consuming. Therefore, more tools are being developed than implemented, leading to a so called ‘implementation gap’. In this narrative review, we explore the current advancements, challenges and opportunities associated with digital diagnostics and digital therapeutics, as part of the broader trend of eHealth adoption.

HISTORY OF EHEALTH AND TELEMEDICINE

The first documented type of eHealth (telehealth) dates back to 1897. During a phone call, a physician diagnosed a child with ‘croup’ based on the typical cough. 1 After this promising start, significant progress was not made until the 1960s, when telephone and radio technologies were more widely accessible and implemented in healthcare to access remote populations. 2 , 3 The concept of ‘telemedicine’ emerged in Pubmed for the first time in the 1990s, initially referring to the digitization of health information. 4 The emergence of the World Wide Web in 1991, along with advances in mobile technologies, further accelerated telehealth. This enabled video consultations, remote monitoring and data exchange, 5 although this was not implemented until the 2000s. Healthcare providers continued to play a central role in patient interactions, with their expertise informing diagnostics and treatment plans. The doctor–patient relationship largely retained its traditional, hands‐on structure.

In 2004, the European Commission published its first eHealth Action Plan. 6 This laid the groundwork for the emergence of more eHealth initiatives. In the years that followed, initiatives increasingly focused on developing eHealth systems designed to supplement, rather than replace, the advice and treatment provided by healthcare professionals. These platforms allowed healthcare providers to track patients' progress between visits, adjust treatment plans accordingly and intervene earlier if necessary. Similarly, mHealth apps began to empower patients with tools to manage their health, while still relying on healthcare providers for expert guidance and diagnosis. This approach enhanced the accessibility and personalization of care, without compromising the critical role of the clinician in decision‐making.

In 2020, the COVID‐19 pandemic acted as a catalyst for quick development of digital medicine. Lockdowns and social distancing demonstrated the necessity to administer healthcare from a distance to maintain continuity of care without physical contact. 7 This rising interest is reflected in the sharp increase in digital health publications during the pandemic (Figure 1). This shift, born of necessity, showed the potential of digital medicine but also exposed key challenges such as digital inequality, data privacy concerns and the need for rapid regulatory adaptation.

DIGITAL DIAGNOSTICS, BIOMARKERS AND THERAPEUTICS

Digital health technologies are developed, studied and integrated across various healthcare domains. Digital diagnostics use technology to support the identification or confirmation of diseases, partially or fully replacing the traditional role of a healthcare professional. 13 Digital biomarkers conceptually overlap with diagnostics but have a broader scope, encompassing not only diagnosis but also monitoring and treatment evaluation. 12 DTx are designed to provide interventions that can assist or partially replace healthcare providers. 14 The possibilities of these eHealth tools are diverse and could influence the current healthcare system across various medical specialties. EHEALTH IN THE DEVELOPMENT PHASE

Each year, the number of studies using machine learning (ML) to predict therapy efficacy, side effects and disease progression is increasing. 49 For example, machine‐ and deep‐learning models are being developed to predict efficacy of immunotherapy in skin 50 and lung cancer. 51 These models seem to outperform traditional decision‐making strategies, enabling more personalized medicine. However, most models never progress to prospective patient studies. ML models are often trained and tested on retrospective data, which is susceptible to selection bias. A recent systematic review showed a high risk of bias in 79% of the externally and in 88% of the internally validated models. 52 This risk was due to relatively small sample size, risk of overfitting (not generalizable to the entire patient population), unclear handling of missing data and undefined inclusion criteria. Additionally, these studies often involve relatively homogeneous patient populations, with underrepresented groups often being excluded. This limits the generalizability of the model. 53 , 54 Prediction models should therefore be conducted in (inter)national collaboration, with clear defined inclusion and exclusion criteria to enhance model generalizability. Another challenge arises when decision‐making models are applied to costly or potentially life‐saving treatments, raising ethical concerns. 55

Predictive models may have the potential to accurately predict disease progression, adverse effects and treatment response, but bias, limited representativeness and ethical considerations remain important barriers to clinical implementation.

FIRST PILOT STUDIES

After the development phase, most digital tools enter the pilot phase aimed at evaluating the feasibility, methodology and logistical aspects of the innovations. Despite promising pilot results, the vast majority of digital innovations do not progress to larger randomized controlled trials or clinical implementation. 56 Several factors contribute to this discontinuation including lack of funding, insufficient clinical relevance, technological limitations, regulatory barriers and a lack of demonstrable added value over existing standards of care.

An example of an initially promising digital diagnostic that did not progress beyond the pilot phase is the Cyrcadia Breast Monitor. This device uses thermodynamic data to detect heat patterns associated with breast cancer. Initial results showed 78% accuracy using this technique to recognize patients with breast cancer. 57 However, despite plans for further validation studies, the technology never progressed to robust validation. This accuracy level falls well below the thresholds required for breast cancer screening or diagnosis, resulting in high rates of both false positive and false negative results. Such performance undermines clinical reliability and carries a substantial risk of patient harm, including psychological distress, unnecessary diagnostic procedures and potential delays in appropriate care. In addition, the intended implementation pathway remained unclear, as it was uncertain whether the device should be offered directly to consumers or integrated into hospital care, further complicating regulatory approval and clinical responsibility.

This example demonstrates that although digital health innovations may demonstrate technical feasibility and initial promise, they may fail to provide meaningful benefit to patients. Without clear benefit, these tools risk being ineffective in practice and may even cause unintended harm. THE GAP BETWEEN CLINICAL VALIDATION AND INTEGRATION

Once a digital health tool has undergone clinical validation and secured the necessary regulatory approvals (CE‐marked or FDA‐approved), it enters a new phase in its pathway. At this stage, the key challenge is no longer development or validation but successful implementation into routine healthcare.

The use of home spirometry in pulmonary diseases depicts how clinical validation does not necessarily translate into sustainable clinical implementation. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, a shift towards remote healthcare contributed to the interest in home spirometry to replace clinical spirometry. Numerous pilot studies subsequently evaluated the use of home spirometry, initially reporting it as a feasible and reliable tool to monitor disease activity. 67 , 68 , 69 However, later studies revealed that in long‐term use, adherence rates declined and measurement reliability reduced compared with clinical spirometry. 70 , 71 , 72 Additionally, patients experienced emotional burden and uncertainty in result interpretation as key concerns. 73 Although home spirometry had shown promise in pilot studies, challenges such as declining adherence, reduced reliability and patient burden prevent the successful implementation into routine care.

Even after successful local implementation, scaling the digital health tool beyond local healthcare systems entails boundaries. An example that illustrates the difficulty of international implementation is the use of AI to assist breast cancer screening. 74 A large randomized controlled trial in Sweden, including over 100 000 women, showed that the use of AI software in mammography increased breast cancer detection by 29% while reducing reading time by 44%. Consequently, Sweden introduced AI as a replacement for the second radiologist to screen for breast cancer in daily clinical practice. 75 Although the trial demonstrates the potential of AI‐assisted breast cancer screening, broader implementation across diverse healthcare systems is complicated due to limited external validation of algorithms, differences in regulatory framework and the need for professional acceptance. 76 , 77 Therefore, large trials have to be conducted to replicate findings in the targeted population, a process that substantially delays approval and implementation.

Even after the first validation and implementation, not all initially promising digital tools achieve long‐term success. Pear Therapeutics, for instance, developed applications for substance use disorders (reSET and reSET‐O) as well as mood and anxiety disorders. 78 After FDA‐approval of reSET in 2017, Pear therapeutics was one of the frontiers in digital medicine. However, the company filed for bankruptcy in 2023, before the applications achieved widespread integration into clinical workflows. The bankruptcy was a consequence of the difficulty of securing reimbursement pathways, which limited revenue generation despite regulatory approvals. 79

Collectively, these cases underscore that successful implementation requires more than technical validation. It demands alignment with clinical practice, patient needs, external validation and sustainable reimbursement pathways to enable long‐term integration into routine care.

SUCCESSFULLY IMPLEMENTED DIGITAL TOOLS

In contrast to the previous examples, many devices are already used in daily practice. One of these examples is the AI diagnostic system Deep Ensemble for Recognition of Malignancy (DERM). DERM has already been used in 220.000 patients in 24 NHS hospitals to triage patients referred for suspected skin cancer. 80 Using a smartphone equipped with a magnifying lens, this system can rapidly assess suspicious moles or lesions. The AI system classifies the skin lesion images into benign, uncertain or malignant categories, determining whether patients are discharged, reviewed or urgently referred. Development of DERM began in 2013, and after nearly a decade of refinement and validation, it obtained UKCA Class IIa (regulatory approval for a medical device in the United Kingdom) certification in 2022, marking a step from research to regulated clinical use. This example illustrates the growing potential of digital diagnostics to enhance efficiency and quality of care. DERM has the support of the NHS system enabling real‐world effectivity studies and stimulating integration into routine healthcare pathways. Additionally, DERM imposes no additional measurement or documentation burden on clinicians and instead streamlines workflow, ultimately saving time in routine care.

Another promising area is the use of digital biomarkers in progressive neurological disorders. 81 , 82 Digital tools, like movement detectors, smartwatches and smartphones potentially assist in diagnosing Parkinson's disease even in the prodromal phase. They may also support the detection of disease progression or to evaluate treatment, which is particularly valuable for medication with a narrow, person‐specific therapeutic window. 83 , 84 , 85 The Parkinson's KinetiGraph (PKG) watch provides a concrete example of a digital biomarker already influencing clinical decision‐making. Development of this watch began in 2006, and it received FDA approval in 2016. Since then, the watch is used in 21 different countries to continuously monitor patients' movements to optimize treatment titration. 86 Its successful implementation in clinical practice is the result of its direct utility for patients and caregivers, as continuous remote monitoring allows clinicians to make timely and actionable medication adjustments. 87

Digital therapeutics also continue to emerge, particularly in psychiatry. Priovi, for instance, is a mobile‐based digital therapeutic for adults with borderline personality disorder. A recent randomized controlled trial in Germany demonstrated significant reductions in borderline personality disorder symptoms, highlighting its potential as an adjunct to traditional therapy. 88 Priovi is CE‐marked and available in German clinical centres. Importantly, it is officially listed as a reimbursable digital health application in Germany. Therefore, Priovi can be prescribed by physicians and psychotherapist. This distinguishes Priovi from many other digital health innovations, where lack of reimbursement often limits widespread adoption and patient access, highlighting reimbursement as a crucial factor for successful implementation in routine care.

SYSTEMIC BARRIERS

As seen with the previous examples, digital diagnostics, biomarkers and therapeutics carry the potential for a futureproof model for healthcare delivery. With fewer resources and limited capacity to provide all diagnostics, monitoring and treatments within hospital walls, there is a growing need to shift parts of the care pathway into the community and home settings. However, despite numerous initiatives in the past 25 years, many barriers in health care systems remain in place. Thus far, we focused on individual examples of digital interventions highlighting challenges at the project level. The following section of this review adopts a broader perspective, focusing on systemic barriers to implementation that affect digital health more widely.

Reimbursement strategies

Traditional reimbursement strategies pose a challenge for the eHealth industry. Healthcare reimbursement systems remain volume‐based rather than value‐based. Digital health solutions often reduce in‐person visits and hospital admissions, leading to lower billable activities. As a result, healthcare providers face financial disincentives to adopt digital care, even when it improves efficiency and patient outcomes. After approval, traditional reimbursement structures can hinder broader implementation and sustainability, as was observed with PEAR Therapeutics. Without adapted reimbursement mechanisms, eHealth innovations struggle to achieve sustainable integration into routine care resulting in an implementation gap. 89 Table 3 provides several examples of award‐winning companies who discontinued, with reimbursement challenges being one of the most prominent contributing factors. In the United Kingdom, initiatives like the DERM application are supported and reimbursed by the NHS, providing a strong initial incentive for development and evaluation.

Safety and privacy

Patient safety concerns remain a barrier to the implementation of digital health tools. Algorithms are trained and validated using large datasets or patient trials that often reflect homogeneous patient populations, underrepresented groups are frequently excluded. Therefore, the given dataset to train and validate algorithms is often not representable for the entire population. 53 , 54 This can cause underdiagnoses or misclassify conditions outside its training scope, resulting in patient harm. 90

The process of data collection further raises significant privacy and security concerns that are intrinsic to the adoption of digital healthcare systems. 91 Patient files, names and sensitive health information were hacked or stolen affecting more than 250 000 000 individuals in 2024. 92 The involvement of multiple external stakeholders with their own privacy regulations amplifies these challenges. 93 Consequently, although digital innovations offer significant promise, patients and care providers are often unaware of the safety and privacy risks inherent in their use. This highlights the need for mitigation via institutional policies. This should be complemented by structural support and targeted education for researchers and healthcare professionals, enabling them to adequately identify, manage and address privacy and safety concerns.

Regulatory frameworks

Another important barrier to widespread integration is the diversity in international rules and regulations. Regulatory pathways remain fragmented across countries, and approval processes often lag behind technological innovation. The differences in approval processes complicate and delay international collaboration and cross‐border implementation. International alignment is essential to enable international cooperation. The European Health Data Space (EHDS) regulation and European AI act are promising initiatives to promote the international development and implementation of digital interventions. Ideally, such initiatives should extend beyond the European Union to encompass broader international collaboration. 97 , 98

Lack of transparency and collaboration

The limited availability of open‐access evidence poses an additional problem to widespread adoption of digital health. Many technologies and AI algorithms are developed within research teams that do not disclose their underlying methods. This lack of transparency hampers independent validation and restricts reliable use of innovations in diverse patient populations.

Most research is currently conducted within national or regional teams, often leading to unnecessary duplication. 99 To create transparency and promote international collaboration, a centralized non‐profit, modular eHealth platform could be introduced. This platform could serve as a catalyst for international collaboration. By streamlining access to shared resources and insights, it would lower barriers for developers, clinicians and researchers. 100 The necessary legal frameworks for international collaboration and data sharing should be provided, reducing the need for clinicians to navigate the complex regulatory landscapes.

Lack of patient empowerment

Digital innovations may transform a clinician‐led healthcare model in a more collaborative and patient centred approach. For example, remote healthcare facilitated by wearables and mobile health applications enhances patients' insight into their own health data and enables patients to take a more active role in managing their health. This may lead to sense of autonomy and responsibility often referred to as patient empowerment. 101 , 102

It is important that patients are well informed and understand the process behind digital interventions. Although digital health promotes patients' autonomy, it also reduces the frequency of direct clinician‐patient contact. Patients may delay seeking medical attention, misinterpret symptoms or overestimate their ability to interpret the outcomes using digital healthcare. The shift in responsibility assumes a level of health literacy and digital competence not all patients possess.

Thus, patient empowerment is a key factor in the development of digital health solutions. Actively involving patients in the design and implementation of these tools can enhance adherence to treatment and enable personalized medicine tailored to individual needs. Interventions should only be initiated when patients fully support their use, ensuring they can engage with the technology responsibly and derive maximum benefit from its application. 103

Adoption of value‐based healthcare principles

Aligning the development of digital tools with the principles of value‐based healthcare can further enhance their impact. Digital innovations are more successfully adopted when they support outcomes that patients themselves find meaningful, such as quality of life, functional improvement and overall well‐being. By ensuring that digital solutions contribute to what patients value most, their implementation becomes more sustainable and truly adds value to the patient experience. 104

It should be noted that commercial eHealth companies may prioritize profit motives, which can sometimes shift focus away from true health outcomes. As a result, these companies may prioritize traditional endpoints, such as clinical measures or standardized questionnaires, rather than evaluating whether the technology actually makes a meaningful difference in patients' lives. Although many pilot studies focus on these traditional endpoints, the long‐term health benefits are essential for digital tools to be successfully integrated into routine care.

THE WAY FORWARD—FROM BENCH TO BEDSIDE

The ultimate goal of developing digital diagnostics, monitoring and therapeutics is their widespread implementation within routine digital care. Successful implementation of digital health tools requires not only technical validation and regulatory approval but also integration into clinical workflows, user acceptance, reimbursement pathways and demonstrable patient benefit. Failure to address these factors can significantly hinder adoption, even for clinically effective innovations. Table 5 summarizes the facilitators and barriers of successfully implementing digital health innovations. Despite numerous promising initiatives, implementation remains limited due to the challenges outlined above. Although progress is being made, true transformation will require more than isolated innovation. Ideally, a comprehensive redesign of healthcare systems should be undertaken, with digital diagnostics and therapeutics both embedded as foundational components rather than add‐ons. This demands a coordinated, multi‐stakeholder effort involving governments, regulatory bodies, insurance providers, industry, research funders, hospital leadership, clinicians and, most importantly, patients. It calls for new reimbursement models that recognize the value of digital interventions, socially aware digital health companies that put patient care first, regulatory frameworks that accommodate (adaptive) algorithms, clinicians who are ready to innovate their practice and electronic health records that integrate real‐time data from patients' daily lives. Equally important is ensuring access, digital literacy and trust in these technologies across diverse populations. Only through such a concerted and inclusive approach can digital health tools move from pilot projects to scalable, sustainable solutions to improve outcomes, reduce costs and empower patients in their own care.

All data are automatically uploaded, analysed and summarized in a secure digital dashboard accessible to the pulmonologist. Among the findings, a dry cough pattern consistent with asthma is detected, and spirometry result was accurate and support the diagnosis. Additionally, the patient's physical activity is significantly below average for their demographic, indicating substantial impairment in daily functioning.

By the time of the first in‐person visit, the diagnosis of asthma is confirmed. The consultation focuses immediately on shared decision‐making and the development of a personalized treatment and monitoring plan. Given the severity of symptoms, the patient is started on a combination of a long‐acting beta‐agonist and high‐dose inhaled corticosteroids, with a plan to taper treatment as symptoms improve. Cough is identified as the patient's most prominent symptom and is used as a personal biomarker to monitor treatment response. A tailored algorithm is implemented to detect deviations from the patient's baseline cough pattern. If abnormalities are detected, the system advises the patient to initiate a short‐acting beta‐agonist and automatically notifies the pulmonary care team. If symptoms persist, the team proactively reaches out to adjust the treatment plan. Between exacerbations, the patient can schedule virtual consultations with the pulmonologist through the digital platform.

Monthly, the pulmonary care team reviews aggregated data from all patients enrolled in the digital asthma programme. This month, 5% of patients are flagged as having uncontrolled asthma—an increase from 2% the previous month. The system generates hypotheses based on environmental and behavioural data. Elevated pollen levels in certain neighbourhoods are identified as a contributing factor, whereas a subgroup of patients shows reduced medication adherence. This subgroup receives a targeted questionnaire to explore barriers to adherence, and follow‐up appointments are scheduled to revise their personalized care plans.

From PK/PD to digital dosing: translating pharmacology to digital medicine

Although pharmacologists are not traditionally involved in the development of digital health systems, many concepts taught in pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamics (PD) can be translated to digital health. DTx and traditional pharmacotherapy have a common goal: to deliver medical benefit. Yet, unlike traditional compounds, DTx delivers therapeutic effects without an active substance in the systemic circulation. Still, the similarities between traditional pharmacology to digital medicine are numerous. The dose administered can be described as the digital dose or the amount of digital interventions. The exposure can be defined as the cumulative therapeutic exposure or total user engagement with a digital intervention over time. The resulting response can be measured using digital biomarkers, comparable to traditional pharmacodynamics. Digital biomarkers provide objective, measurable indicators of underlying physiological processes and treatment response. The digital therapeutic window then can be defined as the optimal frequency or intensity of digital interventions for maximal effectiveness without overload or fatigue. These concepts open the door for pharmacometric modelling based on digital engagement and outcomes, as demonstrated in studies linking higher digital therapeutic ‘doses’ to improved adherence and clinical results. 105 These approaches are very similar to core clinical pharmacology principles: exposure‐response relationships, dose optimization and safety monitoring.
