The osteogenic effect of 3D-printed bone grafting guides in PAOO: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Han Bao, Yiran Luo, Qingfei He, Yan Jiang, Ling Chen, Yun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) effectively increases alveolar bone volume and broadens orthodontic treatment options. However, bone grafting during PAOO faces challenges such as graft displacement, membrane collapse, and insufficient coronal bone regeneration. This study aimed to evaluate whether 3D-printed digital bone grafting guides could improve graft stability and alveolar bone regeneration compared with conventional PAOO.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 10 patients who underwent PAOO surgery were evaluated. Patients were divided equally into GBG (digital guided bone grafting) and Control groups (conventional PAOO). Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images and periodontal clinical parameters, including probing depth (PD), attachment loss (AL), keratinized gingival width (KW), and gingival biotype, were assessed preoperatively (T0), immediately postoperatively (T1), and at 12 months follow-up (T2). Changes in alveolar bone morphology were analyzed using Mimics software, with data statistically compared using SPSS 25.0.ResultsImmediately postoperatively, the GBG group exhibited significantly greater vertical bone augmentation and horizontal bone augmentation in crown and mid-root areas compared to the control group ( < 0.05). At the 12-months follow-up, GBG maintained significantly superior vertical bone regeneration and coronal horizontal bone volume, particularly in the coronal third of the alveolar bone ( < 0.05). Both groups demonstrated significant improvements in KW compared to baseline ( < 0.05), while PD and AL showed no significant intergroup differences.ConclusionsThe application of 3D-printed bone grafting guides significantly improves graft stability, reduces postoperative bone resorption, and enhances coronal alveolar bone regeneration after PAOO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06753-1.Keywords: Bone grafting guides, Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics, Bone regeneration

Background

With the increasing demand for adult orthodontics, the need for fast, aesthetic and functional treatment is growing. However, adults respond much more slowly to orthodontic forces than younger patients, and the average duration of orthodontic treatment is much longer than in children and adolescents [1]. However, the longer the duration of orthodontic treatment, the greater the risk of potentially harmful effects such as tooth demineralization, tooth decay, root resorption and gum inflammation [2]. To some extent, prolonged tooth movement has limited the ability to provide appropriate treatment procedures for many adults seeking orthodontic treatment. In this context, Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics (PAOO) is gaining attention as a technique that not only accelerates orthodontic tooth movement but also improves the alveolar bone the utilization of bone grafting, thus expanding the indications for orthodontic treatment [3–5]. PAOO is a distinctive orthodontic approach that diverges from conventional methods. It integrates clinical periodontal treatment with orthodontic movement, encompassing procedures such as selective cortical osteotomy, bone grafting, and the application of orthodontic forces. The method is predicated on the principle of regional acceleratory phenomenon (RAP), whereby a transient state of increased bone metabolism and decreased bone mass offers the possibility of accelerated tooth movement [6, 7]. A substantial body of research has demonstrated that PAOO possesses considerable benefits in comparison to conventional orthodontic techniques in a number of aspects, including:1) The accelerated movement of teeth, with a range of three to four times faster than conventional orthodontic treatment, is a notable benefit. 2) This increased velocity enables a wider range of treatment options, leading to reduced treatment time. 3) The process of alveolar bone augmentation is enhanced, resulting in an increased range of motion for the teeth. 4) The treatment also reduces root resorption, contributing to the overall effectiveness of the procedure. Notably, these outcomes are maintained for a period of at least ten years [8–10].

Conversely, clinical manifestations such as bone dehiscence and bone fenestration has been documented in the existing literature pertaining to alveolar bone defects [11, 12]. The prevalence of mandibular anterior bone defects was found to be higher in patients with Class II and Class III malocclusions, with prevalence rates of up to 41.11% and 45.02%, respectively [13]. The presence of such defects during orthodontic treatment frequently results in root exposure, gingival recession, and in some cases, orthodontic relapse or failure. This poses a significant challenge to the successful completion of orthodontic treatment [14–16]. It is imperative to ascertain the most effective methods by which to optimize the alveolar bone conditions and enhance the stability of bone grafts prior to orthodontic treatment, thereby ensuring the optimal execution of the PAOO procedure.

The process of alveolar bone regeneration in PAOO is a multifaceted procedure that is achieved through a combination of three distinct surgical techniques. These techniques include selective alveolar cortical osteotomy, granular bone grafting, and postoperative orthodontic treatment [17, 18]. Bone grafting is a pivotal component in this field. However, challenges such as bone graft displacement, overlying membrane collapse, and insufficient bone formation pose significant risks to the long-term stability of PAOO bone regeneration [19]. Some studies on conventional PAOO have demonstrated that the regenerative capacity of the alveolar bone is predominantly concentrated in the mid-root and apical regions. In contrast, the regenerative effect of the coronal side is less pronounced during the 6-month follow-up period following the PAOO procedure. This phenomenon may be attributed to the displacement of the bone grafts and the subsequent collapse of the overlaying membrane [20, 21].

It is evident that achieving bone regeneration in the coronal and vertical alveolar directions of the root during conventional PAOO procedures poses a considerable challenge. To solve these problems, methods for membrane fixation have been discussed in a number of studies. For example, Kim et al. utilized temporary skeletal anchorages to achieve the fixation of the absorbable membrane [22]. In his study, a secondary surgery was required to retrieve these fixation materials. Ma et al. introduced a dumpling technique, in which the graft was fixed to the periosteum for stability [23]; Liu et al. designed a ‘sack-shaped’ suturing technique, which reduces graft displacement by suturing the collagen membrane to the underlying periosteum [19]. Additionally, Brugnami F et al. developed an improved tunneling technique, a flapless procedure that is more minimally invasive compared to conventional PAOO, achieving favorable coronal alveolar bone regeneration [24]. Although these improved techniques offer certain advantages, they all have issues such as high technical sensitivity. To overcome these limitations, the present study employed a digital guide-assisted bone grafting strategy to improve graft stability and optimize bone regeneration, with a comparative analysis against the conventional bone grafting approach. The present retrospective clinical study was conducted with the objective of evaluating the osteogenic effect of digital guide-guided bone grafting in the immediate postoperative period and one year postoperatively after PAOO. The study is intended to provide a more evidence-based value for future clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-center retrospective study included 10 patients who were referred to the Stomatological Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between December 2022 and December 2024 for PAOO surgery. The subjects were divided into two groups: the first group (n = 5) underwent transplantation using digital guided bone grafting (GBG group), while the second group (n = 5) underwent transplantation using conventional PAOO surgery (control group). Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) preoperative orthodontic decompensation for Class III malocclusion, and (3) CBCT confirmed thin labial bone plate with bone fenestration or dehiscence in the lower anterior region. Exclusion criteria included: (1) prior orthodontic or orthognathic treatment, (2) active periodontal disease, (3) current medication causing gingival enlargement (e.g., calcium channel blockers), (4) uncontrolled systemic diseases, and (5) medication affecting bone metabolism. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki principles and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (approval number: mydfyllyy [2022] No.721). All participants provided informed consent after receiving comprehensive information regarding the study’s purpose and duration. The flowchart depicting subject recruitment is presented in Fig. 1.

Guide design

GBG group

The digital bone grafting guides used in this study comprised two types: Guide A and Guide B. Guide A was designed to accurately define the optimal bone graft placement area based on the specific location and extent of the alveolar bone defect, thereby minimizing graft material displacement through precise control of graft range and thickness. Guide B was employed to compact and stabilize the bone graft within the defined graft window, ensuring immediate post-operative graft density and stability.

Initially, an accurate digital replica of the patient’s jaw was created using an intraoral scanner. Subsequently, this intraoral digital impression, together with the CBCT data, was imported into specialized dental design software (Exocad Dental CAD, Germany). Within the software, the precise dimensions and position of the bone grafting area were meticulously planned according to each patient’s clinical condition. Specifically, the grafting zone was designed to extend 2 mm towards the root at the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) and 2 mm apically beyond the root apex. Horizontally, it covered the entire buccal region extending from tooth 33 to tooth 43, with a standardized graft thickness of 2 mm. Additionally, a customized in-situ observation window was integrated on the buccal side of Guide A for intraoperative visualization and verification of graft placement accuracy.

Based on the delineated grafting zone, Guide B was subsequently designed. Its configuration required careful attention to ensure adequate thickness, thereby facilitating effective compaction of the graft material during the surgical procedure. The edges of Guide B were specifically contoured for precise adaptation to the graft window, preventing displacement during compaction. Moreover, the central portion of Guide B featured a specially designed handle structure to enhance intraoperative handling, allowing the surgeon to firmly grip and press the guide, thereby achieving optimal graft density and uniformity within the graft area. After finalizing the digital design, both guides were fabricated using advanced 3D printing technology. Finally, their accuracy and fit were verified on physical dental models prior to surgical application (Fig. 2).

Control group

The surgical procedure employed in control group cases were used conventional PAOO grafting procedure, which were carried out without the use of guide plates.

PAOO procedure

All patients received the surgery under local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine by the same periodontist (Y.W.). A horizonsulcular incision was performed along the buccal of teeth 33–43, extending one tooth position distally on both sides. Additionally, vertical releasing incisions were made on the mesial sides of teeth 34 and 44. Subsequently, a full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap was carefully elevated up to the level of the apices of the mandibular anterior teeth, exposing the buccal cortical plates. Particular caution was exercised during flap elevation to protect the mental nerve and associated blood vessels, thereby preventing any inadvertent tissue tearing or perforation [25]. Initially, a cortical bone osteotomy guide was placed to safeguard tooth roots. Nutrient holes were created in the bone cortex between the roots and at the root ends using a ball drill, with the objective of ensuring adequate penetration of the bone cortex to facilitate bleeding and subsequent bone regeneration [26]. Finally, bone graft preparations were completed.

GBG group: The bone graft material (Bio-Oss, Geistlich, Switzerland) was meticulously placed into the grafting window, with the process being conducted under the precise guidance of the bone graft guide (A) The bone graft material was then compacted and shaped using the bone graft guide (B). Following the confirmation of the stability of the bone graft material stacking pattern, the guide was carefully removed.

Control group: Following cortical bone osteotomy, bone graft material was directly applied onto the labial aspect of teeth 33–43 without the assistance of a guide.

After shaping the bone graft material, a resorbable collagen membrane (Bio-Gide, Geistlich, Switzerland) was trimmed to extend approximately 2–3 mm beyond the borders of the graft area in all dimensions. The collagen membrane was then positioned over the graft and fixed securely using a 5 − 0 absorbable suture with a cross-suspension suturing technique to maintain membrane stability. Subsequently, a periosteal releasing incision was performed at the base of the trapezoidal flap to relieve tissue tension. The flap was repositioned passively and sutured in place utilizing a combination of Gottlow mattress sutures and continuous sling suturing techniques (Figs. 3 and 4).

Postoperative management

Postoperatively, patients were routinely prescribed antibiotics and analgesics, along with instructions to use chlorhexidine mouthwash to ensure optimal oral hygiene. They were also advised to refrain from repeated lip pulling. The sutures were removed during a subsequent follow-up appointment, which occurred 14 days after the surgical procedure. Following this, the orthodontic augmentation process was initiated. All subjects were advised to undergo regular clinical examinations and imaging evaluations.

Assessments

To effectively control radiographic bias, all measurements for the participants were conducted by the same well-trained investigator, who remained blinded throughout the measurement process and was completely unaware of the group allocation and surgical procedures. Prior to the study, the investigator underwent self-reproducibility testing, with a Kappa value required to be greater than 0.95 to ensure consistency and reliability of the measurements. All measurement results were derived from the average of repeated measurements to minimize individual measurement errors and ensure the accuracy of the data.

Clinical examination

The following clinical parameters were systematically assessed and recorded during the follow-up period: (1) Probing depth (PD): the distance from the gingival margin to the base of the gingival sulcus; (2) Attachment loss (AL): the distance from the epithelial margin to the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) on the coronal side; (3) Keratinized gingival width (KW): the distance from the base of the free gingival sulcus to the mucogingival junction; (4) Gingival biotype: classified based on gingival transparency when using a periodontal probe. If the probe outline was visible through the gingival margin, the gingival biotype was categorized as thin; otherwise, it was classified as thick.

Radiographic measurements

Radiographic measurements were obtained by standardized CBCT (KaVo Kerr, Germany) using an iCAT scanner in a single 360 rotation with a.

20-s scan time at 110 kV, 0 to 20 mA and a 0.125-mm voxel size. The long axis of the tooth to be measured was rotated to be perpendicular to the horizontal plane. In coronal plane, we adjusted the section so that it passes through the midpoint of the incisal edge or the line connecting the cusp tip and root apex. In horizontal plane, we adjusted the section so that it passes through the most prominent points of the labial and lingual sides. In sagittal plane, we adjusted the section at the maximum labial (lingual) surface of the tooth. We measured: (1) CHBT, measured from the midpoint of the coronal third to the labial cortical surface; (2) MHBT, measured from the midpoint of the middle third to the labial cortical surface; (3) AHBT, measured from the midpoint of the apical third to the labial cortical surface; and (4) VBL, measured from the alveolar crest to the cemento-enamel junction (Fig. 5).

Selection of reference points and definition of measurement items. VBL: Vertical alveolar bone level (distance from the cementoenamel junction to the alveolar crest); CHBT: Horizontal bone thickness at the middle level of the coronal third; MHBT: Horizontal bone thickness at the middle level of the middle third; AHBT: Horizontal bone thickness at the middle level of the apical third

Statistical analysis

This study used SPSS 25.0 statistical software for data processing. First, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to check the normality of the data. Data that followed a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while data that did not follow a normal distribution were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR). Categorical data were presented as frequency (percentage). Data from the two groups of patients at preoperative (T0), immediate postoperative (T1), and one-year postoperative (T2) were analyzed. For continuous variables that followed a normal distribution, an independent samples t-test was used. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Patient characteristics

A total of 10 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in this study, involving 55 teeth. All patients completed the surgical procedure and subsequent follow-up, and no adverse reactions such as infection or material loss occurred post-surgery. Based on the bone grafting technique used, patients were divided into two groups: the digital guided bone grafting (GBG) group and the Control group. Table 1 presents a comparison of the basic demographic characteristics between the two groups. No significant differences were observed in terms of gender, age, height, weight, or BMI ( >0.05). Regarding preoperative periodontal parameters (Table 2), there were no significant differences in PD, AL, or KW between the GBG and CBG groups ( >0.05). Although a significant difference in the distribution of gingival biotype was noted between the groups ( < 0.05), this did not affect the overall comparability of the clinical characteristics. In conclusion, both groups exhibited high consistency in demographic and clinical indicators, providing a solid foundation for comparing the subsequent postoperative outcome and ensuring the validity and fairness of the study results.

Postoperative bone grafting effect comparison

At the immediate postoperative stage and at the one-year follow-up, we assessed and compared the alveolar bone thickness and graft density between the two groups (Table 3). The results indicated that the GBG group showed significant improvements in bone thickness compared to the Control group. Specifically, the vertical bone level change (VBL, T1-T0) in the GBG group was 2.28 mm, significantly higher than the 0.80 mm observed in the Control group ( < 0.001). Additionally, the horizontal bone change at the coronal 1/3 (CHBL, T1-T0), middle root 1/3 (MHBL, T1-T0), and apical 1/3 (AHBL, T1-T0) regions in the GBG group were 1.45 ± 0.56 mm, 2.73 ± 0.71 mm, and 2.91 ± 0.70 mm, respectively, all of which were significantly greater than those in the Control group ( < 0.05). These findings suggest that the use of bone grafting guides significantly enhances bone thickness immediately post-PAOO, particularly in the coronal and middle root regions. Compared to conventional bone grafting, the GBG group demonstrated significantly greater benefits. The one-year follow-up further reinforced these findings, as the GBG group demonstrated significantly superior bone regeneration mark compared to the Control group. Specifically, the GBG group demonstrated more improvements in vertical bone height (VBL, < 0.001), coronal bone regeneration (CHBL, < 0.001), and middle root bone regeneration (MHBL, = 0.007). However, there were no significant differences between the two groups in apical bone regeneration or changes in PD and AL ( >0.05). Overall, the GBG group showed superior efficacy in bone regeneration and maintenance, especially in the coronal region, with similar effects on periodontal health compared to the Control group.

Preoperative and one-year postoperative effect comparison

In the comparison of preoperative and one-year postoperative effects (Tables 4 and 5), both the GBG and Control groups showed similar trends in changes to periodontal parameters. Specifically, one year after PAOO, there were no significant differences between the two groups in PD and AL ( >0.05), but both groups exhibited a significant increase in KW and gingival biotype compared to preoperative values ( < 0.001). Regarding alveolar bone regeneration, the GBG group showed substantial improvement in VBL, CHBL, MHBL, and AHBL ( < 0.001) (Fig. 6). In contrast, the Control group also demonstrated significant bone regeneration in VBL, MHBL, and AHBL ( < 0.001), but the change in CHBL did not reach statistical significance ( = 0.096). Overall, both groups demonstrated significant improvements in alveolar bone height, bone regeneration in the middle and apical root areas, and soft tissue after one year. However, the GBG group exhibited markedly better coronal alveolar bone regeneration compared to the Control group.

Discussion

This study performed a retrospective analysis to compare the bone regeneration outcomes between bone grafting guide-assisted PAOO surgery and conventional PAOO surgery at both the immediate postoperative and one-year follow-up stages. Previous research has indicated that after traditional PAOO surgery, bone regeneration primarily occurs in the middle and apical thirds of the root, while the coronal area is more susceptible to graft material loss and bone resorption, leading to difficulties in maintaining bone volume [27, 28]. However, through the use of bone grafting guides, this study demonstrates that coronal bone regeneration is significantly better preserved at the one-year follow-up in the GBG group compared to the control group. These findings suggest that the bone grafting guide technique has potential clinical value in preventing bone resorption and ensuring long-term bone stability.

The bone grafting guide technique, which is characterized by its precision and controllability, is being utilized with increasing frequency in clinical practice [29–31]. Firstly, the employment of digital technology facilitates the preoperative design of the range and thickness of the bone graft material to be implanted, according to the location of the bone defect and the precision of the bone grafting material. On the basis of this design, the bone grafting guide A with bone grafting window can be produced, thus avoiding the uncertainty of completely hand-stacking the bone grafting material during the operation. This technique has been shown to reduce the sensitivity of the procedure, shorten the surgical time, and reduce the incidence of postoperative adverse reactions. Combined with the use of bone grafting guide B, the bone graft material is fully fused with the blood to form an initial blood clot, which has a solid shape and is not easy to deform, which facilitates space maintenance [32]. Secondly, the use of guide-assisted bone grafting has been demonstrated to optimize the distribution of bone grafting material, thereby facilitating a more homogeneous incorporation into the host bone. This, in turn, results in the creation of a more stable bone-soft tissue interface and a reduction in early resorption. Furthermore, variations in bone density during the immediate postoperative period may also be an important contributing factor to the benefits of bone graft guides [33]. Based on our observation of the immediate postoperative CBCT images of both groups, the GBG group appeared to exhibit higher grayscale values compared to the Control group, especially in the crown region. This phenomenon may be attributed to the utilization of bone graft guide B. Subsequent to the injection of bone grafts into the grafting window, the employment of the guide to compact the bone grafts resulted in denser bone grafts within the graft window. The increased bone grafts density may have furnished superior support for the crown region and diminished bone resorption and loss, thereby engendering a substantial augmentation in bone regeneration in the crown region [34].

In addition to bone regeneration, the improvement of soft tissue is another important consideration following PAOO surgery [35–38]. In this study, while the primary focus was on bone regeneration, improvements in soft tissue were also observed. Changes in gingival biotype and KW were noted in both groups. As demonstrated in the existing literature, there is a robust correlation between PD and the presence of plaque biofilms in the gingival region. Furthermore, there is a positive correlation between KW and the capacity of periodontal tissues to resist inflammatory stimuli [36]. Consequently, enhancement of keratinized gingiva has been shown to augment the resistance of periodontal tissues to inflammation, a development that is particularly advantageous for orthodontic patients afflicted with concomitant oral diseases, such as periodontitis. In this study, it was found that, at one year postoperatively, the mean increase in keratinized gingival width in the GBG group was 1.76 mm ( < 0.001), which was significantly higher than that of the Control group. Moreover, the proportion of thick gingival biotype in the GBG group reached 100% postoperatively, indicating a certain degree of soft tissue improvement. This enhancement may be ascribed to the interplay between bone regeneration and soft tissue. Current research suggests that thick gingival biotype may provide a more favorable osteogenic environment for guided bone regeneration (GBR) by offering a thicker bone foundation, stronger soft tissue support, and a lower risk of complications [39]. Interestingly, the GBG group in this study had a significantly lower proportion of thick gingival biotype at baseline compared to the Control group. However, our results showed that the GBG group appeared to achieve greater soft tissue regeneration at the one-year follow-up, which may be attributed to the enhanced bone regeneration facilitated by the use of digital bone graft guides. This improvement in bone regeneration likely contributed to the shift toward a thicker gingival biotype, allowing the GBG group to gain more benefits in terms of soft tissue outcomes. Improved bone regeneration provides more stable support for the soft tissue, reducing soft tissue recession. The substantial increase in bone regeneration undoubtedly laid a solid foundation for soft tissue enhancement [40]. In turn, soft tissue regeneration may further promote bone regeneration through mechanisms such as improved blood supply, reduced mechanical disturbance, and enhanced barrier function.

A plethora of factors have been identified as contributors to bone remodeling subsequent to PAOO, including mechanical stress, local blood supply, and soft tissue management. The magnitude and direction of orthodontic force have been demonstrated to directly influence the rate of postoperative bone resorption. The implementation of personalized bone grafting guides, meticulously designed to address distinct bone defects, has the potential to optimize the morphology of bone grafting and generate a range of bone augmentation techniques that are tailored to individual patients. The application of these guides facilitates the uniform distribution of high-quality bone grafting materials, thereby reducing the concentration of local stresses and minimizing the risk of bone resorption.

Deproteinized bovine bone mineral (DBBM) exhibits osteogenic properties via a combination of multiple biological pathways. Specifically, its biomimetic porous hydroxyapatite structure confers exceptional osteoconductivity by providing a three-dimensional scaffold, facilitating the migration, attachment, and proliferation of neovascularization and bone progenitor cells. This scaffold-mediated regeneration is further optimized through high-density implantation, promoting the well-established process of “creeping substitution“ [41–43]. High-density grafting assisted by the guide may provide a larger contact interface for osteoblasts, thereby enhancing the expression of osteogenic marker genes (e.g., Runx2, osteocalcin, and alkaline phosphatase) and accelerating bone mineralization [44]. Moreover, DBBM accelerates osteogenesis by modulating the local microenvironment, where its extensive surface area and nanoscale roughness significantly enhance protein adsorption and cell adhesion, thus optimizing cellular responses [45]. Regarding angiogenesis, the porous structure of DBBM supports rapid vascular ingrowth and facilitates the local release of calcium and phosphate ions, thereby modulating the local pH environment. This ionic release directly promotes endothelial cell migration and enhances vascularization, critically supporting the overall bone regeneration process [46].

The findings of this study demonstrate that the digital guide-assisted PAOO procedure enhances bone grafting precision, minimizes bone resorption in the postoperative crown 1/3 region, and offers enhanced bone support for orthodontic treatment. This technique optimizes the bone grafting strategy, ensures uniform distribution of the bone grafts, and improves the osteogenic potential of the bone grafts. It is suitable for patients with severe bone defects in the lower anterior region. Moreover, in comparison with the bone grafting process of conventional PAOO surgery, the guide plate technique has been demonstrated to offer enhanced bone retention in the first third of the crown region. This contributes to a reduction in the risk of root exposure and gingival recession during the orthodontic process, while concurrently ensuring long-term stability for orthodontic treatment.

Although the present study demonstrates the potential benefits of digital guides in bone regeneration, certain limitations still exist. Firstly, the sample size in this study was relatively small, and the retrospective design limits our ability to establish causal relationships. While we endeavored to minimize intergroup differences by comparing baseline demographic characteristics and periodontal parameters, the results still cannot entirely rule out potential selection bias. Therefore, it is essential that future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes be conducted to mitigate such bias and provide more robust evidence. Secondly, this study primarily focused on the impact of the technique on soft and hard tissue regeneration, without assessing its effects on the acceleration of orthodontic tooth movement following PAOO. Future research will need to include more comprehensive assessments of orthodontic outcomes to better understand the full range of this technique’s effects. Although 3D-printed guides demonstrated significant advantages in bone regeneration following PAOO in this study, practical challenges such as high costs and logistical requirements may limit their widespread clinical use. The fabrication of these guides requires advanced technical equipment and specialized training, which could pose barriers in resource-limited settings. However, as technological advancements continue and production costs decrease, this technique is poised to become a standard treatment option for complex cases. In light of these considerations, future prospective studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods are crucial to further evaluate the cost-effectiveness, clinical value, and long-term efficacy of this bone grafting strategy in improving the success rate of PAOO surgery.

Conclusion

The results of this study suggest that bone grafting guide-assisted PAOO surgery can more effectively preserve alveolar bone height and coronal 1/3 alveolar bone thickness within one year postoperatively. The use of the bone grafting guide enhances grafting accuracy, reduces bone resorption, and provides better stability for maintaining bone integrity in PAOO surgery.
