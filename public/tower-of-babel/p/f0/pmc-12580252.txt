Comparison of Two‐Dimensional and Three‐Dimensional Preoperative Planning Measurements for High Tibial Osteotomy
Authors: Haohao Bai, Yadi Sun, Jianxiong Ma, Ying Wang, Yan Wang, Bin Lu, Lei Sun, Hongzhen Jin, Xingwen Zhao, Xinlong Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveKnee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a prevalent condition characterized by cartilage degeneration, tissue destruction, and bone hyperplasia, with pain being the primary symptom. High tibial osteotomy (HTO) has emerged as an effective treatment for symptomatic unicompartmental KOA, focusing on realigning force vectors to redistribute mechanical stress and alleviate pain. This study aims to address the question of whether two‐dimensional (2D) and three‐dimensional (3D) preoperative planning methods yield different correction angles and distraction distances in open‐wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO). By comparing these methodologies, we seek to evaluate their impact on surgical outcomes and patient prognosis.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted involving patients diagnosed with unicompartmental medial knee osteoarthritis (KOA) who exhibited deviations in the mechanical axis of the lower limbs. These patients underwent OWHTO between January 2021 and August 2022. Both 3D and 2D preoperative planning procedures for OWHTO were employed, targeting an ideal alignment with the weight‐bearing line (%WBL) set at 62.5%. The study quantitatively assessed the differences in correction angles and distraction distances associated with the two surgical planning methods using paired t‐tests and non‐parametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests.ResultsThe study comprised a total of 102 patients. It was observed that the mean correction angle in the 2D approach was significantly greater than that recorded in the 3D approach (p < 0.001), with an average difference of 1.49° ± 1.70°. The medial opening gap differences for OWHTO between the two approaches measured 2.1 ± 2.06 mm.ConclusionsTo summarize, the 2D preoperative planning method for OWHTO necessitates a larger correction angle and a wider medial opening gap relative to the 3D planning approach when aiming for the same WBL. Therefore, it is crucial for surgeons to take into account the variances between 2D and 3D planning and to evaluate potential correction errors during the surgical procedure while strategizing for OWHTO.

Introduction

High tibial osteotomy (HTO) is a minimally invasive and effective surgical method for the treatment of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) [1]. With the popularization of the concept of knee preservation in recent years, HTO has been used as an important knee preservation treatment method compared to joint replacement surgery due to its advantages such as less surgical trauma, faster recovery, and no change in the intra‐articular structure [2]. Especially for younger patients whose OA is limited to the medial compartment of the knee, joint replacement surgery may be an option at a later date even if surgery fails [3].

In patients with knee OA, the mechanical axis of the lower limb passes through the medial side of the knee joint, causing changes in the mechanical stress of the knee joint and leading to uneven pressure load distribution between the medial and lateral compartments of the knee joint [4]. Different joint morphologies and changes in lower limb force lines can cause changes in the pressure load within the joints. The medial load on the knee joint increases during walking. Changes in the kinematics and load characteristics of the knee joint during walking can cause degenerative changes in the articular cartilage, which are closely related to the progression of KOA [5].

As we know, it is very important to obtain accurate force correction after HTO surgery. Appropriate valgus correction can relieve the pressure of the medial compartment and transfer the weight‐bearing center of the lower limb to the relatively healthy lateral compartment, thereby maximizing the service life of the knee joint [6].

By shifting the load from the degenerated medial compartment to the relatively healthy lateral compartment, HTO can delay knee degeneration and relieve knee pain symptoms [7]. In addition to restoring the force line of the lower limb, HTO also substantially changes the dynamic load and function of the original inverted knee joint. Six months after surgery, HTO showed a positive state in joint kinematics, not only in the coronal plane, but also in the sagittal and axial planes [8]. Compared with the complications such as joint stiffness after joint replacement, the knee joint function and gait after HTO are restored to the level of normal lower limb force line, and patients can resume normal movement after surgery. With the improvement of surgical techniques and the improvement of implant materials in the steel plate, HTO has achieved relatively satisfactory mid‐ and long‐term clinical follow‐up results [7]. With advances in medicine and increasing awareness of knee preservation techniques, open‐wedge high tibial osteotomy (OWHTO) has emerged as a viable treatment option for unilateral KOA during its early to intermediate stages, and its use has gradually broadened [9]. The effectiveness of HTO is contingent upon the precise realignment of lower extremity structures, which is essential for the patient's postoperative functional recovery and the longevity of the autogenous knee joint [10, 11].

Healthcare professionals primarily use full‐length radiographs (FLRs) of the lower limb taken in a weight‐bearing position as a benchmark for preoperative planning [12]. This method is widely used across hospitals and maintains a certain degree of standardization [13]. It relies on 2D visual assessment and protractor‐based measurements. The results of these indicators are largely affected by subjective artifacts and can only directly measure coronal correction, resulting in considerable inaccuracy [14]. In the surgical correction of deformities, there is an increasing trend toward using the patient's three‐dimensional (3D) image to measure cuts [15]. It allows measurements to be made in the sagittal plane under the control of the tibial slope, thereby improving surgical precision and reducing the possibility of errors exceeding acceptable safety limits [16, 17]. However, few studies have directly compared the outcomes of 2D and 3D approaches, and the accuracy of preoperative planning directly affects the success of HTO, patient recovery, and preservation of the native knee joint [18].

Therefore, this case–control study aimed to compare the preoperative planning measurements for OWHTO between 2D FLR and 3D methods in terms of correction angles and distraction distances, evaluate the differences in the angle and height of the surgical opening gap between the two methods in the same patients, and provide a foundation for future surgical planning.

Methods

General Information

This study included patients suffering from medical KOA due to unicompartmental lesions that were misaligned with the mechanical axis of the lower limb, who received OWHTO from January 2021 to August 2022. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tianjin Hospital (Ethics No. 2020‐165). Consent was obtained from all patients. The criteria for inclusion were: (1) presence of symptomatic medial compartment KOA; (2) varus deformity with HKA exceeding 5°; (3) flexion contracture under 15°; and (4) absence of arthritic changes in the lateral or patellar areas. The exclusion criteria comprised: (1) individuals with lower extremity prosthetics; (2) a prior history of surgery on the lower extremity; or (3) insufficient lower extremity FLR and the absence of full‐length lower extremity computed tomography (CT) imaging.

Surgical Planning for Osteotomy Around the Knee Joint

Two‐Dimensional Planning

Weight‐bearing full‐length radiographs (WLR) of both lower limbs were routinely assessed to facilitate precise 2D preoperative planning, illustrated in Figure 1A. Patients were positioned for the WLR in a standardized manner, guaranteeing that their feet were symmetrically oriented, with the first metatarsal heads in alignment and the patellae facing forward. This consistent orientation of the feet is vital when relating 2D measurements to the 3D planning, as it accurately represents the actual weight‐bearing conditions and alignment of the joints.

Two‐dimensional preoperative planning of OWHTO based on full‐length radiograph of both lower extremities in the weight‐bearing position. (A) Standard full‐length X‐rays of both lower limbs in the weight‐bearing position are taken, and the patient's feet are placed symmetrically to ensure accurate reflection of the actual weight‐bearing situation and joint alignment. (B) Determine the weight‐bearing line (WBL), extending from the center of the femoral head to the center of the upper articular surface of the talus, calculate the WBL ratio, and set the target WBL to 62.5%. (C) Draw a line from the starting point of the osteotomy to the lateral hinge point. The hinge point is located at 85% of the osteotomy line to protect the lateral hinge. (D) Draw a line from the hinge point to the center of the ankle joint, and then draw equal‐length lines. The angle between the two lines is the correction angle required for osteotomy.

To determine the weight‐bearing line (WBL), a line is extended from the center of the femoral head to the center of the superior articular surface of the talus. When calculating the WBL ratio, the overall width of the tibia, measured with a ruler, functions as the denominator. The points where the WBL intersects the medial and lateral borders of the tibia are marked as 0% and 100%, respectively, serving as the numerator, as depicted in Figure 1B. This approach to calculating the WBL ratio is critical for precise preoperative planning, taking into account the unique anatomical variations that could influence the surgical result.

The preoperative WBL was meticulously established based on the state of the medial compartment, with the typical target WBL ranging from 60% to 65%. For this study, we standardized the WBL to 62.5% to unify our measurements. At this specified WBL percentage, additional lines were drawn to define the angles for osteotomy and correction. One line was drawn from the point where the osteotomy begins, situated 40 mm below the joint line, to the lateral hinge point, identified at the proximal tibiofibular joint near the fibular head's superior border, 15 mm from the lateral platform. Positioned at 85% of the osteotomy line, the hinge point is essential for safeguarding the lateral hinge throughout the procedure, as shown in Figure 1C.

A third line connected the hinge point to the center of the ankle joint, and a fourth line, equal in length to the third, was drawn from the hinge point to the first line. The angle formed between these two lines represented the correction angle necessary for the osteotomy. This detailed description of the 2D measurement process provides a foundation for comparison with the 3D planning methods used in this study, highlighting the importance of standardized procedures in achieving reliable and replicable results.

Three‐Dimensional Planning

In our preoperative planning process involving three dimensions (3D), the CT data of patients were exported into Mimics 22.0 (Materialize, Belgium) in DICOM (digital imaging and communications in medicine) format. Furthermore, in the Mimics software, a 3D model including the complete skeletal structure of the lower limb was generated using threshold segmentation, a dynamic region growing algorithm, and optimized post‐processing, as shown in Figure 2. We employed the Mimics software to carefully delineate crucial anatomical landmarks and surgical parameters. The center of the femoral head was determined using a polyline sphere fitting technique, which provided a precise reference point for our examination, as described in the study of Hodel S [19]. Following this, we located the anatomical centers of both the knee joint and the ankle joint through the visualized environment of the software. Utilizing these landmarks, we constructed the 3D force lines for the lower limbs, as illustrated in Figure 3A–C.

The patient CT data were imported into Mimics 22.0 software to generate a 3D model containing the complete bone structure of the lower limb.

Three‐dimensional preoperative planning of OWHTO based on full‐length lower extremity computed tomography. (A) Determine the center of the femoral head, knee joint, and ankle joint in Mimics software to construct the three‐dimensional mechanical line of the lower limb. (B) Determine the Fujisawa point, which is the 62.5% position on the tibial plateau width related to the load line. (C) Mark the upper boundary of the connection between the proximal tibia and fibula as the hinge point to protect the lateral hinge during osteotomy. (D) Select the osteotomy position, determine the three‐dimensional coordinates of the hinge point as the rotation axis, rotate the distal tibia so that the lower limb force line passes through the Fujisawa point, and measure the dispersion angle and height of the osteotomy.

To identify the Fujisawa point on the proximal tibial plane, we determined the 62.5% location across the width of the tibial plateau concerning the load line, adhering to recognized clinical protocols and biomechanical research [20]. This position is essential for enhancing joint load distribution and attaining the needed correction. The upper boundary of the connection between the proximal tibia and fibula was labeled as the hinge point, consistent with standard procedures to safeguard the lateral hinge during osteotomy [21]. An osteotomy position was chosen approximately 40 mm medial to the tibial plateau on the proximal tibia, with the direction of the osteotomy directed at the upper edge of the junction where the proximal tibia meets the fibula, as shown in Figure 3D. The hinge point was established at 85% of the bone line, a well‐known technique that helps ensure an incomplete osteotomy while preserving the structural stability of the lateral tibial cortex, which is essential for safeguarding the lateral hinge, as depicted in Figure 3D. The 3D coordinates of this hinge were treated as the axis of rotation. Next, we rotated the distal tibia within the coronal plane of the 3D model to align the force line of the lower limb through the Fujisawa point (WBL = 62.5%), as demonstrated in Figure 3F. After correction, we measured the distraction angle and height of the osteotomy, illustrated in Figure 3G. This procedure was carried out in the coronal plane using a 3D model obtained from CT reconstructions. To confirm the precision of our 3D planning, we assessed the posterior tilt of the tibia both prior to and following the osteotomy to ensure the original posterior tilt was maintained afterwards. Additionally, we simulated orthopedic distraction in a localized 3D model around the knee joint at the planned 2D angle, executing the osteotomy as detailed earlier. This provided the opportunity to quantify the 2D planned distraction height (Figure 1D) and to statistically analyze the variations in the orthopedic angle and distraction distance between the two surgical approaches.

Statistical Methods

To calculate the sample size, a priori power analysis was carried out using G*Power (Germany) showed that a minimum of 75 samples were required to detect the difference in the correction angle and distraction height with a power of 0.95 and an α of 0.01.

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of our data, two independent observers conducted the radiographic measurements twice; the time interval was 2 weeks. The reliability of the measurements, both within a single observer (intraobserver) and between different observers (interobserver), was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Values > 0.75 were considered significant. The reliability is shown in Table 1.

The statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 (IBM, USA) software. Both observers measured the correction angle, a vital parameter in the HTO procedure, and the correlation of their measurements was examined through Spearman's correlation coefficient, aligning with our evaluation of the total radiographic measurements. Data were presented in the standard format, with values expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x ± s). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For datasets that conformed to a normal distribution, differences in values between the two groups were compared using the paired t‐test. Conversely, when the data did not satisfy the normal distribution criteria, the non‐parametric Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was utilized. A level of statistical significance was designated at p < 0.05.

Results

In this retrospective study, we analyzed data from a total of 102 patients who underwent medial OWHTO. The patient cohort comprised 38 males and 64 females, with an average age of 60.2 ± 9.2 years. Upon examination, 54 patients had the procedure on their left knee joints, while 48 had it on their right knee joints. During the preoperative virtual planning phase for HTO, we compared the orthopedic angles determined from the full‐length radiographs (FLR) of the lower limbs and the CT 3D reconstruction model. The average orthopedic angles were found to be 11.4° ± 3.22° for the FLR method and 9.9° ± 2.58° for the CT 3D reconstruction, respectively. Discussion

Main Findings of This Study

This study compared 2D and 3D preoperative planning methods for OWHTO in patients with medial KOA. Key findings revealed that 2D planning, which relies on weight‐bearing full‐length radiographs (WLR), yielded slightly higher average correction angles and osteotomy heights compared to 3D planning, which uses non‐weight‐bearing CT scans to reconstruct 3D lower limb models and simulate mechanical axis alignment. This discrepancy was attributed to the limitations of 2D planning in capturing the knee's complex 3D anatomy and biomechanics, particularly under non‐weight‐bearing conditions. Despite these differences, both methods facilitated surgical planning, with 3D planning offering enhanced precision in simulating osteotomy adjustments and predicting postoperative alignment. Additionally, the diagnostic value of 2D planning lies in its cost‐effectiveness and accessibility, as it uses conventional X‐ray imaging for rapid preoperative assessment. However, its susceptibility to projection errors and inability to account for 3D anatomical variations may lead to suboptimal surgical precision. In contrast, 3D planning provides superior diagnostic accuracy by enabling virtual surgical simulations, precise anatomical measurements, and evaluation of osteotomy effects on joint mechanics, thereby minimizing intraoperative errors and enhancing postoperative alignment predictability. The integration of weight‐bearing radiographs with periarticular CT imaging could serve as a balanced strategy, reducing radiation exposure and financial burden while improving diagnostic reliability.

Differences Between 2D and 3D Surgery

In the case of 2D planning, we used weight‐bearing full‐length radiographs, while non‐weight‐bearing CT in 3D planning. Consistent with previous reports [22, 23], 2D planning is unable to fully capture the intricate anatomical structure and mechanical features of the knee joint within a 3D context. As such, clinicians might prefer to employ 3D planning techniques in more complex scenarios. In this virtual environment, adjustments can be made to the cutting position and angle, allowing for an evaluation of their effects on the knee joint, ultimately enabling the selection of the most effective surgical plan by constructing a 3D model of the patient's knee joint.

The main advantage of 2D preoperative planning lies in its cost‐effectiveness and ease of operation [24]. It relies on traditional X‐ray imaging, which is sufficient for rapid assessment and surgical planning. However, 2D planning may be affected by projection errors, leading to insufficient surgical precision. In contrast, 3D planning offers more accurate anatomical and mechanical measurements, allowing for more detailed surgical simulation and planning in a virtual environment [25]. This approach can reduce errors during surgery and improve the precision and effectiveness of the operation. However, 3D planning requires more advanced technology and equipment, is more costly, and involves more complex data processing and analysis. Although 3D planning offers greater precision and insight into the patient's specific anatomical structures, the differences between 2D and 3D planning may impact surgical outcomes and patient prognosis. 2D planning may not fully capture the complex anatomical structures and mechanical characteristics of the knee joint, potentially leading to under‐ or over‐correction during surgery [26]. Such differences can affect postoperative joint stability and functional recovery, thereby impacting the patient's long‐term prognosis. Therefore, understanding these differences is crucial for optimizing surgical strategies and enhancing patient satisfaction.

Effective preoperative planning plays a vital role in minimizing the likelihood of both under‐correction and over‐correction [27]. Success in HTO depends on proper indications, thorough deformity assessment, meticulous planning, skilled surgical execution, and attentive postoperative care [28]. It is essential to carry out precise preoperative strategizing to lower the chances of errors in correction. In particular, excessive correction can lead to poor outcomes, and inadequate adjustments may result in a recurrence of symptoms [20]. Dugdale et al. [29] proposed relocating the load‐bearing line to around 62.5% of the tibial plateau, typically observed within the lateral compartment at the apex of the lateral tibia, promoting a shift of 3°–5° in the mechanical axes when everted. Moreover, Shin et al. [30] illustrated that supine radiographs yield more consistent results than standing radiographs in achieving the intended alignment. Jones et al. [31] utilized CT scanning's 3D reconstruction technology to recreate the 3D images of the femur and tibia in the lower extremities, allowing for measurements that effectively mitigate errors associated with X‐ray film assessments. Similarly, Ariumi et al. [32] restructured full‐length CT data of the lower extremities, measuring pertinent angles and activities to yield the most precise data, which holds significant importance for biomechanics research. Willcox et al. [33] conducted a correlation analysis between the MLA as measured by FLR and CT_CAS. Their findings indicated that the range of differences between these two techniques was [−9.4°–8.6°] prior to surgery and [−5.1°–5.4°] postoperatively. In this investigation, no notable difference was found between the correction angles derived from 3D planning and those obtained through standing and supine radiographs [34]. It is our belief that the main factor influencing the change in postoperative WBL% was the challenge in anticipating the relaxation of soft tissues surrounding the knee joint, particularly the medial stabilizing muscles, after OWHTO. A negative correlation was observed between postoperative WBL% and the corrected angle derived from 3D planning, alongside the preoperative HKA angle. Conversely, a positive correlation was found with the preoperative original medial proximal tibial angle (MPTA) [34]. Fujisawa et al. [35] recommended that the target area for the weight‐bearing axis (WBA) be set between 65%–70% of the tibial plateau, measured from the inner to the outer side, later refining it to 62.5% (with a range of 62%–66%). Meanwhile, Martay et al. [36] advocated for establishing 55% as the new target line location, while also taking into account potential errors related to over or under‐correction during the procedure, thereby adjusting the WBA's safety margin to around 50% of the tibial plateau, moving from inside to outside at approximately 60%. Despite ongoing investigations into HTO, the ideal positioning continues to remain ambiguous [37]. In their 10‐year follow‐up study on the effectiveness of HTO surgery, Schuster et al. [38] indicated that while HTO can be an effective treatment, both under‐correction and over‐correction of the mechanical medial proximal tibial angle may result in unfavorable long‐term functional results.

One of the fundamental principles of HTO is executing axial correction as accurately as possible, since either under‐correction or over‐correction can significantly contribute to clinical failure [39]. Another hurdle faced during HTO is the preservation of the posterior tibial slope (PTS). Regardless of the chosen surgical approach or the design of the surgical strategy, precision in measuring the relevant anatomical angles remains essential [40]. Through 3D reconstructions of the proximal tibia, we noted that the proximal tibia resembles a triangular cylinder with a slender front and a broader rear, deviating from a conventional cylinder shape. There exists a specific angle between the anatomical and gravitational axes [41]. Additionally, techniques for osteotomy can be utilized to address procedure‐related instability; HTO can realign the tibia in the coronal plane, but it may also alter the PTS, which has a direct influence on anterior tibial translation. Reducing the PTS may help minimize anterior tibial translation in knees lacking an anterior cruciate ligament [42]. However, it is important to note that HTO can also lead to an increased PTS. Kuriyama et al. [43] engaged in three‐dimensional planning and discovered that the subsequent corrections in coronal and sagittal bone alignment were largely precise. Implementing preoperative 3D planning for OWHTO can help reduce surgical discrepancies; nonetheless, the postoperative WBL% continues to show variability. Lee et al. [44] investigated the differences between expected and actual postoperative corrections in OWHTO, revealing that the WBL post‐OWHTO increased with a larger planned osteotomy gap. In contrast to the anticipated WBL outcomes, the gap between pre‐planned corrections and the actual corrections following OWHTO was significantly more pronounced when substantial correction was required.

Limitations and Prospect

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and no horizontal correlation analysis was performed between 3D techniques. Second, differences in weight‐bearing conditions (2D vs. 3D imaging) may have influenced comparisons. Future research should prioritize larger randomized controlled trials with long‐term follow‐up to validate these findings. Additionally, integrating weight‐bearing radiographs with CT‐based 3D models could optimize planning while minimizing patient radiation exposure and costs. Emerging technologies, such as dynamic 3D simulations accounting for soft tissue laxity and intraoperative navigation systems, may further refine surgical precision. Clinicians must also individualize planning based on patient‐specific anatomy, functional goals, and socioeconomic factors to achieve optimal outcomes. Addressing these challenges will advance HTO as a reliable treatment for medial KOA, balancing technical innovation with practical feasibility.

Conclusion

This research investigated the measurements from 2D and 3D preoperative planning for OWHTO, revealing that the distraction height and angle derived from 2D planning exceeded those from 3D planning. Consequently, it is essential for surgeons to thoughtfully assess the distinctions between 2D and 3D preoperative planning and to anticipate potential correction errors during the surgical procedure when preparing for an OWHTO.
