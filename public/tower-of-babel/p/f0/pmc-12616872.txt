The Dorsal Forearm as a Novel Target for Intramuscular Injections: Clinical Pharmacokinetic Results With Naloxone
Authors: Thomas F. Krol, Bryan G. Beutel, Carlos Fierro, Scott J. Weir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe dorsal forearm represents a potentially viable location for a convenient wearable device for the administration of intramuscular (IM) injections, but this site has not previously been formally targeted for this purpose. The objectives of this study were to administer naloxone via IM injection into the dorsal forearm, characterize the pharmacokinetic (PK) profile of this injection site, and compare it to that of a standard IM injection. The study was an outpatient, open‐label, single period, single treatment, single dose proof of concept (PoC) trial in 12 healthy subjects. After screening, participants provided a time zero (pre‐dose) blood sample and then were administered 2 mg naloxone HCl as an IM injection in the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) muscle of the dorsal forearm. Serial blood (plasma) samples were obtained from the contralateral arm prior to dosing and serially thereafter for PK analysis. Naloxone concentrations were determined using a validated assay. PK parameters were generated from the naloxone concentration‐time data. The means and ranges of the PK profile of naloxone administered IM in the forearm were: C max 5.3 ng/mL; T max 0.29 h; AUC[0–∞] 7.96 ng * h/mL, and the relative bioavailability of naloxone administered into the dorsal forearm was 87% compared to IM naloxone administered in the thigh. The subjects noted minimal discomfort with the forearm injection, and there were no adverse neurovascular events. This represents the first study wherein a drug has been administered in the EDC of the dorsal forearm as an IM target muscle.

Introduction

Opioid overdoses (OD) and deaths have increased substantially over the last 30 years. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), over 1 million people have died since 1999 from a drug overdose. More than 75% of these deaths in recent years involved an opioid. Moreover, the number of OD deaths, such as those involving prescription opioids, heroin, and synthetic opioids like fentanyl, in 2021 was 10 times the number in 1999 [1]. Although recent trends show a slight reduction in deaths from OD, nearly 80,000 people die annually from OD in the US, with the majority of deaths involving synthetic opioids [2, 3, 4].

Opioids are drugs that function in the nervous system of the body or in specific receptors in the brain to reduce the intensity of pain. Naloxone (17‐allyl‐4,5α‐epoxy‐3,14‐dihydroxymorphinan‐6‐one HCl) is a high‐affinity opiate receptor antagonist that has been delivered by the parenteral route of administration to treat the symptoms of OD for over 50 years.

The IN form of naloxone, sold as the brand Narcan nasal spray, is now available in approximately 70% of pharmacies and is being made available in vending machines by various municipalities, government and nongovernmental agencies (NGOs). The product Evzio, approved in April 2014, was an autoinjector for intramuscular (IM) injection of 2 mg naloxone that provided audible instructions for its use, but it was removed from the market in 2019. The initial approval was for administration of 0.4 mg in 0.4 mL, but a subsequent approval was for a 2 mg dose and a 4 mg dose.

A new medical drug‐device combination, D‐Cuff, is being developed for rapid and easy IM administration. The prototype proprietary device is designed to administer any drug in muscles typically not considered for IM administration.

This proof of concept (PoC) study was designed to evaluate if IM injection of naloxone in the extensor digitorum communis (EDC) muscle, distal to the elbow in the dorsal forearm, is approximately equivalent to administration via the vastus lateralis thigh muscle, based on previously studied pharmacokinetics (PK) of naloxone from NIDA.

The need for an improved IM form of naloxone administration is perhaps most evident when evaluating onset of action (OOA). When naloxone is administered intravenously (IV), the OOA is generally apparent within 2–3 min, with a duration of 45–90 min. The OOA is slightly less rapid when it is administered IM, often within approximately 5–7 min, and even longer using IN, which can be as long as 12–18 min [5, 6]. A shorter OOA, especially when a patient is not breathing, has the potential to save more lives and lead to better outcomes. In addition, since fewer than 1% of ODs are treated with naloxone or another tool, an improved IM administration form may be useful to combat the current opioid crisis.

Naloxone hydrochloride injection, USP, may be administered IV, IM, or subcutaneously (SC). The generic naloxone parenteral formulation is a sterile, nonpyrogenic solution of naloxone hydrochloride in water for injection. Each milliliter (mL) contains 0.4 mg (or 1 mg) naloxone hydrochloride and sodium chloride to adjust tonicity in water for injection. It may contain hydrochloric acid for pH adjustment to a pH 4.0 (3.0–6.5). For the current study, we used a sterile compounded formulation of naloxone HCl at a concentration of 4 mg/mL, administered IM in the EDC as 0.5 mL for a dose of 2 mg IM in the forearm.

The primary study objective was to compare IM injection into the musculature of the dorsal forearm and measure the PK profile compared to IM injection in the thigh. Secondary objectives of this study were to evaluate comfort or discomfort at this IM site, subjective and objective measures of side effects, local pain, local erythema, neurovascular and/or motor complications, etc. In addition, the PK findings of this study will assist in the design of the definitive PK study required for FDA registration.

Due to differences in vascularity, the rates of absorption differ slightly by site, with the arm having the highest rate, followed by the thigh and then the buttocks [6]. Drug absorption from the IM route typically takes several minutes, and can be more variable than IV. The volume of injection, osmolality of the solution, depth of injection, lipid solubility and degree of ionization all influence absorption. Importantly, most HCPs assume that the IM route is a reliable and consistent route of administration.

These four aforementioned sites are selected because they are large and relatively easy to target with a needle while avoiding iatrogenic injury to major and bones. IM injection in the muscles of the forearm is uncommon, and typically not used for this reason. The EDC muscle belly is large enough to provide a reliable target for IM injections (Figure 1). No data are available for IM injection in the forearm area, such as the EDC, and surrounding muscles.

Rationale for Study

There is a significant unmet medical and patient need for products in the treatment of acute and intermittent conditions that can be delivered by injection in precise amounts, provide rapid therapeutic onset, and are simple, easy and convenient to use by anyone in a nonhospital setting. Our solution is the D‐Cuff. The product is being designed to deliver drugs IM to rapidly achieve systemic blood levels. The overall concept is to provide a new product that will allow anyone the ability to easily administer an IM injection. The D‐Cuff system, when deployed, will administer injectable drugs IM in a fashion that avoids nerves, blood vessels and bones. As such, it can be used for administration into smaller muscles, such as the EDC in the area of the forearm as contemplated in this study.

This device will eventually provide rapid onset and ease of use by all parties (caregiver, self, or HCP), and does not require patient cooperation. The initial target for this device is administration into the EDC muscle of the forearm. The device is being designed and engineered; however, the goal of this study is to confirm the pharmacokinetics (PK) of a representative drug (e.g., naloxone) into this forearm muscle area. Thus, the purpose of this study is to administer naloxone into the forearm muscle, primarily the EDC, by a trained HCP, mimicking the device.

Based on these studies, we predicted that naloxone PK upon administration in a muscle, such as the EDC of the forearm, would be approximately equivalent. This is the hypothesis of the current study. This pilot study provides details required for the design of a definitive study comparing D‐Cuff naloxone to standard IM naloxone. In addition, this archetypal study will serve as a basis for IM equivalence for other injectable drugs administered IM in the forearm.

The selection of 2 mg naloxone as the dose in this study will allow for direct comparison of PK parameters to the previously published studies. The data from the NIDA study (Krieter 2019) were provided for comparison [7]. In addition, the 2 mg dose is associated with less variability (an approximately 50% lower coefficient of variation [CV]). Although not part of the same study, we have endeavored to create a similar study design for comparable purposes.

Methods

Study Participants

The study protocol was approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board on March 25, 2024, and all participants gave written informed consent before participation. The study site was Johnson County Clin‐Trials (Lenexa, KS), and the study was conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonization for Good Clinical Practices guidelines. It is registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT07180420). Healthy volunteers of both sexes, aged 18–55 years, with body mass index (BMI) 18–30 kg/m2, participated in this study. Participants were not taking either prescription or over‐the‐counter medications, and they were either nonsmokers or smoked 10 or fewer cigarettes per day. Screening procedures conducted within 30 days of study initiation included the following: medical history, physical examination, 12‐lead electrocardiogram, complete blood count, clinical chemistry, coagulation markers, hepatitis and human immunodeficiency screening, urinalysis, and urine drug screen. Female participants were also tested for pregnancy at screening and admission to the clinic. Participants were excluded if they had an upper respiratory tract infection, used opioid analgesics for pain relief within the previous 14 days, or, in the judgment of the investigator, had significant acute or chronic medical conditions.

Study Design

The study was an outpatient, open‐label, single period, single treatment, single dose PoC trial. Subjects were admitted to the study center and were present for the entire study duration. A Schedule of Events is presented in Table S1 and Inclusion/Exclusion criteria in Table S2. On the day of clinic admission following an overnight fast, participants provided a pre‐dose blood sample and were then administered naloxone HCl as an IM injection in the EDC muscle of the dorsal forearm by an orthopedic surgeon hand specialist.

Subjects received a single 2 mg IM dose of naloxone administered via standard aseptic technique (Figure S1). The needle size was 25G to administer the full 2 mg dose in 0.5 mL. Blood draws were taken from the contralateral arm at the following time points: 5 min prior to dosing and 5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 40, and 50 min and 1, 1.25, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, and 6 h post‐dose. Blood samples were collected using Vacutainer tubes containing K2 EDTA and placed in an ice bath until centrifugation, which occurred within 60 min of sample collection. Tubes were centrifuged at 1500 g/10 min. Plasma was pipetted to round bottom, polypropylene, 5 mL polypropylene tubes and frozen at −70°C until analyzed.

General safety assessments were performed for all 12 participants in the study throughout the study as blood draws took place. The PK population included all 12 participants. A thorough neurovascular assessment was performed prior to each participant's dismissal, specifically evaluating sensorimotor function of the radial nerve. Study Drug

Naloxone was selected as the PoC study drug because data were available from the National Institute on Drug Abuse (NIDA) from IM injection comparisons to IN forms, it is safe in healthy volunteers not taking opioids, and its half‐life is relatively short.

Naloxone HCl was supplied by a sterile compounding pharmacy as a sterile solution with a concentration of 4 mg/mL. The product was assayed by a third‐party laboratory for potency and sterility prior to study initiation. The naloxone was drawn into a 1 mL syringe and administered by the same trained orthopedic hand surgeon as a 0.5 mL injection (2 mg of naloxone) into the EDC of each of the 12 subjects.

Analytical Methods

Plasma naloxone concentrations were determined using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC MS/MS) assay. Plasma concentrations of naloxone were determined in 200 μL sample volumes using reversed‐phase HPLC with MS/MS detection over a concentration range of 20.0 pg/mL to 20,000.0 pg/mL. The concentrations were calculated using peak area ratios and the linearity of the calibration curve was determined using a weighted (1/x 2) linear (y = mx + b) least squares regression analysis for naloxone. The calibration curves were constructed across the concentration range of 20.0 and 20,000.0 pg/mL with a lower limit of quantitation of 20 ng/mL. Quality control samples at 20.0 pg/mL, 60.0 pg/mL, 10,000.0 pg/mL, and 15,000.0 pg/mL were included in each batch. Inter‐day accuracy and precision values (%CV) ranged from −6.2% to −0.5% and 3.9% to 7.9%, respectively, across the standard curve concentration range. A full validation report is on file and available from AltaSciences Company Inc. (Quebec, Canada).

Data Analyses

PK parameters were calculated using standard noncompartmental methods and a validated installation of WinNonlin Phoenix, version 8.4 (Certara Inc., Radnor, PA). Values of peak plasma concentrations (C max) and the time to reach C max (T max) were the observed values obtained directly from the concentration‐time data. The terminal elimination half‐life (t½) was estimated by linear regression analysis. The area under the concentration‐time curve from time zero to the last quantifiable concentration (AUC0‐t) was determined by the linear up/log down trapezoidal method and extrapolated to infinity (AUC–∞) by adding the value of the last quantifiable concentration divided by the terminal rate constant (λz). The apparent total clearance (CL/F) was calculated as the dose (D) divided by the AUC.

Pharmacokinetics

The PK results following IM administration of naloxone in the EDC obtained in twelve healthy volunteers are summarized in Figure 2. The mean C max was 5.3 ng/mL with a range of 2.21–11.07 ng/mL, and the mean T max value was 0.29 h with a range of 0.08–0.67 h. Additionally, the AUC‐0‐inf was 7.96, ng * h/mL with a range of 5.85–10.68 ng * h/mL. The mean apparent elimination half‐life of naloxone was 1.36 h and ranged from 0.95 h to 1.74 h. Apparent elimination rate constants were estimated based on a minimum of four time points contained within the terminal phase of the plasma naloxone concentration‐time profiles. R‐squared values for these terminal rate constant estimates for each subject ranged from 0.9343 to 0.9982. The serial blood (plasma) sample collection scheme employed for this study did an excellent job characterizing the area under the plasma naloxone concentration‐time curve. Collection of serial blood samples over 6 h post‐dose captured 95% of AUC[0–inf].

Mean ± SD plasma naloxone concentration‐time profile following intramuscular administration in the forearm.

These data demonstrate that the bioanalytical method employed, with a lower limit of quantitation of 20 pg/mL, was more than adequate to support the objectives of the study. Safety, Adverse Event, Vital Signs, and EDC Assessment

Safety assessments included observations during the study period, evaluation of the injection site, and vital signs prior to discharge. A total of three treatment‐related adverse events were reported by the participants. A summary of AE is presented in Table S3. They were all categorized as “not related” to the study drug and likely related to the number and swiftness of blood draws. Two were vasovagal responses following blood draws which resolved within minutes, and one was reported as transient dizziness upon standing 6 h after receiving naloxone.

Upon IM administration into the forearm, most volunteers indicated that the injection did not cause any or only minimal brief discomfort, and they felt mainly slight pressure. No volunteers complained of discomfort upon IM injection to the forearm. There were no complaints of burning upon injection, though one subject mentioned a pressure sensation for a few seconds. Many subjects noted that the placement of the IV line for blood draws was more painful than the IM injection itself. No patients experienced any neurovascular complications following naloxone administration.

Figure S1 illustrates photos of actual injection into the forearm muscle, which the subjects agreed we could share. Each subject's anatomical site of injection was evaluated prior to discharge for inflammation as well as motor and sensory function associated with the radial nerve, the nearest nerve to the injection site. No abnormalities were found during the course of the study.

Discussion

Naloxone is well‐known as a reversal agent for ODs, which have become exceedingly prevalent in recent years. According to Shatterproof, 49 million Americans struggle with addiction [12]. According to the CDC, there were over 112,000 fatal overdoses in the 12‐month period ending in May 2023, which reflects the highest number of overdose deaths ever recorded in the US in a single year. Approximately 60% of those deaths were linked to illicitly manufactured or adulterated fentanyl.

Recent studies have also evaluated family members of addicts affected by OD. Findings in a cross‐sectional study from 2011 to 2021 noted that an estimated 321,566 community‐dwelling children in the United States lost a parent aged 18–64 years to drug OD, and the rate of children who lost a parent to overdose increased during this period [13]. Lessons learned from this study included the fact that responses to ODs must account for the full burden, including the economic, social, educational, and healthcare needs of children [14]. A new product that allows anyone the ability to easily administer IM naloxone would be a significant addition to the tools needed for this crisis.

To reverse an OD, the naloxone blood level needs to achieve rapid absorption and distribution. Previous studies have demonstrated that the IV route is the most rapid, followed by IM and IN [15]. In one real‐world study, the OOA measured by median time to respiratory rate of at least 10 breaths per minute was 8 min via IM administration, compared with 17 min for IN administration, which equated to an 81% increase in hazard. Absorption of medications IN may be reduced by nasal secretions or blood as well as vasoconstrictors such as decongestants or cocaine. Since OD occur frequently in individuals with substance use disorders, the approach to dosing may vary, and IM may be the most expeditious route of administration [16].

The intent of the present study was to demonstrate clinical PoC that injection in the EDC muscle of the forearm is approximately the same as other IM routes. The dose of 2 mg was selected based on previous values, a good therapeutic dose for synthetic OD, and to reduce variability among subjects. The study was designed such that the bioanalytical method was sufficiently sensitive for the dose administered, the sampling scheme was designed for good characterization of the AUC, and the results showed that this IM site is bioavailable.

IM dosing to the forearm is not traditional but was selected because a new drug‐device combination product is being developed in which anyone (layperson, caregiver, or health care professional [HCP]) can use it for fast, easy, and reliable administration. Even with traditional muscle injection sites, errors in IM administration technique lead to an injection that misses the mark in up to 60% of cases [17].

The PK of naloxone generated in this study will be compared to published data following administration of naloxone into other muscles such as the dorsogluteus (gluteus maximus) and vastus lateralis. Together, these data will establish PoC for a new device that will administer drugs into the extensor digitorum. This cross‐study comparison is sufficient to establish PoC for this drug‐device combination and will provide key data for the design and conduct of subsequent pivotal studies.

We compared the PK profile of previously administered IM naloxone to that of this study. Table 3 shows the comparison of 2 mg IM naloxone administered in the anterolateral thigh muscle to 2 mg IM naloxone administered in the EDC. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use this forearm muscle, the EDC, to inject a drug.

While an IV dose is the fastest means of achieving a high plasma concentration, the general public and many first responders, such as police, are not trained or equipped to administer naloxone IV. The IN naloxone products are convenient and relatively easy to use, but have other limitations in terms of OOA, reliability, and the amount of drug that can be delivered. Of additional note is that the dose of naloxone required to reverse some of the synthetic opioids (e.g., fentanyl, carfentanil, sufentanil, nitazenes, etc.) now on the street, which are 100 s and even 1000s times more potent than morphine, can be 20 mg naloxone or more [16]. The IV and IM routes provide a means for these higher doses of naloxone.

Training is needed to understand how to assemble and use some of the IN devices, and even with training, there is a 45% failure rate in its use by laypersons [18]. A portion of the naloxone solution delivered using the IN device may be lost by dripping down the nasopharynx or externally from the nose due to the introduction of 1 mL solution into each naris. The optimum volume for nasal delivery is approximately 0.10–0.15 mL [19]. The variability of dose with the new Narcan nasal spray, which is now OTC, can be as much as 70% (13). The Naloxone Hydrochloride Nasal Spray Product Monograph states as a general warning that the effectiveness of IN naloxone has not been assessed in people with intranasal conditions such as abnormal nasal anatomy, nasal symptoms (e.g., congested airway or rhinorrhea, nasal polyps, etc.) or in people having a product sprayed into the nasal cavity prior to naloxone administration. It is not known if these conditions affect naloxone's effectiveness when administered by the IN route. The bioavailability of naloxone IN is approximately 50% [20].

Various factors can also affect absorption of an injectable product. Absorption rates for IM injections can vary due to dependence on blood flow to the injection site, rate of blood flow to and from the site, amount of muscle and adipose tissue present, and formulation characteristics such as drug lipophilicity, pH, volume, etc. [21] It must be recognized that, for both the IN and IM routes, there are potential sources of variability that could affect absorption and, subsequently, the efficacy of naloxone. However, we believe that another tool to combat the opioid epidemic would be vital to these efforts.

As noted above, IM administration is faster and more reliable than IN administration. However, the current IM products are not simple, easy, convenient, or cost‐effective for the general public to use. The choice of the EDC muscle is based on creating a drug‐device combination product that will be simple, easy, convenient, and cost‐effective so that anyone can administer an IM injection of naloxone quickly, easily, safely, and reliably.

This clinical trial was designed as a feasibility study, designed to generate PoC data necessary to make a go/no go decision to advance this drug‐device combination product through development (and into pivotal trials). We believe these data show enough consistency to pursue a medical device that can inject a product like naloxone into the forearm as an easy alternative to other IM routes and to nasal administration.

The limitations of this study naturally lead to additional questions for future studies. This PoC clinical PK study was conducted in healthy adult volunteers under controlled conditions. Future work to expand the generalizability of the findings to target patient populations is needed. Healthy subjects typically do not exhibit the physiological or pathological variations present in patients, such as altered organ function, comorbidities, or concomitant medications, which may affect the absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) of naloxone. One limitation to the use of this route of administration to treat overdose may be the limited muscle mass of especially long‐term substance users. Future study designs could also evaluate potential PK differences in special populations, including pediatric, geriatric, or renally/hepatically impaired individuals and involve a crossover design for direct comparison. In addition, because other drugs have different chemical and PK properties these results will need to be demonstrated with other IM drugs administered to the EDC muscle. Finally, the single dose administration using traditional needle and syringe without using the contemplated autoinjector will require additional clinical testing.

In conclusion, this PoC study met its key objectives. Administration of naloxone in the dorsal forearm/EDC muscle is approximately equivalent to IM injection in the anterolateral aspect of the thigh from previous studies. The site of injection was associated with minimal discomfort and did not result in any motor or sensory complications.
