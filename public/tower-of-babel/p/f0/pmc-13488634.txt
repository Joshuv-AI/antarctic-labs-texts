Reply to correspondence on the comment regarding the article “Shortened telomere length, anxiety and psychological stress in systemic lupus erythematosus: a cross-sectional study” (Advances in Rheumatology, 2025;66(1):8).
Authors: Anna Carolina Faria Moreira Gomes Tavares, Carolina Ruas Freire Santos, Jéssica Martins Amaral, Ivonne Carolina Bolaños Burgos, Daniela Valadão Freitas Rosa, Marco Aurélio Romano-Silva, Maria Aparecida Camargos Bicalho, Gilda Aparecida Ferreira, Adriana Maria Kakehasi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We would like to thank Elia and Khan for their thoughtful interest in our article entitled “Shortened telomere length, anxiety and psychological stress in systemic lupus erythematosus: a cross-sectional study”, published in Advances in Rheumatology [1]. We appreciate the opportunity to further clarify methodological aspects and to contextualize our findings within the broader and evolving field of telomere biology in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

We fully agree with the authors that our study entitled “Shortened telomere length, anxiety and psychological stress in systemic lupus erythematosus: a cross-sectional study” presents inherent limitations, many of which were explicitly acknowledged in the original manuscript. First and foremost, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences regarding the temporal relationship between telomere attrition, psychological factors, and disease-related variables. Our work was conceived as a pivotal, hypothesis-generating study, intended to open a novel line of investigation rather than to provide definitive mechanistic conclusions. As emphasized in our discussion, longitudinal studies are essential to clarify the directionality, stability, and clinical implications of telomere shortening in SLE.

Regarding telomere assessment in peripheral blood, we concur that telomere length varies across tissues and that organ-specific measurements may provide additional biological insight. However, leukocyte telomere length remains the most widely used and validated surrogate marker of systemic cellular aging and immunosenescence, particularly in immune-mediated inflammatory diseases [2]. Large population-based studies have demonstrated meaningful correlations between leukocyte telomere length and telomere dynamics in multiple tissues, supporting its translational relevance in clinical research [3]. Moreover, peripheral blood sampling is currently the most feasible and ethically acceptable approach for studies involving living patients with chronic autoimmune diseases.

The complexity of the relationship between telomere length, SLE susceptibility, and disease expression is well recognized. Recent genetic and Mendelian randomization studies suggesting that genetically determined longer telomeres may increase SLE risk further underscore the multifaceted and context-dependent nature of telomere biology [4]. Our study does not aim to position telomere shortening as a unidimensional marker of disease severity, but rather as a potential indicator of cumulative biological burden arising from chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and psychosocial distress.

We also acknowledge the possibility of residual confounding, despite careful multivariable adjustment. SLE is characterized by a highly heterogeneous clinical course, and factors such as inflammatory burden, pain, sleep disturbances, medication exposure, and cumulative life stress are intricately interrelated. Importantly, our regression models were not designed to explain the entirety of telomere length variability, but to identify independent associations within the constraints of a modestly sized clinical cohort. The relatively low proportion of explained variance reinforces the notion that telomere dynamics are influenced by a wide array of biological, genetic, and environmental determinants.

With respect to anxiety and stress, our findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal, highlighting a potential link between psychological symptoms and cellular aging pathways in SLE. This observation is biologically plausible and consistent with growing evidence demonstrating that chronic psychological distress can modulate telomerase activity, oxidative stress, and inflammatory signaling [5]. Nonetheless, we explicitly agree that anxiety should not be viewed in isolation as the primary driver of telomere erosion, but rather as one component of a complex biopsychosocial framework.

In summary, we believe that our study contributes original and clinically relevant data by integrating telomere biology with psychological dimensions in a genetically diverse SLE population. While acknowledging its limitations, we maintain that this work provides a solid foundation for future longitudinal, multi-tissue, and mechanistic studies, which are indispensable to determine the prognostic value of telomere length and its potential role as a biomarker of disease burden and aging-related comorbidities in SLE.

We sincerely thank the authors for their constructive comments, which enrich the scientific dialogue and help refine future research directions in this emerging field.

Sincerely,

Anna Carolina Faria Moreira Gomes Tavares, MD, on behalf of the authors.
