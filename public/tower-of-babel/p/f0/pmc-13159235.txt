Bacteremia complicating urinary tract infections in hospitalized patients in a tertiary care hospital in Palestine, retrospective study
Authors: Razan Nafee, Enas Abu Arrah, Motasem Dweekat, Jamal Qaddumi, Adham Abu Taha, Ali Sabatin, Ayman Dawoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBloodstream infections secondary to urinary tract infections (UTIs) are associated with prolonged hospital stays, increased complications, higher costs, and elevated mortality rates. Previous studies reported mortality rates ranging from 25% to 60%. To our knowledge, no studies have yet investigated bloodstream infections secondary to UTIs in Palestine. Therefore, we conducted this study to identify common risk factors associated with bloodstream infections secondary to UTIs, and to describe their clinical progression, outcomes, and mortality.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted at An-Najah National University Hospital ( ANNUH) and included 140 adult patients aged 19–90 years who had concurrent positive urine and blood cultures for the same pathogen. Demographic, microbiological, and laboratory data were collected from 2018 to 2023. Mortality outcomes were assessed and analyzed in correlation with these findings.ResultsA total of 140 patients were enrolled in the study. Of the isolates, 71.4% were bacterial and 28.6% were fungal. Candida species were the most common isolates (28.6%), followed by Escherichia coli (26.4%). Among the participants, 43% died. Candida bloodstream infection, mechanical ventilation, admission to the intensive care unit, and a history of chronic pulmonary disease were significantly associated with higher mortality. Laboratory parameters including low white blood cell count, hemoglobin, and platelets were also associated with death.ConclusionThis study is the first in Palestine to examine bloodstream infections secondary to UTIs. The findings indicate an epidemiologic shift from predominantly Escherichia coli to Candida species. Mortality was significantly associated with Candida bloodstream infection and risk factors such as intensive care unit admission, mechanical ventilation, and central line use. Ongoing surveillance and periodic monitoring of resistance patterns are essential to improve outcomes and guide empirical therapy.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections acquired in both community and hospital settings, affecting around 150 million people worldwide each year [1, 2]. They are caused mainly by Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, and in some cases by certain fungi [2]. Among these pathogens, uropathogenic Escherichia coli is the leading cause, responsible for approximately 80% of cases. However, in older individuals, E. coli accounts for only 40–60% of UTIs [3].

The term “urinary tract infection” encompasses a wide range of clinical presentations, including cystitis, pyelonephritis, prostatitis, and catheter-associated UTIs [4, 5]. While most UTIs are mild and resolve spontaneously or with oral antibiotics, some may progress to severe complications such as bloodstream infections, septic shock, or even death [3, 6].

Secondary bloodstream infections occur when pathogens spread from the urinary tract into the bloodstream, where the urinary tract serves as the primary source of infection [7]. Approximately 4%–15% of UTI cases are complicated by secondary bloodstream infections [8]. Regional data on the prevalence and clinical outcomes of bacteremia secondary to UTIs in the Middle East, particularly in Palestine, remain limited. Several host-related risk factors increase the likelihood of such progression, including older age, use of urethral catheters, urologic procedures, male sex, malignancy, diabetes, smoking, and immunosuppressive therapy [9]. Identifying patients at higher risk is crucial, as these individuals may benefit from early, aggressive management.

Previous studies have shown that E. coli remains the most common pathogen, representing between 35% and 75% of isolates [3, 6, 7, 10, 11]. Other studies have reported Enterococcus spp. (28.7%), Candida spp. (19.6%), and Staphylococcus spp. (36.5%) as frequent isolates [9, 12]. These findings may suggest a shift from the historically reported Gram-negative predominance. Such changes in microbial patterns highlight the importance of revising empirical antimicrobial regimens used for UTI management.

Bloodstream infections secondary to UTIs are associated with increased length of hospital stay, higher complication rates, greater healthcare costs, and increased mortality [11, 13]. Reported mortality rates range from 25% to 60%, underscoring the importance of prevention and timely treatment [6].

To our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated bloodstream infections secondary to UTIs in Palestine.

Objectives

This study aimed to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients who developed bloodstream infections secondary to UTIs, to identify the most common causative pathogens and their distribution, and to analyze the relationship between empirical antibiotic regimens, risk factors, and mortality among patients admitted to An-Najah National University Hospital between 2018 and 2023.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational cohort study was conducted at ANNUH, a tertiary-care referral center in Nablus, Palestine. The study included adult patients diagnosed with bloodstream infections (BSIs) secondary to UTIs between January 2018 and December 2023.

Study population and sampling

A convenience sample was used, including all consecutive patients who met the predefined eligibility criteria during the study period. Adult patients aged 18 years or older with both positive blood and urine cultures for the same organism were included. Patients were excluded if they were younger than 18 years, had polymicrobial bloodstream infections not related to a urinary source, discontinued treatment for any reason, or were managed in the outpatient setting. After applying these criteria, a total of 140 patients were included in the final analysis.

Diagnostic criteria

BSI secondary to UTI was defined as the presence of the same pathogenic organism isolated from both urine and blood cultures obtained within one week of each other, suggesting the urinary tract as the primary source of infection. The diagnosis of UTI was based on medical records documenting a physician-confirmed diagnosis along with a concurrent positive urine culture during the same hospitalization.

Sample sampling method

Once the hospital BSIs secondary to UTIs admissions were identified, all of the data regarding the demographics, past medical data, and other related information for each and every patient meeting our inclusion criteria retrospectively added to a data collection form. Convenience sampling was used in this retrospective study.

Data collection and variables

Complete medical records of all eligible patients were reviewed, and relevant data were extracted. The collected information included demographic characteristics (age and gender); comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, chronic liver or kidney disease, chronic pulmonary disease, and malignancy (solid or hematologic); and risk factors including recent hospitalization within the previous six months, major surgery, presence of catheters, mechanical ventilation, ICU admission, and the use of immunosuppressive agents or corticosteroids. Immunosuppression was defined as the use of immunosuppressive medications or corticosteroid therapy within six months prior to hospital admission.”, ICU admission was defined as being in the intensive care unit at the time of blood culturing or having been discharged from the ICU within seven days prior to the bloodstream infection episode.”

Microbiological and clinical data included the causative pathogens, laboratory findings at diagnosis, empirical and definitive antimicrobial regimens, and clinical outcomes. Data on clinical parameters and outcomes were collected during the BSI period, which was defined as the time between the index blood-culture collection and the completion of antimicrobial therapy.

Only cases with positive blood and urine cultures for the same organism during the same clinical episode were included.

Validity and reliability

The data collection form was assessed for face and content validity by a panel of experts that were included internal medicine specialist, infectious and communicable diseases specialist and nephrologist. The final version of data collection form was developed based on the suggestions of the panelists.

Microbiological methods

Identification of bacterial and fungal isolates and antimicrobial-susceptibility testing were performed using standard microbiological techniques in the institutional laboratory, following CLSI guidelines (2023). Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as non-susceptibility to at least three antimicrobial classes.In our study, Pseudomonas aeruginosa isolates were resistant primarily to carbapenems, fluoroquinolones, and aminoglycosides, whereas Acinetobacter baumannii isolates exhibited carbapenem resistance in all detected cases.”

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test. For continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data and Student’s t-test for normally distributed variables. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with a 95% confidence interval.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences, ANNUH (Approval No.: Med.Oct.2023/41). Additional permission to access patient data was granted by ANNUH. As this was a retrospective study based on previously recorded data without direct patient interaction, the IRB waived the requirement for informed consent. Data confidentiality and patient anonymity were strictly maintained; only the research team had access to de-identified data, which were used solely for research purposes.

Results

A total of 2200 patients had positive blood cultures during the six-year study period. Among them, 142 patients met the inclusion criteria between January 2018 and December 2023, as they had both blood and urine cultures positive for the same organism. Two patients were excluded because they were younger than 18 years, leaving a total of 140 eligible patients for final analysis (Fig. 1).

Of these, 76 (54.3%) were males and 64 (45.7%) were females, with ages ranging from 19 to 90 years and a mean age of 57 ± 17.1 years. Patients younger than 65 years represented 62.1% (n = 87) of the study population, while 37.9% (n = 53) were 65 years or older. Table 1 summarizes the demographic characteristics and their association with in-hospital mortality.

Although there was no statistically significant association between gender or age group and in-hospital mortality (p > 0.05), a slightly higher mortality rate was observed among males and younger patients.

The majority of patients (88.6%) had at least one comorbid condition that complicated their clinical course. Hypertension was the most common comorbidity (39.3%), followed by diabetes mellitus (38.6%) and malignancy (58.6%).

A history of recent hospitalization within the preceding six months was reported in 105 patients (75%), and 54 (38.6%) underwent surgical procedures during the same admission.

Regarding immunologic and respiratory risk factors, 67 patients (47.9%) had received immunosuppressive therapy, 56 (40%) corticosteroids, 61 (43.6%) required intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and 57 (40.7%) required mechanical ventilation.

Mortality was significantly associated with ICU admission (p = 0.001), mechanical ventilation (p < 0.001), and the presence of a central venous line (p = 0.003) as determined by the Chi-square test (Table 2). Mortality was also notably higher among patients with chronic pulmonary disease, indicating that underlying respiratory conditions and critical illness substantially influenced outcomes in patients with bloodstream infections secondary to urinary tract infections.

In this study, 34 patients (24.3%) received monotherapy as empirical treatment, whereas 106 patients (75.7%) were treated with combination (combination therapy) regimens. The most commonly used antimicrobial in empirical therapy was vancomycin in 66 cases (47.1%), followed by penicillins 51 (36.4%), carbapenems 40 (28.6%), aminoglycosides 37 (26.4%), fluoroquinolones 34 (24.3%), cephalosporins 33 (23.6%), azole antifungals 26 (18.6%), and colistin 13 (9.3%). Among empirical regimens, no significant difference in mortality was observed between monotherapy and combination therapy groups (p = 0.155).

Regarding definitive antimicrobial therapy, the most frequently prescribed drugs were vancomycin 55 (39.3%), followed by carbapenems 39 (27.9%), azole antifungals 38 (27.1%), fluoroquinolones 36 (25.7%), and penicillins 37 (26.4%). Mortality was significantly higher among patients treated with vancomycin (p < 0.001) and fluoroquinolones (p = 0.012). Other agents, including tigecycline, colistin, and amphotericin, were used less frequently and did not show statistically significant associations with outcome (Tables 5 and 6).

These empirical and definitive antimicrobial regimens were consistent with the hospital’s antimicrobial policy and the IDSA guidelines, reflecting local resistance patterns observed during the study period.

Among the 140 patients who met the diagnostic criteria, the mean duration of antimicrobial therapy was 14.2 ± 10.6 days, and the mean length of hospital staywas 15.3 ± 17.8 days. Death and association with different variables

During the study period, 60 patients (42.9%) died during hospitalization. Male patients accounted for 36 of the deaths, but no statistically significant difference was observed between sexes. Likewise, age ≥ 65 years was not an independent prognostic factor for mortality (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

A significant association with mortality was identified among patients with chronic pulmonary disease (p = 0.018), where 15 patients died. Moreover, specific clinical interventions—central line insertion, ICU admission, and mechanical ventilation—were significantly correlated with increased mortality (p = 0.003, 0.001, < 0.001, respectively) (Table 2).

No significant difference in mortality was observed between infections caused by Gram-negative and Gram-positive organisms. However, Candida bloodstream infections were associated with significantly higher mortality (p = 0.027), with more than half of the affected patients dying (Table 3).

Laboratory findings demonstrated that lower values of white blood cell count (< 8.7 × 10⁹/L), hemoglobin (< 8.7 g/dL), and platelet count (< 108.4 × 10⁹/L) were significantly associated with death (p = 0.008, 0.027, 0.001, respectively) (Table 4).

Among treatment groups, no significant difference in mortality was noted between empirical monotherapy and combination therapy regimens. However, patients treated with Amphotericin had significantly higher mortality (p = 0.043), as all recipients of this agent died (Table 5). In contrast, definitive therapy with fluoroquinolones (p = 0.012) and vancomycin (p < 0.001) was also significantly associated with mortality (Table 6).

Given that Candida sp. and Escherichia coli were the most frequently isolated pathogens, further analysis revealed significant associations between Candida infection and ICU admission (p = 0.004), mechanical ventilation (p = 0.001), central line insertion (p = 0.003), and diabetes mellitus (p = 0.03).

In contrast, E. coli infections were more common in females (59.5%) and were significantly associated with recent hospitalization (< 6 months) (p = 0.001), ICU admission (p = 0.006), central line use (p = 0.003), and diabetes mellitus (p = 0.038). The increased frequency of Candida infections among ICU patients may be related to the presence of multiple comorbidities and immunosuppressive conditions, which predispose to fungal bloodstream infections.

Discussion

In all age groups, UTIs continue to be a major cause of morbidity, and in older age groups, they are the most common cause of sepsis [14, 15]. Studies on bloodstream infections in elderly individuals have been conducted in the past. According to these research, the most often found single source of BSIs is UTIs, which account for 40% of nosocomial BSIs and 32% of community-acquired BSIs [16, 17]. BSIs secondary to UTIs have been described in relation to the specific places of acquisition, community and hospital, and specific pathogens. Our study contributes to this growing body of evidence by providing the first comprehensive analysis of BSIs secondary to UTIs in Palestine, adding regional data to an area where published literature is limited.

Among 140 patients, males represented 54% of the sample, consistent with findings from the University of Michigan Hospitals and Health Centers (male prevalence 55%) [9], and with the Veterans Affairs cohort in the United States (96% male, median age 68) [12]. The mean age of our study (57 years) was slightly younger than in other international studies, possibly reflecting differences in life expectancy and healthcare-seeking behavior in our region. Mortality did not significantly differ between those ≥ 65 and < 65 years (40% vs. 45%), the study’s findings support earlier findings were higher in-hospital mortality was not linked to advanced age [18, 19]. In this set of observations, the majority of patients who died from BSIs secondary to UTIs also had other potentially fatal conditions.

Gram-negative bacteria (GNB) comprised the majority of isolates (59.2%), Gram-positive bacteria (12.2%) and nonbacterial pathogens (28.6%) were less frequent but clinically significant. The dominance of E. coli (26%), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae (18%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (7%), and Acinetobacter baumannii (7%) aligns with global data from large cohort studies [3, 6]. Also, in agreement with Veillette JJ et al., who analyzed over 1,200 cases of UTI-related GNB bacteremia and confirmed E. coli as the leading pathogen [20]. However, A. baumannii was significantly more frequent in our study. The increasing prevalence of resistant pathogens in critical care units remains a global concern, largely driven by inappropriate therapeutic and prophylactic antibiotic use. Consistent with prior studies identifying immunosuppression and mechanical ventilation as key risk factors for A. baumannii infection, 48% of our patients were immunosuppressed and 41% required mechanical ventilation [21, 22].

Gram-positive bacteria(GPB) accounted for 12% of isolates in our study with enterococcus being the most common gram-positive bacterium isolated (8%), followed by staphylococcus aureus (4%). These results are in conjunction with a retrospective cohort study conducted by Li C from 2018 to 2020, with staphylococcus formed 7% followed by enterococcus 6% [7]. Also similar to retrospective study took place in a large community-based, with staphylococcus formed 8% followed by enterococcus 4% [6].

A particularly striking observation was the high frequency of Candida species (29%), suggesting a potential shift in the epidemiology of secondary BSIs. Several recent studies confirm a similar rise in fungal etiologies in high-risk inpatients, especially those with malignancies, central venous catheters, and prolonged ICU [23–25]. In our study, 44% of Candida patients were in the ICU (p = 0.004), 41% required mechanical ventilation (p = 0.001), 39% had diabetes (p = 0.032), and 61% had central lines (p = 0.003). These findings align with global patterns emphasizing that broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure and critical illness promote fungal bloodstream invasion.

A particularly concerning finding in our study is that a substantial proportion of the bacterial isolates (41%) were multidrug-resistant (MDR), including ESBL, CRE, VRE, MRSA, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. More recently, a six-year national study from Oman demonstrated an alarming upward trend in MDR pathogens between 2017 and 2022, particularly among ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae, MRSA, CRE, and MDR A. baumannii [26]. These results, combined with our local findings, reinforce that the escalation of antimicrobial resistance in critical-care settings is likely driven by excessive empiric use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, limited antimicrobial stewardship practices, and suboptimal infection-control strategies in resource-constrained settings.

The observed 43% mortality rate in our study is alarmingly high compared to international series (Ackermann, 17%; Meyers, 30%) and may be attributed to multiple overlapping risk factors. These include malignancy, immunosuppression, invasive devices, mechanical ventilation, and the presence of fungal pathogens all strongly linked with worse outcomes [8, 11, 13]. In addition, longer length of hospital stays and ICU exposure further amplify risk [23].

Overall, this study highlights a concerning shift in pathogen spectrum, resistance trends, and mortality burden among patients with BSIs secondary to UTIs in Palestine. Compared to regional and global reports, our findings emphasize the urgent need for infection control interventions, rational antibiotic policies, and early risk stratification—particularly for immunocompromised or critically ill patients.

Limitations and strength

There are restrictions on our investigation. Initially, our data came from a single center. Research conducted in several geographical places and centers might offer a more comprehensive view. Second, since not all UTI patients had blood cultures taken routinely, we may have underestimated the prevalence of BSIs secondary to UTIs. Furthermore, it’s possible that some UTI patients received empirical antibiotic medication before blood cultures were taken, which might have decreased the sensitivity of the cultures. Third, because our analysis was retrospective in nature, this affects our ability to distinguish between UTIs that originated from a hematogenous location and those that were the result of a primary urinary infectious nidus. To combat this, though, we removed patients thought to have a distinct, competing bloodstream infectious source like cholangitis. Fourth, the specific information required to verify that the bloodstream infection connected to the urinary tract was the main cause of death was not available to us. Our case fatality rates therefore represent approximations, but still highlight the potentially fatal consequences of this serious healthcare-associated infection.

Despite these limitations, this study is the first in Palestine to determine the microbial profile of bloodstream infections secondary to urinary tract infections. Our research adds to the scant body of literature and suggests that the epidemiology of bloodstream infections related to the urinary tract may shift from being primarily caused by Gram-negative bacteria to being primarily caused by Candida species. This shift in epidemiology may be the result of ongoing efforts to reduce infections related to healthcare. An additional explanation for this finding is that the majority of high-risk hospitalized patients (such as those who are transplanted or neutropenic) receive prophylactic antibiotic coverage and more aggressive coverage, which preferentially eliminates bacterial pathogens while leaving space for fungal pathogens to grow more easily.

Conclusion

This study represents the first comprehensive investigation of BSI secondary to UTIs in Palestine. Our findings revealed a notable epidemiologic shift from Escherichia coli to Candida species as the predominant causative organism, a trend that carries significant implications for empirical therapy and infection control practices in Palestinian hospitals. The high mortality rate (43%) observed underscores the severity of these infections, particularly among critically ill patients requiring intensive care unit admission, mechanical ventilation, or central venous access. The identification of Candida bloodstream infections as a major contributor to mortality highlights the growing burden of fungal infections in tertiary-care settings, likely driven by increased antibiotic exposure, immunosuppression, and invasive procedures. In addition, the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacteria such as ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter baumannii poses a critical challenge to local antimicrobial stewardship and patient safety. Clinically, these results emphasize the urgent need to strengthen infection prevention strategies, implement antimicrobial stewardship programs, and promote early recognition and management of high-risk patients in Palestine. Further multicenter studies across Palestine are recommended to validate these findings and to better inform regional treatment guidelines. Addressing these emerging challenges is essential to improving patient outcomes, optimizing antimicrobial use, and enhancing the overall quality of care in the Palestinian clinical setting.
