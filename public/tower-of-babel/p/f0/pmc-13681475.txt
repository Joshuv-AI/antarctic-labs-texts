An ALOG family transcription factor promotes capsanthin production by activating capsanthin-capsorubin synthase expression in pepper
Authors: Fan Zhang, Xiaotong Sun, Zhen Zhuang, Zeyu Zhang, Zhi Yang, Qinyan Li, Jing Wang, Qing Fan, Xinjing Wang, Yaping Tang, Zhiquan Wang, Shengnan Zhao, Pengfei Pan, Xiaoyan Liang, Yulei Sui, Liyu Huang, Shengbao Yang, Xu Wang
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Chili pepper (Capsicum spp.) is one of the most widely cultivated vegetable crops worldwide. However, unlike its distant relative tomato, basic research aimed at dissecting the molecular mechanisms of gene function in pepper lags far behind. Consequently, the genetic basis of most important traits in pepper remains poorly understood and the number of elite genes identified for pepper breeding is limited. Capsanthin accumulation is a characteristic trait in pepper and is crucial for fruit quality. The formation of capsanthin is catalyzed by capsanthin-capsorubin synthase (CCS), using antheraxanthin and violaxanthin as substrates, which are synthesized through the core carotenoid biosynthetic pathway. The key structural genes in this pathway are highly conserved in plants, and some have been characterized in pepper, including phytoene synthase (PSY) (Huh et al., 2001), β-carotene hydroxylase (CHY) (Borovsky et al., 2013), and zeaxanthin epoxidase (ZEP) (Lee et al., 2021). However, how transcriptions of capsanthin biosynthetic genes (CBGs) are controlled remains largely unknown, and none of the transcription factors (TFs) reported to regulate capsanthin accumulation, such as MADS-RIPENING INHIBITOR (Wang et al., 2025), CaNAC2 (Song et al., 2025), and DIVARICATA1 (Song et al., 2023), have been evaluated for their potential use in pepper breeding to increase capsanthin content.

To identify candidate TFs associated with the expression of key CBGs, we performed an in vitro DNA pull-down assay coupled with mass spectrometry (DNApd-MS) using the 1.5-kb promoters of PSY1, PSY2, ZEP, and CCS as bait, respectively. We collected pepper pericarps at the breaker stage (∼35 days after anthesis) for protein extraction. Biotin-labeled DNA oligonucleotides corresponding to the PSY1, PSY2, ZEP, and CCS promoters were separately coupled to streptavidin resin and incubated with pericarp protein extracts; these were referred to as the “promoter” samples. A parallel assay using streptavidin resin without DNA served as a reference and was referred to as the “blank” control. The pulled-down proteins were analyzed by MS using label-free intensity-based quantification (Supplemental Figures 1A and 2). Enrichment analysis of proteins in the promoter samples relative to the blank control (p-value < 0.01 and fold change [FC] ≥ 2.0) identified a large number of proteins associated with the PSY1, PSY2, ZEP, and CCS promoters (Supplemental Figure 1A; Supplemental Table 1), although false-positive TFs may also have been identified due to non-specific DNA binding. Fourteen overlapping TFs from the four datasets were assessed for transcriptional regulatory activity using a previously established luciferase (LUC) reporter assay (Zhang et al., 2025) (Supplemental Figure 1B). The results showed that 11 of the 14 TFs regulated the activity of at least one reporter gene; CaLSH10 was the only TF able to activate two reporters, PSY1pro::LUC and CCSpro::LUC (p-value < 0.0005; Supplemental Figure 1C), suggesting that CaLSH10 may function as a transcriptional activator of CCS and PSY1.

CaLSH10, a member of the ALOG (Arabidopsis LIGHT-SENSITIVE HYPOCOTYL 1 and Oryza G1) TF family, contains a conserved ALOG domain which is responsible for DNA binding (Rieu et al., 2024). Although the ALOG family is conserved throughout the plant kingdom, only a few ALOG family genes have known functions, and no studies on pepper ALOG TFs have been published to date. The pepper genome (CA59) encodes 12 ALOG proteins (Supplemental Figure 3A), four of which are highly expressed in the pericarp transcriptome (transcripts per million [TPM] ≥ 10) (Liao et al., 2022), including CaLSH10 (Supplemental Figure 3B). CaLSH10 has a high structural similarity to the ALOG domain of Arabidopsis LSH3 (template modeling score [TM-score] = 0.698; root mean square deviation [RMSD] = 1.27; Supplemental Figure 3C). An amplified DNA affinity purification sequencing assay (ampDAP-seq) identified 266,360 high-quality CaLSH10-binding peaks across the pepper genome (Supplemental Table 2), approximately 5% of which were mapped to promoter regions (Supplemental Fig. 3A, B). A DNA motif search uncovered a 7-bp CaLSH10 recognition motif, YACTRTW (Y = T/C, R = G/A, W = T/A), which was present in 94% of CaLSH10-binding peaks (Supplemental Figure 1D). Importantly, we found that CBGs, such as PSY1, PSY2, ZEP, and CCS, were bound by CaLSH10 in putative regulatory regions (Supplemental Figure 4). Binding of CaLSH10 to the YACTRTW motifs in the CCS promoter was validated by fluorescent electrophoretic mobility shift assays (fEMSAs); full-length CaLSH10 recombinant proteins could bind to all tested dsDNA oligonucleotides, but not the ssDNA probes (Supplemental Figures 1E, 5A, and 5B). Microscale thermophoresis analysis and LUC reporter assays further revealed that mutations in the CaLSH10 recognition motif essentially abrogated DNA binding and transcriptional regulatory activity of CaLSH10 (Supplemental Figure 5C–5E). Overall, these results strongly suggest that CaLSH10 is a dsDNA-binding ALOG TF.

To investigate the function of CaLSH10 in pepper, we generated transgenic pepper plants overexpressing CaLSH10 under the control of the cauliflower mosaic virus 35S promoter (Figure 1A). Immunoblot analysis confirmed high expression of the CaLSH10 transgene in two independent lines (#23 and #25; Figure 1B). Whole-genome resequencing data confirmed that none of the T-DNAs were inserted into coding regions in either line (Figure 1C). Compared with the wild-type (WT; ST-8), no overt changes in fruit morphology or weight were observed in the CaLSH10-overexpression lines (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 6A). We then measured the contents of capsanthin and other carotenoid intermediates in fruits at the breaker and red ripe stages. Only capsanthin content was consistently elevated in both lines relative to WT (up to 50%; Figure 1E; Supplemental Figure 6B). This phenotype was consistent with the quantitative real-time RT-PCR (real-time RT-qPCR) result showing that CCS mRNA levels in CaLSH10-overexpressing plants were markedly higher than in WT plants (Figure 1F), which indicates that CaLSH10 is an activator of CCS expression and promotes capsanthin biosynthesis in pepper. Nevertheless, we noticed that CaLSH10 transcript levels in WT pepper during the fruit color transition were not correlated with those of CCS (Supplemental Figure 6C), which suggests the existence of additional regulatory layers. We then asked whether impairing CaLSH10 activity would affect capsanthin production. To account for potential functional redundancy between CaLSH10 and other ALOG TFs (e.g., CaLSH10-like TFs), we used chimeric repressor silencing technology to generate pepper plants in which CaLSH10 activity was suppressed (Hiratsu et al., 2003). A SUPERMAN repressor domain X (SRDX) was fused to the C-terminus of CaLSH10 to convert it into a dominant transcriptional repressor that blocks gene activation by functionally related ALOG TFs. This approach was first evaluated in tobacco using a CCSpro::LUC reporter assay. The results showed that CaLSH10-SRDX and CaLSH10 exerted opposite effects on CCS expression, and the activity of CaLSH10-like TFs was substantially repressed by co-expression of CaLSH10-SRDX (Supplemental Figure 6D–6F). Two CaLSH10-SRDX transgenic lines (#59 and #190) were obtained for phenotypic analysis (Figure 1G). qPCR analysis indicated that line #190 accumulated much higher levels of CaLSH10-SRDX mRNA than line #59 (Figure 1H). The T-DNA insertion sites in both lines were confirmed by whole-genome resequencing (Figure 1I). In line #59, the T-DNA disrupted Caz03g04710, which encodes an NF-X1-type zinc finger protein. Nevertheless, CCS expression and capsanthin content decreased to a similar extent in both lines relative to WT (Figure 1J–L). Real-time RT-qPCR analysis further revealed that Caz03g04710 expression differed markedly between lines #59 and #190 (Supplemental Figure 7), which indicates that the observed phenotypic alterations were unrelated to Caz03g04710 expression and were more likely attributable to CaLSH10-SRDX through inhibition of CaLSH10 and interference with other ALOG TFs. To determine whether CCS and other CBGs are directly regulated by CaLSH10 in pepper, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) and transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) assays using pericarps of pepper at the breaker stage from CaLSH10-overexpression line #23, with non-transgenic WT as the control (Supplemental Tables 3 and 4). Rigorous peak calling identified 300,208 genomic sites bound by CaLSH10, 34.1% of which occurred in coding regions (Supplemental Figure 8A). CaLSH10 primarily bound to proximal promoters close to transcription start sites (TSSs), with weaker enrichment around transcription termination sites (TTSs) (Supplemental Figure 8B). Furthermore, genes with higher expression levels generally displayed higher ChIP-seq signals in regions surrounding TSSs and TTSs (Figure 1M), thereby confirming that CaLSH10 enrichment correlates with gene activity in pepper. The recognition motif, HACTRTW (H = A/C/T, R = A/G, W = A/T), was significantly enriched within approximately 77% of the ChIP-seq peaks (p value = 10–41851), as expected. Peaks at the promoters of four CBGs, namely CCS, PSY1, CHYB2, and CRTISO, were directly detected (Figure 1N; Supplemental Figure 8C), but only CCS transcription was notably upregulated by CaLSH10 overexpression, consistent with the results of the LUC reporter assay in pepper cotyledons (Supplemental Figure 8D), indicating that CaLSH10 directly stimulates CCS transcription in pepper. However, the reasons for the nonproductive binding of CaLSH10 to other CBGs remain unclear.

Collectively, our findings shed light on the essential function and underlying mechanism of CaLSH10 in promoting capsanthin biosynthesis in pepper. Our genetic evidence also identifies CaLSH10 as a promising candidate gene for pepper breeding aimed at increasing capsanthin content. In addition, the identification of transcriptional regulators of CaLSH10 in pepper will be useful for enabling precise control over its expression levels.
