Intrathecal baclofen obviating the need for bladder stimulator use in a patient with secondary dystonia: illustrative case
Authors: Ryan K Wang, Victoria Jane Horak, Sunny Abdelmageed, Melissa A LoPresti, Maryam N Shahin, Benjamin Katholi, Jeffrey S Raskin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDChildren with cerebral palsy often have neurogenic bladders. Bladder function is further affected by complex medical management and multifactorial disease processes, leading to worsened function and poorer quality of life. Intrathecal baclofen (ITB) therapy has been used to treat hypertonia and spasticity, but implications in neurogenic bladder management have not been well described.OBSERVATIONSA 20-year-old female with a history of cerebral palsy and neurogenic bladder treated with sacral neuromodulation underwent ITB therapy and subsequently experienced improvement in bladder control, obviating the need for bladder stimulator use.LESSONSITB improves hypertonia and can effectively obviate the need for neurostimulation to treat neurogenic bladder in patients with cerebral palsy. Cerebral palsy (CP) is a nonprogressive brain injury within the first 2 years of life, most commonly associated with intraventricular hemorrhage during the perinatal period.1, 2 Most patients develop a movement disorder characterized by spasticity, dystonia, or mixed hypertonia.3 These movement disorders impact striated muscles throughout the body secondarily causing dysfunction of multiple organ systems.

Patients with CP have concurrent chronic conditions requiring polypharmacy. Bowel and bladder dysfunction is extremely common, with toileting affected by cognitive and physical disabilities, pathological movement disorders, and polypharmacy. Micturition is controlled by a complex neuronal network involving the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves to the bladder and urinary sphincters. Disruption of information transfer anywhere along this pathway can lead to dysfunction of the urinary system, commonly called “neurogenic bladder.”4 Neurogenic bladder is classically defined as a low-volume, spastic bladder caused by detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia (DSD) or a large, high-capacitance bladder incapable of fully emptying. It manifests from neurological injury to the pathways controlling bladder storage and voluntary voiding.5, 6 Neurogenic bladder increases the risk of urinary tract infections (UTIs) and cystitis, is socially stigmatizing, and reduces the quality of life.

While the mechanism is not completely understood, a sacral nerve stimulator (SNS) is used in neurogenic bladder control treatment, as it activates the pudendal nerve via S3 stimulation to increase sacral somatic efferent nerve impulses, thus improving voluntary pelvic floor activity contributing to bladder control.7 Intrathecal baclofen (ITB) is another treatment posited to improve bladder control via decreased spasticity of the striated sphincter muscle. Baclofen is a pre- and postsynaptic gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) B receptor agonist in the anterior horns of the spinal cord, leading to muscle relaxation via downregulation of high-voltage calcium channels, thus inhibiting neurotransmitter release.8 Low GABA activity has been shown to cause detrusor overactivity and DSD in in vivo studies.9 We present, to our knowledge, the first case of interactions between ITB and an SNS in a patient with CP.

Illustrative Case

A 20-year-old female with a history of spastic quadriplegia secondary to CP, bilateral hamstring release, neuromuscular scoliosis, deformity correction by posterior instrumented spinal fusion, and neurogenic bladder with SNS insertion at age 19 years presented with refractory hypertonia (Fig. 1). She had no history of UTIs but had experienced 5 years of the sensation of incomplete bladder emptying, difficulty with urinary voiding, sometimes requiring 30 minutes to void without complete emptying, and frequent nocturnal enuresis. Urodynamics demonstrated a small bladder capacity. She was taught clean intermittent catheterization (CIC) to reduce bladder volume. CIC was difficult due to the spinal fusion, and a Foley catheter was uncomfortable and caused bladder spasms. An SNS was implanted, and the patient was able to void her bladder in 30 seconds, with minimal post-void residuals (PVRs) noted. The patient also noted a decrease in urinary leakage to once per week with an urge to urinate. Because she had refractory mixed hypertonia, the patient underwent an ITB trial, resulting in improved modified Ashworth Scale (mAS) scores from 23/30 to 16/30 in the bilateral lower extremities and Barry-Albright Dystonia Scale (BADS) scores from 8/12 to 6/12 (Table 1). She was judged to be a good candidate for ITB pump insertion.

Surgery

The patient had a baclofen pump and catheter system implanted in the typical sterile manner. Her bolus baclofen test dose was administered through a heterotopic osteotomy of the spinal fusion. Her previous incision was opened, the osteotomy was accessed, and the spinal catheter was placed with the tip at T9. A 40-mL pump was placed in a subcutaneous pocket in the right subcostal margin (Fig. 2). The surgery lasted 92 minutes with 10-mL blood loss. The pump was filled with 500 µg/mL of baclofen and was programmed to deliver 100 µg daily by simple continuous dosing.

Postoperative Outcomes

As occurred during the ITB trial, the patient immediately noted subjective improvement in hypertonia, and there were objective reductions in mAS and BADS scores. The patient was discharged home in stable condition.

Three months following ITB pump insertion, the patient did not endorse having any UTIs, but she reported constant flooding of urine without urgency or stress, prompting urology to turn the SNS off. She was able to control her bladder without it and experienced one episode of urine leakage since the SNS had been deactivated. Given her current condition and response to therapy, it was decided to maintain the same dosage delivery of baclofen. Two months after SNS deactivation, urological evaluation demonstrated an elevated PVR of 326 mL. The patient reported an improvement in leakage, having only experienced leakage once since SNS deactivation. At this point, the patient decided to pursue a custom setting of SNS use in conjunction with the ITB pump to maximize coordinated emptying while balancing appropriate emptying and inappropriate retention. Three months after SNS deactivation, the patient ultimately decided to turn the bladder stimulator off, as she was still able to control her bladder with the baclofen pump (Fig. 3).

Discussion

We present, to our knowledge, the first report of interactions between ITB and an SNS in the setting of CP. This patient’s neurogenic bladder responded well to the SNS, and her spasticity responded to ITB; however, the combination caused unacceptable urinary incontinence. Although ITB is common in the CP population, its effects on neurogenic bladder are poorly described, particularly in the setting of an existing SNS.

Observations

Bladder function is controlled by somatic, sympathetic, and parasympathetic nerve activities. The primary bladder control center lies in the pontine micturition center (PMC), which receives input from the periaqueductal gray (PAG) and cells from the anterior and caudal hypothalamus.10 After receiving excitatory input from the PAG, triggered by bladder afferents stimulated by maximum urine capacity, the PMC can activate micturition, where the detrusor contracts, expelling urine, and the urinary sphincters relax, providing a conduit. This is termed the “spinobulbospinal reflex,” and its set point of activation (i.e., maximum bladder volume) serves as the threshold for triggering the micturition reflex (Fig. 4). The spinobulbospinal reflex thus acts as the on-and-off switch of the bladder.11–13 In the case of CP, the lower urinary tract function can be diminished due to a lack of neural and volitional control of the lower bladder and urethral sphincters as a result of suprapontine injury, leading to detrusor muscle overactivity paired with an inability to release sphincter tone during urination.5, 6

Neural control of micturition. Stretch receptors on the detrusor muscle detect bladder fullness and transmit the sensation to the spinal cord through parasympathetic pelvic nerves. The afferent fibers synapse with interneurons and activate the spinobulbospinal reflex, which projects neuronal signals through the PAG and the PMC. This stimulates parasympathetic motor fiber projections from the sacral region to contract the detrusor muscle and relax the internal urethral sphincter. The somatic motor fibers of the pudendal nerve are inhibited by the parasympathetic outflow and relax the external urethral sphincter to allow voiding.

Neuromodulation changes the function of the nervous system using medications or electricity.14 Our patient was at her urological baseline using an SNS with standard electrical dosing parameters. The addition of a second neuromodulation technique, ITB, resulted in a decreased bladder-filling sensation and incontinence.

An SNS is commonly used by adult patients with urinary dysfunction associated with neurological conditions, including Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and traumatic brain injury, although the exact mechanisms of neuromodulation via an SNS remain unclear.15–18 It is theorized that somatic afferents remain intact in cases of congenital neurological conditions, and through their stimulation, pediatric patients have an increased sensation of bladder fullness. There is level III evidence suggesting that neuromodulation for urinary dysfunction in pediatric patients can effectively increase bladder control among motivated pediatric patients and aid in the sensation of bladder fullness.19, 20 This is possible through pulsatile electrical stimulation of the S3 nerves via a surgically implanted device.19 Overactivity of the detrusor muscle is also inhibited, and the tone of the sphincter and pelvic floor is increased through the activation of myelinated Aδ fibers.21–23 This allows for increased time to reach the toilet and avoid episodes of incontinence. Additionally, the stimulation of the micturition reflex is potentially strengthened.9

Similarly, the effects of ITB on bladder control have been observed in case studies of adults with increased spasticity.18, 24 ITB contributes to bladder emptying by decreasing the spasticity of the urinary sphincters and detrusor muscle, allowing for the flow of urine.25 The increased urinary leakage observed when both the SNS and the ITB pump were active may signal overrelaxation of the urethral sphincter, leading to a loss of voluntary voiding control. Decreased urinary leakage occurred when the stimulator was turned off. Additionally, the loss of bladder control and its emergence after 3 months could be attributed to the cumulative effects of baclofen. The SNS could have maintained sufficient bladder control and prevented the full expression of baclofen’s effects until they reached a certain threshold.

Through increased sensitization of the Aδ myelinated nerve afferent cell bodies by the SNS and increased sphincter relaxation by ITB pump use, the spinobulbospinal reflex may have become more active. This, combined with decreased voluntary bladder control, could have led to increased episodes of incontinence.

Limitations

We review a novel case to highlight the use of the ITB pump and considerations regarding its use in incontinence management in spastic pediatric patients. This study is a retrospective single-case study and is therefore limited by recall bias and its sample size from which larger, statistically significant, or generalizable conclusions cannot be drawn. Furthermore, the data gathered are qualitative rather than quantitative, additionally limiting generalizability.

Lessons

Patients with CP have multiple medical conditions that limit patient and caregiver quality of life. We describe a case that highlights an unexpected interaction between ITB therapy and an SNS. When used alone, the SNS resulted in improved bladder control, whereas the concurrent use of ITB worsened the patient’s neurogenic bladder symptoms. We propose that the two forms of neuromodulation lead to an overrelaxation of the bladder through synergistic effects. Further investigation assessing formal urodynamics in similar patients may be warranted.
