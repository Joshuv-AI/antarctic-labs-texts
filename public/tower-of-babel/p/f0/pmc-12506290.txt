Systemic inflammation and fibrocalcific remodeling in aortic stenosis: the interplay of Lipoprotein(a), sex, and valve morphology
Authors: Veronika A Myasoedova, Vincenza Valerio, Valentina Rusconi, Francesca Bertolini, Ilaria Massaiu, Sergio Pirola, Paola Gripari, Valentina Mantegazza, Francesco Cannata, Kamil Stankowski, Saima Mushtaq, Gianluca Pontone, Paolo Poggio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic valve stenosis (AS) is a progressive valvular disease characterized by fibrocalcific remodeling of the aortic valve leaflets, contributing to significant cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. While valve calcification has been extensively studied, the relationship between valve fibrosis, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)], systemic inflammation, sex differences, and valve morphology remains less explored.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 45 patients with severe AS undergoing preoperative echocardiography and contrast-enhanced cardiac computed tomography (CT) at Centro Cardiologico Monzino. Aortic valve calcium and fibrosis volumes were quantified using threshold-based segmentation on CT images. Lp(a) was measured by ELISA, while a multiplex Luminex assay measured a panel of 44 cytokines. Patients were stratified by Lp(a) levels (≤ 50 vs. > 50 mg/dL), sex, and valve morphology (bicuspid vs. tricuspid) to explore associations with the fibrocalcific components of stenotic aortic valves.ResultsThe median Lp(a) level was 34.4 mg/dL (12.6; 93.4). No significant differences in valvular calcium load were observed between low and high Lp(a) groups. However, a modest increase in fibrotic volume was noted in patients with elevated Lp(a), particularly among men (p = 0.075). Several cytokines, including IL-1RA, IL-8, and TGF-α in men, and EGF, GM-CSF, IP-10, and IL-10 in women, were positively correlated with calcium burden. Fibrotic volume was associated with elevated eotaxin and PDGF-AA levels, with sex-specific patterns. Patients with bicuspid valve exhibited higher fibrocalcific volumes but lower circulating levels of several cytokines compared to patients with tricuspid valve morphology.ConclusionsIn patients with severe AS, circulating cytokine profiles show sex- and valve-morphology-specific associations with the fibrocalcific composition of the valve. However, Lp(a) was not associated with valvular calcification, and any relationship with fibrosis appeared at most modest, more evident in men. These results support the relevance of inflammatory profiling in assessing AS pathophysiology.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07061-6.Keywords: Fibrocalcific aortic valve stenosis, Lipoprotein (a), Circulating cytokines, Valve morphology

Introduction

Aortic valve stenosis (AS) is the most common progressive valvular disease characterized by fibrocalcific remodeling of the aortic valve leaflets, ultimately leading to increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1]. While the calcific component of AS has been extensively studied, emerging evidence suggests that fibrosis also plays a critical role in disease progression, particularly in relation to sex-specific and inflammatory mechanisms [2]. Among the various risk factors implicated in AS pathogenesis, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] has garnered increasing attention due to its pro-inflammatory and pro-calcific properties [3, 4].

Elevated circulating Lp(a) levels have been associated with the initiation and progression of aortic valve calcification (AVC), as demonstrated in several large-scale population studies [5–7]. Furthermore, the potential contribution of Lp(a) to fibrotic remodeling of the aortic valve, a process less well-characterized than calcification, has been less explored.

While inflammation has been recognized as a key contributor to AS progression [8], the precise role of systemic inflammatory activity, reflected by circulating cytokines, in fibrocalcific remodeling of stenotic aortic valve leaflets remains insufficiently characterized. Notably, the sex-specific impact of these inflammatory mediators on valvular calcification and fibrosis has yet to be systematically explored.

Additionally, aortic valve morphology, bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) vs. tricuspid aortic valve (TAV), is known to influence the pathophysiological course of AS, with BAV often associated with earlier onset and more aggressive disease progression [9]. However, the precise inflammatory profiles underlying these phenotypic differences remain unclear, highlighting a critical gap in our understanding of AS pathophysiology.

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between circulating Lp(a) levels, systemic inflammatory markers, and the fibrocalcific remodeling of aortic valve leaflets in patients with severe AS. We further explored how these associations differ by sex and valve morphology, providing new insights into the complex and heterogeneous pathobiology of AS.

Materials and methods

Study population

In this study, we enrolled consecutive AS patients who were scheduled for aortic valve replacement, had the availability of blood samples, and had good-quality cardiac computed tomography (CT) scans performed within 3 months before aortic valve surgery at Centro Cardiologico Monzino (CCM), IRCCS, from 2017 to 2021. Exclusion criteria were: AS due to rheumatic disease or radiotherapy, previous infective endocarditis, and severely impaired renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 30 ml/min/1.73 m2). Patients with BAV and TAV morphology were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of CCM (R518/16-CCM547), and the participants gave written informed consent. The study protocol conforms to the ethical guidelines of the 2024 Declaration of Helsinki.

Echocardiographic evaluation

2D TTE in various views was used to assess cusp morphology, mobility, and calcification. Left ventricular (LV) volumes and LV ejection fraction (LVEF) were measured using the biplane Simpson’s method. Doppler-derived parameters (peak aortic valve velocity, mean pressure gradient, and velocity-time integral) were assessed from multiple windows. Aortic valve area was calculated using the continuity Eq. (10).

CT examination and imaging acquisition

Contrast-enhanced cardiac CT (Revolution CT, GE Healthcare, WI) was performed using the following parameters: section configuration 256 × 0.625 mm, voxel size 0.625 mm, spatial resolution along the X-Y planes 0.23 mm, gantry rotation time 280 msec, and iterative reconstruction. A Body mass index-adapted (BMI) scanning protocol (tube voltage and current) was used [11]. All patients received a 50 mL bolus of contrast (Iomeprol 400 mg/mL, Bracco, Milan, Italy). Contrast attenuation values (Hounsfield Units, HU) and contrast-to-noise ratio were measured at the level of the ascending aorta. Total AVF was assessed based on HU ranging between 30 and 350, adjusting the upper threshold by increments of 25 HU in either direction until blood pool was not highlighted, as previously published for non-calcific aortic valve tissue evaluation [12]. The calcium and fibrosis volumes were assessed and quantified using the ImageJ (Java-based image processing program developed at the National Institutes of Health) thresholding method [13]. Indexed contrast-enhanced CT calcium volume and fibrosis volume were calculated by dividing the volume by the aortic annular area for each patient.

Blood sampling

Peripheral blood samples were drawn from patients before surgery, fasting in Vacutainer Z tubes (Vacutainer Systems, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes NJ, USA; Cod: 367614). Whole blood was incubated for two hours at 37 °C in a thermostat-controlled bath to promote coagulation. The blood was centrifuged at 2000 rcf for 10 min at room temperature. Serum sample in the supernatant was separated and aliquots were stored at -80 °C until analysis.

Circulating cytokine evaluation

Circulating cytokine levels were assessed using human XL cytokine magnetic Luminex performance assay 44-plex fixed panel (Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA; Cod: LKTM014). Briefly, after defrosting, each serum sample was centrifuged for five minutes at 4 °C at 16,000 rcf. Then, the samples were diluted 1:2 and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample and standard were loaded in duplicate into a 96-well plate containing 50 µL of microparticle cocktail. The plate was then incubated for 2 h, under constant shaking at 850 rpm at room temperature. After placing the beads on a magnet to retain beads with the bound antibodies and protein, it was washed three times, and incubated with 50 µL of diluted Biotin-Antibody Cocktail for 1 h, under constant shaking at 850 rpm at room temperature. Following these passages, each well was washed three times and incubated with streptavidin for 30 min, under constant shaking at 850 rpm at room temperature. The controls, samples, and standards were then washed three times, resuspended in wash buffer, and analyzed within 90 min using the Bio-plex 200 system (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA), with the following acquisition settings: at least 50 beads per region, with a sample time out of 45 s. Given known limitations of multiplex immunoassays, including potential antibody cross-reactivity and matrix effects that can inflate absolute values, we interpreted cytokine concentrations with caution and prespecified analyses focusing on relative group comparisons and rank-based associations rather than absolute quantification.

Lp(a) levels were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA; Cod: ab212165) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 28) and GraphPad Prism (Version 10.4.1). The normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared between groups using the Student’s t-test. Variables not normally distributed were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the Pearson Chi-square (χ2) test. Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the relationship between continuous variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Lipoprotein(a) and fibrocalcific remodeling in patients with severe AS

Elevated concentrations of Lp(a) have been previously linked to pro-inflammatory processes and accelerated progression of AS [3, 14]. In our cohort, the median Lp(a) level was 34.4 mg/dL (IQR: 12.6; 93.4 mg/dL). We assessed the fibrocalcific components of the aortic valve in patients with severe AS, stratifying these patients by current European guidelines on an Lp(a) threshold of 50 mg/dL, which is considered a high-risk level for cardiovascular events [15].

Our analysis revealed no significant differences in valvular calcium load between patients with low (≤ 50 mg/dL, 62% of patients) and high (> 50 mg/dL, 38% of patients) Lp(a) levels (Fig. 1A). However, the fibrotic component was modestly increased in patients with elevated Lp(a) levels (p = 0.088; Fig. 1B). Given the known sex-specific differences in the fibrocalcific composition of stenotic aortic valves, the population was stratified by sex. Even in this case, no significant differences in valvular calcium load were observed between low and high Lp(a) groups in either men or women (Fig. 1C and E, respectively). Nevertheless, the increase in the fibrotic component associated with elevated Lp(a) levels appeared to be primarily driven by the men subgroup (p = 0.075; Fig. 1D) rather than the women one (Fig. 1F). Finally, in sensitivity analyses treating Lp(a) as a continuous measure, we found no statistically significant linear or non-linear associations with indexed aortic valve calcium or fibrosis volumes.

Circulating cytokine levels in AS patients stratified by calcium

Inflammatory processes are known to precede and facilitate valve calcification [16, 17]. In particular, local pro-inflammatory cytokines contribute to the osteogenic transformation of valve interstitial cells, which are the main resident cell type of the aortic valve and are responsible for maintaining extracellular matrix homeostasis [18]. This, in turn, promotes calcium accumulation in the valve tissue [19]. To explore and clarify the potential involvement of systemic inflammation in this process, we measured a panel of serum cytokines and examined their relationship with the calcium load of stenotic aortic valves (Supplementary Table S1). Notably, no significant differences were observed between AS patients with low and high calcium load in clinical characteristics, Table 2. Our analysis revealed that several cytokines were significantly elevated in patients with a higher calcium burden (above median level; 925 mm3/mm2 for men, 376 mm3/mm2 for women) in the aortic valve leaflets. Notably, levels of epidermal growth factor (EGF), Fms-like tyrosine kinase 3 ligand (Flt3-Lig), interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (IP-10), macrophage inflammatory protein-1 beta (MIP-1β), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1/B7-H1), and transforming growth factor alpha (TGF-α) were significantly upregulated in the high-calcium group (Fig. 2A-G).

Circulating cytokine levels in AS patients stratified by fibrosis

Given that fibrosis in AS is also driven by local pro-inflammatory mechanisms contributing to early tissue remodeling [20], we assessed whether circulating cytokine levels are associated with high fibrotic component of stenotic aortic valve leaflets (above median level; 691 mm3/mm2 for men, 763 mm3/mm2 for women; Supplementary Table S3). Clinical characteristics of the AS patients with low and high fibrotic component represent in Table 3. Our analysis revealed that eotaxin and platelet-derived growth factor AA (PDGF-AA) were significantly upregulated in patients with a higher fibrotic component (Fig. 4). Of note, fibrotic volume showed a positive correlation with circulating eotaxin levels both in men and women (Fig. 5A and B, respectively). However, in women, we also found a significant positive association between PDGF-AA and fibrosis (Fig. 5C), while IL-1β, IL-1RA, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) were negatively correlated with the fibrotic component (Fig. 5D-F; Supplementary Table S4).

Circulating cytokine levels in AS patients stratified by valve morphology

Finally, to assess the impact of valve morphology on fibrocalcific remodeling and its association with systemic inflammation, we compared circulating cytokine profiles between patients with BAV and TAV morphology (Supplementary Table S5). Patients with TAV morphology exhibited a greater burden of comorbidities. In particular, they were older, more frequently dyslipidemic, and showed higher echocardiographic mitral peak velocity and pulmonary artery systolic pressure. Additionally, they were more likely to be treated with diuretics and antidiabetic drugs, while patients with BAV morphology demonstrated significantly larger aortic root and ascending aorta diameters (Table 4). Both the calcific and fibrotic components were higher in BAV patients compared to those with TAV (Fig. 6A and B). We did not observe any difference in Lp(a) levels between the two valve morphologies (Fig. 6C). However, several circulating cytokines were significantly downregulated in the BAV group, such as Flt3-Lig, MIP-3β, and TNF α, Fig. 6D-F), with no evident sex differences.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the relationship between circulating Lp(a) levels, systemic inflammatory markers, and the fibrocalcific remodeling of aortic valve tissue in patients with severe AS. While previous research has implicated elevated Lp(a) in the pathogenesis and progression of AS, particularly through pro-inflammatory and pro-calcific pathways [3], in our cohort of end-stage AS, no significant differences in valvular calcium load were observed between patients with low and high Lp(a) levels. However, we identified a trend toward increased fibrotic remodeling in those with elevated Lp(a), particularly among men. Furthermore, cytokine profiling revealed distinct inflammatory signatures associated with both calcific and fibrotic components of the aortic valve, with notable sex-specific differences. These observations suggest that systemic inflammation may differentially influence the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying AS. Additionally, patients with BAV appeared to have increased fibrocalcific remodeling of aortic leaflets but with reduced circulating levels of several cytokines, further underscoring the heterogeneity of AS pathobiology.

In our cohort of severe AS patients, fewer than 40% exhibited elevated Lp(a) levels (> 50 mg/dL), as defined by current European guidelines [15]. This finding aligns with recent data from Durr et al. [21], who reported that only one in four AS patients had markedly elevated Lp(a) levels (> 100 nmol/L), as per the Canadian Cardiovascular Society’s Lipid Guidelines.

Previous studies have shown a positive correlation between Lp(a) levels and AVC volume [5–7]; however, in our cohort, we did not observe a significant association between high Lp(a) levels and increased AVC burden. Therefore, our results suggest that this relationship may not persist at the end stage of the disease. In line with our results, the Rotterdam Study found that Lp(a) was strongly associated with baseline and incident AVC, but not with AVC progression [22]. Similarly, Lp(a) did not emerge as a significant predictor of calcium burden in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement [23]. These findings collectively suggest that while Lp(a) may play a role in the initiation of valvular calcification, its influence may diminish as the disease advances, potentially limiting its utility as a biomarker in late-stage AS.

To date, the relationship between the fibrotic component of AS valve leaflets, quantified by CT, and circulating Lp(a) levels is lacking. We observed a modest increase in fibrotic remodeling among patients with elevated Lp(a), a trend that appeared to be primarily driven by men. This finding is particularly intriguing given that previous literature has consistently reported a greater fibrotic burden in women AS patients [12, 24, 25]. The apparent sex-specific divergence in our results may reflect differences in disease progression and underlying inflammatory pathways, which warrant further investigation. However, when modeling Lp(a) as a continuous variable, no significant associations were observed with either the calcific or fibrotic components of the valve. This suggests that, in end-stage AS, any potential relationship between Lp(a) and aortic valve tissue remodeling is likely attenuated.

To explore this hypothesis, we further examined systemic inflammation by analyzing circulating cytokine profiles and their association with the fibrocalcific components of AS in men and women. Our findings revealed distinct inflammatory signatures linked to both calcification and fibrosis, with notable sex-related differences. In particular, in men, increased calcium burden was positively correlated with IL-1RA, IL-8, and TGF-α levels, while in women, higher calcium load was associated with elevated EGF, GM-CSF, IP-10 (CXCL-10), and IL-10. Many of these cytokines have known pro-calcific or pro-inflammatory roles. Notably, IL-8 has been implicated in promoting the progression of AVC progression in AS patients as well as in inducing calcification of human aortic valve interstitial cells [26, 27]. Additionally, TGF-α, a member of the EGF family, and EGF itself have been implicated in vascular calcification through their influence on calcium signaling and vascular cell differentiation [28, 29]. It has also been shown that GM-CSF increases inflammatory activity and may indirectly contribute to calcification [30, 31], while IP-10 (CXCL-10) promotes an inflammatory environment, inducing osteogenic transformation [32]. Of note, we found two cytokines (IL-10 and IL-1RA), with known anti-inflammatory properties, positively associated with increased AVC burden. IL-10 has previously been linked to valvular calcification, probably through genetic polymorphisms that affect its expression [33]. In contrast, the association of IL-1RA with increased calcium load in stenotic aortic valve leaflets of men warrants further mechanistic investigation to determine whether it reflects a compensatory feedback mechanism aimed at dampening inflammation or if IL-1RA itself plays an active role in promoting calcific remodeling under chronic inflammatory conditions.

Our results also showed a positive correlation between fibrosis of stenotic aortic valve leaflets and circulating eotaxin levels in both sexes. Eotaxin is a chemokine known to play a role in inflammation and fibrosis by recruiting eosinophils and other immune cells to sites of tissue injury [34, 35]. These immune cells, in turn, can activate fibroblasts and contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling. In addition, we observed a sex-specific association between fibrosis and PDGF, a well-established fibrogenic cytokine [36], which was positively associated only in women with higher fibrotic burden. These findings indicate that a distinct inflammatory stimulus may underlie fibrotic remodeling in men and women with AS. Together, our results highlight the complex interplay between systemic inflammation, sex, and fibrocalcific remodeling in AS, supporting the need for personalized approaches to better understand and manage disease progression.

The processes driving disease progression in AS patients appear to differ based on valve morphology, with BAV and TAV phenotypes exhibiting distinct pathophysiological features. Chronic inflammation plays a central role in BAV degeneration, and severe AS in BAV patients has been associated with a more aggressive inflammatory response, characterized by increased immune cell infiltration and neovascularization compared to TAV [37]. Consistent with previous findings [38, 39] and our data, both the calcific and fibrotic components were significantly higher in BAV patients than in those with TAV. However, a surprising observation in our cohort was that all cytokines found to be differentially expressed between BAV and TAV patients were significantly lower in the BAV group. This unexpected result could reflect a complex regulatory feedback mechanism or a shift in the inflammatory profile in advanced stages of the disease, where local inflammation at the tissue level may not be reflected by systemic cytokine levels. Moreover, in our cohort, the TAV subgroup was notably older and presented with a higher burden of comorbidities compared to the BAV subgroup, which could contribute to the elevated systemic inflammatory markers observed in the former. Further studies are needed to clarify whether these differences are due to intrinsic valve biology, disease stage, or compensatory immune modulation.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size (n = 45) may limit the statistical power to detect subtle differences, particularly in subgroup analyses stratified by sex or Lp(a) levels. The modest p-values observed in some comparisons (e.g., fibrotic component differences by Lp(a) levels and sex) suggest potential trends that may not have reached statistical significance due to limited sample size. Furthermore, future studies with larger cohorts will be essential to validate the observed associations using multivariate approaches. Second, while we stratified patients by sex and valve morphology (BAV vs. TAV), the study may still be underpowered to fully explore the complex interactions between sex, valve phenotype, and inflammatory pathways. Additionally, the use of a single Lp(a) threshold (50 mg/dL) based on current guidelines may not capture the full spectrum of Lp(a)-related risk in this population. In addition, the Lp(a) assay employed was not isoform-independent. Consequently, the measurements could be affected by analytical bias arising from apo(a) size polymorphism, particularly due to the variable number of kringle IV-2 repeats. This potential bias should be considered when interpreting the results. Third, medication use was common in this cohort, and because statins, ACE inhibitors, and beta-blockers can affect circulating cytokines, residual confounding by concomitant therapy cannot be excluded. Our study was underpowered for medication-stratified or fully adjusted analyses. Fourth, although we assessed a broad panel of cytokines, local tissue-level expression and activity were not evaluated, which may provide more direct insights into the mechanisms driving fibrocalcific remodeling in AS. Finally, the multiplex Luminex platform used for cytokine profiling is susceptible to antibody cross-reactivity and matrix effects that may overestimate absolute concentrations; therefore, absolute levels should be interpreted with caution.

In conclusion, in patients with severe AS, we observed sex- and valve-morphology-specific associations between circulating cytokine profiles and the fibrocalcific composition of the valve. Lp(a) levels were not associated with calcific burden, but showed a modest association with greater fibrosis, more evident in men. Cytokine patterns associated with calcification differed from those linked to fibrosis, with several mediators showing sex-related differences. Notably, lower circulating cytokines in bicuspid aortic valve, despite greater fibrocalcific burden, may reflect local tissue processes not captured systemically. Given the cross-sectional design, restriction to severe end-stage AS, and potential residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Overall, the results highlight the heterogeneity of AS biology and motivate longitudinal and mechanistic studies, while emphasizing the value of integrating inflammatory and morphological profiling in future assessments of AS pathophysiology.
