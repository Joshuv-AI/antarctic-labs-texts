Greater Splanchnic Nerve Ablation in Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction: a Mini-Narrative Review of Emerging Evidence
Authors: Ikponmwosa Jude Ogieuhi, Chidera Stanley Anthony, Victor Oluwatomiwa Ajekiigbe, Habibah Lami Jiwo, Ayodeji Akinmeji, Victor Olamiposi Olaiya, Adedamola Solomon Oyenuga, Tolu Comfort Oladipo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExercise intolerance and volume overload are key symptoms that characterize the complex syndrome called heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). The increased pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) also contributes significantly to these symptoms. A promising intervention, greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation, is an approach that alleviates this volume overload by modulating sympathetic tone. This mini-narrative review discusses data from six (6) clinical trials investigating the effects of GSN ablation in HFpEF. These studies focus on procedural safety, hemodynamic outcomes (PCWP), clinical measures (NYHA class, KCCQ score), and adverse events. The trials varied in terms of sample size, patient characteristics, and follow-up durations, with a total of 259 patients. In addition, the outcomes were assessed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. The studies report positive outcomes with reductions in PCWP, especially during exercise. The NYHA classes and KCCQ scores were also improved in patients. Exercise capacity was measured by the 6-min walk test, and studies reported notable gains. NT-proBNP levels were also decreased, indicating an improvement in heart failure status. Some trials reported mild procedural complications such as pain and site injection; however, no significant adverse events were reported. GSN ablation presents a therapeutic approach for patients with HFpEF. While short-term benefits are evident, further large-scale randomized trials are necessary to confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of this procedure. In addition, its application in patients with diverse characteristics and comorbidities needs to be investigated and validated before it is incorporated into clinical practice.Keywords: Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, Pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, Greater splanchnic nerve ablation, Therapy, Clinical trials

Introduction

Background of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a complex clinical condition. Its diagnosis and treatment remain difficult due to its complex pathophysiology, diverse epidemiological features, and the continual evolution of diagnostic criteria and therapeutic strategies [1]. Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) refers to a form of heart failure in which the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) remains at 50% or higher. According to the European Society of Cardiology, HFpEF is diagnosed when patients exhibit typical heart failure symptoms and signs, along with structural or functional heart abnormalities and/or elevated natriuretic peptide levels, in the presence of preserved LVEF [1, 2]. Heart failure (HF) affects approximately 6.5 million adults in the U.S, and around 50% of these cases are classified as heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). [3]. Despite its growing prevalence, effective treatment options for HFpEF remain limited, as most pharmacologic interventions have failed to show a mortality benefit [4].

Role of the sympathetic nervous system and the splanchnic circulation

The sympathetic nervous system plays a crucial role in maintaining cardiovascular homeostasis, particularly in regulating venous tone and distributing blood volume. One key component of this system is the splanchnic circulation, which acts as a dynamic blood reservoir capable of storing approximately 25% of the total blood volume [5]. The splanchnic vascular compartment comprises splanchnic arteries and veins. The splanchnic arteries represent all arterial vessels of the splanchnic vascular compartment, including the stomach, small intestine, large intestine, pancreas, spleen, and liver. The splanchnic veins represent the pooled venous blood from all these organs [6]. The sympathetic nervous system controls splanchnic vascular tone via the greater splanchnic nerve (GSN). Under stress, such as during exercise, GSN activation can lead to pronounced vasoconstriction, redirecting blood to the central circulation and potentially exacerbating chronic heart failure [7]. In healthy individuals, sympathetically driven splanchnic vasoconstriction shifts blood to the heart and lungs during exercise. In heart failure (HF), however, this shift causes excessive increases in cardiac filling pressures, worsening exercise intolerance and potentially triggering decompensation [8].

Rationale for GSN ablation in HFpEF

Several studies have demonstrated an improvement in cardiac function in patients with HFpEF following GSN ablation. GSN ablation targets the sympathetic nerves supplying the splanchnic vessels to promote vasodilation, enhance venous capacitance, and retain blood within the abdominal compartment. GSN blockade lowers pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) at rest and during exercise while improving cardiac output and exercise capacity [9]. Gajewski et al. demonstrated that permanent right GSN ablation in HFpEF patients led to reduced intracardiac filling pressures during exercise as early as 24 h post-procedure [10]. Splanchnic nerve ablation for volume overload represents a novel treatment strategy for HFpEF, with the potential to improve patients’ quality of life, physical ability, and overall outcomes [11].

This review provides an overview of the existing clinical and scientific evidence on greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation as a treatment for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). It examines the physiological basis, insights from preclinical studies, initial results from human trials, and potential future developments in this emerging therapy. This review also briefly outlines how the procedure is performed and summarizes any available guideline perspectives.

Methodology

Search strategy and data sources

This study was conducted as a mini-narrative review with a focus on evaluating clinical evidence regarding greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation as an interventional therapy in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF).

An extensive review of relevant published literature was done across the following databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar. Search terms and keyword combinations included “greater splanchnic nerve”, “ablation”, “heart failure with preserved ejection fraction”, “HFpEF”, “splanchnic nerve modulation”, and “interventional therapy in heart failure”. To optimize search results, Boolean operators “AND”, “OR” were used with keyword searches in the databases reviewed.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Solely original studies were eligible for inclusion in the scope of this study, with a focus on clinical trials, primarily randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Reviews, editorials, reports, and expert opinion articles were excluded, as well as non-interventional studies, such as cross-sectional studies, cohort studies, and case–control studies, due to the insufficient evidence that these types of studies provided regarding the efficacy of the intervention. Only studies in the English language were considered for inclusion.

The population considered in this study consisted of patients (aged ≥ 18 years) with a diagnosis of HFpEF who were undergoing greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation, regardless of ethnicity or gender.

Data extraction and synthesis approach

A narrative synthesis approach was used, given the heterogeneity across studies. Data were extracted on study design, patient characteristics, procedural details, clinical outcomes (including pulmonary capillary wedge pressure, exercise tolerance, and New York Heart Association [NYHA] Class), and adverse events.

In the synthesis of the reviewed studies, emphasis was on efficacy, safety, and study design characteristics. Findings were summarized descriptively, with particular attention to clinical outcomes, procedural safety, and mechanistic insights related to GSN ablation in HFpEF (Fig. 1).

Results

Overview of included studies

The focus of this study was based on six clinical trials conducted between 2021 and 2024, with the majority designed as single-arm, randomized studies.

Works by Fudim and colleagues [8, 9, 11] contributed 50% of the reviewed studies, which had varying study designs and sample sizes, including 11, 90 and 110 participants, with follow-up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months for the first and second studies, and a 1.3-month follow-up duration for the third study, which had 110 participants [8, 9, 11]. The majority of the participants in these studies were elderly overweight females (BMI > 30 kg/m2) with NYHA II heart failure and a high prevalence of coexisting co-morbidities like hypertension, atrial fibrillation, alongside hemodynamic parameters such as pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≥ 15mmhg at rest or > 25mmhg during exercise and left ventricular ejection fraction > 50% [8, 9, 11].

In 2022, a group of scientists, conducted a single-arm, two-center study consisting of 10 participants, majorly overweight (BMI > 28 kg/m2) males with NYHA III heart failure and a high prevalence of comorbidities such as hypertension, atrial fibrillation, alongside hemodynamic parameters such as pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≥ 15mmhg at rest or ≥ 25mmhg during exercise, left ventricular ejection fraction > 46%. The participants were followed up at 1, 3, and 12 months [10].

Another single arm, two center study was conducted, constituting of 10 patients, elderly and overweight (BMI > 29 kg/m2) with an equal gender distribution in NYHA III heart failure alongside co-morbidities like hypertension, atrial fibrillation, diabetes with hemodynamic parameters such as pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≥ 15 mmHg at rest or ≥ 25mmhg during exercise, left ventricular ejection fraction > 47%. The participants were followed up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months [12].

In 2025, researchers conducted a prospective, multicenter, double-blinded study consisting of 90 participants, primarily overweight females with NYHA II & III heart failure, characterized by hemodynamic parameters such as pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≥ 15 mmHg at rest or ≥ 30 mmHg during exercise, and a left ventricular ejection fraction > 55%. The participants were followed up at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months [13] (Table 1).

Intervention details

The studies reviewed utilized two primary modalities in denervating the great splanchnic nerve, ranging from minimally invasive procedures using radiofrequency to thoracoscopic surgery.

Right-sided greater splanchnic nerve ablation, also known as splanchnic ablation for volume management (SAVM), can be performed endovascularly using an intravascular catheter via the intercostal and azygos veins with transvenous radiofrequency ablation. This procedure was well tolerated by all participants [8, 9, 11, 13].

Splanchnicectomy can also be done surgically via surgical ablation of the right-sided greater splanchnic nerve using a multiport video-assisted thoracoscopic approach. The operation was successful among participants, with significant results evident as early as 24 h post-operation [10, 12].

Outcomes reported

In the study conducted by Fudim et al. [8], baseline assessments included a 6-min walk test, the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), NYHA functional class assessment, laboratory (blood) testing of N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and comprehensive echocardiography, which were performed before and after the SAVM. The baseline KCCQ score rose from 48 (IQR: 35–52) to 65 (IQR: 58–77) and 80 (IQR: 77–88) at 1 and 12 months, respectively, with p < 0.05. The 6-min walk test distance increased from a baseline of 292 ± 82 m to 341 ± 88 m at 1 month and 359 ± 75 m at 12 months, with p < 0.05. The NT-pro BNP decreased from a baseline of 1,292 ± 1,186 pg/ml to 1,202 ± 797 pg/ml (p = 0.585), 379 ± 165 pg/ml (p = 0.039) at 1 and 12 months, respectively. All patients also improved by at least one NYHA class within the first month, and this improvement was sustained for up to 12 months. There were four procedure-related adverse events, of which one was acute bradycardia attributed to the anesthesia, and the other was acute renal failure attributed to the amount of contrast used during the procedure. The other two were access site hematoma and diarrhea [8].

Another study conducted by Fudim et al. [9] involving SAVM evaluated baseline assessments at 1 and 3 months, and 39% (p = 0.02) and 50% (p < 0.01) of patients experienced at least one NYHA class improvement compared to the baseline, respectively, at 1 month and 3 months post-SAVM procedure. The KCCQ score improved by 22.1 points (p < 0.01) at 1 month and 18.3 points (p < 0.01) at 3 months. The median NT-proBNP was 334 pg/ml at baseline but decreased to 262 pg/ml (p < 0.05) and 291 pg/ml (p < 0.05) at 1 and 3 months, respectively. The 6-min walking distance changed from 283 m at baseline to 276 m and 342 m at 1 and 3 months, respectively, post SAVM procedure. The peak exercise pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) decreased from 39.5 to 31.9 at p = 0.01 at 1 month post SAVM procedure. There were no changes in left ventricular systolic function, and no serious adverse events occurred [9].

Gajewski et al. [10] study on GSN ablation revealed significant reduction in PCWP 24 h after the procedure as compared to baseline during 20- minute walk test (baseline (28.0 ± 4.3 mmHg) to 24 h (19.6 ± 6.9 mmHg); p = 0.0124) and peak exercise (baseline (25.6 ± 2.4 mmHg) to 24 h (17.4 ± 5.9 mmHg); p = 0.0025) with no serious adverse event reported [10].

Malek et al.'s [12] study on surgical ablation of GSN revealed a reduction in resting central venous pressure at subsequent follow-up intervals compared to baseline. The average PCWP at rest was 16.5 mmHg; however, a significant decrease was observed, with resting PCWPs of − 4 mmHg, − 2.5 mmHg, and − 5 mmHg at 1, 3, and 12 months, respectively. Approximately 70% of patients reported an improvement of one NYHA functional class after one month, and all patients experienced a reduction of at least one NYHA functional class by three months. In addition, two patients experienced a decrease of two NYHA classes by 12 months. Overall, there was no change in arterial blood pressure, N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide level, left ventricular ejection fraction following GSN ablation, and no observed serious adverse events were reported [12].

Salah et al. (2025) conducted a study involving SAVM comparing a treatment group with a sham group. It was noted that at 1 month, there was a significant decrease in the treatment group’s estimated stressed blood volume (eSBV) across all right heart catheterization (RHC) exercise stages compared to baseline, with an average reduction of −256 ± 65 mL (p < 0.01). There were also reductions in both right atrial pressure and PCWP across all RHC groups, averaging − 3.4 ± 0.8 mmHg (P < 0.001) and − 4.2 ± 0.8 mmHg (P < 0.001), respectively, with adverse effects reported [13].

Fudim et al. [11] conducted a study on SAVM comparing a treatment group with a sham group; however, the study did not identify any difference between the PCWP in the two groups, and no difference in adverse events was reported between the two groups [11].

Discussion

There is growing evidence suggesting greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation in HFpEF could offer potential symptomatic and hemodynamic benefits in selected patients. Initial research consistently reported improvements in pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), functional capacity, and quality of life [11, 12]. In these studies, it was also noted that exertional symptoms were alleviated and exercise tolerance improved after GSN ablation, through the modulation of central blood volume.

More rigorous trials are needed, however, as the REBALANCE-HF randomized controlled trial failed to show any statistically significant improvements in the primary endpoints after one month. This discrepancy with earlier trials could be attributed to patient heterogeneity, the possible placebo effect, and trial design [11, 13–15].

Worth discussing are the favorable trends in the secondary endpoints in patients with HFpEF, as well as post hoc findings. These include reductions in estimated stressed blood volume (eSBV) and hints at physiological changes that might not necessarily translate into immediate clinical improvement but could be significant over time [13].

These findings highlight the central challenge in managing HFpEF, further underscoring the heterogeneity and complexity of the syndrome. In contrast to heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) encompasses a spectrum of pathophysiological phenotypes, including hypertensive remodeling, obesity-related inflammation, and chronotropic incompetence [16, 17].

There is reasonable plausibility to argue that GSN ablation primarily is beneficial to a subset of HFpEF patients with increased sympathetic tone and significant volume redistribution [18]. Future research should consider phenotyping and strategies for stratification to identify responders using echocardiographic parameters, biomarkers, or stress hemodynamics that could benefit from GSN ablation.

Regarding the biological plausibility and mechanistic insights, the rationale for GSN ablation is targeted and compelling. In HFpEF, the diastolic reserve is markedly impaired, with dynamic congestion during exertion exacerbated by overactivity of the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in constriction of the splanchnic venous reservoir [19]. By ablating the GSN, the reflex system is disrupted, allowing the splanchnic vessels to relax and blood to be redistributed away from the central circulation, thus reducing preload and pressure in the pulmonary circulation during exercise [19]. The consistent reduction in NT-proBNP levels and PCWP across numerous studies supports this mechanistic model [8–11, 13]. See Fig. 2.

Hemodynamic effects of greater splanchnic nerve ablation

The concept of “decongestive sympathectomy” through GSN modulation represents a novel approach that differs from conventional therapies, such as diuretics or vasodilators, which often have limited efficacy and/or adverse effects [20].

Importantly, studies have also shown GSN ablation can lead to hemodynamic relief without compromising the systolic function in HFpEF patients or necessitating aggressive fluid removal [21].

Several knowledge gaps remain, including the durability of interventions, the optimal timing (early versus late HFpEF), and whether repeated or bilateral interventions yield additive benefits—all of which are currently unknown.

In addition, studies exploring neurohormonal, endothelial, and inflammatory pathways following ablation should be conducted. The absence of significant changes in left ventricular ejection fraction post-ablation reaffirms that its primary benefits lie in modulating preload rather than contractility. The long-term safety and efficacy of this treatment have yet to be fully characterized.

Although the current findings suggest that GSN ablation could become a useful adjunct in selected HFpEF patients, especially those with exercise-induced congestion, integrating it into routine clinical care remains a distant prospect. Future studies should employ larger, multi-center trials with extended follow-up, rigorous sham controls, and pre-specified responder analyses. Temporary GSN blocking with anesthetic agents like lidocaine or bupivacaine has previously been used to study the role of the splanchnic nerves in acute and chronic heart failure. In the Splanchnic-HF trials, transient GSN blocking resulted in rapid decreases in PCWP and systemic vascular resistance, accompanied by simultaneous increases in cardiac index and symptomatic relief of symptoms in both HFrEF and HFpEF patients [18, 19, 22].

However, the results were short-lived, which led to a demand for a more sustainable and durable approach. Surgical excision of the right GSN, as described by Málek et al. [12] in their study, gave effects equivalent to the endovascular approach, which showed significant decreases in exercise PCWP, improved NYHA class, and better KCCQ scores. However, the invasiveness of surgery limited its scalability, including consequences such as intercostal artery injury and post-thoracotomy discomfort [23].

In comparison, the endovascular approach offers a less invasive and safer technology with equivalent efficacy and faster recovery periods [13, 14, 21].

When compared with other interventional techniques in HFpEF, such as inter-atrial shunt devices, baroreceptor stimulation, and renal denervation, GSN ablation stands out for its focused mechanism and favorable safety profile. Inter-atrial shunting, although useful in selected patients, poses hazards such as right-to-left shunting, device thrombosis, and paradoxical embolism [22]. Baroreceptor treatments and renal denervation are still being studied for long-term safety and effectiveness in HFpEF [23]. Also, unlike sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), which improve outcomes via metabolic and diuretic effects [24]. GSN ablation directly modifies neurovascular dynamics, thereby bypassing the limitations of systemic pharmacotherapy.

Given its specific influence on splanchnic volume regulation, GSN ablation may be best positioned as a supplementary treatment for symptomatic HFpEF patients with volume-sensitive dyspnea and exercise intolerance. It may be beneficial in patients with maintained renal function and stable comorbidities who are resistant or intolerant to high-dose diuretics, and it could serve as an adjunct for patients’ refractory to medical therapy or ineligible for shunts due to right heart dysfunction. Its procedural nature aligns it closer to device-based interventions, yet its focus on sympathetic regulation sets it apart, potentially creating a niche for patients with splanchnic-driven congestion. Comparative trials are needed to explore its efficacy against these alternatives; however, its unique mechanism suggests a potential role in personalized HFpEF management. See Fig. 2.

Variation in outcomes across study designs

The differences in outcomes between the earlier observational studies and the later randomized trials are essential to acknowledge. The initial studies were primarily small, single-arm or open-label designs, which may have introduced selection bias and placebo effects, leading to more favorable results. In contrast, the randomized and sham-controlled trials applied stricter methodology, included more heterogeneous patient groups, and had shorter follow-up periods. These factors may have contributed to the neutral findings observed. It is also possible that only a specific subgroup of HFpEF patients benefits from GSN ablation, and this effect becomes less apparent when broader populations are studied without stratification by subgroup. We advocate for larger randomized trials with better phenotyping and longer follow-up to clarify these differences.

Reported adverse and secondary effects of GSN ablation

Although early studies report that GSN ablation is generally well tolerated, some adverse and secondary effects have been noted. The most commonly reported events are transient hypotension, mild abdominal discomfort, and procedural-site pain [12, 21]. In endovascular approaches, vascular access complications such as hematoma or bleeding have been rare but documented [12]. Surgical techniques have shown higher risks, including intercostal nerve irritation, pleural injury, and post-thoracotomy pain [25, 26]. To date, no significant long-term neurological deficits have been reported; however, data on durability and delayed complications remain limited. Careful patient selection and procedural planning are essential to minimize these risks.

Guideline position on GSN ablation

Current major heart failure guidelines, including those from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) [27] and the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) [28], do not yet include recommendations for greater splanchnic nerve ablation in HFpEF. This is mainly because the available evidence comes from small early-phase studies, and large randomized controlled trials are still ongoing. At present, GSN ablation is considered an investigational therapy and is not part of standard clinical practice. Future guideline updates may address this approach as more robust clinical data become available.

Future directions

Advancing GSN ablation as a viable therapy for HFpEF requires targeted study and development. Patient selection is crucial, as identifying responders who are likely to have a high sympathetic tone or a significant exercise-induced PCWP increase can enhance efficacy. Subgroup analyses from REBALANCE-HF demonstrate phenotypic diversity, with some patients showing eSBV reductions exceeding 250 ml. Biomarker profiling (e.g., NT-proBNP) or imaging studies, such as splanchnic vein capacitance, might refine inclusion criteria, as observed in case scenarios where patients with atrial fibrillation and hypertension benefited more. Procedural optimization is another priority. Comparing surgical versus endovascular procedures can help determine the ideal approach, balancing efficacy against invasiveness. The Satera System’s radiofrequency ablation has proven feasibility [20], but advances such as real-time hemodynamic monitoring during ablation could further increase precision. Long-term safety data are crucial, given reports of chest discomfort or orthostatic hypotension in several groups [12, 21]. Larger trials should track these over 2–5 years, equivalent to follow-ups in shunt investigations.

Future research should also prioritize multicenter, randomized controlled trials with sufficient power (e.g., n > 200) to demonstrate both efficacy and safety. A systematic review aggregating current data could inform trial design, addressing gaps in adverse event patterns and consistency of follow-up. Comparative effectiveness trials against SGLT2i or shunts would establish GSN ablation in the treatment hierarchy, potentially integrating it into guideline-directed therapy [29]. For instance, a hypothetical case of a 70-year-old woman with NYHA III HFpEF, unresponsive to diuretics, would benefit from GSN ablation if trials support its relevance in refractory cases. Finally, mechanistic investigations examining neurohormonal regulation, gut peptide release (e.g., GLP-1), and autonomic function following GSN denervation could provide valuable insights into the systemic consequences of GSN denervation, thereby widening its indications beyond HFpEF. Ultimately, these initiatives will determine whether GSN ablation transforms from an experimental technique to a cornerstone of HFpEF therapy.

Conclusion

Greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation has emerged as a promising therapeutic option for patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), particularly those with symptoms related to volume overload who may not respond well to conventional treatments. This review highlights the role of Greater splanchnic nerve (GSN) ablation in improving clinical outcomes by targeting the splanchnic circulation, which contributes to volume overload and elevated cardiac pressures during physical activities.

Based on the analysis of six studies that met our criteria, both surgical and minimally invasive endovascular approaches to GSN ablation were associated with reductions in pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), improvements in New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, and increased exercise tolerance. These outcomes are believed to result from increased splanchnic venous capacitance, which allows more blood to be sequestered within the abdominal compartment, thereby decreasing preload and reducing cardiac and pulmonary vascular stress during physical activity. While early findings are encouraging, several limitations should be noted. Most studies are small, with relatively short follow-up periods, and differ in study design, procedural technique, and patient selection. Although improvements in quality of life and hemodynamic parameters have been frequently reported, findings from larger controlled trials have been mixed. Further research is essential to determine the most appropriate patient groups, optimal procedural methods, and long-term safety of GSN ablation. There is a particular need for larger, multicenter, randomized controlled trials and comparative studies evaluating its effectiveness alongside existing medical and interventional therapies for HFpEF. With ongoing research and refinement, GSN ablation may become a valuable therapeutic addition for this challenging and increasingly common condition.
