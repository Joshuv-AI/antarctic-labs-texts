Current pesticide suicide surveillance methods used across the African continent: a scoping review
Authors: Maxine Jonker, Ayanthi Karunarathne, Leah Utyasheva, Michael Eddleston, Flemming Konradsen, Hanna-Andrea Rother
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide poses a significant global health challenge in low- and middle-income countries and is a leading cause of death worldwide. Although global suicide rates have decreased by 36% between 2000 and 2019, the decline in Africa has not been as substantial. Suicide surveillance data, particularly from pesticide ingestion, is lacking.ObjectivesThis scoping review aimed to identify pesticide suicide surveillance methods currently used in African countries and to assess their viability for improving reporting of pesticide suicide deaths.DesignA scoping review was conducted using a five-stage methodological framework across several databases.Eligibility criteriaPeer-reviewed articles published in English that investigated (a) pesticide poisoning and (b) suicide in humans only and specific to the African continent.Sources of evidenceMEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus (with substantial Embase content), Web of Science Core Collection, Biological Abstracts, SciELO (on Web of Science platform), Academic Search Premier, Africa-Wide Information, Biological and Agricultural Index, CINAHL, Health Source Nursing/Academic, APA PsycInfo and General Science (EBSCOhost platform). The search was performed in January 2022, and the review of the results took place from February to May 2022.Data charting methodsAll authors developed and tested the data extraction tool. The charting framework remained dynamic, continuously updated as reviewers gained a deeper understanding of the studies included. Four independent reviewers charted the data to ensure inter-rater reliability. To address discrepancies, a sample of eligible articles was cross-rated by the reviewers.ResultsWe identified 110 relevant studies describing eight different surveillance systems, conducted in 30 different African countries. Of these studies, 50.1% (56/110) reported on the number of pesticide suicides (totalling 1554); however, 49% (54/110) did not differentiate the cause of death further than either a poisoning death or a suicide, making it difficult to determine if pesticides were involved in the death or if the death was an intentional poisoning case. The surveillance systems identified included health facility records (hospital admissions data), poison control centre (PCC) data, forensic/mortuary data (including police reports), media reports, surveys/interviews with patient/family, case reports, systematic/literature reviews and civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) data. Hospital admissions data was the most frequently used surveillance system (61/110, 55.5%).ConclusionsThe number of studies and surveillance methods found was higher than anticipated. While this is a positive sign, several areas of improvement were identified for pesticide suicide surveillance in Africa. These included improving reporting of the specific pesticides (including the active ingredient) linked to suicide cases for improving policy (since this is required for pesticide regulation) and making use of more than one surveillance system to enhance surveillance of pesticide suicides, allowing for under-used sources, such as PCCs, to be used more effectively in pesticide suicide surveillance. Finally, although a comparison of these surveillance methods outside of Africa was not directly possible for each surveillance system due to a lack of similar high-quality reviews, we did refer to publications where similar pesticide suicide surveillance systems were discussed.

Background

Over 700 000 people die by suicide every year, with suicide being among the leading causes of death worldwide.1 It is estimated that 77% of all suicides occur in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where most of the world’s population live.1 In LMICs, where small-scale farming is common, pesticide ingestion accounts for an estimated 14%–20% of suicides, making it the second most common means of suicide.2 Approximately 110 000–168 000 deaths due to pesticide ingestion happen annually.2

The WHO estimated in 2019 a global age-standardised suicide rate of 9.0 per 100 000 population.1 Regionally in Africa, the age-standardised suicide rate is higher than the global average at 11.2 per 100 000 population. Suicide rates among men in the African region are considerably higher than the global average (18.0 compared with 12.6 per 100 000 globally).3 Between 2000 and 2019, global suicide mortality rates decreased by 36% (at an average annual percentage change (AAPC) of −2.4% per year); however, the decrease in the African region is less substantial (AAPC: −1.5%).1 3 Along with the high likelihood that suicide is underestimated and under-reported in many African countries,4 the African region is home to six of the 10 countries with the highest suicide rates in the world.5

When reviewing the reporting of suicides and available data in the African region, a primary challenge arises. Less than 10% of African countries report mortality data to the WHO, with official statistics available for only 15% of the continent’s total population.4 Many countries in the region do not have high-quality registration data and thus rely on the WHO modelling for estimating. With few exceptions, death registration data are unavailable or unusable due to quality issues.1 With this lack of mortality data, it is no surprise that suicide data from Africa is scarce.

A pesticide, as defined in the Food and Agricultural Organisation and WHO’s International Code of Conduct on Pesticide Management, is ‘any substance or mixture of substances of chemical or biological ingredients intended for repelling, destroying, or controlling any pest, or regulating plant growth’ (page 6).6 Available data on pesticide suicide in Africa are primarily based on small studies conducted among different populations with diverse economic, social and cultural backgrounds.7 Available estimates highlight the challenges with surveillance and the need for standardised surveillance systems and coverage. In addition, information on suicide surveillance methods is lacking. A study by Gunnell and colleagues in 2007 estimated that 15%–33% (n=7800 deaths) of African suicides annually (1990–2007) were due to pesticide poisoning.8 A 2014 systematic review by Mars and colleagues reported that only 16 of Africa’s 54 countries had suicide incidence data available. Additionally, only nine of the 16 reported on the different methods used to identify suicides or attempted suicides (also known as non-fatal self-harm). Despite the limited data, poisoning was identified as one of the two most common methods used for suicide.4 However, a systematic review by Mew and colleagues in 2017 provided a very conservative estimate of 3.5% (n=2100) of suicide deaths in Africa due to pesticides.

Many countries in Africa are predominantly agricultural communities, with increasing access to pesticides.9 Considering the abovementioned estimates, better surveillance of suicide cases and pesticide suicide cases in general could lead to a more realistic picture of pesticide suicide rates in Africa.

With limited mortality data, it is imperative to understand how suicide is reported to develop a comprehensive response. Mortality by suicide was included as a United Nations (UN) Sustainable Development Goals indicator under target 3.4.10 It was also included in WHO’s Mental Health Action Plan 2013–2020 which has been extended to 2030.11 This requires member states to implement measures aimed at achieving the global target of reducing suicide by one-third.10

The extent to which suicides and attempted suicides are reported is sometimes represented as an iceberg, as presented by the WHO in their ‘Practice manual for establishing and maintaining surveillance systems for suicide attempts and self-harm’.12 The visible tip represents reported suicide deaths. The lower levels indicate suicide attempts presenting to hospitals, primary care and emergency services, and suicidal thoughts. The majority of suicides, suicide attempts and behaviours that impact suicide-related outcomes are ‘hidden’ under the surface and not reported.12 Thus, it is vital to improve surveillance systems of suicide and self-harm related incidents for informed and evidence-based prevention interventions and policies.

Given the sensitivity of suicide and the illegality of suicidal behaviour in nine(at the time of writing this article, suicide was illegal in nine African countries. At the time of publishing, the authors have become aware that Ghana has decriminalised suicide, resulting in it being illegal in eight countries now: https://www.thelancet.com/action/showPdf?pii=S2215-0366%2824%2900182-2) African countries,13 it is likely that under-reporting and misclassification present more significant problems for suicide than other causes of death. Stigma surrounding suicide contributes to its misclassification, along with poor-quality death certificates, inadequate vital registration data and the classification of suicide as accidental or undetermined deaths.1 All these reasons have resulted in a lack of African suicide surveillance and poorly informed suicide prevention strategies.

Pesticide poisoning as a means of suicide and attempted suicide is a complex condition for surveillance, as it includes several clinical presentations depending on the type of pesticide used, and the circumstances under which exposure occurred.7 14 Pesticide poisoning from organophosphates, for example, can be misdiagnosed as health professionals have limited training on the wide range of pesticide classes and organophosphate poisonings can present with flu-like symptoms.7 More data is needed to fill these knowledge gaps and improve the identification and reporting of pesticide poisoning, suicide and attempted suicide cases.14

Methods of suicide surveillance

Surveillance is the ongoing systematic collection, analysis, interpretation and dissemination of data to plan, implement and evaluate public health interventions.15 For the purposes of this review, a broader definition was adopted that primarily encompasses the collection of data and does not look at interpretation, dissemination or implementation. Timely registration and regular monitoring of suicide are the backbone of effective suicide prevention.16 For every suicide, there are 20 attempts; having engaged in one or more acts of attempted suicide or self-harm is the single most important predictor of death by suicide.1 12 A comprehensive system needs to combine surveillance of fatal suicide and non-fatal self-harm to understand the full extent of pesticide suicides and formulate adequate prevention measures. Globally, several different suicide and attempted suicide surveillance systems are already in use. The two primary methods currently employed for surveillance of attempted suicide include self-reporting in population surveys and medical records.17 Information relating to pesticide suicide attempts could also be monitored or obtained through available data from poison information centres (PICs) and community surveillance.17 A combination of these pathways is needed for a full picture, as each presents advantages and disadvantages.

Suicide surveillance

Suicide surveillance belongs to a broader category of injury surveillance. While most injury-related deaths are unintentional or ‘accidental’, suicide is the leading cause of intentional injury, accounting for 15% of total fatal injuries in mortuaries and hospitals worldwide in 2008.16 There are several guidelines developed by the WHO on suicide surveillance.16

The WHO notes that there are two points in the death registration process where information on causes of death is obtained.16 If death occurs in a hospital setting, information on the cause of death is obtained through the death certification process. For deaths that occur outside a hospital setting, and where the deceased is brought into a mortuary, information on the cause and circumstances of death is obtained through the post-mortem examination and certification process. Death certification is conducted by a coroner, forensic pathologist or medical examiner.

Where official civil registration and vital statistics (CRVS) systems are available, the death certification data feeds into the CRVS system. The CRVS system relies on medical certification of cause of death using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes.18 Should this be used more often, it could result in a more reliable suicide surveillance system with data readily available and correctly categorised to quantify the prevalence of suicide in a population. If a CRVS system is unavailable, alternatives could include data collected from health facility records (including Health Management Information Systems (HMIS) that are also based on ICD codes), police reports (if the cause of death is reported to the police) and mortuaries using autopsy or forensic data. Where official death certificate data are lacking, the WHO recommends community surveillance such as verbal autopsy, household surveys or community key informants’ interviews.1 17

The need for improvement of suicide surveillance in Africa

As mentioned above, LMICs—particularly in most African countries—do not have reliable data collection or reporting systems. In addition, many countries do not have national strategies for suicide prevention, nor do they include suicide prevention goals in their national health programmes. This, combined with the stigma and criminalisation of suicide, has resulted in poor suicide and pesticide suicide surveillance.

Understanding which methods are currently being utilised across Africa to improve surveillance efforts is essential. This will help determine which data are readily available in the region and how suicide surveillance is being conducted. It also identifies the potential barriers or limitations to these existing surveillance systems. This scoping review aims to explore the current methods used for pesticide suicide surveillance.

Methods

A scoping review was conducted guided by the five-stage framework developed by Arksey and O’Malley and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI).19 20 The research question formulation followed JBIs guidance, utilising the population, concept, context (PCC) mnemonic. The resulting research question focused on a population segment involving individuals engaged in self-poisoning with pesticides, encompassing both fatal and non-fatal cases.7 Considering the potential stigma associated with suicide reporting, cases that could be labelled as unintentional pesticide poisoning were also incorporated. The concept of the review centred around the exploration of varying reporting and surveillance methods, while the context was situated within the African continent, factoring in associated limitations as stipulated by the inclusion criteria.7 A protocol for this scoping review was published in BMJ Open in 2022 and can be accessed here (doi:10.1136/bmjopen-2021–0 55 923) (online supplemental document 1).

Public and patient involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research as it was not appropriate or possible.

Search

MJ, along with a medical school librarian, conducted searches in January 2022 across several databases, including MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus (with substantial Embase content), Web of Science Core Collection, Biological Abstracts, SciELO (on Web of Science platform), Academic Search Premier, Africa-Wide Information, Biological and Agricultural Index, CINAHL, Health Source Nursing/Academic, APA PsycInfo and General Science (EBSCOhost platform). No grey literature was explored, as a reasonable number of papers from the database search were identified by the authors as containing the most relevant and useful information. Grey literature presents many challenges as well, including the literature not being peer reviewed and variability in the quality of the sources. The search strategy focused on population (suicide+pesticide) and context (African continent) aspects. Notably, concept-related filters (Surveillance) were omitted to ensure the inclusion of studies related to data collection or reporting of pesticide suicides. See Brassell, Karunarathne, Utyasheva et alfor the search terms used.

Screening

The screening process adhered to the inclusion and exclusion criteria as defined in our protocol7 and in table 1 below and took place between February 2022 and May 2022. MJ carried out database searches with the assistance of a librarian from the University of Cape Town. We employed the Rayyan tool to eliminate duplicate records, and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram was used to illustrate the various stages of the screening process visually. The identified papers were divided into two groups, and the task of screening titles and abstracts was independently undertaken by two reviewers assigned to each group (group 1, MJ and AK; group 2, LU and AS), with any discrepancies resolved through discussion. Full-text reviews were conducted for sources warranting further scrutiny. Inclusion and exclusion decisions were recorded using Rayyan.

Data extraction and analysis

All authors developed and tested the data extraction tool (see online supplemental table 1). Data extraction provided a comprehensive framework for evaluating eligible articles obtained through the systematic search. The charting framework remained dynamic, continuously updated as reviewers gained a deeper understanding of the studies included. Four independent reviewers charted the data to ensure inter-rater reliability. To address discrepancies, a sample of eligible articles was cross-rated by the reviewers. As stated in the protocol for this review, a narrative analysis of this data was performed and a numerical and thematic analysis presented in the results below.7

Identified Africa surveillance systems

In this section, we discuss the applicability, advantages and disadvantages of each of the eight pesticide suicide and attempted suicides surveillance systems identified in the reviewed studies.

CRVS systems.CRVS systems and the data within them are often used to report mortality figures to the WHO. The official cause of death certification is reported following the WHO ICD-10—an international standard diagnostic classification for mortality and morbidity used for epidemiological and health management purposes.18 ICD coding includes actual cause of mortality and the determining factors which led to death. This information on causes of death is compiled from individual death certificates as recorded in civil registries and collected by the WHO through a compulsory and routine system. These are then reported alongside other global statistics.18 This is advantageous as it allows for uniform reporting of cause of death statistics worldwide and enables routine collection of this data in each country.While beneficial, in 2019, only 87 of the 183 WHO member states had good quality vital registration data on suicide mortality. The 87 states consisted primarily of high-income countries.1 Less than 10% of countries in Africa report mortality data to the WHO.4 Due to the lack of data, suicide rate estimates need to be modelled for many of the remaining countries.17A study by Osafo, Asante and Akotia has revealed that out of 18 surveyed African countries, one-third reported there are no certifications issued for death in general.5 The personnel responsible for ascertaining cause of death varied by country and included coroner’s reports (Ghana, Kenya, Senegal and South Africa), medicolegal authorities (Algeria, Botswana, Burundi, Lesotho, Madagascar, Mozambique, Seychelles, South Africa, Uganda and Cape Verde) and family doctors (Algeria, Comoros, Ethiopia, Madagascar and South Africa).5 Reasons for the lack of death certification included deaths unverified by a medical doctor, death due to suicide and death that occurred at home. More than half of the countries that reported certification of death reported that suicide is not an option for certifying cause of death.5 These challenges, therefore, impact the ability of many African countries to establish working CRVS systems.A key limitation of using a CRVS system for pesticide suicide surveillance is its inability to determine the poisoning agent and accurately classify deaths as pesticide suicide. The ICD-10 coding contains both suicides and poisoning categories, which complicates the correct identification of pesticide suicides. Furthermore, suicide can be miscoded as ‘event of undetermined intent’ or ‘accidental deaths’ (ie, accidental poisoning).21

Forensic/mortuary data (including police reports).Mortuary data can provide an alternative source of cause of death information where a vital registration system is lacking or in cases where deaths have occurred outside of a hospital setting.12 In addition to this, any unnatural death that occurs must be reported to the police.24 The police then investigate whether the death is suspicious and decide whether to file a case or not. Part of the investigation process involves the mortuaries for determination of cause of death.24 Therefore, mortuary data are a good source of mortality data. This kind of data may be well established through the police channels and may be available in settings where data collection (surveillance) through administrative or healthcare pathways is not available. A disadvantage of using this kind of data only is that all cases of attempted suicide are missed. In addition to this, due to stigma and the illegality of suicide in some countries, suicide may not be recorded as the cause of death in some cases. Furthermore, the data are usually collected by the Ministries of Internal Affairs or Police; it is usually not shared with Ministries of Health and is not available for public health decision-making.

PCCs data.PCCs are an important source of information about poisoning in many LMICs but do not belong to a broader suicide surveillance system.17 They can be helpful in determining the extent of pesticide suicides and self-harm. There are 12 PCCs in Africa (Algeria, 2; Angola; Ghana; Kenya, 2; Senegal; South Africa, 3; Tanzania; and Zimbabwe). An advantage of using this system is that it can collect specific data about the cause of poisoning cases and the type of poison implicated in each case, which would be useful in the context of pesticide suicide surveillance. A disadvantage to this system is that it requires additional resources to be effective and will need to be linked to a national systematic recording system to effectively provide data on pesticide poisonings. Another limitation of this reporting is that it relies mostly on self-reported information on self-harm and attempts but excludes data on fatal poisonings. Also, most patients are treated without being referred to the PCC, meaning that the data are often quite biased and represent only a small subsample of cases.

Surveys/interviews with patients/family.A way in which the community can be used to collect data on suicide and pesticide suicide is using the verbal autopsy system, although this will not provide information on attempted suicide.17 This in-depth investigation includes a review of written case-related material (eg, police documents, medical records) and interview data from the deceased’s next of kin, using a structured questionnaire to determine the cause of death.25 An advantage to this system is that it can collect extensive information about a single case (such as which poison was used in a suicide case) and can collect anecdotal evidence of suspected suicide cases. In addition to this, where the stigma of suicide is strong and official administrative records weak or absent, verbal autopsy data may help to fill the gaps or identify more suicide cases. A disadvantage to this system is that only suicide cases will be investigated, missing all non-fatal suicide attempts. In addition, where suicide is illegal or stigmatised, as is commonly the case in Africa, families may hide the cause of death from researchers. This makes verbal autopsies less successful in identifying suicide as the cause of death compared with other ‘more legitimate’ causes.

Systematic/literature reviews.Systematic/literature reviews are often useful sources that provide the reader with a big picture view of the topic of discussion. A systematic/literature review aims to gather and synthesise data from multiple literature sources to provide a snapshot of what already exists in the literature and what gaps potentially still exist.26 Therefore, for pesticide suicides and attempted pesticide suicides, a systematic/literature review will only provide a high-level snapshot of the current pesticide suicide rates within the studied population based on existing published literature.A strength of this surveillance tool is that it would indicate the extent to which pesticide suicides and attempted pesticide suicides have been researched and published, while also identifying potential gaps in this area of study. A weakness of this surveillance source is its reliance on existing research. Some cases may be missed as not all reported suicides or attempted suicides are researched and published in every country. The purpose of a systematic/literature review does not include surveillance of health outcomes, and therefore, this kind of source would not be the ideal one for such purposes.

Systematic/literature reviews.

Systematic/literature reviews are often useful sources that provide the reader with a big picture view of the topic of discussion. A systematic/literature review aims to gather and synthesise data from multiple literature sources to provide a snapshot of what already exists in the literature and what gaps potentially still exist.26 Therefore, for pesticide suicides and attempted pesticide suicides, a systematic/literature review will only provide a high-level snapshot of the current pesticide suicide rates within the studied population based on existing published literature.

A strength of this surveillance tool is that it would indicate the extent to which pesticide suicides and attempted pesticide suicides have been researched and published, while also identifying potential gaps in this area of study. A weakness of this surveillance source is its reliance on existing research. Some cases may be missed as not all reported suicides or attempted suicides are researched and published in every country. The purpose of a systematic/literature review does not include surveillance of health outcomes, and therefore, this kind of source would not be the ideal one for such purposes.

For example, it was found that in India, there are nearly 300–500 suicides a day, but only 2%–3% of these are reported in the media.28 Another disadvantage, according to the WHO, is that media reporting on suicide may lead to an increase in suicides due to readers imitating the suicidal act.29 While this is a concern, it does not mean that these cases should not be reported but rather should be reported in a responsible manner. Discussion

This review identified 110 studies to include for data extraction and analysis, substantially more than expected when the project started. Given the stigma surrounding suicide and the illegality of the act in some African countries, it was expected that minimal studies would be found that have reported such statistics.13 However, 110 studies assisted with determining the current surveillance systems of pesticide suicides in African countries with these statistics. Grey literature was initially planned for inclusion in the search strategy of this review; however, this was changed when the number of included studies was deemed sufficient to assess the surveillance situation in Africa.

Eight different surveillance systems were identified within the 110 included studies. Some were more frequently and more successful than others (ie, hospital admissions data vs case reports). However, the presence of eight different tracking methods proved that there is enough evidence and data to support further investigation and routine reporting on pesticide suicides. While each of these surveillance systems has its strengths and weaknesses (see below), it seems that basic surveillance of pesticide suicides is possible, even in under-resourced countries.

It is important to note the number of publications that reported on pesticide suicides seen in their studies compared with the total number included in this review. In table 4, there were 56 out of the 110 publications included in this review that reported on the number of pesticide suicides in their studies. While analysing the remaining 54 studies, it was observed that some of the suicide cases involved pesticides, but no specific number was attributed to these claims. Therefore, the authors were unable to determine how many pesticide suicides had occurred in those studies and could not include those deaths in the analysis of this review. It must also be noted that poor study design could have contributed to the lack of pesticide suicides being captured.

Furthermore, in some cases, suicides were reported, but the specific poison used was not identified, making it impossible to link the suicides back to pesticides alone. In these cases, pesticides were listed as one of the methods of intentional poisoning or suicide among other poisons, such as alcohol, drugs and other common household chemicals, but the specific numbers associated with pesticide use were not disaggregated in the reported data. Therefore, the number of pesticide suicides could be clearly determined in only 56 of the 110. This alludes to further improvements needed in the already existing reporting structures and systems of pesticide suicides.

Distribution of studies across Africa

Across the 110 studies included in this review, 30 countries in Africa were recognised as having participated in or conducted a study documenting pesticide poisoning and/or pesticide suicide cases. Given that there are a total of 54 countries in Africa, this is much higher than expected. Less than 10% of the African WHO member states report adequate death statistics.4 This led to the expectation that cases of pesticide poisoning and pesticide suicide would similarly be as poorly recorded and reported. However, the fact that 55% of all African countries had some form of pesticide poisoning and pesticide suicide data is a positive sign that this information may be more readily available and accessible in Africa. While reporting and surveillance could be improved, as some countries were not included in the studies of this review, there seems to be a foundation on which pesticide poisonings and pesticide suicide reporting can be further established.

Identified surveillance systems

As previously mentioned, eight surveillance methods were identified in the included studies of this review (table 3). The most accessible method of surveillance for the included studies was hospital admissions data, given that it was the most frequently used. Hospital admissions data are often readily available as it is routinely collected information of all patients that present at any emergency or primary healthcare facilities, perhaps explaining why this was the most frequently used method.12 While this method captures several pesticide-related poisonings and suicides, it only captures patients who present at these facilities, therefore missing all pesticide poisonings and suicides that do not present at primary healthcare facilities or emergency departments.33 In table 3, 61 publications made use of this form of surveillance, with these publications coming from 13 African countries. Although this seems to be the most used form of surveillance across the African continent, it appears there are still many countries not investigating pesticide suicides in this manner.

Use of a combination of multiple data sources rather than a single surveillance system had the second highest number of publications (n=18) and the highest number of clearly reported pesticide suicides (n=487). For example, Poukam, Lemnyuy, Ndikontar et al made use of hospital admissions data and interview data obtained from farmers and healthcare facility staff. The fact that this was the second most frequently used form of surveillance and those studies using multiple data sources for surveillance reported the highest number of pesticide suicides indicates the strength of this method. Where one surveillance system may lack data or miss cases of pesticide suicides (as mentioned with hospital admissions data), another data source may be able to fill that gap. For example, where hospital admissions data may miss fatal cases of pesticide suicides due to the case never presenting at a healthcare facility, forensic/mortuary data or police reports may be able to capture these cases. Using these two systems in conjunction with one another would allow for more cases to be captured and establish a better picture of the pesticide suicide rates in Africa. A study conducted in Sri Lanka by Buckley and colleagues34 also showed that the use of hospital records and prospective data collection using interviews to identify the exact pesticide used helped to improve overall surveillance of pesticide poisonings and pesticide suicides. The remaining 31 included studies made use of the other six surveillance systems (tables3 4). Collectively, they recorded 59% of the total number of pesticide suicides reported across all the publications.

PCCs appear to be a good source for reporting pesticide suicides, with 354 cases reported across only four publications. This resulted in the second-highest average number of pesticides reported per publication (n=88.5). However, these studies occurred across only three African countries (Egypt, Morocco and South Africa). Despite the presence of PCCs in 12 African countries, these data are not being widely used for pesticide suicide surveillance. While these centres are not part of routine suicide surveillance, their high average number of reported pesticide suicides per publication suggests that these centres may be under-used in pesticide suicide surveillance. These centres provide a service that collects specific information for poisoning cases, such as the type of poison, circumstances surrounding the poison and patient information.17 These data would be useful in determining which poisoning cases are suicide related and which of these can be linked back to the use of pesticides for suicide attempts. For example, a study conducted in Malaysia using data from the National Poison Centre (2006–2015) identified 11 807 pesticide poisoning cases. Of these, 60% were intentional, with 93.6% being self-poisoning.35 This kind of system does require additional resources (such as human resources, telephones and data capturing systems). Given that many studies used data that is routinely collected and resourced (ie, hospital admissions data), the use of this system would require more consideration around the resources that are available or could be availed for this purpose. A major problem with PCC data is bias in the data collected as many pesticide poisoning cases will be managed without phoning a PCC. A PCC will typically only hear about a very small proportion of cases. PCCs are also not informed about deceased patients, so severe cases will be excluded. In addition to this, seven studies in total made use of PCC data, but only four reported pesticide suicides. Therefore, if this source is used in the future, the data must be clearly disaggregated by the type of poison used for each suicide case to avoid ambiguity on whether a case involved pesticide poisoning or a different substance.

When looking at the use of CRVS data, only five publications used this method to track pesticide suicides, four of which were conducted in South Africa and one in Zimbabwe. This gives a better picture of the potential availability, or lack thereof, and reliability of this data in the rest of Africa. Furthermore, it highlights the lack of reporting of death registration data across Africa and the absence of CRVS systems used for surveillance in our included studies. A CRVS system is beneficial as it allows for a uniform method of reporting for deaths by using ICD codes to classify causes of death.18 Using such a system relies on death certification data being readily available and accurate. Only five of the included studies used this method for pesticide suicide surveillance, and these were done in just two African countries. This suggests that death certification data needs improvement and that a CRVS system is not yet viable for the African continent. CRVS systems are also very poor at identifying the individual pesticides or even class of pesticides used for attempted suicide (see, eg, Eddleston, Nagami and Lin et al). This would explain why only one of the five studies that used this data source was able to report pesticide suicide numbers. This is further echoed by a study conducted by Bonvoisin and colleagues,36 where suicide data were obtained from a national crime registry but details on which pesticides were used could not be obtained and it was not clear as to how the suicide data was collected for this registry.

Community-based surveillance methods such as surveys/interviews with patients/families were only used by two of the included studies. These studies reported no pesticide suicide deaths. Both studies included suicide attempts but did not record any deaths from suicide.37 38 Both studies were also able to report on the involvement of pesticides in the recorded suicide attempts. This indicates that the WHO model of verbal autopsies with patients or family members or community members of deceased patients may result in more pesticide suicide cases being captured and correctly reported. However, as only two of the 110 included studies made use of this method, the popularity of such surveillance is low. This could be linked to the resources and time required to conduct these interviews. Additionally, the stigma associated with suicide in some countries may make it difficult for families to report deaths as suicides. Nevertheless, this system should be further considered and adapted for use by more countries as it is able to report on the method of suicide. A study conducted by Vijayakumar and colleagues39 used a community-based surveillance method of interviews with third party key informants and compared this with hospital record and police records. Although they did not focus on pesticide suicides in particular, they found that the community-based surveillance approach was able to identify more suicide cases compared with those identified through police and hospital records.

As each of these systems is broken down into its relative advantages and disadvantages, it becomes clearer that relying on one method of surveillance is not beneficial when determining the full picture of pesticide suicide. Instead, a strategic combination of the different surveillance systems may yield better results. The combination of the systems would need to be determined by considering the resource limitations of each context, data availability and reliability, and the already existing structures and systems in place.

Strengths of our review

Our review made use of a broad search strategy that allowed for a high number of relevant papers to be drawn out of the search results for this review. Additionally, by including papers that focused on pesticide poisonings only, some nuanced pesticide suicide data was obtained through the full-text review of these papers. This allowed for more data to be included in this review. The screening process of this review was also robust, with four reviewers working in groups to screen the search results for all relevant papers. This process helped to instil confidence in the search strategy as all the relevant papers were included.

Limitations of our review

Our review excluded any papers not written in English. This was due to resource constraints around having translators or team members who could comprehend and understand other languages. This may have limited the results and could have resulted in relevant studies being excluded from our review. However, 110 articles were regarded as a high number for this review, and sufficient relevant data were obtained to reach sound conclusions from this review. The exclusion of grey literature may have also limited this review in some respects. The quality of the surveillance methods used in each included paper was not assessable, which has also added a limitation to our study. Finally, a comparison of these surveillance methods outside of Africa was not possible for each surveillance system due to a lack of similar reviews of these surveillance systems outside of Africa.

Conclusion

More studies were included than initially anticipated, suggesting that perhaps pesticide suicide surveillance in Africa may be more developed than originally thought. While eight different pesticide suicide surveillance systems are currently in use across the African continent, there is no uniform reporting method. All eight approaches have advantages and disadvantages; thus, the authors believe that using a combination of these methods would be beneficial to improve the effectiveness of surveillance of pesticide suicides. This would allow for currently underutilised sources of data, such as PCCs, to be more effectively used in routine surveillance. Pesticide suicide surveillance across the whole continent still has room for improvement, with opportunities to introduce more detailed suicide surveillance and disaggregated reporting of cases and their relative causes or agents (such as what pesticide or class of pesticide was used or the active ingredient involved in the case, since this is required for pesticide regulation). Without accurate—or at least improved—data on pesticides used in suicide attempts, effective regulation to reduce harm from pesticide poisoning will remain insufficient.
