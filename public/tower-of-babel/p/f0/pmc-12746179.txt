Pimobendan alleviates myocyte apoptosis and fibrosis associated with mitral regurgitation by targeting endoplasmic reticulum stress
Authors: Pakit Boonpala, Sushawadee Tongta, Nakkawee Saengklub, Vudhiporn Limprasutr, Sirinapa Srikam, Wilawan Ji‐au, Tussapon Boonyarattanasoonthorn, Yaowalak Panyasing, Sarawut Kumphune, Sarinee Kalandakanond‐Thongsong, Anusak Kijtawornrat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress is an important factor in the development of numerous cardiovascular disorders; nevertheless, the association between ER stress and mitral regurgitation (MR) remains inadequately characterized. The molecular mechanism of pimobendan (PIMO) that contributes to the delay in congestive heart failure (CHF) in MR associated with apoptosis and fibrosis is still unclear. Our aim was to examine the impact of PIMO on ER stress, apoptosis, and fibrosis in a chronic MR rat model.MethodsMR was surgically induced in 10 Sprague–Dawley rats, with 5 serving as sham operation controls. At 8 weeks postsurgery, the MR animals were randomly allocated into two groups: MR and MR + PIMO groups. PIMO was administered twice daily through oral gavage for 4 weeks, whereas the sham and MR groups were administered similar quantities of drinking water. Echocardiography was conducted before the delivery of PIMO as a baseline measure and at the end of the study. At the end of the investigation, hearts were procured for histopathological and ER stress evaluations.ResultsPIMO significantly maintained heart function and structural remodeling in the MR animals. PIMO significantly reduced MR‐induced myocyte apoptosis (p = 0.044) and fibrosis (p = 0.002) by reducing the messenger RNA expression of genes associated with ER stress (GRP78 [glucose‐regulated protein 78], ATF4 [activating transcription factor 4], and CHOP [C/ERP homologous protein]) compared to the MR group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001, respectively).ConclusionPIMO demonstrated cardioprotective benefits on heart function, myocyte apoptosis, and fibrosis by regulating ER stress in an MR‐induced CHF rat model.

INTRODUCTION

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the most common valvular heart disease in aging small‐ to medium‐sized dogs 1 and is also prevalent in the elderly human population. 2 In MR, compensatory mechanisms eventually fail, leading to maladaptive cardiac remodeling characterized by left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, myocardial fibrosis, arrhythmias, heart failure (HF), and death. 3 Cardiac fibrosis, a hallmark of pathological remodeling, impairs cardiac function and contributes to HF progression. 4

Recent studies using surgically induced MR in rats 5 and a knock‐in mouse model of genetic dilated cardiomyopathy 6 have shown that volume overload from MR leads to both cardiomyocyte apoptosis and fibrosis. In our previous work, we demonstrated that pimobendan (PIMO) attenuated LV dysfunction and remodeling while preserving mitochondrial quality, 7 suggesting that maintaining mitochondrial function may protect against MR‐induced cardiac damage.

Mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) coordinate to regulate calcium homeostasis. PIMO may exert cardioprotective effects by inhibiting nuclear factor kappa B, a transcription factor activated during the unfolded protein response (UPR), which is a cellular response to ER stress. 8 , 9

ER stress occurs when the ER's protein‐folding capacity is disrupted, leading to accumulation of misfolded proteins. This can activate the UPR, which aims to restore cellular homeostasis; however, prolonged stress may instead trigger apoptosis. 10 In mice treated with ER stress inducers, increased rough endoplasmic reticulum (rER) formation has been observed at dyadic microdomains. 11 Although ER stress has been implicated in several cardiovascular diseases, 12 its role in MR remains unclear.

PIMO has demonstrated clinical benefits in delaying the onset of congestive heart failure (CHF), 13 reducing refractory HF symptoms, 14 improving clinical condition, 15 prolonging survival, 16 , 17 and restoring heart rate variability. 18 These effects are primarily attributed to phosphodiesterase‐III inhibition and increased troponin C sensitivity to calcium. 19 In a rat MR model, PIMO also preserved mitochondrial integrity and cardiac ultrastructure. 7 Importantly, ER stress can promote apoptosis by releasing calcium from the ER to mitochondria, leading to excessive production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species. 20

Due to this mechanistic link, we hypothesized that PIMO may reduce ER stress and thereby attenuate apoptosis and fibrosis in MR. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of PIMO on ER stress, apoptosis, and fibrosis in a rat model of MR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and ethics approval

Seventeen male Sprague–Dawley rats (300–350 g) were obtained from Nomura Siam International Co., Ltd. Rats were housed in individually ventilated cages with a barrier system. They were provided commercial autoclaved feed and water ad libitum. The environment was maintained at a temperature of 22 ± 1℃, a relative humidity of 50% ± 10%, and a 12 h light–dark cycle.

Induction of MR

Twelve rats underwent open‐chest surgery to induce MR, whereas five rats underwent the same surgery without mitral valve damage and served as sham controls. The MR induction technique followed a previously described method. 7 MR was confirmed using echocardiography with a 4‐ to 10‐MHz phased array probe, where an MR jet occupying 45%–60% of the left atrial area was considered successful. Two MR rats were killed due to acute HF and pulmonary edema. All the remaining animals were allowed to develop systolic dysfunction and cardiac remodeling for 8 weeks before initiating treatment.

Experimental design

Eight weeks after surgery, echocardiography was performed to confirm no baseline differences between MR groups. The surviving MR rats (n = 10) were randomly assigned to two groups: MR (n = 5) and MR + PIMO (n = 5). Sham‐operated rats (n = 5) and MR rats received drinking water (5 mL/kg), whereas MR + PIMO rats were administered 0.15 mg/kg of PIMO orally every 12 h, as previously described. 7 Dosage volumes were standardized across groups.

After 4 weeks of treatment, the rats were killed using isoflurane overdose. The LV free wall was collected and stored in RNAlater (Qiagen, NRW, Germany) for real‐time PCR. Trabecular muscle was collected for TEM, and heart samples were fixed in formalin for histological and TUNEL analyses. The experimental design is shown in Figure 1.

Study design to evaluate the impact of pimobendan on cardiac remodeling and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress–induced myocyte apoptosis and fibrosis in mitral regurgitation rat model (figure created using BioRender.com).

Echocardiographic measurements

Echocardiography was used to assess cardiac geometry and function, including the LA/Ao, LVIDd, and FS. Imaging was performed 4 weeks after treatment began, 1.5–3 h after PIMO or placebo administration. Two‐dimensional and M‐mode images were obtained following published recommendations. 21 Measurements were averaged over six consecutive cardiac cycles.

Histopathology

Heart tissues were cut into 5‐μm‐thick sections and stained with hematoxylin and eosin and Masson's trichrome. Fibrosis was quantified as the collagen‐stained area divided by the total tissue area, expressed as a percentage of fibrosis. 22

Apoptosis detection using TUNEL

Apoptotic nuclei were detected using the ApopTag Peroxidase In Situ Apoptosis Detection Kit (Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Whole‐heart cross sections at the papillary muscle level (3 μm thick) were stained, counterstained with hematoxylin, and scanned using an Aperio CS2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems, CA, USA). Quantification was performed using Aperio ImageScope software (version 12.3.2.5030) with the Positive Pixel Nuclear version 9 algorithm. Apoptosis was expressed as the percentage of TUNEL‐positive nuclei relative to total nuclei.

Real‐time PCR analysis

The mRNA expression of ER stress–related genes—including GRP78, CHOP, ATF6, PERK, phosphorylated eIF2α, ATF4, IRE1, TRAF2, and JNK—was evaluated using real‐time PCR. Primer sequences are presented in Table 1. Total RNA was extracted from LV tissue using the AccuPure Tissue RNA Mini Kit (AccuBioMed, Taiwan, China) and reverse transcribed using the iScript Reverse Transcription Supermix (Bio‐Rad, CA, USA). Relative gene expression was calculated using the comparative threshold (Ct) method and expressed as 2−ΔCt. Fold changes relative to the sham group (MR vs. sham, MR + PIMO vs. MR) were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. 23

Transmission electron microscopy

Two trabecular LV muscles per group were fixed in 3% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer and stored at 4°C. Samples were postfixed in 2% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated in graded ethanol, and embedded in Epon 812 (Electron Microscopy Sciences, PA, USA). After overnight polymerization at 70°C, ultrathin longitudinal sections (65–70 nm) were cut using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate.

Images of the sections were obtained at 100 kV using a JEOL JEM‐1400 Plus transmission electron microscope. Images (three fields per animal) were obtained at 6000× magnification using a CCD camera. Two blinded electron microscopy specialists independently evaluated SR swelling—defined as SR cisternae >200 nm in diameter—and calculated the percentage of swollen SR as (enlarged SR)/(normal SR + enlarged SR) × 100. The number of rER structures was also quantified across all fields, and averages were recorded per group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, version 29.0.2. Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. One‐way analysis of variance and Tukey's post hoc test were used to compare treatment groups. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

PIMO prevents cardiac geometry and functional remodeling

The left atrial‐to‐aortic root ratio (LA/Ao; Figure 2A) and left ventricular internal diameter at end‐diastole (LVIDd; Figure 2B) were significantly increased in the MR group compared to the sham group (LA/Ao: 2.65 ± 0.04 vs. 1.33 ± 0.01; LVIDd: 1.08 ± 0.03 vs. 0.77 ± 0.02 cm; both p < 0.001). In contrast, rats treated with PIMO (MR + PIMO) exhibited significantly lower LA/Ao and LVIDd values than untreated MR rats (1.49 ± 0.03 and 0.73 ± 0.04 cm, respectively; both p < 0.001). Fractional shortening (FS; Figure 2C) was significantly reduced in the MR group compared to the sham group (31.37% ± 2.13% vs. 46.48% ± 3.53%, p < 0.01). However, FS significantly increased in the MR + PIMO group (52.03% ± 1.14%) compared to the MR group (p < 0.001).

Echocardiography was employed to assess (A) the left atrial‐to‐aortic root ratio (LA/Ao), (B) left ventricular internal diameter at end‐diastole (LVIDd), and (C) fractional shortening (FS) in all experimental rats prior to the day they were killed. Data are presented as mean ± SEM (standard error of the mean, n = 5 per group). Statistical analysis was performed using one‐way ANOVA (analysis of variance) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. **p < 0.01 and ***p < 0.001.

PIMO ameliorates ER stress in MR

To assess the effects of PIMO on ER stress, we measured messenger RNA (mRNA) expression of related genes using real‐time polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) (Figure 3A–I). The MR group exhibited a significantly higher expression of glucose‐regulated protein 78 (GRP78; Figure 3A), activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4; Figure 3F), and C/EBP homologous protein (C/ERP homologous protein [CHOP]; Figure 3B) compared to the sham group (p < 0.01, p < 0.01 and p < 0.001, respectively).

Other ER stress genes—protein kinase RNA‐like ER kinase (PERK; Figure 3D), phosphorylated eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α; Figure 3E), and tumor necrosis factor receptor‐associated factor 2 (TRAF2; Figure 3H)—tended to increase (p < 0.10), and ATF6 (Figure 3C), inositol‐requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1; Figure 3G), and JUN N‐terminal kinase 1 (JNK; Figure 3I) also exhibited elevated expression, though not statistically significant. Overall, MR induced greater than or equal to twofold increases in several ER stress genes.

PIMO significantly reduced GRP78, ATF4, and CHOP expression in the MR + PIMO group compared to MR alone (p < 0.05). The expression of other ER stress–related genes also declined, although without statistical significance.

PIMO preserves myocyte ultrastructure

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (Figure 4A–C) revealed well‐organized sarcomeres in sham rats (Figure 4A), with longitudinally aligned myofibrils and clearly defined A‐bands, I‐bands, and Z‐lines. The rER was positioned near nuclei, I‐bands, and Z‐lines. Dyads, composed of T‐tubules and sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) cisternae, appeared intact.

Ultrastructural analysis of cardiomyocytes from (A) sham rats, (B) mitral regurgitation (MR) rats, and (C) MR rats treated with pimobendan (MR + pimobendan). Images were obtained at 8000× magnification. There was more sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) dilation in MR rats than in MR + pimobendan or sham rats, and the number of rough endoplasmic reticulum (rER) increased in MR rats more than in the MR + pimobendan rats. White arrowhead indicates rER, asterisk (*) indicates SR, and “t” indicates T‐tubule. Scale bar: 1 μm.

In MR rats, sarcomeric disorganization, mitochondrial swelling, and crista disruption were observed (Figure 4B). Rough ER networks were greatly expanded, particularly around dyadic microdomains. These structural abnormalities were present but less severe in MR + PIMO rats (Figure 4C).

Rough ER counts were 2 and 17 in two sham rats, 342 and 406 in MR rats, and 53 and 55 in MR + PIMO rats. SR cisterna diameters ranged from 250 to 800 nm (sham), 250 to 1500 nm (MR), and 250 to 900 nm (MR + PIMO). Swollen SR was observed in 8% and 24% of sham myocytes, 35% and 40% of MR myocytes, and 20% in both MR + PIMO samples.

PIMO attenuates myocyte apoptosis

Apoptotic myocytes were detected using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) (Figure 5A–D). Apoptosis was significantly higher in the MR group (Figure 5B,D) compared to sham (Figure 5A,D) (12.32% ± 1.28% vs. 1.83% ± 0.67%, p < 0.001). PIMO (Figure 5C,D) significantly reduced apoptosis (MR + PIMO: 7.52% ± 1.58%, p < 0.05 vs. MR). These results suggest that PIMO reduces MR‐induced apoptosis, potentially via modulation of ER stress and related apoptotic pathways.

PIMO reduces cardiac fibrosis

Histological analysis of the left ventricle using Masson's trichrome staining revealed significantly increased fibrosis in the MR group compared to sham (3.24% ± 0.47% vs. 0.92% ± 0.14%; p < 0.001) (Figure 6A–C). PIMO treatment significantly reduced fibrosis (MR + PIMO: 1.32% ± 0.22%; p < 0.01 vs. MR).

DISCUSSION

MR continues to pose a significant therapeutic challenge globally, affecting both humans and small‐ to medium‐sized adult dogs. This challenge is largely due to the lack of an effective treatment strategy that can mitigate the cardiac remodeling and dysfunction associated with the condition. In veterinary medicine, PIMO is currently the only medication capable of delaying the onset of CHF when administered at the appropriate stage of disease progression. 1 , 13 However, its underlying molecular mechanism remains incompletely understood.

Due to the challenges in obtaining cardiac tissue from clinical patients, small animal models are essential for studying HF secondary to MR. Previous studies have shown that rat models of MR enter a compensatory phase soon after surgical induction. 5 , 22 , 24 , 25 , 33 Chronic MR subsequently leads to ventricular remodeling and impaired cardiac contractility, resembling the progression observed in human MR. 25 Therefore, the MR rat model is suitable for evaluating therapeutic interventions targeting LV remodeling.

In our previous study, we demonstrated that MR induces mitochondrial dysfunction by increasing reactive oxygen species production and mitochondrial depolarization, both of which were ameliorated by PIMO. 7 These findings suggest that preserving mitochondrial function may help counteract the detrimental effects of MR. Because mitochondria and the ER communicate to regulate calcium homeostasis, the current study focused on investigating whether PIMO could modulate apoptosis and fibrosis through ER stress pathways in MR‐induced LV dysfunction.

We found that PIMO improved cardiac function, attenuated myocyte apoptosis and fibrosis, and reduced the expression of ER stress–related genes, particularly those involved in the PERK–ATF4–CHOP branch of the UPR. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to propose a mechanistic explanation for PIMO's effects on MR via ER stress modulation. These results suggest that PIMO may mitigate ER stress, thereby reducing cellular damage through decreased apoptosis and fibrosis.

The ER plays a central role in protein synthesis, modification, trafficking, and calcium homeostasis. 26 Disruption of these functions—particularly the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins—activates ER stress, which triggers adaptive pathways like the UPR to restore proteostasis. However, prolonged UPR activation can lead to maladaptive responses and apoptosis. 27 , 28 The UPR is regulated through three major signaling pathways: IRE1, ATF6, and PERK. 29 Under resting conditions, these sensors are inactivated by binding to the chaperone GRP78. 30 Upon ER stress, GRP78 dissociates to bind misfolded proteins, enabling activation of the sensors.

Our data indicate that MR activates ER stress, primarily through the PERK–ATF4–CHOP axis. CHOP is a proapoptotic factor that inhibits Bcl‐2 and enhances Bax, leading to cytochrome c release from mitochondria and subsequent caspase activation. 34 , 35 Although not statistically significant, IRE1, TRAF2, and JNK expression also increased in MR rats, suggesting possible involvement of the IRE1–TRAF2–JNK pathway in apoptosis. 34 Thus, MR‐induced apoptosis may involve both PERK–ATF4–CHOP and IRE1–TRAF2–JNK pathways.

In addition, ER–mitochondria crosstalk via calcium signaling contributes to apoptosis. The Sigma‐1 receptor (Sig‐1R), located in mitochondria‐associated ER membranes, forms a complex with GRP78 and inositol 1,4,5‐trisphosphate receptor type 3 (IP3R3), facilitating calcium transfer from the ER to mitochondria. This transfer supports bioenergetics under mild stress but causes calcium overload and apoptosis during prolonged stress. 36 , 37 Moreover, disruption of the Sig‐1R–GRP78–IP3R3 complex can impair calcium homeostasis, exacerbating ER stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. These interactions position Sig‐1R and IP3R3 as central mediators of ER–mitochondrial crosstalk under pathological conditions. Their role in modulating cardiomyocyte fate highlights a potentially novel target for therapeutic intervention. In this context, our findings suggest that PIMO may preserve ER–mitochondria calcium signaling by downregulating GRP78 and CHOP, thereby attenuating the maladaptive signaling cascade associated with prolonged ER stress. Enhanced elaboration of these mechanisms supports the novelty of PIMO's protective effect and may provide new insight into its application beyond its known inotropic and vasodilatory properties.

Sig‐1R also interacts with Lon protease 1 (Lonp1), which regulates ER and mitochondrial function. 38 Inhibition of Lonp1 reduces Sig‐1R levels and increases the expression of GRP78 and CHOP, exacerbating ER stress–induced apoptosis. TRAP1, another mitochondrial chaperone, maintains protein homeostasis in mitochondria and the ER. Its inhibition leads to mitochondrial swelling, ER stress, and apoptosis. 39

In our study, PIMO significantly decreased GRP78, ATF4, and CHOP mRNA expression, suggesting that it alleviates ER stress through the PERK–ATF4–CHOP axis. Given the established relationship between ER stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis, it is likely that PIMO's cardioprotective effects involve modulating ER–mitochondria signaling.

TUNEL staining confirmed that MR increased myocyte apoptosis, which was significantly reduced by PIMO. Similarly, Masson's trichrome staining revealed increased LV fibrosis in MR rats, which was also reduced with PIMO. These findings support the hypothesis that ER stress–induced apoptosis contributes to cardiac remodeling and progression to HF. 40 , 41 In aortic coarctation model mice, CHOP‐mediated apoptosis was implicated in the transition from cardiac hypertrophy to failure. 42 Our results suggest that PIMO reduces ER stress, thereby preventing fibrosis and apoptosis and preserving cardiac function in MR‐induced HF.

Ultrastructural analysis via TEM further supports our findings. MR rats exhibited a reorganization of the reticulum network and significantly increased rER near dyadic regions, indicating heightened protein synthesis and maladaptive UPR activation. These changes were less severe in PIMO‐treated rats, suggesting a reduction in ER stress. Swelling of the SR, a key calcium‐regulating compartment, was also observed in MR rats, indicating disrupted intracellular calcium homeostasis. This aligns with previous findings where tunicamycin‐induced ER stress impaired calcium regulation and excitation–contraction coupling in transgenic mice. 43 Therefore, the impaired contractility observed in MR rats may be partly due to altered calcium homeostasis in addition to ER stress.

Figure 7 shows the proposed mechanism of PIMO's action based on our findings. This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size limits statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Nonetheless, ethical considerations and practical constraints necessitated minimizing the number of animals while maintaining meaningful results. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these outcomes. Second, our study was limited to a 1‐month treatment period. Long‐term studies are essential to assess the sustainability of PIMO's cardioprotective effects. Third, echocardiographic assessment was performed only at the study's conclusion. Although this limited the ability to track progression over time, previous sequential studies 24 , 25 and our earlier work 7 , 22 support the observed outcomes. Fourth, although we assessed mRNA expression of ER stress markers, we did not validate changes at the protein level. Although mRNA and protein levels often correlate, 43 , 44 protein‐level confirmation (e.g., via Western blot or immunohistochemistry) would strengthen our findings. This is an important direction for future research.

The postulated mechanism of action of pimobendan to highlight its impact on improvement in mitral regurgitation (MR)–induced myocyte apoptosis and fibrosis through modulation of ER (endoplasmic reticulum) stress pathways (figure created using BioRender.com).

In conclusion, our findings suggest that PIMO may possess cardioprotective properties, as it appears to reduce cardiac remodeling, improve cardiac geometry and function, and decrease cardiac fibrosis. These potential benefits may be attributed to the ability of PIMO to mitigate myocyte apoptosis induced by ER stress (Figure 7). Although these results are promising, they should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size. The findings presented in this study align with previous research on the effects of PIMO, contributing to the growing body of evidence supporting its therapeutic role in the management of MR. Further studies with larger cohorts and protein‐level validation are warranted to confirm these mechanisms.
