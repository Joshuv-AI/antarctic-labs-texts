Human-centered design-based lightweight wearable IMU human pose estimation
Authors: Lidong Wang, Juanjuan Liu, Jingxuan Xue, Qinyu Tan, Minmin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a wearable IMU–based human pose estimation framework that couples knowledge distillation with an involution–based student and a principled structural re–parameterization for on–device deployment. A high–capacity Transformer serves as the teacher to learn rich temporal–spatial representations, while the student adopts input–adaptive involution operators. During deployment, structural re–parameterization collapses the training graph by folding batch normalization and fusing in–branch cascades and cross–branch parallel paths, yielding a single inference–time module that is equivalent to two 1D CNN passes. This design decouples training expressiveness from inference efficiency and makes the model hardware–friendly for low–power wearables. Extensive experiments on two public benchmarks, DIP–IMU and IMUPoser, demonstrate that our approach preserves near state–of–the–art accuracy while achieving sub–millisecond latency. Concretely, the proposed model attains 81 mm MPJPE on DIP–IMU and 94 mm on IMUPoser, with per–frame latencies of 0.012 ms and 0.011 ms, respectively—delivering one to two orders of magnitude speedups over heavy Transformer baselines and matching the best accuracies within ≤ 1.25% relative difference. The consistent gains across datasets indicate strong robustness and cross–subject generalization, highlighting the suitability of the method for real–time wearable applications.Keywords: Human pose estimation, Inertial measurement unit (IMU), Wearable devices, Knowledge distillation, Structural re-parameterization, Convolutional neural network (CNN)Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Human Pose Estimation (HPE) aims to infer future action states or activity categories based on historical sensor data and is an important research topic in the field of intelligent perception and human–computer interaction. Unlike traditional Human Activity Recognition, pose estimation not only requires identifying the current state but also capturing the dynamic evolution patterns in time series to proactively anticipate potential action changes1. This capability has significant application value in proactive intervention during rehabilitation training, performance guidance in sports, hazardous action warning in industrial safety, and interaction optimization in virtual/augmented reality (VR/AR) scenarios2.

In recent years, wearable sensors such as inertial measurement units (IMUs), electromyography (EMG) sensors, and flexible pressure sensors have gradually become important data sources for pose estimation due to their portability, low cost, and robustness to lighting conditions and occlusion. Leveraging deep learning techniques, methods based on Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN)3–6, temporal convolutional networks, and self-attention networks7 have been widely applied to extract multi-scale spatiotemporal features from wearable time-series signals, achieving remarkable success in modeling long-term dependencies and complex temporal relationships.

However, these high-performance models generally rely on multi-layer RNNs or Transformers, which have large parameter counts, high computational costs, and long inference latency, making it difficult to meet the low-power and real-time requirements of deployment on resource-constrained wearable or mobile devices. In contrast, lightweight modeling techniques such as depthwise separable convolutions, group convolutions, channel shuffling, and feature redundancy elimination have been widely adopted in computer vision tasks (e.g., MobileNet8, ShuffleNet9, GhostNet10 but remain relatively underexplored for wearable time-series signal modeling. Moreover, most existing lightweight work focuses on recognition accuracy, with insufficient attention to temporal continuity, future information modeling, and cross-scenario generalization in pose estimation. In addition, while there are studies on lightweight IMU signal transmission (e.g., compressed sensing for XR/Metaverse to reduce signal redundancy, focusing on communication efficiency and IMU placement optimization), there is still a lack of a systematic approach that combines lightweight structures with distillation strategies.

To address this gap, we propose a lightweight-oriented wearable IMU-based 3D pose estimation framework. By combining knowledge distillation and structural re-parameterization, the training and inference processes are decoupled, enabling significant reductions in inference time while fully learning discriminative features. During training, a Transformer-based spatiotemporal feature extractor is employed, and knowledge distillation is introduced to transfer multi-level knowledge from a teacher model to a compact student model based on the involution operator, thereby reducing model complexity at an early stage. Furthermore, we derive the structural re-parameterization process for the involution operator, such that the inference process is fully simplified to a single convolution computation.

We evaluate on the DIP–IMU and IMUPoser datasets. Our model achieves per-frame inference latencies of 0.012 ms (DIP-IMU) and 0.011ms (IMUPoser), while keeping accuracy essentially unchanged compared with the best baselines — 81 mm vs. 80 mm on DIP-IMU and 94 mm matching the Transformer on IMUPoser (i.e., ≤ 1.25% difference). These results indicate strong robustness and cross-subject generalization.

In this paper, we use the term human-centered design (HCD) in a deployment-oriented sense: our algorithmic choices are guided by real wearable users and usage contexts, where perceived latency, battery life, reliability, and comfort can be as critical as benchmark accuracy. For wearable IMU pose estimation, typical stakeholders (e.g., patients in rehabilitation, athletes, and XR users) commonly require (i) real-time feedback with minimal delay, (ii) stable on-device runtime under limited compute and power budgets, and (iii) robust performance across different subjects and sparse sensor layouts. These requirements directly motivate our design. The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

Related work

IMU-based 3D pose estimation and prediction

Wearable human pose estimation has attracted considerable attention in recent years, with its core objective being to utilize sensor data-particularly from inertial measurement units (IMUs)-to predict future activity states. Jaramillo et al.11 proposed a Seq2Seq and Transformer-BiLSTM-based framework that predicts future IMU signals and performs classification, achieving 97.96% accuracy even with a compact sensor configuration. Kim et al.12 employed a one-dimensional residual network (1D-ResNet) to process single-axis acceleration signals, attaining 97.09% recognition accuracy while reducing the input dimensionality. Att-ResBiGRU13 integrated an attention mechanism with bidirectional GRUs, and demonstrated strong robustness to variations in sensor placement. Li et al.14 introduced the HARMamba model, which incorporates an efficient bidirectional state-space structure and exhibits strong generalization in cross-dataset prediction tasks. In addition, Gong et al.15 proposed MobileHARC, and Batool et al.16 developed an LSTM-GRU hybrid model. Both approaches attempt to reduce computational cost without significantly sacrificing accuracy, highlighting the increasing focus on deployment feasibility.

However, most of the above approaches primarily focus on improving recognition or prediction accuracy, often relying on deep recurrent architectures or complex attention-based models with high computational overhead. Such designs, while effective in performance benchmarks, pose significant challenges for deployment on resource-constrained wearable devices due to their large parameter counts, high FLOPs, and latency-sensitive inference.

Lightweight modeling studies

Achieving high-accuracy, low-latency human pose estimation on resource-constrained wearable devices requires lightweight optimization at both the model architecture and training strategy levels. From an architectural perspective, the computer vision field has proposed numerous efficient network designs. For example, MobileNet reduces computation through depthwise separable convolutions; ShuffleNet improves parallelism via group convolutions and channel shuffling; and GhostNet reduces convolutional computation by generating redundant features more efficiently. These concepts have also been successfully applied to one-dimensional time-series modeling. Gomaa et al.17 designed MobileHARC with a lightweight CNN structure, achieving high recognition performance on embedded devices; Ek et al.18 proposed HART, a lightweight Transformer architecture that reduces parameters and FLOPs while maintaining competitive accuracy across multiple datasets; and Ferrara et al.19 employed an efficient state-space model in HARMamba to reduce computational load while preserving strong cross-dataset generalization.

In the domain of wearable human activity prediction, knowledge distillation (KD) has been widely used to transfer the representational capability of high-performance teacher models to lightweight student models. Mardanpour et al.20 applied both response and feature distillation to multi-axis IMU data, enhancing small model performance. The KD-HAR framework by Gonçalves et al.21 achieved an 18× − 42× model compression ratio on the UCI-HAR and WISDM datasets while maintaining high recognition accuracy. Yuan et al.22 further proposed a multiscale semantic graph distillation approach, improving accuracy by 2.31% and 0.29% on the MMAct and UTD-MHAD datasets, respectively. Bello et al.23 developed the TSAK two-stage KD method for smart manufacturing scenarios, reducing model parameters by 79%, FLOPs by 96.6%, and improving inference speed by more than 8×. Chen et al.24 introduced the COMODO framework, a cross-modal self-supervised distillation approach that transfers semantic representations from a video model to an IMU model, thereby enhancing generalization and performance without requiring labeled data.

Another effective lightweight strategy is low-rank transfer/adaptation. This method performs low-rank decomposition on weight matrices (e.g., via singular value decomposition or low-rank approximation) or incorporates low-rank parameterization in adaptation layers, decomposing the original high-dimensional parameters into two or more low-rank matrices. This significantly reduces parameter counts and computational cost. In pose estimation tasks, low-rank transfer can reduce redundant representations while retaining key discriminative features, particularly in compressing large fully connected layers and attention weight matrices in Transformer or RNN architectures. Recent studies have shown that combining KD with low-rank transfer can further enhance the accuracy and stability of compressed models, demonstrating strong performance in cross-subject and cross-scenario generalization tasks.

Although several lightweight approaches have been proposed, most are primarily designed for the training process. We extend the re-parameterization formulation of the lightweight involution operator, making the inference process more computationally efficient.

Framework

The proposed framework employs a two-stage strategy to reduce the time cost of model inference, thereby enabling more efficient deployment on wearable human sensing devices, as illustrated in Fig. 1. In the training phase, signals collected from wearable devices are first fed into a complex Transformer model for fully supervised learning. Subsequently, a compact student model based on the involution operator leverages transfer learning and knowledge distillation to extract the learned features and representations from the Transformer, accomplishing the first stage of model lightweighting. Prior to the inference phase, a structural re-parameterization technique is applied to transform the involution-based student model into a multi-layer architecture composed entirely of CNN. This transformation effectively decouples the inference complexity from that of the original training network, thereby significantly reducing the computational overhead during inference.

Complex transformer

During the training phase, we first use a complex transformer-based structure to carefully extract the features of the input signal, as shown in Fig. 2. We adopt a temporal-spatial encoding block following the design in25. The block jointly models temporal dynamics and spatial feature dependencies through two parallel Transformer-based encoders. Specifically, the time embedding and feature embedding are first concatenated and then split into two branches. The temporal encoder focuses on long-range dependencies along the time dimension, while the spatial encoder captures correlations among features and sensor channels.

Feature extractor of complex transformer structure with temporal and spatial feature interaction.

To enhance mutual awareness between the two encoders, cross-connections are established to exchange intermediate representations between temporal and spatial branches. Within each branch, the outputs are concatenated and projected by a 1 × 1 convolution to reduce dimensionality and improve computational efficiency. Finally, the processed features are passed to the subsequent block. By integrating this design into our framework, we are able to effectively capture both temporal evolution and spatial interactions in wearable sensor data.

We stack the three modules mentioned above and add a normalization operation in the middle to build the teacher model.

Re-parameterization of involution operator and inference process

Although Transformer-based architectures have demonstrated strong representational capacity, their inherent reliance on attention mechanisms makes direct re-parameterization challenging. This is because attention involves dynamic token-to-token interactions that cannot be easily reformulated into static convolutional weights. This is also the reason why we introduced the involution structure in the previous subsection. Involution provides input-adaptive kernels that can capture fine-grained local variations, thereby serving as an effective bridge between the expressive yet computationally expensive Transformer and compact convolutional structures.

In contrast, CNN have benefited greatly from re-parameterization techniques, such as those used in RepVGG26 and structural re-parameterization frameworks, where multiple branches during training can be equivalently transformed into a single convolution layer during inference. These methods substantially reduce inference cost without sacrificing training-time expressiveness. Schematic diagram of the structural reparameterization process

Although involution is input-adaptive by design, our re-parameterization does not convert it into a static convolution with fixed weights. At inference, the kernel generator still takes the input feature map and produces location-specific kernels . Re-parameterization here refers to folding training-time components (e.g., BN) into linear/conv layers and restructuring the computation so that both kernel generation and kernel application can be executed using standard convolution primitives (pointwise/depthwise/grouped convolution and tensor reshaping), which are highly optimized on common hardware. Thus, the adaptiveness of involution is preserved, while the inference graph is simplified and deployment-friendly.

Experimental setup

Datasets

In the experiments, the DIP-IMU dataset is employed, which is a publicly available large-scale benchmark for human pose estimation from wearable inertial sensors. This dataset comprises recordings from 10 subjects performing a wide variety of activities (e.g., walking, running, sitting, exercising), with ground-truth 3D poses captured by a marker-based optical motion capture system. Each subject wore 17 Xsens IMUs covering major body parts, and the data were recorded at 60 Hz. The dataset provides over 330 K frames of synchronized IMU and MoCap data. To evaluate model performance, the official protocol follows a subject-wise split, where approximately 80% of the sequences are used for training and 20% for testing, ensuring that test subjects are unseen during training. Data download link: https://dip.is.tuebingen.mpg.de.

In addition, the IMUPoser dataset is utilized, which is a recently released large-scale dataset designed for human motion reconstruction from sparse IMU configurations. Unlike DIP-IMU, IMUPoser employs only 6 IMUs placed on key body locations (wrists, ankles, waist, and head), making it closer to real-world wearable scenarios. The dataset contains recordings from 30 subjects performing diverse daily and athletic activities, with ground-truth 3D joint positions and orientations obtained from a high-precision optical motion capture system. The recordings span more than 1 million frames at 60 Hz, providing substantial variability in motion patterns. For evaluation, the dataset is split into training and testing sets according to the official protocol (subject-independent split). Data download link: https://github.com/FIGLAB/IMUPoser.

On DIP–IMU, our method achieves an MPJPE of 81 mm, essentially matching the strongest baseline FIP (80 mm) and slightly outperforming the Transformer variant (82 mm), while clearly surpassing earlier approaches such as DIP (114 mm), DynaIP (91 mm), Fusion Poser (97 mm), and IMUPoser (92 mm). We further include two generic lightweight backbones (MobileNet and GhostNet) adapted for time-series modeling. Although they yield relatively low latency (0.253 ms and 0.272 ms), their accuracy is substantially worse (127 mm and 131 mm MPJPE), suggesting that lightweight architectures alone—without task-specific modeling for pose dynamics and sensor–pose mapping—are insufficient for high-fidelity IMU pose estimation. Crucially, our per-frame latency is only 0.012 ms, corresponding to a throughput of 83,333 inf/s and a normalized time of 0.042× versus DIP. This yields a 20.5× speedup over FIP (0.246 ms) and an 82.8× speedup over Transformer (0.993 ms), while also being over 21× faster than MobileNet and GhostNet. The per-epoch training time (44.5 min) is shorter than Transformer (49.5 min) but longer than FIP (27.2 min) and the generic lightweight backbones (15.3–16.4 min), reflecting the additional teacher supervision and distillation training overhead.

On IMUPoser, our model attains 94 mm MPJPE—tying the Transformer (94 mm) and outperforming FIP (99 mm), Fusion Poser (124 mm), DynaIP (121 mm), DIP (137 mm), and IMUPoser (120 mm). MobileNet and GhostNet again show fast inference (0.239 ms and 0.264 ms), yet their accuracy drops markedly (148 mm and 160 mm MPJPE), reinforcing that general-purpose efficient backbones do not adequately capture the IMU-to-pose mapping without dedicated design. In contrast, our latency further reduces to 0.011 ms (90,909 inf/s; 0.042× vs. DIP), translating to 20.8× and 90.2× accelerations over FIP (0.229 ms) and Transformer (0.992 ms), respectively, and over 21× speedups compared to MobileNet/GhostNet. The per-epoch training time is 43.2 min, faster than Transformer (46.4 min) but higher than FIP (26.9 min) and the lightweight backbones (12.3–12.8 min), which is consistent with the extra distillation-related training cost.

Accuracy-efficiency trade-off Across both datasets, our approach lies on (or very close to) the Pareto frontier: it preserves near-state-of-the-art MPJPE while delivering one to two orders of magnitude lower latency than strong baselines. This behavior is consistent with the design—knowledge distillation compresses the Transformer’s temporal-spatial representations into a compact student, and structural re-parameterization collapses training-time branches into a single inference-time operator.

Throughput implications Given typical wearable sampling rates (60–200 Hz), the reported latencies (0.011–0.012 ms/frame) provide a very large real-time margin (well beyond the 16.7ms/frame requirement at 60 Hz), indicating that our model can support high-rate sensing and additional on-device processing. In contrast, Transformer-based variants remain the most accurate among heavy models but incur the highest delay, which can be prohibitive for strict real-time constraints.

Complexity perspective The observed speedups are aligned with the algorithmic complexity: after re-parameterization, the student’s core computation reduces to two 1D convolutional passes with linear complexity in sequence length L and channels C (O(LCK)), whereas attention modules in the teacher scale at least quadratically with L in the temporal dimension. This gap explains the consistent latency advantage while retaining accuracy via distillation.

Cross-dataset robustness The ranking of methods is stable across DIP-IMU and IMUPoser: our model maintains top-tier MPJPE with the lowest latency, indicating good robustness under distribution shifts (sensor placement and motion diversity). Together, these results confirm that the proposed student model achieves a favorable balance between accuracy and efficiency, making it well-suited for real-time deployment on resource-constrained wearable platforms.

Energy-aware efficiency analysis Energy consumption is a key concern for wearable deployment. Since we do not have access to a standardized on-device power measurement setup, we analyze energy-related proxy indicators under the same runtime environment (PyTorch, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 Ti). In addition to the reported inference latency (ms), we derive two proxy metrics: throughput (inferences/s, computed as 1000 / inference time in ms) and normalized inference time (relative to DIP, computed as t/). Under a fixed hardware and software configuration, energy per inference is typically proportional to average power multiplied by runtime; therefore, reducing runtime and computational overhead generally indicates improved energy efficiency trends, although these proxies do not represent absolute wearable energy consumption. Across both DIP-IMU and IMUPoser datasets, our method consistently achieves the lowest inference latency and the highest throughput. On DIP-IMU, our model reduces inference time from 0.274 ms (DIP) and 0.246 ms (FIP) to 0.012 ms, corresponding to approximately 0.044× normalized time versus DIP (i.e., a ~ 23× latency reduction) and a throughput increase from ~ 3–4k inferences/s to over 80k inferences/s. A similar trend is observed on IMUPoser, where our inference time is 0.011 ms, yielding 0.042× normalized time versus DIP and over 90k inferences/s, substantially higher than other lightweight baselines. These results suggest that the proposed design—especially structural re-parameterization that converts training-time multi-branch modules into a single, hardware-friendly inference form—provides strong deployment-oriented efficiency benefits that are closely related to energy-saving trends in practice.

Embedded deployment note and limitation While on-device benchmarking on mobile/embedded SoCs would provide the most direct validation of absolute latency and energy consumption, we currently do not have access to a standardized embedded evaluation and power measurement setup. Therefore, we report detailed and reproducible runtime measurements on a fixed platform (batch = 1, FP16, eval mode, synchronization) and provide throughput/normalized latency as proxy indicators. Importantly, after SRP the deployed student is implemented with hardware-friendly primitives and exhibits linear-time complexity with respect to sequence length, which explains the large empirical latency margin under typical wearable sampling rates (60–200 Hz). We leave end-to-end deployment testing (NNAPI/CoreML/TensorRT), memory footprint, and on-device power measurements on representative mobile platforms as future work.

Distillation strategy ablation To further demonstrate the performance comparison of different distillation strategies with structure reparameterization, we implemented two distillation methods (feature distillation and attention distillation) and reported the results in Tables 3 and 4. On DIP–IMU, both strategies achieve the same MPJPE (81 mm), and the student inference time is comparable (0.207 ms for feature distillation vs. 0.191 ms for attention distillation). On IMUPoser, feature distillation yields slightly better accuracy than attention distillation (91 mm vs. 92 mm), with comparable inference time (0.198 ms vs. 0.176 ms). Overall, the results indicate that feature distillation provides slightly more consistent accuracy across datasets, while attention distillation remains competitive and can be used as an alternative or complementary supervision. After structural re-parameterization, the final model achieves extremely low latency (0.012/0.011 ms) with competitive accuracy (81 mm on DIP–IMU and 94 mm on IMUPoser), demonstrating the deployment advantage of our design.

Effect of involution window size K We further study the influence of the involution window size K, which controls the local context used for position-specific kernel generation in the involution operator. In our implementation, the residual branch uses a fixed window size K = 5, and we only vary K in the main-branch feature extraction modules to isolate its effect. As shown in Table 5, on DIP–IMU, K = 3 and K = 7 obtain the best accuracy (81 mm), while K = 5 is slightly worse (82 mm) and a larger window K = 9 significantly degrades performance (86 mm). On IMUPoser, smaller/moderate windows work best (K = 3 or K = 5, 94 mm), whereas larger windows reduce accuracy (96 mm at K = 7, 100 mm at K = 9). In addition, increasing K consistently increases the average training time (43.9→47.2 min), suggesting higher computational overhead. Overall, these results indicate that overly large windows may introduce unnecessary smoothing and reduce robustness, while smaller/moderate K achieves a better accuracy–efficiency balance under our setting.

Discussion

Differences and complementarity with other lightweight strategies

In addition to distillation and structural re-parameterization, there exist other common lightweight strategies such as low-rank decomposition and quantization. These approaches are complementary but operate at different levels. Low-rank decomposition reduces parameterization by factorizing weight matrices/tensors, which can be effective when the model contains substantial redundancy; however, it may require careful rank selection and can affect accuracy, especially for temporal dynamics that rely on fine-grained correlations. Quantization reduces memory footprint and can accelerate inference on supported hardware, but it typically introduces additional constraints (e.g., calibration or quantization-aware training) and may incur accuracy degradation due to reduced numerical precision. In contrast, our framework emphasizes (i) distillation, which improves the student’s representation quality under a compact architecture, and (ii) structural re-parameterization, which converts multi-branch training-time modules into a single-branch inference-time form using standard operators, leading to low-latency and predictable deployment behavior. Importantly, these design choices are largely orthogonal to low-rank and quantization, and can be integrated with them as additional deployment optimizations in future work.

Time–frequency attention for robust IMU modeling

Recent advances in time–frequency attention mechanisms (e.g., Fourier- and wavelet-based attention) suggest that incorporating explicit spectral representations can improve the modeling of nonlinear and partially observed time-series signals. For example, recent wavelet-aware attention frameworks have demonstrated the potential of combining explicit time–frequency transforms with attention to better capture nonlinear dependencies in high-dimensional temporal signals35. Although these techniques have been actively studied in neuroimaging and other high-dimensional temporal domains, the underlying idea is also relevant to IMU signals, which often exhibit non-stationary patterns and can suffer from sensor noise, drift, and missing segments in real wearable settings. A promising direction is to integrate lightweight time–frequency transforms (e.g., FFT or wavelet decompositions) with efficient attention to capture complementary temporal and spectral cues, and to combine them with missing-data-aware training (e.g., masking or drop-sensor augmentation). We leave such time–frequency attention integration as future work, with a focus on maintaining the low-latency and deployment-friendly properties required by wearable devices36–38.

High-dimensional and multi-sensor modeling

Another direction is to improve robustness under higher-dimensional inputs (e.g., more IMUs, additional modalities, or richer feature stacks) by exploring tensor-structured representations and factorization-based learning. Recent studies combining generative modeling with non-negative tensor decomposition highlight the promise of extracting structured factors from high-dimensional and partially observed data39. Such ideas could inspire more scalable and noise/missing-aware fusion mechanisms for wearable sensing in future work.

Conclusion

This paper presents a wearable IMU-based human pose estimation framework that combines knowledge distillation () with an involution-based student and a principled structural re-parameterization () for deployment. We derive a complete SRP pipeline—BN folding, in-branch cascaded kernel composition, and cross-branch parallel fusion—so that the training-time operator collapses to a single inference-time module equivalent to two 1D CNN passes. Together with multi-level from a Transformer teacher, the proposed design decouples training expressiveness from inference efficiency. Extensive experiments on DIP–IMU and IMUPoser show that our model preserves near state-of-the-art accuracy (81 mm on DIP-IMU; 94 mm on IMUPoser, matching the best baseline on the latter) while achieving sub-millisecond per-frame latency (0.012 ms and 0.011 ms, respectively), yielding one to two orders of magnitude speedups over heavy baselines. The gains are consistent across datasets and subjects, indicating strong robustness and cross-subject generalization. Ablations further confirm that KD is critical for accuracy retention, whereas SRP is the dominant factor for ultra-low inference cost, and that a mid-range balance between hard labels and distillation () provides the most stable convergence. Future work includes extending the framework to variable or missing IMU layouts, online personalization and calibration, uncertainty-aware prediction, and broader multi-sensor fusion (e.g., barometer/EMG). Hardware-aware compression (mixed precision, quantization) and automated search of student backbones are also promising directions for further improving on-device efficiency.
