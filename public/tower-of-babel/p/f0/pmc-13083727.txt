A global review and perspective on prostate cancer survival: findings from survival studies of cancer registration data
Authors: Yuxin Zhou, Yixin Zou, Qiuming Shen, Zhuoying Li, Yuting Tan, Honglan Li, Yongbing Xiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo better understand global status, temporal trends, and geographic disparities of prostate cancer survival, we systematically collected published survival studies on prostate cancer from cancer registries worldwide.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in databases such as PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, SinoMed, CNKI and SEER for survival studies from cancer registries up to April 30, 2025. We synthesized data on observed, relative and net survival since the 1990s, along with age-standardized survival, and further stratified based on age at diagnosis and stage.ResultsThe overall survival was favorable and exhibited a continuous improving trend. In most countries and regions, the 5-year relative survival rate exceeded 70%. However, substantial geographic disparities were observed. The prognosis was better in developed countries in Europe and America than that in less developed countries in Asia and Africa. Since 2000, the age-standardized 5-year relative survival was the highest in the United States at 98.5% (2007–2010), while it was the lowest in Punjab of India at 30.3% (2013–2016). The prognosis was poorest for patients aged ≥ 75, or for those diagnosed with distant or stage IV.ConclusionsAlthough the survival has been improving over the past three decades, disparities between developed and less developed countries still persist. The extent of the popularization of prostate screening programs, the age or stage at diagnosis, and the sophistication of healthcare systems are likely to be significant contributing factors to these differences. Our results can provide fundamental statistics for formulating and refining relevant policies.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is a malignant epithelial neoplasm originating in the prostate gland. According to GLOBOCAN 2022,1 prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in the world and the fifth leading cause of cancer death among men. It is estimated that there are 1.5 million new cases and 397,000 deaths worldwide. In nearly two-thirds of countries around the world, prostate cancer is the most frequently diagnosed tumor among men. Descriptive epidemiological studies indicate marked regional and national differences in prostate cancer incidence and mortality.

The incidence in Asian countries is significantly lower than in developed countries in Europe and the Americas. Among high-incidence regions, Northern Europe, Australia, New Zealand, the Caribbean, and North America report the highest incidence. In contrast, the distribution of mortality does not follow the same pattern: the highest mortality is found in economically less developed regions, such as the Caribbean and sub-Saharan Africa.1 China has traditionally been considered to have low rates of prostate cancer incidence and mortality, below the global average.2 However, in recent decades, the incidence has increased substantially. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study, the age-standardized incidence in China was 9.34 per 100,000 in 2021, showing a notable increase since 1990. In China, prostate cancer ranks sixth in incidence and seventh in mortality among male-specific cancers, with both rankings rising, highlighting an emerging public health challenge.3 In addition to incidence and mortality statistics, survival is a critical indicator for assessing the effectiveness of cancer treatment and prognosis, and serves as an important reference for evaluating cancer control in specific regions.4 Prostate cancer, characterized by subtle early symptoms and highly heterogeneous progression, exhibits significantly higher survival in Western developed countries than elsewhere. Clinical outcomes are influenced by multiple factors, such as screening practices, early detection capacity, treatment standards, race and ethnicity, and stage at diagnosis. These may contribute to the wide variations in survival and mortality observed across countries and regions.5,6

Currently, comprehensive studies on global prostate cancer survival remain limited. Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to systematically examine and synthesize population-based survival data in order to provide a comprehensive description of survival patterns and trends, as well as to identify potential factors contributing to regional variations in survival. Understanding the overall outlook is fundamental for guiding the optimization of prevention and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and data extraction

The primary focus of this study was to collect population-based survival data from cancer registries worldwide in order to investigate the survival patterns and characteristics of prostate cancer. A comprehensive literature search was conducted through PubMed, Embase, SinoMed, Web of Science and CNKI databases, covering all publications up to April 30, 2025. The search strategy combined terms such as “prostate cancer”, “survival rate”, “cancer survival”, “population-based”, and “cancer registry” (Supplementary Table 1). Additionally, survival studies utilizing the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database for prostate cancer were also included. Cancer cases were coded using the Ninth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9), the Tenth Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10), and the Third Edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3). Cases coded as 185 or C61 were identified as prostate cancer.

Two researchers (Yuxin Zhou & Yixin Zou) independently screened the titles, abstracts, and keywords of all retrieved articles according to the predetermined criteria. Initially, the titles and abstracts of the candidate literature were screened. If population-based survival analysis or cancer registration was not mentioned, then the research was excluded. Then, full texts of the articles were reviewed using the exclusion criteria as follows: (1) The results were not related to observed, relative, or net survival; (2) The survival were not based on cancer registries; (3) No full text; (4) Conference abstracts; (5) Duplicated results; (6) The research calendar years were repetitive or partially overlapped. If multiple survival studies for a region during the same or overlapping time periods, we select the most recently published study or the one with the longest time span. Two researchers independently extracted the observed survival rate (OSR), relative survival rate (RSR) and net survival rate (NSR) of prostate cancer from the included studies to assess survival trends across specific regions. Considering the variations in population age structures across countries and regions, age-standardized 5-year relative and net survival rate were further collected to minimize the influence of age composition, thereby enhancing the comparability of survival across populations, time periods, and geographical areas. Given that clinical stage and age at diagnosis are critical factors influencing survival, stage-specific and age-specific RSR were also extracted. Staging was categorized in two primary ways: (1) Classified based on the anatomical location of the tumor into localized, regional, and distant prostate cancer. (2) According to the TNM staging system,7 prostate cancer is categorized into Stage I, Stage II, Stage III, and Stage IV. For age groups, the most commonly adopted category from the included studies was used, comprising five age groups: ≤ 44 years, 45–54 years, 55–64 years, 65–74 years, and ≥ 75 years. The aim was to compare the survival of specific age subgroups in different regions and periods.

We also extracted relevant information from each study, including the authors, publication year, country/region, cancer registry, calendar period, number of cases, age range, closing date of follow-up, data quality indicators, survival calculation method, and the follow-up method, in order to assess and minimize potential bias (Supplementary Table 9). Notably, most studies assessed data quality in accordance with the standards set by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC)/International Association of Cancer Registries (IACR),8,9 and the "Guidance Manual for Cancer Registration in China",10 ensuring the reliability, completeness, and representativeness of the extracted data. The excluded studies and exclusion reasons are shown in Supplementary Table 10. It should be noted that, for a few countries, the reported survival data were derived from secondary sources rather than from primary studies.

Statistical analysis

The method of Kaplan-Meier or life table was used to calculate the OSR. The OSR denotes the proportion of patients with a cancer who remain alive during a specific time period, including deaths from all causes. Also known as the all-cause survival, it is a crude measure that reflects real-world survival outcome. The RSR was estimated using the Ederer II or Hakulinen method. The RSR serves as an approximate estimate of the NSR. It is defined as the ratio of the observed survival of patients with a cancer to the expected survival of the general population with comparable characteristics during the same period. It indirectly excludes other causes of death, thereby reflecting the influence of the cancer itself on survival.11,12 The NSR represents the cumulative probability that cancer patients will survive at a specific time point after diagnosis, under the assumption that cancer is the sole possible cause of death. It provides a more accurate estimate of cancer survival than RSR. The NSR was estimated using the Pohar-Perme estimator.13,14 Due to the similar connotations of RSR and NSR, we employed these two indicators in combination. Age-standardized relative and net survival rates were estimated by applying the weights derived from the International Cancer Survival Standard (ICSS) cancer patient population.15

Considering the importance of survival from the past three decades in guiding current cancer prevention, treatment, and prognosis management, this review focuses specifically on data from 1990 onward. Zotero 7.0 and Excel 2021 were employed to manage and analyze the data from literature.

Given the predominantly indolent nature of prostate cancer, survival outcomes were generally more favorable than those of more aggressive malignancies, as reflected in relatively high short-term survival. Since the beginning of the 21st century, 1-year and 3-year OSRs were reported to be approximately 90 % and 80 % in Europe and the Americas, with corresponding figures of around 80 % and 60 % in Asia and Africa.

The 5-year survival, the most commonly reported clinical metric, enabled robust assessment of long-term survival trends. Overall, the 5-year OSR in Europe and the Americas was reported to be around 70 % across most regions, with the United States recording the highest at 93.0 % (1989–1995).30 In Asia and Africa, the 5-year OSR was generally close to 50 %. Moreover, the lowest 5-year OSR was reported in Qidong of China and Setif of Algeria, at 18.2 % (1992–1996) and 19.6 % (1986–2005), respectively.16,37

The global long-term survival has improved significantly since 1990. Particularly, in Qidong of China,16 South Korea,58, 59, 60,62 the United States,63, 64, 65, 66, 67 and Estonia,76 the increase in the 5-year RSR exceeded 30 percentage points. The United States has maintained the highest 5-year RSR, approaching 100 % between 1999 and 2020 and remaining relatively stable.63, 64, 65, 66, 67 In Europe, 5-year RSR was generally around 75 %, and Germany demonstrated favorable long-term outcomes, with Munich reporting the highest 10-year RSR at 91.3 % (2000–2004).44

However, the 5-year RSR remained relatively low in Asia and Africa, including China, and large parts of Africa, around 70 %. Notably, Japan, South Korea, Kenya, Mauritius and Namibia exhibited substantially higher survival than the regional averages in Asia and Africa.

Subgroup analysis of survival rate

Supplementary Table 6 shows the age-specific 5-year RSR of prostate cancer in China,16,22 India,84 South Korea,27,60 North America,85,123 Europe,45,47,52, 76,78,80,86,113,124,125 and Africa.82 Overall, survival improved over time across all age groups. Patients aged 45–54 and ≥ 75 had a relatively poor prognosis. Survival in Qidong of China tended to increase with age, but the trend was not statistically significant.16 In Asian populations, the 55–64 age group showed the most favorable prognosis, whereas in Europe, survival peaked among those aged 65–74. Contrary to the typical trend, prognosis among patients aged ≥ 75 years in Africa did not generally decline compared with those aged 65–74 years.

Supplementary Table 7 shows the available stage-specific 5-year RSR for Japan,57 South Korea,60,62 the United States,64, 65, 66, 67,126, 127, 128, 129 Europe,76,77,86,113,130 and Africa.82 Survival stratified by anatomical site revealed that prostate cancer with distant metastasis was associated with a markedly poorer prognosis, with 5-year RSR typically around 30 %. Localized prostate cancer demonstrated the most favorable outcomes, with 5-year RSR generally ranging from 80 % to 90 %, and reaching approximately 100 % in the United States and South Korea. Over time, survival for regional prostate cancer improved steadily, narrowing the gap with localized disease. However, improvements in the survival of distant metastatic prostate cancer remained limited. In contrast, survival for prostate cancer of unknown stage varied significantly across countries. Prostate cancer with stage I was associated with the most favorable prognosis, with 5-year RSR exceeding 90 % in countries such as the Czech Republic and Lithuania.77,86 Patients with stage IV had the lowest survival, typically ranging from 15 % to 40 %.

Prostatic small cell carcinoma (SCC) is a rare, highly aggressive cancer with limited survival outcomes. Supplementary Table 8 included two studies on SCC of prostate cancer.131,132 The 1-year RSR was only 43.9 % (2004–2015). Notably, the 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year survival of SCC in the United States have all shown a significant downward trend over time, contrasting with improvements seen in more common prostate cancer types.

Discussion

This study systematically collected and summarized the survival data on prostate cancer worldwide over the past three decades. Population-based survival is of great significance for exploring the survival patterns and trends across countries and regions. By comprehensively collecting, describing, and comparing global data, this research aims to provide valuable guidance for epidemiological researchers and clinicians in the field of prostate cancer.

It should be noted that, although survival is an important indicator for evaluating disease prognosis and healthcare quality, it must be interpreted cautiously due to lead-time bias, especially for prostate cancer, which is amenable to screening.100 Most prostate cancers are indolent, with slow progression and well-differentiated cells. The implementation of prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening in many Western countries was associated with a substantial rise in prostate cancer incidence and survival. By 2008, prostate cancer had become the most commonly diagnosed cancer in men in Europe and the Americas.106,123,126,133,134 However, the mortality reduction benefit of population-based PSA screening remains uncertain, and its potential for over diagnosis,135,136 over treatment,137 and reduced quality of life,138,139 thereby increasing the burden on healthcare systems. In the United States, an estimated 28 % of PSA-detected cases were over diagnosed.140 PSA screening detects a large number of indolent cases in advance, leading to an advance in diagnosis time and creating a false impression of improved survival. Therefore, prostate cancer survival trends should be interpreted cautiously. When comparing cancer survival across countries and regions, several limitations should be considered. Most studies are based on one or a few cancer registries, which may not fully represent the entire population, and differences in coverage, completeness, and quality further limit the reliability of cross-regional comparisons; moreover, instability in the population coverage of certain countries or regions further undermines the validity of time-series survival analyses. Variations in follow-up methods also affect data quality: passive follow-up may misclassify outcomes if registries are incomplete or outdated, as delayed or failed linkage between diagnosis and death records can result in missed deaths, creating an apparent excess of long-term survivors and thus overestimating survival, while active follow-up cannot entirely prevent loss to follow-up due to migration or death. If loss to follow-up is related to patient prognosis, covariate-based statistical adjustments, such as the model proposed by Ganesh et al., are necessary to reduce bias.141

Over the past three decades, the global survival of prostate cancer has exhibited an overall upward trend. However, substantial disparities remained in survival outcomes across countries and regions. In particular, the prognosis in less developed regions of Asia and Africa was markedly poorer compared to that observed in high-income countries in Europe and the Americas. Since the 21st century, both the 5-year observed and relative survival have generally been around 80 % in Australia and most European countries, and the 5-year RSR has approached 100 % in the United States. However, the 5-year survival in China and Africa has remained low, well below the levels reached in Europe in the 1990s. Specifically, the age-standardized 5-year relative/net survival generally exceeded 80 % in European countries and surpassed 95 % in the United States; in most Chinese regions, it remained close to 60 %.

Prostate cancer is typically characterized by an insidious onset, with early-stage symptoms often being non-specific or absent. Consequently, many patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage. The American cancer research institutions recommend early screening for prostate cancer, with serum PSA testing serving as a key method for detecting early lesions. Since the 1980s, the United States has widely promoted PSA screening nationwide. According to the National Health Interview Survey in 2000, over half of men aged ≥ 65 years had undergone PSA screening within the preceding year.142 In the management of early-stage or low-risk prostate cancer, active surveillance has been increasingly adopted in the United States as an alternative to immediate intervention. By closely monitoring tumor progression and initiating treatment only when necessary, this approach minimizes the negative impact of overtreatment on quality of life and may contribute to improve overall survival.143 The United States has led global advancements in minimally invasive surgery for prostate cancer. As early as 2001, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved the use of the Da Vinci Surgical System for radical prostatectomy. The precise operation of this system optimizes the preservation of organ function, thereby significantly improving quality of life of cancer patients.144 In addition, a multidisciplinary treatment strategy is employed for patients with advanced prostate cancer, integrating androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), chemotherapy, bone-targeted therapy, and radiotherapy. The aim of this strategy is to prolong survival.145 Moreover, variations in survival between countries and over time can be partly attributed to differences in the stage distribution at diagnosis. In the United States, for example, approximately 82 % of prostate cancer cases are diagnosed at the localized stage. Localized prostate cancer has the most favorable prognosis, which helps explain the relatively high survival observed in the United States.60

In Europe, prostate cancer survival was generally high in Nordic countries. As of 2010, the age-standardized 5-year RSR of prostate cancer in Sweden, Finland, Norway all exceeded 90 %. Beyond the impact of PSA screening, these outcomes can also be attributed to significant advances in risk-adapted (based on factors such as PSA, Gleason score, and clinical stage) and stage-adapted diagnosis and treatment. Such advances included brachytherapy, external beam radiotherapy, radical prostatectomy, chemotherapy, and the use of androgen deprivation therapy in adjuvant or neoadjuvant settings. Additionally, for castration-resistant disease, the use of 223Ra chemotherapy or radiotherapy, as well as the introduction of androgen receptor axis-targeted therapies in recent years, have further improved treatment efficacy.146 In Germany, the age-standardized 5-year RSR showed a marked increase between 2000 and 2010. However, this improvement disappeared after adjusting for stage, which may be associated with the implementation of PSA screening for asymptomatic men.113,114 Research further has indicated that the survival in Munich has been significantly higher than in other regions of Germany since the early 21st century. This disparity may be attributed to several factors: the broad use of PSA testing led to a more favorable T classification at diagnosis, initial treatment strategies shifted toward more comprehensive tumor resection, and advancements in therapeutic techniques have also contributed substantially.44 In Italy, the 5-year survival increased steadily from the late 1980s through the early 1990s. This trend was primarily driven by the introduction of PSA testing into routine clinical practice. Additionally, a decline in the proportion of metastatic cases enabled more patients to undergo radical treatments, which also contributed to improved survival outcomes.78

PSA screening was more prevalent in Japan and South Korea compared to other Asian countries such as China, India, and Singapore, contributing to better survival outcomes. Furthermore, developed countries benefit from abundant medical resources and well-structured healthcare systems. With advanced medical facilities, high institutional standards, and standardized treatment protocols, patients are more likely to receive high-quality care with better treatment adherence. In addition, studies suggest that the rising incidence of latent prostate cancer in Japan may also be linked to prolonged survival in recent years.147 In contrast, China has seen a significant increase in prostate cancer cases in recent years, primarily due to westernized lifestyles and dietary habits. However, compared with developed Western countries, China has not implemented a nationwide policy for PSA screening and primary prevention, resulting in a relatively low overall screening rate.148 Two observational studies reported prostate cancer detection rates of only 1.28 % (1998–2000) and 0.68 % (2004–2005), respectively, far lower than those in other countries.149,150 Public awareness of prostate cancer also remains limited, and there is no national consensus on biennial PSA screening for high-risk groups. As a result, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced or metastatic stage, missing the optimal treatment window. This is a major factor contributing to the survival gap between China and more developed countries.151 Additionally, China still faces challenges in the innovation and adoption of advanced treatment technologies and novel therapeutics for prostate cancer. Regional disparities within China further highlight the issue. Between 1987 and 2016, although improvement can be seen in Qidong, the survival remained lower than in Jiangyin, Wuxi, Shenyang, and other regions of China. As a predominantly rural area with slower economic and healthcare development, Qidong illustrates the urban-rural disparities in cancer diagnosis and treatment in China. The relatively high survival in Shanghai is primarily due to its well-developed healthcare infrastructure, plentiful medical resources, and advanced diagnostic and therapeutic services. Nonetheless, a considerable gap remains compared to the United States, highlighting disparities in comprehensive cancer care between China and developed countries. To improve the low survival, Shanghai has taken the lead since 2017 by implementing citywide community-based screening and precision treatment strategies.

Similar to China, prostate cancer control in Africa faces systemic challenges, including the absence of standardized PSA screening, limited public awareness, and insufficient healthcare resources, which result in a disproportionately high proportion of late-stage diagnoses. In low-income settings, underdeveloped healthcare systems further exacerbate delays in diagnosis and treatment; for example, a hospital-based study in South Africa found that patients waited an average of three months from biopsy to treatment planning. These shared barriers largely account for the poor survival outcomes observed in both China and sub-Saharan Africa compared with high-income countries.152

Subgroup analysis provided us with more informative evidence regarding prostate cancer survival. Although survival disparities were observed across age groups, they likely reflect differences in tumor stage, grade, or related clinical factors rather than age itself. Brenner et al. reported no excess mortality across age groups among patients with high- or moderately differentiated localized or regional prostate cancer, indicating that age is not an independent prognostic factor when stage and grade are comparable.123 Similarly, Winter and colleagues showed that most age-specific survival differences lost statistical significance after adjustment for clinical stage.113 Among age groups, the 5-year RSR was the lowest in cases diagnosed at age ≥ 75. This may be explained by diagnosis at more advanced stages, limited treatment intensity, and a higher comorbidity burden, leading to poorer prognosis and quality of life.153,154 Given the rarity of prostate cancer before age 50, younger patients are more often symptom-diagnosed and tend to present with biologically aggressive tumors, which may partly account for the lower survival observed in the 45–54 age group in some Asian countries.155 In certain European countries, survival was paradoxically higher in the 65 to 74 group than in the 55 to 64 group, likely due to more frequent PSA screening and lead-time bias. Clinical staging is considered the most important prognostic factor for prostate cancer, with substantial survival heterogeneity across different stages. Globally, the 5-year RSR for distant metastatic prostate cancer remain low at approximately 30 %, which is less than one-third of the rate observed for localized disease, with minimal improvement over time. Moreover, cross-country variation in the survival of “unknown stage” cases likely reflects differing proportions of early-stage patients within this category, further highlighting the predominant role of stage in shaping outcomes.

When using survival data from population-based cancer registries, attention will also be given to other prognostic factors of prostate cancer such as the types of cancer cells. SCC of the prostate is extremely rare and often asymptomatic in early stages. By the time symptoms appear, the cancer is usually advanced, resulting in a poor prognosis.156, 157, 158 The two included studies indicated that the survival of SCC of the prostate in the United States has been on a downward trend.131,132 Possible explanations included increased aggressiveness and malignancy of recent SCC cases; and there may be a misdiagnosis bias, especially in the early stage. Specifically, technical limitations may have led to the misclassification of high-grade adenocarcinomas as SCC, resulting in an overestimation of past survival.132 In addition, treatment completion is a key prognostic factor for prostate cancer. Hospital-based cancer registration data from India showed that 50 %−60 % of patients did not complete targeted therapy. Many faced barriers to timely diagnosis and treatment, especially in rural Punjab, whereas healthcare quality lags behind urban areas. These challenges likely contribute to Punjab’s lower age-standardized 5-year RSR compared to Mumbai and other Asian countries.84,159,160

In conclusion, this study summarized global prostate cancer survival characteristics and trends over the past three decades. While overall survival has improved, significant disparities persist between countries and regions. Patients with prostate cancer in developed countries, particularly in Europe and North America, have better survival than those in less developed regions. The results also suggest that age at diagnosis, stage, and pathological type are key prognostic factors that influence survival. These findings can inform the development and refinement of relevant policies.
