Leukoencephalopathy, brain calcifications, and cysts (LCC): Two unique cases
Authors: Julia Grafstein, Yuka Aoyama, Rena Godfrey, Colleen Howe, Joshi Stephen, May Christine Malicdan, William A Gahl, Camilo Toro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeukoencephalopathy with brain calcifications and cysts (LCC), also known as Labrune Syndrome, is a rare cerebral microangiopathy caused by biallelic variants in the SNORD118 gene, which encodes the small nucleolar RNA (snoRNA) U8, a critical component of ribosome biogenesis. We present LCC cases with unusually protracted diagnostic trajectories. Case 1 had symptom onset in adolescence but remained undiagnosed until adulthood, while Case 2 had a transient occurrence of seizures in childhood and developed disabling motor symptoms in adulthood. Both were initially misdiagnosed—one with neurocysticercosis, the other with Fahr’s disease and multiple sclerosis—leading to inappropriate treatments. On evaluation, both probands exhibited asymmetrical spasticity with upper motor neuron weakness and pseudobulbar features. Genetic analyses confirmed compound heterozygous SNORD118 variants, including a rare upstream non-coding change (n.–6G>A). Neuroimaging revealed extensive leukoencephalopathy and calcifications, with cysts present in only one case. Notably, the severity of neuroimaging abnormalities contrasted with the milder or delayed clinical manifestations, underscoring phenotypic variability and the insidious nature of the disease process. These cases expand the clinical and imaging spectrum of SNORD118-related LCC, highlight diagnostic pitfalls and demonstrate challenges in variant detection due to incomplete coverage or reporting of non-coding regions in exome and genome sequencing. Greater awareness of LCC and improved interrogation of non-coding RNAs through clinical sequencing are essential for timely and accurate diagnosis.Keywords: Leukoencephalopathy, Calcifications, Cysts, LCC, Labrune Syndrome, SNORD118

Introduction

Leukoencephalopathy with brain calcifications and cysts (LCC, OMIM #614561), historically referred to as Labrune syndrome, is a rare cerebral microangiopathy characterized by a triad of neuroimaging findings, i.e., diffuse leukoencephalopathy, asymmetrical intracranial calcifications, and parenchymal fluid-filled cysts. Since its first description several decades ago, LCC has been recognized as a clinically heterogeneous disorder, typically presenting in childhood with developmental delay, spasticity, dystonia, and seizures. Increasingly, however, adolescent- and adult-onset cases have been reported, broadening the phenotypic spectrum and underscoring the diagnostic challenges posed by this condition.

The genetic basis of LCC was established in 2016, when biallelic pathogenic variants in SNORD118 were identified as causative [1]. SNORD118 encodes U8, a highly conserved C/D box snoRNA essential for pre-rRNA processing and ribosome assembly [2,3]. Dysfunction of U8 snoRNA places LCC among the ribosomopathies, a growing group of disorders caused by impaired ribosome biogenesis [4]. Despite this molecular insight, the mechanisms linking SNORD118 variants to the associated neuropathology remain poorly understood.

In this study, we describe two individuals with LCC who exemplify diagnostic hurdles. One case developed symptoms in adolescence but remained undiagnosed until adulthood. The second case had atypical imaging lacking parenchymal brain cysts and presented as an adult with profound spasticity and dystonia, having had a self-limited interval of recurrent seizures in childhood. Both endured prolonged diagnostic odysseys and were initially misdiagnosed with unrelated neurological conditions. Research reanalysis of clinical exome and genome sequencing ultimately revealed compound heterozygous SNORD118 variants, including a reported pathogenic rare upstream non-coding change (n.–6G>A). By presenting these cases in the context of prior pediatric and adult reports, we aim to expand the clinical spectrum of SNORD118-related LCC, highlight the limitations of current sequencing pipelines for non-coding RNAs, and emphasize the need for improved awareness of this disorder across all ages.

Case presentations

Both affected individuals were enrolled in National Institutes of Health Undiagnosed Diseases Program (NIH UDP) [5,6] under a clinical protocol approved by the institutional review board of the National Human Genome Research Institute (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT00369421).

Case 1

A 42-year-old male was evaluated under the auspices of our program with a three-decade history of progressive neurological deficits, including progressive left spastic hemiparesis and spastic gait. There was no consanguinity or family history of a similar disorder.

Clinical findings

Case 1 was the product of an uneventful pregnancy and birth and developed normally. At age 12, he vacationed in Mexico; a few weeks later, he developed a severe febrile illness involving nausea, vomiting, myalgias, and diarrhea. By age 15, he began to develop progressive weakness, clumsiness, spasticity of his left upper extremity, and numbness and tightness of his left leg. A cranial CT scan was obtained because of the impact of these deficits on balance and coordination while playing sports. The CT showed areas of intracranial calcification and several deep cystic white matter lesions, some adjacent to the ventricular surface. Spinal fluid examination and serum and CSF titers for cysticercosis were negative. However, because of his history of travel to a cysticercosis endemic region and the multiple cystic brain lesions, Case 1 was treated empirically twice for neurocysticercosis (NCC) with albendazole, with no impact on the clinical course or neuroimaging features. At the time of our evaluation, he exhibited findings of progressive spastic left foot drop and recent onset of pseudobulbar affect (PBA).

Diagnostic evaluation

On clinical evaluation, the primary complaints of Case 1 included decreased dexterity, difficulty walking due to a left lower extremity spastic foot drop, spastic gait, and emotional lability consistent with PBA. He had brisk reflexes and generalized spasticity worse in the left lower extremity. Dilated eye exam revealed normal retinal vasculature.

Extensive laboratory testing, including autoimmune evaluation, blood counts, metabolic profiles, thyroid, renal, and liver functions, were unremarkable. The cerebrospinal fluid contained no white blood cells or oligoclonal bands and normal protein, amino acids, lactate, neurotransmitters, and IgG index. CDC cysticercosis serology was normal.

MRI of the brain showed several parenchymal cystic lesions, some with adjacent, faint contrast-enhancement areas of dense T2 hypo intensity corresponding to calcifications on cranial CT scan (Fig. 1). The lesions were mostly located in the deep and periventricular white matter; some were surrounded by leukomalacia, accounting for cerebral volume loss for his age. There were extensive areas of abnormal T2 signal hyperintensity in the white matter. A CT of the chest and neck had no ectopic calcifications. A mild to moderate degenerative cervical spondylosis was noted.

Genetic analysis

Previous genetic studies included negative mtDNA sequencing and negative quintet exome sequencing. At the NIH, nuclear family quartet clinical genome sequencing was reported as negative. Direct interrogation of genes associated with imaging findings of leukoencephalopathy and calcifications, including those for Aicardi-Goutières Syndrome (AGS types I thru VII), CTC1, PDGFBR, PDGFB, SLC20A2, and CSF1R, were negative. Targeted research reanalysis and Sanger sequencing, however, revealed compound heterozygous variants in the small non-coding RNA gene SNORD118, specifically NR_033294.2: n.3 C>T (Chr17(GRCh38):g 8173586 G>A) inherited from his mother and NR_033294.2: n.20 C>T (Chr17(GRCh38):g. 8173569 G>A) inherited from his father.

Case 2

A 53-year-old female was evaluated by the NIH UDP in 2017. She presented with disabling spastic quadriparesis, bradykinetic-rigid syndrome, dystonia, leukoencephalopathy and intracranial calcifications. There was a remote history of seizures emerging in infancy and into her early teen years with no recurrence. There was no family history of a similar disorder or consanguinity.

Clinical findings

After a normal uncomplicated pregnancy and birth, Case 2 had a first seizure at 5 days of age and was maintained on antiepileptic medication initially with 1–2 seizures per week but gradually achieved full seizure control by age 15 years. Seizure medicines were discontinued without recurrence. No brain imaging studies were available for the earlier part of her history. She was generally healthy until age 28, when she was noticed to toe walk; this progressed to scuffing with both legs. One to two years after the initial symptoms, Case 2 had difficulty with upper extremity dexterity, especially on the left, and dystonic posturing of her hands. She developed progressive spastic dysarthria and had difficulty swallowing foods. She has been unable to speak for the past 11 years and confined to a wheelchair.

Diagnostic evaluation

Due to her dense basal ganglia and dentate calcifications seen on brain imaging, Case 2 was initially diagnosed as having Fahr’s disease. This diagnosis was later questioned because of the presence of extensive leukoencephalopathy and the distinctly asymmetric nature of the lesions. Primary progressive multiple sclerosis (MS) was then considered given her sex, young age, increasing spasticity, white matter abnormalities on imaging and the presence of oligoclonal bands on spinal fluid analysis. This MS diagnosis was quickly discarded at a neuroimmunology clinic and she was referred to the UDP.

On physical examination at the NIH Clinical Center, she followed commands and answered appropriately with profound spastic dysarthria. She exhibited dystonic posturing of the shoulders, neck and face. Muscle tone was increased throughout, worse on the left. She required assistance to stand, turn and walk; these actions were accomplished with very slow shuffling steps. Reflexes were brisk throughout. She had distinct features of PBA.

Laboratory evaluations included negative or normal ANA, rheumatoid factor, MMA, ACE, ANCA, anticardiolipin antibody, Lyme, PTH, RPR, lactate, calcium metabolism, fatty acids, heavy metal screen, manganese, arylsulfatase A, and anti-SSA/SSB. A prior spinal tap indicated normal CSF IgG levels and IgG index.

A CT scan of the brain without contrast revealed large asymmetrical deep hemispheric and cerebellar calcifications. MRI of the brain revealed diffuse bilateral leukoencephalopathy, bulky bilateral asymmetrical areas of T2 hypo intensity corresponding to calcifications in the deep brain nuclei and a large arachnoid cyst within the left sylvian fissure (Fig. 2A). Generalized volume loss and corresponding mild ventricular dilatation were present. A small calcification in the soft tissues of the thoracic inlet anterior to the trachea and inferior to the right lobe of the thyroid gland appeared on the neck CT scan.

Genetic analysis

Prior genetic studies included singleton exome sequencing identifying a missense variant of unknown significance (VUS) (NM_001703: c.3916 A>G; p.Met1306Val) in the gene ADGRB2 and a homoplasmic variant of unknown significance in the MT-ND6 gene (NC_012920.1: m.14520 C>T; p.Gly52Ser). Retrospective targeted analysis of the short-read alignments from her exome sequencing study revealed that the 3’UTR of the gene TMEM107, and thus SNORD118, were not covered (Fig. 2B). A subsequent clinical genome study failed to report on SNORD118. Prompted by clinical suspicion, targeted re-analysis of the genome sequencing data and Sanger sequencing of SNORD118 and subsequent segregation to the parents, identified compound heterozygous previously reported pathogenic changes, i.e., NR_033294.2: n.–6G>A (Chr17(GRCh38):g.8173594 C>T) inherited from her father, and n.131 C>G (Chr17(GRCh37):g.8173458 G>C) inherited from her mother (Fig. 2B, C).

Discussion

This study provides insights into the phenotypic spectrum of LCC, highlighting two adults in whom the diagnosis was achieved after distinct, prolonged diagnostic trajectories. While LCC is most often recognized in childhood, both our cases were diagnosed as adults but were symptomatic at younger ages. Our first case presented with symptoms in adolescence yet remained undiagnosed until adulthood; our second case developed seizures early in life with complete remission, only to re-present later in adulthood with symptoms of profound motor impairment and PBA. These cases underscore the need for greater awareness of SNORD118-related imaging and clinical phenotypes across all ages and highlight the dissociation between striking neuroimaging abnormalities and relatively milder or delayed clinical manifestations, which likely reflect the slowly progressive nature of the microangiopathy and long-term neural/vascular compensation.

Diagnostic challenges and pitfalls

Accurate molecular diagnosis of LCC remains difficult due to both technical and clinical challenges. From a molecular perspective, SNORD118 is a small, non-coding RNA locus that is often incompletely captured and annotated in exome or genome sequencing studies. In both cases described here, variants were initially missed and only detected after targeted reanalysis of the SNORD118 region and confirmatory Sanger sequencing (Fig. 2B). This illustrates a persistent blind spot in current sequencing pipelines and emphasizes the need for improved strategies to reliably detect disease-causing variants in non-coding RNAs.

Clinically, diagnosis can be confounded by phenocopies. Case 1 was twice treated for neurocysticercosis based on the presence of brain cysts, focal calcifications, and a travel history to an endemic region. However, the lack of inflammatory markers in the CSF, negative serologies, and the deep white matter location of the cystic lesions were atypical for neurocysticercosis. Case 2, who had a history of seizures but with complete remission, was diagnosed with Fahr’s disease as an adult due to basal ganglia calcifications [7]. Progressive MS was subsequently suggested on the basis of young age, sex, relentless progressive spasticity, T2 white matter hyperintensities on cranial MRI and oligoclonal bands in the CSF. Both diagnoses, however, were refuted based on atypical MRI imaging findings. These misdiagnoses highlight the importance of considering LCC when confronted with patients at any age exhibiting imaging findings that include intracranial calcifications, leukoencephalopathies and/or cystic lesions (Table 1).

Phenotypic spectrum across ages

Epileptic seizures are the predominant clinical symptom in children and have been reported in almost half of the pediatric cases [8]. Both developmental delay and motor disorders have been reported in almost a quarter of pediatric cases [8]. Raised intracranial pressure appeared in five cases reported in that review, and intellectual disability and chronic headaches are documented in one case each. In adults, intracranial hypertension was the most common symptom. One ischemic and one hemorrhagic stroke were reported in two adult patients [9]. Progressive neurological deficits, such as focal motor, sensory, and visual deficits, dystonia, ataxia, and aphasia, have been noted in adults and children. “Adult-onset” presentations likely reflect adult recognition of a childhood process. Case 1 had adolescent onset with decades of diagnostic delay, whereas Case 2 had infantile-onset seizures that remitted before adult re-presentation. Adult presentations therefore often invovle longer diagnostic odysseys, competing differential diagnoses, and at times an incomplete imaging triad. For example, our second case lacked parenchymal cysts on available neuroimaging, underscoring that the absence of cysts should not exclude consideration of LCC when calcifications and leukoencephalopathy are present.

Oveall, there does not appear to be a clear correlation between the severity of imaging findings and disease severity. The extent, anatomical location and rate of expansion of these lesions contribute to the broad heterogeneity of clinical manifestations. Histological examination of resected focal lesions contain angiomatous-like blood vessels structures accompanied by gliosis and Rosenthal fiber deposition [9].

Molecular considerations

Both SNORD118 variants in Case 1 (n.3 C>T and n.20 C>T), and the n.131 C>G variant in Case 2 have been observed in other non-related families with LCC [1]. Additionally, Case 2 has the rare n.–6G>A variant, located six nucleotides upstream of the annotated SNORD118 transcription start site, within the immediate 5’ flanking region. Although outside the mature snoRNA sequence, this promoter-proximal variant could plausibly influence RNA polymerase III–dependent transcription initiation or disrupt interactions with regulatory proteins required for efficient U8 processing. Previous studies have shown that non-coding and flanking SNORD118 variants can impair U8 pre-snoRNA maturation and stability, leading to defective ribosome biogenesis and U8 snoRNA function [1,10]. Thus, n.–6G>A may contribute to disease by reducing transcriptional output or interfering with U8 processing, consistent with pathogenic mechanisms described for other SNORD118 variants.

Most patients reported in the literature are compound heterozygous and typically carry one ultra-rare allele and one more frequent allele, suggesting that homozygosity for the ultrarare alleles might be embryonically lethal [1]. Recent studies in murine and cellular models suggest that defective ribosomal assembly can lead to oxidative stress, altered neuronal viability, and aberrant gliovascular interactions [11]. How these processes ultimately contribute to the pathogenesis of LCC remains an open question.

Therapeutic considerations

Management of LCC is currently limited to symptomatic interventions, including surgical resections to address the mass effect of large cystic brain lesions, antiepileptic and anti-spasticity interventions, physical therapy, and psychotropic medications for those with behavioral symptoms [12]. Recent reports describe neuroradiological and clinical improvement with a vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitor (bevacizumab) [13,14]. Nonetheless, interpretation of this anti-VEGF treatment remains complicated by the very small number of treated patients, advanced disease stages at initiation, and reports of spontaneous radiological improvement [8,15]. While bevacizumab may represent a potential disease-modifying therapy, systematic studies and long-term follow-up are lacking. Given the central role of U8 snoRNA in ribosome biogenesis, future work should also explore ribosome-targeted approaches as potential therapeutic avenues.

Conclusion

These cases demonstrate that SNORD118-related LCC can present across a wide age range, from childhood to adulthood, and may be misdiagnosed as more common neurological conditions. Diagnostic delays are compounded by technical limitations in sequencing pipelines that frequently fail to capture or annotate small non-coding RNAs such as SNORD118. Recognition of this disorder at all ages, combined with improved methods for detecting non-coding RNA variants, will be essential to shorten the diagnostic odyssey. Understanding how the SNORD118 pathogenic changes converge onto this unique clinical, pathological and imaging phenotype is fundamental to targeted therapeutic interventions.
