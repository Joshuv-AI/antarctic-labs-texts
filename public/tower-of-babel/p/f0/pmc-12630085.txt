Introduction

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is a common endocrine disorder caused by a solitary parathyroid adenoma in approximately 85 % of cases. Parathyroid adenomas are typically located within the parathyroid gland however, they can also be found at ectopic sites such as thyroid, thymus, retropharyngeal space…

Indeed, intrathyroidal localization accounts for only 1 to 3 % of parathyroid adenomas, but poses significant diagnostic challenges, both on ultrasound and scintigraphy (1,2).

The aim of this report is to present a rare case of intrathyroidal oncocytic parathyroid adenoma and discuss its clinical, diagnostic, and therapeutic aspects. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE guidelines (3).

Case presentation

We report the case of a 72-year-old female patient with a medical history of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, early menopause at age 40, and osteoporosis. The patient had initially been hospitalized at endocrinology department for the management of severe hypercalcemia (3.5 mmol/l), associated with abdominal pain, vomiting, and agitation. Emergency treatment included intravenous hydration (6 l/day), administration of six ampoules of furosemide (Lasilix®), and dual therapy with bisphosphonates.

ENT examination revealed a firm, painless, right-sided cervical swelling that was mobile on swallowing, and no palpable cervical lymphadenopathy. The electrocardiogram showed a regular sinus rhythm without conduction abnormalities, particularly no QT interval shortening.

Upon her transfer to our department, biological assessment showed: hypercalcemia at 2.7 mmol/l (normal range: 2.2–2.6 mmol/l), hypophosphatemia at 0.45 mmol/l, elevated parathyroid hormone (PTH) at 494.8 pg/ml (normal: 12–65 pg/ml), moderately decreased vitamin D at 14.2 μg/l, normal calcitonin at 2.6 pg/ml, and an estimated creatinine clearance of 114 ml/min. Bone densitometry confirmed osteoporosis with a T-score of −2.5 SD.

Cervical ultrasound examination revealed an isoechoic nodule in the right thyroid lobe measuring 33 × 17 mm, classified as EU-TIRADS 3, with peripheral hypervascularity and no associated lymphadenopathy. No ectopic parathyroid gland was identified.

Technetium-99 m sestamibi (Tc99m-MIBI) scintigraphy combined with SPECT/CT demonstrated intense uptake in the right intrathyroidal nodule with central hypodensity. The patient underwent a right loboisthmectomy under general anesthesia. Intraoperative exploration revealed a well-circumscribed, brownish nodule approximately 3 cm in size, embedded in the lower right thyroid parenchyma, suggestive of an intrathyroidal parathyroid adenoma (Fig. 2). The lesion was excised en bloc. Macroscopic examination revealed an encapsulated nodule measuring 3 × 2.3 × 2 cm within the lower pole of the right thyroid lobe. Histological analysis confirmed an oncocytic parathyroid adenoma, composed of trabeculae of polygonal cells with abundant eosinophilic cytoplasm, without atypia or mitotic activity. There was no evidence of capsular or vascular invasion. The surrounding thyroid parenchyma was unremarkable.

Postoperative evolution was favorable, with normalization of serum calcium levels to 2.3 mmol/l by the third postoperative day and a decrease in PTH levels to 39.5 pg/ml at one-month follow-up. The patient reported significant improvement in bone pain, with no postoperative complications or hypocalcemia after 9 months of follow-up.

Discussion

IPAs represent a rare ectopic localization of parathyroid adenomas. Their incidence is estimated to range between 0.7 % and 6 % of primary hyperparathyroidism cases (4). Recent series suggest that approximately 2 % of patients undergoing surgery for primary hyperparathyroidism have an intrathyroidal parathyroid adenoma (5). They most often involve adenomas of the inferior parathyroid glands (approximately 70–75 % of cases), due to the embryological characteristics of parathyroid migration (5).

This unusual localization is explained by the common embryologic origin of the thyroid and inferior parathyroid glands, both derived from the third pharyngeal pouch, which may result in ectopic migration into the thyroid parenchyma (6).

Clinically, IPAs present with symptoms of primary hyperparathyroidism, similar to that of eutopic adenomas. Patients may be either symptomatic with renal stones, osteoporosis, or neuropsychiatric disturbances or can be asymptomatic (4). Patients present with hypercalcemia with elevated PTH levels that do not differ significantly from levels seen in conventional parathyroid adenomas (5).

A palpable cervical nodule is rare in benign parathyroid adenomas, including intrathyroidal ones, and its presence should rather raise suspicion of a thyroid pathology or a parathyroid carcinoma (4). It is noteworthy that patients with IPAs frequently present with associated thyroid nodules, which can complicate the clinical picture and preoperative localization (7). In summary, the clinical presentation of an oncocytic IPAs does not significantly differ from that of a conventional parathyroid adenoma, apart from the diagnostic challenge related to its ectopic location.

Once primary hyperparathyroidism is confirmed, accurate preoperative localization of the adenoma is essential to guide the surgeon.

Given that IPAs are rare and often small in size, a combined imaging approach is recommended to increase the likelihood of successful localization (4).

Preoperative identification of IPAs is challenging. Ultrasound is the first-line imaging modality. However, the detection of an intrathyroidal adenoma by ultrasound is difficult and highly variable, with a reported sensitivity ranging from only 29 % to 67 % (8). Indeed, an IPA typically appears as an homogeneous, hypoechoic intraparenchymal thyroid nodule, which can mimic a benign thyroid nodule (5), as was the case in our observation. Some features may suggest a parathyroid origin: a solid nodule without cystic component, markedly hypoechoic, and often vascularized by a single polar feeding vessel on Doppler imaging, which has been reported in approximately 84 % of IPAs (9). When applying these criteria retrospectively, the sensitivity of ultrasound for detecting IPAs can reach up to approximately 76 % (4).

Technetium-99 m sestamibi (Tc99m-MIBI) scintigraphy is considered the gold standard imaging modality, with a sensitivity ranging from 70 % to 90 %. However, its specificity decreases in cases of intrathyroidal lesions due to potential tracer uptake by oncocytic or hypercellular thyroid nodules.

In the specific context of IPAs, series including more than 10 patients report a sensitivity of 60 % to 83 % for MIBI techniques (planar imaging, SPECT, or SPECT/CT) in detecting IPAs (4). The combination of ultrasound and scintigraphy is therefore recommended, as these complementary modalities enhance the overall accuracy of preoperative localization compared to either technique alone (10).

In cases of discordant or inconclusive first-line imaging, second-line techniques may be considered. Four-dimensional computed tomography (4D-CT) is increasingly used in expert centers to localize parathyroid adenomas not identified by ultrasound or MIBI scintigraphy. It has demonstrated good sensitivity, of approximately 85 % particularly for ectopic or small-sized lesions (10).

Cervical MRI offers an alternative without radiation exposure or iodinated contrast injection. Its sensitivity in parathyroid imaging is variable (approximately 50–75 % according to studies), but it can be useful for mediastinal adenomas or in cases of contraindication to CT (9,10).

Differential diagnosis includes benign, malignant thyroid nodules, benign and metastatic group VI lymph nodes.

Histologically, this case report illustrates an oncocytic variant of parathyroid adenoma, a rare subtype. Oncocytic parathyroid adenomas (also known as oxyphilic adenomas) represent a histological subtype characterized by the predominant presence of oncocytic cells defined as exceeding 75 % to 90 % of the tumor, depending on the classification criteria (11).

In oncocytic parathyroid adenomas, the cells are very large and adherent to each other, nuclei are small and darker, nucleoli are barely visible, with numerous single cells and no colloid, but a similar substance. The monotonous cell population presents indistinct cytoplasmic borders forming large cohesive sheets with pseudo papillary patterns (11).

Immuno-histochemistry demonstrates TTF-1 (nuclear labelling) and thyroglobulin (colloid labelling) in thyroid tumours, while these antigens are absentin parathyroid tumours (11).

Compared to classic chief cell adenomas, oncocytic adenomas tend to be larger in size in published series and may present with more pronounced hypercalcemia, likely due to their greater tumor mass (12,13).

Surgical management remains the curative treatment for parathyroid adenomas. Any symptomatic form including renal stones, bone involvement such as osteoporosis or fractures, or neuromuscular symptoms constitutes a formal indication for surgery.

In cases of suspected intrathyroidal parathyroid adenoma (IPA) or diagnostic difficulties, a lobectomy may be indicated to allow for complete excision of the lesion. This procedure may be preferred when imaging suggests an IPA, with no evidence of a eutopic adenoma or an ectopic adenoma at other locations (4). Intraoperative PTH monitoring can also be used to verify adenoma removal.

In our case, loboisthmectomy enabled an en bloc resection of the lesion.

Postoperative outcomes were favorable, with rapid normalization of serum calcium and PTH levels, confirming the effectiveness of the surgical procedure.

Excision of an oncocytic intrathyroidal parathyroid adenoma generally results in complete resolution of hyperparathyroidism. The prognosis is excellent, with sustained normalization of biochemical markers and an extremely low recurrence rate in sporadic forms (5). However, long-term follow-up is recommended to detect any recurrence or associated pathology.

Conclusion

Although rare, IPAs are clinically important, as their atypical location may lead to failed surgical exploration if unrecognized. This type of adenoma highlights the need for close collaboration between clinicians, radiologists, surgeons, and pathologists to ensure optimal management of atypical forms of hyperparathyroidism. A multimodal diagnostic approach including ultrasound, MIBI scintigraphy and careful surgical planning are recommended in suspected IPA cases.
