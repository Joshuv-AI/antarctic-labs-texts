Sustained co-release of ciprofloxacin and dexamethasone in rabbit maxillary sinus using polyvinyl alcohol-based hydrogel microparticle
Authors: Maryam Jalessi, Yasaman Tavakoli Moghaddam, Mehdi Khanmohammadi, Sajad Hassanzadeh, Zahra Azad, Mohammad Farhadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Topical delivery to paranasal sinuses through sustained-release stents is one of the new horizons in treating chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS). This study aims to introduce and evaluate sustained co-release of encapsulated ciprofloxacin (CIP) and dexamethasone (DEX) in polyvinyl alcohol-based carriers within the maxillary sinus of rabbit animals. DEX and CIP were loaded in a tyramine-substituted polyvinyl alcohol microparticle (PVATyr MP). The mechanical stability, degradability, and sustained-release patterns of both drugs as well as cellular cytocompatibility were assessed in vitro. The PVATyr MPs were then injected into the maxillary sinus of rabbits and they were monitored weekly for 21 days. Nasal endoscopy, MRI imaging, and tissue microscopy were used to follow the changes and compared them with the control condition. Also, the concentrations of drugs were evaluated in the maxillary sinus and blood samples over the study period. Produced PVA-based MPs possessed a relatively narrow particle size distribution (CV 7.7%) with proper physical stability until 30 days of incubation. The uniform-sized PVATyr MPs and their surrounding hydrogel showed sustained-release profiles for DEX and CIP for up to 32 days in vitro. The injected drugs-loaded hydrogel showed complete clearance from the maxillary sinus of rabbits within 28 days. The concentrations of DEX and CIP in mucosal remained within the therapeutic window when measured on days 7, 14, and 21, which were well above the plasma concentrations without any pathological changes in endoscopy, MRI imaging, and histological examinations. DEX/CIP loaded PVATyr MPs provided an effective, controlled, and safe sustained-drug delivery in both in vitro and in vivo analyses at therapeutic concentrations with minimal systemic absorption, suggesting a promising treatment approach for CRS.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS), with an incidence rate of 10.9%–13%, is among the most prevalent chronic human diseases [1, 2]. It exhibits a complex, heterogeneous, and multifactorial nature, stemming from the dysfunctional interplay between impaired physical and immune barriers and exogenous factors such as bacterial and fungal infections. These factors synergistically evoke prolonged and excessive inflammatory responses [1, 3]. Biofilm formation poses an even more serious challenge for patients with hereditary mucociliary dysfunction, such as cystic fibrosis. These biofilms strongly resist antibiotic penetration, and fluoroquinolones are among the few options capable of eradicating them [3, 4]. To date, about 30% of CRS patients require additional therapy within two years of medical management, and many will eventually need surgical interventions [5].

Intranasal corticosteroid (INCS) sprays and systemic antibiotics remain the primary conventional treatments for CRS, with wide applicability in both pre- and post-surgical conditions [6, 7]. While INCs present limited intranasal complications, issues such as low patient adherence to daily medication, physical/anatomical obstacles that restrict access to inflammation sites, and the rapid clearance of drugs from the mucosa can adversely affect treatment success rates [6, 7].

A topical drug delivery system is considered a highly effective approach that minimizes systemic and local side effects. It ensures the desired concentration of the drug remains at the intended tissue site, addressing the challenges associated with traditional treatments [8–11]. This system has rapidly progressed from packs, dressings, and spacers to stents and meshes, and continues to evolve into more recently developed methods such as hydrogel nanocarriers and microparticles (MP), or a rational combination of both [2, 12, 13]. Injectable MP-based drug delivery systems are superior due to their enhanced surface area and the ability to provide a safe, targeted, and controlled release. Production of spherical drug carriers using microfluidic biofabrication technique is also a growing field in encapsulation technology, which involves the manipulation of microscale polymeric solutions and oil fluids within micro-channels through water-in-oil-in-water (w/o/w) emulsion systems” [14–16]. Among synthetic polymers, polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) has been widely used in the pharmaceutical and biomedical industries for controlled drug delivery and tissue regeneration/repair purposes due to its exceptional biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and cost-effectiveness [17–19]. Several studies have used PVA as a carrier for dexamethasone (DEX; a glucocorticoid), due to its numerous anti-inflammatory effects, and ciprofloxacin (CIP), for its favorable antibacterial properties in CRS treatments [17, 20]. The motivation of this research is to investigate the impact of the fabrication of PVA-based MP for the sustained co-release of anti-inflammatory and antibiotic drugs in the treatment of CRS. The goal is to ensure that both anti-inflammatory and antibiotic activities are retained following drug release, as a potential treatment to prevent biofilm formation associated with inflammation in CRS [3, 21, 22]. We are particularly interested in optimizing their physicochemical properties and, crucially, assessing their potential for sequentially co-delivering chemotherapeutic drugs, with a specific focus on treating CRS. Also, doses of Dex and CIP used in injecting PVA-based MPs for animal studies were approximately 400 µg and 800 µg/animal, respectively. These concentrations are multiple times higher than the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) [20, 23–25].

Here, we have designed size-controlled tyramine-conjugated polyvinyl alcohol MP (PVATyr MP) by adopting a co-flowing microfluidic device and HRP-mediated reaction (see Fig. 1A, B for schematics and details). These PVATyr MPs serve as a cytocompatible drug carrier for the sequential co-delivery of dexamethasone (DEX) and ciprofloxacin (CIP) in a sustained manner to the maxillary sinus of rabbits (Fig. 1C). The PVATyr MPs loaded with DEX and CIP were evaluated through morphological and size distribution analyses, as well as physical characterization for stability, drug release, and mechanical properties. Additionally, the sustained release of DEX and CIP, along with potential inflammatory responses, was assessed in rabbits. Materials and methods

Poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA, Mw 85,000–124,000, 99% hydrolyzed), 4-dimethylamino pyridine (DMAP), succinic anhydride (SA), HRP (150–250 units/mg solid; using pyrogallol), H2O2 aqueous solution (30% (w/w)), N-2-(N-morpholino) ethane sulfonic acid (MES), hydroxysulfosuccinimide (NHS), tyramine hydrochloride (Tyr), dialysis tubing (MWCO.12 kDa), and 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, USA). DEX and CIP were donated by Tehrandarou Co., Iran. Human Olfactory Ectomesenchymal stem cells (OE-MSC) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium: Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12) media supplemented, fetal bovine serum (FBS) 10%, penicillin-streptomycin 100 µg/mL, which were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Grand Island, NY, USA). All other reagents were purchased commercially and used without further purification unless otherwise specified.

In vitro experiments

PVATyr synthesis

PVATyr was synthesized according to a published protocol by mixing PVA with SA and DMAP for preliminary carboxylation and in following step with collected PVA product with tyramine, NHS and WSCD for phenolation [17, 18]. The PVA was dissolved in distilled water at a concentration of 5.0 wt% and at a temperature of 60 °C. Next, 122 mM SA and 87 mM DMAP were added to the reaction mixture, which was then stirred at 60 °C for 24 h. The resulting PVA-COOH was precipitated by adding it to a three-fold excess of acetone and was subsequently washed sequentially with 80% ethanol (three times), 90% ethanol, and 99% ethanol to remove chemical residues. The PVA-COOH product was then freeze-dried. Following this, Tyr groups were coupled to the carboxyl groups on PVA-COOH using a conventional carboxyl-amine coupling reaction. The dried PVA-COOH (5 g) was dissolved in 60 mM MES buffer (100 mL) at 60 °C. Once fully dissolved, the polymeric solution was allowed to cool to room temperature. Chemical reactants, including 1.25 g tyramine, 1.25 g NHS, and 1.80 g WSCD, were added to this solution. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 16 h. The synthesized PVA-tyramine (PVATyr) was then harvested using the same procedure as for the PVA-COOH synthesis, involving organic solvent precipitation and freeze-drying. The Ph conjugation and its quantity were analyzed by using H-NMR and ultraviolet (UV) absorption respectively [26, 27].

PVATyr microparticle production

The PVATyr MPs were prepared through coaxial double-orifice microfluidic technique in water-oil emulsion system [17]. In brief, 300 mg of PVATyr was dissolved in PBS at 60 °C to achieve a final concentration of 6% (w/v) and then allowed to cool to room temperature. Subsequently, 10 mg of DEX, 20 mg of CIP, and 2 mg of HRP were added to the solution. The resulting mixture was transferred to a syringe connected to the first syringe pump. Meanwhile, 60 mL of liquid paraffin containing 4% (w/v) lecithin was saturated with 150 µL of H2O2 (35%) using a homogenizer for 10 min and then centrifuged at 3500 rpm for 5 min to remove non-dispersed water molecules. The H2O2-saturated oil solution was poured into a separate 60 mL syringe connected to a second syringe pump. Both the PVATyr solution and the liquid paraffin were connected, respectively, to the inlets of the microfluidic system and to the outer ports of the coaxial double-orifice device using polyethylene microfluidic tubing. The polymeric solution (at a rate of 50 µL/min) and liquid paraffin (at a rate of 1.5 mL/min) were flowed into coaxial device. The PVATyr MP were formed in intersection of two fluids in microfluidic channel and covalent bonds among PVATyr molecules formed by diffusion of H2O2 into PVATyr MPs [17]. Then, the collected PVATyr MPs in the Corning tube were washed with PBS solution to remove residual oil from the MP surfaces via centrifugation at 1500 rpm for 3 min. The average diameter of the produced MPs was measured from images obtained using an optical microscope. The morphology and microstructure of the prepared PVATyr MPs were analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Durability of microparticles

To assess durability and stability of MPs under different fluid dynamic and external glass bead stress conditions [10, 15], a specific number of MPs were placed in a 24-well tissue culture plate with 1.5 mL of PBS solution in each well. Additionally, 0.1 g of glass beads (with an average diameter of 500–600 µm) was added to the wells. The Well plate was then placed on an orbital shaker operating at 100 rpm. At specific time intervals, the number of intact MPs in each well was counted microscopically. The percentage of intact MPs was calculated by comparing the number of intact MPs to the initial number at each time point, up to 96 h.

Degradability test

For this, 1 mL of PVATyr solution (6 w/v %) was placed into cylindrical silicon mold. Subsequently, HRP (1 unit/mL) and H2O2 (1 mM) were added to the solution, and the mixture was left to crosslink for 12 h. Once crosslinking was complete, the cylindrical hydrogels were washed and incubated in Milli-Q water for two days to ensure full swelling. The hydrogel samples were accurately weighted (W0) and then immersed in PBS at 37 °C. At regular time intervals, the buffer solution was removed from the samples and the hydrogels were weighted (Wt). The weight remaining ratio was defined as Wt/W0 × 100%.

Release of drugs from MP

The release rates of DEX and CIP from PVATyr microparticles (DEX/CIP/PVATyr MPs) and from encapsulated MPs in injectable PVATyr bulk hydrogel were determined using UV-Vis spectrophotometry, with detection wavelengths of 242 nm for DEX and 277 nm for CIP [8, 17, 28]. For this study, 20 mg of freeze-dried DEX/CIP/PVATyr MPs were dispersed in 5 mL of PBS and placed in a dialysis membrane (MWCO 12 kDa). Additionally, 20 mg of MPs were mixed with 200 µL of 1% (w/v) PVATyr, HRP (1 unit/mL), and H2O2 (1 mM), and the mixture was transferred into a dialysis membrane containing 5 mL of distilled water. The samples were then placed in 50 mL of PBS in an orbital shaker set at 100 rpm. At various intervals, 500 µL aliquots were withdrawn from the PBS solution, and each withdrawn sample was replaced with an equal volume of fresh release PBS. Cell viability assay

To assess the cytotoxicity of drug-loaded MPs, OE-MSCs were seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells/cm2 into 24-well plate. The DMEM culture medium was supplemented with 200 µL of the prepared MPs. At specified time intervals, cellular morphology and growth were observed using an inverted light microscope. Additionally, mitochondrial activity of each samples was analyzed using a colorimetric assay kit (WST-1) according to a previously published method. For this analysis, the supernatant medium was removed from samples and after washing with PBS fresh culture medium was poured into 24-well plate. Then, WST-1 reagent was added to the each well and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 450 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu RF-5000). The assessment of mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity in the seeded cells was indicated by an increase in the quantity of water-soluble formazan dye derived from the tetrazolium salt dissolved in the culture medium.

In vivo experiments

Experimental design

Thirteen female New Zealand white rabbits (2.5–3.5 kg) were divided into three experimental groups: the first group consisted of pilot animals treated with drug-free PVATyr MPs (n = 2); the second group served as the control and received drug-free PVATyr MPs (n = 3); and the third group comprised animals treated with drug-loaded PVATyr MPs (n = 8). Animals were kept in separate cages within a temperature- and humidity-controlled facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle. They were quarantined and acclimatized to the facility for 1 week before the study. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed 2 days before the procedure to confirm the normal condition of the sinus mucosa. In the preliminary study, drug-free PVATyr MPs were injected into the right maxillary sinus as a pilot group. They were followed by MRI every seven days until the complete clearance of hydrogel from the sinus which approve the surgery method and the degradation time period of the injected hydrogel required to determined study time period. Additionally, the gel injection for the other groups was performed in the right maxillary sinus.

Hydrogel injection and endoscopic evaluation

Rabbits were anesthetized with an intramuscular injection of xylazine hydrochloride (20 mg/kg) and ketamine hydrochloride (10 mg/kg). Approximately 30 min before the procedure, a single dose of cefazolin (25 mg/kg) was administered to prevent infection at the incision site. No further antibiotics were administered to the treated rabbits. Nasal endoscopy (1.7 mm, 30-degree scope, Karl Storz, Tuttlingen, Germany) was performed bilaterally to exclude pre-existing infections or abnormal lesions. To locate the precise area for intramaxillary sinus injection, the rabbit’s nasal dorsum was prepped and then a sagittal incision of approximately 2 cm on the midline of the nasal dorsum down to the periosteum was performed. The skin and periosteum were then elevated to expose the bony midline suture and the anterior walls of both maxillary sinuses. The injection site was 5 mm off the midline (internasal suture) and 1.5 cm below the fronto-maxillary suture [29–31]. The needle was directed laterally at a 45-degree angle (Fig. 2A, B). The volume of the injected substrate was 200 µL per rabbit. The skin was then stitched closed. Endoscopic evaluation

Nasal endoscopies (with A 1.7 mm 30-degree endoscope) were performed in all rabbits preoperatively and postoperatively at the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd weeks. Before the gel injection, an endoscopy was applied to exclude animals that had an infection in the paranasal sinuses and any lesion. Nasal cavity and superior, middle and, inferior meatus were inspected bilaterally.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of maxillary sinus

An MRI scan was performed using a 3.0 Tesla (3 T) Siemens system equipped with a 20-channel H1 head coil, while the rabbits were under ketamine (35 mg/kg) and xylazine (4 mg/kg) anesthesia. Two MRI sequences were obtained: T2-weighted transverse plane (TR/TE 6200/97) and T2-weighted coronal plane (TR/TE 4530/90) for all animals at 7, 14, and 21 days postoperatively.

Histological evaluations

Rabbits were euthanized, and their heads were harvested for histologic sectioning and staining. Histological analysis was conducted at specific sites on the lateral wall of the maxillary sinus according to previously reported method [23, 24]. For decalcification, tissue specimens were immersed in a formaldehyde solution (10% w/v) for 48 h and subsequently placed in an ethylene diamine tetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for 14 days. Serial cross-sectional slices, ranging from the nose to the skull, were created. Representative sections of the maxillary sinus were then chosen, dehydrated, clarified, and embedded in paraffin. Following this, the specimens were cut on a microtome into 5–7 µm thick slices and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess tissue inflammatory response patterns. (Fig. 2C–E). Ten slides were evaluated by a veterinary pathologist who was blinded to the specimen, side, and groups.

Pharmacokinetics

At 7, 14, and 21 days post-injection, the CIP and DEX concentrations were measured in the maxillary sinus mucosa (n = 1 at each time point) and blood plasma (n = 2 at each time point). The purification and pre-concentration procedures for the maxillary sinus mucosa and blood plasma were performed according to previously described methods [32–34]. The quantities of drugs in each sample were analyzed using HPLC with a UV detector. The system was calibrated with standard DEX and CIP at various concentrations.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using Excel 2021 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (La Jolla, CA), with significance set at P < 0.05. The statistical evaluation employed unpaired Student t-tests, and data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean.

Results

Analysis of synthesized PVATyr

The PVA structure was successfully modified by carboxylation through grafting succinic anhydride onto the PVA chain, as evidenced by new peaks appearing around 2.6 ppm (Fig. 3A). These peaks correspond to the methylene protons of succinic acid in PVA-COOH [8, 19]. Subsequently, Tyr was conjugated to PVA-COOH through aqueous carbodiimide chemistry. The PVATyr spectrum demonstrates reproducible amidation between PVA-COOH and Tyr, with aromatic protons of the phenolic group appearing at the ortho and meta positions at 6.8 and 7.2 ppm, respectively (Fig. 3A) [18, 35]. Additionally, the quantitative amount of Tyr introduced onto the PVATyr backbone was measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer [17, 18]. The value was determined from a calibration curve obtained from known percentages of tyramine chloride. The amount of conjugated Tyr moiety was 1.95 × 10−4 mol-Ph/g-PVATyr (Fig. 3B) which was high enough for the MP production through enzyme-mediated crosslinking reaction and microfluidic system [14, 16, 36].Fig. 3A 1H-NMR spectrums of intact PVA, synthesized PVA-COOH and PVATyr, B UV-spectrophotometry of synthesized PVATyr, C–E Microphotograph, size distribution, and SEM of PVATyr microparticles loaded with dexamethasone and ciprofloxacin respectively

PVATyr MP characterization

The diameter of the MPs and their distributions were determined using the software ImageJ on microphotographs [10, 14]. The PVATyr MPs exhibited a significantly narrow particle size distribution, with an average size of 162 µm, making them highly appealing for topical drug delivery applications (Fig. 3C, D) [7, 37, 38]. This finding was further supported by SEM analysis, which showed that the MPs had regular and uniform structures with smooth surfaces (Fig. 3E). The smaller MPs observed in the SEM image are attributed to the dehydration and drying process used in preparation for SEM imaging.

Mechanical stability and degradability of MPs

The number of intact PVATyr MPs exposed to both dynamic fluid and external bead stresses decreased for both the drug-free MPs and those containing DEX/CIP (Fig. 4A). Over an extended period of up to 96 h, there was about a 70% increase in the number of ruptured PVATyr MPs, indicating the need for improvement in the strength of the hydrogel membrane. Interestingly, under the dynamic fluid condition alone, the PVATyr MPs remained completely stable and maintained their shape and structure. In addition, a significant mass loss in the hydrogel occurred during the initial days of incubation, followed by a more moderate and gradual loss in the subsequent days (Fig. 4B). Sustain release of drugs

The PVATyr MPs released a significant amount of drugs during the first two days, with cumulative release percentages of approximately 43% for DEX and 39% for CIP. The total drug release was completed within 16 days. (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, the PVATyr MPs embedded within the injectable PVATyr hydrogel matrix limited the burst release of both DEX and CIP to less than 20% within the first 2 days. Following this initial burst, the release rates decreased and continued at a reduced rate, with approximately 90% of the total drug released by 28 days and complete release achieved by 32 days. (Fig. 4C).

Cellular viability

Next, we evaluated the effect of drug-loaded PVATyr MPs on the viability of OE-MSCs. In all experimental groups, most cells demonstrated high vitality and maintained a fibroblast-like morphology, characterized by a fully spindle-shaped appearance (Fig. 5). The overall cell morphology remained unchanged in all groups. The cells treated with both drug-free and drug-loaded PVATyr MPs proliferated rapidly, reaching 90% confluence after 96 h. Discussion

Recalcitrant CRS is a real therapeutic challenge in otolaryngology due to its complex multifactorial underlying pathology. Additionally, the anatomically difficult-to-access inflamed frontal and sphenoid sinuses can barely benefit from INCS sprays, which are the mainstay of CRS treatment [6, 7]. Polyposis, mucosal edema, and secretions are additional physical barriers that interfere with the optimal function of steroid sprays, particularly in the immediate postoperative condition. Even when the steroids reach the site of inflammation, ciliary clearance rapidly washes them off the mucosal surface, preventing sufficient mucosal-drug contact time [33, 39]. A recent prospective study of 174 patients with CRS reported patient non-adherence rates as high as 50% for intranasal steroids and 75% for saline irrigation, highlighting the need for alternative drug delivery approaches to ensure successful treatment of CRS [39].

In this study, we demonstrated a sustained co-release of CIP and DEX from PVA-based MPs that maintained therapeutic local concentrations of both drugs simultaneously for 3 weeks. Chemical analysis of the synthesized drug carrier through 1H-NMR characterization of PVA derivatives revealed significant differences between the peaks of PVA, PVA-COOH, and PVATyr, as well as the presence of new peaks corresponding to both carboxyl and aromatic phenol groups in the synthesized substrates (Fig. 3A) [18, 19]. The MPs are commonly used in drug delivery systems due to their structural and functional advantages. They are well-suited for the convenient and well-tolerated administration of drugs through multiple routes, such as oral, intranasal, transdermal, and intravenous delivery [2, 7, 9]. The narrow particle size distribution and regular shape of MPs directly impact the effectiveness and performance of the drug delivery system, ensuring a consistent and predictable controlled release profile during patient treatment [2, 7, 40]. The produced PVATyr MPs exhibited a uniform structure and narrow particle size distribution, and the incorporation of DEX/CIP did not cause any variation in their shapes and morphologies [8, 17]. The average size of PVATyr MPs was about 162 µm, with a Poisson-like distribution (Fig. 4C, D), demonstrating the system’s ability to produce finely controlled-size MPs by choosing suitable flow velocities in a microfluidic device [10, 16, 41]. Additionally, properly sized MPs can be easily administered through intranasal delivery devices, allowing for convenient and straightforward injection into the sinuses since was no channel blockage or clotting of the hydrogel vehicles during injection and during the topical delivery, these do not penetrate into the tissue and perform a local effect [25, 38, 40]. We did not observe any structural instability, including fragmentation or coalescence, of PVATyr MPs in extended time of incubation in physiological condition. This indicates that the proper concentrations of PVATyr and other chemical reactants, as well as the appropriate volumetric flow of solutions within the axisymmetric microfluidic device, were applied during the fabrication process. Furthermore, the uniform and smooth surface topography of PVATyr MPs ensures homogeneous and consistent release of encapsulated drugs in therapeutic delivery applications [7, 9, 11, 37].

Mechanical durability of MPs is another essential requirement for the stability of drug-loaded vehicles during tissue regeneration. Although PVATyr MPs were subjected to mechanical stresses from stirring and external glass beads, they were not sufficiently strong, and the membranes broke and ruptured over extended experimental periods. This highlighted the need for supportive substrates to enhance MP stability in tissues that experience substantial mechanical stress. Interestingly, the proposed MPs remained completely stable, and we did not observe any deformation or loss of shape even after extended incubation under fluid dynamic stress conditions. This suggests that PVATyr MPs could be utilized in body tissues subjected to low mechanical stress or moderate fluidity [15]. The degradability of MPs allows for optimal drug release kinetics during treatment and eventual tissue clearance. Although degradation results showed that PVATyr hydrogel was mostly stable, this can be attributed to the natural properties of PVA and covalent bonds among Tyr groups [18, 35, 42]. In detail, both bi-phenols with C–C or C–O linkages between the ortho carbons of the aromatic ring or between the ortho carbon and the phenolic oxygen generally exhibit low susceptibility to hydrolysis under physiological conditions and are non-cleavable even under acid-catalyzed hydrolysis. Ether bonds are also typically reported to resist hydrolysis due to their low electron density and minimal reactivity with water [18, 42]. However, grafted ester bonds on PVATyr cause partial hydrolytic degradation, displaying a linear degradation profile as shown in Fig. 4B. Therefore, any degradation observed in the PVATyr hydrogels is proposed to result from the degradation of the ester functional groups rather than from the newly formed crosslinking bonds created through HRP-mediated hydrogelation [35, 42].

The drug release profiles observed in the experimental groups indicate a notably rapid release of both DEX and CIP from PVATyr MPs. This rapid release can be attributed to the large surface area of the MPs and the short diffusion distance of the drugs from the MP matrix to the surrounding media [10, 20, 43]. The integration of drug-loaded PVATyr MPs into injectable bulk hydrogel carrier has garnered significant attention in tissue regeneration and drug delivery [8, 44]. This approach could control the fast-initial release of drugs while promoting sustained release over a longer period. In the present study, the burst releases of DEX and CIP from MPs and the surrounding bulk PVATyr gel were less than 20% in the first 2 days. Eventually, the released drugs reached about 90% over 28 days. The bulk PVATyr hydrogel structure containing the MPs prolonged the release profile of both DEX and CIP to over 32 days, which is significantly longer than drug encapsulation in MPs alone and consistent with previously reported results [8, 20, 45]. The MPs suspended in the additional bulk PVATyr hydrogel carrier exhibited a moderately bi-phasic release profile for both drugs. In the initial phase, DEX and CIP were rapidly released from the MP surface due to molecular diffusion from the water-filled pores of the polymer matrix. The subsequent phase involved the erosion of the PVATyr hydrogel matrix [37, 40, 42]. These results confirm the hypothesis of a prolonged sustained-release profile and a delayed burst release of DEX and CIP from the double encapsulation by MPs and bulk hydrogel carrier as a double protective barrier, guaranteeing a longer system lifespan and a more sustainable release with a significant delay in the initial burst release profile [8, 44]. The injectable drug-loaded MPs and bulk hydrogel carrier from PVATyr act as a double protective barrier and ensure prolonged release of DEX and CIP at the injection site while minimizing the adverse effects associated with systemic therapy. Far et al. performed various strategies to control the burst release of encapsulated drugs in spherical drug carriers. Their products showed a sustained release of the drug from the nanoparticles over 7 days, with approximately 20% of the drug released as an initial burst within the first 20 h [40]. Also, the Propel family products, made from PLGA bioabsorbable polymers coated with mometasone furoate, has been approved for use in the post-surgical ethmoid or frontal sinus pathways. They are the only commercially available stents that provide a slow release of medication for up to 30 days [46–48]. Although these stents are effective in managing the inflammatory aspect of CRS, biofilm formation remains untreated, which is a significant mechanism contributing to poor clinical and surgical outcomes in recalcitrant CRS [2]. Furthermore, the stent itself is susceptible to bacterial colonization over the long term [49]. A study of post-market complications related to Propel sinus stents found that the most common adverse effect was infection, affecting 21.8% of patients [50]. These issues suggest potential benefits from a product that provides dual medication release.

In a recent study of patients with acute exacerbations of CRS, amoxicillin, amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, and clarithromycin demonstrated limited effectiveness in inhibiting or eradicating biofilms, while levofloxacin showed better results. Additionally, planktonic bacteria retrieved from these patients exhibited over 90% susceptibility to quinolones, including CIP and levofloxacin [22].

However, maintaining a sustained high concentration of this medication is necessary for effectiveness, and systemic administration of fluoroquinolones may pose a high risk of complications for patients [51, 52]. The CIP has been the drug of choice for experimentation in vitro and in animal models using sustained-release stents. Studies have shown that to effectively reduce biofilm mass, a prolonged and sustained release is necessary, meaning that the initial burst release must be tightly controlled [23, 53, 54]. Cho et al. demonstrated that a CIP-loaded stent, which released 50% of the drug within the first two weeks, could reduce the biofilm mass of Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 to 10% of control levels at a 20× minimum MIC [24]. In another study, the CIP Ivafactor Sinus Stent, which featured 80% drug release until day 21, decreased the total bacterial load to less than 1% of control levels [53]. he CIP-Azithromycin Sinus Stent, which had a sustained release of 80% for CIP and 93.85% for azithromycin by day 28, successfully reduced the biofilm mass [54]. In the present study, less than 19% of the loaded CIP was released within the first two days, with 90% released over 28 days (Fig. 4C). These results are quite consistent with those reported in the literature. The MIC of PAO1 biofilm, used to test the effect of drugs on biofilm mass, is reported to be 0.06 μg/mL [23, 55]. Here, the CIP mucosal concentration on days 7, 14, and 21 was respectively 4, 3.1, 2.4 times above reported MIC (Table 1) which was properly high enough. Thus, the CIP mucosal concentration is within the effective range, while the plasma concentration remains low in animals [23, 55].

In this study, MRI was used to evaluate the extent of gel reabsorption in the sinuses. MRI is superior to CT for distinguishing between thickened mucosa, retained secretions, and retention cysts [56]. The MRI analysis revealed that the hydrogel gradually disappears from the sinus cavity without adversely affecting the surrounding mucosa (Fig. 6B). Considering the degradation and durability tests performed in vitro (Fig. 4A, B), it can be inferred that the observed results are related to both the dynamic aeration system in the sinus cavity and physiological metabolism, which contribute to the gradual removal of the hydrogel from the implant site [57]. Furthermore, histological evaluation confirmed no morphological changes or infiltration of inflammatory cells in the sinus mucosa and olfactory epithelium (Fig. 7), validating the promising compatibility of the developed PVA-based MPs with co-loaded drugs and the surrounding hydrogel for sustained-drug delivery [24, 29].

Limitations

The developed DEX/CIP/PVATyr MP require a free space and immobile placement for injection to deliver drugs in a sustained manner. Therefore, it is not feasible to place them in the ethmoidal cavity, a common site for the recurrence of polyps post-surgery, where commercially available eluting stents are typically used. However, the prepared drug-eluting MPs could be used in the maxillary, sphenoid, and frontal sinuses, allowing the drugs to eventually flow through the ethmoidal cavity. This drug delivery system also needs to be evaluated in an animal model of CRS with gram-positive /or gram-negative bacteria, including staphylococcus aureus/or pseudomonas aeruginosa, which are common pathogens in CRS [58, 59]. Additionally, the effect of DEX/CIP/PVATyr MPs on biofilms should be investigated in a separate study.

Conclusion

The DEX/CIP/PVATyr MPs offer an effective, controlled, and safe sustained release of drugs both in vitro and in vivo. Their injectability, rapid transition to a more solid form within seconds after injection, suitable direct contact with the mucosa, and complete clearance with no adverse effects on tissues after 21 days make them a suitable topical drug delivery device. This unique formulation combines antibiotic and anti-inflammatory therapeutics into a single product, making it highly applicable for managing CRS and potentially revolutionizing postoperative care following functional endoscopic sinus surgery. Taken together, the data presented here demonstrate the great potential of the designed PVA-based MPs and injectable bulk hydrogel carrier for the efficient co-delivery of drugs needed in CRS treatment.
