Single‑dose levamisole at breeding alters early pregnancy immune–endocrine profiles without affecting fertility in ewes
Authors: Ali Rişvanlı, Abdugani Abdurasulov Kholmurzaevich, Kursantbek Attokurov Sharabidinovich, Pirimkul Mamatkalykov Mamatkalykovych, Aiturgan Duyshobayeva, Elmurat Obdunov, Burak F Yuksel, Mert Turanlı, Ibrahim Seker, Timur Eshimbekov, Rosa Abdyldaeva Tynaibekovna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundReproductive efficiency in sheep is influenced by complex interactions between the endocrine and immune systems, particularly during early pregnancy. Immunomodulators such as levamisole are known to enhance immune responses and have been investigated for their potential to improve reproductive outcomes in various animal species. However, the effects of levamisole administered at the time of breeding on both pregnancy rates and key immunoendocrine markers in ewes remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of levamisole, administered at varying doses during breeding, on pregnancy rates and selected immune and endocrine parameters in ewes. A total of 30 ewes were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 10 each): a control group received subcutaneous saline; the second group received levamisole at 2.5 mg/kg; and the third group received 7.5 mg/kg, all administered at the time of breeding. Blood samples were collected on days 0, 10, and 20 post-breeding. Levels of IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α, progesterone, activin A, activin B, follistatin, and total immunoglobulin (Ig) were measured using commercial ELISA kits.ResultsPregnancy rates did not differ statistically between the groups (p > 0.05). However, on day 10, pregnant ewes in the 2.5 mg/kg group showed significantly higher activin A levels (p < 0.05). Activin B levels were elevated in pregnant ewes in both the control group (day 10) and the 7.5 mg/kg group (day 20) (p < 0.05). Follistatin levels were higher on day 10 in pregnant control animals (p < 0.05). IFN-γ and IL-2 levels increased in pregnant animals, particularly in control and 7.5 mg/kg groups, respectively. Progesterone levels peaked on day 10 in all groups.ConclusionsWhile the measured immune and endocrine parameters varied, these changes did not impact the pregnancy rates.Keywords: Activin, Cytokine, Ewe, Follistatin, levamisole

Background

Levamisole is a broad-spectrum anthelmintic, effective against lung and gastrointestinal tract nematodes. Since the 1970 s, it has been recognized that this drug influences the immune system in both animals and humans. Levamisole exhibits immunomodulatory effects by regulating cellular immunity, humoral immunity, and the functionality of cells such as macrophages. In all living organisms, the typical anthelmintic dose of levamisole is about 7.5 mg/kg, whereas the immunomodulatory dose is roughly 1/4, 1/3, or 1/2 of the anthelmintic dose. Usage of doses higher than these suppresses the immune system. Importantly, the effects on the immune system are also influenced by treatment frequency and dosage. Intermittent applications are reportedly more effective than continuous ones [1–4].

Levamisole has been successfully used for immune deficiency states, chronic infections, inflammatory diseases, melanomas, and lung carcinomas. This medicine reduces the severity, duration, and frequency of seizures. However, levamisole does not treat diseases in animals that have not been immunized. It only lessens the risk of infection in animals that have been immunized against the disease, should they encounter a second exposure to antigens [5].

Activins are protein molecules that exhibit both proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory activity, and they play a crucial role in controlling the cytokine cascade that drives inflammatory responses. Follistatin is another protein that inhibits or neutralizes the functions of activins, potentially regulating the inflammatory process [6].

Activin A mediates the presentation of antigens to naive T lymphocytes in lymph nodes by instigating the activation, differentiation, and migration of dendritic cells. Activin A, released from dendritic cells, inhibits NK cell functions by curbing their ability to proliferate, mature, and produce IFN-γ [7]. Activin A is also produced by Th2 cells and prompts antibody production by B cells, alongside the development of regulatory T cells [8].

Activin B, a homodimer of inhibin B, has its biological functions inhibited by follistatin. Nonetheless, the extent of its activity in inflammation is not widely known [9]. Follistatin can bind many members of the TGF-β protein family, inactivating their functions. This engagement plays a part in regulating the inflammatory process and tissue damage [8].

Despite its beneficial immunological effects, studies have yielded conflicting results regarding levamisole’s impact on reproduction. Some research suggests it may enhance reproductive performance by improving immune regulation during early pregnancy, which is critical for embryo survival. Conversely, other studies—particularly in ewes—have reported that levamisole negatively affects conception rates when administered during the peri-breeding period. This discrepancy underscores the complex relationship between immune modulation and reproductive success and highlights the importance of dose and timing.

To date, limited studies have evaluated how levamisole affects pregnancy-related immune and endocrine parameters, such as cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α), reproductive hormones (e.g., progesterone), and regulatory proteins (e.g., activins and follistatin). These molecules play pivotal roles in establishing maternal immune tolerance, implantation, and early embryo development. The aim of this study was to ascertain the effects of levamisole, an antiparasitic drug with established immunomodulatory properties, on IFN-γ, IL-2, TNF-α, progesterone, activin A, activin B, follistatin, and total Ig levels in pregnant animals.

Material and method

Phase I

Ethics and consent to participate

The study was conducted on a private commercial sheep farm located in Osh, Kyrgyzstan, during the natural breeding season (August to September 2024), when ambient photoperiod and temperature are conducive to estrous activity in ewes. The study population comprised once-born, clinically healthy Kyrgyz coarse-wool sheep, aged 2 to 4 years, and of similar body condition scores. Ethical approval for the study protocol was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of Kyrgyzstan-Turkey Manas University (Approval No. 2024/02; dated 11.03.2024). Informed consent was obtained from the farm owner, and a signed permission form allowed the use of their animals and facilities for experimental purposes.

All animals were housed in a well-ventilated, semi-open barn with adequate shaded and open areas. The total shelter area provided approximately 4–5 m² per animal, with an additional shaded resting area of 2 m² per ewe, ensuring protection from excessive sun exposure and rain. Feeding was carried out twice daily, in the morning and evening, using a standardized ration based on alfalfa hay and a commercial concentrate mix, tailored to meet the nutritional requirements of breeding ewes. Clean drinking water was provided ad libitum throughout the study. No feed restriction was applied during the experimental period.

Study groups and experimental design

A total of 30 multiparous ewes were selected based on clinical health, age (2–4 years), and reproductive history. All ewes belonged to the Kyrgyz native coarse-wool breed and were managed under identical feeding and housing conditionsthroughout the experiment.

To detect estrus, ewes were exposed to teaser rams (vasectomized males) twice daily at 07:00 and 18:00 for behavioral signs such as standing heat, restlessness, tail fanning, mounting behavior, and mucous vaginal discharge. Ewes that displayed clear signs of estrus were transferred to a breeding pen for mating.

The ewes were then observed for natural mating behavior in the breeding pen. Observations were conducted every 30 min between 06:00 and 20:00, allowing detection of the exact time of successful copulation. The mating period for each ewe lasted 1–2 days, after which they were immediately separated from the rams to prevent multiple matings.

Natural mating was performed using fertile, proven rams of the same breed (Kyrgyz coarse-wool). The rams were aged 3 to 4 years, with an average body weight of 70–75 kg, and had a prior history of high fertility under farm conditions. The male-to-female ratio was 1:10, and each ram had successfully completed a breeding soundness evaluation prior to the study.

Following successful mating, the 30 ewes were randomly assigned into three equal experimental groups (n = 10 per group):

Group 1 (Control): Subcutaneous injection of sterile physiological saline (0.9% NaCl) at the time of breeding.

Group 1 (Control): Subcutaneous injection of sterile physiological saline (0.9% NaCl) at the time of breeding.

Group 2 (Immunomodulatory Dose): Subcutaneous administration of levamisole at 2.5 mg/kg body weight (approximately 1/3 of the anthelmintic dose).

Group 2 (Immunomodulatory Dose): Subcutaneous administration of levamisole at 2.5 mg/kg body weight (approximately 1/3 of the anthelmintic dose).

Group 3 (Anthelmintic Dose): Subcutaneous administration of levamisole at 7.5 mg/kg body weight (standard anthelmintic dose).

Group 3 (Anthelmintic Dose): Subcutaneous administration of levamisole at 7.5 mg/kg body weight (standard anthelmintic dose).

Between Days 17 and 21 post-breeding, all ewes were again exposed to teaser rams to detect any return to estrus, which would indicate failure to conceive. Ewes that did not return to estrus were presumed pregnant.

To confirm and monitor pregnancy status and evaluate immune and endocrine changes, blood samples were collected from the jugular vein at three time points: Day 0 (breeding day), Day 10, and Day 20 post-breeding. Blood samples were centrifuged to obtain serum, which was stored at − 80 °C until further analysis.

Pregnancy diagnosis was later confirmed by transrectal ultrasonography on Day 35 post-mating, using a 5.0 MHz linear probe. This non-invasive method enabled detection of embryonic vesicles and fetal heartbeat, ensuring accurate pregnancy confirmation.

Phase II

Laboratory analysis

We utilized commercial ELISA kits specific to each species to assess hormone levels in the collected blood serum. We measured Activin A (Catalog Number: SL00165Sp, Sunlong Biotech, China), Activin B (Catalog Number: SL00166Sp, Sunlong Biotech, China), Follistatin (Catalog Number: SL00167Sp, Sunlong Biotech, China), TNF-α (Catalog Number: ELK8038, ELK Biotechnology, USA), Immunoglobulin G (Catalog Number: ELK8725, ELK Biotechnology, USA), Progesterone (Catalog Number: ELK8876, ELK Biotechnology, USA), IFN-γ (Catalog Number: ELK9131, ELK Biotechnology, USA), and IL-2 (Catalog Number: ELK9808, ELK Biotechnology, USA). We followed the ELISA test application methods as outlined in the manufacturer’s guide. After completing the process, we determined serum hormone levels by reading the plates at 450 nm with an ELISA reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, USA) [10].

The data obtained was then statistically analysed based on whether the animals were pregnant or not, as well as the number of days.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics for all parameters of the obtained data were initially calculated. Next, an analysis was conducted to determine whether the data related to all parameters exhibited normal distribution. The analyses concluded that they did not display normal distribution and hence did not comply with the parametric test assumptions. The normality of data distribution was assessed using multiple complementary methods, including the Shapiro-Wilk test, Q-Q plot analysis, histogram and box plot evaluations, along with examination of skewness and kurtosis coefficients. Upon determination that the normality assumption was not met, the nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis analysis of variance was employed for comparisons of parameters among the three groups. For pairwise comparisons of parameters showing statistically significant differences, Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction was applied as a post-hoc analysis. The Chi-square test was employed for frequency and proportional values when comparing pregnancy statuses and rates among the groups. Additionally, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare pregnant and non-pregnant paired groups. For comparisons between days in terms of pregnant and non-pregnant statuses within all general groups and on a group basis, the Wilcoxon test was applied. SPSS version 22.0 was used for these calculations and analyses.

Discussion

The present study investigated the immuno-endocrine effects of levamisole administered at varying doses at the time of breeding in ewes during early pregnancy. Although levamisole treatment did not significantly alter pregnancy rates among groups, it elicited notable shifts in several immune mediators and reproductive hormones. These findings suggest that levamisole influences early pregnancy physiology through modulation of immune and hormonal pathways, even in the absence of direct effects on fertility outcomes.

Consistent with earlier studies in different species [11–16], both the immunomodulatory (2.5 mg/kg) and anthelmintic (7.5 mg/kg) doses of levamisole failed to significantly impact pregnancy rates in this study. Rişvanlı and Aydın [15] previously reported improved pregnancy rates in rats subjected to repeated levamisole treatments prior to breeding, suggesting a potential cumulative effect. In contrast, Pancarci et al. [16] observed no changes in pregnancy rates or progesterone levels following a single dose in cattle. In the present study, progesterone concentrations peaked around Day 10 post-mating in all groups—a pattern consistent with normal luteal phase progression—but no significant differences were noted between treatment groups, regardless of pregnancy outcome. This implies that a single levamisole dose does not compromise luteal function or disrupt progesterone-mediated support of early gestation.

A key focus of this study was the activin–follistatin system, which plays a central role in regulating implantation, trophoblast invasion, and immune-endocrine interactions at the maternal–fetal interface [17–20]. Our results showed increased activin A levels in pregnant ewes treated with the immunomodulatory dose on Day 10, and elevated activin A in non-pregnant animals from the anthelmintic group on Day 20. These changes suggest that levamisole modulates the activin system in both a dose- and time-dependent manner. Increased activin A early in gestation could support endometrial receptivity and immune cell recruitment, as activin A promotes dendritic cell maturation and TGF-β secretion—both vital for maternal tolerance and implantation. However, elevated activin A levels at later stages in non-pregnant animals may reflect a compensatory immune activation or failed embryo signaling, potentially linked to excessive immune stimulation.

Follistatin, a binding protein that neutralizes activins, showed increased concentrations in pregnant ewes from the control group on Day 10. This may represent a physiological mechanism to modulate excessive activin signaling and maintain the delicate balance between immune activation and tolerance at the implantation site. As O’Connell et al. [21] highlighted, disruptions in the activin–follistatin axis can lead to implantation failure or early pregnancy loss. In this context, levamisole’s influence on this pathway may have beneficial or detrimental effects depending on timing, dose, and the reproductive state of the animal.

Cytokine analysis provided further insights into the immune dynamics during early gestation. IFN-γ levels were highest in pregnant ewes from the control group on Day 20, while IL-2 levels peaked in pregnant animals receiving the anthelmintic dose. IL-2 is critical for T-cell proliferation and regulatory T cell (Treg) differentiation, both essential for establishing maternal tolerance. Meanwhile, IFN-γ, primarily produced by uterine NK and Th1 cells, is involved in vascular remodeling and trophoblast invasion. These findings suggest that levamisole may enhance immune readiness during conceptus recognition and implantation, but the biological implications of this immune activation remain complex.

Interestingly, TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine with known roles in embryo-endometrial signaling and implantation, did not differ significantly across groups or time points. This suggests that levamisole’s immunostimulatory effects may be selective and that TNF-α activity is tightly regulated to avoid cytotoxic effects during early pregnancy.

Levamisole is also recognized for stimulating B-cell-mediated antibody production. In this study, total immunoglobulin (Ig) levels peaked in pregnant animals treated with the anthelmintic dose on Day 20. While this confirms levamisole’s capacity to enhance humoral immunity, the implications for pregnancy are unclear. Elevated Ig levels may reflect a generalized immune activation or adaptive maternal responses, but since no improvement in pregnancy rates was observed, these changes may have occurred systemically rather than locally at the maternal-fetal interface.

The discrepancy between immunological changes and reproductive outcomes highlights the complexity of the immune-endocrine interplay during implantation. While moderate immune stimulation is beneficial and even necessary for implantation and tolerance, excessive or mistimed immune activation can be detrimental. It is plausible that levamisole, depending on dose and timing, could tip the balance from a tolerogenic to an inflammatory state, potentially impairing embryo development or endometrial receptivity.

Conclusion

In summary, while levamisole administration at breeding did not influence pregnancy rates in ewes, it significantly altered several immune (IFN-γ, IL-2, Ig) and endocrine (activin A, activin B, follistatin, progesterone) parameters. These results highlight levamisole’s complex immunomodulatory action and suggest its potential to modulate maternal immune responses during early gestation. Future studies should explore different dosing schedules, repeated administrations, and uterine-level immune dynamics to fully understand levamisole’s role in reproductive immunology.
