Detection of lingual canal in mandibular single-rooted teeth using dental operating microscope and dental loupes: a comparative study
Authors: Emre Çulha, Fatma Tunç, Mikail Nahırcı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMost of the studies on the methods used to detect extra canals are related to the second mesiobuccal canal in maxillary molars. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of dental operating microscopes (DOM) and dental loupes (DL) in detecting lingual canals in extracted single-rooted mandibular teeth. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference in lingual canal detection success between DL and DOM.Materials and methodsSixty single-rooted mandibular teeth were randomly selected by using an online randomisation tool and examined using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), which served as the reference standard. No prior information about canal configuration was provided for blinding of the operator. Lingual canals were identified in 36% of the teeth. Access cavity inspection was performed under three conditions: direct visual inspection (DVI), a 6× magnification DL, and a DOM (1.8×–19.4×). Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess agreement between methods. Sensitivity, confidence intervals, and statistical comparisons were reported.ResultsNone of the techniques detected all lingual canals. Sensitivity was 0.40 (95% CI: 0.22–0.61) for DVI, 0.59 (95% CI: 0.38–0.78) for DL, and 0.68 (95% CI: 0.46–0.85) for DOM. Both DOM and DL showed moderate agreement with CBCT (κ = 0.55 and κ = 0.51, respectively), with no statistically significant difference between them (p = 0.147). The success of DL (p = 0.031) and DOM (p = 0.005) in finding the lingual canal was statistically higher than DVI. The study demonstrated that magnification significantly improves lingual canal detection rates.ConclusionWhile neither tool achieved perfect accuracy, the use of magnification notably enhanced the detection of lingual canals compared to direct vision. The usage of DOM and DL may improve treatment prognosis. DLs may offer a cost-effective alternative to DOMs. However, limitations such as the ex vivo nature of the study and small sample size warrant caution. Further clinical validation is needed to support generalization to in vivo settings.

Introduction

Successful root canal therapy critically depends on the complete debridement and disinfection of the root canal system. Undetected or untreated canals can harbor residual microbial biofilms, leading to persistent periapical pathology and treatment failure [1]. A thorough understanding of root canal anatomy is one of the essential elements of successful endodontic treatment [2]. Nevertheless, comprehending the root canal system and its variations, which may be attributed to the quantity, orientation, and level of accessibility to the root canals, presents a significant challenge because of the intricate internal structure of human teeth [3]. Therefore, accurate identification of all existing canals, including anatomical variations, is essential for optimal endodontic outcomes. Missed canals may contribute to the formation of periapical lesions. In one study, apical periodontitis was found in 90% of teeth with missed canals [4]. In addition, missed canals may also cause bacterial infections to persist in the root canal system. This increases the risk of developing apical periodontitis after treatment. In one study, it was reported that missed canals were found in 12% of treated teeth and apical periodontitis was observed in 98% of these teeth [5].

Especially in mandibular premolars, missing lingual canals is one of the common causes of treatment failure [6]. Traditional methods of canal identification, including direct visual inspection, are inherently limited by the operator’s natural visual acuity, which varies across individuals [7]. However, human natural visual acuity differs among individuals, rendering this method subject to individual limitations [8]. To overcome these limitations, magnification tools such as dental loupes (DLs) and dental operating microscopes (DOMs) have become increasingly integrated into modern endodontic practice [9]. These devices enhance both illumination and visual resolution, facilitating the detection of canal orifices that may otherwise be missed. Furthermore, the application of DOMs increases the lighting of the pulp floor, assisting in the detection of additional canals [10].

Vertucci type I is the most common configuration in mandibular single-rooted teeth [11]. Other configurations identified by Vertucci were classified as teeth with two canals, regardless of their positioning within the canal system. When there are two canals, type III canals are the most prevalent classification. There was no or minimal variation in the incidence of the remaining configurations. In the Vertucci II, IV, and VI categories, canals split from the pulp chamber as two independent canals [12]. In the available research, the minimum and maximum percentages of type II, IV, and VI canal categories in the mandibular central incisors are 0.4–45%, 0.1–14.5%, 0-0.5%, 0.5–35.7%, 0.2–15.4%, 0-0.2%, and 12%, respectively, and those in canines are 0.2–16%, 0.2–13%, and 0-0.98%, respectively [11]. While mandibular single-rooted teeth, such as incisors and premolars, are typically considered to have simple anatomy, recent studies have shown that these teeth may possess additional canals—particularly lingual canals—that often go unnoticed during endodontic treatment. The incidence of such anatomical variations has been reported in varying degrees depending on population, with lingual canal presence reaching up to 21.9% for mandibular central incisors, 26.0% for lateral incisors, and 7.5% for mandibular canines [13, 14]. The overall frequency of lingual root canals in mandibular first premolars (23.6%) was substantially greater than that in mandibular second premolars (5.3%) [15].

While previous research has predominantly focused on the detection of second mesiobuccal canals (MB2) in maxillary molars [16–18], little is known about the comparative effectiveness of magnification tools for detecting lingual canals in mandibular anterior and premolar teeth. The internal anatomy of mandibular incisors, canines, and premolars is exceedingly narrow, making it difficult to detect additional root canals. Despite these findings, the clinical detection of lingual canals remains challenging due to their narrow morphology and limited accessibility. Given the potential clinical implications of missed lingual canals—ranging from persistent infection to retreatment—the development of more reliable detection methods is crucial. Moreover, there is a lack of evidence evaluating whether more expensive and sophisticated tools such as DOMs provide a clinically significant advantage over more accessible options like DLs.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare the effectiveness of DOMs and DLs in detecting lingual canals in single-rooted mandibular teeth under ex vivo conditions. The null hypothesis was that there would be no statistically significant difference between the two magnification methods in terms of detection rates.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Gaziantep University (decision number: 2023/37, decision date: 06.04.2023). All procedures involving human teeth adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all patients whose extracted teeth were used for reasons different from research.

A power analysis was performed using the G*Power 3.9.1 software based on the expected effect size (Cohen’s g = 0.20) for differences in detection rates between DOMs and dental DLs according to a recent study [18]. Using a two-tailed test, with an α value of 0.05 and power (1-β) of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 49 teeth. To ensure statistical robustness, 60 mandibular single-rooted human teeth were included in the study (20 incisors, 20 canines, and 20 premolars). Teeth were included if they had intact roots, closed apices, and no prior endodontic treatment. Teeth with root resorption, open apices, coronal restorations, fractures, or signs of calcification were excluded. All teeth were randomly allocated into 3 groups using an online randomisation tool (www.random.org). Each tooth was assigned a different number. To reduce inter-sample anatomical variation, all samples were embedded in denture wax (Perfectwax, Bilkim, İzmir, Turkey) up to the cementoenamel junction in uniformly aligned rows without knowing the numbers on them.

The prevalence of lingual canals could not be predetermined before CBCT evaluation. CBCT scans were taken using the NewTomVGi® (7G Body Plus, Verona, Italy) with parameters: 120 kVp, 12 mA, 49.92 mAs, 0.3 mm voxel size, a field of view of 17 × 17 cm, a section range of 0.3 mm, and 3.8 s exposure time. Lingual canals were evaluated in the axial plane using the NNT Viewer software (NewTom VGi® version 3.0, Verona, Italy) by a calibrated oral and maxillofacial radiologist with over 10 years of experience. The radiologist had access to all visualization tools within the software and was blinded to subsequent detection attempts via DOM or DL (Fig. 1). As a general rule, a canal was considered present if a continuous radiolucent structure suggestive of a lingual canal was observed in at least two consecutive axial slices. The presence of lingual canals, as determined by CBCT imaging, served as the reference standard for sensitivity and specificity analyses, and also for assessing the lingual canals detected with DL and DOM in the later stages of the study. To assess intra-examiner reliability, 20% of the CBCT images were randomly re-evaluated by the same radiologist after a two-week interval under identical conditions. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated to determine reproducibility. An experienced endodontist with over 10 years of clinical practice, who was blinded to the CBCT findings, performed the entire experimental protocol. The operator had more than 8 years of experience using both DOM and DL in clinical endodontic practice. The teeth were then placed in silicone impression material (Zhermack, Badia Polesine, Rome, Italy) until the cement‒enamel junction was reached to simulate clinical access conditions. To prepare access cavities, a diamond bur (KG Sorensen, São Paulo, Brazil) and Mueller burs (Brasseler, Savannah, GA, USA) were placed on a high-speed handpiece. The pulp chambers were continuously irrigated with 5.25% sodium hypochlorite.

DOM and DL magnification levels were used in the specified range throughout the canal orifice detection process, starting from the smallest level needed among the different samples and increasing. At these stages, the lingual canal was considered localized if a #8 or #10 K file could be inserted up to the working length as determined by the CBCT images of the tooth. The radiologist assessing the CBCT images and the endodontist performing the detection procedures operated independently and were blinded to each other’s findings. While only a single operator was used for the detection steps, intra-examiner reliability was evaluated by repeating 20% of cases after a two-week interval. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to assess reproducibility.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages. Cohen’s kappa coefficient was used to measure agreement between the detection methods and the CBCT reference standard. Interpretations of kappa values were made according to Landis and Koch’s classification (Table 1) [19].

Results

Intra-examiner reliability was assessed by repeating 20% of the cases after a 2-week interval. Cohen’s kappa value for intra-examiner agreement was 0.91. indicating almost perfect consistency. CBCT analysis revealed the presence of lingual canals in 22 out of the 60 extracted mandibular single-rooted teeth (36%). Distribution by tooth type was as follows: 10 incisors (5 central and 5 lateral), 7 canines, and 5 premolars. None of the three clinical procedures studied were effective in detecting lingual canals in all the samples. Without magnification, the lingual canal was located in nine teeth (40.9%). The use of DL allowed for the detection of additional lingual canals in four additional teeth (n = 13, 59.09%). The DOM further improved the detection rate by identifying the lingual canal in two more teeth (n = 15, 68.18%). ROC curve analyses demonstrated the diagnostic performance of each detection method, using CBCT findings as the reference standard. At the 95% confidence interval, the areas under the curves of the DOM, DL, and DVI were 0.841 (CI: 0.718–0.964), 0.795 (CI: 0.661–0.93), and 0.705 (CI: 0.555–0.854), respectively. These findings indicate that DOM offers superior overall diagnostic accuracy, followed by DL, with DVI performing the lowest.

The statistical analysis of paired-sample area differences under the ROC curves revealed disparities across the groups. Pairwise comparisons of the AUC values revealed statistically significant improvements in lingual canal detection with DL and DOM compared to DVI (Table 2). However, there was no significant difference between DL and DOM (p = 0.147).

Discussion

In this study, none of the techniques investigated successfully detected all lingual canals in the mandibular teeth. However. DL and DOM improved the detection of lingual canal orifices. However, the use of DL and a DOM significantly improved the detection of lingual canal orifices. These results confirmed our null hypothesis that the DOM and DL would have similar effects on detecting lingual root canals. While previous studies have demonstrated that DOM can identify additional canals compared to DL [20, 21], others, particularly those focusing on the detection of the MB2 canal, did not find any significant difference between DOM and DL [22, 23]. In line with these findings, our study did not reveal any substantial difference between the two magnification tools in terms of locating additional canals. These conflicting results could be due to variations in the operator’s experience and skills, as clinical success in detecting canal orifices is primarily dependent on the practitioner’s expertise [24]. Additionally, inconsistencies in standardizing study variables and the diverse techniques used in different studies may have contributed to the discrepancies observed. One important takeaway from this study is that the overall value of utilizing magnification should be emphasized over the choice of magnification tool. In a Swiss study, it was suggested that a Keplerian DL, such as the one used in our study, might effectively replace DOM in endodontic treatments, especially when coupled with a coaxial light source [25]. Given the cost differences—where base-level DOMs can be up to ten times more expensive than DLs—the affordability of DLs offers a compelling alternative dental practitioners. Furthermore. DOM features, such as high-resolution image capture and powerful lighting, may provide additional advantages in certain endodontic cases. The improved detection rates observed with DL and DOM may not solely be attributable to the device itself, but also to enhanced illumination.

This study specifically focused on single-rooted mandibular teeth, which are known for their narrow pulp chambers. Mandibular incisors, particularly, have the smallest pulp-to-tooth volume ratios making illumination challenging and complicating canal exploration [26]. Unlike molars, these teeth do not have pulp chamber floors or developmental grooves making canal detection even more difficult. As a result, finding the lingual canals in mandibular single-rooted teeth is more challenging compared to molars. In fact, due to their narrow structure, dentists often assume these teeth have only a single canal, which may lead to missed lingual canals in cases where additional canals are present. However, with advancements in technology, the likelihood of missing a root canal, including the lingual canal, is minimal. Adequate illumination and magnification are essential for successful exploration in these challenging cases. DOM offers superior magnification, better lighting, and increased visibility enabling endodontists to improve their diagnostic accuracy, identify morphological anomalies, and fully comprehend the pulp chamber anatomy [27]. These benefits of DOM may allow for the treatment of previously considered inoperable cases or those with uncertain outcomes [20, 21]. Interestingly, studies focused on MB2 canal detection in maxillary first molars have shown that while DOM alone may not be sufficient, combining it with ultrasonic tools and greater expertise significantly enhances additional canal detection [16, 28]. Additionally, imaging with DOM is typically limited to the straight portions of the root canal along the coronal-to-apical axis, which may pose challenges in detecting lateral or curved canals.

One study compared the effectiveness of DVI, DL, and DOM by using miniature images of the thinnest endodontic instruments (0.06 mm in diameter) inside root canals. In that study. DVI failed to detect images at all levels of the endodontic space, while Galilean DLs with coaxial light enabled detection of even such small canal orifices [29]. The effectiveness of magnification devices is highly influenced by the operator’s experience [24]. In our study, the sensitivity of each technique was evaluated using ROC curves. The sensitivity difference between DOM and DL was found to be just 4.6%, and the difference between DL and DVI was 9%. In other words, the sensitivity between DL and DVI was nearly twice as large as that between DOM and DL. Only a 9.09% increase in lingual canal detection was observed between DL and DOM usage, suggesting that while both tools enhance detection, the improvements in sensitivity are modest enough to question whether DOM should be considered essential for canal detection in single-rooted mandibular teeth.

CBCT was used as the reference standard in this study, and its effectiveness in identifying undetected canals has been well documented [30]. A previous studies have also shown that CBCT is a reliable method for identifying additional canals, especially when compared to destructive methods like dyes, micro computed tomography, or histological sectioning, which cannot be applied in clinical settings [31].

Several limitations should be noted. First, this study was conducted ex vivo, and while it provides valuable insights, it may not fully reflect in vivo conditions. For example, locating canals can be more difficult in certain tooth configurations in vivo, and limited mouth opening in some patients can further complicate the procedure. Abnormal positioning of the teeth in the jaw cannot be achieved in vitro. Another limitation is that the operator in this study may have been more motivated to identify the lingual canal than an average clinician. The detection of additional canals is not solely dependent on magnification but also on the persistence and dedication of the practitioner. Additionally, we used CBCT as the reference standard with a voxel size of 0.3 mm. Previous research has shown that decreasing voxel dimensions can enhance canal detection, especially for MB2 canals [32]. However, smaller voxel sizes may also reveal additional canals not identified with CBCT, suggesting that this imaging technique may still miss some canals [18, 33]. This situation may emerge as a result of calibration problems or differences in CBCT settings. Another challenge is that a single investigator conducted all procedures in this study, which could have introduced an operator bias. Differences in proficiency among operators using DOM and DL could affect the results. Also, only one brand of DOM and DL was evaluated, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other systems. Lastly, our study did not investigate the potential connection between buccal and lingual canals at different root levels in single-rooted mandibular teeth. Although these canals may connect, it is generally recommended to treat them as separate entities in endodontic practice.

Future research should explore whether these findings can be generalized to other tooth types, such as maxillary second premolars. Instead of using CBCT, micro computed tomography or cross-sectioning methods can be compared with magnification methods. Also, patient simulation-based devices can be used to overcome limitations of in vivo studies. In future studies, the number of samples can be increased or more operators can be added to compare between lingual canal finding methods. Prospective studies may also provide deeper insights into the effects of DL and DOM on actual endodontic treatment outcomes.

Conclusion

None of the methods tested were able to detect all lingual canals in extracted single-rooted mandibular teeth. However, both the DOM and DL significantly enhanced the detection of lingual canals compared to DVI. While DOM proved to be the most effective method for detecting lingual canals, there was no significant difference between DOM and DL. DL can effectively replace DOM for lingual canal detection in mandibular single-rooted teeth. The prognosis for endodontic treatment may be enhanced through the use of DOM and DL. These results highlight that the use of magnification tools plays a crucial role in accurately locating lingual canals in mandibular anterior teeth and premolars, emphasizing the importance of magnification in endodontic practice.
