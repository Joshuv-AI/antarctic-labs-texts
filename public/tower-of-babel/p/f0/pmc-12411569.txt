The association between playing Ma-jong, chess, and card games and cognitive function improvement in middle-aged and elderly Chinese: cross-sectional evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Yingshi Du, Gejing Liu, Jing Wang, Jinli Chen, Jinggang Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between participation in Ma-jong, chess, and card games (PMCC) and cognitive function improvement among middle-aged and elderly adults in China is not well-established. This study aims to investigate the potential association between PMCC participation and cognitive function improvement in this demographic, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).MethodsData from the 2018 wave of CHARLS were analyzed. Participants aged 45 and above who met the inclusion criteria were categorized based on their participation in PMCC activities. Cognitive function was assessed using a composite score incorporating episodic memory and mental status, alongside CESD-10 and CSI-D measures. Cross-sectional associations between PMCC engagement and cognitive function were examined using t-tests, chi-square tests, and one-way ANOVA. Group differences were assessed to evaluate the relationship between activity frequency and cognitive outcomes.ResultsA total of 19,816 participants (47.13% male; mean age 61.7 ± 10.3 years) were included in the study. The cognitive function related scores were significantly higher in the PMCC group (16.45%) compared to the non-PMCC group (83.55%) (P < 0.001). Moreover, the findings revealed significant associations between PMCC frequency and overall cognitive function scores (P < 0.001).ConclusionsPMCC activities are associated with cognitive function improvement in middle-aged and elderly adults in China. These findings suggest that engaging in PMCC activities could serve as a potential protective factor for enhancing cognitive health in this population, providing a basis for incorporating PMCC into strategies aimed at promoting cognitive well-being and healthy aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03103-7.Keywords: Playing ma-jong, chess and cards; Middle aged; Elderly adults; China health and retirement longitudinal study; Cross-sectional study; Cognitive function improvement

Introduction

The global challenge of an aging population is particularly pronounced in mainland China [1]. By 2050, the elderly population in China is projected to reach approximately 400 million individuals aged 65 and older, accounting for 26.9% of the total population, with 150 million aged 80 and above [1, 2]. This demographic shift presents considerable socioeconomic challenges, including a rise in age-related diseases such as chronic non-communicable diseases (CNCDs), cardiovascular diseases, and mental health disorders [3, 4]. Among these challenges, cognitive decline stands out as one of the most significant health concerns [5, 6].

Cognitive decline, the hallmark of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and other dementias, significantly increases the risk of functional dependence and diminishes the quality of life among the elderly [ 7 ]. Cognitive decline represents one of the most significant challenges associated with an aging population [ 8 ]. Age-related cognitive impairment encompasses deficits in memory, processing speed, language, and executive function [ 9 ]. Potential mechanisms underlying these impairments include neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and chronic cerebral hypoperfusion. Beyond aging itself, cognitive impairment in older adults is influenced by various factors, highlighting the need for a comprehensive understanding of its multifaceted nature. The World Alzheimer Report estimates that by 2050, dementia will affect 131.5 million people globally, with the highest prevalence among older adults [ 10 ]. The onset of cognitive decline typically begins in midlife but becomes more pronounced with age, leading to an increasing demand for caregiving and healthcare services [ 11 ]. The burden of cognitive impairment is not limited to individuals but extends to their families and society at large, placing a substantial strain on social and healthcare systems [ 5, 6 ]. The global cost of dementia estimated at $818 billion in 2015 and projected to exceed $1 trillion by 2018 [ 10 ].

While cognitive decline is commonly associated with older adults, growing evidence suggests that it may begin during midlife, typically defined as ages 45 to 60. Prior studies have found an increased risk of cognitive impairment in both middle-aged (45 ≤ age < 60) and elderly individuals (age ≥ 60) [12]. In the context of developing countries such as China—where aging populations coincide with lagging environmental protections and socioeconomic disparities—the burden of cognitive decline is particularly pressing. Recent literature has emphasized that midlife represents a critical window for dementia prevention, with early interventions showing long-term benefits [13, 14]. Furthermore, large-scale longitudinal evidence from Chinese populations highlights that modifiable lifestyle and social engagement factors may influence cognitive health trajectories across both middle-aged and elderly groups [15, 16]. These findings support the importance of including both age groups in cognitive aging research and justify our inclusion criteria of individuals aged 45 years and older in this study.

To mitigate or delay cognitive decline in the aging population, effective population-based strategies are imperative. Previous studies have identified various contributing factors, including socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, chronic diseases, and the strength of social networks [17–19]. Understanding and addressing these factors is critical to reducing the societal and economic impact of cognitive decline and improving the well-being of older adults. Among these, social engagement and cognitive stimulation have been shown to play crucial roles in preserving cognitive function and enhancing overall well-being in older adults.

Previous studies have showed that participation in social activities is one of the key determinants of older adults’ health outcomes [20]. A study from Korea identified a significant association between reduced participation in social activities and an increased risk of depression among older adults [21]. Furthermore, findings from Spain indicated that social disengagement is a critical risk factor for cognitive decline in community-dwelling older populations [19]. Additionally, Studies also showed that participation in social activities has beneficial effects on cognitive function among the elderly in developed countries [22]. Among these social activities, playing mahjong, chess, and cards (PMCC) are popular entertainment games among Chinese. Older adults are particularly drawn to PMCC due to its cognitive demands, which include attention control, rapid and extensive information processing, and visual-motor coordination [23]. The competitive and gambling aspects of these games further enhance their appeal [24]. Moreover, mahjong contributes to psychological well-being indirectly through its facilitation of social interaction, fostering connections that support mental health [25]. Although these games are widely practiced, there is limited research examining their direct impact on cognitive function improvement in the middle-aged and elderly population, particularly in China. While prior studies have highlighted the physical and cognitive benefits of leisure activities, such as enhanced fine motor skills and improved cognitive performance [20, 23], there is little literature that deciphers the relationship between PMCC and cognitive function among Chinese middle-aged and elderly. To address this gap, the present study utilizes data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative dataset [26], to investigate the cross-sectional associations between PMCC and cognitive function in Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults (aged 45 years and above) in 2018.

By addressing this gap, our study aims to investigate the cross-sectional association between PMCC participation and cognitive function among Chinese middle-aged and elderly adults. By using a nationally representative dataset, we aim to provide evidence on the potential cognitive benefits of PMCC activities, as well as the impact of frequency and other different variables in these associations. The results could contribute to the development of non-pharmacological interventions aimed at mitigating cognitive decline and enhancing the quality of life for the middle-aged and elderly adults in China.

Materials and methods

Overview and design of the CHARLS project

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a large-scale interdisciplinary survey jointly conducted by Wuhan University and Peking University [27]. Funded as a major project by the National Natural Science Foundation of China, CHARLS aims to collect high-quality micro-level data representative of Chinese individuals and households aged 45 and above. This data serves as a foundation for analyzing population aging in China, fostering interdisciplinary research on aging issues, and providing a scientific basis for developing and refining related policies. A pilot survey was conducted in 2008 in Zhejiang and Gansu provinces, representing typical socioeconomic contexts in eastern and western China. The national baseline survey followed in 2011, covering over 10,000 households randomly sampled from 150 counties/districts and 450 villages/communities across the country [28]. Subsequent national follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, 2020, and from 2021 to 2023. The CHARLS questionnaire design was informed by international benchmarks such as the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), and the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE). The study employed a multi-stage sampling approach, using probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling at both the county/district and village/community levels. Furthermore, CHARLS pioneered the use of the CHALRS-GIS electronic mapping software to create village-level sampling frames via map-based methods, enhancing the precision and efficiency of the sampling process [16].

Data collection methods of the CHARLS database

The CHARLS database is designed to collect and analyze information on the health, economic conditions, family structure, and retirement issues of Chinese individuals aged 45 and above, along with their spouses [26]. CHARLS employed a multi-stage, stratified probability sampling method to ensure fairness and representativeness. Approximately 17,000 households and 28,000 respondents were selected through a carefully designed four-stage sampling process that progresses from counties/districts to villages/communities, households, and finally individuals. In the county/district and village/community sampling stages, CHARLS used probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling based on population size.

In the first stage, counties/districts were selected using PPS sampling based on 2009 population statistics, stratified by region, urban-rural classification, and GDP. A total of 150 counties/districts were randomly chosen from 30 provincial-level administrative regions across mainland China (excluding Tibet, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau). In the second stage, three villages or communities were randomly selected from each of the sampled counties/districts using PPS sampling, resulting in 450 village/community units. All sampling procedures were conducted using STATA software to maintain consistency and accuracy. To minimize potential biases in population data, the 2009 resident population data for the 450 village/community units were cross-checked against 2007 data. Any discrepancies exceeding a set threshold were verified with the National Bureau of Statistics. Additionally, the selected villages or communities underwent further validation by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention to enhance the robustness of the sampling process. The CHARLS sample covers 28 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions in mainland China. It is nationally representative, encompassing diverse geographic regions, urban and rural settings, and socio-economic backgrounds among individuals aged 45 and older. All the described sampling processes were conducted using the STATA software, ensuring no substitutions were allowed for the selected samples.

Assessment of PMCC

To capture data on PMCC while minimizing response bias, the CHARLS questionnaire included questions about ten types of activities, with options for “other” and “never participate in any social activities” as additional responses. All participants were asked, “Have you done any of these activities in the last month?” Those who selected “yes” were classified as engaging in PMCC. They were further asked about the frequency of those activities over the past month. The responses were classified into three categories: not regularly, almost weekly, or almost daily. This systematic approach ensured comprehensive data collection while preserving the reliability of self-reported activity levels.

Assessment of cognitive function

Cognitive function was assessed across two dimensions [29, 30]. The first dimension, episodic memory, included immediate word recall and delayed word recall, each scored on a scale of 0–10 points. The second dimension, mental status, was evaluated using questions from the Telephone Interview of Cognitive Status (TICS) battery, designed to assess an individual’s cognitive integrity and mental state. Mental status was measured through orientation (0–5 points), visuo-construction (0–1 point), and attention (0–5 points). Orientation was assessed by asking participants to identify the current date (month, day, year, season) and the day of the week; visuo-construction was tested by having participants redraw a previously shown figure; and attention was evaluated through serial subtraction of 7 from 100, performed five times. The total cognitive function score, ranging from 0 to 31 points, was calculated by summing the scores from both dimensions, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance.

The 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10) was utilized to assess depressive symptoms, comprising 10 questions designed to evaluate the frequency of affective and behavioral symptoms experienced by individuals over the past week [31]. Responses to each item were scored on a scale from 0 (rarely or never) to 3 (usually), with higher total scores reflecting more severe depressive symptoms. The CESD-10 total score ranges from 0 to 30, and prior studies have recommended a cutoff score of 10 to dichotomize depression status into two categories: ≥ 10 showing depressive symptoms [32–34].

The CSI-D (Community Screening Instrument for Dementia), developed as part of the 10/66 studies [35, 36], comprises seven cognitive assessment items. These include tasks such as describing the use of a hammer, naming an elbow, pointing sequentially to the window and the door, identifying the nearest store, determining the current season, specifying the day of the week, and recalling three words after a delay. The total score ranges from 0 to 9, with higher scores reflecting better cognitive performance.

Covariates

This study included various covariates to account for potential confounding factors, focusing on demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, health status, depression, and chronic diseases. Demographic characteristics comprised age, gender, marital status (categorized as married/cohabitating or divorced/separated/widowed/never married), educational level (no formal education, elementary school, middle school, high school or above), and residential area (urban, rural, integration zone, and special zone). Lifestyle factors included smoking and drinking habits (classified as current/past or never) and daily sleep duration. Health status was assessed using self-rated health, categorized as good (including “very good” and “good”), fair, and poor (including “poor” and “very poor”). Health-related status was identified through responses to the question, “Have you been diagnosed by a doctor with any of the following diseases?” and included 14 conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, cancer, chronic lung diseases, and memory-related disorders. This information was verified by community healthcare providers. To address missing data in covariates, multiple imputation (MI) was performed using IBM SPSS 27.0 software. MI replaces missing values with multiple plausible estimates, leveraging relationships among variables to ensure accurate predictions and minimize bias. This method preserves data integrity and is robust against model misspecifications [37, 38].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Independent-sample t-tests and chi-square tests were employed to compare demographic, lifestyle, and health-related variables between participants with and without PMCC activity. In addition, t-tests were also used to assess differences in cognitive function scores, including CESD-10, CSI-D, and total cognition score, between PMCC participants and non-participants. chi-square tests were applied where appropriate to examine the distribution of PMCC activity frequency across subgroups defined by demographic and health-related characteristics (e.g., age, gender, education level, and residential area). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to examine differences in these cognitive outcomes across three categories of PMCC participation frequency (not regularly, almost weekly, and almost daily). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27.

Results

Demographic and health characteristics of PMCC vs. non-engaged participants

As shown in Table S1, a total of 19,816 participants from 28 provinces in China were included in the analysis, providing a representative sample for exploring the potential effects of activities such as playing mahjong on cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly. The mean age of participants was 61.7 years old (SD = 10.3), with 47.13% of the sample being male. The majority of participants resided in rural areas (71.04%) and had an elementary (42.80%) or lower level of education (22.69% illiterate). Of particular interest is the frequency of engagement in activities such as PMCC, Among the participants, 16.45% reported engaging in these activities in the past month. Regarding cognitive function, the participants had a mean CESD-10 score of 9.58 (SD = 5.498). The average CSI-D score was 5.36 (SD = 1.670). Additionally, the mean Cognition score was 17.19 (SD = 5.281).

Relationship between PMCC frequency and demographic as well as health-related factors

A cross-sectional analysis was conducted to examine the distribution of PMCC activity frequency (categorized as almost daily, almost weekly, or not regularly) across various demographic and health-related subgroups. Frequency tables were used to calculate the proportion of individuals in each PMCC category within each subgroup (such as, by age, gender, education, and residential area). Chi-square tests were used to assess the statistical significance of these associations. As shown in Fig. 1, the analysis demonstrated a significant relationship between PMCC activity frequency and age, with older participants being more likely to engage in activities almost daily (P < 0.001). Gender also showed a significant association (P = 0.006), where male participants were more likely to report frequent PMCC engagement compared to females. Additionally, the frequency of PMCC activities was significantly associated with the educational level of participants (P < 0.001). In terms of residential area (P = 0.003), rural residents engaged in these activities more frequently, suggesting a potential geographical disparity in the frequency of PMCC participation.

Regarding depressive symptoms, as measured by the CESD-10 scale, participants who engaged in PMCC activities had significantly lower depressive symptoms (P < 0.001, as shown in Table 2). However, as shown in Fig. 2, no significant association was found between PMCC engagement and the presence of depression symptoms (P = 0.804), suggesting that the frequency of participation in PMCC activities did not directly influence the severity of depressive symptoms. Similarly, there was no significant relationship between PMCC activity frequency and the presence of memory-related diseases (P = 0.584), indicating that participation in PMCC activities did not show any clear impact on the diagnosis of memory-related conditions.

Association between PMCC frequency and cognitive function scores

To evaluate the association between PMCC participation frequency and cognitive outcomes, we conducted one-way ANOVA analyses among participants who reported engaging in PMCC activities. This within-group analysis allowed us to examine whether cognitive function scores, CESD-10 scores, and CSI-D scores varied significantly across different frequencies of participation (not regularly, almost weekly, almost daily), as presented in Table 3. The broader comparison between PMCC participants and non-participants was conducted earlier in the analysis (see Table 1). The results in Table 3 revealed significant associations between PMCC frequency and overall cognitive function (P < 0.001). Specifically, five cognitive function measures demonstrated significant differences: Immediate word recall (P < 0.001), Delayed word recall (P < 0.001), Orientation (P < 0.001), Drawing (P = 0.003), Attention (P = 0.438). Attention score did not show a statistically significant difference across the three PMCC frequency groups. Similarly, no significant differences were found between PMCC engagement frequency and CESD-10 scores (P = 0.819) or CSI-D scores (P = 0.571). These findings suggest that while cognitive performance, particularly in recall and orientation tasks, is associated with PMCC activity frequency, no similar pattern was observed with depressive symptoms or memory-related disorders.

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the potential benefits of PMCC participation for improving cognitive function among Chinese middle-aged and elderly. Our results suggest a significant association between engagement in PMCC activities and better cognitive outcomes. This aligns with existing research that emphasizes the positive effects of cognitive and social activities on mitigating cognitive decline and promoting healthy aging [20, 39, 40]. Specifically, PMCC activities, which combine cognitive stimulation and social interaction, appear to offer dual benefits for mental well-being and cognitive health in older adults.

The integration of mental and physical components in PMCC games is likely a key factor in their cognitive benefits. These activities require participants to engage in strategic thinking, problem-solving, and memory tasks, all of which contribute to maintaining cognitive reserve [41]. Cognitive reserve is critical in buffering against the onset of cognitive decline, and the mental stimulation provided by PMCC could play a vital role in delaying cognitive impairment. Moreover, the social aspect of PMCC, which often involves interactions with peers and friends, may help reduce feelings of isolation and stress, both of which are known to negatively affect cognitive health in aging populations [42]. By fostering emotional well-being and creating a sense of community, PMCC may mitigate some of the psychosocial factors that contribute to cognitive deterioration.

Our results also highlight the importance of frequency and engagement in these activities. While participation in PMCC was generally associated with improved cognitive outcomes, the frequency of engagement may be a crucial factor in maximizing its benefits. Further research is needed to explore how varying levels of PMCC participation—daily, weekly, or irregular—affect cognitive function and whether a threshold exists beyond which additional participation does not yield further cognitive benefits.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, while we adjusted for various covariates such as age, education, and health status, residual confounding may still exist. Factors such as overall physical activity levels and dietary habits, which could influence both PMCC engagement and cognitive function, were not comprehensively controlled for in this study. Additionally, some of the data relied on self-reporting, which may introduce recall bias. Participants may have overestimated or underestimated their frequency of engagement in PMCC activities, or may have inaccurately reported their cognitive function. The lack of detailed information on the specific nature, duration, and intensity of PMCC activities also limits the ability to draw firm conclusions about the mechanisms behind the observed cognitive benefits. It is possible that different types of PMCC activities, or their duration and intensity, could have varying impacts on cognitive health. Lastly, while our findings suggest an association between PMCC participation and improved cognitive function, causality cannot be definitively established from this observational study. Experimental studies, including randomized controlled trials, are needed to further investigate the causal relationship and explore the underlying mechanisms of this association.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that PMCC participation is associated with better cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults. This suggests that incorporating PMCC activities into regular routines could serve as an effective strategy for promoting cognitive health and preventing cognitive decline in aging populations. Given the rapid aging of China’s population, further research exploring the optimal frequency and types of PMCC activities, as well as their long-term impact on cognitive function, will be crucial in developing evidence-based interventions for healthy aging. Additionally, understanding the mechanisms through which PMCC may protect cognitive function will be important in refining these interventions and optimizing their effectiveness.
