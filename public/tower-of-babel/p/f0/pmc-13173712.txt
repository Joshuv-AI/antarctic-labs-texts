Paxillin mediates ARP2-dependent actin assembly for spindle migration in mammalian oocyte meiosis
Authors: Yue Wang, Zi-Jian Wu, Wen-Lin Pan, Jing-Cai Liu, Jia-Qian Ju, Zhen-Nan Pan, Xin Zhao, Caizhu Wang, Shao-Chen Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paxillin is a multi-domain scaffold protein that is involved in several cellular functions, including focal adhesion, cell motility and cell aging. Previous studies have shown that Paxillin affects the resumption of meiosis in Xenopus oocytes. In the present study, we report the distinct roles of Paxillin in mammalian oocytes including mice and pigs. Our results indicate that Paxillin is expressed in mouse oocytes and is primarily concentrated in the cortical area, which is closely associated with actin. Disruption of Paxillin expression or function did not influence the process of germinal vesicle breakdown in either mouse or porcine oocytes at GV stage, as confirmed by the expression levels of Mos and MPF. Furthermore, Paxillin disruption did not alter spindle morphology but did affect meiotic spindle migration and polar body extrusion, likely due to a decrease in actin filaments. Further analysis revealed that the actin nucleators Arp2/3 and N-WASP were reduced in Paxillin-knockdown oocytes at MI stage. Overexpression of Paxillin also did not impact GV breakdown, spindle formation, or polar body extrusion. However, the supplementation of exogenous Paxillin mRNA rescued the defects in spindle positioning and polar body extrusion caused by Paxillin knockdown. Taken together, our findings suggest that Paxillin plays a critical role in spindle migration during oocyte meiosis by mediating actin dynamics through the Arp2/3 complex, highlighting functional differences between species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00768-6.

Introduction

Mammalian oocytes undergo two rounds of meiosis before forming mature gametes. The first division begins with germinal vesicle breakdown and concludes at metaphase II, resulting in a fertilizable egg; the second division is completed upon fertilization [1]. These two meiotic divisions are highly asymmetric, producing a large egg and small polar bodies to ensure the retention of sufficient cytoplasmic resources, including maternal RNAs, proteins, and other molecules necessary to support early embryo development up to implantation [2]. During oocyte maturation, actin assumes several critical functions to create a highly specialized cytoskeleton that facilitating asymmetric divisions, it is commonly involved in spindle migration, spindle anchorage to the cortex, and spindle orientation [3, 4]. Furthermore, actin usually requires “nuclei”-preformed actin filaments to start polymerizing; once initiated, polymerization continues constitutively [5]. During actin nucleation, actin nucleation factors, such as the actin-related protein 2/3 (Arp2/3) complex and formins, are considered the most physiologically important [6]. The Arp2/3 complex is the first major actin nucleator to be identified. This complex consists of Arp2, Arp3, and five additional supporting subunits [7]. The activation of the Arp2/3 complex is regulated by the Neural Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome Protein (N-WASP) [8, 9]. The N-WASP-activated Arp2/3 complex can nucleate new actin filaments, thereby enhancing actin dynamics, which is particularly crucial during oocyte meiosis.

Paxillin is a multi-domain scaffold protein that plays a role in various cellular functions, including cytoskeletal rearrangements and cell motility; additionally, Paxillin has been found to be involved in cellular aging [10]. Paxillin contains five repetitive leucine-rich LD motifs (Leu-Asp) at its N-terminus (LD1 to LD5) and features four cysteine-histidine-enriched LIM domains at its C-terminus; these structural elements enable Paxillin to interact with various structural proteins, including vinculin, actin, and integrins [11, 12]. The function and localization of Paxillin are primarily regulated by phosphorylation. While Tyr31 and Tyr118 are the main phosphorylation sites, Paxillin contains multiple phosphorylation sites that enable it to perform various functions in cells depending on the specific sites that are phosphorylated [13].

Paxillin was first identified as one of the earliest members of the “focal adhesion proteome” [14]. Paxillin interacts with focal adhesion kinase (FAK), and impaired FAK-Paxillin signaling leads to increased cell migration [15]. The knockout of Paxillin in mouse embryos leads to embryonic death, accompanied by defects in the amnion and allantois defects, indicating Paxillin is essential for early embryonic development and underscores its significant role in cell migration [16]. Recent studies indicate that Paxillin plays a significant role in cell migration. The integrin activator kindlin-2 is associated with Paxillin by interacting with the LIM domain of Paxillin, and the localization and recruitment of Paxillin are kindlin-2 dependent [17]. By mutating the phosphorylation site of Paxillin in CHO-K1 cells, it has been demonstrated that the phosphorylation of Paxillin at S273 is a crucial regulator of cell migration, facilitating the recruitment of βPIX and PAK1 to adhesion [18].

Paxillin is also recognized for its role in actin dynamics. In human breast cancer cells, Paxillin is phosphorylated by 17-β estradiol, which subsequently recruits the small GTPase Cdc42, which triggers the phosphorylation of the Neural Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome Protein (N-WASP), leading to the redistribution of the Arp2/3 complex at sites associated with membrane structures involved in cell movement [19]. Another study indicates that Paxillin depletion disrupts the Cdc42/N-WASP/Arp2/3 complex and further impairs F-actin interactions with other cytoskeletal components in mouse embryonic stem cells, also indicating the role of Paxillin in regulating actin dynamics through the Arp2/3 complex. Additionally, research on gastric smooth muscle cells has shown that Paxillin is involved in Arp2/3 activation, with evidence suggesting that ILK-dependent Paxillin phosphorylation is mediated by PI3 kinase [20]. Furthermore, a previous study demonstrates that the depletion of Paxillin in mouse embryonic fibroblasts results in aberrant spindle orientation [21]. Since the Arp2/3 complex is also implicated in the regulation of spindle orientation [22], this result further underscores the interaction between Paxillin and the Arp2/3 complex. Moreover, Paxillin has been reported to mediate meiotic resumption in Xenopus oocytes. Following ERK2 activation, Paxillin is phosphorylated, which subsequently enhances Mos protein expression, ultimately leading to MPF activation and germinal vesicle breakdown [23]. However, it is important to note that mammalian oocyte meiosis differs from that of Xenopus oocytes, and the role of Paxillin in mammalian meiosis remains unclear.

In our study, we utilized mouse and porcine oocytes as models to investigate the role of Paxillin in mammalian oocytes. Our findings indicated that Paxillin did not influence the resumption of meiosis; however, it did cause defects in polar body extrusion. We demonstrated that Paxillin regulates spindle migration by mediating actin dynamics through the Arp2/3 complex, which is essential for the maturation of mammalian oocytes.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and chemicals

All chemicals used in this study were purchased from Sigma Chemical Company (St. Louis, MO), unless otherwise indicated. Both the M2 medium (M7176) and the M16 medium (M7292) were purchased from Sigma Aldrich Rabbit anti-Paxillin polyclonal antibody (10029-1-lg), rabbit Myc-tag polyclonal antibody (60003-2-lg) and rabbit anti-β-actin polyclonal antibody (20536-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). Rabbit anti-N-WASP monoclonal antibody (ab126626), rabbit anti-Arp2 polyclonal antibody (ab47654), rabbit anti-p-CDK1 monoclonal antibody (ab201008), rabbit anti-CDK1 monoclonal antibody (ab32094), rabbit anti-Cyclin B1 monoclonal antibody (ab181593) and rabbit anti-Mos monoclonal antibody (ab171937) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Mouse anti-α-tubulin-FITC monoclonal antibody (F2168) and Hoechst 33342 were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Corp (St. Louis, MO). Rabbit anti-Gapdh monoclonal antibody (#2118) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Devers, MA, USA). Phalloidin-Alexa Fluor 594 (A12381) used for F-actin staining was purchased from Invitrogen (California, USA).

Oocyte collection and culture

All experiments with mice were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University and were performed according to Animal Research Institute Committee guidelines (NJAU.No202207041845). Fully-grown unmatured oocytes at GV stage were acquired from female ICR mice of 4–6 weeks old. Mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, then removed the ovaries and crushed them into a paste. Under a stereomicroscope, GV oocytes were selected using a pipette, which were uniform in size and contained a distinct germinal vesicle located in the center of GV oocytes. Oocytes at GV stage were collected and washed three times in M2 medium, for the period expression experiment, oocytes at GV stage were directly cultured in M16 medium under paraffin oil at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. As for the other experiments, oocytes were arrested at GV stage by adding IBMX. For the release of IBMX, GV oocytes were washed in M2 medium for three times. The GV oocytes were cultured for 0, 4, 8, and 12 h after release from IBMX, corresponding to GV, GVBD, MI, and MII stages, respectively.

Porcine ovaries were collected from a local abattoir (Nanjing, China) and were transported to the laboratory in sterile physiological saline maintained at 37 ℃ within 2 h. Cumulus oocyte complexes (COCs) were aspirated from antral follicles, and GV oocytes with homogeneous ooplasm and more than three layers of compact cumulus cells were selected and used in the experiments. For in vitro maturation (IVM), approximately 30 COCs in each group were transferred into one well of a four-well dish containing 500 mL of pre-equilibrated IVM medium and were cultured for 26 h (MI) or 44 h (MII) at 38.5 ℃ with 5% CO2. The porcine denuded oocytes (Dos) were prepared by removing the cumulus cells from the COCs using 0.1% hyaluronidase treatment at 38.5 ℃ for 5 min and repeated pipetting with a micropipette. The IVM medium was composed of TCM-199 medium supplemented with 0.91 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.57 mM L-cysteine, 10 ng/mL epidermal growth factor, 10 IU/mL PMSG and hCG, 7.5 mg/mL penicillin, 5.0 mg/mL streptomycin, 0.1% polyvinyl alcohol and 10% porcine follicular fluid.

Microinjection of Paxillin siRNA or control siRNA

Paxillin siRNA and control siRNA were separately microinjected into the cytoplasm of oocytes at GV stage for Paxillin depletion. Both Paxillin siRNA (5’-GCU GCU UCG UGA AGC UCU UTT-3’) and control siRNA (5’-UUC UCC GAA CGU GUC ACG UTT-3’) (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) were diluted with water to give a concentration of 40 µM, and then 5–10 picoliters were injected into GV oocytes using an Eppendorf FemtoJet (Eppendorf AG) with an inverted microscope (Olympus IX53, Japan). After injection, the oocytes were inhibited at GV stage using IBMX for 24 h in M16 media. Then, GV oocytes were washed three times in fresh M2 media, and subsequently transferred to M16 media and cultured under paraffin oil at 37◦C in 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Microinjection of Paxillin antibody

Paxillin antibody microinjection was used to functional block Paxillin in porcine oocytes. The Paxillin-antibody was purified via Pierce Antibody Clean-up Kit (Thermo Fisher). The porcine denuded oocytes (Dos) were first prepared by removing the cumulus cells from the COCs using 0.1% hyaluronidase treatment at 38.5 ℃ for 5 min and repeated pipetting with a micropipette. Then, 10–20 pl of a Paxillin antibody was microinjected in porcine oocytes at GV stage and the same volume of water was injected as a control. After injection, the porcine GV oocytes were transferred into one well of a four-well dish containing 500 mL of pre-equilibrated IVM medium and were cultured for 26 h (MI) or 44 h (MII) at 38.5 ℃ with 5% CO2.

Paxillin expression plasmid construction and Paxillin overexpression

Plasmid construction was performed as described previously [24]. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from 150 mouse GV oocytes with the RNeasy micro purification kit (Thermo Fisher) and the first-strand cDNA was performed by the PrimeScript 1st strand cDNA synthesis kit (Takara). Next, PCR was used to amplify the full-length specific cDNA for Paxillin (Paxillin amplification primers: ggcgccgaggaccagcca and taggccggtgccctatag), and PCR products were digested using KasI and EcoRV (New England Biolabs) and linked into a Myc-tag plasmid. The fusion plasmid was transfected into T1 competent cells (Transgene Biotech). Paxillin mRNA was synthesized from linearized plasmid using the HiS-cribe T7 high yield RNA synthesis kit (New England Biolabs) and capped with m7G(5′)ppp(5′)G (New England Biolabs), tailed with a poly(A) polymerase tailing kit (Epicentre). Finally, Paxillin mRNA was purified with the RNA clean & concentrator-25 kit (Zymo Research). For Paxillin overexpression, a concentration of 600 ng/µl was microinjected into GV oocytes. After injection, the oocytes were inhibited at GV stage for 2 h in M16 media containing IBMX to allow overexpression of Paxillin. Then, GV oocytes were washed three times in fresh M2 media, and subsequently transferred to M16 media and cultured under paraffin oil at 37◦C in 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Immunofluorescence microscopy

Immnuofluorescence staining method was based on our previous studies [25]. At least 30 oocytes in each experiment were fixed at room temperature with 4% paraformaldehyde added to phosphate buffered saline (PBS) for 30 min, then permeabilized oocytes with 0.5% Triton X-100-supplemented PBS for 20 min. After that, oocytes were blocked in 1% BSA-supplemented PBS for 1 h at room temperature. For F-actin staining, oocytes were blocked and directly stained with Phalloidin (diluted with PBS) at 4℃ overnight. For other experiments oocytes were incubated with specific primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. After three times washing, oocytes were stained with the appropriate secondary antibody (1:500) at room temperature for 1 h. Hoechst 33342 (1:10000) was next used for chromosome staining. Finally, oocytes were mounted on glass slides and examined with a laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 700 META; Jena, Germany).

Western blot analysis

A total of 150 oocytes at different stages were lysed in Laemmli sample buffer (SDS sample buffer with 2-mercaptoethanol) and boiled at 100 °C for 10 min, then subjected samples to SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). SDS-PAGE was used for protein separation for 90 polymerase. After that, proteins were transferred onto a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and the PVDF membrane was next blocked with Tris-buffered saline (TBS) which contained 0.1% (w/w) Tween 20 (TBST) and 5% nonfat dry milk at room temperature for at least 1 h. The PVDF membrane was briefly washed and incubated with specific antibody at 4 °C overnight. After washing three times in TBST, membranes were then incubated at 37℃ for 1 h with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:2000). Finally, the PVDF membrane was exposed to an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (EMD Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and protein bands were visualized by Tanon-3900. To quantify the results of Western blot, the relative intensity values were analyzed using Image J software. And for each experiment, the intensity of the target band is divided by the intensity of the internal reference band to obtain the ratio, which is the relative intensity value.

Statistical analysis and relative fluorescence intensity measure

For Western blot, each specific protein band were circled and their intensity were measured, three times replicates were made for each band. For the cortical and cytoplasmic actin measurement, we separately select the cortical area or the cytoplasm regions respectively to measure the fluorescence intensity, and then compared the fluorescence intensity of the cortical region in the experimental group with that in the control group to show the changes in the relative fluorescence intensity of the actin in the cortical area. Meanwhile, the fluorescence intensity of the cytoplasmic region in the Paxillin-KD group was also compared with that in the control group to show the changes in the fluorescence intensity of the actin in the cytoplasmic region (Figure S1).

For each treatment, at least three independent biological replicates were performed and no less than 30 MI oocytes were examined. The results were expressed as means ± SEMs. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 5.0, GraphPad Prism software Inc., San Diego, CA). Fluorescence intensity was analyzed by Image J software. For the measurement of spots profiles, use Image J software to draw a straight line along the diameter of the oocyte on the entire image. On this line, a fluorescence value will be obtained at a distance of 0.003. The fluorescence values on the entire line are then analyzed as line graphs to represent the changes in the relative fluorescence intensity at different positions of the oocyte. Normality and homogeneity of variance of the data were examined using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. Due to the non-normal distribution of our data, statistical comparisons were made by Mann-Whitney U test. The significance was determined as p value < 0.05.

Results

Subcellular distribution and expression of Paxillin during oocyte maturation

We first examined the expression and localization of Paxillin during mouse oocyte meiosis. GV oocytes were collected and cultured for 0, 4, 8, and 12 h after release from IBMX (3-Isobutyl-1-methylxanthine), corresponding to the stages of germinal vesicle (GV), germinal vesicle breakdown (GVBD), metaphase I (MI), and metaphase II (MII), respectively. Immunoblot analysis demonstrated that Paxillin was stably expressed from the GV to MII stages (Fig. 1A). The subcellular localization of Paxillin was then assessed using immunofluorescent staining. As shown in Fig. 1B, Paxillin was primarily distributed in the cytoplasm, with marked enrichment in the oocyte cortex throughout meiotic maturation. To determine whether Paxillin colocalized with actin, we subsequently stained both Paxillin and actin together. As illustrated in Fig. 1C, Paxillin accumulated beneath the cortex actin region in oocytes during the mouse oocyte maturation.

Paxillin depletion had no influence on GV breakdown in mouse and porcine oocytes

To investigate the role of Paxillin during the maturation of mammalian oocytes, we employed a knockdown approach using siRNA microinjection. Western blot analysis revealed that the injection of Paxillin-targeting siRNA significantly reduced the protein level of Paxillin in mouse oocytes at GV stage (0.72 ± 0.04 vs. 0.40 ± 0.03, P = 0.007 < 0.01) (Fig. 2A), indicating effective knockdown of Paxillin. The mouse oocytes at GV stage were injected and cultured for 4 h to assess whether Paxillin influences meiotic resumption in mammals. The results for the GVBD rate showed no significant change compared to control oocytes (83.73 ± 6.75%, n = 111 vs. 81.56 ± 2.54%, n = 98, P = 0.69 > 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 2B and C), suggesting that Paxillin does not affect meiotic resumption in mouse oocytes, which contrasts with its function in Xenopus oocytes. Given that both CDK1 and cyclin B1 are critical components of MPF and play essential roles in meiotic resumption, and considering that Paxillin has been reported to regulate Mos expression in Xenopus oocytes, we examined the changes in these proteins to further elucidate our findings. Our results indicated that p-CDK1, cyclin B1, and MOS exhibited no significant changes compared to control oocytes (1.06 ± 0.07 vs. 1.09 ± 0.06, P = 0.80 > 0.05 for p-CDK1; 0.87 ± 0.06 vs. 0.87 ± 0.05, P = 0.98 > 0.05 for CDK1; 0.35 ± 0.04 vs. 0.38 ± 0.05, P = 0.72 > 0.05 for cyclin B1; 1.23 ± 0.05 vs. 1.18 ± 0.07, P = 0.537 > 0.05 for MOS) (Fig. 2D, E and F). In addition, we also detected the phosphorylation degree of Histone H3, which was the direct substrate of MPF, and the phosphorylation level of Histone H3 (p-Histone H3) remained unchanged following Paxillin knockdown ( 0.47 ± 0.06 vs. 0.44 ± 0.06, P = 0.75 > 0.05) (Fig. 2G). These findings further suggested a distinct function of Paxillin in mouse oocytes compared to its role in Xenopus oocytes. Subsequently, we performed antibody injection in porcine GV oocytes to inhibit Paxillin and to reconfirm its role in meiotic resumption in mammalian oocytes. To validate the effectiveness of the antibody for Paxillin inhibition, we first examined it in mouse oocytes, with the results presented in Fig. 2H. Paxillin antibody injection in mice resulted in a decreased polar body extrusion rate (81.89 ± 2.25%, n = 91 vs. 54.80 ± 4.12%, n = 93, P = 0.028 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) and an increased rate of large polar body extrusion (16.63 ± 5.81%, n = 91 vs. 35.42 ± 3.18%, n = 93, P = 0.047 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments), a polar body of oocyte is identified as a large polar body when its diameter accounts for more than 1/3 of the oocyte, which closely resembled the results observed in Paxillin-depleted oocytes, thereby confirming the effectiveness of the antibody injection. We determined the stages of porcine oocytes based on the morphology of the nuclei and the appearance of the germinal vesicle (Fig. 2I). The results indicated that the GVBD rate of Paxillin-inhibited oocytes did not differ significantly from that of control oocytes (52.10 ± 3.27%, n = 81 vs. 52.57 ± 12.93%, n = 80, P = 0.95 > 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments). In conjunction with the results from the knockdown of Paxillin in mouse oocytes and Paxillin inhibition in porcine oocytes, we concluded that Paxillin did not required for meiotic resumption in mammalian oocytes.

Paxillin mediates polar body extrusion by regulating spindle migration

We subsequently cultured the Paxillin-siRNA injected mouse oocytes at GV stage for 14 h to further investigate the role of Paxillin in mammalian oocytes. The results indicated that polar body extrusion was significantly impaired following Paxillin knockdown when compared to control mouse oocytes, with a notable absence of polar bodies (control: 78.33 ± 3.30%, n = 138 vs. Knockdown: 47.33 ± 6.55%, n = 149, P = 0.039 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments), or the presence of large polar bodies (4.85 ± 1.03%, n = 138 vs. 25.64 ± 1.64%, n = 149, P = 0.007 < 0.01,n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 3A and B). Additionally, we found that antibody injection impaired the first polar body extrusion in porcine oocytes (53.91 ± 3.40%, n = 96 vs. 29.40 ± 4.51%, n = 93, P = 0.014 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 3C), which was consistent with our findings in mouse oocytes.

Since asymmetric division is associated with spindle migration, we examined the spindle structure and location following Paxillin depletion. For the spindle structure analysis, the spindle with distinct bipolarity and a fusiform shape was regarded as normal spindle structure in mice. The injected mouse oocytes at GV stage were cultured for 8 h, and the spindle structures in mouse oocytes at MI stage were analyzed using a confocal microscope (Fig. 3D). No significant differences in spindle structure were observed between control mouse oocytes and Paxillin-depleted oocytes (82.86 ± 3.17%, n = 124 vs. 82.53 ± 6.96%, n = 120, P = 0.93 > 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 3E). Subsequently, we assessed the status of spindle migration. As shown in Fig. 3F, the white line represented the oocyte diameter, we compared the distance from the chromosome to the diameter of the oocytes to observe the approximate position of the spindle. We set the vertical distance from the center of the spindle metaphase plate to the nearest oocyte cortex as D, and the radius of the oocyte as R. A D/R ratio of ≤ 0.25 is regarded as normal; a D/R ratio in the range of 0.25 to 0.5 is defined as an intermediate state; and a D/R ratio of ≥ 0.5 is identified as abnormal. We analyzed the percentage of abnormal spindle distribution, and found the abnormal spindle location rate was obviously higher in Paxillin-depleted group compared with that of control group (18.77 ± 4.25%, n = 111 vs. 45.79 ± 10.03%, n = 103, P = 0.024 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 3G). We measured the distance from the spindle to the cortex of the mouse oocyte at MI stage. We set the longer distance from one pole of the spindle to the cortex as L and the shorter distance from the other pole of the spindle to the cortex as D (Fig. 3H). The ratio of D/L in mouse oocytes was significantly higher after Paxillin depletion than that of control mouse oocytes (0.38 ± 0.03, n = 111 vs. 0.61 ± 0.08, n = 103, P = 0.002 < 0.01, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments). These results indicated that Paxillin played a critical role in spindle migration during mouse oocyte maturation.

Paxillin regulates actin dynamics in mouse oocytes

Since spindle migration is primarily regulated by actin, we next investigated the changes in actin levels in mouse oocytes at MI stage following Paxillin depletion. Injected GV mouse oocytes were cultured for 8 h, and F-actin staining using Phalloidin was employed to assess the actin levels in the mouse oocytes at MI stage, as illustrated in Fig. 4A. Further analysis of the relative intensity revealed that the actin level in the cortical area decreased after Paxillin depletion (83.38 ± 2.02, n = 101 vs. 67.09 ± 3.03, n = 97, P = 0.032 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the results indicated that the level of cytoplasmic actin in Paxillin-depleted mouse oocytes was significantly lower than that in control oocytes (23.77 ± 0.43, n = 101 vs. 18.25 ± 1.87, n = 97, P = 0.015 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 4C).

We next examined the mechanisms by which Paxillin regulates actin dynamics during mouse oocyte meiosis. Given that the level of actin in oocytes is highly dependent on actin assembly, we assessed the localization and protein levels of Arp2, a crucial component of the Arp2/3 complex. Initially, we investigated whether Paxillin colocalized with Arp2 in mouse oocytes and found that Paxillin accumulated in the cortical region next to Arp2 in both GV and MI stages (Fig. 4D and E). Mouse oocytes at the MI stage were subsequently collected for Western blot analysis, and densitometric analysis revealed that Arp2 expression was significantly reduced following Paxillin-siRNA injection. Additionally, we examined the protein level of N-WASP, which plays a critical role in Arp2/3 activation. The results indicated that the level of N-WASP in Paxillin-depleted mouse oocytes was considerably lower than that in the control group. Relative intensity analysis of the Western blot results also demonstrated that both Arp2 and N-WASP levels were diminished after Paxillin depletion (0.82 ± 0.07 vs. 0.56 ± 0.06 for Arp2, P = 0.040 < 0.05; 1.21 ± 0.12 vs. 0.66 ± 0.09 for N-WASP, P = 0.009 < 0.001) (Fig. 4F). Therefore, these findings suggest that Paxillin modulates actin dynamics for spindle migration in mouse oocytes, which was supported by the alteration of Arp2 and N-WASP expression.

Rescue and overexpression of Paxillin on mouse oocyte meiosis

To further verify that the failure of polar body extrusion following Paxillin-siRNA injection was specifically induced by the knockdown of Paxillin, exogenous Paxillin mRNA was utilized for supplementation. Mouse oocytes at GV stage for both Paxillin-KD group and rescue group were initially injected with Paxillin-siRNA and maintained at the GV stage for 24 h. Subsequently, Paxillin mRNA was injected for the mouse oocytes of rescue group, while an equal volume of water was injected into Paxillin-depleted oocytes as a control. The treated mouse oocytes were further cultured for extra 2 h to allow Paxillin expression. We first analyzed the polar body extrusion rates of both Paxillin-depleted and Paxillin-supplemented mouse oocytes. The results indicated that the rate of polar body extrusion was significantly higher after Paxillin mRNA supplementation compared to the Paxillin-depleted group (49.67 ± 4.87%, n = 113 vs. 66.67 ± 4.50%, n = 102, P = 0.043 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments). Additionally, a decreased rate of large polar body extrusion was observed in mRNA-supplemented mouse oocytes compared to control oocytes (18.07 ± 2.05%, n = 118 vs. 11.04 ± 1.36%, n = 95, P = 0.034 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 5A). We also stained the DNA to visualize the metaphase I plates, which indicate the spindle position in mouse oocytes. The typical image of the spindle position is shown in Fig. 5B, which was reflected by the relative distance from the chromosome to the oocyte cortex (marked by the white line). By further quantifying the rates of abnormal spindle positions in these two groups of mouse oocytes at MI stage, we confirmed the central localization of the spindle in different groups. The injection of Paxillin mRNA into Paxillin-depleted mouse oocytes significantly reduced the rate of abnormal spindle positions (55.17 ± 8.31%, n = 120 vs. 34.25 ± 5.97%, n = 104, P = 0.013 < 0.05, n stands for the number of oocytes used in the experiments) (Fig. 5C). Thus, we demonstrated that the failure of polar body extrusion is attributable to the specific knockdown of Paxillin.

Discussion

In the present study, we investigated the role of Paxillin in mammalian oocyte maturation. We hypothesized Paxillin exerts species-specific functions in mouse and porcine oocytes compared to Xenopus oocytes. Our results showed Paxillin had no effect on GVBD during mammalian oocyte meiosis; instead, it regulated actin via the N-WASP-Arp2/3 signaling pathway, disrupting spindle migration and ultimately causing polar body extrusion failure (Fig. 6).

Our results demonstrated that Paxillin was primarily localized in the cortical region of oocytes, which is similar to the distribution patterns of actin in oocytes [27]. The localization of Paxillin suggests that its role in mouse oocytes may be associated with actin dynamics. Then, The siRNA injected was used for Paxillin knockdown; however, the observed reduction in Paxillin levels was not substantial, indicating that the Paxillin protein is relatively stable. This finding aligns with a previous study showing that Paxillin knockdown in Xenopus oocytes induced GV arrest by inhibiting MOS expression [23], we first analyzed the role of Paxillin during the resumption of meiosis in mammals. Our results indicated that both Paxillin knockdown and Paxillin inhibition had no effect on the process of GVBD in mouse and porcine oocytes. We speculate that the causes of these differences may include the following aspects. The cortical actin filaments structure of Xenopus oocytes is denser and forms a regular network compared to that of mammalian oocytes. And the role of actin in exocytosis of cortical granules to prevent polyspermia is also different in Xenopus, mouse and pig oocytes [28]. The functional and structural differences of actin among different species might be one of the important reasons for the different effects of Paxillin on meiosis among different species. Furthermore, we found that Paxillin knockdown led to failed polar body extrusion and impaired asymmetric division in mouse oocytes. This finding is consistent with a previous study that demonstrated Paxillin depletion resulted in failed mitosis in HeLa cells [21]. In human smooth muscle cells, the number of mitotic cells exhibiting monopolar or small bipolar spindles increased in Paxillin-depleted cells, thereby causing a delay in anaphase entry [29]. Our results suggested the distinct roles of Paxillin in Xenopus oocytes compared to mammalian oocytes.

Next, we sought to investigate the reasons behind polar body extrusion defects following Paxillin knockdown. A previous study demonstrated that Paxillin can modulate the formation of actin stress fibers in human coronary artery smooth muscle cells [30]. Furthermore, disrupting the Paxillin gene during early embryonic development proved lethal in mice, as it induced defects in the actin cytoskeleton and impaired cell migration [16]. This finding aligns closely with our results, suggesting that Paxillin mediates the cell cycle by interacting with actin. Additionally, a recent study indicated that kindlin-2 can mediate the activity of microtubule-associated deacetylase histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) through a signaling pathway that involves Paxillin [31]. Another article indicated that Paxillin could interact with Rab5 to mediate HDAC6-dependent microtubule deacetylation [32]. Since this evidence suggests that Paxillin may influence the cellular spindle, we examined the spindle structure and localization following Paxillin depletion. Our results demonstrated that Paxillin depletion induced alterations in spindle location. This finding is similar to the role of the Arp2/3 complex in oocytes, where disruption of Arp2/3 can lead to spindle migration defects and symmetric division [33]. Paxillin is involved in the formation of the cleavage furrow during mitosis and is closely related to asymmetric division and spindle orientation in individual HeLa cells [34]. In light of our findings, it appears that Paxillin plays a role in regulating asymmetric division in both mitosis and meiosis.

To further investigate the potential role of Paxillin in actin assembly, we measured F-actin levels in mouse oocytes and observed a significant decrease following Paxillin knockdown. During spindle migration, the progressive organization of actin filaments serves as the foundation for the actin assembly surrounding the spindle [35]. The spindle migrates to the cortex of mouse oocytes by pulling on a cytoplasmic actin network that dynamically connects it to the cortex [36]. Therefore, we subsequently verified the change of actin dynamics through F-actin staining. We then sought to determine how Paxillin regulates actin dynamics and further influences spindle migration in mouse oocytes. Paxillin has been shown to regulate Arp2/3 activation in breast cancer cells [37]. Phosphorylation of Paxillin inhibited the F-actin/G-actin ratios by affecting N-WASP activation [38]. We measured the levels of N-WASP and Arp2, and the results indicated that both N-WASP and Arp2 were reduced. The reason why the absence of Paxillin causes the decrease in the level of N-WASP remains unclear. However, due to the characteristics of transcriptional silencing in oocytes, we speculate that the decrease in Paxillin may promote the degradation of N-WASP protein or mRNA. A previous study demonstrated that the depletion of another Arp2/3-related protein, SKAP2, also resulted in decreased levels of Arp2 and N-WASP, which further caused defects in spindle migration [39]. Similarly, a study on dynamin2 revealed that its knockdown affected polar body extrusion by inducing changes in Arp2 levels in mouse oocytes [40]. Direct evidence for the interaction between Paxillin and Arp2 was not obtained in the present study; however, studies have confirmed that Paxillin directly interacts with N-WASP to promote its activation [19], and N-WASP regulates Arp2 stability by inducing conformational changes in the Arp2/3 complex [41]. Thus, we hypothesize that Paxillin indirectly regulates Arp2 stability through direct binding to N-WASP, thereby modulating Arp2/3-mediated actin polymerization and cytoskeleton reorganization. Another study showed opposite conclusion compared to ours that Paxillin knockout in granulosa cells enhances reproductive capacity in aged female mice [42]. The reasons for such differences might include multiple factors. Such as, Paxillin knockout in granulosa cells was conducted in an intact cell interaction system, where microenvironmental differences amplified the phenotypic variations. Moreover, Paxillin itself has been reported to be associated with aging [10]; thus, its role in young mice may differ from that in aged mice.

Overexpression of Paxillin in humans has been closely associated with the migration and invasion of cancer cells [43, 44]. Additionally, overexpression increased the levels of lactic acid and ATP in human umbilical vein endothelial cells [45]. Furthermore, a prior study indicated that Paxillin overexpression contracts the osteoclast cytoskeleton [46]. Therefore, we conducted experiments on Paxillin overexpression to further elucidate its role during mammalian oocyte maturation. However, we found that Paxillin overexpression did not influence mouse oocyte meiosis. This might be due to the biological saturation of Paxillin during oocyte meiosis; thus, overexpression exerted no significant effect on the process of mouse oocyte maturation, which also indicates that the role of Paxillin in oocytes and umbilical vein endothelial cells or cancer cell is not exactly the same.

In conclusion, our results suggested that Paxillin may play different functions in mammalian versus Xenopus oocytes, and illustrated that Paxillin regulates spindle migration through the N-WASP-Arp2/3 signaling pathway, ultimately leading to failed polar body extrusion and impaired asymmetric division in mammalian oocytes.
