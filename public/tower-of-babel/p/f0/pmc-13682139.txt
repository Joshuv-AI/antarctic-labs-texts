Safe distances in coracoclavicular stabilization of high-grade acromioclavicular joint injury: a cadaveric study
Authors: Korakot Maliwankul, Chaiwat Chuaychoosakoon, Amornrat Chookliang, Wachiraphan Parinyakhup, Prapakorn Klabklay, Tanarat Boonriong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRegarding treatments for acute high-grade acromioclavicular (AC) joint injury, combined coracoclavicular (CC) ligament stabilization and AC capsule repair are associated with the risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury. However, previous studies have only evaluated the distances from the coracoid base to the neurovascular structure in intact AC joints; thus, it is questionable whether the distances from the existing evidence are reliable. This study aimed to measure and compare the distances from the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular bundle in the beach chair, supine, and lateral decubitus positions in cadavers with high-grade AC joint injury.MethodsThis study included four male and four female full-body fresh-frozen cadavers. We created high-grade AC joint injuries in 8 right and 8 left shoulders, and measured the distances from the anteromedial borders of the coracoid base and tip to the lateral border of the neurovascular structure in the horizontal, oblique, and vertical planes using cadavers in the beach chair, supine, and lateral decubitus positions.ResultsThe average distances from the coracoid base to the neurovascular structure were shortest in the lateral decubitus position, with distances in the horizontal, oblique, and vertical planes of 39.93 ± 9.13, 26.29 ± 7.68, and 32.03 ± 8.87 mm, respectively. All distances were affected by body position, except for the distance in the beach chair and supine positions in the vertical plane.ConclusionsWhen performing CC stabilization for acute high-grade AC joint injuries, the lateral decubitus position may provide a smaller anatomical safety margin compared with the supine and beach chair positions in this cadaveric model.

Background

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint injuries account for approximately 9–11% of all shoulder girdle injuries in the general population [1, 2] and nearly 20% of all shoulder injuries in the active population associated with contact sports [3, 4]. These injuries arise from direct or indirect mechanical trauma to the AC joint, usually involving rupture of the AC joint capsule together with the coracoclavicular (CC) ligament complex, which consists of the conoid and trapezoid ligaments that connect the acromion, clavicle, and coracoid processes [5]. Potential consequences of this type of injury include instability, shoulder pain, and reduced shoulder function [6].

For acute high-grade AC joint injuries, the standard treatment is to realign and maintain the joint. The most popular treatment options are a combination of CC stabilization and AC capsule repair [7–9]. For CC stabilization with suture procedures, the surgeon has two options for managing the coracoid process: (a) create one or two tunnels through the coracoid base or (b) pass a suture under the coracoid base to loop the coracoid process [10]. Both techniques involve the risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury because the neurovascular bundle passes the coracoid base medially and inferiorly in this area [11].

Prior research has significantly advanced our understanding of the proximity of the coracoid process to neurovascular structure. An anatomical cadaveric study measured the nearest distance from the anteromedial aspect of the coracoid base to the axillary nerve, musculocutaneous nerve, lateral cord of the brachial plexus, and axillary artery [12]. The study found that the nearest distances from the anteromedial aspect of the coracoid base to the axillary nerve, musculocutaneous nerve, lateral cord of the brachial plexus, and axillary artery were 24.0, 27.0, 27.0 and 31.0 mm, respectively. Another study expanded upon the findings of this study by examining these distances with cadavers placed in the supine, lateral decubitus, and beach chair positions and with different arm postures [11]. A limitation of these two studies was that they did not perform measurements based on high-grade AC joint injuries; instead, they performed measurements based on intact AC joints. Thus, their findings might not be applicable for reducing the risk of neurovascular injury during surgery for high-grade AC joint injuries. High-grade AC joint injuries involve the CC ligament, AC capsule, and deltotrapezial fascia, where the positions of the clavicle or scapula differ from those of patients with intact AC joints. Paksoy et al. [13] evaluated the clavicular and scapular positions in patients with AC joint injuries. They found that the clavicle was displaced superiorly and the scapula was displaced inferiorly, thus putting the results of the two previous studies in further doubt, as they both measured shoulders with intact AC capsules (Fig. 1A). However, no prior study has quantified the distances from the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular bundle in the setting of high-grade AC joint injuries, where clavicular and scapular displacement may substantially alter these relationships. To obtain more accurate measurements of the distances from both the coracoid base and coracoid tip to the neurovascular bundle, it is necessary to measure the distances between the coracoid base and neurovascular bundle in shoulders with high-grade AC joint injury (Fig. 1B).

To the best of our knowledge, no study has comprehensively evaluated the distance from the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular bundle in shoulders with high-grade AC joint injury in the horizontal, oblique, and vertical planes. Thus, the objective of this study was (1) to measure the distances from both the coracoid base and coracoid tip to the neurovascular bundle in the beach chair and supine and lateral decubitus positions in cadavers with high-grade AC joint injury and (2) to compare the distances among each position and direction of distances. We hypothesized that the distances from the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular bundle would differ among these three positions because of position-dependent changes in gravity and arm tension. The shortest distances in three directions should be in lateral decubitus due to the tension force.

Materials and methods

Four male and four female full-body fresh-frozen cadavers, all selected to have no history of rotator cuff injuries or shoulder surgeries, were included in this study. The cadavers were donated for scientific research with prior informed consent obtained from volunteers when they were alive or from all legal next-of-kin, and anonymity and ethical compliance were ensured throughout the study. The mean age at death was 79.4 ± 4.86 years. All the procedures were performed by a skilled neurovascular surgeon. The dissection began with the cadaver in the supine position by making a curved incision from the proximal humerus to the shaft of the clavicle, following which the deltoid muscle was detached and reflected, and the pectoralis major was turned distally from its clavicular origin. The conjoined tendon and pectoralis minor were cut 5 mm from the insertion site and reflected from the coracoid process. The neurovascular structure (axillary nerve, musculocutaneous nerve, lateral cord of the brachial plexus, and axillary artery) were carefully exposed without altering their natural arrangement to maintain the integrity of subsequent measurements.

Following dissection, the AC capsule and CC ligaments were cut at the mid-part, and the deltotrapezial fascia was detached to create a simulated high-grade AC joint injury type V. The anteromedial aspects of the coracoid base and tip were identified, and 2-mm Kirschner wires were inserted at the anteromedial aspects of the coracoid base and tip. 18-gauge needles were inserted at the lateral borders of the neurovascular bundle to establish three reference planes–horizontal, oblique, and vertical–relative to the coracoid process. The distance from each Kirschner wire to each 18-gauge needle was measured using a digital Vernier caliper with a precision of 0.01 mm (Fig. 2A and B). After these measurements were obtained, the cadaver was moved sequentially to the beach chair and lateral decubitus positions, and the measurements were repeated. In each position, the cadaver’s arm was maintained in adduction to standardize the measurements across the three positions.

To decrease potential measurement bias, the distances in each of the three planes were measured three times by an experienced orthopedist and an anatomist, and the mean distances were calculated and used in the study. Statistical analysis was performed with the R program and “epicalc” package (version 3.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The distances from the anteromedial aspect of the coracoid base and tip to the lateral border of the neurovascular structure at different positions were compared using linear mixed-effects models. A P-value of 0.05 was considered significant. The intra- observer reliability was calculated using Intraclass Correlation Coefficient.

Results

The shortest distances from the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular structure were 12.46 mm and 11.61 mm, respectively, both observed in the lateral decubitus position. The shortest distances at each position are shown in Table 1. The mean differences in distance between positions, together with their 95% confidence intervals, are presented in Table 2 for the coracoid base and Table 3 for the coracoid tip. All distances were affected by the cadaver’s position, except for the distance from the coracoid base to the lateral border of the neurovascular structure in the beach chair and supine positions in the vertical plane (p = 0.456) and from the coracoid tip to the lateral border of the neurovascular structure in the beach chair and supine positions in the vertical planes (p = 0.582). The intra-observer reliability was more than 0.9 for all distances in Tables 4 and 5.

Discussion

The distances between the anteromedial border of the coracoid base and tip and the lateral border of the neurovascular bundle were affected by the cadaver position, except for the distances in the beach chair and supine positions in the vertical plane.

The management of acute high-grade AC joint injuries, particularly Rockwood type III to V lesions, remains a subject of ongoing debate. Evidence suggests no statistically significant differences in functional outcome scores between operative and non-operative treatment for type III injuries, although non-operative management is associated with a faster return to work at the expense of less favorable cosmetic appearance.[14, 15] In contrast, for type V AC joint injuries, surgical realignment is widely recommended to restore joint integrity and function. In this context, our simulated model represents CC stabilization procedures typically performed for high-grade (type III–V) injuries, during which there is a risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury when looping beneath or drilling through the coracoid base. Four previous studies have measured the distance between the coracoid process and adjacent neurovascular structure, underscoring the relevance of defining “safe” zones for these high-grade AC joint reconstructions.[11, 12, 16, 17]. First, Lo et al. [12] investigated the risks of iatrogenic neurovascular injury during surgery around the coracoid process, using anatomical dissections of five cadaveric shoulders by separating the neurovascular bundle into the lateral cord of the brachial plexus, musculocutaneous nerve, axillary nerve and axillary artery, which could disturb the normal relationship between the bone and the neurovascular bundle. They measured the distance from the coracoid tip and the base to the nearest part of the neurovascular bundle in cadavers with intact AC capsules. The closest neurovascular structure to the coracoid tip was the lateral cord of the brachial plexus at an average distance of 28.5 mm, whereas the closest to the base was the axillary nerve at an average distance of 29.3 mm. These measurements indicate that the lateral cord of the brachial plexus is most at risk during dissection around the coracoid tip and that the axillary nerve is most at risk during dissection around the base. They concluded that coracoid surgeries were relatively safe, because all neurovascular structure were at least 25 mm away from the anteromedial aspect of the coracoid. Another study by Chuaychoosakoon et al. [11] assessing the distance from the anteromedial border of the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular bundle provided useful anatomical insights into CC stabilization. This study was conducted on 15 freshly frozen cadavers, and the distances from the anteromedial aspects of the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular bundle were compared between various patient positions. Measurements were performed with the cadaver in the supine, lateral decubitus, and beach chair positions, with additional evaluation of the five arm positions in the beach chair position. The study found that the shortest average distance from the coracoid base to the neurovascular bundle was in the beach chair position, specifically with the arm at the side in the horizontal plane (27.4 ± 4.9 mm), and at 90° of abduction in the vertical (21.8 ± 4.2 mm) and closest planes (19.5 ± 4.2 mm). Their findings were similar for the coracoid tip, with the shortest average distance in the beach chair position at 90° abduction across all planes. These distances were significantly different from those measured in the supine and lateral decubitus positions. The study concluded that shoulder surgery near the coracoid process is safer when the patient is in the supine position, and more hazardous in the beach chair position. The third study by Knudsen et al. [16] aimed to provide arthroscopic measurements and orientations of the axillary and musculocutaneous nerves medial to the coracoid process by utilizing a retrospective review of 29 patients and cadaveric dissection of 23 shoulders. The study found that in vivo, the axillary nerve was, on average, 1.5 cm, and the musculocutaneous nerve was, on average, 1.6 cm medial to the coracoid tip. In the cadavers, similar distances were noted between the axillary (2.0 cm) and musculocutaneous nerves (1.5 cm) from the coracoid tip. Notably, the posterosuperior quadrant of the subcoracoid space contained a crossing nerve in fewer cases (13.8% of patients and 39.1% of cadavers) than the posteroinferior quadrant (55.2% of patients and 73.9% of cadavers). This study found that the axillary and musculocutaneous nerves were consistently closer to the coracoid tip and arch (within 1–2 cm) than previously documented, emphasizing the need for extreme caution and specific quadrant-based approaches to minimize the risk of intraoperative nerve injury during arthroscopic dissection in this area. The fourth study by Pan et al. [17] found that during arthroscopic coracoid surgery, the distance between the lateral cord of the brachial plexus and the coracoid process decreased significantly with increased shoulder abduction from 30° to 60°, highlighting a direct correlation between shoulder positioning and the risk of neurovascular injury. However, Pan et al. were limited by their cadaveric model, which used only shoulder sections that might have had less soft tissue tension than full-body fresh-frozen cadavers. These four studies had a point of concern regarding the applicability of the results to patients with high-grade AC joint injuries, as all four studies performed measurements in cadavers with intact AC joints; thus, the distances in actual high-grade AC joint injuries remain undetermined. It would be more accurate to measure the distance between the coracoid process and the neurovascular bundle in shoulders with actual high-grade AC joint injuries because the relative positions of the clavicle and scapula inevitably change after such an injury.

Paksoy et al. [13] evaluated the positions of the clavicle and scapula in patients with AC joint injuries using radiographs of both clavicles in the anteroposterior view. They found that both the clavicle and scapula were displaced; however, in some cases, only the clavicle or scapula was displaced. This could explain why the measured distances in intact AC joints may not represent the actual distances from the anteromedial borders of the coracoid base and tip to the lateral border of the neurovascular structure. Given the limitations of the existing literature, we aimed to measure these distances in cadavers with high-grade AC joint injuries to better understand the impact of altered anatomy on neurovascular risk. We found that the distances from both the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular structure in the supine position were the farthest in all planes, whereas the distances from both the coracoid base and tip to the neurovascular structure in the lateral decubitus position were the shortest. Body position significantly influenced all distances, except in the vertical plane, where the beach chair and supine positions showed no differences. The minimum distances between the anteromedial border of the coracoid base and the coracoid tip across all three planes were observed in the lateral decubitus position. Our findings were compared with those of Chuaychoosakoon et al. [11], who performed similar measurements in intact AC joints, providing contextual reference for interpreting these anatomical relationships in a simulated injury model. To minimize the risk of iatrogenic neurovascular injury during both open and arthroscopic CC stabilization, instruments should be maintained as close as possible to the coracoid cortex. The measured distances from the anteromedial border of the coracoid base, 30.72 mm, 36.03 mm, and 20.52 mm in the beach chair, supine, and lateral decubitus positions, respectively, and from the far cortex, 25.37 mm, 27.49 mm, and 13.25 mm in the beach chair, lateral decubitus, and supine positions, respectively, may serve as approximate anatomical reference values rather than definitive safety limits. In arthroscopic procedures, particular attention should also be given to oblique-plane distances, with instruments such as shavers, burrs, and suture passers kept close to the coracoid cortex to reduce the risk of encroaching on the neurovascular structure medial and inferior to the coracoid base, recognizing that anatomical variability and intraoperative factors may influence these relationships.

This study had some limitations. First, we dissected the soft tissue around the shoulder areas to demonstrate the coracoid process and neurovascular structure, which may have led to alterations in the anatomical relationships due to the dissection process. Efforts were made to reduce this risk by limiting dissection to the lateral and medial borders of the neurovascular bundles, with the aim of preserving the distance from the coracoid process to the neurovascular structure as close to normal as possible. Second, although we simulated a Rockwood type V injury by sectioning the AC capsule, CC ligaments, and deltotrapezial fascia, this cadaveric model cannot fully replicate the in vivo condition. Factors such as dynamic displacement patterns, muscle tension, soft-tissue swelling, hematoma formation, and inflammatory responses are not reproducible in cadaveric specimens, and therefore the model reflects structural disruption but not the full physiological environment of an acute injury. Third, the pre-existing conditions of the shoulders in the cadavers were not known, but cadavers displaying clear signs of rotator cuff lesions, moderate-to-severe osteoarthritis of the shoulder joint, or damage to the long head of the biceps tendon were not included in the study. Fourth, although the anatomical relationships are expected to be largely comparable to those in younger individuals, our measurements were obtained from an elderly cadaveric population, and age-related tissue laxity or degenerative changes may have influenced the measured distances; therefore, these “safe distances” should be applied with caution when extrapolating to younger patients, who more commonly sustain AC joint injuries. Fifth, the sample size was relatively small, which may further limit the generalizability of our findings and warrants cautious interpretation when applying these distances to broader clinical populations. Finally, the use of a fixed adducted arm position represents an additional limitation. Although adduction is commonly used during CC stabilization procedures, variations in arm positioning, including abduction, flexion, traction, and scapular manipulation, may influence the relationship between the coracoid and adjacent neurovascular structure. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted specifically within the context of the adducted arm position used during measurement.

Conclusions

Our study describes the anatomical relationship between the coracoid and adjacent neurovascular structure in a simulated high-grade AC joint injury model. The measured distances were shortest in the lateral decubitus position and greatest in the supine position, indicating that body position may influence the available anatomical working space. However, as this study did not directly compare intact and injured states within the same specimens, it does not establish whether the injury itself alters these relationships. Furthermore, these findings were obtained in a cadaveric model under controlled conditions, and actual distances in clinical practice may vary due to patient-specific anatomy, muscle tone, and intraoperative factors; therefore, the results should be interpreted as anatomical reference data rather than definitive safety thresholds.
