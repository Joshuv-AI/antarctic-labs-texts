Patient expectations and concerns regarding robotic thyroidectomy in china: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Ling Zhan, Siyi Zhu, Zheng Ding, Bomin Guo, Xianzhao Deng, Bo Wu, Zhili Yang, Minggao Guo, Youben Fan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobotic thyroidectomy (RT) has gained increasing patient interest and clinical adoption in China. However, patient perceptions of RT remain poorly understood. A cross-sectional survey of 211 patients who had previously heard of RT was conducted to explore their attitudes, expectations and concerns regarding the procedure. Of the participants, 158 (74.9%) were female, with a median age of 36. The primary source of RT-related information was hospital or clinic visits (58.8%), followed by the Internet and social media (42.7%). Over half (53.6%) showed a clear preference for RT, while 24.2% remained undecided and 22.3% did not favor the robotic approach. Nearly half of the respondents (48.8%) believed RT provided better overall outcomes compared to the open approach. The most commonly expected advantage was improved cosmetic results (80.6%). Leading concerns regarding RT included the surgeon’s experience and case volume (40.3%), increased cost (29.9%) and risk of conversion to open surgery (25.6%). Multivariable regression analyses identified that familiarity with RT (OR 2.01, p = 0.044) and the perception of RT’s superiority over open surgery (OR 5.36, p < 0.001) were significantly associated with preference for RT. Expense concerns were independently associated with a lower likelihood of choosing RT (OR 0.26, p < 0.001). This study identified that participants’ understanding of RT was limited in certain aspects, along with some unfounded expectations and concerns. These findings highlighted the need for clearer information, structured patient education, and evidence-based preoperative counseling to manage expectations, address misconceptions and support informed decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-025-02957-3.Keywords: Robotic thyroidectomy, Survey, Patient perception, Healthcare information, Patient education

Introduction

Robotic thyroidectomy (RT) was first introduced in China in 2014, experiencing rapid growth thereafter. To date, this technique has been adopted by over 70 hospitals nationwide, with nearly 16,000 procedures performed [1]. Although robotic systems offer three-dimensional magnified vision, enhanced dexterity and precise movements, there is insufficient evidence to prioritize RT over endoscopic or open approaches. The safety and oncological outcomes of robotic thyroidectomy and neck dissection are comparable to those of standard open procedures. Some studies suggested that RT could better preserve voice and swallowing function, as well as reducing postoperative neck paresthesia [2–4]. However, others remained skeptical about these improvements, citing only cosmetic benefits, increased cost and longer operation time [5].

Public perceptions and beliefs regarding the overall concept of robotic surgery (RS) have been studied, revealing discrepancies with clinical evidence [6]. As patient interest in RT continues to grow, potentially leading to greater demand, the existing literature lacks specific investigations into RT. In this context, it is critical to improve our understanding of patient expectations and concerns regarding this technique. This will be essential for shared decision-making, and meeting patient expectations could result in improved postoperative satisfaction [7]. Moreover, we aim to identify any knowledge gaps or misconceptions that patients may have about RT, the areas on which we should focus preoperative counseling and patient education.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited according to the following criteria: (1) aged 18 or over; (2) diagnosed with a thyroid condition requiring surgical treatment; (3) had heard of RT and (4) willing to participate in the study. Patients were excluded if they were unable to understand or complete the questionnaire, or if surgery was not indicated.

Informed consent was obtained from each participant, clearly outlining the voluntary and anonymous nature of the survey, as well as its purpose. Participants could withdraw from the study at any time, with no impact on their subsequent treatment. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai Sixth People’s Hospital [2024-KY-245(K)] and has been registered at https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06730321.

Study design

A cross-sectional, self-administered survey was conducted at our center between January and August 2025. The questionnaire was distributed and collected before surgical counseling to evaluate patients’ knowledge, perceptions and preferences regarding RT. As there was no validated survey available on this topic, the questionnaire items were adapted from previous studies and revised based on the authors’ expertise to align with the study’s aim [7, 8]. A pilot test was conducted with ten patients, who were not included in the final analysis, to assess the clarity and comprehension of the items. Amendments were made accordingly, including the removal of the annual income question, as most pre-interviewees were reluctant to disclose it.

The questionnaire consisted of 16 questions, divided into three sections. The first section collected background information about the participants, including their age, gender, marital status, place of residence, education level, employment status, insurance type, surgical history, and self-rated health status. Cities of residence were categorized into first-tier, second-tier, and third-tier or lower [9]. Employment status was categorized as either employed or not employed, with the latter including students, retirees, homemakers, and the unemployed. The second section focused on participants’ perceptions of and preferences for RT. The third section explored participants’ expectations and concerns regarding RT. All questions were provided in Chinese on paper. An English version is available in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 27.0). Quantitative variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression to determine factors related to patient preference for RT when univariate analysis showed p < 0.1. And p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Between January and August 2025, a total of 486 patients scheduled for thyroid surgery were approached. According to the study’s exclusion criteria, two (0.4%) patients under 18 years old and 256 (52.7%) patients with no prior knowledge of RT were excluded. Of the remaining 228 patients who had heard of RT, 225 agreed to participate in the study. After excluding responses to the pilot survey (n = 10) and incomplete questionnaires (n = 4), 211 responses were included in the final analyses (Fig. 1). Of the 211 participants, 158 (74.9%) were female and 53 (25.1%) were male, with a median age of 36 years. Most reported being in good or fair health. The majority (72.5%) had a college degree or higher. Ninety-one (43.1%) patients had a prior surgical history. Open and laparoscopic procedures were recorded in 71 (33.6%) and 26 (12.3%) cases, respectively. None had prior experience with a robotic procedure. Of the participants, 85 (40.3%) and 110 (52.1%) perceived themselves as having a good and fair understanding of RT, respectively (Table 2). Hospital or clinic visits were their primary source of information (58.8%), followed by the Internet and social media (42.7%). Almost half of the respondents believed that RT was superior to the open approach, while 5.7% thought that RT produced worse results. Half of the participants were aware of endoscopic thyroidectomy (ET), of whom 18.9% said that RT was better. The most commonly expected advantage of RT was improved cosmetic results (80.6%). Approximately one-third also associated RT with faster recovery and less pain. Regarding surgical efficacy and safety, the majority indicated that RT would be equivalent to the open approach (Fig. 2A). The leading concerns about RT included the surgeon’s experience and case volume (40.3%), the high cost (29.9%), and the risk of open conversion (25.6%). Approximately 20% of participants expressed uncertainty about this novel technology, and a few raised doubts about its surgical completeness. Few considerations were given to prolonged operation time (11.8%) or malfunctioning issues (4.7%) (Fig. 2B; Table 3).

A total of 113 (53.6%) patients expressed a preference for RT, 51 (24.2%) were undecided, and 47 (22.3%) did not favor the robotic approach. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to explore differences in sociodemographics and perceptions between participants inclined toward RT and those who were not (Table 4). The intention to undergo RT was significantly correlated with patient knowledge of the procedure (OR: 2.01, 95% CI 1.02–3.95; p = 0.044) and the perception that RT provides better overall results (OR: 5.36, 95% CI 2.31–12.45; p < 0.001). Expense concerns were independently associated with a lower likelihood of favoring RT (OR: 0.26, 95% CI 0.13–0.55; p < 0.001).

Discussion

Although RS is growing rapidly in China, its implementation is uneven and mainly concentrated in a few large medical centers [10]. In the present study, more than half of the patients initially approached had not heard of RT. This finding aligns with reports from other healthcare systems where patient awareness of RS is generally limited [11, 12]. The quality of RS-related information can vary significantly. Unregulated online content and aggressive hospital marketing may exaggerate the benefits while downplaying the potential limitations and risks [13–15]. Such materials with limited clinical evidence can lead to patient misconceptions or overestimation of RS, which may further influence their decision-making process [6, 8, 14]. As RT gains increasing clinical adoption and attention, it is important to understand patient expectations and concerns regarding this procedure. By doing so, surgeons can dispel misconceptions, set clear expectations and facilitate informed consent, which could possibly improve postoperative satisfaction.

Oncological safety remains an overarching theme. Mixed responses have been received on this matter. Some participants believe that RT improved radicality, while others question its completeness. Meta-analyses and comparative analyses have demonstrated that robotic-assisted thyroidectomy and modified radical neck dissection provide non-inferior surgical efficacy and oncological safety comparable to open surgery, as reflected by lymph node (LN) yield, serum thyroglobulin level, postoperative radioactive iodine uptake and dose required [2, 5, 16–18]. Long-term outcomes in terms of the recurrence rate and survival were also shown to be equivalent between the matched robotic and open groups [19, 20]. In the absence of large randomized trials due to the high cost, evidence primarily comes from observational and retrospective studies with strict indications. These oncological profiles should continue to be evaluated in future.

Three-dimensional visualization, tremor filtration and multi-articulated instruments allow important anatomical structures to be identified and preserved more easily during RT [17, 21, 22]. However, these technical capabilities have not consistently translated into lower complication rates, as patients often expect. Most studies reported similar rates of major complications, such as hematoma, seroma, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypocalcemia and chyle leak between RT and other approaches [2, 5, 18, 20, 22, 23]. Voice and swallowing disturbances are common postoperative complaints even without nerve injury. While many cases are self-limiting, some patients experience long-term symptoms that affect their quality of life. Theoretically, RT may play a role in minimizing these symptoms by avoiding a cervical incision, limiting dissection of the strap muscles, reducing tissue adhesion, and protecting critical structures [2–4, 23]. Additionally, the potential advantages of RT lie in its accessibility and surgical dexterity within deep anatomical spaces. The flexibility of the robot system overcomes the technical limitations of endoscopy, particularly in challenging anatomical cases involving males, patients with prominent clavicles, and overweight patients [18]. Some authors have noted that the traction and countertraction provided by robotic arms optimize exposure, facilitating a more thorough LN dissection in technically difficult regions, such as the retronerval space, the suprasternal fossa, and the uppermost region of level II [18, 24]. Furthermore, patients should be informed of the additional risks associated with the remote-access technique, although these are uncommon (e.g., mental nerve injury, skin flap perforation, brachial plexus injury and track seeding along the surgical access) [21, 25].

We also found that participants expect faster recovery and reduced postoperative pain with RT, largely because RS is commonly perceived and promoted as a form of minimally invasive surgery. However, remote-access thyroidectomy has been criticized for its wide subcutaneous dissection. This is also a source of concern since some believe it may incur greater surgical trauma. In terms of hospitalization and drainage duration, RT appears comparable to other approaches for postoperative recovery [17, 21–23, 26], although some studies have reported greater drainage volume [18]. The extensive dissection also draws attention to postoperative pain. However, its assessment is complicated by variations in scoring scales, analgesic use, and evaluation timing across studies [2, 21, 22]. Even studies using visual analogue scales (VAS) yielded contradictory results [22, 26]. Notably, pain severity differs according to access type and surgical extent. For example, significantly lower VAS scores were reported for the transoral approach than for the bilateral axillo-breast approach, probably due to the shorter surgical route [22]. Some authors suggested that reduced neck discomfort might transfer to the tunnel region, but would eventually converging with open surgery levels within three to six months postoperatively [2, 3, 22]. Further well-designed and standardized studies encompassing multiple approaches are needed to clarify the situation.

The expectation most closely matched by RT is its superior cosmetic outcomes, which also serves as the main driver for patients choosing this approach. However, similar cosmetic results can be achieved with ET [21, 27–29]. Given that well-established endoscopic techniques are already in use, RT seems to offer few additional clinical advantages [30]. The key distinction lies in the economic cost. Due to the cost of equipment and consumables, RT is nearly twice as expensive as ET and is not usually covered by national health insurance in China [31]. Consequently, the decision to pursue RT requires a deliberate discussion about whether the technical potential justifies the additional expense, or whether aesthetic concerns alone are sufficient motivation. Investigations have revealed that the perceived value and acceptance threshold of robotic technique varied among individuals. In a survey by Pagani NR et al., 60.5% of respondents still preferred the robotic approach even when results were comparable to conventional methods [7]. Another study reported that one-third of respondents expected extremely better results to justify the use of robot [8]. A public survey found that over half of participants were willing to pay extra for robot assistance, with 16% willing to pay more than $10,000 [14]. In contrast, fewer than one in ten patients in another study indicated a willingness to pay out of pocket, typically not exceeding $ 3,000 [8]. Further surveys assessing local willingness-to-pay and perceived value of RT could provide valuable references when determining the extent to which RT should be provided and covered.

Beyond cost, our findings revealed two major patient concerns: the surgeon’s experience and case volume, and the risk of conversion to open surgery. As RT is a relatively novel and challenging technique, surgeon expertise is a valid consideration. It was estimated that 20–50 cases were required to reach the learning curve of RT, depending on the access type [30]. Earlier cases may be associated with longer operating times and a potentially higher risk of certain complications [32]. Even in the stabilization phase, proficiency can vary among surgeons. Therefore, patients should be encouraged to consider not only the theoretical benefits of RT but also the practical expertise of the team performing it. Transparent communication regarding the surgeon’s experience is essential for building trust and ensuring truly informed consent. Given that remote-access thyroidectomy is often viewed as a “scarless” option, the possibility of conversion warrants explicit discussion. Meta-analyses have reported RT conversion rates of around 1%, comparable to those of endoscopic approaches. Common reasons for conversion include excessive bleeding, unexpected tumor extension and technical challenges, highlighting the importance of meticulous patient selection and comprehensive surgical planning [21, 33]. Future research could investigate whether specific patient or tumor characteristics, such as thyroiditis, enlarged thyroid size or tumors located in the high upper pole of the thyroid gland, constitute high-risk factors for conversion or influence the optimal access route. Such insights would refine patient selection for RT and help ensure that patients are matched with the ideal surgical approach. Interestingly, despite the well-documented fact that RT prolongs operation time due to docking and creating working space, few respondents considered this a major drawback. This likely indicates that patients are unaware of the details of this issue. While longer duration has not been shown to adversely impact outcomes, this information should still be clarified during preoperative counseling. Moreover, robot malfunctions and the respective roles of the robot and surgeon are often cited as leading concerns in other studies [7, 8, 14]. However, only a few respondents mentioned these issues in this research. Misconceptions such as equating robotic assistance with autonomous operation or underestimating the surgeon’s role may fuel doubts about safety and surgical control [11, 12, 14]. Therefore, it is important to inform patients that robotic assistance is a tool that enhances a surgeon’s capabilities while remaining under the surgeon’s full control throughout the procedure. To date, reported malfunctions have been rare and are usually resolved simply by replacing the accessory [34]. Patients should also be informed that proactive equipment maintenance, technical backup and continuous team training are routinely in place to ensure any issues can be promptly addressed with minimal disruption during surgery.

Our study found that a preference for RT was closely linked to greater familiarity and higher expectations. However, participants’ understanding of RT was limited in certain aspects, and some expectations were not entirely consistent with clinical evidence. Some respondents remained open to RT, indicating the need for more detailed information. Therefore, coordinated efforts are required. Hospitals should recognize their unique position of trust and provide evidence-based, procedure-specific educational materials. Surgeons are also obligated to offer structured consultations covering all feasible options, ensuring that the choice of RT is based on clinical indications and informed preference. Furthermore, specialist societies could contribute by regulating the quality of online information and supporting public education campaigns.

The idea for this study emerged after our initial experience with nearly 100 cases of RT at our center, driven by recurring patient questions and feedback during counseling. To our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically investigated public perceptions of RT. Drawing on surveys in other surgical fields and earlier patient interactions, we developed a tailored RT questionnaire. By focusing on the relevant patient population, our study provides practical insights that could serve as a reference in settings where RS is less prevalent and RT is being introduced. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the cohort was diverse in background, the convenience sampling and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the results. Further studies involving more centers and a larger sample size are warranted. Another limitation is that the questionnaire was not formally validated, and certain subjective measures, such as pre-existing RT knowledge and health status, lacked a clear definition. Future versions require more objective, comparable measures and a more valid, structured design. While the responses revealed clinically meaningful information, some areas of interest were left unexplored or inadequately explored in order to improve the response rate and ease of completion. Building on these findings, our ongoing research is investigating personalized information needs and willingness-to-pay amounts, as well as how patient perceptions of RT evolve after the surgical consultation and the key drivers of decision-making.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the general surgical population had limited awareness of RT, along with several unfounded expectations and concerns. Our findings raise questions about how RT is presented to and perceived by patients. Efforts should be made to increase public understanding by providing balanced educational materials and regulating the quality of information. It is the responsibility of surgeons to manage expectations, clarify the concept of robotic assistance and contextualize the realistic benefits and limitations of RT. This may, in turn, better support shared decision-making and enhance postoperative satisfaction.
