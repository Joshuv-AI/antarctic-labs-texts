Prognostic value of systemic inflammation response index in cancer patients treated with PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint inhibitors: a meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaobin Gu, Xiaodan Han, Yanli Shen, Yonggang Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prognostic significance of the systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI) in patients with cancer receiving programmed cell death 1 (PD-1)/PD-1 ligand 1 (PD-L1) immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been widely investigated; however, the results have been conflicting. As such, the present meta-analysis aimed to analyze the precise significance of the SIRI in predicting prognosis in patients with cancer undergoing ICI therapy.MethodsA comprehensive literature search of the Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases for relevant studies, published from inception to April 25, 2024, was performed. The SIRI was analyzed for its prognostic utility in patients undergoing ICI therapy by calculating combined hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsSix studies comprising 1133 patients were included in the analysis. Pooled data revealed that a higher SIRI was significantly associated with poor overall survival (OS) (HR 1.96 [95% CI 1.55–2.47]; p < 0.001) and progression-free survival (PFS) (HR 1.41 [95% CI 1.19–1.67]; p < 0.001) for patients who underwent PD-1/PD-L1 ICI treatment. Subgroup analysis revealed that SIRI was markedly associated with dismal OS and PFS, independent of sample size, cut-off value, and survival analysis (p < 0.05). The findings were verified to be robust against publication bias and sensitivity analyses.ConclusionIn summary, an elevated SIRI was significantly associated with OS and PFS in patients with cancer undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 ICI treatment. SIRI may a candidate indicator for predicting the prognosis of patients undergoing ICI therapy.Keywords: Systemic inflammation response index, immune checkpoint inhibitors, meta-analysis, PD-1, PD-L1

Introduction

Cancer remains a high disease burden on healthcare systems worldwide [1]. According to statistics from GLOBOCAN 2020, in 2020, 19.3 million individuals were diagnosed with cancer, with 9.96 million cancer-related deaths [1]. Recently, immunotherapy, particularly using monoclonal antibody-based immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has become a viable therapeutic option for various cancers in recent years [2]. ICIs are monoclonal antibodies that inhibit T cell activation and function by blocking programmed cell death 1 (PD-1), PD-1 ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte antigen 4, the key inhibitors of T cell activation [3]. Under chronic antigen exposure, PD-1 is highly expressed in B lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, tumour-specific activated T cells, monocytes, natural killer cells, and myeloid cells [4]. There are several PD-1 ligands, one of which is PD-L1 [5]. ICIs targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway facilitate the treatment of various advanced cancers. Currently, antibodies that block PD-1―or its ligand, PD-L1―are used to manage solid and hematological malignancies [6]. However, ICI therapy is expensive and only a small subset of patients benefits from treatment. As such, there is an urgent need to identify novel and effective prognostic biomarkers for patients undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 ICI therapy.

Current evidence indicates that inflammatory cells can affect the growth, invasion, and proliferation of tumour cells in the tumour microenvironment [7]. Some blood-test-derived inflammatory indices, including lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio [8], platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio [9], prognostic nutritional index [10], and C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio [11], are efficient prognostic biomarkers for different cancers. The systemic inflammatory response index (SIRI), a hematological indicator, is based on neutrophil, lymphocyte, and monocyte counts. The SIRI has been widely suggested to play a role in solid tumour prognosis prediction in lung adenocarcinoma [12], gastric cancer [13], primary central nervous system lymphoma [14], rectal cancer [15], and hepatoblastoma [16]. In addition, its prognostic utility in patients with cancer undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 ICI treatment has been previously investigated; however, the results remain controversial [17–22]. In some studies, SIRI significantly predicted the prognosis of patients treated with ICIs [17, 19, 22]. However, in others, SIRI was not significantly associated with prognostic outcomes in patients receiving PD-1/PD-L1 blockades [20, 21]. Accordingly, the present meta-analysis aimed to determine the utility of the SIRI in predicting the prognosis of patients undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 ICI therapy.

Materials and methods

Study guideline

This study was performed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (i.e. ‘PRISMA’) guidelines [23].

Ethics statement

Requirements for ethics approval were waived because this study did not involve humans or animals, and relied solely on previously published, anonymized data.

Study retrieval

A comprehensive literature search of the Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases was performed using the following key terms: (systemic inflammation response index (immune checkpoint inhibitors OR immunotherapy OR programmed death ligand-1 OR programmed death-1 receptor OR PD-1 inhibitor OR PD-L1 inhibitor OR nivolumab OR anti-PD-1 antibodies OR pembrolizumab OR durvalumab OR atezolizumab or avelumab, PD-1 or PD-L1, tislelizumab, toripalimab, immune checkpoint inhibitor, ICI, or ICIs). The search timeline ranged from database inception to April 25, 2024, and only studies published in English were included in this meta-analysis. The reference lists of eligible studies were manually searched to identify other, potentially eligible studies.

Study screening criteria

Studies fulfilling the criteria were included: patients with cancer undergoing treatment with PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs; SIRI calculated as neutrophils × monocytes/lymphocytes; reporting the association between SIRI and any survival outcomes; sufficient or computable hazard ratio (HR) and corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) data using Tierney’s methods; determined cut-off SIRI value; and publication in English.

Reviews, case reports, letters, meeting abstracts, or commentaries, studies with overlapping patients, and animal studies were excluded.

Data collection and quality evaluation

Two independent researchers (X.G. and X.H.) reviewed the eligible studies and extracted the relevant data. Disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third researcher (Y. Shi) until consensus was reached. The extracted information included the following: first author; publication year; country; sample size; age; sex; study design; study period; cancer type; ICI drug(s); ICI type; SIRI cut-off value; study centre; survival outcomes; survival analysis types; follow-up; and HRs and corresponding 95% CIs. The primary and secondary survival endpoints included overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), respectively. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [24] which is scored from 0 to 9 points, with ≥ 6 points indicating high quality.

Statistical analysis

The utility of the SIRI in the prognosis of patients undergoing ICI treatment was assessed according to combined HRs and 95% CIs. Inter-study heterogeneity was estimated using the chi-squared Q test and the I2 statistic. A random-effects model was used for meta-analysis in cases of obvious heterogeneity (I2 > 50% or p < 0.10); otherwise, a fixed-effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the utility of the SIRI in predicting prognosis of the stratified populations. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the ‘leave-one-out’ method to determine new HRs, thereby assessing the robustness and stability of the results. Publication bias of the included articles was assessed using funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test. All statistical analyses were performed using Stata Release 12.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Differences with p < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Study screening process

The primary literature search retrieved 669 studies, of which 531 were retained after eliminating duplicates (Figure 1). After reading titles and abstracts, 473 records were removed because they were irrelevant or were animal studies, leaving 58 for further assessment. After examining the full texts, 52 studies were excluded because they did not report on SIRI (n = 46), had unavailable survival information (n = 2), ICIs (n = 3), or were reviews (n = 1). Ultimately, 6 studies comprising 1133 patients were included in the present meta-analysis [17–22]. A flow diagram illustrating the study selection process is presented in Figure 1.

PRISMA flow diagram of included studies.

Features of the included studies

The basic characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. Five studies were conducted in China [18–22] and 1 in Japan [17]. The publication years were 2021–2023, and the sample sizes ranged from 51 to 435 (median, 121). There were 4 retrospective studies [17, 19, 20, 22] and two prospective trials [18, 21]. Two studies enrolled patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [19, 20]. One study recruited patients with urothelial carcinoma (UC) [17], small cell lung cancer (SCLC) [18], oesophageal squamous cell carcinoma [21], and various cancers [22], respectively. Three studies adopted anti-PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs [19, 20, 22], 2 used anti-PD-1 drugs [17, 21], and 1 used anti-PD-L1 antibodies [18]. Four studies were single-centre studies [19–22] and 2 were multicenter trials [17, 18]. The cut-off SIRI values were 0.94–2.32 (median, 1.25). Five studies addressed the utility of the SIRI in predicting OS [17–20, 22] whereas 4 investigated the relationship between SIRI and PFS [19–22]. Three studies reported HRs and 95% CIs using univariate regression [17, 18, 22], whereas 3 used multivariate regression [19–21]. NOS scores among the included studies were 7–9, demonstrating high quality (Table 1).

SIRI and OS

Five studies involving 1082 patients [17–20, 22] reported the utility of SIRI on the OS of ICI-treated cancer patients. Owing to insignificant heterogeneity (I2=0, p = 0.868), a fixed-effects model was used. Based on pooled data, a higher SIRI was significantly associated with a dismal OS (HR 1.96 [95% CI 1.55–2.47]; p < 0.001) (Table 2, Figure 2). In the subgroup analysis, SIRI was still markedly associated with poor OS, independent of country, sample size, cut-off value, study centre, and survival (p < 0.05) (Table 2). In addition, a higher SIRI was significantly associated with OS in the following subgroups: retrospective design (p < 0.001); HCC (p < 0.001); UC (p = 0.003); studies using anti-PD-1 drugs (p = 0.003); and anti-PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

SIRI and PFS

Four studies enrolling 989 patients [19–22] reported HRs and 95% CIs for the utility of SIRI in predicting PFS. A fixed-effects model was used due to insignificant heterogeneity (I2=0, p = 0.411; HR 1.41 [95% CI 1.19–1.67]; p < 0.001) (Figure 3, Table 3), demonstrating an obvious correlation between high SIRI and inferior PFS in patients undergoing ICI treatment. According to the subgroup analysis, the utility of the SIRI in predicting PFS remained unaffected by sample size, study design, cancer type, ICI type, SIRI cut-off value, and survival analysis type (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed to determine whether the results of the meta-analysis were stable. The pooled HRs and 95% CIs for OS and PFS remained essentially unchanged, suggesting that the findings were robust (Figure 4).

Publication bias

In the present study, potential publication bias was assessed using Begg’s funnel plot and Egger’s test. As shown in Figure 5, publication bias was not detected for OS (p = 0.806 and p = 0.526 using Begg’s and Egger’s tests, respectively) or PFS (p = 0.308 and p = 0.072 using Begg’s and Egger’s tests, respectively).

Discussion

The utility of the SIRI in predicting the prognosis of patients with cancer after PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs treatment among the existing literature is controversial. The present study combined data from 6 studies including 1133 patients [17–22] to investigate this issue and demonstrated a significant relationship between elevated SIRI and dismal OS/PFS in patients receiving ICI therapy. Moreover, the prognostic efficiency of the SIRI was stable in various subgroups. Our findings were verified to be robust using a publication bias test and sensitivity analysis. Collectively, results of the present study revealed that the SIRI was an inexpensive and stable prognostic biomarker for patients undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 ICI therapy. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to analyze the utility of the SIRI in the prognosis of patients undergoing ICI treatment.

The SIRI is an index calculated from data obtained from blood tests and laboratory investigations. An elevated SIRI may be induced by increased neutrophil, monocyte, and/or low lymphocyte counts. Although the mechanisms underlying the impact of SIRI on predicting prognosis in ICI-treated patients remain largely unclear, they are interpreted as follows. First, neutrophil-infiltrating tumours have been recognized as important elements in tumour progression, with several tumour-promoting properties [25]. A large number of tumour-promoting growth factors are produced and secreted by neutrophils, including vascular endothelial growth factor, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, and epidermal growth factor, which enhance cancer cell activation while facilitating cancer occurrence, metastasis and invasion [26]. Increasing evidence suggests that tumour-infiltrating neutrophils may promote angiogenesis, cell mobility, and migration, all of which are associated with a poor prognosis [27]. Second, during tumorigenesis, monocytes differentiate into tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) [28]. The production of growth factors and cytokines by TAMs leads to angiogenesis and anti-immune responses, which, in turn, lead to tumour progression and metastasis [29]. Cancer cells interact with TAMs to promote tumour angiogenesis, migration, and invasion and suppress antitumor immunity, resulting in tumour progression and worse outcomes [30]. Third, lymphocytes play an important role in cell-mediated anticancer responses. Among the significant components of the human immune system, lymphocytes inhibit tumorigenesis and relapse and regulate immune function through cytokines and cytotoxic death [31]. Therefore, the SIRI is a biologically reasonable prognostic marker for patients treated with ICIs.

ICIs are the leading approach for tumour immunotherapy [32]. Prognostic and predictive biomarkers for the immunotherapy of advanced tumours are important for response evaluation in clinical practice. The SIRI has several advantages as a novel prognostic marker for ICI treatment. First, it is easily available because it is derived from laboratory investigations commonly performed in clinical settings. Second, it is cost-effective. Third, it can be continuously monitored throughout patient treatment.

Notably, some recent studies have provided important evidence supporting the antitumor activity of PD-1 [33] and the optimal duration of ICI administration [34]. A recent meta-analysis of phase III trials investigated the efficacy of immunotherapy as a first-line treatment for advanced or recurrent endometrial cancer (EC) [33]. They revealed that the adoption of chemotherapy alongside immunotherapy significantly improved PFS in patients with EC compared with chemotherapy alone. Moreover, survival benefits are enhanced in mismatch repair deficiency/microsatellite instability-hypermutated tumours [33]. This study has important implications for the current meta-analysis because it reports the results of EC [33]. However, the current meta-analysis did not include gynaecological tumours. Another recent meta-analysis investigated the optimal duration of ICI administration [34]. The authors included 57 studies and demonstrated that when patients with melanoma underwent prolonged ICI, their OS was better than when they received 2-year ICI [34]. Furthermore, 2-year ICI therapy led to better OS than prolonged ICI therapy in patients with non-SCLC [34]. However, our meta-analysis did not investigate the effects of various ICI management durations. Bogani et al. provided evidence that a 2-year course of ICI treatment may be an important threshold for survival outcomes [34].

The present study had some limitations, the first of which was its small sample size, with only 6 studies comprising 1133 patients. However, a comprehensive literature search of the most recent literature was performed. Second, there were no uniform cut-off SIRI values reported in the included studies, which may have introduced heterogeneity. Third, all enrolled studies were conducted in Asia. Therefore, our findings may not necessarily apply to all populations. Due to these limitations, more large-scale, multicenter, trials using standard SIRI cut-off values are needed for validation.

Conclusions

In summary, a higher SIRI was significantly associated with OS and PFS in patients with cancer undergoing PD-1/PD-L1 ICIs treatment. The SIRI may be a candidate prognostic indicator for patients undergoing ICI therapy.
