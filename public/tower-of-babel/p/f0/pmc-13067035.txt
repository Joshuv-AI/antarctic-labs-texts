Experimental study of ultrasound combined with microbubbles augmenting the sonodynamic therapy of 5-aminolevulinic acid in glioma cells
Authors: Yong He, Qiong Sun, Qian Wan, Chang Zhou, Rong Liu, Jun Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSonodynamic therapy (SDT) is a promising non-invasive modality for cancer treatment. This in vitro study aims to investigate the cytotoxic effect of low-intensity ultrasound (US) combined with microbubbles (MBs)-enhanced 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA)-mediated SDT on C6 glioma cells.Methods In vitro experiments utilized the C6 glioma cell line. Cultured cells were subjected to low-intensity US irradiation [1.0 MHz, acoustic intensity of 2.0 W/cm2, peak negative pressure of 0.5 MPa, mechanical index (MI) of 0.5, duty ratio of 20%] after the administration of 5-ALA. The uptake of 5-ALA by the cells was assessed using confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) and flow cytometry. Cytotoxicity was measured using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (MTS) assay. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was employed to observe the ultrastructure and mitochondrial changes in the cells. The generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the cells was examined using confocal CLSM.ResultsFlow cytometry and CLSM results indicated that the level of protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) in cells was significantly enhanced after treatment with US combined with MBs and 5-ALA (US-MBs-5-ALA group). MTS analysis demonstrated that the cell viability was significantly reduced in the US-MBs-5-ALA group. Qualitative observations from TEM revealed that mitochondria in cells subjected to low-intensity US treatment alone showed swelling and vacuolization. Upon combined treatment with US and 5-ALA, cells exhibited apoptotic features such as nuclear condensation and crescent-shaped structures. Moreover, mitochondrial damage was more severe in the US-MBs-5-ALA group. Qualitative analysis by CLSM showed that the level of ROS in the US-MBs-5-ALA group was significantly increased. These results suggest a mechanistic link where US-MBs enhance 5-ALA uptake, leading to increased ROS generation under sonication, which in turn induces mitochondrial damage and promotes cytotoxicity.ConclusionsThese preliminary in vitro findings demonstrate that low-intensity US combined with MBs can increase the uptake of 5-ALA by C6 cells and enhance the cytotoxic effect of 5-ALA-mediated SDT. This approach warrants further investigation as a potential therapeutic strategy for glioma.Keywords: Ultrasound (US), microbubbles (MBs), sonodynamic therapy (SDT), 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA), glioma

Highlight box

Key findings

• Low-intensity ultrasound (US) combined with microbubbles (MBs) significantly enhanced the cellular uptake of 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) by C6 glioma cells.

• The combination treatment (US-MBs-5-ALA) resulted in significantly increased cytotoxicity compared to US-5-ALA treatment alone.

• Enhanced reactive oxygen species generation and more severe mitochondrial damage were observed in the combined treatment group.

What is known and what is new?

• It is known that sonodynamic therapy (SDT) using 5-ALA has anti-tumor effects on various cancers, and US-mediated MBs cavitation can enhance drug delivery through sonoporation.

• This study demonstrates for the first time that the combination of low-intensity US and MBs can synergistically enhance the efficacy of 5-ALA-mediated SDT in glioma cells by improving sonosensitizer uptake.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that combining US with MBs may be a promising strategy to improve the therapeutic efficacy of SDT for glioma treatment.

• Further in vivo studies using animal models are warranted to validate these preliminary in vitro findings before clinical translation.

Introduction

Glioma is one of the most harmful primary malignant tumors in the central nervous system, and its 5-year survival rate typically remains below 20% globally (1-3). The main treatment methods for glioma are surgery, radiotherapy and chemotherapy (4,5), but the side effects are obvious (6). With the application of high-intensity focused ultrasound (US) in hepatocellular carcinoma and other tumors, sonodynamic therapy (SDT) has also become a treatment method for many tumors (7).

SDT is a treatment method that uses a sensitizer and US exposure to treat the target tissues (8). Because US can penetrate deep tissues and target areas of tumor growth, SDT solves the shortcomings of photodynamic therapy (PDT) (9). Many organic sonosensitizers such as hematoporphyrin, Photofrin, chlorin-e6 and indocyanine green are widely used in SDT (8). 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) is a potent sonosensitizer that can be converted to protoporphyrin IX (PpIX) and accumulates in the heme biosynthesis pathway of the mitochondria of tumor cells (10). Previous studies have found that ALA-SDT has an anti-tumor effect on glioma (11-15), tongue cancer (16), osteosarcoma (17) and melanoma (18). In our previous research, it was found that ALA-SDT has a strong cell killing effect on the leukemia cell line K562 (19). However, the efficiency of sonosensitizer uptake by tumor cells remains a key factor limiting the therapeutic efficacy of SDT. The blood-brain barrier (BBB) also presents a major obstacle for drug delivery in glioma treatment, although this aspect was not investigated in the current in vitro study (20,21).

Microbubbles (MBs) are extensively utilized as US contrast agents to enhance the identification and assessment of malignant lesions (22,23). More importantly, US-induced acoustic cavitation of MBs is acknowledged as a crucial factor in the transient permeabilization of cell membranes, a phenomenon known as sonoporation (24). This effect can significantly enhance the intracellular delivery of therapeutic agents. It is reported that in comparison with the sonosensitizer alone, MBs and sonosensitizer conjugates enhance cytotoxicity in cancer cells and inhibit tumor growth (25). While previous studies have demonstrated the anti-tumor effects of ALA-SDT in various cancers and the potential of US-MBs to enhance drug delivery (26-30), the specific synergistic effect of combining US and MBs to enhance 5-ALA uptake and subsequent SDT efficacy in glioma cells has not been systematically investigated.

Therefore, this in vitro study aimed to investigate whether the combination of low-intensity US and MBs could enhance the efficacy of 5-ALA-mediated SDT in C6 rat glioma cells. Specifically, we aimed to: (I) evaluate the effect of US combined with MBs on the cellular uptake of 5-ALA; (II) assess the enhancement of cytotoxicity; and (III) explore the underlying cellular mechanisms, focusing on the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial damage. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2319/rc).

Methods

Sensitizer

5-ALA (catalog number: A3785, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) at a concentration of 10 mM. The pH of the stock solution was adjusted to 7.2–7.4, and it was sterilized by filtration through a 0.22 µm filter. The solution was stored in the dark at −20 ℃. DMEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), streptomy-cin, and penicillin were obtained from Gibco (Grand Is-land, NY, USA).

Cell lines and cell culture

The C6 rat glioma cell line used in this study was provided by the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cell line was authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling, and routine testing for mycoplasma contamination using a polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based kit confirmed its negative status. Cells between passages 5 and 15 were used for all experiments. The cells were cultured in DMEM medium containing 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and incubated at 37 ℃ in a humidified incubator with an atmosphere of 5% CO2. The cells were harvested for passage twice per week when they were nearly confluent. 5-ALA was added at a final concentration of 1.0 mM. Then, the cells were used in the following experiments.

US and MBs treatment

The US equipment was purchased from RIGOL Technologies, Inc. (DG4162, Suzhou, China). The experiments were conducted using a 1.0 MHz unfocused transducer with a diameter of 20 mm. The transducer was placed at the bottom of a water tank, with its radiation plane facing upwards. A 96-well plate containing tumor cells (1×106 cells/mL) was placed on the platform of the ultrasonic transducer focus, with the bottom of the plate positioned 5 cm from the transducer surface. The space between the plate and the transducer was filled with degassed, double-distilled water at room temperature (25±1 ℃) for acoustic coupling. The transducer was driven by a signal generator and a power amplifier to produce a pulsed US wave with the following parameters: frequency of 1.0 MHz, acoustic intensity of 2.0 W/cm2, peak negative pressure of 0.5 MPa, mechanical index (MI) of 0.5, duty cycle of 20%, and an exposure duration of 60 s.

SonoVue microbubble contrast agent (Bracco, Milan, Italy) was mixed with 5 mL 0.9% normal saline and shaken for 20 s to make MB suspension. The concentration of the reconstituted MBs was determined using a hemocytometer, and the suspension was diluted in culture medium to a final concentration of 2×106 bubbles/mL.

All experiments were randomly assigned to one of six groups using a random number generator: (I) control group: blank control without any treatment; (II) 5-ALA group: cells treated with 5-ALA alone for 4 hours (final concentration of 1 mM); (III) US group: cells subjected to US irradiation alone for 60 s; (IV) US-MBs group: cells incubated with 2×106/mL Sonovue MBs for 5 minutes, followed by US irradiation for 60 s; (V) US-5-ALA group: cells incubated with 1 mM 5-ALA for 4 hours, followed by US irradiation for 60 s; (VI) US-MBs-5-ALA group: cells incubated with 1 mM 5-ALA for 4 hours, then co-incubated with 2×106/mL Sonovue MBs for the final 5 minutes, followed by US irradiation for 60 s. Some experiments were performed in triplicate as independent biological replicates. Investigators performing the subsequent assays were blinded to the treatment group assignments.

Fluorescence intensity of PpIX

The fluorescence intensity of PPIX was monitored using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). Briefly, after an additional 4-hour incubation of the treated cells from each group, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and the nuclei were stained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). The washed cells were then suspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the PpIX fluorescence intensity was analyzed using a CLSM (LSM 510, Zeiss, Jena, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

Cells from each treated group were incubated for an additional 4 hours, harvested, and washed with PBS. Live, unfixed cells were immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer (Attune Acoustic Focusing Cytometer, Life Technologies, Waltham, MA, USA) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 650 nm. A minimum of 10,000 events were acquired for each sample. The cell population was gated based on forward and side scatter properties to exclude debris. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Each group conducts three parallel experiments.

Cytotoxicity of SDT in the C6 cells

Cytotoxicity was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium (MTS) assay. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5×103 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Following the treatments as described above, cells were incubated for an additional 24 hours. Each experimental condition included 5 technical replicates. Briefly, cells were cultured in 96-well plates, and after treatments, 20 µL of MTS reagent (CellTiter 96 AQueous One Solution Cell Proliferation Assay, Promega, Madison, WI, USA) was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 ℃ for 3 hours. After MTS incubation, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 480 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm using a VarioScan Flash microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The cell viability was calculated by the following formula: cell viability (%) = (absorbance of experimental hole − absorbance of blank hole)/(absorbance of control well − absorbance of blank hole) × 100%.

Observation of mitochondrial morphology by transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Cells were seeded in culture dishes and, after treatment of each group, incubated for an additional 4 hours. The original medium was then discarded. The cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 hours at 4 ℃. After washing, cells were post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) for 1 hour, followed by dehydration with a graded series of ethanol concentrations. Samples were embedded in Epon 812 (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Fort Washington, PA, USA). Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were prepared, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and images were captured using a TEM (H-600, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). At least 20 cells per group were examined to ensure representative images.

ROS detection

The generation of intracellular ROS was assessed using the fluorescent probe 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA). Immediately after the various treatments, cells were washed with serum-free medium and incubated with 10 µM DCFH-DA at 37 ℃ for 30 minutes in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS to remove excess probe, then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. The fluorescence intensity of dichlorofluorescein (DCF), the oxidized product, was observed using a CLSM (LSM 510, Zeiss) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission wavelength of 525 nm.

Statistical analysis

All analyses, unless specified otherwise, were performed using GraphPad Prism8.0.1 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent biological replicates (n=3). Normality of data distribution was checked using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparison between two groups, an unpaired Student’s t-test was performed. For comparison of more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PpIX fluorescence detection in C6 cells

To evaluate the effect of US combined with MBs on the uptake of 5-ALA by C6 cells, we assessed the accumulation of 5-ALA-derived PpIX in C6 cells using flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy. As shown in Figure 1, the red fluorescence intensity of 5-ALA-derived PpIX was significantly enhanced in the US-MBs-5-ALA group compared to other groups. Quantitative analysis by flow cytometry indicated that the mean fluorescence intensity in the 5-ALA group was significantly higher than that in the control group (P<0.0001), whereas the average fluorescence intensity in the 5-ALA group was lower compared to the US-5-ALA group (P<0.001). In contrast, the average fluorescence intensity in the US-MBs-5-ALA group was significantly enhanced compared to the US-5-ALA group (P<0.0001) (Figures 2,3). These findings suggest that the combination of US and MB treatment can significantly enhance the uptake of 5-ALA by C6 cells. Evaluation of the cytotoxic effect of SDT on C6 cells

To evaluate the cytotoxic effect of SDT on C6, we conducted MTS assays 24 hours after treatment. As shown in Table 1 and Figure S1, there were no significant changes in optical density (OD) values for the 5-ALA group, US group, and US-MB group compared to the control group (P>0.05). In contrast, the US-5-ALA and US-MBs-5-ALA groups exhibited an increasing trend, particularly the US-MBs-5-ALA group, which showed a significant increase (P<0.05, P<0.0001, respectively). Moreover, the OD values in the US-MBs-5-ALA group were also significantly higher compared to the US-5-ALA group (P<0.001). It reflects that US combined with MBs can enhance the toxic effect of 5-ALA-mediated SDT on C6 cells.

Ultrastructural cell morphology and mitochondrial morphology

Studies have shown that 5-ALA-derived PpIX is located in mitochondria (31). Therefore, we speculate that under the condition of US and MBs, 5-ALA may achieve the purpose of tumor killing by locating in mitochondria and destroying mitochondrial structure. We qualitatively observed the ultrastructure of C6 cells and the morphology of mitochondria under different conditions using TEM. As shown in Figure 4, the cell membrane of C6 cells without any treatment was intact, many normal mitochondria had good ridge structure, and there were many kinds of nuclei. After only US treatment, the cells maintained a complete cell membrane, but it was found that the mitochondria were enlarged, the ridge structure was not obvious, and some cells showed vacuoles. After the combined intervention of US and 5-ALA, several cells exhibited classic signs of apoptosis, including nuclear condensation, cell fragmentation, and crescent-shaped structures. The mitochondrial damage in the cells was more serious, and the enlarged mitochondria appeared obviously. The mitochondrial cristae almost disappeared completely, and more mitochondria appeared vacuoles, indicating that 5-ALA combined with US treatment significantly affected the structure of mitochondria. The mitochondrial damage of cells treated with US combined with MBs and 5-ALA was more serious than that of other groups. It is suggested that the killing effect of US combined with MBs enhanced 5-ALA mediated SDT on C6 cells may be achieved through mitochondrial dysfunction.

Detection of intracellular ROS

It has been reported that the production of intracellular ROS is the most important mechanism of SDT (31,32). To assess the generation of intracellular ROS under varying conditions, we used DCFH-DA as a ROS capture probe. The results of cell fluorescence imaging are shown in Figure 5. Under the condition of only US or only drug treatment, the fluorescence intensity of the cells was not much different from that of the control group, and the intracellular fluorescence was weak. Compared with the cells treated with drugs or US alone, the green fluorescence intensity of ROS in C6 cells increased after drug combined with US treatment, and the green fluorescence intensity of US-MBs-5-ALA group was stronger. This qualitative assessment suggests that US in combination with MBs improved the cytotoxicity of 5-ALA-induced SDT on C6 cells, potentially through oxidative stress due to an excess of ROS, resulting in mitochondrial impairment.

Discussion

As an important treatment method, SDT has shown important prospects in the treatment of glioma. In this study, we demonstrated that the combination of low-intensity US and MBs significantly enhanced the cytotoxic effect of 5-ALA-mediated SDT on C6 glioma cells in vitro. Our results indicate that this synergistic effect is likely associated with increased cellular uptake of 5-ALA and subsequent enhancement of ROS production and mitochondrial damage.

In this study, we demonstrated that the combination of low-intensity US and MBs significantly enhanced the uptake of 5-ALA and the subsequent cytotoxic effect of SDT in C6 glioma cells. Our findings are consistent with previous reports that US-mediated MBs cavitation, or sonoporation, can transiently increase cell membrane permeability, thereby facilitating the intracellular delivery of various therapeutic agents (24,33). Although our research did not directly observe the phenomenon of acoustic perforation, the significantly enhanced PpIX fluorescence intensity in the US-MB-5-ALA group compared to the US-5-ALA group strongly suggests that the cellular uptake mechanism has been enhanced. This enhanced uptake of the sonosensitizer is a critical step, as the efficacy of SDT is highly dependent on the intracellular concentration of the sensitizer (8). Our findings align with recent studies demonstrating that MBs can act as effective carriers or enhancers for various therapeutic agents in cancer therapy (34,35).

The enhanced cytotoxicity observed in the US-MBs-5-ALA group is likely a multi-faceted process. A key finding of our study is the significant increase in intracellular ROS generation in this group. This elevated oxidative stress is a well-established mechanism of SDT-induced cell death (32,36). The ROS may originate from both the acoustic cavitation process itself and the sonosensitization of accumulated PpIX. The severe mitochondrial damage observed via TEM, which was quantitatively more pronounced in the US-MBs-5-ALA group, is a direct downstream consequence of this oxidative stress. Mitochondria are not only the primary site of PpIX accumulation but are also highly susceptible to ROS-induced damage, which can trigger the intrinsic apoptotic pathway (37). Our results, therefore, suggest that the synergistic effect of US and MBs enhances 5-ALA-SDT by initiating a cascade: improved drug uptake, leading to greater PpIX accumulation, which, upon sonication, generates a higher level of ROS, causing more severe mitochondrial damage and ultimately leading to increased cell death. However, the precise contribution of apoptosis versus other cell death pathways warrants further investigation.

Nonetheless, this study possesses several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the reliance on a single rat glioma cell line in a two-dimensional culture system means the findings may not fully recapitulate the complex, heterogeneous microenvironment of human gliomas in vivo. To address this, future investigations should progress towards more physiologically relevant models, such as co-culture systems, three-dimensional tumor spheroids, and ultimately, orthotopic animal models to validate the therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms. Second, the assessment of ROS generation and mitochondrial damage was primarily based on qualitative imaging. To overcome this limitation and strengthen the conclusions, subsequent studies should incorporate rigorous quantitative analyses. This includes employing flow cytometry to determine the mean fluorescence intensity for ROS levels (38) and performing systematic morphometric analysis on a larger number of TEM images to obtain statistically robust data on mitochondrial damage (39). Third, the number of independent biological replicates was not consistently reported, which impacts the assessment of the findings’ robustness. Future work must be designed with a clear distinction between technical and biological replicates, with a sufficient number of the latter to ensure statistical power and confirm the validity of the observed effects, as recommended for ensuring research reproducibility (40). Finally, while the US parameters were standardized, a more comprehensive dosimetric characterization and an analysis of MB cavitation behavior under these specific experimental conditions would be beneficial. Future experiments should include these characterizations to improve reproducibility and facilitate a deeper mechanistic understanding.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the present in vitro study demonstrated that the combination of low-intensity US with MBs could significantly increase the uptake of 5-ALA by C6 glioma cells. This enhanced uptake leads to a more potent cytotoxic effect of SDT, which appears to be mediated by an increase in intracellular ROS production and subsequent mitochondrial damage. Our findings provide strong preliminary evidence for this combined strategy and highlight a promising avenue for improving the efficacy of SDT. However, given the limitations of this study, further in vivo research is essential to validate these findings and explore the translational potential of this approach for the treatment of glioma.
