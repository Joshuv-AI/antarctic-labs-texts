False-negative sentinel lymph node biopsy for melanoma: a single-surgeon experience
Authors: Julia Downey, Kimberly DeVries, Ian Marie Lano, Christopher Baliski
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The status of the regional lymph node basin is of prognostic importance in patients with melanoma, making the performance of sentinel lymph node biopsies (SLNBs) a key component of patient care management, particularly with the advent of immunotherapy for adjuvant treatment. The primary goal of our study was to assess the false-negative rate of SLNBs among patients with melanoma.Methods:We conducted a retrospective review of patients with melanoma undergoing SLNB by a single surgeon between Jan. 1, 2005, and Dec. 31, 2020. We extracted and cross-referenced patient demographic and pathologic information.Results:During the study period, 501 patients underwent an SLNB. Of these, 97 (19.4%) patients had pathologically positive sentinel lymph nodes and 404 (80.6%) patients had negative results. The latter were subject to further review; 84 (20.8%) patients subsequently developed recurrence, with 25 (6.2%) recurrences within the primary nodal basin. Isolated regional recurrence occurred in 11 (2.7%) patients and conjunction with a false-negative rate was 10.2%. Unadjusted recurrence rates were similar across each lymph node basin, including the axilla (2.7%), groin (3.6%), and neck (1.4%).Conclusion:The false-negative SLNB rate was 10.2% for isolated regional recurrences. These findings need to be considered in the era of using adjuvant systemic therapy for patients with melanoma.

The status of the regional lymph node basin is of prognostic significance in patients with melanoma, making sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) a key component of patient care management. Although the therapeutic value of SLNB and subsequent complete lymph node dissection has come into question, the advent of adjuvant immunotherapy has changed the landscape for melanoma management.1–3 The pathologic status of regional lymph nodes as assessed by SLNB, has become increasingly important in the era of adjuvant immunotherapy, which has been shown to improve recurrence-free, overall, and distant metasis–free survival.4–6 Accurate identification of regional disease is important to guide adjuvant treatment, as well as ensuring regional control of disease. Although SLNB has been performed for more than 3 decades with multiple refinements, occult disease in the regional lymph node basin may be missed, manifesting as lymphadenopathy in the previously sampled basin.7

Rates of false-negative results after SLNB are highly variable, ranging between 0% and 34%,8 with contemporary reports in the range of 4%–14.5%.9–13 Understanding the limitations of and risk factors for a false-negative SLNB has the potential to affect both treatment decisions and follow-up recommendations for patients. Furthermore, ongoing evaluation of surgeon-and institutional-level performance is important for quality assurance and improvement purposes.14 The primary goal of our study was to assess the false-negative rate (FNR) within a cohort of patients with melanoma, with a secondary goal of identifying factors predictive of a false-negative SLNB.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective analysis of patients with melanoma undergoing an SLNB by a single surgeon (C.B.). With the assistance of a previous population-based data set,15 we identified patients who had undergone an SLNB by a single surgeon between Jan. 1, 2005, and Dec. 31, 2020. The original data and subsequent supplementary information were extracted from British Columbia’s population-based Cancer Agency Information System (CAIS), with further information pertaining to follow-up imaging identified from Medi-Tech, the electronic health record of the Interior Health Authority in BC. All patients with a pathologic diagnosis of malignancy are identified in the BC Cancer Registry for the purposes of cancer control and screening; these data are accessible through CAIS, the provincial information system used by BC Cancer for medical and radiation oncology services throughout the province.

We included patients if they had a diagnosis of primary cutaneous invasive melanoma and were treated with a wide local excision and SLNB. We conducted further data abstraction for patients who had a pathologically negative SLNB. We excluded patients younger than 18 years, those with melanomas of the genitalia, and those with unsuccessful SLNBs.

Eligibility for SLNB changed over the time frame of the study and were consistent with prevailing guidelines. These currently state that patients who have clinically lymph node–negative melanomas that are at least American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage T1b (>0.8 mm or ulcerated) be considered for SLNB.16,17 Select patients not meeting these guidelines were potentially offered an SLNB if they had a shave or punch biopsy or had evidence of lymphovascular invasion in the biopsy specimen. Dual tracer was used for all procedures; 30 MBq of filtered technetium-99m sulfur colloid was injected in 4 equal aliquots intradermally around the primary disease site. Dynamic images were acquired routinely for sites on the trunk or head and neck region, followed by static planar images. In select cases, co-registered computed tomography (CT) and single-photon emission CT was subsequently performed to facilitate accurate nodal localization. Lymphazurin blue was injected after induction of anesthesia, generally using 1–2 mL. Sentinel lymph nodes were considered to be all palpable, blue stained, and radioactive lymph nodes. The 10% rule was used to determine the number of sentinel lymph nodes removed, generally restricting the number to 4. Sentinel lymph nodes were serially sectioned at 2–3-mm intervals perpendicular to the long axis of the lymph node and fixed in formalin. Ten serial sections of each cassette were performed with levels 1, 3, and 5 used for hematoxylin and eosin staining, level 6 used for HMB-45 staining, and level 7 used for SOX10 staining.

Clinical care of patients was done without the benefit of a multidisciplinary cutaneous oncology clinic. During the first decade of the study, patients were typically followed by their primary care provider or dermatologist, who performed general skin and ad hoc cancer surveillance. Later in the study period, patients were selectively followed every 6–12 months for 2–3 years by the primary investigator, with selective use of surveillance regional ultrasonography.

We collected patient demographic, clinical, and pathologic characteristics. Pathological variables collected included mitotic rate, satellitosis, ulceration, lymphovascular invasion, Breslow thickness, and histologic type. We used the AJCC Cancer Staging Manual, Eighth Edition, for pathologic variables.18 Surgical variables collected included the number of sentinel and non-sentinel lymph nodes removed, as well as the lymph node basin or basins where the SLNB was performed. The date of disease recurrence after SLNB was identified and categorized as distant, local, in-transit, or lymph nodal. We combined local and in-transit recurrence (LITR) into 1 category, given the ambiguity in differentiating between them. Lymph node recurrences were classified as exclusivity in the lymph node basin (lymph node only), both in the lymph node basin and LITR (regional only), and distant and suspected in the lymph node basin (overall).

The FNR addressed the regional recurrences that were in the lymph node basin only or in association with LITR. We calculated the FNR as the number of false-negative SLNBs, divided by the total of false-negative SLNBs and true-positive SLNBs. We used descriptive statistics to examine patient baseline variables of interest, stratified by isolated nodal recurrence. We used the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test to assess the associations with categorical variables, and the Wilcoxon ranksum test to compare continuous variables.

We used competing risk analysis to evaluate isolated nodal recurrence. We calculated length of follow-up from the date of the SLNB to the date of a pathologically proven lymph node or systemic recurrence. Patients were censored for lymph node disease recurrence within 3 months of the identification of systemic metastasis, as regional disease is unlikely to be pathologically confirmed or adequately documented beyond this time point. Length of follow-up in patients without recurrence was based on the date of last follow-up with an oncology care provider or imaging of the nodal basin. We classified systemic metastases as competing events. We used the Gray test to compare the cumulative incidence of isolated nodal recurrence between strata. We estimated median follow-up time using the reverse Kaplan-Meier method. We estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) from univariate Fine–Gray proportional hazards regression to measure the effect of the association. Multivariate analysis could not be used to assess patient and tumour factors associated with isolated nodal recurrence because of the low number of events. We used SAS version 9.4 for all statistical analysis.

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the University of British Columbia – BC Cancer Agency research ethics board (H20-03423).

Results

Overall, 501 patients underwent SLNBs during the study period. Of these, 97 (19.4%) had pathologically positive sentinel lymph nodes; 404 patients had a pathologically negative SLNB and were the subject of further review. Among these 404 patients, there was a predominance of male patients (59.2%), with a median age of 63 (interquartile range [IQR] 53–72) years (Table 1). The axilla was the most common site for an SLNB (54.2%) and the neck (18.3%) was the least common site. The median Breslow depth was 1.5 mm, with ulceration identified in 25.7% of patients. A median of 3 sentinel lymph nodes were removed per basin, with 11.9% of patients having multiple drainage basins.

Of the 404 patients with a negative SLNB, 84 (20.8%) patients subsequently developed a recurrence of any type, at an estimated median time of 22.3 (95% CI 13.5–34) months (Table 2). Sixty-one (15.1%) patients developed distant metastasis, at a median time of 21.4 (95% CI 13.2–33.7) months, including 38 with only distant recurrence and 23 with concurrent LITR or suspected lymph node recurrence. Twelve (3.0%) patients developed an LITR-only recurrence. Overall, there were 25 suspected recurrences (6.2%) within the primary lymph node basin. Regional-only recurrence occurred in 11 (2.7%) patients, while 14 (3.5%) instances were in conjunction with distant metastases. In those with regional-only recurrences, 9 were in the lymph node only, while 2 followed LITR. The regional FNR was 10.2%. Unadjusted nodal recurrences in the groin, axilla, and head and neck were 3.6% (4 of 110 patients), 2.7% (6 of 219 patients), and 1.4% (1 of 74 patients), respectively (Table 1).

Using the reverse Kaplan–Meier method, the median follow-up time for the entire cohort was estimated at 21.8 (95% CI 13.5–34.0) months. The median follow-up time for isolated nodal recurrence was 14.5 (IQR 10.6–20.4) months. The 1- and 3-year cumulative incidence rates of isolated nodal recurrence were calculated as 1% (95% CI 0.2%–2.7%) and 2.9% (95% CI 1.2%–6.0%), respectively.

On univariate analysis, higher Breslow depth was the only variable predictive of isolated nodal recurrence, both as a categorical variable (p = 0.009) and in the context of AJCC tumour stage among those with a thickness greater than 2.0 mm (p = 0.03) (Table 1). Patient sex, number of sentinel nodes removed, lymph node basin, ulceration, and remaining pathologic variables had no significant effect on the FNR. The 3-year cumulative incidence rate of isolated nodal recurrence was 1% (95% CI 0.1%–4.7%) for those with a Breslow thickness of 0.1–2.0 mm and 5.9% (95% CI 2.1%–12.5%) for those with a Breslow thickness greater than 2.0 mm (HR 4.12, 95% CI 1.11–15.33) (Figure 1).

Discussion

The pathologic status of the regional lymph node basin is a strong prognostic indicator of outcome among patients with melanoma. Until recently, the status of the regional lymph node basin was the main pathologic factor used for selection of patients for adjuvant treatment. Although high-risk patients at AJCC clinical stage IIB or IIC without lymph node metastasis may be recommended to have adjuvant immunotherapy,6 the pathologic information obtained from SLNB is still of value for staging and treatment decisions, as well as improving control of regional disease.19,20

There is wide variability in the FNR after SLNB, with a meta-analysis reporting an FNR of 12.5% (range 0%–34%).8 Among studies performed before the year 2009, the FNR was associated with study quality and length of patient follow-up, with contemporary FNRs in the range of 8%–14.5%.9–13 Sinnamon and colleagues10 reported an FNR of 13.7% among patients with truncal and extremity melanomas, identifying increasing age as the only factor influencing FNR. Thomas and colleagues21 provided a comprehensive review of recurrences in patients following a negative SLNB, addressing recurrences as an isolated or multisite phenomenon. They identified a first-site recurrence in the lymph node basin in 2% of patients, which was less common than first-site recurrence in a distant (4.1%) or LITR (4.1%) location. The FNR based on the nodal basin as a first site of recurrence was 10.3%, with a median follow-up of 32.1 months. Regional nodal recurrence was more common with head and neck and extremity locations, as well as among melanomas with a Breslow depth of greater than 2 mm and LITR.22,23 Lee and colleagues,13 focusing specifically on regional recurrence, reported an overall FNR of 14.5%, with higher FNRs among those with head and neck melanoma, male patients, and those with LITR. They also found high variability between surgeons (range 2.3%–25.0%), with 1 surgeon identified as having a higher FNR.

Comparing studies that address regional recurrence after a negative SLNB is challenging, given their heterogeneity. Some studies report only the biopsy failure rate, calculated as the proportion of false negatives out of the total number of negative SLNBs (2.9%–5.7%), rather than using the FNR, which is a more objective measure of the procedure.24,25 Furthermore, inclusion and exclusion criteria vary widely. Sinnamon and colleagues10 reported an FNR of 13.7%, but limited their calculations to truncal and extremity melanomas, excluding patients with simultaneous distant metastasis or LITR. In contrast, Yee and colleagues9 reported an FNR of 13.2% among those with the regional nodal basin as the first site of recurrence; the FNR was higher among those with nodal recurrence in conjunction with other distant sites (15.7%), but this excluded those with LITR. Scoggins and colleagues12 reported an FNR of 10.8%, and an adjusted FNR of 7.6% when excluding those with previous LITR but provided no information pertaining to systemic metastasis. The FNR of isolated nodal recurrence in our patient cohort was 10.2%, consistent with the aforementioned studies. We identified all sites of recurrence but restricted the analysis to those with a lymph node–only recurrence or recurrence after the detection of LITR for calculation of FNR. Patients were censored upon identification of distant metastasis, given our inability to confidently document simultaneous or subsequent regional disease in this setting. Furthermore, simultaneous distant metastasis represents a biologically distinct disease status, with a vastly different prognosis, as well as different priorities and management strategies. This approach allowed for the best comparison between our report and others, recognizing inconsistent exclusion criteria and institutional follow-up protocols (i.e., frequency and imaging), which are frequently not specified in studies. Ongoing independent evaluation of surgical quality is important, with the FNR of SLNBs considered to be one of a number of quality indicators for melanoma surgery.14

Higher Breslow thickness was the only variable correlated with regional recurrence in our study, consistent with previous reports.10,12,21,25 Both increasing Breslow thickness (p = 0.009) and a categorical thickness greater than 2.0 mm (p = 0.03) were predictive of recurrence on univariate analysis. This latter finding is consistent with other studies, albeit with differing thresholds (> 1 mm, > 3 mm, and > 4 mm).10,21,25 There are inconsistencies in the literature as to other risk factors for an increased FNR, but LITR12,13 as well as head and neck location,11,13,21–23,26 are frequently reported. We identified LITR in a similar proportion to other studies (5.7%),9,11,21 but did not evaluate their effect on regional recurrence. We evaluated the effect of satellitosis within the primary resection, but it did not increase the FNR. Despite a high proportion of head and neck melanomas in our cohort, recurrence at this site was infrequent (1.4%), which contradicts other reports identifying this location to be associated with a higher FNR.11,13,21,26–28 Patient sex, number of sentinel lymph nodes removed, ulceration, and all remaining pathologic variables appeared to have no effect on the FNR, contrary to other reports.10,25,26 Although our regional recurrence rates appear consistent with other studies, there is also merit in identifying strategies to reduce the FNR. This has been shown to be multifactorial, including issues pertaining to nuclear medicine, intraoperative localization of the sentinel lymph nodes, and pathological identification of the disease, all of which are currently undergoing further evaluation.29–32

The unadjusted length of follow-up in our cohort (median 13.0 [IQR 1.0–41.5] mo) was shorter than in other studies that have comprehensively assessed all-site recurrence among patients with negative SLNBs (range 32.1–51 mo).9,11,21,28 These studies originated from single or multi-institutional settings with ongoing, unspecified clinical follow-up protocols. In contrast, our study used a population-based registry with a nonprescriptive follow-up, but benefitted from comprehensive inclusion of all pathologic cancer diagnoses from a centralized cancer program that effectively captured all patients with clinically recurrent disease. Verver and colleagues28 collectively evaluated 3180 patients with negative SLNBs over a 51-month period; 15.6% of patients developed any-site recurrence over this period, with the first site of recurrence distant in 5.0% of patients, in a regional nodal basin in 4.0% of patients, and local or in-transit in 6.0% of patients. Another study with a median follow-up of 32.1 months identified recurrence in 10.4% of patients (4.1% distant and 2.0% regional).21 In comparison, we identified any-site recurrence in 20.8% of our patients, including distant metastasis in 15.1% of patients and regional recurrence in 2.7% of patients. The identification of 25% more overall recurrence suggests that the effective follow-up of our patients is likely far greater than documented, as those that are recurrence-free represent most of our patient cohort. Furthermore, there is also no obvious plausible biologic difference between our cohort and other reports, with a high SLNB positivity rate (19.4%), and comparable Breslow depth of melanoma (1.5 mm v. 1.7 mm28 and 1.3 mm21) and ulceration, suggesting similar disease burden.

The clinical significance of a false-negative SLNB in patients with melanoma is unclear. Although the survival difference attributable to missed disease in the regional lymph node basin is debatable,1 there is an increased need for a therapeutic lymphadenectomy with its associated morbidity.33 Given improvements in disease-free survival associated with adjuvant immunotherapy,4–6 it is conceivable that these patients may be denied optimal treatment for their disease. Furthermore, although fewer than 5% of patients will develop regional recurrence in the absence of distant disease, timely identification of these patients may be of value to ensure regional disease control and initiation of systemic treatment, which improves disease-free survival. Although the focus of this study was on surgical considerations pertaining to SLNB in melanoma, it is important to recognize that, overall, 20.8% of patients with a negative SLNB developed some form of disease recurrence. This included distant metastasis in 15.1% of patients, reaffirming the recurrence risk associated with lymph node–negative melanoma (typically stage IIB and IIC disease) as addressed by KEYNOTE-716.6 Patients with high-risk, lymph node–negative melanoma may now be offered adjuvant therapy, but metastatic disease identified during SLNB may still allow for better decision-making for these patients, while allowing patients the opportunity to access adjuvant therapy despite being otherwise ineligible.

Currently, little evidence guides surveillance strategies for patients with melanoma either clinically or radiologically.34 Although de-escalation of high-intensity clinical follow-up has proven to be safe among patients with melanoma,35 there is likely benefit to more regimented follow-up than has previously existed in our region. Consideration regarding the role of surveillance imaging is also required; its role has been evaluated in the context of systemic recurrence,36,37 but may be worth investigating in the context of regional disease. In particular, ultrasonography of the regional lymph node basin may be worth considering, especially using a risk-based strategy for patients deemed to be at higher probability of a false-negative SLNB.28 An evaluation of the role of imaging surveillance in patients with high-risk melanoma is underway and may provide some guidance.38

Limitations

This study used a population-based registry, which has a high degree of accuracy at identifying patients with recurrent disease and is not subject to referral bias. We were inclusive of a comprehensive number of pathologic characteristics that are consistently associated with increased FNR, including LITR, as well as head and neck melanomas. The influence of institutional and surgeon variability was also controlled for, given the single facility and surgeon setting. The time frame of this study was before the use of effective adjuvant systemic therapies, with recurrences reflecting tumour biology and the collective accuracy of the procedure in our institution. However, the retrospective nature of this study and the small sample size are notable limitations. We were unable to perform a multivariate analysis because of the small number of events. Although the documented length of follow-up was short, the population-based cancer registry provided a high level of certainty that clinical recurrence had been identified. Even so, patients with subclinical disease may not have been identified or alternatively, may have relocated or been declined medical care.

Conclusion

The FNR associated with SLNB was 10.2%, which is consistent with the literature of patients with melanoma. The recurrence of regional disease was associated with Breslow depth but did not appear to be affected by anatomic location. Consideration should be given to risk-based surveillance strategies following a negative SLNB, given the potential for both regional and systemic recurrences.
