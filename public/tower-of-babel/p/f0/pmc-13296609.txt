Differential effects of SARS-CoV-2-targeted infection of ATII, club cells, and macrophages on lung immunopathology and antiviral responses
Authors: Austin W Todd, Sergey A Shein, Anna A Korchagina, Vasily A Kudinov, Brianna N Faz, Ramya S Barre, Raehum Paik, Emma C Collins, Kizil A Yusoof, Ishana Khetarpal, Korri S Weldon, Zhao Lai, Dias Kurmashev, Jordi B Torrelles, Elizabeth A Leadbetter, Yan Xiang, Yidong Chen, Luis Martinez-Sobrido, Ekaterina Koroleva, Alexei V Tumanov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe specific contributions of lung epithelial and immune cells to SARS-CoV-2–induced immunopathology and antiviral responses remain unclear. To address this, we generated mouse models with inducible expression of human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (hACE2) in specific lung cell types. Infection of mice expressing hACE2 on club cells triggered early type I interferon responses, limited viral replication, and caused transient, mild lung pathology. In contrast, hACE2-driven infection of type II alveolar epithelial cells (ATIIs) resulted in higher viral loads and moderate lung damage, accompanied by expansion of interstitial macrophages, virus-specific CD8+ T cell responses, and production of anti-SARS-CoV-2 IgG. Additionally, infection of mice expressing hACE2 on ATIIs increased CD11c+ CD8+ effector T cells and proinflammatory cytokines in the lungs, including IFN-γ, CXCL9, and CXCL10. However, neutralization of CXCL9 and CXCL10 exacerbated disease severity. Moreover, infection of mice with hACE2 targeted to ATIIs and macrophages led to severe lung injury, increased viral burden, enhanced T-cell and neutrophil infiltration, higher IgG responses, and sustained lung pathology. In contrast, hACE2 expression limited to endothelial cells was insufficient to promote SARS-CoV-2 replication in the lung or cause pathology. Together, these findings demonstrate that ACE2-driven infection of ATIIs, club cells, and macrophages plays distinct but cooperative roles in driving SARS-CoV-2-induced lung immunopathology and antiviral immunity.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, ACE2, Type II alveolar epithelial cells, Club cells, Macrophages, Mouse models of SARS-CoV-2 disease

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is the leading cause of illness and death related to SARS-CoV-2 infection.1,2 The mechanisms leading to SARS-CoV-2-induced ARDS are complex, but increasing evidence suggests that the virus infects respiratory epithelial cells and macrophages, triggering an excessive immune response known as a cytokine storm.2,3 While antiviral immune responses are essential for eliminating infected cells, they can also cause collateral damage to surrounding lung tissue.2,3 Elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF, IL-6, GM-CSF, TNFSF14, CCL2, CCL3, CXCL8, CXCL9, and CXCL10, have been detected in the serum and lungs of patients with severe COVID-19.1,2,4,5 Although vaccines and anti-viral therapies are available and efficacious, targeting the immunopathology induced by SARS-CoV-2 has been challenging. Meanwhile, emerging reports indicate that blocking proinflammatory cytokines, such as IL-6, TNF, IFN-γ, and IL-1, may offer therapeutic benefits for severe COVID-19 cases.2,5 Therefore, separating antiviral immune responses from harmful immune-related effects offers a promising approach to treating severe virus-induced ARDS. Achieving this requires a deeper understanding of how lung epithelial and other resident cells contribute to SARS-CoV-2-induced lung damage and immune responses.

Although type II alveolar epithelial cells (ATIIs) are considered primary target cells for SARS-CoV-2 in the lung parenchyma,6-9 their role in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung immunopathology remains poorly understood. In addition to ATIIs, club cells express high levels of ACE2 and TMPRSS2 and can be infected with SARS-CoV-2.10 Furthermore, mouse models with specific hACE2 expression in ATII cells and club cells exhibit variable degrees of lung pathology.11-14 However, the roles of ATIIs and club cells in triggering the immune response to SARS-CoV-2 and their contributions to lung pathology, beyond providing a niche for virus replication and propagation, remain incompletely understood.

Endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark of acute SARS-CoV-2-induced lung disease and long COVID, contributing to increased capillary permeability, interstitial edema, thrombosis, and perivascular immune cell infiltration.15 However, the extent to which this dysfunction is driven by direct SARS-CoV-2 infection of endothelial cells via ACE2 remains controversial.16-18

In addition to epithelial and endothelial cells, several reports have highlighted the roles of lung alveolar and interstitial macrophages in the pathogenesis of SARS-CoV-2-induced lung disease.19-23 Although both ATIIs and macrophages can produce proinflammatory cytokines upon SARS-CoV-2 infection in vitro,19,20,22,24 their relative contributions to disease pathogenesis and the induction of protective immune responses in vivo remain unclear.

To define the role of ACE2-expressing cells in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology and immune responses, we generated mouse models with conditional hACE2 expression in specific cell types. Our results demonstrate distinct roles for ATIIs, club cells, and macrophages during SARS-CoV-2 infection, as well as their contributions to pathology and antiviral immunity.

Results

Mice with inducible global expression of hACE2 succumb to SARS-CoV-2 infection

Wild-type mice do not develop disease after SARS-CoV-2 infection because the viral Spike (S) protein is not compatible with mouse ACE2 (mACE2).25 To define the roles of direct infection of different lung epithelial and immune cells in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung disease, we generated mice with conditional expression of hACE2 in the Rosa26 locus (R26-LSL-hACE2 mice) (Fig. 1a). This approach enables robust hACE2 expression when R26-LSL-hACE2 mice are crossed with cell-specific or inducible Cre recombinase-expressing mice. Briefly, we used CRISPR-Cas9-based targeting with a guide RNA to the first intron of the Rosa26 locus, previously validated for CRISPR-Cas9 mutagenesis,26 to knock-in hACE2 cDNA under a chicken β-actin promoter with a lox-stop-lox sequence (Fig. 1a). To test whether ubiquitous hACE2 expression in our model promotes SARS-CoV-2 infection and pathology, we crossed R26-LSL-hACE2 mice with R26-CreERT2 mice.27 The resulting hACE2global mice were treated with tamoxifen (TAM) to induce hACE2 expression (Fig. 1b). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis revealed high levels of hACE2 expression in the lung, spleen, brain, and liver of hACE2global mice (Fig. 1c). Interestingly, the relative hACE2 expression in multiple organs of these mice was significantly higher than in well-described K18-hACE2 transgenic mice (K18-hACE2-Tg) (Fig. 1c). Both hACE2global and K18-hACE2-Tg mouse models displayed severe weight loss, with 100% mortality by 6 days post intranasal (i.n.) infection with 1x105 PFU of WA1/US 2020 SARS-CoV-2 (Fig. 1d, e). High levels of viral RNA were detected in the brain and lung of hACE2global mice at 3 dpi, although the highest hACE2 mRNA expression was observed in the liver (Fig. 1f).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histological analysis of the lung revealed diffuse alveolitis with exudate and hemorrhage, consistent with severe SARS-CoV-2-induced pneumonia, and limited pathological changes in the brain, liver, and spleen (Fig. 1g). We observed no significant difference in lung pathology between K18-hACE2-TG and hACE2global mice at 6 dpi (Fig. S1a, b. Immunofluorescent staining for the SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid (N) protein (NP) at 3 dpi showed NP+ epithelial cells in the lung and brain, but not in the liver and spleen (Fig. 1g). We found increased expression of TNF and IL-10 in the lung at 3 dpi, as well as marked upregulation of chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, and CCL2 (Fig. 1h). hACE2global mice also showed increased expression of these chemokines in the brain (Fig. 1i), indicating ongoing neuroinflammation caused by SARS-CoV-2 infection. These data show that targeting hACE2 to the Rosa26 locus supports robust global hACE2 expression in multiple organs and results in 100% mortality after SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Infection of mice with hACE2 expression targeted to ATII cells causes moderate lung pathology and expansion of interstitial macrophages

To define the role of ATIIs in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology, we crossed R26-LSL-hACE2 mice with Sftpc-CreERT2 mice, which express Cre recombinase under the surfactant-associated protein C (SFTPC) promoter, generating hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 2a). Based on the published selectivity of the SFTPC promoter for ATII cells,28 hACE2 mRNA expression was restricted to the lung of hACE2ATII mice following tamoxifen treatment (Fig. 2b). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed colocalization of hACE2 and SFTPC in the lung (Fig. 2c).

We next analyzed virus replication and the susceptibility of hACE2ATII mice to SARS-CoV-2 infection with the ancestral WA1/US 2020 strain (Fig. 2d). We chose intratracheal infection of these mice to deliver virus directly to the lung, based on restricted hACE2 expression (Fig. 2b). Compared with infected hACE2neg mice, hACE2ATII mice showed delayed recovery from body weight loss without mortality (Fig. 2e) and positive SARS-CoV-2 NP staining that colocalized with hACE2+ ATIIs (Fig. 2f). To characterize the kinetics of viral infection, we analyzed lung homogenates for SARS-CoV-2 RBD RNA and found the highest viral RNA levels at 3 dpi. By 9 dpi, the amount of viral RNA detected in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice was comparable to the levels observed in infected hACE2neg mice (Fig. 2g). Viral replication in the lung was confirmed by plaque assay (Fig. 2h) and by elevated levels of subgenomic viral N RNA (Fig. 2i) in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice at 3 dpi, confirming productive infection in these mice. H&E staining of lungs showed features consistent with SARS-CoV-2-induced pneumonia, including consolidation, hemorrhage, and alveolar thickening, with increased pathology scores compared with infected hACE2neg mice (Fig. 2j), similar to pathological findings in severe COVID-19 patients.29

Analysis of cytokine expression in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice at 3 dpi revealed increased levels of Ifng, Ifnb, Il10, and Il6, with a trend toward higher Tnf, whereas Il1b expression was decreased (Fig. 2k). Chemokine expression analysis showed increased levels of Ccl2 and Ccl7, as well as IFN-γ-dependent chemokines Cxcl9 and Cxcl10, and their receptor Cxcr3 (Fig. 2l). Immunofluorescence staining of infected lungs revealed clusters of CD4+ T cells around airways, increased CD68+ macrophages, and increased CXCL10 protein in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 2m). In contrast, intranasal infection with the same dose of the virus resulted in no visible lung pathology (Fig. S2a top row), consistent with the absence of ATIIs in the upper respiratory tract. Together, these findings suggest that hACE2ATII mice develop lung pathology, accompanied by macrophage influx and increased production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines after i.t., but not i.n., infection with the ancestral WA1/US 2020 strain of SARS-CoV-2.

We then tested the sensitivity of hACE2ATII mice to the more aggressive delta variant of SARS-CoV-2, administered i.n. or i.t. As with the WA1/US 2020 SARS-CoV-2 strain, i.t. infection of hACE2ATII mice with the delta strain induced moderate lung pathology without signs of morbidity (Fig. S2a, b) and increased lung viral titers compared with i.n. infection (Fig. S2c). Interestingly, we observed a significant increase in Ifng, Cxcl9, and Cxcl10 expression in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice after both i.n. administration and i.t. infection at 5 dpi (Fig. S2d), suggesting that even mild SARS-CoV-2 infection drives an inflammatory response in the lungs. Thus, these results demonstrate that infection of hACE2-expressing ATIIs in hACE2ATII mice is sufficient to induce moderate lung pathology without lethality, and that this mouse model recapitulates several histologic and transcriptional features of severe COVID-19 lung disease.

SARS-CoV-2 infection of hACE2-expressing club cells facilitates viral replication and causes limited, transient lung pathology

In addition to ATIIs, club cells are another major epithelial cell type in the lung that expresses ACE2 and TMPRSS2 for SARS-CoV-2 entry.10 To investigate the role of club cells in SARS-CoV-2 pathology, we generated a mouse model with specific expression of hACE2 in club cells (hACE2Club mice) by crossing R26-LSL-hACE2 mice with inducible Cre, under the club cell secreted protein 10 promoter (CC10-CreERT2) (Fig. 3a). hACE2 mRNA expression was detected specifically in the lung and trachea, with no expression in other organs (Fig. 3b). Immunofluorescent staining showed hACE2 colocalization with CC10, limited to small bronchioles (Fig. 3c), as expected. Both i.n. and i.t. infections of hACE2Club mice with SARS-CoV-2 led to similar levels of viral replication in the lungs (Fig. 3d, e).

hACE2Club mice showed positive luciferase activity up to 4 days after i.n. infection with a recombinant (r)SARS-CoV-2 expressing nanoluciferase (Nluc), confirming that viral replication was restricted to the lung in these mice (Fig. 3f). In contrast, we did not detect luciferase activity in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice after i.n. infection (Fig. 3f), consistent with the absence of ATII cells in the upper respiratory tract. SARS-CoV-2 replication in the lungs of hACE2Club mice after i.t. infection was confirmed by plaque assay (Fig. 3g). Virus RNA levels in the lungs of hACE2Club mice were reduced at 6 dpi compared with 3 dpi (Fig. 3g). Analysis of cytokine expression in the lung revealed a moderate increase in Ifnb, Il6, Cxcl9, and Cxcl10 at 3 dpi (Fig. 3h). In contrast to hACE2ATII mice, levels of Tnf, Il1b, Ifng, Ccl2, and Il10 expression at 3 dpi were unchanged (Fig. 3h), indicating that SARS-CoV-2 replication in club cells induces a less prominent proinflammatory response than infection of ATIIs. H&E staining of lungs confirmed a moderate degree of lung pathology with peribronchiolar inflammatory infiltrates, more pronounced at 3 dpi than at 5 dpi (Fig. 3i,j,k). Staining with αCD4 antibodies revealed peribronchiolar aggregates of CD4+ T cells in hACE2Club mice at 3 dpi, which resolved by 5 dpi (Fig. 3j, k). These results indicate that ACE2 expression in club cells facilitates transient viral replication in the upper respiratory tract, enables viral transmission from the nasopharynx to the small airways in the lung, triggers early production of type I IFNs, and results in temporary relocalization of CD4+ T cells, leading to transient, mild lung pathology.

Distinct immune changes in the lungs of hACE2ATII and hACE2Club mice after infection

To further delineate the specific roles of ATIIs and club cells in the host response to SARS-CoV-2 infection, we performed bulk RNAseq analysis of lungs from i.t.-infected hACE2ATII and hACE2Club mice at 5 dpi. Transcriptomic analysis revealed 2,312 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice and 703 DEGs in hACE2Club mice compared with their respective uninfected controls (Fig. 4a and Fig. S3), indicating a more prominent role for ATIIs as primary epithelial targets of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Principal component analysis (PCA) of DEGs clearly separated infected and control mice in both mouse models (Fig. 4b), underscoring substantial infection-induced transcriptional reprogramming in the lung. Among the 703 DEGs in hACE2Club mice, 464 overlapped with those identified in hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 4c), further supporting more profound changes in hACE2ATII mice in response to SARS-CoV-2 infection. Most genes upregulated in both mouse models were primarily associated with immune response (Fig. 4d). In contrast, genes involved in cytoskeletal organization and epithelial/endothelial integrity were downregulated (Fig. 4d), indicating structural dysfunction that may compromise lung repair and promote fibrotic remodeling. Notably, hACE2ATII mice exhibited a higher number of immune-related genes and a higher enrichment ratio, suggesting a more vigorous immune response than in hACE2Club mice.

Infected hACE2ATII mice displayed elevated expression of T cell-related genes, including Cd3e, Cd3g, Cd4, Cd8a, Cd28, and activation-induced genes such as Cxcr3, Tnfsf14, Pdcd1, Prf1, Gzmb, Klrg1, Klrk1, Fasl, Icos, and Ctla4. In contrast, hACE2Club mice showed more limited upregulation, with increased expression of Cxcr3, Tnfsf14, and Pdcd1 (Fig. 4e). Gene enrichment analysis further revealed activation of pathways related to T cell receptor signaling, chemokine signaling, IFN-γ response, and cell adhesion, particularly in hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 4f). In addition to T cell activation, genes associated with macrophages, such as Cd68 and Cd226, were also upregulated in these mice (Fig. 4e). Together, these findings indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection elicits a robust immune response dominated by T cell and macrophage activation, primarily driven by infection of ATIIs, with club cells playing a more limited but complementary role.

SARS-CoV-2 infection drives expansion of interstitial macrophages, virus-specific CD8+ T cells and antibody responses in hACE2ATII mice

To determine how selective SARS-CoV-2 infection of ATIIs and club cells affects immune cell populations in the lung, we performed flow cytometry on the lungs of hACE2ATII, hACE2Club, and intercrossed ACE2ATII, Club mice at various time points after i.t. infection (Fig. 5a). The gating strategy is shown in Fig. S4. At 5 dpi, we observed an increase in both the number and percentage of Ly6C− interstitial macrophages (SiglecF−CD64+CD11b+MHCII+) specifically in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice compared to control mice (Fig. 5b, c). Meanwhile, only the percentage of these cells was significantly higher in hACE2ATII, Club mice (Fig. 5c). Additionally, there was a trend toward an increased percentage of Ly6C+ interstitial macrophages in both hACE2ATII and ACE2ATII, Club mice (Fig. 5c). There were no substantial changes in the numbers of B cells or other myeloid populations, including alveolar macrophages, dendritic cells, monocytes, and neutrophils, across all three mouse strains. The number of eosinophils was reduced in hACE2ATII, and hACE2ATII, Club mice (Fig. S5a).

Similarly, total CD8+ and CD4+ T cells in the lung were not significantly altered after infection (Fig. S5a). However, infected hACE2ATII mice showed increased CD11c-expressing CD8+ T cells compared with infected hACE2neg mice (Fig. 5d, e). CD11c expression in CD8+ T cells is associated with effector differentiation during viral infection, tumor immunity, and CD137 stimulation.30,31 Notably, hACE2ATII mice also showed a trend toward increased lung accumulation of CD44+ CD8+ T cells, indicating T cell activation (Fig. 5f, Fig. S5b). Further analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) at 5 dpi revealed elevated total CD8+ and CD4+ T cell frequencies in hACE2ATII BAL, along with a decrease in alveolar macrophages (Fig. 5g). Total BAL cell counts are shown in Fig. S5b. To better characterize virus-specific T cell responses, we used the H2-Kb-VNFNFNGL (S539) tetramer, a dominant S protein epitope (Fig. 5h-k, Fig. S5c). hACE2ATII mice exhibited increased frequencies of S539+CD44+CD8+ T cells in the mediastinal lymph nodes at 5 dpi (Fig. 5h) and in the BAL and lung at 11 dpi (Fig. 5i-j, Fig. S5c), followed by a decline at 21 dpi (Fig. 5k). Up to 40% of these virus-specific CD8+ T cells co-expressed CD69 and CD103, indicating acquisition of a tissue-resident memory phenotype (Fig. 5i).

To determine whether infection of ATIIs is sufficient to induce humoral immunity, we measured S-specific IgG levels in the serum of hACE2ATII mice at 18 and 33 dpi. These mice developed significantly higher virus-specific IgG levels than infected hACE2neg controls (Fig. 5l). Collectively, these results show that SARS-CoV-2 infection of ATIIs is sufficient to drive robust CD8+ T cell responses, including the generation of tissue-resident virus-specific T cells, and to elicit virus-specific systemic IgG responses.

Inhibition of CXCL9 and CXCL10 promotes disease severity in hACE2ATII mice

Gene expression analysis revealed increased levels of the chemokines Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 and their receptor Cxcr3 in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice following SARS-CoV-2 infection (Fig. 2l and Fig. 4e). CXCL9 and CXCL10 expression is induced by IFN-γ and correlates with disease severity in COVID-19 patients.32 CXCL9 and CXCL10 recruit CXCR3+ effector T cells and myeloid cells to sites of inflammation,33,34 and their blockade is considered a potential therapeutic approach for severe COVID-19.35 To determine whether these chemokines contribute to disease progression, we treated hACE2ATII mice during infection with blocking antibodies against CXCL9 and CXCL10 (Fig. S6a). Mice treated with αCXCL9/CXCL10 antibodies exhibited greater weight loss and delayed recovery (Fig. S6b) and a trend toward higher lung viral titers by 6 dpi (Fig. S6c). Additionally, CXCL9/CXCL10 blockade led to increased Il6 expression in the lungs, whereas levels of Cxcl9 and Cxcl10 mRNA remained unchanged (Fig. S6d). Histological analysis revealed increased lung inflammation in αCXCL9/CXCL10-treated hACE2ATII mice compared to controls (Fig. S6e). These findings suggest that CXCL9 and CXCL10 contribute to the protective antiviral response. Accordingly, therapeutic inhibition of the CXCL9/CXCL10 axis may exacerbate SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology by impairing host defense.

ACE2 expression by endothelial cells is dispensable for SARS-CoV-2-induced lung disease

To test whether endothelial expression of ACE2 is sufficient to support SARS-CoV-2 replication and drive lung pathology, we generated endothelial-specific hACE2-expressing mice (hACE2Endo) by crossing R26-LSL-hACE2 mice with Cdh5-Cre mice (Fig. S7a). We did not detect increased viral RNA in the lungs of intratracheally infected hACE2Endo mice compared with infected hACE2neg controls at 5 dpi (Fig. S7b), suggesting limited infection of hACE2+ endothelial cells in vivo. Histological analysis of lung tissue from i.n. or i.t. infected mice revealed no significant pathological changes (Fig. S7c-d and data not shown). Collectively, these findings suggest that endothelial expression of hACE2 is insufficient to induce lung disease. This supports the notion that SARS-CoV-2–associated endothelial dysfunction occurs primarily through indirect mechanisms.

ACE2-specific infection of ATIIs and macrophages cooperates in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology

In addition to ATIIs, lung macrophages and neutrophils are increasingly recognized as key contributors to SARS-CoV-2–induced cytokine production and immunopathology, but whether this is due to direct infection of these cells or activation by infected ATIIs remains largely unknown.20,21,23,36 To determine whether direct ACE2-driven infection of macrophages contributes to lung inflammation and viral replication in vivo, we generated macrophage-specific hACE2-expressing mice (hACE2M) by crossing R26-LSL-hACE2 mice with LysM-Cre (Fig. S8a), which is predominantly active in macrophages and neutrophils.37 hACE2 expression in these mice was primarily detected in CD68+ cells, confirming Cre activity in macrophages (Fig. S8b). Following i.t. SARS-CoV-2 infection, hACE2M mice exhibited robust viral replication in the lungs, as indicated by expression of virus RNA, genomic, and subgenomic N RNA (Fig. S8c). Infected hACE2M mice displayed elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including Ifng, Tnf, Ccl2, Ccl7, Cxcl9, and Cxcl10 (Fig. S8d), perivascular inflammation, moderate lung damage, (Fig. S8e), and substantial infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and neutrophils (Fig. S8f). Interestingly, hACE2M mice also showed increased Il10 expression in the lungs (Fig. S8d), which may further promote virus replication in macrophages,21 while Il6 expression remained unchanged. Following SARS-CoV-2 infection, we observed colocalization of the SARS-CoV-2 N protein with CD68+ cells, further confirming the predominant viral replication in hACE2-expressing macrophages (Fig. S8g). Consistent with published in vitro data demonstrating the lack of productive infection of neutrophils,38,39 we did not find colocalization of SARS-CoV-2 nucleocapsid and neutrophil marker MPO (myeloperoxidase) in the lungs of infected hACE2M mice (Fig. S8h).

Because LysM-Cre exhibits residual activity in ~ 25% of ATIIs,40 infection of hACE2-expressing ATIIs may contribute to the pathology observed in hACE2M mice. To clarify the relative contributions of macrophages and ATIIs, we generated double-transgenic hACE2ATII, M mice by crossing hACE2ATII and hACE2M strains (Fig. 6a). Compared with hACE2ATII mice, hACE2ATII, M mice exhibited significantly higher viral titers and increased expression of both genomic and subgenomic viral N RNA at 5 dpi (Fig. 6b-c). Fluorescent staining for NP revealed colocalization of virus with CD68+ macrophages and other hACE2+ cells, likely ATIIs (Fig. 6d). We also found markedly elevated cytokine and chemokine levels, including Ifng, Tnf, and Cxcl10, in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice compared with control and hACE2ATII mice at 5 dpi, whereas expression of Ccl2, Ccl7, and Cxcl9 was comparably induced in both hACE2-transgenic strains (Fig. 6e). Histological examination revealed a trend toward more severe lung pathology, with extensive perivascular and peribronchiolar immune infiltrates in hACE2ATII, M mice compared with hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 6f-g). We also observed focal areas of dense inflammatory consolidation in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice, with complete effacement of the alveolar architecture not seen in hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 6f). Immunophenotyping showed sustained infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and CD68+ macrophages in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice, whereas hACE2ATII mice exhibited only a transient increase in macrophages at 5 dpi (Fig. 6f). Notably, neutrophil infiltration, assessed by MPO staining, was significantly higher in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice (Fig. 6f, Fig. S8i), suggesting a role for macrophage infection in neutrophil recruitment to the lungs. This observation was further supported by increased expression of neutrophil-recruiting chemokines Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice, compared to hACE2ATII mice (Fig. S8j). These findings suggest that hACE2-expressing macrophages facilitate increased SARS-CoV-2 replication and exacerbate lung inflammation by producing proinflammatory cytokines and promoting the recruitment of T cells and neutrophils. Interestingly, while virus replication in the lungs of hACE2ATII mice was reduced to the levels observed in control mice by 9 dpi (Fig. 2g), we detected virus replication in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice at 12 dpi (Fig. 6h), accompanied by sustained lung pathology and increased expression of Ifng and Cxcl9 (Fig. 6i-j), suggesting that infection of macrophages can delay virus clearance and resolution of pathology.

Flow cytometry analysis of immune cell populations in the lungs of hACE2ATII, M mice at 5 dpi revealed marked changes in immune cell populations compared with hACE2neg mice (Fig. S9a, b), consistent with immunofluorescent staining (Fig. 6f). Infected hACE2ATII, M mice exhibited significant increases in neutrophils, CD4+ T cells, monocytes, and dendritic cells, as well as increased interstitial macrophages in the lungs (Fig. S9). In contrast, we did not observe an increase in lung neutrophil numbers in infected hACE2ATII mice (Fig. S5a). These results suggest that immune cell recruitment and lung immunopathology are mainly driven by hACE2-mediated infection of macrophages, rather than ATIIs.

To further define how hACE2-dependent infection of macrophages contributes to virus-specific immune responses, we analyzed virus-specific CD8+ T cells in the BAL and virus-specific IgG at 12 dpi. hACE2ATII, M mice exhibited increased percentages of infection-induced CD11c+ CD8+ T cells and S539+CD44+ CD8+ T cells in the BAL, compared with infected hACE2neg controls, although we did not find a significant increase in these cells in hACE2ATII, M mice compared with hACE2ATII mice (Fig. 6k). We also found significantly higher levels of RBD-specific IgG in the serum of hACE2ATII, M mice (Fig. 6l). These results suggest that infection of macrophages not only supports virus replication in the lung and contributes to lung immunopathology but also cooperates with ATIIs in promoting virus-specific host immune responses.

Discussion

This study aimed to clarify the roles of infection of various lung cell populations in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology and in antiviral host immune responses. To achieve this, we used an in vivo approach by creating mouse models with cell-specific hACE2 expression. Our results indicate that selective infection of hACE2-expressing ATIIs increases viral replication, triggers the production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, promotes the accumulation of interstitial macrophages, and stimulates the development of virus-specific IgG and effector T cell responses, leading to moderate lung damage. Conversely, infection of club cells induces early type I IFN production and causes only limited, transient lung damage. hACE2-expressing macrophages undergo productive SARS-CoV-2 infection in vivo, serving as key mediators of proinflammatory cytokine release, immune cell infiltration, and immunopathology, while also promoting antiviral immune responses. In contrast, hACE2 expression in endothelial cells is dispensable for SARS-CoV-2 replication and lung damage. These findings highlight the distinct roles of ATIIs, club cells, and macrophages in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung damage and host immune responses.

The main advantage of our approach is the ability to target specific host cell types in vivo, enabling precise identification of their roles in both pathogenic and protective responses during SARS-CoV-2 infection. While mouse-adapted SARS-CoV-2 strains41 are helpful for studying host responses, therapies, and vaccines, infection of multiple ACE2-expressing cells complicates efforts to discern the contributions of each cell type. Additionally, our approach allows us to test currently circulating variants directly, without mouse adaptation. Our results indicate that ubiquitous expression of hACE2 in hACE2global mice results in robust viral replication across multiple organs, including the brain, marked production of proinflammatory cytokines, and 100% mortality by 6 dpi, consistent with previous reports in mice with ubiquitous hACE2 expression.13,14

ATIIs are considered a key epithelial cell type in the lungs and may contribute to lung immunopathology during SARS-CoV-2 infection, given their high expression of ACE2 and TMPRSS2, along with increased damage and impaired regeneration of ATIIs in the lungs of severe COVID-19 patients.7-9,42 However, whether ATIIs are critical drivers of the cytokine storm and lung damage in vivo remains unclear. In vitro infection of pluripotent stem-cell-derived ATIIs induced inflammatory signaling.43 In contrast, ex vivo infection of human lung explants showed that ATIIs had limited ability to produce inflammatory cytokines.44 Our results suggest that i.t. infection of hACE2ATII mice with the ancestral WA1/US 2020 strain of SARS-CoV-2 is sufficient to promote viral replication and moderate alveolar damage with features consistent with human disease, including alveolar thickening, consolidation, and increased macrophages.8,29 Our flow cytometry data showed that this increase was driven by the expansion of interstitial macrophages, which have been shown to contribute to proinflammatory signaling in SARS-CoV-2-infected human lung explants.36 In contrast to i.t. SARS-CoV-2 infection, i.n. infection of hACE2ATII mice does not support viral replication or lung immunopathology, presumably because the virus cannot reach the alveolar space when delivered intranasally. Consistent with a previous study,11 our findings did not detect significant lung pathology in mice with selective expression of hACE2 on ATIIs after i.n. administration. However, infection with a more aggressive delta SARS-CoV-2 variant induced moderate lung pathology even after i.n. administration.14

Consistent with this, expression of IL-6, IFN-γ, CCL2, CCL7, CXCL9, and CXCL10 was increased in hACE2ATII mice, suggesting that selective ATIIs infection is sufficient to produce these cytokines and chemokines in the lung. The ability of ATIIs to produce IL-6 in response to SARS-CoV-2 infection was previously demonstrated in vitro,45,46 although the capacity of ATIIs to produce CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL2, and CCL7 is less clear.47 It is possible that early IL-6 production by ATIIs triggers CXCL9, CXCL10, CCL2, and CCL7 production by other cells, such as macrophages and neutrophils. In fact, high levels of these chemokines were detected in the lungs of hACE2M mice, whereas IL-6 levels were not increased. CXCL9 and CXCL10 are associated with disease severity, and their blockade is considered a potential therapeutic approach for severe COVID-19.35 However, our data show that blocking CXCL9 and CXCL10 increases viral replication and lung damage in hACE2ATII mice. This suggests that inhibiting these chemokines to reduce the cytokine storm may not be effective, as it could weaken host immune responses. Our findings also indicate that hACE2 expression on ATIIs is sufficient to generate virus-specific CD8+ T cells and to induce IgG production. Therefore, separating harmful responses from protective ones driven by ATIIs continues to be a challenge.

Club cells are non-ciliated epithelial cells in the distal airways and are known to have immunomodulatory properties and to contribute to protection against respiratory infections.48,49 Our results suggest that selective infection of hACE2-expressing club cells in hACE2Club mice after i.n. or i.t. infection promoted transient virus replication but was insufficient to induce severe lung pathology. A possible explanation is that infection of club cells alone was insufficient to drive significant expansion of interstitial macrophages or to induce a strong proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine response, as observed in hACE2ATII mice. A robust early induction of type I IFNs and protective chemokines, such as CXCL9 and CXCL10, in these mice may also help explain the limited lung pathology.

The role of ACE2-dependent and ACE2-independent mechanisms in SARS-CoV-2-induced endothelial dysfunction and lung pathology remains debated.17,18,50,51 Our results in hACE2Endo mice align with a recent report that used another Cre to drive hACE2 expression in endothelial cells,16 demonstrating that hACE2 expression is dispensable for virus replication and lung pathology. These results suggest that endothelial dysfunction arises from indirect activation of endothelial cells by proinflammatory cytokines rather than from direct viral infection.

Macrophages are central to COVID-19 pathogenesis, and their activation is associated with a cytokine storm.52,53 However, it remains unclear whether direct infection of macrophages is responsible for cytokine storm and subsequent lung immunopathology. Several in vitro studies indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection of macrophages is nonproductive.54,56 Conversely, more recent experimental data from human autopsy tissues and ex vivo lung explant infection demonstrated the productive viral infection in macrophages.36,57,58 Our results show that ACE2-mediated infection of macrophages in hACE2ATII, M mice leads to increased viral titers and lung immunopathology, consistent with human explant studies.36,57,58 Specifically, comparing hACE2ATII with hACE2ATII, M mice allowed us to further define the direct contribution of hACE2-driven infection of macrophages to lung immunopathology. Our results showed that hACE2ATII, M mice exhibited increased viral replication, lung pathology, and production of proinflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, TNF, CXCL1/2, and CXCL10. Increased cytokine production was associated with increased lung infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, monocytes, and neutrophils, which formed large peribronchiolar aggregates. Given the role of CXCL10 in recruiting CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and neutrophils to infected tissues,33,34,59 and the role of CCL2 in recruiting monocytes,36,60 increased levels of these chemokines in hACE2ATII, M mice are consistent with increased lung immunopathology. Severe COVID-19 is characterized by infiltration of immature proinflammatory monocytes52 and tissue damage precipitated by hyperactivated neutrophils and NETosis.22,23,61 Our data support the idea that ACE2-mediated SARS-CoV-2 infection of macrophages promotes the recruitment of monocytes and neutrophils to the lung. hACE2 expression on macrophages also promoted sustained viral replication and lung pathology, with persistent viral replication at 12 dpi and prolonged pathology, compared with hACE2ATII mice. These results highlight the role of ACE2-driven infection of macrophages in promoting SARS-CoV-2 replication, increased induction of proinflammatory cytokines, immune cell recruitment, and lung pathology.

Since LysM-Cre is active in both alveolar and interstitial macrophages,62 our experiments with hACE2M mice could not separate the individual contributions of these macrophage subsets in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung immunopathology. Future studies using a more selective targeting strategy would be required to delineate the relative role of alveolar versus interstitial macrophages in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung disease. In addition to the pathogenic role of macrophage infection in lung immunopathology, our results suggest that ACE2-mediated infection of macrophages promotes the generation of antiviral IgG responses. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting macrophages need to be weighed against the potential side effects, including impairment of protective IgG antibody responses.

Based on our findings, we propose a model that delineates the distinct roles of ACE2-expressing ATII cells, club cells, and macrophages in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology and antiviral responses (Fig. 7). The relative contributions of these hACE2-expressing cells to lung immunopathology and host antiviral responses are summarized in Fig. S10. Overall, these findings indicate that ACE2-expressing ATII cells, club cells, and macrophages play distinct but cooperative roles in driving SARS-CoV-2-induced lung immunopathology and antiviral immunity.

Model of the coordination of ATIIs, club cells, and macrophages in SARS-CoV-2-induced lung pathology and the host immune response.

SARS-CoV-2 can infect several ACE2-expressing cell types in the lung, each contributing uniquely to both lung immunopathology and protection. 1) SARS-CoV-2 infects club cells in the bronchioles, supporting early virus replication and induction of type I IFN, which promotes viral clearance with minimal recruitment of CD4+ T cells to the bronchiolar epithelium and limited lung pathology, resolving by day 5. SARS-CoV-2 replication in club cells leads to viral spread to the lower airways and infection of ATIIs and alveolar macrophages. 2) SARS-CoV-2 infection of ATII cells leads to increased viral replication and production of IL-6 and IFN-γ-dependent CXCL9 and CXCL10 chemokines, which support recruitment and activation of interstitial macrophages and CD8+ T cells, including the effector CD11c+ CD8+ T cell subset. SARS-CoV-2 infection of ATIIs is sufficient to induce moderate lung pathology, generation of virus-specific CD8+ T cells, and IgG production. Inhibition of CXCL9 and CXCL10 leads to increased lung pathology. 3) Additional infection of macrophages further increases viral replication and production of CXCL1/CXCL2, TNF, CCL2, and CCL7. These cytokines further recruit neutrophils, monocytes, and T cells, resulting in prolonged lung pathology and increased antiviral antibody production.

Methods

Mice.

Male and female mice (10–12 weeks old) were used for these studies. R26-CreERT2 mice (Jax 008463), Sftpc-CreERT2 (Jax 028054), CC10-CreERT2 mice (Jax 016225), LysM-Cre (Jax 004781), and Cdh5-Cre (Jax 006137) were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory and intercrossed with R26-LSL-hACE2 mice. To induce hACE2 expression in specific strains, 10- to 12-week-old adult mice were treated with 5 mg of tamoxifen (TAM) in 100 μL of corn oil containing 10% ethanol by oral gavage for 4 consecutive days, as described.63 Both sexes were used for the experiments. Mice were used for experiments one month after TAM treatment, unless otherwise specified. Mice housing and experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC protocol #20210084AR) of the University of Texas Health Science Center, San Antonio. Age- and sex-matched controls or littermate controls were used for all experiments.

Cells and viruses.

SARS-CoV-2 isolates were propagated in VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells in a BSL3 laboratory. Briefly, VeroE6-TMPRSS2 cells were plated in a T175 flask in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and allowed to reach 90% confluence. VeroE6 cells were inoculated with 0.001 or 0.01 multiplicity of infection (MOI) of SARS-CoV-2 WA1/2020 and monitored for cytopathic effect (CPE). At 48–72 h post-infection, the supernatant was collected, centrifuged for 10 min at 1000 x g, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. Virus concentration (PFU/ml) was determined by plaque assay.

SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Mice were infected with 1x105 PFU of SARS-CoV-2 WA1/US 2020 in 50 μl, either i.t. or i.n., as indicated in the figure legends. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 90 mg/kg of Ketamine and 10 mg/kg Xylazine via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. For i.t. infection, anesthetized mice were placed on an intubation platform, the tongue was held with sterile forceps, and a fiberoptic light source was used to visualize the vocal cords and ensure proper placement of a 20-gauge catheter into the trachea and delivery of the viral stock. For i.n. infection, anesthetized mice received 25 μl of viral stock into each nostril. Infected mice were monitored daily to record body weight and survival.

In vivo rSARS-CoV-2 imaging.

In vivo bioluminescence imaging of live mice was performed using an Ami HT in vivo imaging system (IVIS; Spectral Instruments), as described.64 At 1, 2, 4, and 6 dpi, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and retro-orbitally injected with 100 μL of Nano-Glo luciferase substrate diluted 1:10 in PBS. Mice were immediately placed in an isolation chamber and imaged with the Ami HT IVIS. Radiance within the region of interest (ROI) of each mouse was analyzed using the Aura software (Spectral Instruments), and total flux values (protons/s) were normalized to the background signal of mock-infected control.

Pathology scoring.

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded lung tissues were sectioned at 5 μm and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for pathology scoring, as described.65 5 random 10x fields were selected and scored blind to mouse genotype. Scores ranged from 0 to 4 and were defined as follows: 0, normal histology; 1, blood vessel congestion and/or perivascular/peribronchiolar infiltration; 2, alveolar wall congestion; 3, localized alveolitis with airspace infiltration, hemorrhage, or exudate; and 4, diffuse alveolitis. Scores from each mouse were averaged, and statistical significance was assessed using Student’s T-test.

Statistical Analysis.

All statistics were calculated using GraphPad Prism (ver. 10). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test, the Mann-Whitney test, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s correction, or the unpaired Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Survival was assessed using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) and Gehan-Breslow-Wilcoxon tests. Not significant, p > 0.05 (ns); p < 0.05 (*); p < 0.01 (**); p < 0.001 (***); p < 0.0001 (****). Data are presented as means ± SEM unless otherwise specified.
