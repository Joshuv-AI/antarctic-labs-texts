Gut microbiota-derived trimethylamine N-oxide contributes to cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury via the tRF-Glu–ANT1–GSDMD axis
Authors: Tao Wang, Weibin Ren, Xinzhe Chen, Xueyao Wang, Miaomiao Liu, Yanhui Zhang, Zhaoshui Li, Yanbo Wang, Zhiqiang Song, Hongyan Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsRecent research has shown that the gut microbiota arrests the progression of myocardial infarction (MI) by modulating immune inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolism. However, the mechanism by which gut-derived trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) promotes cardiomyocyte pyroptosis following MI remains unclear.Methods and resultsWe found that a high-choline diet exacerbated cardiac injury in mice by disrupting the intestinal barrier. Under high-choline conditions, the expression levels of tRF-1:31-Glu-TTC-2 (tRF-Glu) derived from tRF and tiRNAs (tsRNAs) were elevated, serving as a key target for intervention in cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. Loss of tRF-Glu significantly ameliorated TMAO-induced deterioration of myocardial fibrosis and cardiac function. Mechanistically, tRF-Glu directly binds to the mitochondrial inner membrane protein ANT1 and stabilizes its expression by inhibiting ubiquitination. Cardiomyocyte knockdown of ANT1 significantly blocked the generation of TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte mitochondrial reactive oxygen species, restored cardiomyocyte membrane potential, and reduced mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that tRF-Glu inhibits the ubiquitination of ANT1 under the induction of TMAO, which in turn activates gasdermin D (GSDMD) and mtDNA release, accelerating cardiac remodeling. In conclusion, our study provides new insights into the role of the gut microbial metabolite-driven tRF-Glu–ANT1–GSDMD pathway in blocking cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00873-4.Keywords: Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), tRF-1:31-Glu-TTC-2 (tRF-Glu), ANT1, Mitochondrial DNA, Cardiomyocyte pyroptosis

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) significantly contributes to global mortality and is characterized by the instantaneous loss of cardiomyocyte function and eventual development of heart failure [1, 2]. Myocardial injury can trigger cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and immune-inflammatory modulation [3]. Although myocardial injury triggers a strong inflammatory response that is necessary for cardiac repair, the loss of a large number of cardiomyocytes induces irreversible myocardial fibrosis, leading to dysfunction of cardiac mitochondrial structure and function, which in turn exacerbates pathological cardiac remodeling. Given the insufficient renewal capacity of adult cardiomyocytes, it is essential to actively halt the process of cardiomyocyte pyroptosis after myocardial loss and salvage functional cardiomyocytes for myocardial repair.

Bidirectional communication between the gut and cardiovascular system plays a key regulatory role in the microbiome, immune inflammation, and multiple cardiometabolic diseases [4, 5]. During cardiovascular pathophysiology, impaired intestinal barrier function and bacterial translocation lead to a systemic inflammatory response that exacerbates myocardial injury. Trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) is generated from choline, betaine, and carnitine via gut microbial metabolism. Plasma TMAO levels are independently associated with an increased risk of poor cardiovascular disease prognosis and can trigger cellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammatory signalling pathways, and pathological processes in various metabolic diseases [6–11]. However, the mechanism of action of gut-derived TMAO in MI remains unclear. In chronic disease, TMAO promotes vascular calcification through activation of NLRP3 inflammasome and nuclear factor (NF)-κB signalling, revealing a potential link between intestinal metabolites and vascular calcification [12]. Ultrastructurally, TMAO-treated cardiomyocytes exhibited glycogen accumulation, increased mitochondrial fragmentation, and lipofuscin deposition, indicating altered cellular energy metabolism and increased oxidative damage [13]. Thus, TMAO may induce programmed death of cardiomyocytes. However, the mechanism through which gut-derived TMAO regulates mitochondria-dependent cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury remains unclear.

tRNA-derived fragments (tRFs) and tRNA half-fragments (tiRNAs), also known as tsRNAs, are a class of small tRNA-derived RNAs whose precursors are specifically cleaved to form novel regulatory RNAs, including tRFs and tiRNAs [14–19]. tsRNAs exert their biological effects through various mechanisms, including gene expression regulation, cell cycle control, chromatin regulation, and epigenetic modifications [19–21]. In patients with MI, the expression profiles of tsRNAs are significantly altered, suggesting that tsRNAs are key targets for interventions in the diagnosis and management of cardiovascular disease. Recently, researchers found for the first time that significant 8-oxoguanine (o8G) modification of tsRNAs occurs in patients with pulmonary hypertension, elucidating the mechanism of o8G site-specific modification of tRF-1-AspGTC in pulmonary hypertension vascular remodeling and providing a theoretical basis for its use as a new potential hematological biomarker for the diagnosis of pulmonary hypertension [22]. Furthermore, the expression profiling of tsRNAs in patients with atherosclerosis revealed that tRF-2 was expressed only in healthy controls, whereas tRF-3b expression was significantly reduced in patients with atherosclerosis. Our study revealed that plasma TMAO levels were accompanied by significant changes in the expression profiles of tRF and tiRNAs (tsRNAs) in mice fed with a high-choline diet. Subsequently, we identified a tsRNA (tRF-Glu) associated with the TMAO-induced pyroptosis of cardiomyocytes. However, the mechanism through which tRF-Glu regulates TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis remains unclear.

Cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and inflammation play key roles in the pathophysiology of myocardial injury [23–29]. Gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated cardiomyocyte pyroptosis is a key event during myocardial ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, and inhibition of the caspase-11/GSDMD pathway significantly reduces cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and I/R-induced myocardial injury [30]. NLRP3 inflammasome activation and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis play crucial roles in myocardial I/R injury. In cardiomyocytes, NLRP3 or caspase-1 inhibitors significantly inhibit the pyroptosis process [31, 32]. Recently, researchers have shown for the first time that GSDMD-mediated mitochondrial damage is irreversible and that signal amplification occurs. Moreover, activated GSDMD binds to mitochondrial cardiolipin to form mitochondrial pores, disrupting the mitochondrial membrane and leading to the release of ROS, cytotoxic mediators, and oxidative phosphorylation disorders, which play a key role in cell pyroptosis and inflammation [33]. Adenine nucleotide translocase 1 (ANT1) is localized in the inner mitochondrial membrane, and it is crucial for the regulation of physiological functions, such as mitochondrial ATP/ADP transport, opening of the mitochondrial membrane permeability transition pore, maintenance of mitochondrial membrane potential, and mitochondrial autophagy [34–37]. This study found that TMAO induced elevated levels of ANT1 gene expression, and its deletion promoted mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leakage, preventing cell pyroptosis and progression of myocardial injury. Our findings provide new insights into the search for downstream intervention targets of gut flora metabolites to block cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury.

Methods

Animals and procedures

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Jinan Microecological Biomedicine Shandong Laboratory (Approval No. 202416). The animal experiments described in this study were conducted in compliance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of laboratory animals. The animals selected for this experiment were the C57BL/6 J line of experimental mice, which were purchased from the Pengyue Animal Research Institute in Jinan, Shandong Province, China. All animals were maintained under standard laboratory conditions, following a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with unrestricted access to tap water and food. In this study, C57BL/6 J male mice were treated with 1.2% choline added to standard rodent chow and a 3-week mouse choline diet for MI surgery. Left ventricular function and structure were assessed using two-dimensional echocardiography. To observe the effect of TMAO on antibiotic (ABX) treatment, male C57BL/6 J mice were randomly administered intraperitoneal injections of TMAO (100 μmol/kg/day) for 2 weeks. For the VX-765 treatment, VX-765 (50 mg/kg) was administered to the mice via intraperitoneal injection 20 min after MI. After the experiment, all animals were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation (1.5–2%) and euthanized via cervical dislocation.

To assess the therapeutic effects of tRF-Glu suppression on cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury, C57BL/6 J mice were fed a high-choline diet for 3 weeks. The tRF-Glu antagomir (anta) and its negative control (NC) were administered intravenously before MI surgery. A single dose of 10 mg/kg was administered to each mouse twice weekly. The experimental groups included the Sham-Ctrol, Sham-Cho, MI-Cho, MI-Cho + NC, and MI-Cho + anta groups. The tRF-Glu interfering sequence was synthesized by GenePharma and injected into mice mixed with a neutral lipid material (invivo-siRNA mate), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The adeno-associated virus of ANT1 (AAV-shRNA ANT1, shANT1) and its control (AAV-shRNA NC, NC) were designed and synthesized from Obio Technology Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). For knockdown of ANT1 in vivo, adult mice were injected with shANT1 and shNC (5 × 1011vg/mouse) via tail vein 2 weeks before MI. Heart tissue was collected 2 weeks after aden-associated virus injection. Fresh cardiac tissue was immediately immersed in electron microscopy fixative and stored at 4 °C. The heart tissue underwent embedding and sectioning. Finally, images were captured and analyzed using a transmission electron microscope (HITACHI HT7700 80 kV) to examine the ultrastructural morphology of cardiac mitochondria.

To determine whether a high-choline diet promotes cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac damage in a gut microbiota-dependent manner, we performed an oral broad-spectrum ABX clearance assay in mice. Vancomycin (0.125 mg/mL) (1404-94-9, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), neomycin (0.25 mg/mL) (1405-10-3, Macklin, China), metronidazole (0.25 mg/mL) (443-48-1, Solarbio, China), and ampicillin (0.25 mg/mL) (7177-48-2, Solarbio, China) were mixed in drinking water and given to mice at random for 7 days to deplete their gut microbiota. After treatment with the ABX cocktail, mice were placed in a bacteria-free environment for subsequent experiments. After the experiment, all animals were anesthetized using isoflurane inhalation (1.5%-2%) and euthanized via cervical dislocation.

MI in adult mice

In this experiment, adult mouse MI model was established by permanent ligation of the left anterior descending (LAD) branch of the coronary artery. Briefly, mice were anesthetized continuously with 1.5–2% isoflurane via a respiratory device and body temperature was maintained at 37 °C throughout the procedure. The mouse thoracic cavity was opened in a suitable position, and most of the heart was exposed using a chest expander. The LAD was ligated 2–3 mm distal to the ascending aorta with nylon sutures (6–0) to establish the MI model in adult mice. For the measurement of infarct size, we calculated MI size by measuring the arc length of endocardial and epicardial infarcts and endocardial and epicardial circumferences by using NIH imagej software. The MI size was calculated as [(endocardial infarct rate + epicardial infarct rate)/2] × 100.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed transthoracically several days after surgery in a mouse model of MI using a small animal ultrasound imaging system (Vevo 3100, Fujifilm Visualsonics) equipped with a 30–70 MHz MX550 scanning head. Two-dimensional guided M-mode images were recorded in parasternal long-axis view at the level of the papillary muscles in mice. Systolic and diastolic indices of the mouse heart were calculated using established standard formulae. All measurements consisted of more than three consecutive cardiac cycles and were averaged. Cardiac function was assessed by left ventricular ejection fraction (EF), left ventricular fractional shortening (FS), left ventricular end-systolic diameters (LVDs), left ventricular end-diastolic diameters (LVDd).

Quantification of plasma TMAO and choline

Quantification of plasma TMAO and choline by stable isotope dilution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Briefly, the present invention uses C57BL6/J male mice supplemented with 1.2% choline in a standard rodent chow diet, and plasma TMAO expression levels were assayed in mice on a high choline diet for 3 weeks. Mouse plasma samples (n = 5) were thawed at 4 °C, isotopic internal standard and precooled acetonitrile/water solution (9/1, v/v) were added and vortexed and mixed to extract metabolites. Chromatographic peak areas and retention times were extracted by Multiquant 3.0.2 software. The retention time was corrected using standards of the target substances for metabolite identification and quantitative analysis.

Isolation of neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes

Primary neonatal mouse ventricular cardiomyocytes were isolated from the ventricles of 1-day-old C57BL/6 J mice. Briefly, neonatal mouse hearts were removed with scissors and placed in a Petri dish with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) buffer and placed on ice. Then, the hearts were thoroughly washed 2–3 times and the heart tissue was cut into pieces with surgical scissors. Snipped heart tissue fragments were digested 7–8 times with 0.2 mg/mL trypsin (P-3292, Sigma) and 1.0 mg/mL of type II collagenase (LS004176, Worthington) at 37 °C. The residue was filtered through a cell sieve and incubated in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM/F12; Gibco, USA) containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 1–1.5 h at differential rates to remove most of the noncardiomyocytes. Finally, cardiomyocytes were collected, resuspended in DMEM/F12 containing 5% FBS, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h before being used for subsequent experiments. Cardiomyocytes were seeded into 24-well plates for continued culture. At 24 h after transfection, the cardiomyocytes underwent immunofluorescence staining and were observed using laser confocal microscopy. Statistical analysis was performed on no fewer than 300 cells per group.

Transfection of overexpression and inhibitor sequences in vitro

tRF-Glu mimic and its control (mimic-NC) were transfected with cardiomyocytes. Cellular pyroptosis proteins, ATP levels, and LDH release were assessed at the indicated times. Under hypoxic conditions, cardiomyocytes were harvested at designated time points (0, 3, 6, 12, 24 h) for subsequent experiments. ANT1 small interfering RNA (siANT1) and its negative control (siNC) was synthesized by GenePharma with the sequence: CAGGGCAUCAUCAUCUACATT (sense); UGUAGAUGAUGAUGCCCUGTT (antisense). siANT1 and siNC (50 nM) were transfected into cardiomyocytes and cultured for an additional 24 h. Cardiomyocytes were collected at the indicated time points after 6 h of hypoxia. The ANT1 overexpression construct was prepared by Gema Biotech using the pcDNA3.1 vector. After 24 h of cardiomyocyte culture, anta and the ANT1 overexpression vector were cotransfected into the cells, followed by a further 24 h of culture.

Analysis of tRF and tiRNA sequencing data

Following the euthanasia of mice in the MI-choline and MI-control groups, the hearts were harvested and subjected to tiRNA and tRF sequencing. The purity and concentration of the total RNA samples were determined using a NanoDrop ND-1000. Total RNA samples were pretreated to remove RNA modifications that interfered with the construction of the small RNA-seq library. Total RNA of each sample was sequentially ligated to 3′ and 5′ small RNA adapters. cDNA was synthesized and amplified using Illumina proprietary RT primers and amplification primers. Subsequently, approximately 134–160 bp PCR-amplified fragments were extracted and purified by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Finally, the completed libraries were quantified using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The libraries were denatured and diluted to a loading volume of 1.3 ml and a loading concentration of 1.8 pM. Diluted libraries were loaded onto a reagent cartridge and subjected to sequencing on an Illumina sequencer according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Raw sequencing data generated by the Illumina sequencer were used for subsequent analyses. Trimmed reads were aligned, allowing for one mismatch with the mature tRNA sequences, and then reads that did not map were aligned, allowing for one mismatch only to precursor tRNA sequences, using bowtie software. The remaining reads were aligned, allowing for one mismatch only in the miRNA reference sequences, using miRDeep2. Based on statistical alignment analysis (mapping ratio, read length, and fragment sequence bias), we determined whether the results could be used for subsequent data analysis. The expression profiles and differentially expressed tRFs, tiRNAs, and miRNAs were calculated. Principal component analysis, correlation analysis, pie plots, Venn plots, hierarchical clustering, and volcano plots were performed for the expressed tsRNAs in the R or Perl environment for statistical computing and graphics. The abundance of tsRNAs was evaluated using sequencing counts and normalized as counts per million of the total aligned reads (CPM). For each tRF and tiRNA, the expression level was estimated using the mapped read number and the given ID. tsRNAs were filtered if the CPM was less than 20 in all samples. Differentially expressed tRFs and tiRNAs were analyzed using the R package edgeR. Fold change (cutoff 1.5), P value (cutoff 0.05 performed only when replicated) were used to screen differentially expressed tRFs and tiRNAs.

Histological analyses

The removed cardiac and intestinal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and after a series of graded ethanol dehydration, xylene transparency, the tissues were finally embedded in paraffin. For histological analysis, the sample tissues were serially paraffin sectioned at a thickness of 7 μm. Intestinal tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (HE) (C0105, Beyotime, Shanghai). To examine and assess the level of myocardial fibrosis, heart tissue sections were stained using Masson’s’s trichrome staining (G1340, Solarbio, Beijing). Sections of intestinal and cardiac tissue were scanned and analyzed by fluorescent tissue imager (NanoZoomer S60).

Myocardial evans blue dye staining

Mice were administered Evans blue dye (EBD) at a concentration of 100 mg/mL (1 g dissolved in 100 mL physiological saline) via intraperitoneal injection at a dose of 100 µg/g body weight. After 4 h, MI surgery was performed on the mice. At 24 h postmyocardial infarction, mice were euthanized. Ventricular myocardial tissue was harvested, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT), and cryopreserved. Cardiac tissue sections were prepared at 7 µm thickness. Cardiac muscle cells were labelled with cTnT (green), and myocardial necrosis with EBD (red). Observations and imaging were performed using a laser confocal microscope.

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization assay

RNA fluorescence in situ hybridization (RNA-FISH) was employed to determine the subcellular localization of tRF-Glu within cardiomyocytes. The tRF-Glu biotin-probe was synthesized by GenePharma, with the following sequence: TCCCATATGGTCTAGCGGTTAGGATTCCTGG. In brief, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well in culture dishes and cultured overnight. The cells subsequently underwent fixation, permeabilization, blocking, and probe incubation. Finally, after DAPI staining and mounting, the cells were observed using laser confocal microscopy.

Distribution of tRF-Glu in the nucleus/cytoplasm

Following the manufacturer’s instructions, we utilized the Nuclear/Cytosol Fractionation Kit (Biovision, Inc., CA) to separate the cytoplasm and nucleus from primary mouse cardiomyocytes, subsequently extracting total RNA from the respective fractions. Subsequently, we detected the expression levels of tRF-Glu using specific primers.

Immunofluorescence assay

Mouse heart or intestinal tissues were taken and immediately embedded in OCT, cryosectioned in a frozen sectioning machine, and then the tissue sections were cryopreserved. Tissue sections of mouse samples were dried at room temperature for 10–15 min and washed three times with PBS buffer for 3 min each.Tissue sections were fixed after 4% paraformaldehyde fixation, permeabilized with 0.1% TritonX-100, and incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Then, the blocking solution was removed and primary antibody was added and incubated for 2 h or overnight at 4 °C. Cardiomyocytes were labelled with mouse cardiac-specific troponin T (cTnT, 1:500, Abcam), and 4’, 6’-diamidinophenylindole (DAPI, 1:1000, Invitrogen) was used to label cardiomyocyte nuclei. Rabbit anti-GSDMD (NBP2-33422, NOVUS, 1:200), anti-Caspase-1 (A16792, Abclonal, 1:200), anti-ANT1 (A20842, Abclonal, 1:200), anti-TFAM (A3173, Abclonal, 1:200), anti-ZO-1 (21773-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200), and anti-Muc-2 (27675-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200) were used as primary antibodies for immunofluorescence. TRITC-conjugated Goat anti-rabbit lgG (AS039, Abclonal, 1:200) and FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (AS037, Abclonal, 1:200) were used for fluorescent secondary antibody incubation. Finally, diluted DAPI dye was used to drop onto the tissue surface, and the nuclei were stained for 5 min, washed three times with a drop of anti-fluorescence quencher, and observed and photographed under a laser confocal microscope.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

Total RNA was obtained by collecting treated cardiomyocytes or myocardial tissue. Sample total RNA reverse transcription was performed using RNA reverse transcription kit (11139ES60, Yeasen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After cDNA synthesis, qPCR was performed on a QuantStudio3 instrument system. Data were processed and analyzed according to the qRT-PCR amplification reaction. DNA isolation and mtDNA copy number assay

For cardiomyocyte mtDNA copy number, total DNA was extracted from cardiomyocytes using a universal genomic DNA kit (CW2298S, CWBIO, Beijing, China) and analyzed by qRT-PCR with 10 ng of DNA. The mtDNA copy number was measured using the mitochondria-encoded cytochrome c oxidase subunit 2 (COX2) and normalized to ribosomal protein s18 (Rps18).

Measurement of intracellular mtROS

Cardiomyocyte mtROS levels were detected using laser confocal microscopy. Pretreated cardiomyocytes were incubated with MitoSOX (2 µM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Hoechst 33,258 dye (10 µg/mL, Beyotime, China) for 30 min at 37 °C and washed twice with PBS. Finally, confocal microscopy was performed for detection and analysis.

Western blot assay

Treated cardiomyocytes or cardiac tissues were lysed by adding protease inhibitor-containing cell lysis solution (R0020, Solarbio) on ice for 20~30 min, and the supernatant was collected and added to protein uploading buffer, and the protein samples were boiled immediately and stored at –20 °C. Protein samples were subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gel electrophoresis at the indicated voltages for protein separation. Subsequently, the protein gel was transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane for a controlled time of 60 min. It was blocked with 5% skimmed milk powder for 1 h at room temperature. The membrane is incubated with enhanced chemiluminescence detection kit (P10200, NCM Biotech) and developed with a Ultra-sensitive multifunctional imager (AI800, cytiva).

RNA pull down assay

Biotinylated tRF-Glu (tRF-Glu-Biotin) and its control sequence (NC-Biotin) were designed and synthesized by Gemma Pharmaceuticals Technology Co. Cardiomyocytes were transfected with tRF-Glu-Biotin and NC-Biotin by lip 3000 transfection reagent, and cells were collected by incubation for 48 h. The collected cells were added to 750 μL cell lysis buffer lysate (2% NP40, 40 mM Tris–HCl, 200 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM DTT), lysed at 4 °C for 30 min, and the supernatant was taken and set aside. Streptavidin–Biotin (SA-Biotin) magnetic beads were added to the cell lysis complex and incubated at 4 °C overnight with shaking. The samples were washed several times through High Salt Buffer and Low Salt Buffer, 5 × protein loading was added to the samples and boiled for 5 min. The tRF-Glu-Biotin-bound products were resolved by SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, and the protein gel bands were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. Finally, the specific protein bands will be analyzed by LC–MS/MS (APTBIO, Shanghai, China) mass spectrometry.

LC–MS/MS analysis

Biotinylated-labelled tRF-Glu was transfected into cardiomyocytes, and tRF-Glu-binding protein complexes were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for LC–MS/MS analysis. Solution A was a 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution, and Solution B was a 0.1% formic acid acetonitrile aqueous solution (acetonitrile at 84%). After equilibrating the column with 95% Solution A, the samples were loaded onto a trap column using an autosampler. Following chromatographic separation, the samples were subjected to mass spectrometry. Detection was performed in positive ion mode with a parent ion scan range of 300–1800 m/z. The primary mass spectrometer resolution was set to 70,000 at 200 m/z, with an automatic gain control target of 1 × 106, a maximum injection time of 50 ms, and a dynamic exclusion time of 30.0 s. Mass-to-charge ratios for peptides and peptide fragments were acquired as follows: 20 fragment spectra (MS2 scans) following each full scan. The MS2 Activation Type is HCD, with an isolation window of 2 m/z, secondary mass spectrometry resolution of 17,500 at 200 m/z, normalized collision energy of 27 eV, and underfill of 0.1%. The raw mass spectrometry files were searched against the corresponding databases using the MaxQuant 1.6.14 search engine to yield the final identified proteins.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

Cardiomyocytes cultured for 24 h were collected and added to 750 μL cell lysis solution and lysed on ice for 30 min and the supernatant was collected. The cell lysate was divided into two portions, and 5 μg IgG and ANT1 antibody were added to each portion and incubated at 4 °C overnight. Next, 100 μL of agarose beads were taken and 50 μL of lysate containing IgG and ANT1 antibodies were added to each tube and incubated for 6 h at 4 °C with shaking. The agarose beads were then washed using cell lysis and centrifuged three times at 4 °C, 500 g. Resuspend the agarose bead complexes in TRIzol RNA Extraction Reagent (1 mL) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and extract the RNA for qRT-PCR quantification.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

The effect of tRF-Glu on ANT1 ubiquitination was assessed by Co-IP assay. Briefly, cardiomyocytes were transfected with tRF-Glu mimic (tRF-Glu) and its negative control (NC) for 24 h. Treated cardiomyocytes were collected and lysed on ice for 30 min using Co-IP lysis solution (270 mM NaCl, 40 mM Tris–Hcl Tris, 1 mM EDTA, 2% NP40, 20% glycerol). ANT1 or IgG antibody was added to the cell lysis complex and incubated at 4 °C overnight. At the end of the incubation, Protein A/G agarose beads (sc-2003, santacruze) were added and incubated for 4–6 h at 4 °C with gentle shaking to couple the antibody to the agarose beads. At the end of the reaction, the coprecipitated protein complexes were centrifuged at 4 °C, 3000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, the binding of ANT1 and ubiquitinated molecules was detected by western blot.

Cell viability assays

Cellular ATP levels and LDH release were measured to assess mitochondrial damage and loss of cell membrane integrity. Briefly, cardiomyocyte ATP levels were measured with an ATP assay kit (MA0440, Meilunbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cardiomyocyte LDH levels were measured with the LDH Assay Kit (BC0685, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as arithmetic mean ± standard deviation (SD) and statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Statistical significance between the two groups was assessed using an unpaired, two-sided t-test. Comparisons between multiple groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s multiple comparison test or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s multiple comparison test. P < 0.05 indicates a statistically significant difference.

Results

High-choline diet aggravates intestinal disturbances and myocardial pyroptosis injury in mice

Previous studies have shown that a high-choline diet leads to elevated circulating TMAO levels. However, the specific mechanism by which it exacerbates postinfarction myocardial injury remains unclear. We first confirmed whether a choline diet in mice is directly related to intestinal homeostasis and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. The 8-week-old C57BL/6 J mice were fed a high-choline diet (1.2% choline) for 3 weeks, followed by MI surgery, and intestinal and cardiac functions were assessed at the indicated time points (Fig. 1A). Given that the integrity of the intestinal barrier is strongly associated with myocardial injury, we evaluated the effects of a choline diet on intestinal homeostasis in mice before and after myocardial injury. Mice continuously supplemented with choline for 3 weeks showed significantly increased plasma levels of TMAO and choline (Fig. 1B and C). Furthermore, we found that the gross morphological and histological pathology scores of the gastrointestinal tract were significantly higher in choline-fed mice than in controls, and this pathology was further increased after MI (Fig. 1D and E). Subsequently, analysis of cardiac function phenotypes showed that cardiac left ventricular ejection fraction and left ventricular fractional shortening were significantly decreased in mice on a high choline diet after MI compared with those in mice on a normal diet (Fig. 1F, Supplementary Fig. 1A). Moreover, left ventricular end-diastolic internal diameter was increased in parallel with left ventricular end-systolic internal diameter (Supplementary Fig. 1B and C), and the degree of myocardial fibrosis was augmented (Fig. 1G). To elucidate the extent of cardiomyocyte damage in mice fed a high-choline diet, we assessed the levels of ATP and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) in the myocardial tissue. Our results showed reduced myocardial ATP levels (Fig. 1H) and increased LDH release (Supplementary Fig. 1D) in mice fed a high-choline diet compared with those in controls. Previous studies have demonstrated that in vivo injection of TMAO significantly accelerates the deterioration of cardiac diastolic function [38]. However, we found no significant alterations in cardiac diastolic function (Supplementary Fig. 2A), markers of myocardial hypertrophy (Supplementary Fig. 2B and C), or myocardial cross-sectional area (Supplementary Fig. 2D) in mice after 4 weeks on a high-choline diet. We hypothesized that short-term high-choline diets under physiological conditions are unlikely to significantly affect cardiac diastolic function in mice owing to compensatory mechanisms in the heart.

Given that myocardial injury leads to chronic circulating inflammation in mice, we examined cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. The activation of cardiomyocyte caspase-1 and expression of GSDMD were exacerbated after a choline diet in mice during myocardial injury (Fig. 1I and Supplementary Fig. 3A–D). However, compared with that in mice fed a normal diet, no significant difference was observed in the expression of cardiomyocyte caspase-11 in the cardiac tissue of mice fed the high-choline diet (Supplementary Fig. 3E and F). A significant increase in the area of myocardial damage was detected by Evans blue dye (EBD) staining of heart tissue sections in the choline group (Supplementary Fig. 1E). In addition, interleukin (IL)-18 mRNA levels were expressed in mice on a high-choline diet, but IL-1β mRNA was not affected (Supplementary Fig. 1F and G). Collectively, these data suggest that disruption of the intestinal barrier further aggravates cardiomyocyte pyroptosis in mice fed with a high-choline diet.

High-choline diets facilitate cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury dependent on gut microbes.

During myocardial injury, the gut microbes promote TMAO formation in the host circulatory system. To determine whether the exacerbation of myocardial injury by a high-choline diet is gut microbiota-dependent, we orally administered broad-spectrum ABX to mice 7 days before MI surgery. Briefly, ABX was added to the drinking water of mice to predeplete the gut microbiota before injury and increase TMAO in the rescue experiments (Fig. 2A). We first investigated the effects of detecting intestinal homeostasis by ABX treatment during a choline diet in mice after MI surgery. Following ABX treatment, the expression of Muc-2 (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. 4B) and ZO-1 (Supplementary Fig. 4A) in the mouse intestine was significantly enhanced. In addition, significant changes were observed in the cardiac function of mice treated with ABX. Our study showed a significant recovery of cardiac function in mice after gut microbial clearance. However, this phenomenon was significantly reversed by TMAO supplementation (Fig. 2C–E). Consistent with this, MI size was significantly reduced after ABX treatment (Fig. 2F). EBD staining showed that the number of EBD-positive cardiomyocytes was reduced after ABX treatment (Fig. 2G, H). Cardiomyocyte LDH and IL-18 release was significantly reduced (Fig. 2I, J). In contrast, myocardial ATP levels were significantly increased in ABX-treated mice (Fig. 2K). Taken together, ABX eliminated the high-choline diet-induced increase in intestinal and cardiac parameters in mice by inhibiting the production of circulating TMAO through the inhibition of gut microbes.

TMAO triggers hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis

Consistent with this finding, we observed that the GSDMD expression in cardiomyocytes increased over time under hypoxic conditions (Supplementary Fig. 5A-C). To elucidate the mechanism of TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte death, we found that a cell pyroptosis inhibitor (VX-765) significantly blocked necrosis in cardiomyocytes. However, apoptosis inhibitors (Emr) and ferroptosis inhibitors (Fer-1) showed no significant effects (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Gut-derived TMAO significantly increased the expression of cardiomyocyte GSDMD, Caspase-1, and IL-18 in response to pathological stimuli. To demonstrate the contribution of TMAO to the cell death effects of hypoxia, we added VX-756 under hypoxic conditions in the absence of TMAO. Under hypoxic conditions, VX-765 inhibited the TMAO-induced cell membrane damage (Supplementary Fig. 5E). However, VX-765 treatment reversed the expression of cellular pyroptosis markers (Supplementary Fig. 5F-K). During myocardial injury, VX-765 significantly reduced the high-choline diet-induced increase in EBD-positive cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig. 6C) and MI size (Supplementary Fig. 6A). In addition, VX-765 promoted the recovery of cardiac function and myocardial ATP levels (Supplementary Fig. 6B and D). To elucidate the mechanism linking cardiomyocyte pyroptosis to high-choline diets, we assessed the effects of NLRP3 knockdown on choline-induced myocardial injury. We found that NLRP3 deficiency significantly suppressed GSDMD expression in the myocardium of mice following induction of a choline-rich diet while markedly improving cardiac function (Supplementary Fig. 6E and F). These results suggest that circulating TMAO aggravates myocardial pyroptosis and pathological cardiac remodeling, which was significantly reversed by VX-765.

tRF-Glu contributes to cardiomyocyte pyroptosis during a high-choline diet

To unravel the potential regulatory molecules responsible for the increase in circulating TMAO due to a high-choline diet, which in turn induces myocardial pyroptosis, we used mouse tRF and tiRNA sequencing to detect tsRNA expression levels in the hearts of choline + MI and control + MI mice and analysed them bioinformatically (Fig. 3A). Under pathological conditions, the expression profile of tsRNAs was significantly upregulated in high-choline diet mice compared with that in normal diet mice (Fig. 3B). Pie chart data analysis showed differences in the number of enriched tsRNAs (Fig. 3C) and differential typing between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 7A). Volcano plots showed significantly differentially expressed tsRNAs between the two groups (Fig. 3D). tsRNA expression was significantly upregulated in the hearts of choline + MI mice (Supplementary Table 1). The tRF-5c types are tRF-1:30-Gly-GCC-1, tRF-1:31-Glu-TTC-1, tRF-1:31-Glu-TTC-2, tRF-1:32-Cys-GCA-1-M3, tRF-1:30-Glu-TTC-1, and tRF-1:32-Glu-TTC-1. The tiRNA-5 types identified were tiRNA-1:33-Gly-CCC-1, tiRNA-1:34-Val-TAC-1, tiRNA-1:34-Val-CAC-2, and tiRNA-1:34-Val-AAC-1. Among these, tRF-1:31-Glu-TTC-2 (referred to as tRF-Glu) was the most upregulated among the many differentially expressed tsRNAs (Fig. 3E). We observed a significant difference in tRF-Glu expression between MI and healthy control mice (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Moreover, RT-qPCR revealed that tRF-Glu expression increased in a dose- and time-dependent manner (Supplementary Fig. 7C and D). Among the downregulated tRNAs, tRF-29:42-Leu-TAG-1-M3 ( tRF-M3) was the most significantly affected, exhibiting a marked downregulation in the choline group (Supplementary Fig. 7J). However, tRF-M3 did not significantly modulate the TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte injury (Supplementary Fig. 7K). Furthermore, FISH experiments showed that tRF-Glu was predominantly expressed in the cardiac cytoplasm, with secondary distribution in the nucleus (Fig. 3F and G). Next, we examined tRF-Glu expression in the heart. qRT-PCR showed that tRF-Glu was predominantly concentrated in cardiomyocytes rather than in fibroblasts (Fig. 3H). To clarify the effect of tRF-Clu on cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, we performed functional studies of tRF-Clu in vitro. Cardiomyocytes were transfected with tRF-Glu mimics and their NC for 24 h. The expression of tRF-Glu significantly increased, as detected by qRT-PCR (Fig. 3I). Furthermore, forced expression of tRF-Glu significantly inhibited cellular ATP levels (Fig. 3J) and increased cardiomyocyte pyroptosis proteins GSDMD and caspase-1 (Fig. 3K and Supplementary Fig. 7F–I). LDH release further increased (Supplementary Fig. 7E). These results suggest that a high-choline diet promotes tRF-Glu expression, which exacerbates myocardial injury by activating cardiomyocyte pyroptosis.

Loss of tRF-Glu arrests the progression of cardiomyocyte pyroptosis in mice fed a high-choline diet

Given that tRF-Glu expression levels were significantly elevated in mice fed a high-choline diet, we hypothesized that tRF-Glu is a key target affecting cardiac function. First, we administered tRF-Glu inhibitors to mice fed a high-choline diet before MI surgery. At 24 h after injection, qRT-PCR revealed that the expression of tRF-Glu was significantly inhibited (Fig. 4A). Further findings showed that cardiac LDH release was reduced (Fig. 4B) and ATP levels were significantly elevated (Fig. 4C) after tRF-Glu inhibition compared with those in controls. Phenotypic analysis of cardiac function showed that the in vivo inhibition of tRF-Glu expression significantly improved cardiac function (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 8A–C) and reduced myocardial necrosis (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 8D) and MI size (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. 8E). To elucidate the relationship between tRF-Glu and mitochondrial damage, we measured the levels of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS). We found that tRF-Glu inhibition significantly reduced mtROS production (Fig. 4G). In addition, fluorescence analysis showed that the accumulation of GSDMD and Caspase-1 was significantly increased in the cardiomyocytes (Supplementary Fig. 8F–H). Taken together, these data suggest that tRF-Glu exacerbates myocardial injury by activating cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and that blocking tRF-Glu expression in vivo significantly ameliorates TMAO-induced pathological cardiac remodeling. In conclusion, these data confirm that tRF-Glu acts as a novel regulator of cardiomyocyte pyroptosis.

tRF-Glu interacts with ANT1 and stabilizes its expression

To clarify the mechanism by which tRF-Glu regulates pyroptosis in cardiomyocytes, we performed RNA pull-down assays (Fig. 5A). Biotinylated-labelled tRF-Glu was transfected into cardiomyocytes, and the tRF-Glu-binding protein complexes were separated by SDS-PAGE and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue for LC–MS/MS analysis (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Table 2). Mass spectrometry showed that DDX3X and ANT1 were significantly enriched. Further in vitro validation showed that TMAO significantly promoted mitochondrial ANT1 expression; however, no significant change was observed in DDX3X expression (Fig. 5C). Mass spectrometry data and western blotting showed that ANT1 acts as a mitochondrial transporter protein that binds directly to tRF-Glu (Fig. 5D and E). ANT1 is a transmembrane protein embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane that regulates mitochondrial structure, energy transport, and cellular inflammation [39]. Therefore, we focused on the regulatory role of mitochondrial ANT1 in TMAO-induced myocardial pyroptosis. RIP assays further validated that tRF-Glu interacted with ANT1 (Fig. 5F).

Next, to further investigate the mode of action of tRF-Glu in regulating ANT1, western blotting and qRT-PCR showed that overexpression of tRF-Glu in cardiomyocytes promoted the protein expression of ANT1 without affecting ANT1 mRNA levels (Fig. 5G).

Next, we treated cardiomyocytes with the protein synthesis inhibitor, cycloheximide (CHX), to clarify the effect of tRF-Glu on ANT1 expression. The results showed that overexpression of tRF-Glu in cardiomyocytes prolonged the half-life of ANT1 after CHX treatment, suggesting that tRF-Glu enhances the stability of ANT1 (Fig. 5H). Since the ubiquitin–proteasome system is a major pathway for intracellular protein degradation, it is involved in more than 80% of intracellular protein degradation and has important pathophysiological implications in cardiovascular disease [40]. Thus, the accumulation of endogenous ANT1 in cardiomyocytes transfected with tRF-Glu was significantly higher than that in the NC group after treatment with the proteasome inhibitor, MG132, suggesting that tRF-Glu inhibits the proteasome-dependent degradation of ANT1 in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 5I). Cardiomyocyte overexpression of tRF-Glu significantly inhibited the binding of ANT1 to ubiquitinated molecules in immunoprecipitation assays (Fig. 5J and Supplementary Fig. 9A). To further demonstrate that tRF-Glu regulates ANT1 ubiquitination and stabilizes its expression, we transfected the tRF-Glu antagomir (anta) and its NC under TMAO induction. TMAO significantly suppressed the ubiquitination levels of ANT1. However, the suppression of tRF-Glu significantly increased the ubiquitination levels of ANT1, regardless of the presence or absence of TMAO (Supplementary Fig. 9B). Furthermore, RNA FISH experiments on TMAO-treated cardiomyocytes revealed an increased colocalization of tRF-Glu with ANT1 (Supplementary Fig. 9C). These data suggest that tRF-Glu stabilizes ANT1 protein via ubiquitin–proteasome-dependent degradation.

Suppression of ANT1 attenuates TMAO-induced myocardial mitochondrial damage in vitro

To investigate the regulatory role of ANT1 in TMAO-induced pyroptosis of cardiomyocytes, we inhibited ANT1 expression in cardiomyocytes (Fig. 6A). ANT1 plays an essential role in mitochondrial membrane potential and energy metabolism. We previously demonstrated that ANT1 protein levels significantly increased in TMAO-treated cardiomyocytes, suggesting that ANT1 is involved in regulating TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. Next, we explored whether ANT1 regulates TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and mitochondrial damage. Cardiomyocytes were transfected with siRNA against ANT1 (siANT1) and its negative control (siNC) to inhibit ANT1 expression. Our study showed that TMAO exacerbated mtROS expression during hypoxic injury in cardiomyocytes. However, cardiomyocyte knockdown of ANT1 significantly prevented TMAO-induced mtROS generation in cardiomyocytes and increased their membrane potential (Fig. 6B–E). During the onset of cellular pyroptosis, a severe cellular inflammatory response is often induced, decreasing mitochondrial membrane potential. Overall, we found that TMAO exacerbated mitochondrial depolarization and decreased membrane potential, which is an early event in pyroptosis.

We demonstrated that tRF-Glu and ANT1 regulate cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac injury and that tRF-Glu promotes the protein expression of ANT1. Next, we verified the regulatory relationship between tRF-Glu and ANT1 during the TMAO-induced pyroptosis of cardiomyocytes. To test this hypothesis, tRF-Glu antagomir (anta) was transfected into cardiomyocytes with or without ANT1, and the cells were then exposed to TMAO. This interaction blocked cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and inflammatory signalling pathways, ameliorated pathological cardiac remodeling, and played a critical regulatory role in the treatment of cardiovascular diseases.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that the gut microbial-derived metabolite TMAO is associated with major adverse cardiovascular events [42–44]. Given that GSDMD-mediated cardiomyocyte pyroptosis is a key event in myocardial I/R injury, we hypothesized that TMAO drives this process. TMAO promotes the progression of atherosclerotic lesions by promoting pyroptosis of vascular endothelial cells through the upregulation of SDHB/ROS [45]. Therefore, we focused on exploring the effects of TMAO on mitochondrial function and cardiomyocyte pyroptosis during myocardial injury. The recently developed nonlethal (microbe-friendly) trimethylamine cleavage enzyme inhibitors fluoromethylcholine and 3,3-dimethyl-1-butanol prevent trimethylamine formation and reduce plasma TMAO levels [42, 46]. However, the stability and efficacy of these small-molecule inhibitors remain a concern. More importantly, the interventional targets of gut microbial-derived TMAO that promote myocardial injury remain unclear. In our study, mice fed a high-choline diet exhibited high levels of circulating TMAO, which contributed to mtDNA translocation and increased levels of mitochondrial superoxide in cardiomyocytes, ultimately exacerbating cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and cardiac remodeling. Previous studies have reported that TMAO increases cardiac fibrosis after MI [47]. However, it is highly likely that the process of myocardial injury greatly increases the risk of intestinal barrier disruption and flora translocation [5]. We found that the overall gut length and redox status were impaired in mice fed a high-choline diet, further exacerbating myocardial injury and circulating inflammatory levels. ABX-induced inhibition of gut microbes significantly attenuated myocardial inflammation and myocardial infarct size. In the add-back experiments, mice administered ABX and TMAO reversed this alteration.

In the present study, a high-choline diet exacerbated cardiac fibrosis and was associated with myocardial pyroptosis after MI. Furthermore, to accurately assess the impact of a high-choline diet on MI damage in mice, the infarct area measured 14 days postinfarction reflected myocardial cell loss caused by the infarction. By 28 days postinfarction, stable scar tissue had formed in the mice, reflecting the state of cardiac remodeling at this stage. These two time points are more conducive to evaluating the long-term therapeutic effects of a high-choline diet. Under hypoxic conditions, TMAO triggers the development of cardiomyocyte pyroptosis after myocardial injury. The cardiomyocyte pyroptosis inhibitor VX-765 significantly inhibited TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis; however, the inhibitor of apoptosis did not have significant effects. Furthermore, the signalling pathway involved in TMAO-induced myocardial pyroptosis is selective. In vitro, TMAO activated the expression of caspase-1 and IL-18, whereas caspase-11 and IL-β had no effect.

Although research on tsRNAs has focused primarily on their role in cancer, a growing number of studies have explored the relationship between tsRNAs and cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, MI, and pulmonary arterial hypertension [48]. The expression of tsRNAs varies in these diseases and may be regulated by epigenetic factors, tsRNA structures, and RNA-binding proteins. The identification of potential regulators of a high-choline diet leads to an increase in circulating TMAO, which in turn induces myocardial pyroptosis. tRF and tiRNA sequencing revealed that, tRF-Glu was most prominently expressed after a high-choline diet. Given that the expression profile of tsRNAs is significantly altered in myocardial injury [49], we investigated their roles in cardiovascular disease. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify a tRF-Glu of tsRNA origin as a molecule involved in TMAO-induced myocardial pyroptosis. Next, the expression of tRF-Glu in different tissues was examined and was found to be highly expressed in the heart. By designing FISH probes, we performed subcellular localization experiments using tRF-Glu. tRF-Glu was predominantly expressed in the cytoplasm of cardiac myocytes, with a secondary distribution in the nucleus. qRT-PCR showed that tRF-Glu was mainly concentrated in the cardiomyocytes rather than in the fibroblasts. Because tsRNAs often interact with proteins and regulate gene expression, these data provide a strong foundation for subsequent studies.

tsRNAs affect cellular functions by regulating gene expression, translation, intercellular signalling, and immune responses. tsRNAs play key regulatory roles in apoptosis, pyroptosis, necrosis, ferroptosis, and autophagy, providing new perspectives for the prevention and diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases [19, 50–52]. A novel tsRNA-5008a promotes ferroptosis in cardiomyocytes, leading to susceptibility to atrial fibrillation and structural remodeling [53]. In response to cellular injury or microbial infection, the assembly of NLRP3 inflammasome vesicles induces cellular pyroptosis and the release of inflammatory cytokines [20]. Further, 5′-tsRNAs recruit DDX3X proteins to stress granules, thereby inhibiting the interaction of DDX3X with NLRP3 and the activation of NLRP3 inflammatory vesicles. Under specific stress conditions, tRF binds to small ribosomal subunits to inhibit peptidyltransferase activity and attenuate translation. Thus, tsRNAs are promising targets for the treatment of cellular inflammation-related diseases. However, the specific pathways through which tsRNAs trigger cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and inflammation remain unclear. Through molecular interaction experiments, we found that tRF-Glu binds directly to the mitochondrial protein ANT1 and affects its expression. The mitochondrial ADP/ATP carrier, ANT1, also known as adenine nucleotide translocase, imports ADP into the mitochondrial matrix and exports ATP, a key step in oxidative phosphorylation [49]. Recently, we have identified a subpopulation of cells called C5, which is strongly associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptotic features, and determined that ANT1 expression is increased in the failing heart and that ANT1 downregulation improves mitochondrial function and inhibits apoptosis [37]. In contrast, we found that the deletion of ANT1 prevented TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis by reducing mtROS and mtDNA translocation. In previous studies, ANT1 was not identified as a mitochondria-dependent regulator of myocardial pyroptosis. Our findings indicate that ANT1 undergoes dose-dependent regulation by tRF-Glu. This mechanism stabilizes ANT1 expression by inhibiting its ubiquitination. Reduced ANT1 ubiquitination leads to increased TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and mitochondrial damage.

A question to ponder is whether a high-choline diet stimulates intestinal disorders in mice and whether circulating TMAO directly promotes cardiomyocyte pyroptosis in a mitochondria-dependent manner. The gut barrier and flora shifts are likely to become more severe during myocardial injury. Our study suggests an important link between the gut and heart for bidirectional communication. Furthermore, circulating TMAO significantly promotes the expression of mtDNA-related genes in TFAM and is regulated by ANT1. However, the specific mechanism by which TFAM drives mitochondrial damage-mediated myocardial pyroptosis requires further investigation.

During myocardial injury, we found that tRF-Glu is a novel regulator of cardiomyocyte pyroptosis. tRF-Glu deficiency significantly blocked TMAO-induced cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, impaired mitochondrial function, and mediated epigenetic regulatory mechanisms by directly binding to mitochondrial ANT1, which in turn blocked cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and inflammatory signalling pathways. Moreover, tsRNA-derived tRF-Glu can serve as a novel interventional target for improving pathological cardiac remodeling after MI, which is clinically significant in cardiovascular therapy.
