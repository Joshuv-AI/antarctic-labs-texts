Rubber seed cake supplementation alters meat quality, intestinal health, and gut microbiota in Hu sheep
Authors: Huwei Zhao, Jinling Hua, Wenwen Lu, Xiaokang Lv, Chao Chen, Yan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of rubber seed cake (RSC) supplementation on slaughter performance, intestinal health, and gut microbiota in Hu sheep. Forty-eight Hu sheep (17.01 ± 0.57 kg; 3 months old) were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments: 0% (CON), 6% (R6), 12% (R12), and 18% (R18) RSC. Following an 80-day feeding trial, six sheep per group were randomly selected for slaughter. Samples of the longissimus dorsi, small intestine, and intestinal mucosa were collected for meat quality, morphological, and intestinal health analyses. Additionally, ileal contents were harvested and frozen for metagenomic sequencing. Dietary supplementation with RSC reduced the shear force of the longissimus dorsi muscle (P = 0.043) and lowered meat color L* (P = 0.044) and b* (P = 0.035) values in the R6 group compared to the CON group. Quadratic effects were observed for the villus height to crypt depth (VH/CD) ratio in the duodenum (P = 0.006), jejunum (P = 0.006), and ileum (P = 0.001) with increasing RSC supplementation, and the VH/CD ratio was significantly increased in the R6 and R12 groups (P < 0.05). Ileal pro-inflammatory cytokine concentrations, including tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), decreased both linearly and quadratically with increasing RSC supplementation (P < 0.05). Quadratic effects (P < 0.05) were observed for the concentrations of mucin 2 (MUC2) and tight junction proteins such as zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1), occludin, and claudin in the jejunal and ileal mucosa with increasing RSC supplementation. Specifically, the concentrations of MUC2 and tight junction proteins in the jejunum, as well as MUC2 and ZO-1 in the ileum, were significantly higher in the R6 group (P < 0.05). RSC supplementation significantly altered the relative abundance of specific taxa, including Stenotrophomonas, Piromyces, Lichinomycetes, and Syntrophobacteria, as well as CAZyme gene sequences such as GH119, GT39, and GH13-8 (P < 0.05). In conclusion, these findings indicate that a 6% dietary supplementation of RSC is optimal in Hu sheep, as it improves meat quality and intestinal health by modulating the ileal microbiota composition and CAZyme abundance, thereby strengthening mucosal barrier function and alleviating inflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00556-7.Keywords: Rubber seed cake, Slaughter performance, Intestinal health, Gut microbiota

Introduction

With the steady expansion of natural rubber cultivation and production, valorizing the millions of tons of rubber seed-derived by-products generated annually has become a strategic approach to enhancing value addition within the rubber industry and advancing the circular agricultural economy. In 2023, global natural rubber production reached 14.32 million tons across approximately 10.32 million hectares, with an estimated annual output of 7.7 million tons of rubber seed kernels [1]. Rubber seed cake (RSC), the residual by-product after oil extraction, is a promising unconventional feed resource. Nutritionally, RSC contains 20%–25% crude protein and approximately 2,520 kcal/kg of metabolizable energy [2], boasts a balanced essential amino acid profile, and is rich in trace minerals [3]. The anti-nutritional profile of RSC is characterized by the presence of cyanide, phytic acid, and tannin at concentrations of 75.6 mg/kg, 31.5 g/kg, and 5.2 g/kg, respectively [4]. Its high availability and comprehensive nutritional value position it as a viable candidate to partially replace soybean meal and corn in ruminant diets, thereby mitigating dependence on conventional feed ingredients.

To date, research on RSC in ruminants has primarily centered on production performance and nutrient digestion and metabolism. Specifically, it has been reported that the inclusion of 20% rubber seed kernel in goat concentrate exerts no detrimental effects on nutrient digestibility and rumen fermentation [5]. Furthermore, dietary supplementation with yeast-fermented rubber seed kernels (10%–25%) for tropical lactating cows and heifers did not compromise feed efficiency or microbial protein synthesis [6, 7]. Notably, our previous findings demonstrated that supplementing the diets of Hu sheep with 6% RSC optimized rumen microbial composition and bolstered immune function and antioxidant capacity without compromising growth performance [1]. Collectively, these data suggest that RSC, at optimal inclusion levels, serves as a safe and viable alternative protein and energy source for ruminants. Given its antioxidant properties [1], we hypothesize that RSC may improve meat quality by modulating lipid metabolism and enhancing the oxidative stability of muscle tissue. Concurrently, the balanced essential amino acid profile and mineral content of RSC provide indispensable substrates for muscle protein synthesis and myofiber development.

Intestinal health serves not only as a prerequisite for efficient nutrient utilization but also as the cornerstone of systemic homeostasis. The intestinal mucosa acts as the primary biological barrier, effectively preventing the invasion and colonization of pathogenic microorganisms and endotoxins through tight junction proteins such as occludin and claudins [8, 9]. Maintaining the integrity of this mucosal barrier is critical for safeguarding normal physiological functions [10, 11]. Conversely, a compromised barrier leads to increased intestinal permeability, which facilitates the translocation of luminal lipopolysaccharides and pathogens into systemic circulation [12]. This process triggers systemic inflammatory stress and redirects metabolic energy toward immune responses, thereby significantly suppressing the growth potential. Concurrently, the gut microbiota orchestrates host homeostasis through its metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids, which provide trophic energy for epithelial cells, facilitate immune maturation, and function as a biological barrier through competitive exclusion [13, 14]. Given that RSC is rich in bioactive compounds and antioxidants, we hypothesize that dietary RSC supplementation may modulate epithelial integrity, attenuate inflammatory responses, and optimize microbial ecology, thereby contributing to enhanced gut health. However, a comprehensive evaluation of the effects of RSC on slaughter performance and integrated intestinal health in Hu sheep remains poorly characterized.

Based on the above considerations, the present study aimed to evaluate the effects of dietary RSC supplementation on slaughter performance, meat quality, intestinal health, and the gut microbiome in Hu sheep. By integrating phenotypic data with microbiome analysis, this study provides a theoretical basis for the safe and efficient utilization of RSC in Hu sheep diets.

Animals, diets, and experimental design

A completely randomized design was employed in this study. Forty-eight Hu sheep (17.01 ± 0.57 kg; 3 months of age) were randomly allocated to four dietary treatments, with 12 sheep per treatment. The treatments consisted of dietary inclusion levels of RSC at 0% (CON), 6% (R6), 12% (R12), and 18% (R18) on a dry matter (DM) basis. The experimental diets were formulated according to the Feeding Standard of Meat Sheep (NY/T 816–2004) issued by the Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China. A constant forage-to-concentrate ratio of 55:45 (DM basis) was maintained across all groups. The diets were formulated to be isonitrogenous and nearly isoenergetic, with crude protein (CP) levels ranging from 13.72% to 13.88% and metabolizable energy (ME) levels between 9.09 and 9.28 MJ/kg. RSC was included by replacing portions of soybean meal and corn in the concentrate mixture. The ingredients and chemical composition of the diets are presented in Table 2. The feeding trial lasted 90 days, comprising a 10-day adaptation period followed by an 80-day experimental period. Before the trial, the sheepfold, including pens and feeding troughs, was thoroughly cleaned and disinfected. The sheep were fed twice daily (7:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m.), with free access to water in between. Feed intake, clinical health, and fecal consistency were monitored daily. Throughout the experimental period, no severe clinical diseases were observed, and no veterinary interventions were required. Standardized immunization protocols, including vaccination against foot-and-mouth disease (initial vaccination at 30 days of age, followed by a booster one month later and subsequent boosters every 4 months), were strictly implemented in accordance with farm management guidelines. Furthermore, the pens were regularly cleaned to maintain a hygienic environment.

Slaughter performance and meat quality

On day 80 of the experimental period, six sheep per treatment group were randomly selected for slaughter. The selected sheep were fasted from feed for 24 h prior to slaughter to evaluate carcass traits and organ weights. After recording individual pre-slaughter live weights, the sheep were transported to a local abattoir and humanely slaughtered following high-voltage electrical stunning. The carcass weight and the weights of the rumen, reticulum, omasum, abomasum, heart, liver, lungs, kidneys, and spleen were recorded using an electronic balance (Model KF-H2, Kaifeng Group Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China; 0.5 g precision). Carcass weight was determined after the removal of the head, hooves, tail, and viscera, while retaining the kidneys and perirenal fat. Subsequently, the dressing percentage was calculated as follows: Dressing percentage (%) = (Carcass weight / Pre-slaughter live weight) × 100.

Approximately 1 kg of longissimus dorsi muscle was collected from each experimental sheep for meat quality analysis, including pH, meat color, water loss rate, shear force, and cooking loss. A digital pH meter (S220 K, Mettler Toledo, Switzerland) was employed to evaluate sample pH at 45 min and 24 h post-slaughter, while a reflectance spectrophotometer (CR-410, Konica Minolta Sensing, Japan) was utilized for the assessment of meat color coordinates (L*, a*, and b*). To ensure accuracy, all instrumental measurements were performed in triplicate at different locations on each muscle sample. For cooking loss determination, weighed samples were heated until they reached a core temperature of 70 °C, then removed and cooled for 30 min before being re-weighed. Cooking loss, shear force, and water loss rate were determined following the methodology described in a previous study [15].

Intestinal morphology

For intestinal morphological observation, segments from the mid-duodenum, jejunum, and ileum were collected immediately after slaughter and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were then subjected to standard paraffin embedding and cut into 5-µm-thick sections. Following the protocol described by Cai [16], the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The stained sections were then examined under a light microscope (BA-600-4, Motic, China). Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were quantified using ImageJ software, and the VH/CD ratio was subsequently calculated. Six replicates were used per treatment. For each section, ten measurements were taken, and the average value was used for statistical analysis.

Intestinal mucosal antioxidant indices

The antioxidant indices of the intestine (GSH-PX, CAT, T-SOD, MDA, T-AOC) were determined using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s guidelines (Nanjing Aoqing Bioengineering Institute, Jiangsu, China).

Intestinal mucosal SIgA and cytokines concentrations

ELISA kits by Nanjing Aoqing Bioengineering Institute (Jiangsu, China) were used to determine the concentrations of secretory immunoglobulin A (SIgA) and cytokines (IL-1, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) in the intestinal mucosa, with the detection method strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Intestinal mucosal tight junction protein concentrations

ELISA kits provided by Nanjing Aoqing Bioengineering Institute (Jiangsu, China) were used to determine the concentrations of ZO-1, occludin, claudin, and MUC2 in the intestinal mucosa. The detection method strictly followed the manufacturer’s instructions.

Gut microbiota

The frozen ileal contents (n = 16) were sent to Shanghai Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for metagenomic sequencing, as described in the Supplementary Method S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20.0 software. The individual sheep served as the experimental unit. Prior to statistical analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to verify data normality. A one-way ANOVA was then performed to evaluate the effects of the four diets on the designated parameters, with the dietary treatment considered a fixed effect. This was followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for mean separation. Additionally, orthogonal polynomial contrasts were conducted to analyze the linear and quadratic trends of dietary RSC supplementation. Results are presented as means and standard errors of the mean. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Intestinal microbial composition

The supplementation of varying proportions of rubber seed cake to the diet did not significantly affect the alpha diversity of the Hu sheep’s ileal microbiota, as measured by the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices (Fig. 5A-C). The first two principal component scores of PC1 and PC2 (86.78% and 9.87% of the explained variance) were used to represent the bacterial communities’ separation (Fig. 5D).

Effects of RSC on the intestinal morphology of Hu sheep. (A) Ace index. (B) Shannon index. (C) Simpson index. (D) PCoA analysis. (E) Relative abundances at the phylum level. (F) Relative abundances at the genus level. (G) Differential bacteria at the genus level. (H) Differential bacteria at the class level. * indicates P < 0.05

At the phylum level, the top 5 dominated phyla were Bacillota, Pseudomonadota, Campylobacterota, Mycoplasmatota, and Actinomycetota (Fig. 5E). Bacillota was the predominant phylum, accounting for 44.19% of the total bacterial population in the ileum of Hu sheep in the CON group, 43.16% in the R6 group, 44.95% in the R12 group, and 44.45% in the R18 group. At the genus level, the top 10 dominant genera were Streptococcus, Mycoplasmopsis, Campylobacter, Staphylococcus, Escherichia, Klebsiella, Lactococcus, Listeria, Clostridioides, and Enterococcus (Fig. 5F).

At the genus level (Fig. 5G), supplementing the diet with 18% RSC can significantly increase the relative abundance of Stenotrophomonas and the relative abundance of Piromyces (P < 0.05). At the class level (Fig. 5H), supplementing the diet with 18% RSC can significantly increase the relative abundance of Lichinomycetes (P < 0.05). Supplementing the diet with 6% RSC can significantly increase the relative abundance of Syntrophobacteria (P < 0.05).

Discussion

In the present study, dietary supplementation with RSC did not adversely affect the carcass traits or internal organ weights of Hu sheep. Although RSC contained ANFs, primarily cyanide (75.6 mg/kg) and phytic acid (31.5 g/kg), no toxic effects were observed in this study. This resilience is fundamentally attributed to the unique digestive physiology of ruminants, specifically the potent capacity of the complex ruminal microbiota to degrade or tolerate these ANFs. For instance, ruminal bacteria exhibit robust phytase activity, effectively hydrolyzing high dietary levels of phytic acid to release inorganic phosphorus [17]. Concurrently, specific ruminal bacteria possessing rhodanese activity, such as Enterococcus faecium and E. gallinarum, can biotransform residual cyanide into less toxic thiocyanate, which is subsequently and safely excreted via urine [18]. This efficient ruminal detoxification and adaptation mechanism is directly reflected in the overall growth and health phenotype of the animals. Consistent with our previous findings, dietary inclusion of RSC in Hu sheep did not compromise overall growth performance, feed intake, or nutrient digestibility [1]. Organ weights, which serve as key indicators of growth and developmental status, are critical for assessing overall health and production performance in sheep [19]. In this study, dietary RSC supplementation had no significant effect on organ weights across all experimental groups. This suggests that RSC does not impair the growth and internal organ development of Hu sheep, thereby preserving normal physiological functions.

Dietary supplementation of Hu sheep with 6% RSC reduced the muscle shear force as well as the L* and b* values of meat color. Shear force is a critical indicator of meat tenderness, reflecting the mechanical resistance during chewing, which directly determines the texture and palatability of the meat [20]. The observed decrease in shear force indicates that feeding RSC effectively improves meat tenderness. This improvement is likely associated with enhanced lipid digestion and deposition. Our previous research confirmed that dietary inclusion of 6% RSC significantly increased the digestibility of EE in Hu sheep [1]. Furthermore, corroborating studies have reported that feeding rubber seed kernel enhances the apparent digestibility of EE in ruminants [5, 7]. Such an increase in lipid digestibility likely promoted intramuscular fat deposition. This accumulated intramuscular fat physically lubricates the muscle fiber bundles, thereby reducing structural resistance within the muscle, which ultimately manifests as a decreased shear force. Meat color is a primary factor influencing consumer selection, as it not only affects visual appeal but is also closely correlated with meat freshness and nutritional value [21]. Monitoring changes in L*, a*, and b* values is a standard method for assessing the freshness of meat products. For instance, as meat oxidizes, the a* value may decrease while the b* value typically increases [22]. In this study, the L* and b* values of meat color in the R6 group decreased. This reduction in meat yellowness could be attributed to enhanced biological antioxidant pathways. Trace minerals abundant in RSC serve as essential cofactors for antioxidant enzymes [23]. They likely function synergistically with tannins, which are potent bioactive antioxidants [24], to effectively mitigate postmortem muscle lipid peroxidation and inhibit the oxidative conversion of myoglobin to metmyoglobin [25].

Dietary supplementation with RSC significantly improved the morphology of the small intestinal villi in Hu sheep. The VH, CD, and VH/CD ratios in the small intestine serve as key indicators of the developmental status and intestinal capacity for nutrient absorption and transport [26]. Improved intestinal morphology, characterized by an elevated VH/CD ratio, synergistically enhances mucosal barrier function and nutrient absorption efficiency by expanding the absorptive surface area and accelerating epithelial cell maturation, thereby promoting animal growth [27–29]. Previous studies have demonstrated that dietary supplementation with 15% RSC significantly elevates intestinal trypsin activity in tilapia [30]and boosts overall intestinal digestive enzyme activities in Hemibagrus wyckioides [4]. The elevated digestive enzyme activity facilitates the absorption of nutrients, thereby providing sufficient nourishment to support the growth and repair of intestinal cells. This nutritional support is crucial for maintaining the integrity of intestinal morphology, such as increasing VH and reducing CD. In this study, incorporating RSC into the diet enhanced the VH and VH/CD ratios in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, while reducing the CD in the duodenum and ileum. These findings demonstrate that RSC enhances intestinal integrity and promotes intestinal development, thereby effectively improving the capacity for nutrient digestion and absorption in animals. This enhanced nutrient availability provides essential substrates for muscle development and intramuscular fat deposition, offering a plausible explanation for the improved meat quality observed following dietary RSC supplementation.

Maintaining a dynamic balance between free radical production and the antioxidant defense system is crucial for preserving host health [31]. The present study demonstrated that dietary supplementation with 6% RSC significantly enhanced the activities of GSH-PX, CAT, and T-SOD, while concurrently reducing the MDA content in the jejunum and ileum. Our previous study indicated that RSC can enhance the antioxidant capacity of Hu sheep, thereby potentially reinforcing the protective function of the intestinal mucosa, mainly due to the abundant bioactive compounds in RSC, particularly n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids and essential trace elements, especially zinc [1]. As a crucial cofactor for SOD and GSH-PX, zinc directly bolsters the host’s enzymatic defense system against reactive oxygen species [32, 33]. However, despite its profile of beneficial bioactive substances, RSC also harbors certain ANFs, including hydrocyanic acid, tannins, and phytic acid. When the supplementation level was increased to 18%, the accumulation of dietary toxins likely triggered a toxicological threshold effect. Excessive levels of ANFs can induce mild oxidative stress and exert chemical irritation on the intestinal mucosa, thereby depleting antioxidant enzymes and precipitating localized tissue damage [34]. This observation is consistent with findings in other species (e.g., fish), where elevated MDA levels and suppressed antioxidant enzyme activities were observed following the consumption of high-RSC diets [4, 35]. Therefore, we conclude that a 6% RSC supplementation level represents the optimal balance between nutritional efficacy and safety in Hu sheep.

SIgA serves as the frontline defense of the intestinal epithelium, shielding the gut from luminal toxins and pathogenic microorganisms [36], thereby maintaining intestinal barrier integrity and microenvironmental homeostasis [37]. In the current study, dietary supplementation with 6% and 12% RSC significantly increased jejunal SIgA concentrations, suggesting an enhanced capacity of the intestinal mucosa for pathogen clearance. Concomitant with this bolstered immune defense, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, in the ileum of Hu sheep were significantly reduced. Given that excessive inflammatory mediators induce epithelial damage and disrupt intestinal homeostasis [38], the suppression of these cytokines by RSC effectively attenuates local inflammatory responses, thereby creating a favorable microenvironment for maintaining intestinal barrier integrity. Consequently, this alleviated inflammatory state functionally translates to an improved physical barrier. Our findings revealed that dietary inclusion of 6% RSC significantly upregulated the expression of critical tight junction proteins, including claudin, occludin, and ZO-1, as well as MUC2 in the jejunum and ileum. Rather than acting merely as static structures, these proteins dynamically restrict paracellular permeability and prevent pathogen translocation [39–41]. By reducing local inflammation, the bioactive compounds present in RSC likely protect these barrier proteins from inflammatory cleavage, thereby enhancing overall gut health. However, literature regarding the effects of RSC on ruminant intestinal health remains limited; thus, its specific mechanisms of action warrant further investigation.

As a critical segment of the digestive tract, the small intestine harbors a complex microbiota that forms a profound symbiotic relationship with the host and fundamentally drives animal growth [42]. This intricate microbial ecosystem exerts a pivotal influence on metabolism, immune regulation, and the establishment of biological barriers and defense mechanisms [43]. Taxonomic profiling at the phylum level revealed that Bacillota was the predominant microbial group in terms of relative abundance within the ileum. Bacillota, formerly known as Firmicutes, constitute a significant component of the gut microbiota and play a pivotal role in the host’s metabolism, immune modulation, and interactions with other microorganisms [44, 45]. At the genus level, the relative abundance of Piromyces was the highest in the R18 group. Piromyces plays a significant role in the digestive tract of ruminant animals, particularly in the breakdown of structural carbohydrates in plant cell walls [46]. At the class level, the relative abundance of Syntrophobacteria was higher in the R6 group. Syntrophobacteria, recognized as beneficial bacteria in the gut, collaborate with other microbes to enhance the metabolism of organic matter and the recovery of energy within the intestinal tract. This synergistic action is crucial for maintaining the host’s health and facilitating nutrient absorption [47]. Overall, these findings suggest that dietary inclusion of RSC can improve the intestinal function and health of Hu sheep by modulating the structure of the gut microbiota.

It is estimated that more than 35% of the enzymes required for host digestion and metabolism are synthesized by the gut microbiota, with approximately 25% of these being involved in carbohydrate metabolism [48]. Functional prediction of the microbiome within the intestinal contents of Hu sheep was conducted using the CAZy database. Our analysis revealed that among the carbohydrate-active enzymes, the most abundant were GHs, followed by GTs. GHs are a class of enzymes capable of catalyzing the hydrolysis of glycosidic bonds. They are crucial in breaking down complex carbohydrates into simpler sugar units, such as monosaccharides, essential for digestion and energy acquisition. Additionally, GHs are involved in synthesizing glycosidic bonds, aiding in constructing intricate sugar structures [49, 50]. In this study, the gene abundance of GH13_8 was the highest in the R12 group. Members of the GH13_8 subfamily are classified as glycogen branching enzymes, which play a crucial role in starch modification and glycogen biosynthesis by forming α-1,6-O-glycosidic branches [51]. GTs play a key role in numerous biological processes, such as cell signaling, immune response, and extracellular matrix formation [52]. In this study, the gene abundance of GT39 was significantly higher in the R6 and R12 groups. The GT39 family comprises protein-O-mannosyltransferases, which utilize dolichol-P-mannose as a sugar donor to catalyze the transfer of mannose moieties to proteins, forming O-glycosidic bonds via O-mannosylation. This process represents a critical post-translational modification that plays a significant role in maintaining protein stability and function [53]. Collectively, these findings indicate that dietary supplementation with 6% and 12% RSC enhances the intestinal function and health of Hu sheep by modulating the metabolic capacity of the gut microbiota, particularly through the enrichment of genes encoding enzymes involved in carbohydrate metabolism and protein modification.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of the present study indicate that a 6% dietary supplementation of RSC is optimal in Hu sheep, as it improves meat quality and intestinal health by modulating the ileal microbiota composition and CAZyme abundance, thereby strengthening mucosal barrier function and alleviating inflammation. The findings of this study provide a theoretical basis for the safe and efficient utilization of RSC in Hu sheep diets.
