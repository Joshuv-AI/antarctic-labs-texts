The impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on work participation, sickness absence and general practitioner consultations in the Danish population - a register-based matched cohort study
Authors: Karin Biering, Berit Schiøttz-Christensen, Morten Vejs Willert, Kent J Nielsen, Henrik A Kolstad, Marianne Kyndi, Jesper Medom Vestergaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesMany persons infected with SARS-CoV-2 experienced prolonged symptoms after the acute phase of the infection (long-COVID). We aimed to investigate whether SARS-CoV-2 infection increased the risk of long-term sickness absence, low work participation, and high use of general practitioners in persons in the Danish workforce.MethodsIn a register-based cohort study, we identified 261,325 persons from the workforce who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 in Denmark. These were matched with persons who tested negative by time, sex, age, and industry and analysed with conditional logistic regression. The outcomes were long-term sickness absence, low work participation and high number of contacts to general practitioners, all measured during year 1 after test. We adjusted for work participation or contacts to general practitioners, both measured during year 1 before test, respectively, and for vaccination status, comorbidity, and educational level. We stratified the result by sex, age, and industry to identify any effect modification by these factors.ResultsMost persons infected with SARS-CoV-2 were not on long-term sickness absence (98.3%). However, compared to persons tested negative, SARS-CoV-2 infected had an increased risk of long-term sickness absence (Odds Ratio (OR):2.92[2.82;3.03]) and low work participation (OR:1.32[1.30;1.34]). The risk was higher among women and middle-aged and older persons. Furthermore, the risk was highest in public administration, education and health services, culture and arts, and lowest in agriculture, forestry and fishing. We found an increased risk of high number of contacts to general practitioners among SARS-CoV-2 infected (OR:1.61[1.60;1.63]), however more uniformly distributed across subgroups.ConclusionInfection with SARS-CoV-2 was associated with a higher risk of long-term sickness absence, low work participation and high number of contacts to general practitioners during year 1 after test. The associations differed across sex, age and industry, especially in relation to sickness absence.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, Rehabilitation, Return to work, Sickness absence

Introduction

Since the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic, there has been great concern about whether infection with the virus would result in late sequelae [1]. Persistent symptoms were reported compared with previous types of respiratory infections and a large number of studies have described symptoms that persisted after the acute COVID-19 infection [2–5]. In December 2021, the WHO defined Long-COVID as: “a condition that occurs in individuals with a history of probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, usually three months from the onset of COVID-19, with symptoms that last for at least two months and cannot be explained by an alternative diagnosis” [1]. Symptoms are difficult to monitor at the population level, but long-lasting or severe symptoms may be related to increased consultations with general practitioners, which may be used as a proxy for symptom burden [6].

A growing number of studies have reported increased levels of sickness absence and subsequent reduction in labour market participation during the COVID-19 pandemic [7]. The risk of sickness absence was particularly elevated among healthcare workers and other high-exposure groups, largely driven by infection, quarantine requirements, and increased workload [8–11]. In addition, emerging evidence suggests that COVID-19-related absences may have long-term consequences for labour market participation, with reductions in labour supply observed after health-related absences during the pandemic [12]. A Dutch register-based study found that time to return to work was longer following COVID-19 infection compared to other flu-like symptoms, however this difference declined as other COVID-19 variants emerged over time [13]. When the Omicron variant became dominant in late 2021, fewer infected persons reported long-COVID symptoms [14]. However, most studies on return to work and work ability following COVID-19 have been conducted in hospitalised populations or among patients from long-COVID rehabilitation settings and without a comparison group [7, 15–18]. We have not identified studies examining whether employees in some industries had a higher subsequent risk of sickness absence and increased time to return to work compared to others, but a large study of public sector employees in Finland found only minor socioeconomic differences in sickness absence with the intermediate groups having the highest subsequent risk of sickness absence one year later [19].

In some industries employees faced an elevated risk of COVID-19 infections, especially at the onset of the pandemic, when individuals employed in the health- and social care sector as well as those in the transport sector were at higher risk of hospital admission [20].

A large Danish population-based register study showed an increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 infection in seven healthcare occupations and 42 other occupations in other sectors, mainly social work activities, residential care, education, defence and security, accommodation, and transport with close contact with clients, customers, or patients. They also found that the relative risk of infections in healthcare, residential care, and defence and security declined across pandemic waves, while the relative risk in education and social work increased during the pandemic [21].

However, some industries may have been more affected by workplace closures than others during the pandemic. Some could continue the work as before, while others had to send employees home or lay them off. These conditions affected the possibility of returning to work after SARS-CoV-2 infection in the individual industries.

The purpose of this study was to:

Investigate whether SARS-CoV-2 infection increased the risk of long-term sickness absence and low work participation during the following year among persons who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 in the Danish labour market compared to persons who tested negative for SARS-CoV-2.

Investigate whether SARS-CoV-2 infection increased the risk of long-term sickness absence and low work participation during the following year among persons who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 in the Danish labour market compared to persons who tested negative for SARS-CoV-2.

Investigate if SARS-CoV-2 infection resulted in high number of contacts to general practitioners during the following year.

Investigate if SARS-CoV-2 infection resulted in high number of contacts to general practitioners during the following year.

Investigate the impact of sex, age and industry on the above-mentioned purposes.

Materials and methods

Design

This study is a matched cohort study comparing persons from the Danish workforce who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 with persons in the Danish workforce who tested negative.

Material

The study was based on the Danish national health and population registries. Every person living in Denmark holds an individual person identification number (CPR number) that links data from all registries [22]. The Danish healthcare system and the path to registration in the population-based registries are described in detail elsewhere [23].

We restricted the study to individuals who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 between March 1st 2020 and December 1st 2021, before the less severe Omicron variant became dominant in Denmark [24]. During this period, case numbers varied substantially. In the early weeks, testing was not widely available, and some cases may not have been identified. The first confirmed case in Denmark was on the 27th of February 2020 [25].

Exposure

We defined exposure to SARS-CoV-2 infection based on the Danish SARS-CoV-2 surveillance data. SARS-CoV-2 infection was identified by reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction tests (RT-PCR) [26, 27]. Initially, RT-PCR tests were carried out for diagnostic purposes for symptomatic persons and for contact tracing, but from autumn 2020, testing capacity in Denmark was expanded and tests were offered free of charge to the entire population, regardless of symptoms and contact status.

We also used COVID-19 surveillance data to identify hospital admissions and admissions to intensive care units due to COVID-19.

We defined SARS-CoV-2 infection as the first positive RT-PCR test before 1st of December 2021 and considered those with only negative tests before this date as uninfected. We used this information for the matched design described later in the Methods section. Individuals who were never tested, first tested after 1st of December 2021, or had only inconclusive test results were excluded. The test date defined the start of follow-up for each person (index date).

Outcomes

We examined three different outcomes in this study: long-term sickness absence, low work participation, and high number of contacts to general practitioners, all defined below.

Long-term sickness absence and low work participation were based on data from the Danish Register for Evaluation of Marginalisation (DREAM). The DREAM register includes information on all public transfer payments, as well as information regarding migration and death, for all Danes aged 18 years or older on a weekly basis and contains more than 100 different types of transfer payments. We categorized the transfer payments into six main groups: (1) Work, educational grants, and parental leave; (2) Sickness absence and other temporary health benefits (part-time and full-time) (3) Social benefits and unemployment benefits; (4) Permanent health-related benefits; (5) Retirement and early retirement, (6) Migrated or deceased. Sickness absence is usually reimbursed (and registered) if the sickness absence exceeds 30 days, but during the COVID-19 pandemic, this reimbursement coverage period was changed, so reimbursement was possible already from the first day of sickness absence if it was due to COVID-19 (quarantine, disease, or child’s quarantine). This change was valid from 17th of March 2020 to 31th of June 2021 [28]. We defined long-term sickness absence as more than six weeks of absence starting within 3 weeks of the index date and without sickness absence during the four weeks before the index date to make the period comparable to the normal regulations. For descriptive purposes, we furthermore calculated the number of weeks with sickness absence starting from the index date.

Work participation was defined as the proportion of working weeks (work, unemployment, education and parental leave) during the specific period of time (one year before and one year after index date) divided by the number of possible working weeks (defined as all weeks where the person has not left the workforce permanently due to permanent health-related benefits, retirement, migration or death) [29]. In this case, we coded unemployment as equivalent to work, since many became unemployed due to different types of lockdowns, based on the assumption that work ability was similar among those employed and those unemployed. We used a 90% cut-off for dichotomization, classifying individuals who worked less than 90% of the possible working time during the period as having low work participation.

High number of contacts to general practitioners was defined using the Danish National Health Service Register. This register contains data on interactions with general practitioners, dentists, physiotherapists, medical specialists, and other private healthcare providers operating under public financial reimbursement schemes, for all individuals residing in Denmark who hold a CPR number. In this study, data on consultations with general practitioners were utilized to construct outcome measures for both high healthcare utilisation during the follow-up period and prior healthcare use. Since the data are connected to reimbursement, the coverage is high [30]. We used the data to count the number of contacts with general practitioners, including in-clinic consultations, online contacts, home visits, and telephone consultations for each person during the 12 months before and the 12 months after the index date, not including the date of the SARS-CoV-2 test. We defined high number of contacts to general practitioners as more than twice the mean number of contacts before the index date in the study population. As the mean number of yearly contacts was 4.5, the cut off was 9 contacts per year. Furthermore, we calculated the number of contacts with general practitioners during the year preceding the first positive COVID-19 test as a proxy for individual healthcare-seeking behaviour.

Covariates

Comorbidity was defined based on data from diagnoses from the National Patient Registry. We calculated the Charlson Comorbidity Index (a scoring system for comorbidities) during the 5 years prior to the index date [31]. The Danish National Patient Registry contains data on all hospital contacts with public and private hospitals in Denmark [32]. The Charlson Comorbidity Index was categorised as 0 (no comorbidity), 1 (low comorbidity, Charlson = 1) and 2 (High Comorbidity, Charlson > 1).

We obtained age, sex, migration, and death from the Danish Central Person Register. Based on registrations of migration, we censored persons when they emigrated from Denmark during follow-up and censored persons who died during follow-up.

We restricted the sample to include only persons aged 18–67 years as the study focused on the workforce. Age was grouped as 18–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59 and 60–67 years.

The COVID-19 Vaccines Registry contains data on all COVID-19 vaccinations administered to the population at vaccine centres, by general practitioners, and by private vaccine clinics, thereby covering all vaccinations provided in Denmark from 2020 onwards. COVID-19 vaccines were first administered in Denmark by the 27th of December 2020. We defined vaccination status at the index date (no vaccine, one vaccine dose and two vaccine dose) and included it as a potential confounder in the association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the outcomes. We did not distinguish between different brands of vaccines.

Industry was obtained from the Labour Market Account Registry that provides a complete overview of the population’s labour market status. We used it to specify the primary industry of each person at the index date, or the latest registered industry. We classified the population in ten industry groups in accordance with the Danish version of the European classification of industries (DB-07) [33]. Furthermore, we added an additional group ‘unknown’ to be able to include those who were not assigned to a main industry.

Educational level was obtained from The Attainment Registry. We classified levels of education based on the nine categories of highest attained education in the Danish version of the International Standard Classification of Education (ISCED) version 2011 and further recoded these into three groups: (1) Basic (primary school or less), (2) Intermediate (secondary education) and (3) Advanced (tertiary education or above) [34].

Methods

This prospective matched cohort study is based on the Danish working population as of 1st of January 2020. Among individuals who were tested at least once using an RT-PCR-SARS-CoV-2 test (PCR test) (n = 1,842,292), we excluded 636,200 who had an unknown address or resided in Greenland, had died before the test results, were retired or not part of the workforce (i.e. students, unemployed persons or persons on parental leave), or had no registered educational level (Fig. 1 Flowchart).

Flowchart

As the DREAM data were updated later than data from the Danish National Health Service Register, the follow-up periods differed slightly by outcome. In the DREAM register, individuals were followed for the sickness absence outcome until the end of follow-up (2nd of April 2023), while the work participation outcome was assessed during year 1 after the index date. In The Danish National Health Service Register, high number of contacts to general practitioners was assessed for one year up to 30th September 2022; however, individuals with the most recent positive test dates were followed for at least 10 months. During follow-up in DREAM, 418 SARS-CoV-2 positive individuals and 2,565 SARS-CoV-2 negative individuals were censored as they died before end of follow-up whereas 3,096 SARS-CoV-2 positive individuals and 13,614 SARS-CoV-2 negative individuals migrated before the end of follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Cases were matched to up to ten test-negative controls on sex, age, industry, and test date within a ± 7-day window. Matching on test date was used to account for temporal variation in testing availability, testing indications, public health restrictions, vaccination coverage, and circulating SARS-CoV-2 variants during the study period. The ± 7-day window was specified a priori to ensure close temporal comparability while allowing identification of suitable controls.

This yielded between zero and ~ 5,600 potential controls per case. The 1:10 matching ratio was chosen to increase statistical precision while retaining a high proportion of eligible cases [35]. Matching was preferred to conventional adjustment for calendar time because the probability of SARS-CoV-2 testing and infection, as well as healthcare utilisation and labour market conditions, changed substantially throughout the pandemic.

We limited the number of controls to ten per case and excluded 177 cases that could not be matched to at least one control. A few of these were from the first weeks, when access to tests was limited, but most were from 2021 and from the smaller industries (Agriculture, forestry and fishing and Real estate activities).

First, characteristics of the study population was stratified by test status. Characteristics were described with numbers and proportions and compared by chi2 test. Those who tested positive were further described with respect to any hospital admission and admission to intensive care.

We analysed the data using conditional logistic regression for the three outcomes: long-term sickness absence, low work participation, and high number of contacts to general practitioners. The analyses were adjusted for relevant prior behaviour including low work participation during year 1 before index date and number of contacts to general practitioners during year 1 before index date, as well as educational level, vaccination status and comorbidity. Conditional logistic regression was used to account for the matched design and estimate associations between SARS-CoV-2 infection and the study outcomes [36]. Additional covariates were included to control for potential residual confounding not addressed through matching.

Results

The analysis comprised 261,325 persons with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test in the period from 1st of March 2020 to 1st of December 2021 and 2,593,909 matched control observations (542,540 individuals), after the exclusion of those who could not be matched and redundant match controls (Fig. 1 Flowchart).

We found that 1.7% of the infected persons started long-term sickness absence within 3 weeks after index date, compared to 0.6% of the non-infected. The mean number of weeks on long-term sickness absence during year 1 after the index date was 23.1 for the SARS-CoV-2 infected and 25.2 for the non-infected. 1.7% of the infected persons were admitted to hospital and 10% of these were in intensive care units at some point during admission. Among individuals with COVID-19 who were hospitalised, the mean duration of sickness absence during year 1 after index date was 29.4 weeks, increasing to 38.0 weeks for those admitted to intensive care.

Among persons included in the analysis, a slightly larger proportion of women than men tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 during the study period and younger persons more often tested positive than older persons. Individuals with two or more comorbidities were less likely to be tested positive than those with fewer comorbidities. Most persons had not received a vaccination before the index date, and the infection rate was higher among those persons (Table 1).

Discussion

In this matched cohort study of the Danish working population, individuals who tested positive for SARS-CoV-2 had an increased risk of long-term sickness absence compared with individuals who tested negative. The increased risk was more pronounced among women than among men and among individuals older than 30 years compared with younger individuals. The highest risks were observed in the Financial and insurance, Public administration, Education, and health, and Arts, entertainment, and recreation industries.

We also observed an increased risk of low work participation during year 1 after the index date among individuals who tested positive, although the associations were weaker than those observed for long term sickness absence and more evenly distributed across sex, age, and industry subgroups. Finally, individuals who tested positive had an increased risk of frequent general practitioners, without clear subgroup differences.

A major strength of this study was the nationwide register coverage of all residents in Denmark, including comprehensive information on SARS-CoV-2 testing, vaccination, general practitioner contacts, education, and transfer payments. However, some groups may not have been fully captured, including workers who were never tested, undocumented migrant workers employed outside registered companies, and newly arrived migrant workers who had not yet received a CPR number. Because travel and access to testing were restricted during the early phase of the pandemic, some SARS-CoV-2 infections among migrant workers may not have been registered.

As the study relied exclusively on register data, potentially relevant factors such as lifestyle, health behaviours, and work environment characteristics could not be directly accounted for and may not have been fully controlled for through matching. Limited testing capacity early in the pandemic may also have resulted in underascertainment of SARS-CoV-2 cases. Only 177 SARS-CoV-2-positive individuals could not be matched with a control; although some of these unmatched individuals occurred early in the pandemic, most were distributed across the study period. The matching strategy was specified a priori to ensure comparability between SARS-CoV-2-positive and test-negative individuals with respect to age, sex, industry, and calendar time. We did not perform sensitivity analyses using alternative matching windows around the test date and therefore cannot exclude the possibility that different matching windows would have produced slightly different estimates. However, only 177 SARS-CoV-2-positive individuals could not be matched with a control, suggesting that the chosen approach achieved a reasonable balance between temporal comparability and retention of eligible cases.

The use of test-negative controls may appear problematic because these individuals were unlikely to have sickness absence due to COVID-19. However, PCR testing in Denmark was initially reserved for symptomatic individuals and healthcare workers. Later in the pandemic, routine screening was increasingly performed using rapid antigen tests or self-tests, while PCR testing was primarily used to confirm positive results from these less accurate tests. Consequently, individuals with negative PCR tests may also have been likely to have experienced symptoms, which may have reduced confounding by indication. This potential bias is likely less relevant for the outcomes work participation during year 1 after the index date and general practitioner consultations.

Another limitation relates to the use of logistic regression. When outcomes are common, odds ratios may overestimate the corresponding relative risks. Consequently, estimates for outcomes with relatively high prevalence, particularly high use of general practitioners, should be interpreted with some caution with regard to the magnitude of the associations.

Following regulatory changes introduced on 17 March 2020, employers and self-employed persons could receive reimbursement from the first day of absence. Consequently, registered sickness absence may reflect not only illness but also quarantine due to household exposure to SARS-CoV-2. To reduce this potential misclassification, we excluded sickness absence periods shorter than six weeks. However, long-term sickness absence may still reflect conditions unrelated to COVID-19, as register data included all causes of sickness absence. Although sickness absence could be registered immediately after a positive SARS-CoV-2 test, some periods may therefore have been attributable to other illnesses or injuries. We consider this potential misclassification to be largely non-differential. Nevertheless, individuals testing negative generally had longer sickness absence periods and may therefore have been more affected by other severe illnesses or injuries.

Most participants were followed for up to 12 months, and all were followed for for at least 10 months, for work participation during year 1 after the index date and high use of general practitioners. Long-term sickness absence was assessed using all available follow-up time. For individuals tested late in the study period, data on general practitioner consultations were only available for 10 months. In addition, complete follow-up was unavailable for individuals who emigrated or died during follow-up. For work participation during year 1 after the index date, this was considered a minor issue because the measure was calculated based only on available weeks. For high use of general practitioners, incomplete follow-up would likely bias the estimates towards the null.

The youngest individuals in the cohort may not have been eligible for transfer payments before the age of 18 years. However, work participation during year 1 after the index date was calculated using only available weeks, and data on general practitioner consultations were available regardless of age. We therefore consider this limitation to have minimal impact.

Another limitation was that inclusion ended when the Omicron variant became dominant. Because outcomes were assessed after this time point, some participants may have experienced new SARS-CoV-2 infections during follow-up that were not captured in the exposure assessment. Such misclassification would likely increase the occurrence of outcomes among previously test-negative individuals, thereby attenuating the observed associations.

The apparent higher infection rate among vaccinated individuals may reflect the Danish vaccination strategy, in which groups at increased risk of infection, including workers with frequent interpersonal contact, as well as older individuals and persons with chronic diseases, were prioritised for vaccination.

For the outcome high use of general practitioners, we were unable to distinguish consultations related to COVID-19 from those due to other conditions. In addition, restrictions and lockdowns during the pandemic may have influenced healthcare-seeking behaviour and patterns of contact with general practitioners. Both in-person and telephone and online consultations were included in the analyses, and because matching accounted for calendar time, we consider any resulting bias to be largely non-differential.

Participants were categorised according to their main industry. However, substantial heterogeneity likely existed in occupational exposures and work tasks within each industry category. In particular, the classification did not account for differences in work tasks and therefore could not capture how persistent symptoms might affect work ability differently across occupations. Opportunities to work from home may also have varied within industries and could potentially have reduced the need for sickness absence among individuals with milder symptoms who retained partial work capacity. In contrast, individuals with more severe symptoms would likely require sickness absence regardless of opportunities for remote work.

We found that a larger proportion of individuals with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test experienced long-term sickness absence compared with test-negative individuals. However, the average duration of sickness absence was shorter among test-positive individuals than among test-negative individuals, suggesting that SARS-CoV-2 infection may have resulted in shorter sickness absence periods than other illnesses. According to clinical observations by the chief medical officer at the Clinic for Post COVID Complications in the Central Denmark Region (the second author), many patients with long COVID initially had only short periods of sickness absence and attempted to maintain employment, for example by working from home or reducing working hours. Over time, however, fatigue and cognitive difficulties became barriers to performing work tasks, potentially resulting in reduced work participation in the longer term. This interpretation is consistent with findings from cross-sectional studies reporting fatigue, reduced functional ability, and cognitive impairment among patients with COVID-19 [37] and the findings from the systematic review of Rahmati et al. which concluded that a range of symptoms may persist for at least three years after infection [17].

Our findings are difficult to compare directly with previous studies on return to work following COVID-19 infection. First, many earlier studies focused exclusively on healthcare workers infected during the initial phase of the pandemic or on patients referred to rehabilitation services. Second, differences in national regulations regarding sickness absence compensation and social welfare systems may substantially influence work absence and return-to-work patterns across countries.

We did not identify previous studies specifically examining work participation after COVID-19 infection in the general working population. However, a German study reported reduced self-reported work ability among workers primarily performing physical work tasks after COVID-19 infection, whereas no reduction was observed among workers with predominantly mental work tasks [38]. Several studies have described sickness absence following COVID-19 infection and our findings were in line with these [7, 10, 11, 19]. Regarding use of health services, we did not identify studies examining this outcome from COVID-19, but a previous Canadian study found increased use of healthcare services in SARS survivors [39]. Although the increased risk of sickness absence, low work participation during year 1 after the index date, and high use of general practitioners among SARS-CoV-2-infected individuals compared with test-negative individuals may not be unexpected, the observed differences across sex, age, and industry represent an important finding of the present study. Supporting this interpretation, a large British study among healthcare workers reported varying patterns of sickness absence depending on diagnostic category [11]. They also found that most other disease categories (apart from infectious diseases) decreased in 2020 for short-term sickness absence, while the pattern was more mixed in relation to longer sickness absence with an increase in sickness absence due to asthma, chest and respiratory diseases, headache/migraine, mental health and pregnancy-related disorders. We presume that if these differences were present in our population, they were most likely evenly distributed in COVID-19 infected and non-infected persons.

Our findings can be generalised to countries that have similar regulations regarding sickness absence reimbursement and similar transfer income possibilities. Furthermore, our study can help to determine the impact of long-COVID symptoms in the working population, especially the differences across industries.

Conclusion

Infection with SARS-CoV-2 was associated with higher risk of long-term sickness absence, low work participation during year 1 after index date and high use of general practitioners. The associations differed across sex, age and industry, especially in relation to sickness absence.
