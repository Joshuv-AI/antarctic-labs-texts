Differential roles of sense of coherence components in mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic in a nationwide survey of public health center directors in Japan
Authors: Yuri Akamatsu, Toshiyuki Ojima, Hiroko Shimada, Rie Fujita, Sanae Haruyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic health centers (PHCs) played a critical role in managing health crises during the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic in Japan. Sense of coherence (SOC), comprising comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness–a key framework for stress resilience–is important for mental health, with meaningfulness especially being important. This study examined the associations between SOC components and mental health among PHC directors during the pandemic.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional questionnaire survey was conducted among all 468 PHC directors in Japan between September and November 2022. Mental health was assessed using the six-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6), and SOC using the University of Tokyo Health Sociology version of the SOC3 scale (SOC3-UTHS). Information on the maximum number of consecutive working days and average sleep duration during the most demanding pandemic period were obtained. Logistic regression analyses were performed with K6 as the outcome and total SOC3-UTHS or each component as explanatory variables, adjusting for consecutive working days or sleep duration, and the interaction with SOC component.ResultsOverall, 189 valid responses were analyzed. After adjusting for the number of consecutive working days, higher K6 scores were significantly associated with lower total SOC3-UTHS scores (Odds Ratio (OR) = 0.349), comprehensibility (OR = 0.413) and manageability (OR = 0.278), but not meaningfulness. Similar associations were observed after adjusting for average sleep duration. However, the interaction between comprehensibility and consecutive working days suggests the protective association of higher comprehensibility with mental health may be attenuated under prolonged working conditions. Shorter sleep duration also tended to be associated with worse mental health.ConclusionsSOC components may play different roles for leaders operating under crisis conditions, and comprehensibility, manageability, and adequate sleep may warrant attention in this context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44192-026-00535-z.Keywords: Public health center director, Sense of coherence, Six-item Kessler psychological distress scale (K6), Sleep duration, Consecutive working days, COVID-19, Health crisis

Introduction

In Japan, public health centers (PHCs; hokenjo in Japanese) are local governmental agencies that serve as specialized and technical hubs that support the health of local residents [1–3]. Their wide range of responsibilities includes consultation on intractable diseases and mental health, infectious disease control, maternal and child health, health promotion for disease prevention or monitoring and guidance on pharmaceuticals, food hygiene, and environmental hygiene. To carry out these responsibilities, PHCs are staffed by professionals with specialized knowledge and technical expertise, including public health nurses, physicians, pharmacists, veterinarians, dietitians, and social workers in mental health.

Additionally, PHCs were crucial in managing health crises during the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic [3–7]. Their COVID-19-related responsibilities were diverse, including the administration and transportation of polymerase chain reaction tests, coordination of hospital admissions and patient transfers, health monitoring of patients recovering at home or in non-hospital settings, issuance of work restrictions and hospitalization recommendations, and communication and coordination with medical institutions, educational facilities, and other relevant stakeholders. Furthermore, they were responsible for conducting active epidemiological investigations and entering patient data into official systems.

This excessive workload had negatively impacted the mental health of PHC staff [6–9]. These existing studies have primarily focused on frontline staff, such as public health nurses, not PHC directors as organizational leaders. Compared with frontline staff, PHC directors may have experienced qualitatively different stressors due to their high levels of responsibility and accountability, such as organizational decision-making, resource allocation, and coordination across sectors. Given their central role in crisis management, understanding the determinants of mental health among PHC directors is essential for strengthening the resilience and preparedness of public health systems.

Sense of coherence (SOC), developed by Aaron Antonovsky in 1987 as a critical part of his salutogenic model of health, offers a useful framework for understanding mental resilience [10–12]. Unlike models focusing on risk factors for disease, the salutogenic model focuses on salutary factors that promote health, and SOC represents the extent to which individuals perceive and cope with stress in a health-promoting way. SOC comprises mainly three key components: comprehensibility (the ability to understand and/or predict possible difficulties or problems in life), manageability (the ability to address these difficulties and/or problems), and meaningfulness (viewing difficulties and problems as worthy of facing and overcoming them). Higher SOC is generally associated with better mental health and, especially meaningfulness is related to mental health rather than the other components. Aaron Antonovsky conceptualized sense of coherence (SOC) as a relatively stable disposition in adulthood, although it is not entirely fixed and SOC may change with age across the life course [12, 13]. Under stressful or challenging situation, the stability of SOC under stressful conditions remains controversial; previous studies suggest that individuals with higher SOC may exhibit greater stability [14, 15]. In light of the professional and leadership characteristics of PHC directors, their SOC may be relatively high and stable. However, in the context of a public health crisis, the association of SOC with mental health and whether the association varies across different levels of stressful conditions have not been sufficiently examined.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the association between SOC and mental health among PHC directors during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a particular focus on the associations with each SOC component, considering potential interaction effects between SOC and stressful working conditions because the potential influence of stressful working conditions on SOC cannot be excluded.

Methods

Study design and data source

This cross-sectional study employed a questionnaire survey targeting all 468 directors of public PHCs in Japan as of April 1, 2022.

Questionnaire survey method

An anonymous questionnaire, which included an explanation of the study, was distributed via email as an Excel file to all PHC directors. We asked the Japanese Association of Public Health Center Directors to disseminate our survey. Given the potential differences in the degree of strain across PHCs during the pandemic, directors serving concurrently at multiple PHCs were requested to complete separate questionnaires for each PHC. Completed surveys were submitted either by uploading the file to a designated URL provided in the questionnaire or, if this was unfeasible, by sending it to a dedicated email address. All email communications related to this survey were handled by research assistants who anonymized the completed surveys before forwarding them to the research team, ensuring the anonymity of participants regardless of the submission method.

The survey period was initially scheduled from September 12 to October 7, 2022. However, in order to improve the response rate, a follow-up request was sent out again through the Japanese Association of Public Health Center Directors, and the submission deadline was extended to November 8, 2022. The timing of the survey followed the peak of the 7th wave of COVID-19 in Japan, which occurred from late July to early August of 2022 [16, 17].

Questionnaire survey items used in this study

The questionnaire items were developed by a research team of the “Comprehensive Research on Health and Safety Crisis Management” project, funded by the Health and Labour Sciences Research Grant of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW), Japan [18]. The team comprised researchers with experience in management support for multiple PHCs, those engaged in COVID-19 response activities at PHCs, and current PHC directors. Here, the following items out of all questionnaire items were selected; occupation (two options: physician or others), years of administrative experience, and SOC (details provided below). Additionally, their maximum number of consecutive working days, average sleep duration, and the six-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6; details provided below) during the most demanding period of the COVID-19 pandemic prior to the survey were assessed.

Six-item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K6)

The K6 is a validated instrument for assessing nonspecific psychological distress [19, 20]. Its Japanese version, developed based on population surveys, is also a reliable and valid brief screening tool [21]. The K6 consists of six questionnaire items, rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“none of the time”) to 4 (“all of the time”) with total scores ranging from 0 to 24. A K6 score of ≥ 13 has been used to identify serious mental diseases with high specificity, whereas a lower threshold of 5 has been used in order to detect moderate mental diseases [20, 22]. In previous research in Japanese populations, a cut-off value of ≥ 5 was used to identify individuals with at least mild psychological distress [23, 24]. In this study, in order to capture PHC directors with a wide range of psychological distress, we used the ≥ 5 cut-off. The overall score was converted into a binary variable.

Sense of coherence (SOC)

SOC was originally developed as a 29-item scale and a shorter 13-item version, both of which were rated on a 7-point Likert scale [10–12]. In this study, we used the University of Tokyo Health Sociology version of the SOC3 scale (SOC3-UTHS), a three-item Japanese version with demonstrated reliability and validity [25]. The SOC3-UTHS consists of three items representing comprehensibility, manageability, and meaningfulness, each rated on a 7-point Likert scale, yielding total scores ranging from 3 to 21. Higher scores indicate a stronger SOC.

Statistical analysis

First, we described the characteristics of the PHC directors, presenting proportions for occupation (n [%]) and means with standard deviations (SD) for “years of administrative experience”, “maximum consecutive working days”, “average sleep duration”, “total K6 score”, and “total SOC3-UTHS score”.

Next, we dichotomized the total SOC3-UTHS score, as well as each of its three components and the maximum consecutive working days, based on the median values. Moreover, based on previous studies, average sleep duration was dichotomized at 6 h per night [26–29].

Binary logistic regression analysis was performed, using the dichotomized K6 and total SOC3-UTHS scores as the dependent and explanatory variable, respectively. The dichotomized variable for maximum consecutive working days, its interaction with the explanatory variable, and years of administrative experience as a continuous variable were included as covariates. Additional models replaced the total SOC3-UTHS score with each of the three subcomponents. Finally, we conducted similar logistic regression analyses by replacing consecutive working days with dichotomized average sleep duration as the explanatory variable. We conducted a complete-case analysis including only respondents with non-missing data for all variables used in each model. Three types of sensitivity analyses were performed; First, considering the possibility of the high prevalence of worse mental health (K6 ≥ 5), the Poisson regressions with robust variance were performed, instead of binary logistic regression models. Second, additional sensitivity analyses were conducted by treating SOC3-UTHS scores as continuous variables and by categorizing total SOC scores into tertiles, applying the same binary logistic regression models as in the main analysis. Third, the other sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding questionnaire responses from directors serving at multiple PHCs.

Ethics

This study and survey were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jichi Medical University Hospital (Approval No. 22–088). Participation in the anonymous survey was entirely voluntary, and potential participants were informed in the document indicating that refusal to participate in this study would not result in any disadvantage. Informed consent was obtained via a checkbox (consent or non-consent to participate) in the questionnaire. Only responses with confirmed consent were considered to be valid and included in the analysis.

Results

A total of 189 valid responses were obtained, yielding a valid response rate of 40.4%. All PHC directors except for one were physicians, and 12.2% of PHC directors concurrently served at multiple PHCs. The mean number of years of administrative experience was approximately 20 years. During the most demanding period, the mean maximum number of consecutive working days, average sleep duration, and mean total K6 score were just under 50 days, 5.4 h/night, and 5.9, respectively. The mean total SOC3-UTHS score was 14.5 (Table 1). Based on the median value, the cut-off value of the total SOC3-UTHS score, each component, and maximum consecutive working days was identified as 16, 5, 21 days, respectively.

Discussion

Our findings indicate that higher SOC, particularly in the domains of manageability and comprehensibility, and ensuring sleep duration were associated with better mental health among public PHC directors—those in leadership roles—during the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan.

The association between SOC and mental health is consistent with the findings of previous research targeting various occupational populations, which indicated that individuals with a stronger SOC are more resilient to stress and less likely to experience mental health issues [30–34]. Moreover, the results of the sensitivity analyses suggest a potential non-linear association between SOC and mental health, and further studies are warranted to clarify this relationship. This study also revealed that PHC directors faced extremely challenging working conditions, with an average of nearly 50 consecutive working days and only 5.4 h/night of sleep during the most demanding period of the COVID-19 pandemic. In order to help support their mental health under such challenging working conditions in future emergencies, improving their resilience to the conditions, as well as ensuring such extreme working conditions are avoided, may be important.

Of the three components of SOC, only meaningfulness was not associated with mental health in either case of adjustment. This result is inconsistent with most previous findings, which demonstrated that meaningfulness was associated with mental health [35–37]. This discrepancy may be explained by the unique nature of the stressors in this public health crisis and the role of directors as organizational leaders. During an unpredictable and unprecedented public health crisis, PHCs in Japan were tasked with various COVID-19-related responsibilities [3–7]. The unprecedented and overwhelming workload faced by PHC directors during this public health crisis may have differed qualitatively from stressors encountered during ordinary times. Additionally, as the top leaders of their organizations, PHC directors were responsible not only for managing these tasks but also for appropriately distributing them among staff members, potentially imposing significant psychological burdens. Considering such circumstances and roles, the ability to calmly and objectively comprehend and anticipate the situation (comprehensibility), as well as the belief that one is capable of effectively managing the demands (manageability), may be crucial in maintaining mental health. Conversely, meaningfulness—the belief that some difficulties are worth confronting and addressing—may have been less relevant for maintaining mental health during the pandemic for directors who are committed to protecting community health even during ordinary times. Furthermore, even if these individuals perceived their work as meaningful, the extreme task volume they faced may have rendered this sense of meaning insufficient to buffer against psychological distress.

Importantly, this survey was conducted when the Omicron variant was dominant, a period marked by explosive case numbers but lower mortality risk compared with earlier waves [4, 6, 17]. Under these circumstances, public health center directors may have questioned the extent to which they should remain responsible for intensive countermeasures. Such doubts about the meaningfulness of their role may have diminished the protective effect of this component and attenuated its association with mental health. However, simply developing workplace environments and organizational structures that enhance comprehensibility and manageability may not always be beneficial. The observed interaction between comprehensibility and consecutive working days (OR = 4.00) suggests that the protective association of higher comprehensibility with mental health may be attenuated under longer consecutive working days. One possible explanation is that the workload of PHC directors with higher comprehensibility was likely to increase, which leads to poorer mental health outcomes, because they were more likely to recognize a wide range of workplace demands or be required to take on additional responsibilities. Therefore, further research is warranted to more carefully examine the relationship between SOC and mental health among PHC directors in the context of public health crises.

The finding that the association with mental health differs between the two components—manageability and comprehensibility—and meaningfulness is consistent with the results of previous studies. This may be explained by the different mechanisms affecting mental health between meaningfulness and the other two components [38, 39]. Meaningfulness reflects a motivational dimension related to purpose and value in life, helping individuals maintain their mental health by attributing internal meaning to stressful situations. Conversely, comprehensibility and manageability are more cognitively and practically oriented, contributing to mental health through understanding and perceiving external demands. Therefore, meaningfulness may require a more stable or long-term context than the other two components in order to exert its protective influence.

Furthermore, our findings emphasize the importance of sleep as a relevant covariate in mental health models. Short sleep duration (< 6 h) was significantly associated with higher psychological distress in some models, consistent with the results of previous studies on sleep and mental health [26–29]. Nevertheless, the association between consecutive working days and mental health was not statistically significant, which is inconsistent with the findings of previous research [40, 41]. This conflict may be explained by the nature of their role as leaders of PHCs. Unlike frontline staff, who experience physically demanding tasks, they are in a position to oversee the overall leadership, decision-making, and coordination of operations across departments or organizations, serving as the top of the command structure in the Incident Command System. Therefore, directors may have had some self-direction over their schedules, for example, by avoiding meetings on weekends or limiting their duties to on-site monitoring and providing instructions to staff, which was less likely to lead to psychological distress, even if consecutive working days were long. Conversely, short sleep duration may have had a more direct and immediate impact on their mental health.

This study had some limitations. First, this study is a cross-sectional study and the association between mental health and SOC could not be demonstrated as causation. In addition, although SOC is generally considered relatively stable, reverse causality cannot be entirely ruled out. Further longitudinal studies are needed. Second, the valid response rate was approximately 40% and the number of valid responses was 189. This indicates the potential for selection bias between respondents and non-respondents. Although the survey was conducted outside the COVID-19 peak period, some PHC directors experiencing heavier workloads or more severe psychological distress may have been less likely to respond, which could have led to an underestimation of the observed associations. And the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical precision of the estimated associations, as indicated by the relatively wide confidence intervals for some results. Furthermore, because the analyses aimed to explore the associations between K6 and each SOC component, multiple regression models were conducted without formal correction for multiple comparisons. The possibility of type I error cannot be excluded, particularly given the relatively small sample size. Third, recall bias should be acknowledged because the participants were asked to retrospectively report their sleep duration and mental health (K6) during the most demanding phase of the pandemic. Fourth, the variables included in this study were limited by the design of the questionnaire.

Demographic variables such as age and sex were not collected. Therefore, we were unable to adjust for these potential confounders, which may have resulted in residual confounding. This limitation may have affected the interpretability of the observed associations, and the findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, considering the results of model fit statistics in Supplemental Table 1, where the Hosmer–Lemeshow test did not indicate a lack of fit but the Nagelkerke R² values were relatively low. Additionally, SOC was assessed using the three-item SOC3-UTHS rather than the 13- or 29-item versions, because of the limited space of the questionnaire. The 13- or 29-item versions capture more multidimensional nature of SOC. Future studies using more comprehensive SOC scales may provide a more detailed understanding of the association between SOC and mental health among PHC directors. Finally, although the proportion of missing data was relatively modest (10.1% for the outcome variable and 13.8% for maximum consecutive working days), the possibility of bias due to missing data cannot be completely excluded.

In conclusion, this questionnaire-based study examined the association between SOC and mental health among PHC directors during the most demanding COVID-19 period in Japan. Stronger SOC—particularly in the domains of comprehensibility and manageability—was associated with lower psychological distress, after adjusting for demanding working conditions, such as consecutive working days or sleep duration. Conversely, meaningfulness was not associated with mental health. However, under longer consecutive working days, the association of higher comprehensibility with mental health may be attenuated. Additionally, short sleep duration was a relevant factor associated with increased psychological distress, whereas the number of consecutive working days was not significant. In preparation for future health crises, further research designed to overcome the limitations of this study, including potential residual confounding, is needed in order to clarify how to enhance manageability and comprehensibility, as well as whether ensuring adequate sleep, could help support the mental health of PHC directors.
