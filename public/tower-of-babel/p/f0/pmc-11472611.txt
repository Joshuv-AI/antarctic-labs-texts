A novel codominant plumage color pattern of white breast patches in WugangTong geese was controlled by EDNRB2
Authors: Yunzhou Yang, Huiying Wang, Yi Liu, Shaojia Zhai, Haodong Liu, Daqian He
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Two basic plumage color patterns are observed in adult geese: solid grey (G) or colorless white (W). However, a Chinese indigenous breed, the Wugangtong goose (WGT), continues to be subject to selective breeding efforts as it displays segregation of plumage colors, including G, W, and a novel color pattern designated Wb (G with white breast circles). The underlying genetic mechanisms responsible for the Wb phenotype are yet to be determined. The current study employed the population differentiation index (FST) to analyze 90 geese exhibiting diverse plumage colors, identifying the fifth intron of EDNRB2 as a particularly noteworthy region with the highest FST values. Sanger sequencing of the region surrounding the EDNRB2 gene identified a 14-bp insertion within exon 3 as the causal mutation. The heterozygosity of this 14-bp insertion and wild-type alleles was completely associated with the Wb phenotype, thereby substantiating the codominant nature of the G and W phenotypes. An inter-species corroborated this finding cross between the graylag (no 14-bp insertion) and the swan goose (homozygous for the 14-bp insertion) breeds, as hybrids from this cross exhibited the Wb phenotype. Transcriptomes from white breast patches and gray dorsal skins of 4 Wb geese were compared. A significant downregulation of genes involved in melanin synthesis and melanocyte development was observed, including EDRNB2 and MLANA. The downregulation of MLANA indicated that the mutated EDNRB2 resulted in melanocyte loss in specific body regions, as MLANA is a marker gene for melanocytes. The findings were corroborated by melanin staining using the Mansson-Fontana method, which revealed no melanin particles deposited in the white breast patches. In summary, the gray plumage color was codominant to the white color in WGT geese, and plumage color variations were controlled by EDNRB2. The findings of our study offer valuable and practical guidance for the purification of plumage colors among WGT, whether through traditional phenotype selection or molecular breeding methods.

INTRODUCTION

Domesticated animals have been under long-term and systematic artificial selection (Hunter, 2018), and their phenotypic appearances underwent significant changes compared to their wild ancestors, especially for the coat or plumage colors, as they were always under extremely high selection pressure. Selections have left imprints on genomes, which facilitates the detection of selection signals using genetic and genomic methods (Rubin et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2018,2021; Gazda et al., 2020). In some cases, plumage color under selection was associated with laying and growth performance. Japanese quails with wild-type and brown colors had higher egg production and laying rates than those with black and yellow plumage colors (Lan et al., 2021). Before this, white plumage color has been reported to significantly increase body weight and improve carcass traits (Nasr et al., 2017). The underlying reason for this association between plumage colors and production phenotypes was not completely clear. One persuasive explanation for this was that candidate genes also activated a metabolic pathway involved in coloration, growth, and development (Minvielle et al., 1999; Matsushima et al., 2002). However, the association between plumage color and production traits should be inspected carefully due to possible false positive results. Padovana chicken, an Italian historic breed, has been selected in certain plumage colors, and the growth performance differs significantly among different lines (Rizzi 2018,2019). An association between plumage color and growth-related traits is not rigorous.

The exploration of genetic mechanisms underlying plumage color variation, in conjunction with the development of molecular assistant selection methods, has been identified as a potentially valuable approach to producing commercial breeds, notably for recessive traits for which phenotypic selection can often prove to be a lengthy process for the elimination of recessive alleles. Commercial lines typically necessitate populations exhibiting uniform plumage and coat colors, with specific colors often demonstrating greater market appeal. For instance, white is the most prevalent plumage color in most poultry species, as poultries with white feathers exhibit superior carcass cleanliness and more appealing packaging characteristics. Chickens with yellow skins controlled by BCDO2 were extremely popular in China (Rubin et al., 2010; Huang et al., 2020). These qualities may enhance consumer demand and increase the marketability of the product.

In China, poultry with disparate plumage colors is held in equal esteem due to the country's diverse dietary practices and culinary traditions. However, the underlying prerequisite for such preferences is to create different poultry breeds with different plumage colors rather than attempting to create a single breed with multiple colors. Therefore, color purification is a critical step in such breeding programs, particularly when the population is still undergoing plumage color segregation, as in the WugangTong goose case.

Solid grey and complete white are the most common plumage colors for these geese among indigenous breeds in China, while WugangTong geese (WGT) are an exception that typically show three different plumage colors: solid grey (G), white (W), and grey with white belts around breasts (Wb). The segregation of plumage color patterns among the WGT breed indicated that this phenotype is still under artificial selection. This phenomenon differed markedly from other commercial or primitive goose breeds with monotonous plumage colors (Xi et al., 2020; Wen et al., 2021). During spontaneous and planless breeding practices, the locals found that white geese in the WGT breed—although in low frequencies—were always observed among G and Wb populations, indicating a recessive heredity feature. Although we can empirically assume that WGT plumage color variations are controlled by the same loci reported in previous studies (Xi et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022), solid experiments and determinant evidence are required. Moreover, the presence of a novel phenotype of Wb might be a hint of different genetic mechanisms underlying the goose plumage color variations. These unresolved questions have never been studied before and remain to be answered. The grey WGT geese were preferred by local people as their appearance fits customers’ demands better, and they are more suitable for local cuisines. Further, breeding a completely either grey or white WGT population for commercialization was an effective and practical way to utilize and conserve this unique Chinese indigenous genetic resource.

In the present study, causal genes and mutations in the WGT breed were fine-mapped using comparative genomics and transcriptomics. EDNRB2 was identified as the major gene controlling plumage color variations among the WGT breed. The 14-bp insertion was the causal mutation, and one copy of this mutation, together with the wild-type allele, led to the Wb phenotype. Such mutations might modify the migration of melanocytes as skins in the Wb region lacked melanin synthesis and deposition.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All animal protocols were carried out according to guidelines and regulations approved by the Science and Technology Commission of Shanghai Municipality (STCSM) under license number SYXK (HU): 2015-0007.

In this study, 90 WGT geese at the age of 70 d were selected based on their plumage color patterns and classified into three groups: 1) 30 geese (male:15; female: 15) in the first group (WW) showed completely the W phenotype, 2) 30 geese (male:15; female: 15) in the second group (WB) were covered by the Wb phenotype, and 3) 30 geese (male:15; female: 15) in the third group (WG) showed classic solid grey plumage color. Another four WGT geese with the Wb phenotype (male: 2; female: 2) were selected for subsequent transcriptome sequencing and histological experiments. The gender of the geese was identified by observing the morphology of the anal vent.

Goose breeds used for comparative genomics analyses include three graylag goose breeds and three swan goose breeds, which were reported in our previous study (Yang et al., 2022). The three used graylag goose breeds were Landes (LD, solid grey, male: 6; female: 4), Roman (RM, white, male: 1; female: 9) breeds, and Hortobagyi (HT, white, male: 5; female: 5) goose breeds. LD and HT were collected from Ningbo Langde Company in the Zhejiang province, while HT was from Zhuanghang Research Farm of Shanghai Academy of Agricultural Science. Three swan goose breeds include Lion-head (ST, grey, male: 3; female: 4), Magang geese (MG, grey, male: 1; female: 9), and Zi (HZ, white, male: 3; female: 7) goose breeds. ST and MG geese were from the Shantou Baisha Research Institute of Original Species of Poultry and Stock in the Guangdong Province, and HZ geese were obtained from the Institute of Husbandry Science, Heilongjiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

An interspecies cross between male Zhedong white geese (ZDW, Anser cygnoides, n = 3, white) and 12 grey female Landes geese (LD, Anser anser, n = 12, solid grey) was created to investigate the variations in plumage color patterns.

For each individual goose, 0.5-1.0mL blood samples from the wing vein were collected and kept in vacuum glass tubes (2.0mL volume) containing the anticoagulant of sodium citrate at -20°C for subsequent genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction.

Whole-Genome Resequencing for Each Individual and Pooled Samples Among Goose Breeds

Genomic DNA was extracted using two different commercial DNA extraction Kit. They were UE-MN-BL-GDNA-250 Kit (UElandy, China) and QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Germany). DNAs with OD260/280 equal to 1.8–2.0 would be used to construct WGS libraries.

The pooling sequencing method was performed in 3 WGT groups with different plumage colors, and an equivalent amount of individual DNA was mixed into a DNA pool, which was then used for library construction and subsequent whole-genome sequencing on a DNBSEQ-T7 platform (BGI, China). Each sample in the DNA pool was recognized by a unique barcode and sequenced with 1.5× depth. The total sequencing depth for each pool was more than 30 times that of the goose genome (GCF_002166845.1). The sequencing reads of geese within the same group (WW, WG, or WB) was merged using fastp for subsequent analysis (Chen et al., 2018). The raw reads quality was assessed using fastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/); the low-quality reads and adapter sequences were removed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) (Bolger et al., 2014). The forward and reverse sequencing adapter sequences for the DNBSEQ platform were AAGTCGGAGGCCAAGCGGTCTTAGGAAGACAA and AAG TCGGATCGTAGCCATGTCGTTCTGTGAGCCAAGGAGTTG, respectively (Jeon et al., 2021).

For every sample from LD, RM, HT, ST, MG, and HZ, they were sequenced on the Illumina X10 platform and sequencing depth among all goose breeds was between 8.8 and 17.32× (Yang et al., 2022). For both sequencing platforms, PE150 strategy was applied.

After quality control, clean reads were obtained. The BWA software was used to align the quality-controlled reads to the reference goose genome (GCF_002166845.1), SAMtools was used to transform data from SAM to BAM format, and Picard (https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) was applied to mark the PCR duplicates (Li et al., 2009; Li and Durbin, 2010; Gao et al., 2016). The module of HaplotypeCaller in the GATK software package (version 3.8) produced GVCF files for all positions along the genome, and the module of GenotypeGVCFs was used for final variants calling. SelectVariants and VariantFiltration function modules with default values in the GATK software package were selected to filter SNP and Indels. Parameters for filtering variants in the GATK software were set as follows: quality by depth (QD) < 2.0, mapping quality (MQ) < 30.0, and genotyping quality (GQ) < 20. VCFtools was used in the second round of variants filtration with settings: 1) only bi-allelic sites with minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05; 2) MQ > 60; 3) missing rate < 0. 2.

Differentiation Index Values Between Different Groups

The fixation-index values (FST) represent genetic differentiation between two or more groups. FST values range from 0 (no differentiation) to 1 (complete differentiation). FST in this study was calculated using PoPoolation2 (Kofler et al., 2011) because samples with different plumage colors were mixed in DNA pools and sequenced spontaneously. Functional modules of mpileup, mpilleup2sync.jar, and fst-sliding.pl in PoPoolation2 were used to calculate the FST between 2 subpopulations. The minimum count of minor alleles was set to 6, the minimum and maximum coverage were set to 30 and 500, respectively. The window size and step size in the module of fst-sliding.pl were 100 and 50, respectively. Differentiation between WG and WW groups was firstly compared to confirm the causal gene controlling white and grey plumages. Thereafter, WB group was used to compare to WG and WW groups, respectively. If there were novel different loci controlling the Wb phenotype, they should be detected in these two different comparative genomic analyses. Finally, the size of each DNA pool was 30, and default values were used for other parameters in PoPoolation2. The threshold of FST values was set to 0.6 arbitrarily in this study to define candidate genomic fragments under selection.

Validation of Variants in Exons 3/4 and 5′Untranslated Region of EDNRB2 Using Sanger Sequencing

Since significantly high signals of genomic sweep analyses were detected in the EDNRB2 gene, we designed primers to verify the variants associated with plumage color patterns, especially the 14-bp insertion in exons (3 and 4) and partial regions in 5′UTR (Table S1). The primers were designed using the NCBI tools (Blast and Primer Search) and synthesized at Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). PCR was carried out using Sangon 2× SanTaq PCR Mix solutions according to the manufacturer's instructions (Sangon, China); the PCR products were purified and sequenced using the Sanger method at Sangon Biotech. Sequence alignments and genotyping were carried out using ChromasPro (v2.1.3).

The reported 18 bp deletion in KIT gene was also genotyped in WGT geese by designing specific primers (Table S1) (Wen et al., 2021). In total, 18 samples (WB: 5; WW: 6; WG: 5) from WGT were genotyped at the KIT loci using the method mentioned above.

Transcriptome Sequencing of Skin Samples From Wb Geese

The samples from dorsal back skin covered with grey plumage and white breast patch skin covered with white plumage were collected from four WGT geese (Wb) and frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent RNA-seq assays. The transcriptome sequencing was carried out at Novogene (Beijing, China) with nearly 6-Gb raw data per sample (∼6x in sequencing depth for geese) for each sample. The prepared libraries using freshly extracted RNA were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE150). FastQC was used to assess the sequencing quality of raw reads, and Cutadapt (v1.15) was used to filter out low-quality reads (Martin, 2011; Auwera et al., 2013). STAR and edgeR were used for read alignment to the genome (GCF_002166845.1) and differential gene expression analysis, respectively (Robinson et al., 2009; Dobin et al., 2013; Gao et al., 2016). Genes with absolute values of log2(fold-change) > 1 and adjusted p-values < 0.05 were labeled as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses were conducted using GOseq software (Kanehisa and Goto, 2000; Young et al., 2010; Kanehisa, 2019; Kanehisa et al., 2023). The detected terms or pathways with adjusted P-values < 0.05 were considered significant. ggplot2 in R software was used to plot KEGG pathways.

Histological Analysis of Skin Samples

Hematoxylin-Eosin Staining (HE): Skins samples were collected immediately after the geese were slaughtered and stored in 4% paraformaldehyde for at least 24 h. Then, reshaping by removing the extra tissues (feathers, fat, and muscle), the samples were dehydrated in ethanol and xylene with different concentrations and embedded in paraffin (65°C) for 1 h. Subsequently, the embedded samples were cut into 4-μm-thick slices and subjected to hematoxylin staining for 5 min. Then, the sections were dehydrated in 95% ethanol for 1 min and stained in eosin for 15 s. Before sealing with neutral gum, the sections were dehydrated in graded ethanol. The images were captured under the upright microscope (Nikon Eclipse E100) using the Nikon DS-U3 software.

Melanin-Staining: For Masson–Fontana staining, the solution (G1067, ServiceBio, China) was prepared just before usage and followed manufacturer's instructions. The paraffin sections were dewaxed and dehydrated in xylene and absolute ethanol at different concentrations. Then, the sections were immersed in Masson–Fontana solution for 12 to 18 h and washed in water for 3 to 5 min before hyposulphite treatment for 5 min. Finally, the sections were immersed in 95% absolute ethanol for dehydration and xylene to transparentize the tissue before sealing with neutral gum for imaging.

Validation of Populations by Crossing Geese With and Without the Causal Mutation

In the inter-species cross experiments, male Zhedong white geese (ZDW, Anser cygnoides, n = 3) and female LD geese (Anser anser, n = 12) were selected to produce hybrids carrying expected genotypes. ZDW geese carried 2 copies of the 14-bp alleles, while the Landes geese carried no mutated alleles at the same loci. Subsequently, all hybrid offspring were heterozygous for the 14-bp insertion and the wild-type alleles. Overlapped reproduction periods for the 2 breeds were between December and March in a year. This short reproduction period made it difficult to collect many offspring. Eggs from this crossed population in these three months were collected, disinfected, and hatched every week in a Keyu incubator (Shandong, China). Different temperatures and humidity during 31 incubation days were set manually according to manufacturer's instructions. Goslings at hatch (1–2 days- old) were marked by wearing unique shank rings for individual identification. Plumage color patterns for both goslings and adults at the age of four months were recorded.

Haplotypes Reconstruction and Frequency Calculation Among Different Goose Breeds

Variants call format (vcf) files holding all variants among six swan and graylag goose breeds were firstly phased using Beagle 4.0 software, and defaults values for all parameters were selected (Browning and Browning, 2007). After phasing, variants that showed genotyping quality (GQ) lower than 0.9 in 10% of samples and minor allele frequency (MAF) lower than 0.05 were removed. Allele frequency of bi-allelic variants in all goose breeds (MG, ST, HZ, HT, RM, and LD) was obtained using the –min-alleles 2, –max-alleles 2, and –freq2 function of the VCFtools package (Danecek et al., 2011). Thereafter, the difference in allele difference (AFD) between white (swan: HZ; graylag: HT and RM) and grey (swan: MG and ST; graylag: LD) goose breeds were calculated using basic statistic functions in R software (v4.4.1). Haplotype reconstruction and haplotype frequency in each goose breed were carried out using modified home-in R scripts (https://github.com/YunzhouYang/Geese_Coat_Color).

These geese have black beaks and brown eyes (type 1 in Figure 1A). The second plumage color pattern is grey with white breast patches (Wb). These geese also have black beaks and brown eyes (type 2 in Figure 1A). Investigations of Plumage Colors in Offsprings of WGT Geese With G and Wb Phenotypes

Local farmers applied the traditional strategy for breeding grey WGT geese. Geese with G and Wb phenotypes were retained in the breeding population, while those with the W plumage were separated out for breeding a subpopulation with white feathers. The offspring produced from the reserved breeding parents were segregated according to plumage color patterns. Before molting, the goslings displayed three kinds of plumage colors and are termed types 1, 2, and 3. Types 1 and 2 showed grey-down colors, but geese with type 2 color patterns show a white breast patch (yellow arrow in Figure 2A). Goslings with type 3 plumage color patterns were covered with yellowish down feathers, while the other body parts (beak, eyes, flippers, and shanks) lacked melanin deposition (Figure 2A). Subsequently, geese showing types 1, 2, and 3 plumage color patterns would exhibit the three classic plumage color patterns in adulthood (Figure 1, Figure 2B). These results confirmed that loci controlling G and Wb plumage colors were also responsible for the W phenotype in WGT geese.Figure 2Plumage variations in offspring of grey WGT geese. Crosses Between White Swan Geese (A. cygnoides) and Grey Graylag Geese (A. anser)

To confirm the codominance led by the mutations in EDNRB2 coding regions, a white swan goose breed (ZDW) was crossed with a graylag goose breed (LD). LD did not carry mutations in the coding regions, whereas ZDW carried both mutated alleles at 230,180 and 230,787 bp. Therefore, offspring from this cross were heterozygous at both loci. A total of 69 hybrids of this cross showing grey plumage color patterns were collected. These geese were covered with grey plumage, neck stripes, and white breast patches (Figure 4). Despite the lack of exact calculations, the area size of white patches among geese varied across interspecies hybrids and was enlarged in most hybrid birds compared to WGT geese with the Wb phenotype. Transcriptomic Difference Between Pigmented and Depigmented Regions Within Wb Geese

Dorsal skins (n = 4) and skins located in the white breast (n = 4) from Wb geese were collected for transcriptome sequencing. The read number and average depth of raw data for all eight samples were 25,822,799 ± 2,799,698 and 6.92 ± 0.75×, respectively. After removing adapters and reads with low quality, the read number and average depth of clean data for all samples became 24,541,231 ± 2,753,234 and 6.57 ± 0.73×, respectively. Most clean reads (95.03%) were mapped to the reference genome, while 72.62% of them were properly mapped. These properly mapped reads covered 14.19% of the genome with an averaged depth of 4.64×.

A total of 1,416 genes were differentially expressed between grey dorsal skins and white breast skins, including 1,127 up- and 289 downregulated DEGs, respectively (Table S4). The DEGs comprised melanin synthesis- or melanocyte development-related genes with high expression levels in dorsal skins, including TYRP1, TYR, MLANA, OCA2, EDNRB2, KIT, and MYO5A (Table S4). Assigning all DEGs into KEGG pathways resulted in nine significantly enriched pathways, including Wnt signaling (gga04310), melanogenesis (gga04916), and Hedgehog signaling (gga04340) pathways which were related to melanin synthesis (Table S5). GO enrichment analysis did not reveal significantly enriched terms related to melanin synthesis (Table S6).

Effects of EDNRB2 Mutation on Melanocyte Distribution

Melanin synthesis was assessed in the dorsal skins (S1 in Figure 6A), skins in white breast (S3 in Figure 6A), and skins covered with grey plumages (S2 in Figure 6A) using the Masson-Fontana method. The results showed that the feather follicles were intact for skins in all three body parts (Figures 6B–6D), and melanin particles in the dorsal skins were stained black and observed clearly (S1 in Figure 6A, 6E). Moreover, melanin particles were observed in grey breast skins (S2 in Figure 6A, 6F), but the intensity was lower than that of the dorsal skin. In white breast regions (S3 in Figure 6A), no melanin deposition was detected, and no black-stained particles were observed (Figure 6G).

Haplotype Dissection Around the EDNRB2 Gene

We also constructed haplotypes by extracting variants with allele frequency difference (AFD) bigger than 0.9 after they were phased by BEAGLE4. Consequently, 59 variants were selected from the regions of EDNRB2 and its 3′UTR in both swans and graylag geese to construct 71 haplotypes. However, most haplotypes occurred in very low frequency (< 0.1) and could not be used for further analysis (Figure 7A–7C; Table S7). Hap1CS and Hap7CS were only observed in HZ geese at 0.65 and 0.1 frequencies, respectively. These haplotypes, combined with five other low-frequency haplotypes detected in HZ geese, contained the 14-bp insertion. Hap2CS was only observed in grey geese with medium frequency (0.45 and 0.36 in MG and ST, respectively). Furthermore, Hap1CS, Hap2CS, and Hap7CS were not detected in the graylag geese, whereas Hap3CS and Hap5CS were only observed in the white and grey graylag geese, respectively (Hap3CS: 0.4 and 0.5 in HT and RM, respectively; Hap5CS: 0.32 in LD). In addition, Hap4CS was detected in LD, HT, and RM breeds with frequencies of 0.16, 0.15, and 0.35, respectively. On the other hand, Hap6CS was observed in white HT geese with a frequency of 0.1 but was absent in RM geese.

Among the graylag geese, 21 variants were selected in the 5′UTR with allele frequency difference (AFD) > 0.9, and 35 haplotypes were detected in all six goose breeds (Table S7). Among these, four haplotypes with frequencies > 0.1 were used for downstream analysis. Within the graylag geese, Hap1AS was fixed in white goose breeds (HT and RM) and had only one copy in the grey breed (5% in LD geese). Additionally, Hap1AS was observed in all swan goose breeds with frequencies of 0.45, 0.07, and 0.05 in HZ, ST, and MG, respectively. Hap2AS was only observed in grey LD geese with a frequency of 0.44 and was absent in all the other five breeds. Hap3AS was distributed in all three swan breeds (A. cygnoides) with frequencies of 0.95, 0.93, and 0.35 in MG, ST, HZ, respectively, whereas Hap4AS was detected only in HZ with a frequency of 0.1.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we identified EDNRB2, as the causal gene for a novel plumage color pattern in WGT geese. The 14-bp insertion and other hitchhiked variants around this variant were completely associated with the Wb phenotype. Herein, multi-omics provides further valuable information to understand the mechanisms underlying plumage variations among WGT geese.

Wb Phenotype Among WGT Geese was Controlled by EDNRB2

Based on the segregation features of the G, W, and Wb phenotypes, we speculated that the Wb loci should be linked with the c loci, which was reported as a 14-bp insertion in EDNRB2 exon 3 (Xi et al., 2020; Ouyang et al., 2022). The possibility that the novel phenotypes in WGT breed were controlled by novel mutation couldn't be excluded until extremely strong selective sweep signals were detected in EDNRB2. Interestingly, significant signals were only detected between grey (G) and white geese (W), while no clear selection sweeps were found between Wb and G/W geese. The unique peak signal detected in the EDNRB2 gene demonstrated that EDNRB2 is the sole gene responsible for plumage color variations observed in this species. EDNRB2 and EDN3 constitutes a signaling pathway known widely as “EDN-EDNRB signaling pathway” which play biological roles in a broad spectrum of physiological events, including neural crest cell (NCC) proliferation, differentiation and migration, pigmentation, and hypertrophy of vascular smooth muscle cells, etc. (Bondurand et al., 2018). In birds, the EDN3-EDNRB2 signaling pathway regulates melanocyte development, and it is essential for final pigmentation and plumage patterns (Miwa et al., 2006,2007; Kinoshita et al., 2014; Li et al., 2015). EDN3-EDNRB2 signaling pathway exerts effects on downstream genes by activating other pathways of PI3kinase and PLCβ (resulting in fast calcium release and diacylglycerol production), and adenylyl cyclase (AC)/cAMP/CREB (Takagi et al., 1995). These serial reactions lead to upregulation of transcription factors involved in cell growth, survival, and differentiation (Rosanò et al., 2004; Bagnato and Rosanò, 2008; Horinouchi et al., 2013). Our results again demonstrated the critical functions of the EDN3-EDNRB2 signaling pathway in melanocyte developments.

However, results in this study were different from previous research, which detected KIT as the major gene regulating plumage color variations among 6 swan goose breeds (Wen et al., 2021). In their study, an 18 bp deletion in the KIT intron was completely associated with white feathered geese, indicating contributions of KIT to swan goose plumage color variations. Different genes that control the same plumage colors were common in poultries, such as MITF and EDRNB2 both leading to white feathered ducks (Li et al., 2015; Zhou et al., 2018). This finding referred that different genetic basis were responsible for the plumage color variations due to genetic background differences. As high selection signal was detected close to KIT, the 18 bp deletion in KIT was also tested in WGT geese, but no significant association was found. The 18 bp deletion was surprisingly very common in WGT, as the allele frequency of this mutation was very high in G, Wb, and W geese (Table 1). Currently, little evidence about the effects of the 18 bp deletion in KIT on the plumage color variations was reported. However, KIT did regulate the coat color formation (Pielberg et al., 2002). The high signal detected in 5′-UTR of KIT might indicated a transcription regulating mechanism which remains to be studied. This makes the mechanism of plumage variations in WGT much more complicated due to possible synergistic effects among pigmentation genes, which has been reported previously (Candille et al., 2007).

The Codominance Between Grey and White Plumage Colors Among WGT Geese

Results in this study showed that that only those geese carrying the heterozygotes of the 14-bp insertion and wild alleles showed the Wb phenotype (Table 1) while those with homozygous of either 14-bp or wild allele were covered with G and W phenotypes. This phenomenon indicated that G and W phenotypes were codominant instead of completely dominant to each other. Furthermore, an interspecies cross between swan and graylag geese was constructed. The parents in this cross were pure for either the mutated (W) or wild-type alleles (G) (Xi et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2022). Hence, their hybrid offspring were heterozygotes at the candidate locus, providing genetic materials that confirm the codominance of the white and grey plumage color patterns as expected. The exact same mutation of EDNRB2 in another swan breed resulted in a recessive white plumage color. The heterozygous geese in their research were all gray, which was totally different from the Wb phenotype among WGT geese (Xi et al., 2020). It is still hard to summarize the reason for these pleotropic effects among goose breeds based on currently limited information and we tend to attribute this to diverse genetic backgrounds. Even in WGT, geese with certain genotypes showing unexpected phenotypes could be observed. Two birds were found to carry both 14 bp and wild alleles but were covered by grey plumage (not Wb). This referred that there might be some other loci involved in plumage color variations, such as the KIT discussed above.

This finding also suggested that one copy of 14 bp insertion was enough to modify melanocyte migration to produce Wb phenotype, while two copies would stop the migration, leading to W phenotype. This was consistent with that from a previous study on duck plumage color variations, in which they confirmed the dosage effects of mutations in EDNRB2 on duck plumage color variations (Xi et al., 2021).

Exceptions in W Group Indicated Independent Selection on the Extremely Long Haplotypes Around EDNRB2

The genotypes among WGT geese also suggested that not all white WGT geese were homozygous at the 14-bp insertion locus. Among these, one bird harbored only one copy of 14-bp insertion, and they were expressed as white plumage color. Paradoxically, other studies claimed that the 14-bp insertion was fixed in white geese. This could be simply attributed to incorrect Sanger genotyping or phenotype record errors. However, we speculated the presence of other causal mutations in these birds. The genome fragment surrounding the EDNRB2 gene were arranged in an extremely long haplotype (15.75–15.8 Mb) (Ouyang et al., 2022), and mutations in the 5′UTR could also result in white plumage color variation (Yang et al., 2022). The putative combinations of the 14 bp and other mutations in 5′UTR might underlie white plumages, especially the sp loci involved in white plumage color formation among graylag geese (Jerome, 1954; Yang et al., 2022). Our haplotype constructions in all six graylag and swan goose breeds showed that swan geese carry haplotypes, which were detected as the causal ones for white plumages in graylag geese (Hap1AS). The frequency of Hap1AS in the white swan breed (HZ) was much higher than in grey breeds (ST and MG, Figure 7). This phenomenon indicated that 5′UTR regions have been under selection for white plumage color in both graylag geese and swan geese. A previous study demonstrated that all domesticated geese had the same ancestor 3.4 million years ago and carried a mutual gene introgression since domestication (Heikkinen et al., 2015,2020); hence, the swan geese might have retained all variants from common ancestors while graylag geese retained those in 5′UTR. People should pay attention to extremely large difference in genetic background between swan and graylag goose breeds which might produce false positive signals. To some extent, our finding broadens our understanding of the genetic basis underlying parallel evolution.

How Did the Mutation Change the Migration Path

One of the mechanisms through which EDN3-EDNRB2 controls melanoblasts (melanocyte stem cells) migration is that normal expression of both EDN3 and EDNRB2 facilitates the melanoblasts to overcome the effects of the inhibitors in the skins of various body parts. This guaranteed that the melanoblasts can be colonized all over the body (Oakley and Tosney, 1991; Oakley et al., 1994; Santiago and Erickson, 2002). The temporal changes in the functions and the levels of these molecules alter the adhesiveness along melanoblast migratory routes and lead to phenotype variations, such as dominant white spots and piebald due to mutated KIT and EDNRB, respectively (Wehrle‐Haller, 2003; Park et al., 2003). Mutated EDNRB2 could disrupt melanocyte migration and lead to poultry plumage variations, which were summarized in Figure S1. The first category of mutations modified the sulfur-containing amino acid (Cysteine, Cys) to Phenylalanine (Phe), and this led to the loss of disulfide bond when EDNRB2 began to form their secondary structure. This mutation could stop melanocyte migration almost completely and only left some pigmented spots along dorsal-ventral lines in chicken (Kinoshita et al., 2014). The 14-bp insertion in EDNRB2 exon 3 might also resulted in white mutants following this mechanism as it produced a pre-mature stop codon which deleted most amino acids, including the Cys (Xi et al., 2020). Though the previous study claimed the 14 bp insertion control the white plumage color through decreased expression levels. Mutations in other coding regions of EDNRB2 could also lead to white plumages but always with large parts of pigmented feathers along the body, such as the Arg332His substitution (Miwa et al., 2007; Kinoshita et al., 2014; Li et al., 2015). Mutations in non-coding regions of EDNRB2 didn't change the secondary structure but only decreased gene expression. This mechanism could also result in plumage variations but with less influence on melanocytes migration (Yang et al., 2022).

The different types of white plumage colors in species governed by EDNRB2 demonstrated 2 facts. Firstly, mutations in EDNRB2 dispute the migration path of melanoblasts (melanocyte stem cells) in the embryonic stage, as reported before (Harris et al., 2008). In this study, melanocyte loss could be validated by the absence of melanin particles and much lower expression levels of MLANA in neck skins of white breast patches (Figure 6D, 6G and Table S4). This was also the reason why pigmentation-related genes were downregulated in neck skins of white breast patches, and the singling pathway of melanogenesis can be enriched (Figure 5B). Thus, we speculated that mutations in EDNRB2 decreased its expression and hindered suppression of the inhibitors’ functions. Although this study elucidated the functions of EDNRB2 during pigmentation in geese, many unknown aspects, including the mechanism of melanocyte migration in different body parts and its interaction effects with different genetic backgrounds, need an in-depth investigation. Moreover, the influence of epigenomic changes deserves to be studied in the future as it can interact with environmental factors, leading to the emergence of specific phenotypes (Shokri et al., 2023; Jafari et al., 2023). At times, multi-level interactions between the genome, epigenome, and environmental factors might occur (Mohamadipoor et al., 2021; Shahsavari et al., 2023). Furthermore, numerous lines of evidence suggest the influence of epigenome variation on health and production (Safaei et al., 2022; Bordbar et al., 2022).

CONCLUSIONS

Herein, we located a well-known causative gene EDNRB2 in the WGT goose breed, responsible for the unique feather color phenotype. The genetic locus controlling Wb phenotype also led to goose white plumage colors among swan geese (the 14-bp insertion in EDNRB2 exon 3). However, Wb was produced only in the heterozygous state, proving that the gray and white feather color in swan geese populations constitutes a pair of codominant traits. Furthermore, we confirmed that the abnormal distribution of melanocytes and null melanin synthesis are the direct causes of the Wb phenotype due to the 14-bp insertion in the gene. According to results in this study, same gene can exert different effects on plumage color variations due to genetic backgrounds and potential synergistic effects between genes. Taken together, these findings provided novel insights into the molecular mechanisms of plumage color variations underlying artificial selection.
