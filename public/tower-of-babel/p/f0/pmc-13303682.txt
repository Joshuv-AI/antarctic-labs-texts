Antitumor effect of the AP5Z1 small-molecule inhibitor PGG in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Zhipeng Quan, Qianxin Hu, Mingjiang Liu, Guangtao Ma, Zhilong He, Yu Liu, Yingjian Wei, Yige Wei, Zheng Xu, Chuang Qin, Zeyuan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, small-molecule inhibitors targeting AP5Z1 were developed, and their antitumor activity against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and related mechanisms were evaluated. Candidate molecules were initially screened via computer-aided virtual screening, and CCK-8 assays were performed to verify their inhibitory effects on the proliferation of HCC cells. The interactions between compounds and targets were confirmed using techniques such as docking simulations, dynamics analyses on a molecular level, cellular thermal shift assays (CETSAs)and Surface Plasmon Resonance(SPR). In vitro cellular experiments, including colony formation, flow cytometric apoptosis detection, Western blotting, and dual-fluorescent autophagy systems, were performed to assess how pentagalloylglucose (PGG) modulates the malignant phenotypes of HCC. A subcutaneous xenograft model was constructed using nude mice to analyze the suppressive effect of PGG on tumor growth in vivo. Through virtual screening and activity validation, PGG was identified as the core candidate molecule. PGG stably binds to the predicted binding pocket of AP5Z1, forming an extensive hydrogen bond network. In vitro tests showed that PGG inhibits the proliferation of HCC cells and induces their apoptosis, accompanied by changes in autophagy-related markers. The results of in vivo experiments confirmed that PGG significantly decreases the volume and weight of tumors, decreases the expression of AP5Z1 and Ki67, and increases the levels of markers associated with apoptosis and autophagy. PGG acts by inhibiting AP5Z1-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of the tumor suppressor PTEN, thereby blocking the downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. Pentagalloylglucose (PGG) acts as an orally available small-molecule inhibitor targeting AP5Z1 and suppresses the progression of HCC both in vitro and in vivo by regulating the ubiquitination of PTEN and downstream signaling cascades. These findings indicate that PGG is a novel candidate for targeted therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12032-026-03322-6.

Background

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the predominant form of primary liver cancer, comprising 75–85% of all diagnosed cases of liver cancer. HCC is the third leading contributor to cancer-related deaths worldwide and plays a significant role in cancer-associated mortality [1]. With deeper research on the molecular mechanisms underlying HCC, targeted therapy has emerged as a key strategy for improving patient prognosis. However, as traditional targeted agents like sorafenib and lenvatinib can lead to drug resistance, new, highly effective, low-toxicity targeted drugs need to be developed [2]. Small-molecule inhibitors have become an important part of cancer research in recent years. They typically include organic molecules with a molecular weight below 1000 Da. They function by binding to the active site of target proteins and interfering with their function. Their mechanisms of action primarily include competing with substrates for binding sites, inducing changes in protein structure, or preventing necessary conformational transitions, thereby decreasing the activity of the target protein [3]. As they can easily pass through the cell membrane, small-molecule inhibitors have become an important part of modern drug development. They are extensively used in various fields, covering anti-cancer, anti-viral, and immune regulation directions [4, 5].

Members of the adapter protein (AP) complex family are heterotetramers; their main function is to guide proteins into transport vesicles. AP5Z1 is a subunit of the adapter protein complex AP-5. It is a newly identified and relatively understudied member associated with HCC. It plays a role in the transport of cargo in late endosomes, including the movement and organization of the crucial liver cancer molecular indicator, Golgi Membrane Protein 1 (GOLM1), within cells [6, 7]. In previous studies, we discovered that AP5Z1 acts as a key oncogenic scaffold protein, which recruits the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIM21 to form a complex and mediates the ubiquitination and degradation of the tumor suppressor protein PTEN, thereby relieving its inhibitory effect on the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. This drives the proliferation of HCC, apoptosis resistance, and autophagy imbalance, making it an important upstream regulatory factor driving the malignant progression of liver cancer. AP5Z1 is significantly positively correlated with poor patient prognosis, which suggests that it has strong potential as a druggable tumor-associated therapeutic target [8].

In this study, a small-molecule inhibitor targeting AP5Z1 was developed, and its anti-HCC efficacy was evaluated. Based on the predicted three-dimensional structure of AP5Z1 from AlphaFold, a computational virtual screening strategy was implemented to identify pentagalloylglucose (penta-O-galloyl-β-D-glucose; PGG) as a candidate molecule from a library of 75,000 compounds. Subsequently, by conducting cellular thermal shift assays, molecular dynamics simulations, and functional validation, PGG was found to specifically bind to AP5Z1, exert inhibitory effects on AP5Z1, and subsequently modulate downstream signaling pathways and the ubiquitination level of target proteins, thereby regulating the progression of HCC.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The SNU-449 and LM3 cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and grown in RPMI-1640 medium (GIBCO, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cultures were kept in a stable-temperature incubator set at 37 °C under a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂.

Virtual screening and molecular docking of small-molecule inhibitors

Using the Schrödinger Maestro 12.8 platform, virtual screening was performed against HUMAN AP5Z1, with structural visualization performed in PyMol. The AP5Z1 structure from AlphaFold was prepared using the software tool Protein Preparation Wizard. A 20 Å cubic receptor grid was generated based on SiteMap predictions. Compound libraries (total 75,000 molecules from MCE), including HY-L001P Bioactive Compound Library Plus and HY-L901 50 K Diversity Library, were processed using LigPrep to generate 3D structures. Multi-stage virtual screening was performed in the Virtual Screening Workflow using the Glide module, proceeding through HTVS(High-Throughput Virtual Screening), SP(Standard Precision), and XP(Extra Precision) protocols to identify high-affinity candidates. Technical support for this part of the study was provided by MCE Biotech, Shanghai.

Molecular dynamics simulations

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2022. The receptor was modeled with the Amber14SB Force Field, and ligand parameters were derived from GAFF2(General Amber Force Field 2) using the AutoFF(Automatic Force Field) tool. The system was solvated in a 1 nm³ TIP3P(Transferable Intermolecular Potential 3-Point) water box and neutralized with ions. PME(Particle-Mesh Ewald) electrostatics (1 nm cutoff) and SHAKE bond constraints were applied with a timestep of 1 fs using the Verlet integrator. After the energy was minimized using steepest descent and conjugate gradient methods, a 100 ns NPT(Isobaric-Isothermal Ensemble) simulation was run at 310 K and 1 bar. System stability and interactions were assessed by conducting trajectory analysis of RMSD(Root Mean Square Deviation), RMSF(Root Mean Square Fluctuation), Rg(Radius of Gyration), SASA(Solvent Accessible Surface Area), and binding free energy.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

After the cells were lysed, the supernatant was collected and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the corresponding antibodies. Protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were added, and incubation was performed for 2 h at 4 °C. Immune complexes were washed, eluted by boiling in loading buffer (Solarbio, China), and evaluated by Western blotting analysis.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA)

After routine culture reached the logarithmic growth phase, cells were switched to a medium containing either PGG (MCE, USA) or DMSO (Solarbio, China) and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were heated across a temperature gradient from 50 to 75 °C for 3 min to induce protein thermal denaturation, followed by Western blotting detection to analyze the soluble proportion of the target protein at different temperatures. Thermal denaturation curves were plotted, and melting temperatures (Tm) were calculated. The ΔTm value between the drug treatment group and the control group was compared to evaluate the effect of the compound on the thermal stability of the target protein. A ΔTm ≥ 2–3 °C was considered to indicate a significant thermal shift effect.

GFP-mCherry-LC3 dual fluorescent autophagy detection system

Cells were transfected with the GFP-mCherry-LC3 plasmid (Beyotime, China) for 24 h before they were observed and imaged via confocal microscopy. Red and yellow fluorescent puncta represented autophagosomes and autolysosomes, respectively, allowing assessment of the extent of autophagy.

Other methods

Additional experimental methods implemented in this study, including Western blotting (WB) analysis, CCK-8 assay, colony formation assay, flow cytometry assay, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay, are provided in the supplementary materials.

Subcutaneous xenograft model

Tumor cells were prepared as single-cell suspensions (approximately 1 × 10⁶ – 5 × 10⁶ cells/100 µL) and subcutaneously injected into the flanks of nude mice. Tumor volume was measured regularly (V = 0.5 × L × W²). When tumors grew to predetermined sizes, mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues were harvested for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. The data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The differences between any two groups were determined by conducting Student’s t-tests, whereas those among multiple groups were determined by conducting one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All differences among and between groups were considered to be statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Results

Virtual screening of small-molecule inhibitors targeting AP5Z1

Virtual screening was performed against the AP5Z1 protein. The binding affinity between the target and compounds, along with compound structures, was evaluated, and docking scores were calculated. Based on these scores, the top 200 compounds from both libraries were selected for output, from which 10 candidate compounds were selected for analyzing the activity (Table 1).

Next, the anti-cancer effects of the candidate compounds were determined by conducting the CCK-8 assay to identify the optimal small-molecule compound for suppressing the growth of HCC cells. The experimental results demonstrated that PGG inhibited the growth of HCC cells (Fig. 1A and B). PGG is a gallotannin compound that exerts antitumor effects by inducing apoptosis and autophagy. It stabilizes β-catenin by inhibiting the activity of GSK3β, thereby activating the expression of downstream genes related to apoptosis and autophagy. In pancreatic cancer organoid models, PGG significantly blocks UBE2T-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of p53, resulting in the accumulation of the p53 protein and downregulation of the expression of the key ribonucleotide reductase subunits RRM1/RRM2, ultimately inhibiting tumor proliferation. Moreover, PGG has pleiotropic activities in biological systems, including scavenging free radicals, inhibiting gene mutation, and modulating inflammatory factors, thereby exerting anticonvulsant, cardioprotective, antiallergic, hypocholesterolemic, and antitumor effects [9–14].

As PGG exerts its antitumor effects by inducing apoptosis and autophagy, as well as by inhibiting ubiquitination pathways, its mechanism of action closely aligns with that of AP5Z1. Moreover, PGG can inhibit the proliferation of HCC cells. Therefore, it was selected as the core candidate molecule for subsequent pharmacodynamic validation.

Molecular docking simulation of AP5Z1 with the small-molecule inhibitor PGG

Molecular docking simulations were performed to investigate the molecular binding mode between AP5Z1 and PGG. Systematic analysis of the 3D conformation (Fig. 2A) and the 2D interaction network (Fig. 2B) revealed the mechanism underlying the binding of PGG to human AP5Z1. The results showed that PGG embeds into the predicted binding pocket of AP5Z1 (Fig. 2A; left), exhibiting excellent spatial complementarity and forming a stable complex. The magnified 3D view (Fig. 2A right) highlighted multiple strong hydrogen bond interactions between the ligand and the protein, involving key amino acid residues, such as TRP-441, ARG-548, MET-1, HIE-402, SER-403, and ALA-362. Over 80% of these residues formed hydrogen bonds that were ≤ 2.1 Å in length, demonstrating that the complex was stabilized mainly via intensive hydrogen bonding. The 2D binding mode diagram summarized the full interaction profile in a planar layout, additionally annotating the involved residues, including ARG-365, GLN-551, and ALA-547, confirming the details observed in the 3D representation. To summarize, PGG specifically binds to human AP5Z1 by inserting into the predicted binding pocket and establishing a robust hydrogen bond network. Among them, short-length hydrogen bonds contributed by residues such as TRP-441, SER-403, and ARG-548 play a key role in maintaining the stability of the complex.

Molecular dynamics simulation of AP5Z1 with PGG

Although previous studies have identified key binding sites between PGG and the AP5Z1 protein via structural analyses, the dynamic stability of the ligand-protein complex, the conformational changes induced by the inhibitor, and quantitative characterization of binding affinity still need to be comprehensively investigated. In this study, MD simulations were implemented along with free energy calculations to dynamically track the interaction between PGG and AP5Z1 at the atomic level, aiming to elucidate the binding mechanism and functional regulatory relationship from a dynamic perspective.

The results of the MD simulations showed that the AP5Z1-PGG complex reached systemic equilibrium after 25 ns and demonstrated high conformational stability (Fig. 2C); a reduction in the radius of gyration suggested that the complex had a more compact structure (Fig. 2D), while minor fluctuations in solvent-accessible surface area reflected limited influence of the binding on the microenvironment around the small molecule (Fig. 2E). Additionally, the low root mean square fluctuation of residues (Fig. 2F) and the presence of approximately five stable hydrogen bonds within the system (Fig. 2G) illustrated favorable interaction and structural rigidity between PGG and AP5Z1. Finally, free energy landscape analysis showed that the dynamic process converged to a distinct low-energy basin (Fig. 2H). These findings comprehensively demonstrated strong binding stability and greater energetic compatibility between PGG and the target protein AP5Z1.

Validation of PGG targeting AP5Z1 via in vitro experiments

The binding stability between AP5Z1 and PGG was determined by conducting the CETSA. The results showed that compared to the DMSO-only control group, the thermal denaturation curve of AP5Z1 in the PGG experimental group (10 µM, 24 h) shifted to the right. As the temperature increased, PGG interacted with AP5Z1 and stabilized it (Fig. 3A and B). Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was performed to determine whether PGG directly binds to AP5Z1. The binding response exhibited a substantial dose-dependent increase with the concentrations of PGG ranging from 6.25 to 100 µM. Kinetic fitting revealed that the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD) was 15.45 µM, indicating a moderate direct interaction between PGG and AP5Z1. The sensorgram showed rapid association, reversible dissociation, a stable baseline, and no noticeable non-specific adsorption (Fig. 3C).

Suppression of AP5Z1-Mediated ubiquitination and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway by PGG

Next, the ability of PGG to inhibit the function of AP5Z1 and its role in facilitating PTEN ubiquitination were investigated, along with its modulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade. For this, the HCC cell lines SNU-449 and LM3 were treated with either DMSO or PGG (10 µM for 24 h), and a notable decrease in the expression of AP5Z1 was found after treatment with PGG (Fig. 4A). The results from the ubiquitination assays demonstrated that PGG significantly decreased the ubiquitination of the PTEN protein, supporting the notion that PGG hinders the ubiquitination process mediated by AP5Z1 (Fig. 4B). Moreover, WB analysis of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway showed that PGG effectively decreased the phosphorylation levels of crucial components within this signaling network (Fig. 4C). Hence, these findings highlight the role of PGG in inhibiting the pathway by targeting AP5Z1.

Next, we co-transfected Flag-AP5Z1, HA-PTEN, and His-TRIM21 plasmids into 293T and MHCC-97 H cells. The cells were treated with either DMSO or PGG 12 h after transfection. The results of the Co-IP assays indicated that PGG treatment did not affect the protein-protein interactions among AP5Z1, PTEN, and TRIM21 (Fig. 4D). This indicates that PGG functions by specifically inhibiting the active site of AP5Z1 without disrupting their mutual interactions.

To determine whether the anti-tumor effect of PGG depends on the expression of AP5Z1, rescue experiments were performed using AP5Z1-knockdown (shAP5Z1) and control shRNA (shCtrl) HCC cells. The results of the colony formation assays showed that PGG significantly inhibited colony formation in shCtrl control cells. In contrast, this growth-inhibitory effect of PGG was considerably attenuated in AP5Z1-knockdown cells (Figure S1).

These results indicate that PGG regulates its downstream target proteins and signaling pathways by inhibiting the activity of AP5Z1, and that the anti-proliferative effect of PGG on HCC cells depends on AP5Z1.

Effects of PGG on the development of HCC in vitro

To assess the effect of PGG on the malignant phenotype of HCC cells, experiments were conducted using LM3 and SNU-449 cells with three groups: blank control (WT), DMSO vehicle control, and the PGG treatment group (10 µM, 24 h). The results of the colony formation assays indicated that PGG significantly inhibited the proliferative capacity of HCC cells (Fig. 5A). Flow cytometry assays confirmed that PGG can induce apoptosis (Fig. 5B), and WB assays revealed that the expression of the pro-apoptotic protein BAX was upregulated, whereas the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 was inhibited in the PGG group (Fig. 5D).

In autophagy analysis, the GFP-mCherry-LC3 dual fluorescence labeling system revealed increased autophagosome formation after treatment with PGG (Fig. 5C), while WB analysis showed that PGG upregulated the expression of Beclin1, increased the LC3-II/I ratio, and decreased the expression of p62, suggesting altered autophagy-related marker profiles (Fig. 5E).

Effect of PGG on the progression of HCC In vivo

To assess the effectiveness of PGG in vivo, a mouse model was constructed with subcutaneous xenografts. The mice were divided into a control group and a PGG treatment group, with each group comprising five animals. When the tumors grew to approximately 150 mm³ in volume, the mice in the treatment group were intraperitoneally injected with 10 mg/kg PGG every two days. After 20 days, the mice were euthanized, and their tumors were removed for weighing. The tumor weight and volume were significantly lower in the PGG treatment group than in the control group (Fig. 6A and B). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the expression of AP5Z1 and Ki67 was considerably lower, whereas the levels of Caspase3 and LC3B were higher in the treatment group (Fig. 6C). These results collectively demonstrate that PGG effectively suppresses tumor growth in vivo by reducing the expression of AP5Z1 and promoting caspase-dependent apoptosis, accompanied by changes in autophagy markers.

Discussion

Small-molecule agents are promising candidates for the targeted treatment of HCC due to their favorable stability, cell permeability, and target-binding properties [15, 16]. In this study, PGG was identified as a direct binding ligand of AP5Z1 through virtual screening, MD simulations, CETSA, and SPR. Unlike conventional kinase inhibitors, PGG targets the non-enzymatic adaptor protein AP5Z1 and prevents it from mediating the ubiquitination of PTEN, thereby blocking the downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. This approach expands the scope of druggable targets for HCC beyond classical enzyme-centered strategies [17–19].

However, single-target inhibitors often fail to achieve durable clinical responses in HCC due to tumor heterogeneity and adaptive signaling rewiring; therefore, unconventional targets and multi-modal therapeutic strategies need to be investigated [20]. Additionally, several studies have emphasized that metabolic reprogramming plays a key role in the progression of HCC and therapy resistance, suggesting that integrating metabolic considerations into the development of targeted therapy may improve treatment efficacy [21].

Computer-aided drug design (CADD) is a methodology rooted in computational chemistry. In this technique, computer simulation, calculation, and prediction technologies are used to study the interactions between candidate drug molecules and biomacromolecular receptors, guiding the design and optimization of lead compounds, representing an important direction in modern drug discovery [22, 23]. Virtual screening (VS), also known as in silico screening, is a core technique in CADD. It is usually performed before experimental bioactivity screening, simulating the binding modes of candidate compound libraries to specific targets using molecular docking software and calculating their binding affinities. Using this approach, potential bioactive molecules are enriched, the number of compounds requiring testing is significantly reduced, and the efficiency of lead compound identification is improved. The principle of VS relies on analyzing the three-dimensional structure of the target and characterizing the features of its binding site. Molecular simulation software can be used to determine the key physicochemical properties of the binding site, such as electrostatic distribution, hydrophobic regions, and hydrogen bond networks. Based on this information, database searching or de novo drug design techniques can be implemented to identify or construct compounds whose molecular shape and physicochemical properties are highly complementary to the target binding site. These screened or designed candidate molecules are subsequently synthesized and tested for biological activity. Iterative rounds of optimization are conducted based on experimental results, ultimately leading to the discovery of novel lead compounds [24–27].

AP5Z1 is a poorly characterized molecule in HCC, and its druggability has not been previously evaluated. The findings of this study confirmed that PGG binds directly to the predicted binding pocket of AP5Z1 with a moderate affinity (KD = 15.45 µM by SPR) and stabilizes the AP5Z1 protein in cells (CETSA). The anti-proliferative effect of PGG was significantly weakened in AP5Z1‑knockdown cells, which indicates that the action of PGG depends on AP5Z1.

Mechanistically, PGG inhibited AP5Z1-mediated ubiquitination of PTEN and restored its expression, leading to inactivation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade. These findings establish a functional AP5Z1–PTEN axis in HCC and validate AP5Z1 as a novel actionable target. However, this study has several limitations. First, PGG is a pleiotropic natural compound, and its other intracellular targets cannot be excluded. Second, the binding affinity of PGG to AP5Z1 is moderate; further structure optimization is required to improve the potency of PGG. Despite these limitations, this study provides proof-of-concept evidence that targeting AP5Z1 represents a feasible strategy for treating HCC. To determine how the small-molecule inhibitor PGG functions, ubiquitination assays were conducted in this study. The findings revealed that PGG hinders the ubiquitination of PTEN mediated by AP5Z1, effectively disrupting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling cascade and reducing the proliferation of HCC. Both in vitro and in vivo studies confirmed that PGG considerably decreases the growth of HCC cells and induces apoptosis, accompanied by changes in autophagy-related markers. Supporting this finding, in vivo experiments confirmed that PGG substantially suppresses the onset and progression of HCC in animal models.

Through VS coupled with manual selection, PGG was found to serve as a small-molecule inhibitor targeting AP5Z1. In vitro and in vivo studies confirmed that PGG strongly inhibits the growth of HCC cells and induces apoptosis, along with altered expression of autophagy-associated proteins. Investigations into the mechanism of action revealed that PGG exerts its anti-cancer effects by inhibiting AP5Z1-mediated ubiquitination of PTEN, subsequently blocking the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signal transduction pathway. This finding provides a new theoretical basis for developing small-molecule inhibitors targeting AP5Z1 and offers a novel strategy for the targeted treatment of HCC.
