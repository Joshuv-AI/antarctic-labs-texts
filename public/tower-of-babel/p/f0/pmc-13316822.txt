Patterns of Scabies Treatment Among Syrian Dermatologists: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Rahaf Toutounji, Luma Haj Kassem, Mohamad Nabhan Sawas, Mohammed Abdulrazzak, Silva Ishkanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionScabies, caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, remains a significant public health problem in Syria, where socioeconomic hardship and overcrowding contribute to persistent transmission. This study examines current treatment practices among Syrian dermatologists and evaluates the extent to which these practices align with internationally recommended management approaches.MethodsA structured online questionnaire was administered to 159 dermatologists across multiple Syrian provinces. The survey captured detailed treatment practices, including the exact permethrin regimen applied, frequency and pattern of applications, alternative or intensive regimens, reasons for deviating from guideline‐recommended weekly application, management of household contacts, estimated adherence rates, perceived treatment resistance, and reported adverse effects. Additional items assessed the use of adjunctive therapies and clinician demographics. International guideline adherence was defined as applying 5% permethrin to the whole body for 8–12 h and repeating after 1 week. Responses were analyzed in SPSS using descriptive statistics and appropriate inferential tests.ResultsOnly 54 physicians (34.0%) of participants reported using the classical weekly permethrin regimen, 87 (54.7%) used modified permethrin‐based regimens (e.g., three times weekly for 2 weeks), and 18 (11.3%) used sulfur as first line. The most reported reasons for not following the classical protocol included poor patient or household‐contact adherence (57.1%) and medication‐related concerns such as perceived resistance or reduced drug effectiveness (37.1%). Skin irritation was reported variably, and combination therapy was used by nearly one‐third of respondents. Most respondents (N=139, 87.4%) estimated contact adherence to be below 80%.ConclusionSyrian dermatologists employ a variety of treatment strategies for scabies, largely influenced by patient adherence, perceived drug resistance, and conflict‐related limitations. The findings underscore the need to strengthen patient and household education, enhance physician training, and renew adherence to guideline‐based management.Keywords: Permethrin, Scabies, Syria, Treatment options

Introduction

Scabies is a highly contagious skin infestation caused by Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis, primarily transmitted through direct skin‐to‐skin contact, with occasional spread through fomites. It affects an estimated 200 million people worldwide at any given time, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income regions where poverty, overcrowding, and limited access to hygiene facilities promote sustained transmission [1, 2].

In Syria, no official prevalence statistics exist, and the true burden of scabies is difficult to assess because of healthcare disruption, diagnostic challenges, and treatment barriers related to availability and cost. These difficulties have been further intensified by the prolonged conflict, which has strained the healthcare infrastructure, limited diagnostic resources, and hindered consistent access to appropriate treatment. Nonetheless, observations from dermatologists practicing predominantly in three major Syrian provinces suggest that many clinicians encounter more than 10 cases per month, indicating that scabies remains a considerable and persistent problem in the country. Diagnosis, therefore, continues to rely primarily on clinical assessment, including the identification of burrows or nocturnal pruritus, given the limited availability of confirmatory diagnostic tools in resource‐limited settings [2, 3]. In high‐burden settings, this reliance is further reinforced by the large number of patients requiring treatment, limited consultation time, insufficient hands‐on training in microscopic techniques, and the high cost and limited availability of appropriate diagnostic equipment during the ongoing conflict.

Permethrin 5% remains the most effective first‐line treatment for classical scabies, and international guidelines recommend applying it to the entire body for 8–12 h, with a second application after 1 week to ensure complete eradication of mites and eggs. In infants and some older adults, application to the scalp and face is also recommended, avoiding the periocular area [4]. Despite these clear recommendations, clinical observations from Syrian dermatologists indicate considerable variation in real‐world practice, with many clinicians employing modified regimens rather than the classical weekly protocol.

In light of this heterogeneity, and amid increasing scabies incidence and the constraints of a conflict‐affected healthcare system, we conducted a cross‐sectional study to document prevailing scabies treatment practices among Syrian dermatologists, evaluate their alignment with international guidelines, and explore factors influencing treatment choices within this specific humanitarian context.

Methods

Study Design

A cross‐sectional study was conducted using an anonymous online questionnaire created with Google Forms and distributed to dermatologists practicing in Syria. Data collection was conducted between September 2020 and February 2021. Participation was open nationwide, although most responses were received from dermatologists practicing predominantly in three major Syrian provinces.

Data Collection

The structured questionnaire collected information on physicians' demographic and professional characteristics, including age, years of experience, workplace, specialty level, and estimated monthly scabies caseload. The survey focused exclusively on classical scabies and assessed multiple treatment‐related parameters, including the specific permethrin regimen used, frequency and pattern of application, reasons for deviating from the classical weekly protocol, and management strategies for household contacts.

Additional items evaluated physicians' estimated patient and contact adherence to treatment, perceived reasons for treatment failure or reduced effectiveness, reported complications such as skin irritation and the use of adjunctive therapies including antihistamines and corticosteroids.

Definition of Guideline Adherence and Deviation

Adherence to international guidelines was defined as the application of 5% permethrin to the entire body for 8–12 h, followed by a second application after 1 week. Alternative application frequencies or intensive regimens were classified as deviations from guideline‐recommended practice [4]. The use of topical sulfur as first‐line therapy was also classified as a deviation, as sulfur is not recommended as first‐line treatment for classical scabies in current international guidelines and is generally reserved for specific situations such as treatment failure or confirmed resistance.

Statistical Analysis

The data were exported from the online questionnaire to Microsoft Excel 365 and then were analyzed using SPSS PC version 23.0 statistical software. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to describe categorical variables. As well, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for proportions were estimated using the Wilson score method.

Associations between the use of the classic method for scabies treatment and participants' demographic/professional characteristics and treatment‐related variables were assessed using the Pearson Chi‐square test when the expected cell counts were adequate. When expected cell counts were small, Fisher's exact test was applied. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and results were interpreted at a 95% confidence level. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Treatment Practices

Regarding scabies treatment approaches, 54 participants (34.0%) reported using the classical permethrin regimen, defined as whole‐body application for 8–12 h with a repeat application after 1 week. In contrast, 105 participants (66.0%) reported using alternative treatment approaches. Among these, 87 participants (54.7%) reported modified or intensified permethrin‐only application schedules. The most frequently used regimen was applying permethrin three times per week for two consecutive weeks, reported by 22 participants (13.8%), followed by a regimen consisting of three applications during the first week and one application during the second week, reported by 21 participants (13.2%). In addition, 18 participants (11.3%) reported using topical sulfur as first‐line therapy (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Reported Adverse Effects

Skin irritation was the most frequently reported adverse effect among participants using the classical permethrin regimen.

Factors Associated With Use of the Classical Permethrin Regimen

Use of the classical permethrin regimen was significantly associated with city of graduation (p = 0.002), city of practice (p = 0.012), and monthly scabies caseload (p = 0.045) (Table 3). No significant associations were observed with age or professional position.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the majority of participating dermatologists did not adhere to the classical permethrin regimen recommended in international guidelines. The observed variation in treatment practices was significantly associated with the city of graduation, the monthly number of scabies patients treated, and physicians' perceptions of poor adherence among household contacts. This finding underscores a notable gap between evidence‐based recommendations and real‐world clinical practice in a conflict‐affected healthcare setting.

Similar trends have been reported in the literature, where some clinical investigations explored alternative or supplementary strategies to improve permethrin treatment efficacy under specific conditions. For example, Riebenbauer et al. evaluated intensified permethrin‐based protocols in infants and young children and reported higher cure rates compared with standard dosing; however, the study was conducted in a narrowly defined pediatric population and under controlled conditions [5].

Similarly, Yürekli et al. demonstrated improved treatment success through mechanical removal of burrows prior to permethrin application, representing a procedural adjunct rather than a modification of permethrin dosing itself [6]. Collectively, these studies illustrate targeted interventions in selected settings rather than broad evidence supporting routine modification of classical permethrin regimens in general clinical practice.

Among dermatologists who did not follow the classical permethrin regimen, patient‐related factors—particularly perceived poor adherence of patients or household contacts—were the most frequently reported reasons. This aligns closely with our quantitative findings, where lower perceived adherence among contacts was significantly associated with the use of non‐classical treatment regimens. Concerns regarding reduced permethrin efficacy were also commonly cited, reflecting clinical uncertainty rather than confirmed drug resistance.

The use of topical sulfur as a first‐line treatment was reported by a notable proportion of participants in this study. Although sulfur is an established scabicidal agent with documented efficacy against both mites and eggs, it is not recommended as a first‐line therapy in current international guidelines. Its use is generally reserved for specific situations, such as intolerance to standard treatments and pregnancy [7, 8, 9].

In our study, sulfur use appeared to be primarily driven by concerns regarding reduced permethrin effectiveness rather than guideline‐based indications.

Taken together, these findings suggest that physicians' perceptions of limited patient and contact adherence play a central role in shaping treatment decisions and may drive the adoption of intensified or alternative regimens in routine practice. This is especially relevant in settings where overcrowding, limited health literacy, and restricted follow‐up are common [10]. While oral ivermectin has demonstrated efficacy in several settings and may represent a useful alternative in cases of poor compliance, its availability, cost, and prescribing practices vary across regions. Further studies are needed to evaluate its feasibility and role in the Syrian context [11].

Notably, the use of the classical permethrin regimen was significantly associated with the physician's training background. This association may reflect differences in exposure to updated clinical guidelines, access to continuing medical education, or variations in institutional practice patterns. Rather than indicating differences in competence, this finding highlights the importance of standardized postgraduate training and continuous professional development, particularly in resource‐limited and conflict‐affected settings.

Another important finding was the association between monthly scabies caseload and treatment approach. Dermatologists managing a higher number of scabies cases per month were more likely to use non‐classical regimens. In clinical settings characterized by high patient volume and limited consultation time, treatment decisions may be shaped by pragmatic constraints rather than strict protocol adherence. Within the Syrian context, prolonged strain on the healthcare system due to ongoing conflict has led to overcrowded clinics and limited follow‐up capacity, providing an important framework for understanding deviations from standard treatment protocols despite awareness of international guidelines.

One possible explanation for the observed deviation from classical treatment regimens could be the presence of emerging drug resistance, although this has not been proven in our setting. Indiscriminate use of scabicides in the region may theoretically contribute to reduced treatment efficacy. Laboratory‐based studies are needed to explore whether resistance plays a role in the treatment patterns observed in this study.

Although not classified as deviations from international guidelines, the frequent prescription of adjunctive therapies such as topical corticosteroids and antihistamines was noted. These agents are well‐recognized as supportive treatments for post‐scabetic pruritus and inflammatory symptoms [8, 12]. However, a secondary observation from our data suggests that many physicians prescribe these medications from the outset of treatment, possibly due to time constraints and anticipated difficulties with patient follow‐up. This observation was not included in the classification of treatment deviation but provides additional insight into real‐world prescribing behavior.

Skin irritation was assessed based on physicians' subjective perception of their patients' experience, rather than standardized clinical evaluation, which may limit the accuracy and comparability of these findings.

This study has several limitations. The reliance on physicians' self‐reported practices and perceptions may introduce reporting and recall bias, and limit the accuracy and comparability of the findings. Additionally, the cross‐sectional design precludes causal interpretation of the observed associations.

Overall, these findings underscore the impact of contextual and systemic factors on scabies management in Syria. The observed treatment variability highlights the need for epidemiological studies to better define the true burden of scabies in the country. Moreover, the present study may serve as a foundation for future research evaluating the clinical effectiveness, safety, and cost–benefit of intensified permethrin regimens compared with guideline‐recommended treatment, particularly in high‐burden and resource‐limited settings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates substantial variability in scabies treatment practices among Syrian dermatologists compared with internationally recommended regimens. Treatment decisions appear to be influenced by contextual factors such as perceived poor patient and contact adherence, high clinical workload, and regional practice differences. In the setting of ongoing conflict and a considerable scabies burden, these findings highlight the need to improve the implementation of existing guidelines through patient education, physician support, and context‐sensitive strategies. Strengthening collaboration between clinicians, researchers, and health authorities is essential to promote evidence‐based management and improve scabies control in affected communities.
