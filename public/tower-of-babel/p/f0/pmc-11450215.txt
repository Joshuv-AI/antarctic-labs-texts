Inventory of shallow landslides triggered by extreme precipitation in July 2023 in Beijing, China
Authors: Hao Ma, Fawu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe extreme meteorological events caused by climate change have increasingly caused serious clustered landslides. A systematic and timely inventory of triggered landslides is a prerequisite for quantifying and evaluating the impact of extreme meteorological events. In addition, a landslide inventory can provide basic data for any subsequent analysis of the event or be used innovatively in landslide risk analysis. The Rainfall-induced Landslides in Beijing (RLBJ) inventory presented here contains data on 15,383 rainfall-induced shallow landslides triggered by a single extreme precipitation event in July 2023 in an area of ~3,250 km2 in western mountainous areas of Beijing, China. High-resolution satellite images before and after this rainstorm event were used to visually analyze the landslides. All landslides were reported as vectorized points and polygon features and classified according to their motion forms. This inventory is now freely available for the benefit of international geohazard researchers.Subject terms: Natural hazards, Environmental health, Environmental impact

Background & Summary

Landslides and related mass movement processes are widespread natural hazards that occur in mountainous areas and frequently cause severe damage to infrastructure, properties, and human life1–4. Large or clustered landslides can also significantly affect the landscape of an area5–9. Rainfall is a primary trigger of landslides, especially shallow ones10. Heavy rainstorm events can trigger up to tens of thousands of landslides simultaneously in rainfall-affected regions11–13. Against the backdrop of global climate change, extreme precipitation events are becoming increasingly common and are hitting various parts of the world14–16, even in many traditionally arid and low-rainfall areas17. These rare extreme precipitation events in typically less rainy areas are likelier to trigger unexpected landslides18–20.

The preparation of landslide maps to register the extent of historical landslides in an area is important for investigating the distribution, types, pattern, and recurrence of slope failures from the slope or catchment scale to regional or national scales21–25; assessing landslide susceptibility, hazard, vulnerability and risk1,26–30; and studying the evolution of local or regional landscapes and ecological environment dominated by mass-wasting processes31–36. Landslide inventory maps commonly consist of locations and, when available, detailed information on geometry, dates of occurrence, material involved, mass motion forms, damage, and remediation work13,25,37,38. Vectorized geometric shapes (i.e., polygonal shapes and line or point features) depicted in the geographical information system (GIS) environment are the most common and convenient reporting forms for landslide maps13,25,37,39. Producing event inventory maps is an important aspect of landslide mapping. An event landslide map can fully describe the extent and impact of landslides after every triggering event (e.g., rainstorms11,40, rapid snowmelt events41, or earthquakes)39,42 and contain crucial data for any subsequent analyses of the ground damage effects of the event13. The information in these maps can also provide valuable guidance for emergency and post-event recovery efforts36,38.

Several methods have been developed to map landslides, including field surveys13,43; expert interpretations of aerial photographs44 and optical45,46, multispectral47, or synthetic aperture radar (SAR) satellite images48; morphometric analyses of digital elevation models (DEMs)37; etc. Classical field surveys can provide the most detailed information on the landslide masses (e.g., materials, motion path, and deposit area) and is least affected by shading of tree canopies, clouds, or structures; however, it requires significant manpower, material resources, and time and relies on the accessibility of hazards13. Interpretation based on optical satellite or aerial images can provide a timely approach to the hazard as it is not limited by road blockage; however, cloud cover, a lack of natural lighting, and image resolution can strongly affect the accuracy and efficiency of the interpretation13,38. Manual visual identification is the most frequently used method38,45,46,49. Innovative automatic or semi-automatic algorithms have been developed to improve the efficiency of interpretation; however, their results need to be verified based on data from visual interpretation50,51. The SAR data can be used to construct a time series of surface deformations and are suitable for identifying inapparent landslides that are still in the creep stage48. The DEM contains rich terrain information and is mostly used to assist in optical image interpretation38, but it can also be used separately to identify large ancient or subaqueous landslides37, which have clear morphometric features. Therefore, visual interpretation from optical images is still the most feasible way to map event-triggered landslides in a timely and accurate manner.

From July 29, 2023 to August 2, 2023, affected by the residual circulation of Super Typhoon Doksuri52, a rare extreme precipitation event occurred in Beijing, which is located in an area of northern China that typically does not receive much rainfall. The average precipitation in the city reached 331 mm over 83 hours, or approximately 60% of the city’s annual average precipitation52. The maximum local 83-hour cumulative precipitation recorded by the meteorological station is 744.8 mm, which is the highest recorded in Beijing since the first rainfall records were taken 140 years ago52. The rainfall center was located at the mountainous western areas of Beijing (Fig. 1)—this resulted in widespread mountain floods and landslides. This event caused a total of 1.29 million people to be affected by the disaster, and 33 human deaths and resulted in 18 persons being declared missing. At least 59,000 houses collapsed, and 147,000 houses were severely damaged52. This paper describes the processing procedures and record structure of the landslide inventory map for this rare extreme precipitation event. The RLBJ presented here is the result of an extensive remote sensing interpretation for the area most affected by the intense rainfall, spanning ~3250 km2 (Fig. 1).

Available data

The data used to produce and verify this inventory mainly include optical satellite images taken before and after the focused rainfall event. Acquisition and viewing of satellite images are fully conducted through the OvitalMap map browser (Version: V10.0.3.32078; Beijing Ovital Software Co., Ltd., https://www.ovital.com), which integrates electronic maps and satellite images from multiple sources. The satellite images used in this study are provided by SIWEIearth (https://siweiearth.com), and the images can be directly viewed in the OvitalMap map browser. These images have been precisely merged with an overall cloud cover of less than 20% and possess a high resolution (ranging from 0.8 m to 2 m). To target our research areas of interest, we mainly utilized images taken on May 13, 2023 (before the rainfall event; Fig. 3a) and August 19, 2023 (after the rainfall event; Fig. 3b). However, a small portion of the study area is affected by cloud cover and terrain shadows; when this occurred, images from dates before March 15, 2024 were also used. These high-resolution images allowed us to identify the vast majority of landslides triggered by the studied event. The smallest landslide identified from the satellite images had an area of less than 100 m2.

To focus on the most severely affected areas during this extreme precipitation event as the primary study areas, we firstly collected rainfall data (Fig. 1; Source: Beijing Meteorological Service; https://weibo.com/2611704935/NcoOkkeyG?pagetype=profilefeed), news reports52, ALOS 12.5 m DEM53, and administrative divisions (Source: CnOpenData; https://www.cnopendata.com/data/m/recent/chinese-administrative-divisions-shp.html) as the auxiliary data, and then conducted preliminary field investigations. All of these preliminary efforts indicate that the western mountainous areas of Beijing, which include all mountainous areas within the Fangshan and Mentougou Districts, and the mountainous areas in the southwest of Changping District and northwest of Haidian District, experienced the highest rainfall (Fig. 1) and suffered the most severe property damage caused by the rainfall-induced geological disasters. Therefore, mountainous western areas were designated as our areas of interest, as shown in Fig. 1.

Landslide morphometric mapping

The delimiting and mapping procedure for the landslide areas was carried out using the OvitalMap map browser (https://www.ovital.com), which provides both 2D and 3D visualization perspectives of the required optical satellite images. The 3D view allows for fully flexible navigation over a virtual mosaic of periodically updated satellite images, including features such as free zoom, rotation, and angle of view. Furthermore, the OvitalMap supports commonly used graphic vectorization operations, including marking points, lines, and polygons. Therefore, the identification, delimiting, and labeling of landslides can be completed in one sitting through the OvitalMap. All identified landslides were marked as points and polygons (Fig. 3c). Prior to mapping the landslide features, we found through our pre-analysis that the landslides triggered by this precipitation event were commonly small slides at first that eventually caused larger disasters by subsequent fluidized motion and entrainment of materials along the motion path. Therefore, all points were located at the initial position of the registered landslide (Fig. 3c), which can help improve the accuracy of future analysis on landslide causes. Each polygon covers the entire identifiable area affected by a landslide (Fig. 3c). All landslides were manually identified and delimited. Compared to some emerging automated methods, manual visual interpretation takes longer but is more accurate39.

Classifying the registered landslides is another fundamental work for preparing the inventory38. Classifications according to the material, motion form, and velocity of landslide mass, as proposed by Hungr et al. (2014), are commonly made worldwide54. However, fully identifying the mass materials and motion velocity through satellite images is infeasible due to the small sizes of many of the studied landslides, despite the images used being of the highest available quality. Therefore, we mainly adopted the motion form to ease and homogenize landslide classification when preparing the RLBJ inventory (Table 1). Table 2 presents typical excerpts of the identification characteristics of each type of landslide. All identifications for the landslide type were based on a 3D view of the images.

Data merging

The manually depicted points and polygons in OvitalMap need to be converted into shapefiles for further merging into the final inventory map in the ArcGIS platform. The final form of the RLBJ inventory is a vectorized multilayer electronic map merged in ArcGIS 10.8 (Table 3). OvitalMap can directly output landslide features (i.e., points and polygons) to the desired shapefile format; however, some post-processing is required for the final map. First, to improve versatility, the map coordinate system was converted to the internationally recognized World Geodetic System-1984 (WGS-84) coordinate system. A topology check was then conducted in the ArcGIS platform to ensure that the points and polygons were matched and to avoid overlapping polygons and other artificial graphical errors. The points and polygons corresponding to the same landslide were assigned a unique ID (UID) to homogenize landslide numbering.

To further enrich the inventory data, some fundamental geometric indices of the landslide features (i.e., area and perimeter) and topographic indices of the initial position (i.e., elevation, topographic position index55, slope aspect, and slope gradient) were extracted from the ALOS 12.5 m DEM53 and listed in the attribute tables. The area and perimeter were calculated in the WGS-1984 UTM Zone 50 N projected coordinate system.

The RLBJ inventory56, publicly available for download from Figshare, includes 15,383 total landslide records distributed over the mountainous western areas of Beijing. This inventory can provide an important source for analyzing rainfall-induced landslides in GIS environments.

Technical Validation

After the integral version of the RLBJ inventory was prepared, a hybrid technical validation process was conducted to verify: (i) inventory compilation completeness and integrity; (ii) geographic accuracy, i.e., size, location, and shape of the individual landslides; and (iii) thematic accuracy, i.e., detection rate and landslide classification13. Activities to validate inventory completeness and correctness were conducted for all landslide records of the dataset. Other validations were carried out based on field investigation on a random sampling of ~2% of the features originally mapped. Field investigations are mainly conducted along accessible roads in areas with a dense distribution of landslides. Due to the fact that many landslides occurred along high mountains, that were unreachable by roads, a telephoto lens with a maximum focal length of 200 millimeters was used to obtain high-definition photos of landslides from a distance. A total of 403 landslides were confirmed during field investigations (field photos and necessary validation logs are included in the “Field logs” document in the repository).

Inventory completeness and integrity were checked on the GIS platform. The attribute tables of all mapped features (i.e., points and polygons) were systematically checked to prevent any empty records, typing errors, exception values, and referential integrity issues. During the first checking process, empty records and wrong attribute values (i.e., polygon areas with negative values) of the key geometric properties were found in the attribute table. After checking for elements with incorrect attributes, we found that these errors were caused by (i) the wrong line crossing at polygon edges, (2) the topological overlap of adjacent polygons, and (3) the meaningless geometric elements left behind by errors during the vectorization process. Referential integrity issues existed regarding the point and polygon layers, indicating that the number of points and polygons was not equal in the raw version of the merged inventory. This is because the points and polygons are labeled separately. Considering the huge number of landslides, the entire study area was divided into multiple regions during the interpretation process; therefore, incorrect repetitive marking led to overlaps in the boundary zones of these regions during data merging. The topology-checking tool in ArcGIS can efficiently identify the mismatch between points and polygons. These errors were all identified and corrected one by one, and the final version of the inventory showed no empty records, wrong attributes, or referential integrity issues.

Geographic accuracy mainly involves the accuracy of landslide location and area limits. The geometric features of each identified landslide were marked based on the best visible satellite images, and at least two satellite images from other dates (before and after the landslide, respectively) were used to further confirm the location and boundary of the landslides. Therefore, we confirmed that the positions of the initial points of all identified landslides were accurate. However, the accuracy of the landslide boundaries requires additional validation, as it is controlled by the inherent limitations of the manual visual interpretation method (e.g., image resolution, and visual error). Image resolution and visual errors have a significant impact on landslide delineation, especially for landslides with small areas. After conducting the field investigation, we carefully checked the polygons of the landslides that could be identified in field photos. The area of the initial polygon was generally 5% to 10% larger than that of the verified polygon; for some small landslides, the maximum error can reach ~30%. In addition, the coordinate projection in the GIS platform may cause area measurement errors. We selected five landslides with clear boundaries and measured their geometric dimensions (i.e., landslide length and width) in the field using a handheld rangefinder. The deviation between the values measured in the field and those measured by GIS tools was less than 1 m.

For thematic accuracy, our study mainly focuses on the detection rate and classification accuracy of remote-sensing landslide identification. Therefore, our verification mainly relies on on-site data. We used the field photos, locations of the landslides depicted in the photos, and views of the satellite images to match the landslides identified through field investigations with those identified through remote sensing interpretation. If a landslide confirmed by field investigation was identified by remote sensing interpretation and the classification is consistent, then the landslide was deemed to be correctly identified (Table 2). For the 403 landslides identified in the field (Fig. 8a), 337 landslides (83.6%) were identified by remote sensing interpretation, among which 312 landslides were correctly classified (accounting for 77.4%), 24 landslides were misclassified (accounting for 6%), and one polygon was confirmed not to be a landslide but the artificially deposited gravels manually moved from the adjacent road surface during emergency efforts. However, 16.4% of landslides were not detected by remote sensing interpretation. The main reason for this omission is that the area of these landslides was too small, and the characteristics presented on satellite images cannot be used to fully determine whether these events could be considered landslides. Figure 8b further summarizes the thematic accuracy of each category of landslides. Landslides without flowing movements had the highest missed detection rate. This type of landslide mostly forms on steep road-cutting slopes, and the area it covers on satellite images is relatively small; therefore, only the landslides of this type with clear, recognizable features (similar to the cases shown in Table 2) were marked during interpreting. Similarly, the omission rate of landslides flowing on slopes with the same smaller area is also higher (18%, Fig. 8b). The proportion of misclassifications of different types of landslides is similar (Fig. 8b). In addition, landslide mass material is also an important basis for landslide classification; however, the resolution of the satellite images used in this study was not sufficient to effectively identify the material of each landslide. The field investigations found that the masses of over 98% of the identified landslides were composed of shallow residual soil and highly weathered rocks, while the others contained only rocks.

Although remote sensing interpretation has identified a large number of landslides, this inventory does not include all landslides triggered by this event. Based on the detection rate obtained from actual investigations, it can be estimated that the total landslide number in the study area may be nearly 20,000. We maintain this inventory to provide as complete a snapshot as possible of the massive landslides formed on both natural and artificial slopes triggered by the extreme precipitation that hit mountainous western regions of Beijing, China, from July 29, 2023 to August 2, 2023.

Usage Notes

All researchers interested in the disaster effects of climate change, rainfall-induced landslides, regional susceptibility and risk of landslides, and the application of machine learning methods in landslide analysis can benefit from this landslide inventory. This dataset can be used for any purpose for free. The final integration of this inventory was completed in ArcGIS 10.8. The dataset provided in the repository can be immediately reproduced in ArcGIS or other GIS platforms for further statistical analysis and integration with other databases. Despite the high number of features mapped, the current version of the RLBJ inventory still lacks many small-scale landslides and has limitations in landslide classification. Further versions of the RLBJ will strive to integrate more detailed metrics when higher-resolution satellite images or additional field validation data are available. The authors will continue to monitor rainfall-induced landslides in the same area in the future to evaluate the impact of long-term climate change on geological disasters in Northern China. Researchers are welcome to contribute to the integrity and richness of the mapped features or attributes in the inventory.
