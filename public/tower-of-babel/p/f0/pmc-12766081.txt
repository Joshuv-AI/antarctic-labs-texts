Changes in quality characteristics of yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) during cold storage and their correlation with lipid deterioration
Authors: Hui Yu, Zhou Zhao, Jiaqi Wu, Lilan You, Changwen Huang, Zhiqiang Guo, Juan Xiao, Zhiyuan Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a marine high-fat fish, yellowfin tuna is prone to quality deterioration during storage. In this study, dynamic changes in quality indicators and lipid profiles of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage at 4 °C, as well as their correlations, were investigated. Results showed a gradual deterioration of fillet quality with time, as evidenced by darkening color, softening texture, increased centrifugation loss, elevated K value, heightened microorganisms, reduced sensory score, and severe protein and lipid oxidation. Through LC-MS/MS-based lipidomics analyses, 665 lipid species from 29 subclasses were detected in fillets, with significant differences observed in the lipid profiles between fillets stored for varying storage times. Moreover, a total of 16 key differentially abundant lipids (DALs) were identified as potential lipid biomarkers of freshness. Correlation analysis further revealed a strong relationship between these DALs and quality characteristics. Overall, this work will offer valuable insights for the efficient storage of yellowfin tuna fillets.Keywords: Yellowfin tuna, Cold storage, Quality, Lipidomics, Lipid changes

Introduction

Yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) represents an important commercial fishery resource prized for its nutritional profile, containing premium proteins, essential amino acids, vitamins, and unsaturated fatty acids (Palanisamy et al., 2025). Due to its bright colors, delicate texture, and smooth taste, yellowfin tuna has become one of the most popular ingredients for making sushi and sashimi (Bu et al., 2022). Notably, as a ready-to-eat delicacy, sashimi requires certain standards of freshness, appearance, presentation, texture, and flavor (Chen et al., 2023). Unfortunately, the highly unsaturated lipid content and abundant endogenous enzymes of yellowfin tuna fillets make them susceptible to undesirable deterioration (Singh, Benjakul, Zhang, et al., 2021). Cold storage at 4 °C can maintain relatively stable fillet quality for a short period of time by slowing down microbial proliferation and endogenous enzyme activity (Eranda et al., 2024). However, many enzymatic, microbiological, chemical, and physical processes still inevitably occur in fillets at a slower rate during cold storage (Yan et al., 2022). The deterioration of fillet freshness and commercial value stems from these reactions, which impair key quality characteristics such as color, texture, and flavor (Liu et al., 2023; Rostini et al., 2025). Based on these, various indicators have been developed to determine the freshness of fillets during storage, including K value, water holding capacity (WHC), total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), and thiobarbituric acid-reactive substances (TBARS) (Liu et al., 2023; Nguyen et al., 2024). However, these established methods offer only a restricted view on the overall quality of fillets and cannot determine the mechanisms that affect fillet quality.

Considering the high lipid content and oxidative susceptibility of tuna, exploring the mechanism of quality degradation based on lipid changes may be an effective approach (Wang, Xie, et al., 2021). As an advanced analytical method, lipidomics enables complete characterization of lipid compositions, elucidates specific lipid functions, identifies lipid biomarkers, as well as reveals the mechanism of lipid action in various biological processes (Wang et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2022). LC-MS/MS-based lipidomics techniques have been used to explore the effect of chilled storage on the extent of lipid oxidation in hairtail muscle tissues (Yan et al., 2022). LPC (17:0), LPC (18:0), LPC (22:2), and PC (18:4/16:1) were used as freshness indicators of farmed Atlantic salmon through lipidomic analysis (Chen et al., 2022). Previous studies have applied lipidomics to tuna, including the authentication of three different tuna species (Hu et al., 2022), changes in lipidomic profiles during wet aging of longfin tuna (Shen et al., 2022), and phospholipid profile alteration of bigeye tuna during daily cooking processes (Song et al., 2023). Despite extensive lipidomic applications in tuna research, comprehensive analyses of cold storage-induced lipid changes in yellowfin tuna, as well as screening of new lipid biomarkers, remain unexplored.

Notably, as fundamental structural and metabolic components, lipid changes directly influence critical quality parameters in fish fillets (Palanisamy et al., 2024; Yang et al., 2024; Zhang, Fu, et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2024). Specifically, lipid oxidation and myoglobin (Mb) oxidation are mutually promoting (Palanisamy et al., 2024), as evidenced by the close correlation that exists between color (MetMb content) and lipid oxidation (TBARS level) in southern bluefin tuna during refrigerated storage (Bu et al., 2022). Meanwhile, lipids serve as essential growth nutrients for microorganisms, fueling their growth and reproduction, and accelerating the spoilage process (Yang et al., 2024). Zhang, Fu, et al. (2024) claimed that the unsaturation degree of fatty acids can stimulate the formation of protein carbonyls and induce protein cross-linking polymerization, which reduces the quality of salted large yellow croaker over time. Therefore, systematic exploration of associations between lipid molecule changes and quality loss in yellowfin tuna fillet during cold storage could deepen the existing understanding of the underlying mechanisms affecting fillet quality while providing insights for optimizing storage conditions for fish products (Xu et al., 2022).

Thus, this study aimed to assess the correlation between quality loss and lipid changes in yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage. The objectives included: (1) to quantify changes in fillet quality during cold storage by measuring quality indicators (physicochemical properties, microbiological properties, sensory scores, and protein/lipid-related properties); (2) to clarify the dynamic changes in lipid profiles and identify differentially abundant lipids (DALs) of yellowfin tuna fillets through LC-MS/MS-based lipidomics analyses; (3) to analyze correlations between quality parameters and DALs to screen potential lipid molecular biomarkers related to fillet quality. The comprehensive insight gained lays the foundation for the storage, preservation, and quality control of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage in the future.

Materials and methods

Sample preparation

Yellowfin tunas were commercially captured and slaughtered from the South China Sea (17°24′N, 110°36′E) by Hainan Orca Sea Fishing Service Co., Ltd. To obtain representative composite samples, the back muscle was collected from 3 individuals of yellowfin tuna, cut into blocks, placed in aluminum foil bags, and stored at −80 °C for a maximum of 1 month for later use.

Yellowfin tuna back muscles were thawed at 4 °C and then sliced into fillets of 2 cm × 3 cm × 1 cm. These fillets were divided into four groups at random and placed in self-sealing polyethylene bags in a 4 °C refrigerator. Fresh yellowfin tuna fillets stored for 0 days were used as the control (labeled L0), while fillets stored for 3, 6, and 9 days served as the experimental groups (labeled L3, L6, and L9, respectively).

Physicochemical properties

Color analysis

(1)Determination of colorimetry

Determination of colorimetry

A colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Japan) was employed to assess the color changes of yellowfin tuna fillets. The L* (lightness), a* (+redness/−greenness), and b* (+yellowness/−blueness) values were measured on the surface of the anterior dorsal regions.

(2)Determination of total pigment content

Determination of total pigment content

The determination of total pigment content was conducted using the acetone extraction method described by Bu et al. (2022). The final result was expressed as mg of pigment/kg of the sample.

Determination of the Mb relative proportion

The Mb content of the yellowfin tuna fillet was determined using the method by Liu et al. (2022). The supernatant was determined at 500 nm (A500), 525 nm (A525), 540 nm (A540), and 555 nm (A555). Texture profile analysis (TPA)

The yellowfin tuna fillet samples were cut into 1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm pieces. The texture parameters (hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, resilience, chewiness, and gumminess) of the sample were measured using a texture analyzer (TA-XT plus, Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) equipped with a 50 mm cylinder probe (P/50) under the following conditions: pre-test speed 5 mm/s; test speed 1 mm/s; post-test speed 5 mm/s; force 5g.

Six kinds of ATP-related compounds, including ATP, adenosine diphosphate (ADP), adenosine monophosphate (AMP), inosine monophosphate (IMP), hypoxanthine ribonucleoside (HxR), and hypoxanthine (Hx), were extracted with perchloric acid using the method described by Yu et al. Microbiological analysis

The TVC and psychrophilic bacteria count (PBC) were determined following the method described by Cao et al. (2020). Briefly, 2 g of yellowfin tuna fillet was homogenized in 18 mL of sterile saline (0.85 %, w/v) and serially diluted (1:10, v/v). 100 μL of each sample was spread on the surface of plate counting agar, and incubated at 30 °C for 3 days (TVC) and 4 °C for 10 days (PBC), respectively.

Sensory analysis

Sensory evaluation was conducted following Dai et al. (2025) with some modifications. Our institution (Hainan University, Haikou, China) did not require ethical permission for the sensory evaluation of food. This experiment was performed following the Declaration of Helsinki, and informed consent was obtained from panelists. Sensory evaluation was conducted in a sensory lab with individual booths and illuminated by natural light. Ten trained panelists (5 males, 5 females; aged 20–50) assessed yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage for color, odor, and texture using a 5-point scale (1 = very poor to 5 = excellent). The overall acceptability score was calculated as the mean of these three attributes.

Determination of protein-related properties

Total sulfhydryl content and carbonyl content

Following the kit instructions, the protein carbonyl and sulfhydryl contents of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage were determined by the corresponding assay kits (A087-1-2 and A063-1-1) from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The protein carbonyl and sulfhydryl contents are expressed as μmol/g protein.

Determination of TVB-N

The TVB-N content was estimated following the National Standard of China (GB 5009.228-2016), using an automatic Kjeldahl apparatus (KJELTEC 8400, Foss TecatorAB, Hogans, Sweden).

Free amino acids (FAAs) composition analysis

The extraction of FAAs was performed according to the method of Chu et al. (2025) with minor modifications. In brief, FAAs in yellowfin tuna fillets were extracted using 5 % trichloroacetic acid solution (TCA, w/v), and then measured by an automatic amino acid analyzer (Biochrom 30+; Biochrom Ltd., Cambridge, UK).

Determination of lipid-related properties

Determination of peroxide value (PV)

The PV was determined using the ferrous thiocyanate method described by Singh, Benjakul, Zhou, et al. (2021). PV was expressed as milliequivalents of peroxide per kilogram of lipid (meq/kg).

Determination of TBARS value

The TBARS value was determined using the UV–visible spectrophotometric method described by Dai et al. (2022). The TBARS value was defined as mg malondialdehyde (MDA)/kg sample.

Lipidomics analysis

Sample preparation

50 mg of the yellowfin tuna fillets was weighed, then 400 μL of extraction solution (methanol:water = 4:1, v/v) containing 0.02 mg/mL of internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine) was added for metabolite extraction. Grinding beads with a diameter of 6 mm were added, and the sample solution was ground at −10 °C and 50 Hz for 6 min using a cryogenic tissue grinder, followed by ultrasonic extraction at 5 °C and 40 kHz for 30 min. The samples were kept at −20 °C for 30 min and then centrifuged for 15 min (13,000 rpm/min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. All samples' metabolites were mixed at an equal volume to prepare a quality control (QC) sample.

LC-MS/MS detection

The LC-MS/MS analysis in this study was performed using a UHPLC-Q Exactive HF-X system from Thermo Fisher Scientific. 2 μL of the sample was injected and separated using an Accucore C30 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 2.6 μm; Thermo) at a flow rate of 0.40 mL/min. The mobile phase was composed of two components: (A) a 50 % acetonitrile aqueous solution (0.1 % formic acid, 10 mmol/L ammonium acetate); (B) a ternary mixture of isopropanol, acetonitrile, and water (88: 10: 2, v/v/v) with 0.02 % formic acid and 2 mmol/L ammonium acetate. The separation gradient (B) was as follows: 0–4 min,35 %–60 %; 4–12 min, 60 %–85 %; 12–15 min, 85 %–100 %; 15–17 min, 100 %; 17–18 min, 100 %–35 %; 18–20 min, 35 %.

The mass spectrometry signals of the samples were collected in positive and negative ion scanning modes, with a mass scanning range of m/z 200–2000. Key instrument parameters included: an ion spray voltage of 3000 V, a sheath gas pressure of 60 psi, an auxiliary heating gas pressure of 20 psi, an ion source heating temperature of 370 °C, and collision energy cycling between 20, 40, and 60 V.

Lipid identification and bioinformatics analysis

The raw data were processed using Lipidsearch (Thermo Fisher, USA) to perform baseline filtering, peak identification, integration, retention time correction, and peak alignment. For feature peak identification, the software matched both MS and MS/MS spectra against a metabolic database, applying a MS mass tolerance of <10 ppm. Metabolites were identified based on the MS/MS spectrum matching scores.

Statistical analysis

The indicators were analyzed using three different batches of samples, and the results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's test for multiple comparisons among means in the SPSS 26.0 software. Before statistical analysis, the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene's test were performed to determine normality and homogeneity of variance. The preprocessed data were uploaded to MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/) for principal component analysis (PCA), partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), permutation tests of PLS-DA (1000 permutations), and calculation of the variable importance in projection (VIP) value. The significant difference (P < 0.05) and VIP > 1 from PLS-DA were utilized to discriminate DALs of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage. Correlation heatmaps were conducted using the Metware Cloud online platform (https://cloud.metware.cn). Other figures were generated using OriginPro 2022 or Prism 9.5.1.

Results and discussion

Changes in physicochemical properties

Color stability

(1)Color

Color

Color is the key visual attribute of yellowfin tuna fillets that affects consumers' perception of the edible quality of the product. As shown in Fig. 1A, obvious color changes in yellowfin tuna fillets were observed during storage. Fresh yellowfin tuna fillets exhibited a vivid red color that gradually transitioned from bright red to reddish-brown after 3 days of storage under refrigeration. It was presumed to be associated with the oxidation of red OxyMb to brown MetMb, which gave the fillets an unappealing brown color and led to a decrease in redness (Jiang et al., 2021). The edges of the fillets turned green on day 6, and by the 9th day, the whole fillets appeared green. This was due to the fact that the presence of the green-colored sulfmyoglobin produced by the reaction between MetMb and inorganic sulfides, which led to further greening of the fillets (Dai et al., 2022).

Values were expressed as mean ± SD in each group (n = 3). Means with different letters differ significantly at P < 0.05.

Instrumental color analysis was carried out to demonstrate the color change of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage more objectively (Fig. 1B). The a*/b* value denotes the ratios of a*: b*, with an increase in its value signifying greater redness and less discoloration. Several studies have shown that a*/b* is better as a parameter than a*, b*, and L* values for evaluating the color of tuna meat (Bu et al., 2022; Jiang et al., 2022). The a*/b* value of yellowfin tuna fillets decreased sharply from 6.60 ± 0.48 to 2.71 ± 0.27 (P < 0.05) after 3 days at 4 °C, and the value further decreased to 0.16 ± 0.08 (P < 0.05) with the extension of storage days under refrigeration to 9 days. A previous study also revealed a downward trend in the a*/b* value of southern bluefin tuna with refrigerated storage (Bu et al., 2022). The lowest and highest L* values were detected in the yellowfin tuna fillets stored for 0 d and 9 d, respectively (P < 0.05). The L* value is thought to be related to the scattering properties and WHC of meat. As the WHC of the meat decreases, free water in the extracellular interstitial space of the tissue increases, reflecting more light and consequently increasing the L* value (Jiang et al., 2021).

(2)Total pigment content

Total pigment content

The storage time significantly affected (P < 0.05) the total pigment content of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage (Fig. 1C). Specifically, the total pigment content of fillets decreased by 9.34, 29.25 and 50.41 % (P < 0.05) over storage periods of 3, 6 and 9 days, respectively, as compared to fresh fillets (36.42 ± 0.52 mg/kg). The total pigment content of southern bluefin tuna also showed a downward trend with cold storage time progression (Bu et al., 2022). This may be due to the oxidation and denaturation of pigments in muscle fibers during storage (Orkusz et al., 2024).

(3)Mb relative proportion

Mb relative proportion

The color stability of tuna meat is principally governed by Mb content and its redox state. The red color in tuna meat is mainly due to the existence of oxymyoglobin, while the occurrence of metmyoglobin leads to its discoloration. Changes in the relative proportion of MetMb, DeoMb, and OxyMb in the yellowfin tuna fillets over the storage time are shown in Fig. 1D. The relative proportion of OxyMb showed a downward trend during the storage period, starting at 33.52 ± 1.48 % and then steadily decreasing to 10.61 ± 1.20 % after 6 d, finally reaching below 1 % (P < 0.05). Accordingly, the relative content of MetMb increased from 46.91 ± 2.86 % at 0 d to 58.95 ± 2.32 % at 3 d, and gradually increased to 71.14 ± 3.12 % after 6 d, reaching 77.14 ± 2.62 % at 9 d (P < 0.05). These changes can be primarily attributed to the autoxidation of yellowfin tuna fillets during storage, which refers to the conversion of OxyMb to MetMb (Palanisamy et al., 2025).

TPA

The TPA parameters, such as hardness, adhesiveness, springiness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience, are critical determinants of the palatability and eating quality of fish muscles. As shown in Table 1, the hardness, springiness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience of yellowfin tuna fillets gradually decreased during storage and were significantly lower than the initial values at the end of storage (P < 0.05). All this was due to the gradual breakdown of proteins in fillets by endogenous proteases and microorganisms, and the integrity of the muscle tissue was compromised (Chen et al., 2024). These textural changes are consistent with the trend of texture alterations during cold storage as reported by others (Bu et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2025). Conversely, adhesiveness exhibited an increasing tendency over the storage period, with significantly higher values on days 3, 6, and 9 compared to day 0 (P < 0.05). A plausible explanation for this is that microbial activity leads to the spoilage of fillets and the production of mucus, thereby enhancing adhesiveness (Bu et al., 2022).

Centrifugation loss

Water constitutes the primary component of fish muscle, and its content exerts a significant influence on the flavor and quality of fillets. The centrifugation loss can be an indication of the ability of fillets to retain their water during storage (Wang et al., 2022). The centrifugation loss of fresh yellowfin tuna fillets was 20.16 ± 0.75 %, which gradually increased to 21.82 ± 0.25 % (3 d), 24.34 ± 0.74 % (6 d), and 25.92 ± 0.79 % (9 d) with the increase in storage time (P < 0.05, Fig. 1E). In line with the current results, increased centrifugation loss in salmon and olive flounder during cold storage was also previously reported by other authors (Park et al., 2022). The rapid increase of centrifugation loss in fillets was highly relative with the degree of protein oxidation. Protein oxidation results in the exposure of hydrophobic groups, which in turn diminishes the protein's ability to bind with water and raises the centrifugal loss in fillets (Zhao et al., 2021). It is undesirable because excessive loss of water from the fillets can result in the loss of some water-soluble nutrients such as proteins and amino acids (Xie et al., 2020).

ATP-related compounds and K value

Nucleotide degradation in fish muscle occurred frequently during cold storage, in which degradation of ATP proceeded to ADP, AMP, IMP, HxR, and Hx (Dai et al., 2024). As revealed in Fig. 1F–I, the initial contents of AMP, IMP, HxR, and Hx in yellowfin tuna fillets were 0.11 ± 0.0074, 12.17 ± 0.11, 0.39 ± 0.014, and 1.08 ± 0.066 μmol/g, respectively. Following an extension of storage time to 9 days, AMP and IMP decreased to 0.08 ± 0.0038 and 5.24 ± 0.16 μmol/g, and HxR and Hx increased to 1.70 ± 0.051 and 10.94 ± 0.55 μmol/g, respectively (P < 0.05). Likewise, the IMP content in bighead carp fillets exhibited a rapid downward trend, while HxR and Hx increased within 6 days of refrigeration at 4 °C (Dai et al., 2024). AMP and IMP are capable of enhancing the pleasant and umami flavor of fish, with their declining trends attributed to degradation into the next level of degradation products (Cheng et al., 2023). In contrast, HxR and Hx usually impart an undesirable bitter taste and off-flavor to fish muscle, whose accumulation resulted from the degradation of IMP caused by both autolytic and microbial enzymes (Cheng et al., 2023).

It is worth noting that ATP and its related compounds function both as key flavor determinants and reliable freshness indicators in aquatic products (Chu et al., 2024). The K value, a quantitative freshness metric, categorizes fish quality into three distinct levels: premium freshness (K < 20 %, suitable for raw consumption), acceptable freshness (K = 20–60 %, requiring cooking), and spoilage (K > 60 %, unsafe for consumption) (Omwange et al., 2021). On day 0, the K value of yellowfin tuna fillets was 10.33 ± 0.38 % (Fig. 1J), close to the result of fresh bigeye tuna previously reported by Lan et al. (2020). In particular, the K value of the fillets exceeded the prime freshness criterion on day 3 and increased to 70.73 ± 1.48 % on day 9, indicating spoilage of the fillets. The rising K value reflects progressive freshness deterioration in fillets, which is primarily caused by muscle autolysis and enzymatic activity (Zheng et al., 2017).

Changes in microbiological properties

The main cause of fillet spoilage is the action of microorganisms, and the change of TVC is an important indicator for evaluating the quality and spoilage of yellowfin tuna fillets. The initial TVC in yellowfin tuna fillets was 2.90 ± 0.016 lg CFU/g (Fig. 2A), which was at the first level of freshness. On the 3rd day of storage, the TVC reached 4.61 ± 0.14 lg CFU/g, exceeding the maximum recommended limit of 4 lg CFU/g in tuna for raw consumption (Chinese aquaculture industry standards, SC/T 3117-2006). The TVC of fillets kept increasing and reached 8.03 ± 0.22 lg CFU/g on day 9, higher than the maximum edible limit (7 lg CFU/g) for fish (Yu et al., 2022).

Changes in TVC (A), PBC (B), and sensory scores (C) of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage. Values were expressed as mean ± SD in each group (n = 3). Means with different letters differ significantly at P < 0.05.

Psychrophilic bacteria negatively affect the sensorial and hygienic attributes of fish due to their high proteolytic and lipolytic bioactivities (Pei et al., 2022). Therefore, PBC is extremely important for judging the quality of fresh fillets when they are stored under aerobic conditions at chilled temperatures. As shown in Fig. 2B, PBC increased rapidly during cold storage of yellowfin tuna fillets (P < 0.05), with a trend similar to that of TVC. The PBC of fillets after 3, 6, and 9 days of storage was 1.59, 2.41, and 2.72 times higher than that of fresh fillets, respectively.

Sensory analysis

Sensory analysis was employed to evaluate the sensory attributes of yellowfin tuna fillets, including color, odor, texture, and overall quality, offering insights into participants' preferences and acceptance (Fig. 2C). The sensory attributes of the fresh fillets scored nearly a perfect 5 (4.8 ± 0.17), indicating their excellent appearance, aroma, quality, and acceptability. As storage time prolonged, all sensory scores declined gradually. The overall quality score of the fillets reached the limit of acceptance on the 3rd day of cold storage with an overall score of 3.4 ± 0.31, as the limit for acceptance was kept at 3 (Khodanazary & Mohammadzadeh, 2023). From the 6th day, the color of the fillets began to darken, had softer textures, and a strong fishy odor began to develop. The overall quality score decreased from 2.27 ± 0.21 (6 d) to 1.5 ± 0.36 (9 d), all participants found it difficult to accept, and the fillets were considered to exceed the upper limit of acceptability.

Changes in protein-related properties

TVB-N

TVB-N is an alkaline nitrogen-containing substance generated in animal foods as a result of protein degradation by endogenous enzymes and specific spoilage bacteria during the process of corruption (Zheng et al., 2017). TVB-N is widely recognized as an important indicator of seafood freshness and spoilage, with higher TVB-N values indicating higher levels of corruption. The initial TVB-N content in fresh yellowfin tuna fillets was 8.09 ± 0.24 mg/100 g, and the content gradually increased to 29.56 ± 0.86 mg/100 g at day 9 (P < 0.05, Fig. 3A), exceeding the maximum recommended limit of 25 mg/100 g in tuna for raw consumption (SC/T 3117–2006).

Changes in TVB-N (A), total sulfhydryl content (B), total carbonyl content (C), PV value (D), and TBARS value (E) of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage. Values were expressed as mean ± SD in each group (n = 3). Means with different letters differ significantly at P < 0.05.

Total sulfhydryl content and carbonyl content

The extent of protein oxidation in fillets can be assessed based on the loss of sulfhydryl groups or the generation of carbonyl groups. As depicted in Fig. 3B, the total sulfhydryl content in yellowfin tuna fillets exhibited a significant decreasing trend with increasing days of storage, decreasing by 6.26 %, 9.60 %, and 13.14 % on days 3, 6, and 9 of storage, respectively (P < 0.05). The findings indicated that the active sulfhydryl groups in proteins were exposed and oxidized to disulfide bonds, leading to a reduction in total sulfhydryl content. This phenomenon has been consistently observed in various fish species during cold storage, including southern bluefin tuna (Bu et al., 2022), sea bass (Lan et al., 2025), and other fish species (Gong et al., 2025) stored at 4 °C.

The carbonyl content of yellowfin tuna fillets showed a significant increasing trend after being stored for 9 days (Fig. 3C, P < 0.05). The carbonyl content of fresh fillets was 2.54 ± 0.10 μmol/g and significantly increased at 3 d (5.27 ± 0.29 μmol/g), 6 d (9.03 ± 0.34 μmol/g), and 9 d (11.95 ± 0.34 μmol/g) of storage, respectively. Consistent with findings from a prior similar study, the carbonyl content in common carp fillets also increased significantly (P < 0.05) following 10 days of cold storage (Gong et al., 2025). Elevated carbonyl contents promote protein cross-linking, adversely affecting fillets' quality attributes like juiciness and tenderness (Ganjeh et al., 2024).

FAAs composition

The content and composition of FAAs usually reflect the nutritional value and taste of fish. The relative changes in 17 FAAs detected in yellowfin tuna fillets during storage are shown in Table 2. During the first 6 days of storage, the total content of FAA in fillets increased significantly (P < 0.05), which was mainly due to the gradual formation of FAA caused by protein degradation (Lin et al., 2025). In the subsequent three days of storage, the total FFA content decreased significantly from a maximum value of 1594.29 ± 24.53 mg/100 g (day 6) to 1562.56 ± 18.30 mg/100 g (day 9, P < 0.05). Much of this can be ascribed to microbial activities that break down amino acids (e.g., amino acid deamidation and decarboxylation) into biogenic amines, ammonia, and volatile organic compounds during the later stages of storage (Zhuang et al., 2023). Similarly, the refrigerated sturgeon fillets showed a significant increase in FAA content in the first 6 days of storage, after which it dropped rapidly (Tan et al., 2023).

In fresh yellowfin tuna fillets, His was the most abundant FAA, followed by Cys and Lys. This finding differs from the study of Wang et al. (2022) on bluefin tuna, which identified His, Lys, and Ala as the predominant FAAs in naked body, big belly, and middle belly parts, likely attributable to interspecies variation and tissue-specific composition. The concentration of His and Lys in yellowfin tuna fillets was relatively stable during the entire cold storage period (P > 0.05), while Cys content showed a marked decline (P < 0.05). It is undesirable since Cys is a flavor precursor that contributes to meaty aroma compounds (Ding et al., 2023).

FAA is further categorized into umami, sweet, bitter, and non-flavor amino acids according to their taste properties and structures (Yin et al., 2023). The content of Glu makes up over 80 % of the total umami amino acid content in fresh yellowfin tuna fillets, reaching its maximum on the 3rd day of storage and finally decreasing to 21.50 ± 7.64 mg/100 g on the 9th day (P < 0.05). Pro, which actively contributes to the sweetness of aquatic products, its content in fillets increased from 11.50 ± 0.69 mg/100 g on day 0 to 14.43 ± 1.16 mg/100 g on day 3, and then decreased to 6.50 ± 0.44 (6 d) and 9.50 ± 0.46 (9 d) mg/100 g (P < 0.05). Moreover, the contents of some of the bitter amino acids, including Met, Ile, Tyr, and Phe, increased significantly (P < 0.05) during storage. These findings align with Yu et al. (2018), who reported that during the refrigerated storage of grass carp fillets, the contents of umami and sweet amino acids such as Asp, Glu, Gly, and Pro decreased, while the contents of Phe, Leu, and Ile increased. The consumption of umami and sweet amino acids, as well as the accumulation of bitter amino acids, would lead to a decline in the taste of tuna, which was detrimental to maintaining flavor during fish storage (Wang et al., 2022).

Changes in lipid-related properties

PV

Measurement of the amount of PV enabled the determination of the amount of primary oxidation products (hydroperoxides) formed during the storage of fillets. As shown in Fig. 3D, the PV of fresh yellowfin tuna fillets was 5.33 ± 0.17 meq/kg, and when storage at 4 °C for 3, 6, and 9 days, the PV of fillets increased to 7.08 ± 0.90, 13.10 ± 0.32, and 29.77 ± 0.73 meq/kg (an increase of approximately 0.33-fold, 1.46-fold, and 4.59-fold, P < 0.05), respectively. These results indicated that longer storage time could produce more peroxide and increase the oxidation rate of yellowfin tuna fillets. Comparable results have been observed in refrigerated catla (Gibelion catla) fillets (Kundu et al., 2024).

TBARS

The TBARS value is mainly used to evaluate the generation of secondary oxidation products like MDA, and is a key indicator limiting the shelf life of high-fat aquatic products (Mehraie et al., 2023). Fig. 3E demonstrates a progressive elevation (P < 0.05) in TBARS values for yellowfin tuna fillets throughout refrigerated storage, about 0.32 ± 0.15 mg MDA/kg in fresh fillets and about 7.82 ± 0.52 mg MDA/kg at storage termination. This progressive increase confirmed the occurrence of substantial lipid oxidative deterioration in yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage. Similar findings were reported by Cheng et al. (2023), lipids and unsaturated fatty acids enriched in tilapia are susceptible to oxidation, which produces MDA and increases the TBARS value.

Lipidomics analysis

Lipid composition analysis

Tuna fillets are prone to oxidation and rancidity as they contain a high level of unsaturated lipid molecules. By studying the lipid changes and key lipid markers in yellowfin tuna during cold storage, relevant evidence can be provided for its quality identification and control. In this study, changes in the lipid spectrum of yellowfin tuna fillets after 0, 3, and 6 days of cold storage were analyzed using lipidomics, and the storage time of 9 days was excluded as the fillets on the 9th day of storage already showed visible spoilage.

As depicted in Fig. 4A, a total of 665 lipid molecules in yellowfin tuna fillets were identified, covering five categories and 29 subclasses. These lipid categories mainly consisted of 405 glycerophospholipids (GP, 60.90 %), 126 glycerolipids (GL, 18.95 %), 122 sphingolipids (SP, 18.34 %), 9 fatty acids (FA, 1.35 %), and 3 saccharolipids (SL, 0.45 %). Among them, the most abundant lipid subclass was phosphatidylcholines (PC) with 22.26 %, accounting for 22.26 % of the total lipids, followed by triacylglycerols (TG, 17.59 %), and phosphatidylethanolamines (PE, 15.94 %). TGs (neutral lipids), serving as the primary lipids for energy storage and fatty acid transport in fish, undergo degradation into diacylglycerol (DGs) and FAs, which further contribute to off-flavors (Zhou et al., 2024). In this study, a trend of TG hydrolysis was observed during the cold storage of yellowfin tuna fillets, manifested as a decrease in TG (such as TG (16:0/16:1/18:3), TG (16:1/16:1/22:6), and TG (16:1/14:1/20:5)) and a simultaneous increase in DGs (DG (16:0/20:1), DG (18:3/22:6), and DG (20:5/22:6)) and FAs (FA (18:2), FA (20:5), FA (22:6) and FA (20:4)). PCs and PEs are the primary phospholipid components in aquatic muscle tissue, critically involved in maintaining cell physical structure, modulating membrane fluidity, and facilitating signal transduction (Chu et al., 2025). PCs primarily provide fluidity to cell membranes, while PEs primarily provide rigidity (Yan et al., 2022). Furthermore, PCs can undergo further oxidation to generate large amounts of volatile substances (Zhou et al., 2024). Similar to the findings of previous studies, TG, PC, and PE are also the dominant lipid subclasses in bigeye tuna (Wang, Xie, et al., 2021), grouper (Zhou et al., 2024), and grass carp (Chu et al., 2025).

Lipid composition (A), PCA (B), PLS-DA (C), and permutation test of PLS-DA model (D) of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage detected by lipidomics analysis.

PCA and PLS-DA

In the present study, PCA and PLS-DA models were constructed to compare the overall lipid profile variations among yellowfin tuna fillets under different storage times. The PCA analysis results are shown in Fig. 4B, where the two principal components (PC1 and PC2) account for 57.5 % and 13.6 % of the variance. The three groups were clearly separated, showing significant differences in lipid composition among fillets stored for different cold storage days.

The supervised PLS-DA results (Fig. 4C) showed that three groups of samples can be divided into three well-separated clusters in the scatter plots. The parameters of Q2 and R2Y in the PLS-DA model were calculated as 0.721 and 0.956, respectively, demonstrating that the constructed PLS-DA model in this study was robust, with strong discriminative and predictive capability. Moreover, the validity and reliability of the PLS-DA model were further assessed through a permutation test (n = 1000) (Fig. 4D). Taken together, the clear separations among fillets samples under different storage times in the PCA and PLS-DA models further demonstrated that the prolonged refrigeration storage time leads to significant alterations in the lipid profiles of yellowfin tuna fillets, laying a foundation for further analysis of lipidomics.

DAL analysis

The top 30 differential lipids with the highest VIP values from the PLS-DA in each comparison group were selected for visual analysis (Fig. 5A-C). Through the heatmap and VIP bar chart, the expression pattern of lipids in each different storage time group and the P value of lipids in VIP were presented, facilitating the intuitive identification of the importance of differential lipids and their expression trend changes. The higher VIP value paired with a smaller P value signifies more notable differences in target lipids between samples. Fig. 5A showed PE (18:2/20:4) had VIP = 5.52. As shown in Fig. 5B, six lipids had VIP scores >5, including PE (16:0/16:0) (VIP = 7.37), PC (18:1/14:0) (VIP = 6.50), PC (14:0/20:4) (VIP = 5.95), PC (16:0/22:6) (VIP = 5.88), PE (18:2/20:4) (VIP = 5.51), and CL (18:2/20:1/20:4/20:4) (VIP = 5.23). There were also six lipids had VIP scores >5 in Fig. 5C, including PC (14:0/20:4), Cer (t18:1/25:3), PE (18:0/16:0), PC (31:1/18:1), PC (32:1/18:1), and PE (16:0/16:0). They were the lipids with the highest VIP scores in each comparative group, which could be the potential lipid species for differentiating the cold storage time of yellowfin tuna fillets.

To further figure out the main differential molecules, lipids with a VIP >1 and P < 0.05 were regarded as DALs. A total of 123 DALs (74 upregulated and 49 downregulated) were detected in the L0 vs L3 group, 53 DALs were found in the L3 vs L6 group (22 upregulated and 31 downregulated), and 102 DALs (55 upregulated and 47 downregulated) were detected in the L0 vs L6 group (Table S1). A Venn diagram (Fig. 5D) was used to analyze the shared and unique differentially abundant lipids among the three groups. As a result, there were 16 shared DALs were identified (Fig. 5E), including Cer (t18:1/25:3), PE (18:0/16:0), PC (31:1/18:1), PC (32:1/18:1), PC (30:1/18:1), PAF (20:5), PC (14:0/22:6), PE (16:0/22:6), PC (32:1/20:4), PC(31:1/22:6), PC (30:1/22:6), PC (30:1/20:4), PC (32:1/22:6), PC (14:0/20:5), PS (22:6/22:6), PC (15:0/20:4).

PE, PC, and PS represent the predominant species of GPs, critically involved in cellular proliferation, metabolic regulation, and immune function enhancement (Chen et al., 2022). PC and PE act as substrates for PS synthesis, undergoing base exchange with serine under the catalysis of phosphatidylserine synthase (Jia, Shi, et al., 2021). Additionally, PEs can be generated through the decarboxylation of PS in mitochondria (Guo et al., 2022). In the current research, the significant changes in the PC, PE, and PS contents of yellowfin tuna fillets indicated that they were converted to some extent during cold storage. Several identified PCs (PC (15:0/20:4) and PC (14:0/20:5)) contain polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), including arachidonic acid (ARA, 20:4) and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5), which are highly susceptible to lipid oxidation (Zhang et al., 2020). Their reduced content implied potential lipid oxidation and the generation of volatile secondary oxidation products such as aldehydes, ketones, and alcohols (Chu et al., 2023), which may explain the formation of volatile odors (such as fishy smell) observed in sensory evaluation. Significantly, the content of saturated fatty acids (PE (18:0/16:0)) in fillets increased during cold storage. PE (18:0/16:0) was also screened as a lipid biomarker in marble beef during irradiation (Zhang et al., 2023). As a component of related organelles and plasma membrane, Cer in SPs has been associated with cellular apoptotic signaling and growth regulation in biological systems (Panevska et al., 2019). In this study, the progressive elevation of Cer (t18:1/25:3) throughout storage suggested a potential link to apoptotic-like processes in yellowfin tuna muscle cells. This was consistent with the reported Cer (d18:1/16:0) increases in 24-day refrigerated Tan sheep, which demonstrated that apoptosis in cells for the whole cold chain was accompanied by a change in intracellular levels of corresponding Cer (Jia, Li, et al., 2021). PAF, a potent phospholipid mediator, which is secreted by cells in response to various stimuli and might function as a propagation factor to accelerate ferroptosis in the cell population (Zhang, Sun, et al., 2024). Elevated PAF (20:5) level in cold-stored fillets accompanied by increased lipid peroxidation products suggest PAF may participate in ferroptosis-related pathways, consistent with its known role in promoting oxidative stress. However, the specific roles of Cer (t18:1/25:3) and PAF (20:5) in apoptosis or ferroptosis in yellowfin tuna require targeted experimental validation in future studies.

To further contextualize the biological specificity of these DALs, a comparative analysis was conducted against lipid markers reported in other pivotal tuna lipidomics studies. The 27 lipid biomarkers proposed by Hu et al. (2022) for distinguishing three commercial tuna species (skipjack tuna, bigeye tuna, and yellowfin tuna) are all GPs. Similarly, the vast majority of DALs identified as freshness of yellowfin tuna fillets in this study were also GPs. This finding reinforces the pivotal role of GPs as core lipid “reaction carriers” in tuna. Significantly, FA (C22:6), PL (18:3/22:6), and PL (18:1/22:6) served as markers of thermal oxidation-induced degradation in cooked bigeye tuna (Song et al., 2023); FA (C22:6) and PI (C18:0/22:6) reflected prolonged oxidative damage during the wet-aging stage of albacore tuna (Shen et al., 2022); in this study, six docosahexaenoic acid (DHA)-containing GPs (PC (14:0/22:6), PE (16:0/22:6), PC (31:1/22:6), PC (30:1/22:6), PC (32:1/22:6), and PS (22:6/22:6)) were similarly identified as DALs driven by cold storage-induced oxidative stress. These results highlight that DHA-containing lipids constitute a universal indicator of oxidative disruption in tuna, likely due to DHA's high degree of unsaturation, making it susceptible to peroxidation under various stressors (heat, aging, cold storage). Among the 16 DALs identified in this study, PAF (20:5) and Cer (t18:1/25:3) have not been previously reported as lipid biomarkers in tuna lipidomics studies, perhaps due to their insensitivity to cold storage, and distinguishing them from species-, thermal processing-, or aging-related lipid biomarkers. In general, the abundance of these lipids significantly changed during storage, suggesting that they could serve as potential lipid markers in yellowfin tuna during cold storage.

Correlation analysis

Pearson's correlation analysis was further performed between the above 16 DALs and the quality parameters of yellowfin tuna fillets with the correlation matrix corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) to avoid false positives. As illustrated in Fig. 6A, all 16 DALs had significant correlations with a*/b*, total pigment content, Mb content, and the relative proportion of MetMb and OxyMb, indicating that color deterioration of fillets was strongly related to lipid changes. In line with this, Bu et al. (2022) have shown that the color darkening of tuna during storage was closely associated with lipid oxidative degradation. The primary and secondary products generated from the oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids exhibit strong reactivity and accelerate the oxidation of OxyMb to MetMb, leading to discoloration of tuna fillets (Bu et al., 2022). All 16 DALs showed negative correlations with hardness, gumminess, chewiness, and resilience, and positively correlated with adhesiveness, with only a weak correlation between PC (30:1/18:1) and springiness. These findings suggested that texture softening during cold storage was affected by the changes in lipid profile. TG (50:6e) in Litopenaeus vannamei stored under low temperature showed strong negative associations with cohesiveness and hardness, and was identified as a potential biomarker of lipid oxidation (Wang, Chen, et al., 2021). The close relationship between texture and key differential lipids in oysters further supports this notion (Hu et al., 2025).

Pearson's correlation analysis (A) and Mantel test (B) between quality parameters and DALs of yellowfin tuna fillets.

Cer (t18:1/25:3), PE (18:0/16:0), PC (31:1/18:1), PC (32:1/18:1), PC (30:1/18:1), PAF (20:5), PC (14:0/22:6), PE (16:0/22:6), PC (32:1/20:4), PC (31:1/22:6), PC (30:1/22:6), PC (30:1/20:4), and PC (32:1/22:6) showed strong positive correlations with centrifugation loss, Hx, HxR, and K value, and exhibited highly significant negative correlations with AMP and IMP. Conversely, PC (14:0/20:5), PS (22:6/22:6), and PC (15:0/20:4) had negative correlations with centrifugation loss, Hx, HxR, and K value, and had significant positive correlations with AMP and IMP. All 16 DALs had highly significant positive correlations with TVC and PBC. These findings aligned with established mechanisms in meat biochemistry where microbial activity and lipid oxidation mutually influence each other (Chu et al., 2025). On the one hand, specific spoilage bacteria (e.g., Shewanella putrefaciens and Pseudomonas spp.) prevalent in cold-stored fish secrete lipases and phospholipases that directly hydrolyze GPs and SPs (the primary categories among the 16 DALs identified in current research), producing fatty acids with fewer double bonds or saturated fatty acids (Chu et al., 2025; Dou et al., 2025). On the other hand, these lipid degradation products subsequently create a nutrient-rich biochemical environment, which in turn promotes the proliferation of bacteria possessing lipolytic capabilities (Deng et al., 2020). Thus, a bidirectional interaction between lipid oxidation and microbial growth manifests, forming a self-accelerating cycle of spoilage within cold-stored fillets.

Moreover, all 16 DALs were closely correlated with the protein (TVB-N, total sulfhydryl, and carbonyl) and lipid (PV and TBARS)-related properties of fillets. PV and TBARS are indicators that reflect the oxidation degree of lipids, thus, they were closely related to the changes of DALs. The strong correlation between DALs and protein oxidation properties highlighted the intricate interplay between lipid changes and protein oxidation in yellowfin tuna fillets. Hu et al. (2025) discovered that the reactive oxidation products formed by lipid oxidation in oysters played a key role in driving protein oxidation during frozen storage. Among the identified DALs in this study, PC and PE species containing PUFAs (such as PC (32:1/20:4), PC (14:0/22:6)), and PE (16:0/22:6)) are prone to being attacked by free radicals, forming aldehydes (e.g., MDA, 4-HNE, and 4-ONE) (Huang et al., 2024). These aldehydes covalently bind to cysteine sulfhydryl groups and lysine amino groups in proteins to induce intra- or intermolecular cross-linking (Zhang, Fu, et al., 2024), directly explaining the observed loss of protein sulfhydryl groups and formation of protein carbonyls. PAF is widely recognized as a potent phospholipid mediator involved in multiple physiological processes, capable of inducing the release of reactive oxygen species (Lordan et al., 2019). PAF (20:5) identified in this study likely plays an indirect but amplifying role in lipid-protein oxidation crosstalk. PAF-induced ROS accelerate the oxidation of PC/PE, and the resulting oxidized lipid products further enhance protein oxidation, thereby strengthening the correlation between DAL and protein oxidation. Taken together, these results indicate that lipid changes and oxidative degradation of proteins are not separate occurrences but interconnected processes that reinforce one another, jointly contributing to the quality decline of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage. Unfortunately, no significant correlation was observed between these 16 DALs and the content of total FFAs and most FAAs.

To further visualize the relationship between the quality parameters with the DALs in the yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage, a correlation heatmap was generated using the Mantel test. As shown in Fig. 6B, a robust association was observed between the 16 detected DALs and the changes in color, TPA, WHC, ATP-related compounds, microbiological properties, protein, and lipid-related properties of fillets during cold storage. Overall, the screened 16 DALs were significantly associated with the vast majority of quality characteristics, which means that these DALs could be potential lipid biomarkers for evaluating the quality of yellowfin tuna fillets comprehensively.

Conclusion

The study investigated the changes in quality and lipid profiles of yellowfin tuna fillets during cold storage and explored the correlations between quality changes and key DALs. Results showed that, as the storage time increased, the color, texture, centrifugation loss, freshness, and sensory score of fillets gradually deteriorated. Simultaneously, protein oxidation and lipid oxidation occurred in fillets during cold storage, as evidenced by increased TVB-N, carbonyl content, PV, and TBARS, and decreased sulfhydryl content. Lipidomics analyses further revealed substantial changes in lipid profiles and identified 16 highly significant DALs. Correlation analysis indicated that these DALs were significantly related to the vast majority of quality characteristics and could be used as potential biomarkers of quality deterioration in yellowfin tuna fillets. This study advances a better understanding of the quality deterioration mechanism of yellowfin tuna fillets. Moreover, further researches are required to innovate the storage methods of fillets and to monitor the effects of lipid profiles modifications on the quality of tuna products.
