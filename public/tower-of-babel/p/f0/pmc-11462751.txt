Effect of pneumonia on the outcomes of acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fangbin Zheng, Xuqin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess the effect of pneumonia on the risk of mortality and other clinical outcomes in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD).MethodsPubMed, EMBASE and Scopus were screened for observational cohort and case-control studies that reported outcomes in AECOPD patients with and without pneumonia. Pooled effect sizes were reported as relative risks (RR) or hazard ratio (HR) for categorical outcomes and as weighted mean difference (WMD) for continuous outcomes. The primary outcome was mortality. Secondary outcomes were risk of admission to intensive care unit (ICU), need for assisted ventilation and readmission as well as duration of stay at the hospital. The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the GRADE approach.ResultsThirteen studies were included. AECOPD patients with pneumonia had significantly higher risk of in-hospital mortality (RR 2.29, 95% CI: 1.40, 3.73), mortality at 1 month (RR 1.84, 95% CI: 1.09, 3.13), and 1 year or more of follow-up (HR 2.30, 95% CI: 1.15, 4.61) compared to AECOPD patients without pneumonia. Pneumonia was associated with significantly higher risk of admission to ICU (RR 2.79, 95% CI: 1.47, 5.28), need for assisted ventilation (RR 2.02, 95% CI: 1.52, 2.67), and longer hospital stay (in days) (WMD 3.31, 95% CI: 2.33, 4.29). The risk of readmission was comparable in the two groups of patients (RR 1.07, 95% CI: 0.97, 1.19). The overall quality of evidence for the outcomes was judged to be “Low”.ConclusionPneumonia during acute exacerbation of COPD may lead to increases in both short-term and long-term mortality as well as increased hospital stay, need for ventilatory support and admission to ICU. Our findings suggest the need for close monitoring, early intervention, and long-term follow-up, to improve the outcomes in AECOPD patients with concurrent pneumonia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03305-1.Keywords: COPD, Acute exacerbation, Pneumonia, Mortality, Readmission, Ventilation support, Intensive care, Complications, Hospital stay, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition that is characterized by airflow limitation with episodes of acute exacerbations that significantly impact patients’ well-being [1]. Around 200 million cases of COPD were reported globally in the year 2019 alone, with nearly 3 million deaths and 75 million disability adjusted life years (DALYs) [2]. COPD patients are highly susceptible to developing respiratory infections [3]. Viral upper respiratory tract infections, bronchitis, and other respiratory tract infections can be a triggers of COPD exacerbations that significantly increase risk of hospitalization and clinical complications in this population of patients [4, 5].

Although pneumonia has traditionally been recognized as a cause of acute exacerbation of COPD (AECOPD), there is still no consensus on whether coexistence of these conditions contributes to increased mortality and adverse clinical outcomes [6, 7]. While some studies showed that pneumonia is associated with higher mortality risks in COPD patients, other studies did not support this association [8–12]. A recent systematic review by Yu et al. (2021) found that the presence of community acquired pneumonia (CAP) was associated with higher mortality, longer length of hospital stay, more need for mechanical ventilation, and an increased ICU admissions in hospitalized COPD patients. The review concluded that coexisting CAP may be associated with increased mortality and morbidity in hospitalized COPD patients. Our aim of conducting this review was to provide updated evidence. The meta-analysis by Yu et al. (2021) included studies published up to December 2019 [13]. In the nearly four years since then, numerous additional studies have been published on this topic. Given the rapid advancements in the quality of health care, including that of diagnosis and management, we believe an updated review is necessary to potentially inform clinical practice. The intention is to review the current literature and to document the impact of pneumonia on clinical outcomes of AECOPD patients.

Materials and methods

Search strategy and databases searches

A comprehensive systematic search of PubMed, Embase, and Scopus databases was done with no restriction on the publication date. The search criteria encompassed terms: (COPD OR chronic obstructive pulmonary disease OR obstructive pulmonary disease) AND (community-acquired pneumonia OR pneumonia OR bacterial lung infection OR viral lung infection OR nosocomial pneumonia) AND (mortality OR death OR survival OR hospital stay OR complications OR readmission OR ICU admission OR ventilator support OR adverse outcomes). The search was confined to studies published until November 30, 2023. In addition to electronic searches, reference lists and relevant review articles were manually searched to ensure a thorough exploration of the available literature.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: Observational cohort and case-control studies; Studies in adult (> 18 years old) population; Confirmed diagnosis of AECOPD; Studies with a comparator group of AECOPD patients without pneumonia; Studies, directly comparing outcomes between COPD patients with and without concurrent pneumonia during acute exacerbations; Studies with at least one of the specified outcomes of interest: mortality, ICU admission, need for assisted ventilation, readmission or duration of hospital stay; English-language peer-reviewed studies. The included studies confirmed COPD through spirometry or by reviewing medical records or documentation based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. Pneumonia was identified either through radiological confirmation with or without associated clinical symptoms, or ICD coding/medical records.

Exclusion criteria: Studies with patients having respiratory disease condition other than COPD; Case reports, letters, reviews, and conference abstracts; Studies with duplicate reports; Studies with incomplete or unclear data regarding relevant outcomes.

Study selection and data extraction

After the initial search and identification of relevant studies, the subsequent step entailed the removal of duplicate studies. Study authors then independently conducted a review of the titles and abstracts of each study, and selected eligible reports were subjected to further full-text evaluation to determine their eligibility for inclusion. Any disagreements were resolved by discussion. Data extraction was independently done by the two authors using a standardized form. All disagreements were resolved by consensus. Risk of bias was assessed by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [14]. Pooled effect sizes were presented as relative risks (RR) or hazard ratio (HR) for categorical outcomes and weighted mean difference (WMD) for continuous outcomes, accompanied by corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Random-effects model was used for all analyses to address potential variations in the baseline characteristics of the included studies. Egger’s test and visual inspection of the funnel plot [15] were used to evaluate publication bias. P < 0.05 indicated statistical significance. Subgroup analyses was conducted based on NOS score (≥ 8 and < 8), sample size (≥ 500 and < 500), type of associated pneumonia (community associated pneumonia, CAP and others such as ventilator associated/nosocomial pneumonia/pneumonia, nonspecific) and method of COPD diagnosis (spirometry based and ICD code based, or medical record based). Additionally, subgroup analysis was performed comparing studies where corticosteroid use was more prevalent in the pneumonia group with those where corticosteroids were used more in the non-pneumonia group. A similar subgroup analysis was also conducted for antibiotic use. All subgroup analyses were done post-hoc and not as part of the preplanned analysis. We assessed the credibility of effect modification in subgroup analysis using the Instrument to assess the Credibility of Effect Modification Analyses (ICEMAN) [16]. The credibility of effect modification was assessed for outcome(s) and subgroup(s) that included three or more studies within each subgroup and where the observed association varied across the subgroups. We evaluated the certainty of the overall pooled evidence using the standard GRADE approach and GRADE Pro software [17].

Additional details

The meta-analysis was performed based on to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [18], and the study protocol was pre-registered in PROSPERO, with the registration number CRD42024499958.

Results

General characteristics of the included studies

The search process identified 584 studies (Fig. 1). After de-duplication, 411 distinct studies were retained. Of them, 387 studies were excluded after title and abstract review. Full texts of the remaining 24 studies were then thoroughly examined, resulting in the exclusion of 11 more studies. Ultimately, 13 studies met the criteria for inclusion [8–12, 19–26] (Fig. 1).

Selection process of studies included in the review

Table 1 summarizes study characteristics. There were eight studies with a retrospective cohort design, four with a prospective cohort design and one study was a cross-sectional one. Majority of the studies (n = 7) had a follow-up period of 3 months or less, two studies had a 6-month period of follow-up and three studies had a follow-up period of one year or more (maximum of 68 months). In the included studies, six studies made a diagnosis of COPD based in Spirometry and in the remaining seven studies, it was based on medical records or ICD- based coding (Table 1). Furthermore, there were six studies that made a diagnosis of pneumonia based on medical records or ICD-based coding and seven studies that based their diagnosis on radiological findings of new or persistent infiltrate with or without associated clinical symptoms (Table 1). There were five studies where specific aetiology of pneumonia was not provided. In three studies, the pneumonia had a bacterial aetiology, and five studies reported community-acquired or nosocomial pneumonia. Two studies each were conducted in United Kingdom, Spain and China. One study each was conducted in France, India, Egypt, South Korea, USA, Denmark and Norway. Overall, the included studies reported data of 77,650 patients, 25,397 of them with pneumonia. Ten studies reported on the use of corticosteroids, with four of them documenting a higher proportion of subjects with AECOPD and associated pneumonia on corticosteroids (either systemic or inhaled), compared to those with AECOPD without associated pneumonia (Table 1). In the remaining six studies, a higher proportion of corticosteroid use was observed in subjects without pneumonia (Table 1). Data on antibiotic use was provided in five studies. In three of these studies, a higher proportion of subjects with AECOPD and associated pneumonia were started on antibiotics compared to those with AECOPD but without associated pneumonia (Table 1). In the other two studies, a higher proportion of antibiotic use was noted in subjects without pneumonia.

In most studies, the mean age and gender distribution, as well as the proportion of patients with comorbidities, were comparable (P > 0.05) among the two patient groups (with and without associated pneumonia). A higher proportion of patients with associated pneumonia required use of corticosteroids and antibiotics and had elevated inflammatory markers (e.g. c-reactive protein), white blood cell (WBC) and neutrophil counts, as well as higher rates of dyslipidaemia and long-term oxygen therapy (P < 0.05). The mean NOS score of the studies was 7.46. There were seven studies with a score of 8 (of the maximum possible score of 9), five studies with a score of 7 and one study with a score of 6.

Risk of mortality

Patients with AECOPD and pneumonia had significantly higher risk of in-hospital death (RR 2.29, 95% CI: 1.40, 3.73; N = 8, n = 23,327; I2 = 90.9%) and mortality at 1 month (RR 1.84, 95% CI: 1.09, 3.13; N = 3, n = 63,996; I2 = 98.0%) follow-up compared to AECOPD patients without accompanying pneumonia (Fig. 2). The overall in-hospital mortality rates in the two groups i.e., with and without pneumonia was 12.3% (732/5976) and 5.5% (959/17351) respectively. Similarly, the rates of mortality at 1 month follow up were 12.5% (2812/22488) and 8.1% (3357/41508) for groups with and without pneumonia. Risk of mortality between 3 and 6 months of follow up was also higher in this group (RR 1.38, 95% CI: 0.78, 2.46; N = 2, n = 9646; I2 = 78.0%) but no statistical significance was reported due to a small number of studies (n = 2) reporting the outcome at this follow-up period (Fig. 2). The overall mortality rate between 3 and 6 months of follow up in the two groups i.e., with and without pneumonia was 17.1% (280/1639) and 12.2% (974/8007) respectively. Risk of mortality was significantly higher in the AECOPD + pneumonia group at one year or more of follow-up (HR 2.30, 95% CI: 1.15, 4.61; N = 3, n = 11,283; I2 = 86.8%) (Fig. 3). The long-term mortality rate was 63.2% (2372/3755) in the pneumonia group and 61.9% (4667/7528) in the non-pneumonia group. No publication bias for the mortality outcomes was identified on Egger’s test (p > 0.05) and on funnel plots (Supplementary Figs. 1–4). For the mortality related outcomes, the overall quality of evidence was judged to be “Low” according to the GRADE assessment criteria (Table 2).

Subgroup analysis based on type of pneumonia (CAP and others such as ventilator associated/nosocomial pneumonia/pneumonia, nonspecific), sample size (≥ 500 and < 500), method of COPD diagnosis (spirometry based and ICD code based, or medical record based) showed that the increased risk of in-hospital mortality in patients with associated pneumonia remained sustained across all these subgroups (Supplementary Table 1). However, only the subgroup with studies having a higher NOS score (≥ 8) showed a significantly increased risk of in-hospital mortality in those with associated pneumonia; however, the credibility of this subgroup finding was assessed to be “very low” (Supplementary Table 2). Further, in some of these subgroups, the observed heterogeneity was substantially reduced (in those with CAP, studies with sample size less than 500, in those where the diagnosis of COPD was made based on spirometry and in analysis based on corticosteroid or antibiotic use) (Supplementary Table 1). Subgroup analysis based on the use of corticosteroids or antibiotics among pneumonia and non-pneumonia groups did not provide any indication suggesting that the mortality differences observed were because of variations in receiving of these medications (Supplementary Table 1). However, the number of studies contributing to this subgroup analysis were small and therefore, should be carefully interpreted.

Risk of ICU admission, need for ventilation and readmission

AECOPD patients with pneumonia had significantly higher risk of admission to ICU (RR 2.79, 95% CI: 1.47, 5.28; N = 4, n = 54,168; I2 = 93.9%) and assisted ventilation requirement (RR 2.02, 95% CI: 1.52, 2.67; N = 10, n = 75,735; I2 = 89.9%) than AECOPD patients without pneumonia (Fig. 4). The overall rate of admission to ICU was 13.0% (2525/19387) in the pneumonia group and 7.8% in the group without pneumonia (2698/34781). Also, the risk of need for assisted ventilation was 15.1% (3751/24,877) in the pneumonia group and 8.9% (4549/50,858) in the non-pneumonia group. Risk of readmission within 6 months of discharge was similar in both groups (RR 1.07, 95% CI: 0.97, 1.19; N = 6, n = 63,654; I2 = 63.3%) (Fig. 4). The overall readmission rates in the two groups i.e., with and without pneumonia was 19.5% (4134/21,196) and 20.2% (8597/42,458) respectively. There was no evidence of publication bias on Egger’s test (p > 0.05) and on funnel plots (Supplementary Figs. 5–7). For these outcomes, the overall quality of evidence was judged to be “Low” according to the GRADE assessment criteria (Table 2).

Findings of subgroup analysis has been presented in Supplementary Table 1. The findings are largely similar to the overall pooled analysis and in some of the subgroups e.g., in the subgroup with COPD diagnosis made through spirometry or in the subgroup with studies having sample size of less than 500, the heterogeneity was much less than observed in the overall pooled analysis. For these subgroup analysis, the credibility of findings were assessed to be “very low” (Supplementary Table 2) In the subgroup analysis based on corticosteroid or antibiotic use among the pneumonia and non-pneumonia groups, the findings did not suggest any differences in the aforementioned outcomes attributable to the variations in medication received. However, the number of studies in these subgroup analyses were few and therefore, precaution should be taken to avoid over-interpretation of these findings.

Length of hospital stay

The overall mean (SD) number of days of hospital stay (in days) was 11.7 (2.9) for the pneumonia group and 8.21 (2.3) for those without pneumonia. Length of hospital stay (in days) (WMD 3.31, 95% CI: 2.33, 4.29; N = 9, n = 57,099; I2 = 99.7%) was statistically higher in patients with AECOPD accompanied by pneumonia compared to those without pneumonia (Fig. 5). Overall, mean duration of hospital stay in AECOPD patients with pneumonia was around 3 days higher. The overall quality of evidence was judged to be “Low” according to the GRADE assessment criteria (Table 2). The findings of the subgroup analysis have been presented in Supplementary Table 1. Similar to the overall pooled analysis, the high heterogeneity was also observed in the subgroup analyses. This is in contrast to the other outcomes where in certain subgroups, a substantial reduction in heterogeneity was noted. Findings from subgroup analysis for this outcome had “very low” credibility (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

This study shows that pneumonia during AECOPD correlates with significantly higher short-term and long-term mortality, longer hospital stay, higher need for ventilatory support and higher rate of ICU admission. The certainty of these findings was judged to be “low”, thereby suggesting that more studies with methodological rigor are needed to confirm these findings.

Nonetheless, our results suggest the need for close clinical attention and proactive management strategies to, possibly, mitigate the pneumonia-associated mortality risk in patients with COPD. The observed elevated mortality risks persisted beyond the immediate hospitalization period, with around 2-fold increased risk of mortality at the 1-month follow-up. Importantly, our analysis extends beyond short-term outcomes, indicating a sustained and statistically significant risk of mortality at 1 year or more of follow-up. This indicates towards the enduring impact of pneumonia on the prognosis of AECOPD patients, and further supports the suggestion for the need of long-term monitoring and tailored interventions to optimize patient outcomes.

Our findings also highlight the increased vulnerability of AECOPD patients with pneumonia for clinical deterioration. Specifically, we found that AECOPD patients with pneumonia had a significantly elevated risk of admission to ICU, and a greater need for assisted ventilation. Additionally, AECOPD patients with pneumonia required significantly (about 3 days) longer hospitalization, which could be associated with a marked burden on the healthcare system.

The observed increased risk of mortality, rates of ICU admission and a higher need for assisted ventilation could be attributed to the synergistic respiratory insult inflicted by pneumonia and COPD, which collectively exacerbate airway obstruction, inflammation, and impair gas exchange [3, 5]. Pneumonia-induced alveolar consolidation may further compromise the already compromised respiratory function of COPD patients, leading to acute respiratory failure [3, 27]. The impaired oxygenation and systemic inflammation resulting from both conditions may contribute to organ dysfunction and a higher likelihood of mortality in the immediate aftermath of the exacerbation [6, 28]. Additionally, the coexistence of AECOPD and pneumonia possibly heightens susceptibility to secondary infections, perpetuating a cycle of worsening health and escalating long-term mortality risk [5, 29, 30]. Furthermore, presence of pneumonia may signify more severe underlying COPD, adding an additional layer of complexity to the prognostic landscape [3, 6]. The intricate interplay of respiratory compromise, inflammatory responses, delayed recovery, and increased susceptibility to complications underscores the multifaceted nature of the risks associated with the concurrent pneumonia in AECOPD patients.

Our results showed comparable risks of readmission in AECOPD patients with and without pneumonia. We may speculate that this lack of difference may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, comparable readmission rates could be reflective of effective management strategies employed during the initial hospitalization: it is possible that both groups received comprehensive and comparable care, leading to similar post-discharge outcomes. Adherence to evidence-based guidelines, early initiation of appropriate therapies, and targeted interventions during hospitalization might have minimized the risk of subsequent exacerbations, irrespective of the presence of pneumonia. Additionally, similarity in readmission rates may suggest that while pneumonia contributes to the severity of the acute exacerbation, it might not independently predispose individuals to recurrent exacerbations in the post-hospitalization period. Factors influencing readmission, such as patient compliance with prescribed medications, engagement in pulmonary rehabilitation, and adherence to follow-up care, could play a crucial role in mitigating the potential exacerbation risk associated with pneumonia [31–33]. Furthermore, similar readmission rates in both groups of patients may be indicative of similar duration and intensity of post-discharge monitoring and support. Continuous follow-up, patient education, and proactive management of COPD symptoms after hospital discharge may emerge as essential elements in preventing exacerbations. If implemented consistently, these measures may attenuate the impact of pneumonia on readmission rates [33, 34].

There are some strengths of this current review that builds on the foundational work of Yu et al., which covered studies published up until 2019. By incorporating more recent studies, this review provides an up-to-date synthesis of the evidence, reflecting the latest research and clinical developments in the field. This probably allows for more current and relevant conclusions that may better inform clinical practice. A significant strength of this review is the use of the GRADE approach to systematically evaluate the certainty of evidence. The review also employed an extensive search strategy across multiple databases, ensuring broad coverage of relevant studies. This may have minimized the risk of missing important studies and reduce the likelihood of publication bias. Each included study was critically appraised using a standardized tool, allowing for the identification of potential biases and limitations. We also did a subgroup analysis based on the quality score obtained. The review carefully evaluated heterogeneity among the included studies, by doing subgroup analysis.

It is essential to acknowledge limitations of our study. Substantial heterogeneity was found for most of the outcomes, possibly due to the differences in baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the study subjects as well as differences in patients management practices across different studies. However, we have done subgroup analysis to understand possible cause(s) of this heterogeneity and observed that some factors such as the sample size, the type of associated pneumonia and method of establishing the diagnosis of COPD may contribute, to some extent, to the observed heterogeneity. Our post-hoc subgroup analyses were based on a small number of studies. In such cases, the test for interaction may not be appropriate and the likelihood of chance findings and spurious subgroup effects is also increased. To address this, we used the ICEMAN tool to assess the credibility of these subgroup findings, which were determined to be of ‘very low’ credibility. Future studies should pre-plan subgroup analyses before conducting the study or meta-analysis. All included studies were observational in nature and consequently, the quality of evidence is low. Additionally, some confounders may have been not accounted for in the analysis due to differences in patient characteristics and comorbidities. It is possible that some temporal changes in management practices for AECOPD and pneumonia over the study durations were not accounted for, and might have influenced our outcomes. The included studies confirmed COPD through spirometry or by reviewing medical records, which included previous diagnoses by respiratory specialists or documentation based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. Pneumonia was identified either through radiological confirmation or ICD coding. However, it is important to note the inherent difficulty in confirming pneumonia as a clinical diagnosis, which can affect the consistency of case identification across studies. Additionally, the studies lacked important stratifying factors, such as a pneumonia severity index or an APACHE score, which would have allowed for more nuanced comparisons between groups. Furthermore, the potential for COPD under-diagnosis and over-diagnosis is a concern, particularly in studies lacking pulmonary function test (PFT) data to validate the COPD diagnosis. This variation in diagnostic accuracy could influence the reported outcomes. Variation in clinical practice, including the use of corticosteroids and/or antibiotics, was also observed across the studies. This is particularly relevant in light of recent recommendations suggesting a potential mortality benefit of steroids in the treatment of community-acquired pneumonia [35, 36]. However, our subgroup analysis based on the use of corticosteroids or antibiotics among pneumonia and non-pneumonia groups did not reveal any indication that these medications influenced the outcomes. The lack of observed variation in outcomes may be due to the small number of studies contributing to this subgroup analysis. Nonetheless, this remains an important area for further investigation.

Conclusion

The meta-analysis suggests that pneumonia may have an adverse impact on the outcomes of AECOPD, although the evidence supporting these findings is of low certainty. We found indication of potential risks associated with pneumonia during acute exacerbation of COPD, including increased in-hospital, short-term, and long-term mortality. The findings suggest the need for tailored management strategies, including close monitoring, early intervention, and long-term follow-up, to improve the prognosis of AECOPD patients with concurrent pneumonia. However, the limitations in the evidence base prevent us from drawing strong conclusions and it would be useful to have further research with more robust study designs to confirm these associations and to better understand the mechanisms involved.
