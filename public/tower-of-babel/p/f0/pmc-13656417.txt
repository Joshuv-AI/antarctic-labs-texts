Genome-level insights into coordinated ethanol production and chaperone-driven stress tolerance in Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 isolated from petroleum refinery sludge
Authors: Janayita Biswa Sarma, Anurabh Chakravarty, Lingaraj Sahoo, Preetom Regon, Kunal Boro, Hemen Deka, Bhaben Tanti, Saurov Mahanta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPetroleum hydrocarbon contamination creates specialized ecological niches that present a source for mining microorganisms with exceptional metabolic flexibility and stress resilience. In this study, we performed a comprehensive genome-level analysis of Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1, isolated from petroleum refinery sludge, to elucidate the molecular coordination between ethanol production and stress tolerance, two important traits associated with microbial adaptation to ethanol-rich environments. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a 5.41-Mb circular genome with 35.3% GC content, comprising 5393 coding sequences and complete glycolytic and fermentative pathways. Functional annotation and KEGG pathway mapping identified genes associated with ethanol metabolism and protein quality-control systems involved in stress adaptation. Although several ethanol-related enzymes were detected, a complete canonical AdhE/PFOR ethanol fermentation pathway was not reconstructed. Quantitative gene expression analysis (qRT-PCR) under fermentative and ethanol-stress conditions showed significant upregulation of key fermentative genes (adhE, pdhA) and protein quality-control genes (groEL, groES), whereas the small heat-shock protein hsp20 was selectively downregulated. This transcriptional expression profile suggests differential involvement of chaperone systems during ethanol stress adaptation. Moderate ethanol exposure (5%) was associated with increased expression of groEL and selected metabolic genes, indicating a potential relationship between protein quality-control processes and ethanol-associated adaptation. The contrasting regulation of hsp20 and groES suggests differential transcriptional responses to ethanol exposure. However, these observations are based on targeted gene expression analyses and should be considered preliminary until validated through broader functional and systems-level investigations. Comparative analysis with supported possibilities of ethanologens underscores the metabolic versatility and robust stress-management capacity of strain GRJBSBT-1. Introduction

Petroleum refinery sludge is a complex environmental contaminant containing hydrocarbons, organic solvents, and other toxic compounds that exert strong selective pressure on resident microbial communities [1]. Such extreme conditions promote the evolution of microorganisms with remarkable metabolic versatility and stress tolerance, making these habitats valuable reservoirs of microbes with potential applications in bioremediation and renewable bioenergy production [2, 3]. Among these, Bacillus cereus has frequently been reported from petroleum-contaminated environments owing to its ability to withstand chemical toxicity, oxidative stress, and nutrient limitation [4]. Its environmental persistence is attributed to extensive metabolic flexibility and efficient stress response systems, characteristics that support both hydrocarbon degradation and biofuel-related applications [5, 6].

Ethanol is an important biofuel, but its accumulation imposes significant stress on microbial cells by disrupting membrane integrity, protein stability, and redox balance [7]. Therefore, microorganisms intended for bioethanol production must simultaneously sustain ethanol biosynthesis and tolerate increasing solvent concentrations [8]. Understanding the molecular mechanisms governing ethanol production and tolerance is consequently essential for improving microbial biofuel processes [9].

In B. cereus, ethanol metabolism is coordinated through a network of fermentative and stress-response pathways [10]. Alcohol dehydrogenases, including AdhE, AdhP, and PdhA, catalyze key redox reactions involved in ethanol production and utilization, thereby maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and facilitating ethanol detoxification [11]. Concurrently, protein quality control systems protect cells against ethanol-induced proteotoxic stress. Small heat shock proteins such as Hsp20 prevent protein aggregation, whereas the GroEL/GroES chaperonin complex promotes the refolding of damaged proteins [12–16]. These chaperones also provide cross-protection against heat and oxidative stresses, contributing significantly to cellular survival under adverse conditions [17–19].

The expression of these metabolic and stress-associated genes is regulated by global regulatory proteins, including the heat shock repressor HrcA and the alternative sigma factor σ^B [10, 13, 20, 21]. These regulators coordinate transcriptional responses to environmental and intracellular stress signals, enabling rapid adaptation to changing conditions [22, 23]. Comparative genomic studies further indicate substantial strain-specific variation within B. cereus, reflecting evolutionary adaptations that enhance environmental fitness and stress resilience [24, 25].

While gene expression studies have improved our understanding of ethanol tolerance, genome-scale analyses provide a more comprehensive view of microbial adaptation by linking genetic information with metabolic functions and regulatory networks [26]. Such approaches facilitate the identification of alternative metabolic routes, pathway bottlenecks, and stress-response mechanisms that collectively support ethanol production and tolerance [27, 28].

Despite growing interest in microbial biofuel production, ethanol-responsive metabolic and chaperone systems remain poorly characterized in environmentally adapted B. cereus strains isolated from hydrocarbon-contaminated habitats. In particular, genome-supported analyses of ethanol stress responses are limited. Therefore, the present study investigates Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1, isolated from petroleum refinery sludge, using an integrated approach involving whole-genome sequencing, functional annotation, metabolic pathway reconstruction, and expression profiling of key fermentative and stress-response genes. By comparing gene expression under fermentative and ethanol-stress conditions, we identify molecular mechanisms that support ethanol production and tolerance in this strain [29]. These findings provide insights into microbial adaptation in polluted environments and may facilitate the development of robust microbial platforms for sustainable biofuel production through the identification of novel metabolic pathways and regulatory networks [30, 31].

Materials and methods

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis

Bacterial strain and genomic DNA extraction

Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1 was isolated from petroleum refinery sludge using enrichment culture in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium supplemented with 5% (v/v) ethanol. Cultures were grown overnight in LB broth at 37 °C and 180 rpm, and cells were harvested by centrifugation (8000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C) [32]. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAGEN DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit following the Gram-positive bacteria protocol with lysozyme (20 mg mL−1, 30 min, 37 °C) and RNase A (10 mg mL−1, 10 min) treatments. DNA quality and concentration were assessed using a NanoDrop™ spectrophotometer, and integrity was confirmed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis [33].

Library preparation, sequencing, and read processing

Sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (2 × 151 bp paired-end reads) targeting > 100 × genome coverage [34]. Raw reads were quality-checked using FastQC [35] and trimmed with Trimmomatic [36] to remove adapters and low-quality bases (Phred score < 20). Quality-filtered reads were re-evaluated using FastQC before downstream analyses.

Genome assembly and annotation

Trimmed reads were assembled de novo using SPAdes [37]. Assembly quality was assessed using N50, genome size, and GC content. Contigs were subsequently corrected, scaffolded, and patched against the reference genome Bacillus cereus ATCC 14579 (GCA_046524075.1) using RagTag. Genome completeness and contamination were evaluated using BUSCO. Annotation was performed with Bakta, which identified coding sequences, rRNAs, tRNAs, and other genomic features [38].

Comparative genomic analysis

Metabolic pathways of GRJBSBT-1 were compared with those of B. cereus ATCC 14579, focusing on glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and ethanol metabolism. Pathway completeness was assessed using KEGG-based pathway reconstruction and manual verification of enzyme annotations identified in the genome of Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1 (Fig. 4). For each metabolic pathway, a set of core reference enzymes was compiled from KEGG annotations and standard descriptions of bacterial central carbon metabolism. Data availability

Raw sequencing data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under BioProject PRJNA1092116, BioSample SAMN40621395, and SRA accession SRR28464323.

Gene expression analysis

Growth conditions and experimental design

For ethanol production studies, B. cereus GRJBSBT-1 was cultured in LB broth (control) and Fermentation Medium-1 (FM1) containing glucose (20 g L−1), peptone (10 g L−1), yeast extract (5 g L−1), NH4Cl (4 g L−1), KH2PO4 (0.5 g L−1), and MgSO4·7H2O (0.5 g L−1). Cultures were incubated at 30 ± 2 °C and 180 rpm, and samples were collected at 6, 8, 10, and 12 h [32, 40–43]. For ethanol tolerance studies, cells were grown in LB broth containing 0, 5, or 8% (v/v) ethanol and incubated at 30 °C and 180 rpm. Samples were harvested after 12, 18, and 24 h [43, 44]. Three biological replicates were used for each treatment, and each qRT-PCR analysis was performed in technical triplicate.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was isolated using the QIAGEN RNeasy Mini Kit with bead-beating lysis. RNA concentration and purity were determined spectrophotometrically, and integrity was verified by agarose gel electrophoresis. Only RNA samples with A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were used [45]. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using a Thermo Fisher Scientific cDNA synthesis kit and stored at − 20 °C.

Primer design and reference gene selection

Primers for ethanol biosynthesis genes (adhE, adhP, pdhA) and stress-response genes (groES, groEL, hsp20) were designed using Primer3 and validated by BLAST analysis [46]. The housekeeping gene adk was selected as the reference gene based on previous reports of stable expression in the Bacillus cereus group and recommendations for RT-qPCR normalization [45, 47, 48] as because it encodes an essential enzyme involved in cellular energy homeostasis and is widely used as a conserved housekeeping marker within members of the Bacillus cereus group. In the present study, adk exhibited consistent Ct values across treatments, supporting its use for normalization.

Quantitative and semi-quantitative PCR

qRT-PCR was performed using a Rotor-Gene Q system with SYBR Green chemistry in 20 μL reaction volumes. Amplification conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C followed by 35–40 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension, with melt-curve analysis conducted to verify amplification specificity [46, 49, 50]. Negative, positive, and no-template controls were included.

Semi-quantitative PCR products were separated on 1% agarose gels containing ethidium bromide and visualized using a Vilber BioPrint gel documentation system.

Statistical analysis

Relative transcript levels were calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt method with adk as the reference gene. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three biological replicates. Statistical significance was evaluated using two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test after verification of normality and homogeneity of variance. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05. Fold-change values were used to describe expression magnitude, whereas statistical significance was determined exclusively by ANOVA results.

Results

Genome sequencing and assembly

Whole-genome sequencing of Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 revealed a total genome size of 5.41 Mb with a GC content of 35.3%, which is consistent with members of the B. cereus species complex [22, 51, 52]. The genome assembly resulted in a single circular chromosome with an N50 value of 5,416,526 bp, indicating a highly contiguous and high-quality assembly.

Genome annotation performed using Bakta (Galaxy version 1.9.4 + galaxy1) with default parameters identified a total of 5393 protein-coding sequences (CDSs). Of these, 3199 CDSs (59.32%) were assigned to functional categories based on the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) classification system, while the remaining 2194 CDSs (40.68%) lacked COG assignments, suggesting the presence of novel genes or genes with yet-uncharacterized functions [24, 53]. In addition, the genome harbored 108 tRNA genes, 39 rRNA genes, and 15 non-coding RNA elements (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Circular genome representation and functional distribution

A circular genome map was generated to illustrate the genomic organization of Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 (Fig. 1). The assembled genome comprised a single contig with a total length of 5,416,526 bp and a GC content of 35.3%. The outer rings represent coding sequences located on the forward and reverse strands, color-coded according to Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) functional categories.

Genes involved in metabolic functions including energy production and conversion (C), amino acid transport and metabolism (E), nucleotide transport and metabolism (F), carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G), coenzyme transport and metabolism (H), lipid transport and metabolism (I), inorganic ion transport and metabolism (P), and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, transport, and catabolism (Q) were widely distributed across the genome. In contrast, genes associated with information storage and processing, such as translation, ribosomal structure, and biogenesis (J), transcription (K), and replication, recombination, and repair (L), as well as those related to cellular processes and signaling namely cell cycle control and chromosome partitioning (D), cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (M), cell motility (N), post-translational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones (O), signal transduction mechanisms (T), intracellular trafficking, secretion, and vesicular transport (U), defense mechanisms (V), and extracellular structures (W) were more dispersed throughout the genome [54].

In addition, several hypothetical proteins and genes of unknown function (COG category S) were identified. The inner rings of the genome map illustrate GC content variation and GC skew, revealing distinct replication origin (oriC) and terminus regions. The observed GC skew pattern is characteristic of Bacillus genomes, with a clear polarity shift corresponding to the origin and terminus of replication. Structural RNA elements, including tRNA, rRNA, tmRNA, and other non-coding RNA regions, were distributed throughout the genome. Furthermore, the presence of clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeat (CRISPR) elements and associated repeat regions suggests the existence of adaptive immunity mechanisms against bacteriophage infections.

Genome completeness assessment

Genome completeness was evaluated using the Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) framework. The BUSCO analysis of the Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1 genome assembly identified 435 out of 450 orthologs (96.7%) as complete, of which 433 were present as single-copy genes and 2 were duplicated. No fragmented BUSCOs were detected, and only 15 orthologs (3.3%) were missing, indicating minimal gaps in the assembled genome.

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the high BUSCO completeness score indicates that the assembly is suitable for reliable functional and comparative genomic analyses, including the identification of ethanol-associated metabolic and stress-response genes.

Functional genome annotation

Functional annotation of the Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 genome using the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) database classified the predicted coding DNA sequences (CDSs) into 23 major functional categories. As shown in Fig. 3, the functional profile is characteristic of a soil-dwelling Gram-positive bacterium. The largest proportion of genes was assigned to amino acid transport and metabolism (5.3%), followed by transcription (4.8%), translation and ribosomal structure and biogenesis (4.6%), and general biogenesis-related functions (4.7%).

Other prominently represented categories included general function prediction only (4.3%), cell wall/membrane/envelope biogenesis (3.7%), and carbohydrate transport and metabolism (3.7%). Genes involved in energy production and conversion (3.1%), cell cycle control and chromosome partitioning (3.1%), and lipid transport and metabolism (2.8%) were also detected. In contrast, categories associated with extracellular structures and cytoskeleton organization were either absent or minimally represented (< 0.1%).

Overall, the functional composition of the GRJBSBT-1 genome highlights a strong enrichment of core metabolic processes and stress response systems, reflecting the metabolic versatility and adaptive capacity characteristic of B. cereus (Fig. 3).

Pathway mapping and metabolic reconstruction

Genome annotation and KEGG-based pathway reconstruction identified several genes associated with ethanol metabolism in Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1, including alcohol dehydrogenase-related enzymes and other redox-associated proteins. However, a complete canonical pyruvate-ferredoxin oxidoreductase (PFOR)-AdhE ethanol fermentation pathway was not reconstructed from the genome annotation data. Likewise, although genes associated with acetaldehyde and alcohol metabolism were detected, the pathway reconstruction did not support the presence of a fully characterized AdhE-dependent ethanol fermentation route.

An annotated adhE-like aldehyde/alcohol dehydrogenase homolog was identified in the genome and was therefore included in the transcriptional analysis. The presence of this homolog indicates potential involvement in aldehyde metabolism, redox balancing, or alternative fermentative processes. However, its detection alone does not confirm operation of a canonical AdhE/PFOR-dependent ethanol biosynthetic pathway. Consequently, expression data for adhE should be interpreted as transcriptional evidence for an annotated homolog rather than direct evidence of a functional AdhE-mediated ethanol fermentation route. Further biochemical and metabolic studies will be required to establish its precise physiological role.

Central metabolic pathway completeness

Comprehensive pathway analysis of B. cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 (Fig. 4) suggested partial completeness of the major central metabolic pathways. Based on Eq. 1, glycolysis exhibited the highest level of representation, with 9 of 10 reference enzymes identified (90.0%), indicating that most enzymatic steps required for glucose catabolism to pyruvate are encoded in the genome. In contrast, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) was only partially represented, with 3 of 6 enzymes detected (50.0%), suggesting incomplete genomic representation of NADPH-generating and pentose-interconversion reactions. The tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle showed moderate representation, with 5 of 8 reference enzymes identified (62.5%), indicating the presence of several oxidative metabolic functions but the absence of certain canonical TCA cycle components. Ethanol-associated metabolism exhibited a completeness score of 50.0% (3 of 6 enzymes), reflecting the presence of selected enzymes involved in pyruvate-to-ethanol conversion while lacking several components of canonical ethanol fermentation routes [39, 55] (Fig. 4).

Stress response genes

Genome annotation of B. cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 revealed a diverse repertoire of genes associated with ethanol stress response and cellular proteostasis. The major chaperone system GroEL–GroES was identified, underscoring its role in ATP-dependent refolding of denatured proteins under solvent-induced stress. The presence of the small heat shock protein Hsp20 further suggests a rapid protective mechanism for stabilizing partially unfolded proteins and preventing protein aggregation. In addition, components of the DnaK–DnaJ–GrpE chaperone system were annotated, indicating a robust protein quality control network.

Several regulatory genes involved in stress adaptation were also detected, including hrcA, ctsR, rex, and the global stress regulator sigB, along with its regulatory cascade (rsbU, rsbV, and rsbW). Carbon flux regulation was mediated by ccpA and abrB, while redox balance and thiol homeostasis were governed by rex and spx, respectively.

Moreover, multiple members of the alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) family were annotated, including Zn-dependent, Fe-dependent, and short-chain dehydrogenase/reductase (SDR)-type enzymes. The presence of these ADH isoforms provides metabolic flexibility and contributes to ethanol biosynthesis through efficient reduction of acetaldehyde. In addition, genes encoding lactate dehydrogenase (ldh), acetate kinase (ackA), phosphotransacetylase (pta), aldehyde dehydrogenase (aldh), and butanediol dehydrogenase (bdh) were identified, highlighting the existence of alternative fermentative pathways that may compete with ethanol formation. The coexistence of ethanol-producing and competing metabolic routes suggests a finely regulated metabolic balance, with ADH enzymes playing a contributing role in sustaining ethanol flux under stress conditions.

Primer design and validation for qRT-PCR

Gene-specific primers were designed for the amplification of key ethanol biosynthesis genes (adhE, adhP, and pdhA) and ethanol tolerance-related genes (hsp20, groES, and groEL), along with the housekeeping gene adk (adenylate kinase) for normalization. Primer design was based on annotated coding sequences derived from the draft genome of Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 using Primer3 software. The design criteria included a primer length of approximately 20 bp, melting temperatures (Tm) between 55 and 60 °C, GC content ranging from 45 to 55%, and avoidance of secondary structures such as hairpins and primer dimers.

The designed primers were experimentally validated to ensure specificity and efficiency. Agarose gel electrophoresis of PCR amplicons produced single bands corresponding to the expected product sizes for each primer pair, confirming target specificity. Melt curve analysis further verified the absence of non-specific amplification and primer–dimer formation. Amplification efficiencies were calculated using standard curves generated from serial cDNA dilutions, with efficiencies ranging between 95 and 105%, which is within the acceptable range for quantitative real-time PCR.

The validated primer sets (Table 2) were subsequently employed for qRT-PCR analyses to quantify the relative expression levels of ethanol production and ethanol tolerance genes under ethanol stress conditions.

Relative expression of alcohol metabolism genes under different growth media

The expression profiles of three alcohol metabolism–associated genes (adhE, adhP, and pdhA) were examined in B. cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 cultured in Luria–Bertani (LB) medium and Fermentation Medium (FM). Transcript levels were quantified at multiple time points and visualized using bar plots and heatmaps to depict fold-change variation relative to control conditions.

The annotated adhE-like homolog exhibited consistently higher expression in LB medium across most sampling points, whereas expression remained comparatively lower in FM. Because a complete canonical AdhE/PFOR ethanol fermentation pathway was not identified during pathway reconstruction, these expression patterns should not be interpreted as confirmation of AdhE-mediated ethanol production. Instead, the observed transcriptional activity indicates that the annotated adhE-like homolog is responsive to growth conditions and may participate in cellular redox metabolism, aldehyde detoxification, or alternative metabolic functions. The specific biochemical role of this gene in strain GRJBSBT-1 remains to be experimentally validated [39].

In contrast to adhE, the adhP gene showed strong induction in FM at several time points, reflected by high fold-change values in the heatmap. Expression in LB medium remained comparatively moderate to low. These results suggest that adhP may contribute to ethanol tolerance and redox homeostasis during fermentative growth. Previous studies in Escherichia coli, Clostridium thermocellum, and Bacillus spp. have suggested that adhP contributes to maintaining intracellular NADH/NAD⁺ balance and enhances survival under ethanol stress [56, 57]. The marked upregulation of adhP in FM observed here corroborates its role as a fermentation-associated adaptive gene.

Expression analysis of pdhA, encoding the E1 component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex, revealed a condition-dependent transcriptional pattern. PdhA expression was consistently higher in LB medium than in FM across all replicates, suggesting enhanced PDH activity during nutrient-rich growth. Elevated pdhA expression in LB may support increased acetyl-CoA production for energy generation, biomass accumulation, and stress mitigation. Although pdhA expression was reduced in FM, its detectable induction is consistent with reports indicating that PDH contributes to carbon flux rerouting under oxygen-limited or stress conditions, supporting fermentative metabolism and ethanol biosynthesis [58, 59].

Relative expression of ethanol stress-responsive genes

The transcriptional responses of three ethanol stress–responsive genes (hsp20, groES, and groEL) were analyzed in Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 cultured in LB medium supplemented with 0% (control), 5%, and 8% (v/v) ethanol. Gene expression was monitored at multiple time points to capture temporal dynamics and visualized using heatmaps to illustrate fold-change variation.

The small heat shock protein hsp20, which functions as a molecular chaperone preventing protein aggregation, exhibited relatively high baseline expression under control conditions. However, hsp20 expression was downregulated at both 5% and 8% ethanol concentrations. This pattern suggests that Hsp20 may not be specifically induced by ethanol stress but instead contributes to general proteostasis during normal growth or mild stress conditions. The repression of hsp20 under ethanol exposure implies a shift toward alternative chaperone systems better suited to counteract ethanol-induced protein denaturation [30]. Similar repression patterns have been reported in Clostridium acetobutylicum and Bacillus subtilis under solvent stress.

The groES gene, encoding the co-chaperonin that functions in conjunction with GroEL, was consistently downregulated under ethanol stress, particularly at 8% ethanol. This suggests a limited or context-dependent role for groES in ethanol tolerance. One possible explanation is that groEL may exert protective effects independently under solvent stress, thereby reducing reliance on GroES. Alternatively, ethanol exposure may disrupt groES regulation or stability. Similar decoupled regulation of groES and groEL has been reported in Geobacillus thermoglucosidasius under solvent stress conditions [28].

Among the analyzed chaperone genes, groEL showed the most consistent ethanol-responsive transcriptional pattern, exhibiting moderate but statistically significant induction at 5% ethanol. At 5% ethanol, groEL expression increased significantly (>1.3-fold), suggesting a possible contribution in counteracting ethanol-induced protein misfolding. At 8% ethanol, however, groEL expression declined likely reflecting excessive cellular damage and reduced transcriptional capacity under severe stress. The induction of groEL at moderate ethanol concentrations is consistent with a potential role in maintaining protein homeostasis during ethanol exposure. However, the present transcriptional data alone do not establish a direct mechanistic role for GroEL in ethanol tolerance and therefore should be interpreted cautiously. This expression pattern is consistent with reports in Clostridium beijerinckii and Bacillus subtilis, where enhanced GroEL expression is associated with moderately increased ethanol tolerance and improved fermentation performance [11].

Integrated interpretation of ethanol metabolism and stress responses

Collectively, the expression profiles indicate coordinated regulation of ethanol metabolism and stress tolerance mechanisms in B. cereus GRJBSBT-1. The annotated adhE-like homolog was preferentially expressed under nutrient-rich conditions. Given the absence of a complete canonical AdhE/PFOR pathway, this transcriptional response is interpreted as evidence of condition-dependent regulation of an annotated homolog rather than direct evidence of AdhE-mediated ethanol production [39]. In contrast, adhP displayed strong induction under fermentative conditions, highlighting its importance in maintaining redox balance during ethanol stress [60]. The differential expression of pdhA indicates flexible carbon flux distribution among glycolysis, fermentation, and the TCA cycle.

On the stress tolerance front, chaperone genes exhibited distinct expression patterns. While hsp20 and groES were suppressed under ethanol exposure, groEL was selectively induced at moderate ethanol concentrations and displayed the strongest transcriptional response among the chaperone genes examined. This observation suggests a possible involvement of GroEL in ethanol-associated stress adaptation; however, confirmation of its functional significance will require additional experimental validation. The differential expression patterns of the analyzed chaperone genes indicate that multiple components of the cellular proteostasis network may respond differently to ethanol exposure. Nevertheless, the present data do not allow definitive conclusions regarding the relative functional contributions of these chaperones.

Overall, the coordinated transcriptional responses of ethanol-associated metabolic genes (adhE, adhP, pdhA) and selected chaperone genes suggest an association between metabolic adaptation and stress-response processes in B. cereus GRJBSBT-1. However, these findings are correlative in nature and should not be interpreted as evidence of a defined mechanistic tolerance strategy. These features enhance the strain’s potential applicability in bioethanol fermentation processes (Figs. 5, 6).

Discussion

The assembled genome of Bacillus cereus strain GRJBSBT-1 falls within the typical size range of the B. cereus sensu lato (Bcsl) group (~5.3 to 6.3 Mb) with an average GC content of ~ 35%, consistent with high-quality reference genomes such as ATCC14579 and diverse food, environmental, and clinical isolates [61]. This indicates that GRJBSBT-1 is genomically representative of the B. cereus species complex, without evidence of unusual genome expansion or contraction relative to well-characterized lineages [62]. Recent comparative genomic studies highlight the large, open pan-genome of the B. cereus group, reflecting extensive ecological versatility. The functional annotation of GRJBSBT-1 aligns with this paradigm, comprising conserved core housekeeping genes alongside variable accessory traits that facilitate niche adaptation [63, 64]. Population-level analyses of ATCC14579 and related strains similarly emphasize a conserved genomic backbone supporting translation, transcription, and envelope biogenesis, coupled with variable adaptive modules, in agreement with our findings [65, 66].

The circular genome map of B. cereus GRJBSBT-1 (Fig. 1) underscores genomic features linked to metabolic versatility and stress adaptability. The predominance of genes associated with energy production and conversion (COG category C) and carbohydrate transport and metabolism (G) supports its fermentative potential, including ethanol production. The presence of multiple alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) family members further reinforces its ethanol biosynthetic capacity [67]. Genes related to defense mechanisms (V) and post-translational modification, protein turnover, and chaperones (O) likely contribute to ethanol and general stress tolerance, consistent with reports demonstrating the role of heat shock proteins and chaperonins in stabilizing enzymes under solvent stress. The abundance of regulatory genes, including alternative sigma factors such as σ^B, indicates a sophisticated stress-response regulatory network, which is characteristic of ethanol-tolerant Bacillus species [68, 69].

Functional category analysis further revealed a high representation of amino acid metabolism genes, indicating efficient nitrogen assimilation and protein turnover, which facilitate adaptation to diverse substrates. The enrichment of transcription- and translation-related genes reflects active regulatory control and robust protein synthesis capacity, critical for survival under ethanol-induced stress. Genes involved in cell wall and membrane biogenesis were also well represented, supporting their role in maintaining cellular integrity during solvent exposure [10, 70]. In contrast, underrepresented categories such as secondary metabolism, motility, and extracellular structures suggest that GRJBSBT-1 prioritizes metabolic robustness and stress adaptation over specialized ecological functions.

BUSCO analysis suggested a high genome completeness score (96.7%), indicating the reliability of the assembly for downstream functional interpretation. The near-complete recovery of conserved single-copy orthologs, with minimal duplication and no fragmentation, reduces annotation bias and ensures accurate representation of metabolic and stress-related gene families [71]. Comparable completeness levels have been reported for other B. cereus genomes, which typically exceed 95% BUSCO recovery [72]. This high-quality assembly enabled confident identification of ethanol metabolism genes (adhE, adhP, and pdhA) and stress tolerance determinants (hsp20, groES, and groEL). Integration of genomic and transcriptional data suggests the coexistence of ethanol-associated metabolic functions and stress-responsive traits: GRJBSBT-1 not only harbors the genetic repertoire for ethanol production but also exhibits inducible tolerance mechanisms that sustain metabolic activity under ethanol stress. Such coupling of production and tolerance is essential for industrial bioethanol processes, where microbial productivity is often limited by solvent toxicity [73, 74].

Genome analysis identified several genes associated with ethanol-related metabolism, including alcohol dehydrogenase family members and other enzymes involved in pyruvate and acetaldehyde metabolism [75]. However, KEGG pathway reconstruction did not support the presence of a complete canonical PFOR-AdhE ethanol fermentation pathway [39]. The detection of an annotated adhE-like homolog together with its transcriptional activity suggests potential participation in fermentative metabolism or intracellular redox balancing, but does not establish operation of a classical AdhE-dependent ethanol biosynthetic route. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as genome-supported evidence of ethanol-associated metabolic potential rather than confirmation of a fully functional canonical ethanol fermentation pathway [76, 77].

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the pathway reconstruction revealed a largely represented glycolytic network converging on pyruvate, whereas the PPP, TCA cycle, and ethanol-associated pathways were only partially reconstructed. These completeness values were used solely as descriptive indicators of genomic pathway representation and should not be interpreted as evidence of pathway activity, metabolic flux, enzyme expression, or physiological functionality under the experimental conditions examined.

The studies by Tanti et al. [78–81] suggested that variation in hydrocarbon degradation and fermentation-associated traits among Pseudomonas strains arises from differential regulation of genes related to catabolic metabolism, membrane transport, and stress tolerance. Molecular-level differentiation points to strain-specific regulatory mechanisms that influence the expression of hydrocarbon-degrading enzymes, facilitating adaptation and metabolic activity in petroleum-contaminated environments. Stress-response analysis indicates that components of the GroEL/GroES chaperonin system may participate in adaptation to ethanol exposure in GRJBSBT-1. The observed transcriptional responses of groEL, groES, and hsp20 suggest differential regulation of protein quality-control pathways during ethanol stress. However, because the present study relies primarily on targeted qRT-PCR analyses, these observations should be considered associative rather than mechanistic. Additional approaches such as transcriptomics, proteomics, stress-survival assays, and functional genetic studies would be required to determine the specific contributions of these chaperones to ethanol tolerance. This hierarchy contrasts with yeast systems, where membrane remodeling and a broad HSP network (Hsp104, Hsp70, Hsp90) play major roles in ethanol tolerance [28]. The patterns observed in groEL, groES, and selected metabolic genes may provide useful targets for future strain-improvement studies aimed at understanding ethanol-associated stress adaptation. However, experimental validation will be required to determine whether modulation of these pathways influences cellular performance under ethanol stress. Engineering strategies for GRJBSBT-1 may therefore benefit from prioritizing GroEL/GroES-centered designs and redox or medium optimization to enhance PDH and ADH performance, rather than generic sHSP overexpression [82].

The coexistence of multiple ADH families and a strong GroEL/GroES axis provides a genetic basis for the observed gene-specific expression patterns, including time-dependent adhE regulation, medium-responsive pdhA expression, and context-dependent adhP induction. AdhP, reported as a broad-specificity alcohol dehydrogenase in E. coli and Bacillus, appears to support redox balance under variable growth conditions rather than serving as the primary ethanol-synthesizing enzyme [83]. In facultative anaerobes, PDH complements pyruvate formate-lyase depending on oxygen availability, acting as a substrate-supply hub for downstream ethanol formation [84, 85].

The observed transcriptional responses differ from those commonly reported in classical ethanologens. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, ethanol production relies on PDC and multiple ADHs with specialized redox roles [86], whereas Z. mobilis employs a minimal ED–Pdc–Adh system that yields exceptionally high ethanol productivity [30, 87]. Although bacterial systems such as Bacillus often exhibit lower ethanol yields due to competing pathways, they offer superior substrate versatility, stress robustness, and adaptability [88]. Recent studies suggest the importance of AdhE in bacterial fermentative metabolism, demonstrating that its structural organization and enzymatic regulation can influence metabolic flux and redox-associated processes during fermentation [39].

The observed transcriptional responses are consistent with the broader involvement of protein quality-control systems in microbial adaptation to ethanol stress. However, the present data do not establish a conserved or unique chaperone-centered tolerance mechanism in GRJBSBT-1. Ethanol toxicity primarily disrupts protein structure and membrane integrity, leading to misfolding and aggregation. Chaperones such as GroEL/GroES play a critical role in refolding denatured proteins and preserving enzymatic function during fermentation. Similar responses have been documented in S. cerevisiae, Z. mobilis, and solventogenic Clostridium species, where induction of chaperone systems has been associated with cellular adaptation to ethanol and solvent stress [30, 89–91].

Overall, the integration of genome annotation and qRT-PCR expression analysis provides a comprehensive understanding of ethanol metabolism and tolerance in B. cereus GRJBSBT-1. The concurrent transcriptional responses of metabolic and chaperone-associated genes suggest potential coordination between metabolic and stress-response pathways during ethanol exposure. Nevertheless, the current evidence is based on expression profiling of a limited gene set and does not establish causal regulatory relationships. The observed transcriptional variation of the annotated adhE-like homolog is consistent with the established involvement of aldehyde dehydrogenases in fermentative metabolism and cellular redox processes. However, the present data are descriptive and do not establish a specific mechanistic role for this gene in ethanol adaptation in Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1. This intrinsic linkage between production and tolerance, combined with the strain’s metabolic flexibility and stress resilience, suggest potential relevance of the strain for future biofuel-associated research. Biosafety and industrial viability considerations

Although Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1 demonstrated ethanol-associated metabolic and stress-response characteristics, biosafety considerations remain important when evaluating its potential biotechnological applications. Genome annotation identified several virulence-associated genes commonly reported within members of the B. cereus sensu lato group (Supplementary Table S1), including the non-hemolytic enterotoxin operon (nheABC), the hemolysin BL operon (hblABCD), cytotoxin K (cytK), and immune inhibitor metalloproteases (inhA1 and inhA2). These genes have previously been associated with enterotoxicity, cytotoxicity, host interaction, and pathogenicity-related functions in members of the B. cereus group [92–94]. In contrast, genes associated with cereulide (ces) biosynthesis were not detected in the genome. The absence of the ces operon suggests a lack of emetic toxin production potential; however, the presence of these virulence-associated loci was determined solely through genome annotation and does not constitute evidence of toxin production, secretion, or pathogenicity under the conditions examined in this study. Nevertheless, the detection of enterotoxin- and virulence-related genes indicates that comprehensive biosafety evaluation, including phenotypic toxin characterization and risk assessment, would be required before considering any direct industrial application of strain GRJBSBT-1 [48, 92, 93].

Therefore, the findings of the present study should primarily be viewed in the context of understanding ethanol-associated metabolic adaptation and stress tolerance mechanisms in environmentally derived B. cereus. The identified ethanol-responsive genes and stress-associated pathways may also provide useful targets for future functional studies and metabolic engineering approaches. In particular, characterization of these adaptive mechanisms could facilitate the transfer or reconstruction of beneficial tolerance-associated traits in industrially established and Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) microbial hosts, thereby supporting the development of safer biofuel production platforms. Recent advances in synthetic biology and microbial cell factory engineering have highlighted the value of mining stress-tolerant environmental isolates for genetic determinants that can be incorporated into industrial microorganisms while maintaining biosafety standards [95, 96]. Consequently, GRJBSBT-1 may serve as a valuable model for elucidating ethanol tolerance mechanisms and identifying candidate genes for future engineering of robust and safe biofuel-producing strains.

A limitation of the present study is the reliance on a single reference gene (adk) for qRT-PCR normalization without independent validation of its expression stability under ethanol stress conditions (Supplementary Table S2). In addition, the transcriptional analyses were restricted to a targeted set of genes. Future studies incorporating validated reference gene panels, transcriptomic approaches, proteomic analyses, and functional assays would provide a more comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying ethanol-associated adaptation in B. cereus GRJBSBT-1.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated genomic perspective on ethanol-associated adaptation in Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1, an environmental isolate recovered from petroleum refinery sludge. The findings demonstrate that the strain possesses genes associated with central carbon metabolism, alcohol-related metabolic functions, redox homeostasis, and stress-responsive chaperone systems, highlighting its capacity to respond to solvent-induced stress. Differential expression of selected metabolic and proteostasis-associated genes under ethanol exposure further suggests that adaptation to ethanol stress involves coordinated transcriptional adjustments affecting both cellular metabolism and protein quality-control processes.

Rather than indicating a unique or fully defined tolerance mechanism, the results support the concept that ethanol adaptation in B. cereus GRJBSBT-1 is a multifactorial process involving the interaction of metabolic flexibility, stress-responsive regulation, and maintenance of cellular homeostasis. Although direct ethanol production, pathway activity, and the specific functional roles of the identified genes were not experimentally verified in the present study, the combined genomic and transcriptional analyses provide preliminary insights into ethanol-associated metabolic and stress-response features in Bacillus cereus GRJBSBT-1. Overall, this study contributes to the understanding of ethanol-associated adaptation in environmentally derived Bacillus cereus strains and establishes a foundation for future research aimed at elucidating the molecular basis of microbial stress tolerance and metabolic adaptation.

While the transcriptional patterns observed in this study suggest possible roles for protein quality-control systems in ethanol-associated adaptation, the findings remain primarily correlative. Therefore, the proposed involvement of chaperone-mediated proteostasis should be regarded as a working hypothesis requiring validation through comprehensive systems-level and functional investigations, including transcriptomic, proteomic, physiological, and genetic analyses.
