Performance evaluation of strengthened concrete beams: flexural and shear enhancement using chopped strand and woven roving mats
Authors: S. Govindarajan, V. Vandhana Devi, Xu Yong, Choon Kit Chan, C Ahamed Saleel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Concrete, the most widely used construction material, is inherently strong in compression but deficient in tension. This study investigates the effectiveness of externally bonded glass fibre composites namely chopped strand mats (CSM), woven roving mats (WRM), and combined wrapping in a U-shaped wrapping scheme to enhance the flexural and shear performance of reinforced concrete (RC) beams. For specimen preparation, Ordinary Portland Cement (53 grade), natural river sand, granite coarse aggregate, and high-strength cold-twisted reinforcement bars were employed, while the fibre sheets were bonded using epoxy resin. A total of eight beams were cast and grouped into flexural and shear categories, each consisting of a control specimen and beams strengthened with single-layer CSM, WRM, and hybrid CSM + WRM wrapping. Experimental evaluation was carried out under two-point loading for flexural beams and near-point loading for shear beams, focusing on load–deflection response, moment–curvature behaviour, stiffness degradation, and crack propagation. The results demonstrate that fibre wrapping significantly delayed crack initiation and improved energy absorption capacity. Flexural beams exhibited ultimate load enhancements from 18.6 kN for the control (FBO) to 23.6 kN (+ 21.18%), 27.2 kN (+ 31.62%), and 31.84 kN (+ 41.58%) for CSM, WRM, and CSM + WRM strengthened beams, respectively. Similarly, shear beams improved from 18.6 kN for the control (SBO) to 24.0 kN (+ 22.5%), 25.2 kN (+ 26.19%), and 28.32 kN (+ 34.32%) for the corresponding strengthening configurations. The findings confirm that U-shaped hybrid wrapping with CSM and WRM provides superior strengthening efficiency, offering substantial improvements in both flexural and shear capacities compared with single-layer applications.

Introduction

Reinforced concrete (RC) is widely used in construction but suffers from brittle failure modes and limited ductility under flexural and shear loading. To enhance its performance, researchers have explored fiber-reinforced polymer (FRP) composites for external confinement and strengthening. In this study, the effectiveness of chopped strand mats (CSM) and woven roving mats (WRM) of glass fibers, used separately and in combination, is investigated. These fibers are applied in a U-shaped configuration around RC beams to assess improvements in ultimate load capacity, stiffness, and failure behavior. The study aims to evaluate how single-layer and combined CSM + WRM sheets influence flexural and shear performance compared to control specimens. The behavior of beams in terms of load–deflection, moment–curvature response, fracture initiation, and propagation is analyzed. Fibers are kNown to enhance the ductility, energy absorption, and fracture control capacity of fiber-reinforced concrete1. Although FRP-refitted precracked beams show increased load capacity, they also exhibit unique failure modes such as shear cracking and debonding2. Adhesion, shear transmission, and interfacial properties play a key role in the bond between FRP laminates and concrete3. Triaxially braided ductile FRP textiles significantly increase flexural strength and deformation capacity4. However, externally bonded FRP plates may debond under high stresses5, with midspan debonding being a critical failure mode that reduces service performance6. Epoxy-bonded FRP composites effectively improve shear resistance7, Tara Sen conducted a comparative analysis of the Reinforced concrete beam sttrengthen in flexure using natural fibers (jute) and synthetic fibers (glass and carbon)8. Glass fibers specifically have gained attention for their role in enhancing compressive, tensile, and flexural strength9–11. Glass fiber reinforced concrete (GFRC) offers reduced brittleness, improved crack resistance, and enhanced durability. However, durability concerns remain under harsh conditions, as fiber deterioration and matrix cracking may occur12. Despite the benefits, GFRP rebars are linearly elastic and require careful design13. Similarly, GFRP plates improve flexural strength but risk collapse from debonding14. Confining GFRC with GFRP composites increases ductility and compressive strength15. Buildings and bridges made of reinforced concrete (RC) frequently require strengthening and renovation for a number of reasons. These include environmental degradation, exposure to high temperatures, fire, modifications in original use that result in higher loads, and the requirement for structural renovations to meet new design standards and regulations16–20.

When a civil structure collapses, it means that its ability to support loads has been diminished, compromising its structural integrity. Localized failures in a well-designed system shouldn’t cause the entire structure to collapse suddenly or gradually under any stress conditions. A structural component’s degradation is influenced by a number of elements. Structural weakness is caused by a variety of factors, including increased loads, design and construction flaws, and natural aging. Although experimental techniques can evaluate how these weaker structures behave under simulated loads, numerical models offer important insights into their behavior and make parametric research more affordable21–23.

Since RC beams are essential to the stability and saftey of many different types of structures, their structural performance is a basic component of civil engineering. As the load increases, aging RC beams may show signs of shear or flexural failure. Diagonal shear crakcs that are notably broader than flexural cracks are indicative of shear failure, which results in an abrupt and unanticipated collapse of a beam. Rapid loss of structural integrity results from this type of failure, which occurs when the shear force exceeds the beam shear resistance24–26. Unlike flexural failures, which often show warning signs through progressive cracking, shear failures happen rapidly and can significantly affect the overall stability of the structure. The failure mechanism and the necessity of sufficient shear reinforcement in such beams are revealed by the diagonal shear fractures27. External strengthening is a suitable method to improve the shear strength of existing RC beams, which may show shortcomings in their shear reinforcement due to one or more of the previously described reasons28.

Carbon fiber-reinforced polymer (CFRP) sheets and plates29, basalt fiber-reinforced polymer (BFRP) grid30, jute fiber-reinforced polymers (JFRP)31, U-shaped polyethylene terephthalate (PET) FRP32, glass fiber textile mesh33, strain-hardening cementitious composite34, prestressed near-surface mounted bars35, and other structural materials have been used in the past to strengthen RC beams in shear. Furthermore, textile fiber-reinforced composite cement-based mortar has become widely accepted as a successful technique for increasing the shear strength of structural parts made of reinforced concrete in recent years. The performance of these composite materials has been the subject of numerous investigations in the literature, with a focus on the kind of fibers used and the specific cementitious matrix. Mineral-based composites (MBC)36, textile-reinforced concrete (TRC)37–39, and textile-reinforced mortar (TRM)40,41 are important examples.

Fiber-reinforced cementitious matrix (FRCM)42–45 and fiber-reinforced concrete (FRC)46,47 illustrate variety of applications, each of these materials exhibits distinct qualities and performance traits that support increased resilience and structural integrity. A possible substitute for conventional reinforcement techniques, the incorporation of textile fibers enhances the mechanical performance of RC beams while also providing benefits like less weight and increased durability.

Few studies in the literature, as far as the authors are aware, begin to employ an external strengthening strategy that combines fibers with certain cementitious matrices as a bonding agent between the fiber sheet or plate and the concrete surface. Furthermore, most previous studies used externally bonded vertical jackets to strengthen RC beams under shear48–50. There is a dearth of experimental research on the impact of fiber layer inclination on strengthening orientations in RC beams51,52. This field remains relatively understudied in comparison to more conventional methods. Novel fiber-reinforced cementitious composites, like strain-hardening cementitious composites (SHCC) and engineered cementitious composites (ECC), are currently receiving a lot of interest53,54.

The highest compressive strength (49–51 MPa) and optimum durability against acid, sulfate, and water absorption have been proven by M40 concrete adding 1% glass fiber55. Due to better fiber–matrix bonding and stress distribution, alkali-treated kenaf fiber epoxy composites containing 3% TiO₂, MgO, and ZnO nanofillers showed notable increases in tensile, flexural, and impact strength56. Combining palm kernel, periwinkle, and coconut shells as 25% coarse aggregate replacements in concrete achieved adequate strength while enabling sustainable waste recycling and conservation of the environment57.

The literature study has been comprehensively expanded to include recent studies on advanced hybrid configurations, alternative materials, and sustainability. Specifically, investigations have been undertaken utilizing aluminum box sections, high-strength steel-reinforced NSM-SHCC composites, FRCM jackets, optimal FRP post-tensioning methods, and ECC-enhanced dowels for the transmission of pavement loads. These selections were made as they closely reflect the hybrid concept explored in this study and signify the increasing global demand for environmentally sustainable reinforcement solutions. The investigations are encapsulated in a new subsection, detailing their impact on the current research strategy and interpretation of results.

Materials and mix design

Ordinary Portland Cement (OPC), 53 grade, conforming to IS 12269:20139, was used. River sand was adopted as fine aggregate, while crushed rock of 10 mm and 20 mm sizes, conforming to IS 383:1970, served as coarse aggregate. The fineness modulus and specific gravity of aggregates were determined as per IS 2386 (Part I):1963 and IS 2386 (Part III):1963, respectively. The mix was proportioned for M20 grade concrete following IS 10262:2019. A target mean strength higher than the characteristic compressive strength was adopted to ensure quality and performance. The mix comprised 405.64 kg/m3 of cement, 535.72 kg/m3 of fine aggregate, 1356.47 kg/m3 of coarse aggregate, and 192.54 kg/m3 of water, corresponding to a water–cement ratio of 0.47, which is within the permissible limit of 0.55 for durability. The resulting mix proportion by weight was 1: 1.32: 3.34 (cement: fine aggregate: coarse aggregate). This mix ensures adequate workability, strength development, and durability for structural applications. The relatively higher cement content facilitates proper binding, while the optimized aggregate ratios promote a dense matrix with reduced voids, thereby improving overall structural performance.

Figure 5a shows the Moment–Curvature behavior of the control beam. Initially, the uncracked section exhibits linear elastic response with both concrete and steel resisting stresses. At the first crack load (≈8 kN), the curve deviates as stiffness reduces, the neutral axis shifts upward, and steel carries most tensile stresses. After cracking, moment continues to rise but with reduced stiffness, while curvature increases rapidly due to crack propagation. The beam maintains stable flexural behavior through stress redistribution between steel and concrete. At a load of 16–18 kN, the tensile reinforcement yielded, causing a sharp increase in curvature with only a small rise in moment. This stage was marked by large deflections and widening cracks. At the ultimate stage, curvature increased significantly without a notable gain in moment, indicating plastic hinge formation. Failure occurred by crushing of concrete in the compression zone after steel yielding, demonstrating a desirable ductile flexural behavior in RC design.Fig. 5Control beam (flexure) (a) moment vs. curvature, (b) load vs. stiffness, (c) load vs. deflection and (d) strain vs. shear stress.

Control beam (flexure) (a) moment vs. curvature, (b) load vs. stiffness, (c) load vs. deflection and (d) strain vs. shear stress.

Figure 5b presents the load–stiffness and moment–twist response of the control beam. The initial uncracked stage exhibits a linear elastic response with high stiffness. At the first crack load (~ 8 kN), tensile failure of concrete initiates stiffness reduction as stresses are transferred to the reinforcement. In the cracked-elastic stage, stiffness decreases, curvature increases, and cracks widen. Approaching yield, nonlinear strains develop in concrete while the steel yields, resulting in large deflections. At the ultimate load (~ 18–19 kN), plastic hinge formation accompanied by concrete crushing and steel yielding leads to ductile flexural failure.

Figure 5c shows the load–deflection behavior of the control beam. The beam initially responds linearly in the uncracked elastic stage. First cracking occurs at ~ 8 kN, reducing stiffness and increasing deflections. With further loading, cracks propagate and widen, producing nonlinear deflections. Near the ultimate load (~ 19 kN), stiffness decreases sharply, and failure occurs due to crushing of concrete and yielding of reinforcement. The response highlights the three characteristic stages of RC flexural behavior: uncracked elastic, cracked elastic, and ultimate failure.

Figure 5d illustrates the shear stress–strain behavior of the control beam. The response is initially linear until the onset of cracking at ~ 8 kN. Thereafter, stiffness reduces as tensile stresses are transferred to steel. With increasing strain, shear stress rises nonlinearly owing to crack widening and redistribution of stresses. Close to the ultimate load (~ 19 kN), stiffness drops markedly, and failure occurs by concrete crushing and steel yielding, again confirming the three distinct stages of RC flexural response.

Conclusion

Flexure beam

The experimental study on flexure beams indicates that exterior U-shaped wrapping with Chopped Strand Mat (CSM), Woven Roving Mat (WRM), and their combination substantially enhances the load-bearing capability of the controller beam. The control flexure beam (FBO) broke at 18.6 kN, but the CSM wrapping (FBC1) raised the ultimate load to 23.6 kN, which is a 21.18% increase. With 27.2 kN, WRM wrapping (FBW1) performed better than the rest, which is a 31.62% improvement. The FBCW1 wrapping, which combined CSM and WRM, reached the highest capacity of 31.84 kN, which is a 41.58% improvement over the control. These results show that WRM is better at flexural strengthening than CSM when used alone. The hybrid combination is the best since it better confines, bridges fibres, and stops cracks. The study shows that fibre wrapping not only makes reinforced concrete beams stronger, but it also slows down the spread of cracks and makes them more flexible.

Shear beam

The experimental investigation on shear beams indicates that U-shaped wrapping with Chopped Strand Mat (CSM), Woven Roving Mat (WRM), and their amalgamation substantially improves shear strength relative to the control specimen. The control shear beam (SBO) broke at 18.6 kN, whereas the CSM-wrapped beam (SBC1) broke at 24 kN, which is a 22.5% improvement. The WRM-wrapped beam (SBW1) raised the ultimate load to 25.2 kN, which is a 26.19% increase. The CSM + WRM wrapping (SBCW1) performed the best, with a load of 28.32 kN, which is 34.32% more than the control beam. These results show that WRM works better than CSM when applied alone, and that the hybrid wrapping gives the best strengthening effect by making the material more confined and resistant to cracking. The study shows that wrapping reinforced concrete beams with fibres from the outside is an excellent way to make them stronger when they are under shear. This greatly increases both their load capacity and their structural performance.

To maintain stringent experimental control and differentiate the critical behavioural variations among strengthening materials, a single strengthening layer was used for each design. The interaction of layers enhances tensile capacity and alters the effective strain distribution, resulting in significant modifications to stiffness, bond-slip characteristics, crack propagation patterns, and failure modes. Essential trends may be concealed if multiple layers are introduced prior to comprehending the baseline behaviour of a single layer.
