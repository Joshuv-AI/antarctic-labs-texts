Promoting brain health in older surgical patients: A pre-post evaluation of an educational module
Authors: Melanie Li, Yasmin Alhamdah, Ellene Yan, Majid Nabipoor, Aparna Saripella, Sazzadul Islam, David He, Jean Wong, Frances Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder surgical patients are at a high risk for perioperative neurocognitive disorders, such as postoperative delirium (POD), yet awareness and engagement in brain health are low. The preoperative period represents a key opportunity to improve knowledge and preparedness through targeted education. This study evaluates the impact of a brief educational module on older adults’ knowledge of brain health and POD, as well as its acceptability and perceived usefulness in the preoperative setting.MethodsA multicentre prospective pre-post interventional study was conducted in pre-admission clinics in Toronto, Canada. Adults ≥ 60 years old undergoing non-cardiac surgery completed a 20-item true/false questionnaire on brain health, cognitive impairment, and POD before and after a brief educational module. The primary outcome was the change in knowledge score, evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Secondary outcomes included participant satisfaction (5-point Likert scales) and a thematic analysis of open-ended feedback.ResultsA total of 141 participants were included. Median knowledge scores improved significantly from 17 (15–18, interquartile range) to 19 (18–20) (p < 0.001; Cohen’s d=0.79). Overall, 73% of participants demonstrated improvement, and 57.4% achieved a clinically meaningful gain of at least 1.2 points— equivalent to answering one additional answer correctly. Regression analysis confirmed the educational module as the only significant predictor of improvement. Gains in knowledge were greatest in delirium recognition, prevention, and risk factors. Satisfaction was high, with > 90% of patients finding the module to be helpful, understandable, and relevant. Qualitative feedback identified key themes including healthy lifestyle strategies, promotion of brain health, and delirium awareness.ConclusionsA brief educational module significantly increased older surgical patients’ knowledge about brain health and POD. High satisfaction supports its potential role as part of routine perioperative care. These findings support the integration of a targeted module into preoperative care as a simple and acceptable strategy to promote patient knowledge, engagement, and readiness for surgery.

Introduction

The world’s aging population is increasing exponentially, with an estimated 1.7 billion individuals aged 65 and older by 2054.1 Older adults already account for more than 40% of all surgeries in the United States, a proportion expected to increase with rising life expectancy.2, 3 Cognitive impairment (CI), ranging from subjective decline to dementia, is common in this population, with 37% of older surgical patients having unrecognized preoperative CI.4, 5 This cognitive vulnerability places them at a higher risk for adverse postoperative outcomes, including postoperative delirium (POD), prolonged hospital length of stay (LOS), non-home discharge, and mortality.6, 7, 8

Perioperative neurocognitive disorders (PNDs), including POD, delayed neurocognitive recovery, and postoperative neurocognitive disorder, affect a substantial proportion of older surgical patients.9 Specifically, the incidence of POD following both elective and emergent non-cardiac surgery is high in older adults, at 19% and 32%, respectively.10 Aside from distress for patients, POD has far-reaching consequences for patient recovery, health system burden, and caregiver well-being.11, 12, 13 Currently, preoperative discussions often fail to address brain health explicitly,14, 15 and many patients remain unaware of the cognitive risks of surgery.16

In a recent survey study, only 38% of older adults were willing to undergo preoperative cognitive testing even if it could improve their care, indicating a need for greater education and awareness of cognitive risks in the perioperative period.17 Without sufficient patient engagement, even improvements in standards of care may have limited impact on outcomes. Importantly, perioperative cognitive risk is modifiable: in a randomized clinical trial, older surgical adults who adhered to cognitive prehabilitation experienced a significantly lower incidence of POD.18 Preventative interventions may reduce up to 30–40% of POD cases.19 In addition, given that nearly 40% of dementia risk can be mitigated by addressing modifiable lifestyle factors,20 limited patient engagement in perioperative cognitive health represents a huge missed opportunity for early intervention and empowerment.

The preoperative period can serve as a “teachable moment” during which older adults are particularly receptive to information relevant to their upcoming surgery and overall health.21 Participation in education programs can lead to increased dementia literacy, empowerment, and active engagement in strategies for promoting brain health, with a positive effect on risk reduction.22, 23 While cognitive training interventions for surgical patients have been developed, there are currently no standardized patient-facing cognitive educational materials developed or evaluated specifically for older adults in perioperative settings.24, 25, 26 Focused, accessible education is urgently needed to allow older adults to make informed decisions about their care and facilitate active participation during the surgical experience.

The primary objective of this study was to determine the impact of an educational module on older surgical patients’ knowledge of brain health and CI by comparing their knowledge scores before and after education. Secondarily, participants completed a post-module evaluation survey comprising both Likert-scale and open-ended questions to assess their satisfaction with the content and perceptions regarding the module’s usefulness. This study represents an initial step in the development and validation of a perioperative brain health education program.

Methods

Study population

Participants were recruited between 15 August 2024, and 8 May 2025, at pre-admission clinics of Toronto Western Hospital (TWH) and Mount Sinai Hospital (MSH) in Toronto, Canada. Ethics approval was received before recruitment (TWH: #24‐5067, MSH: 24‐0019‐E), and written informed consent was obtained from each participant during their visit to the pre-admission clinic. The trial was registered on www.clinicaltrials.gov (registration number: NCT06540638) on 12 August 2024.

The inclusion criteria were individuals: (1) ≥ 60 years old, (2) undergoing elective non-cardiac surgery, and (3) capable of giving informed consent in English. Exclusion criteria were individuals with: (1) previous major neurocognitive disorder (e.g., dementia) diagnosis, (2) uncontrolled psychiatric illness, and (3) hearing and/or vision impairment.

Module design and study procedures

A literature review of existing patient education materials revealed a lack of specific resources tailored to older surgical patients addressing both the risks and prevention strategies for POD and CI. As a result, we developed a concise, evidence-based educational module addressing the following topics: (1) the relationship between surgery and the brain, (2) definitions, signs, causes, and risk factors for POD and CI, (3) modifiable risk factors and lifestyle strategies, and (4) practical, actionable delirium-prevention tips (e.g., sleep hygiene, sensory aids, etc.). Content was compiled from current, authoritative sources and guidelines, including national and international patient-facing resources and perioperative brain-health initiatives,27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33 as well as recent perioperative trials and reviews such as the PROTECT trial and CAN-THUMBS UP.3, 23, 34 Development and final content decisions were made by the study team which included anesthesiologists.

The module, presented as a printed slide-deck, was completed by participants during their routine preoperative assessment clinic visit, which occurred at variable time points prior to surgery, ranging from the day before surgery to approximately one month in advance. Typical time to completion was 10–15 min. It was identical across both sites and available only in English. To ensure accessibility, it included concise, plain language and essential content specifically for older surgical patients. A Patient Education specialist from the University Health Network (UHN) Patient Partners Program reviewed it for clarity, readability, and design.

Study objectives

For our primary objective, the effectiveness of the module was assessed through a knowledge questionnaire comprising 20 true/false questions, providing a maximum possible score of 20 (Supplementary File 1). The questionnaire items, adapted from the education module and reviewed by a Patient Education specialist, were phrased in both positive and negative directions to reduce response bias. Participants completed the questionnaire before and immediately after the module in the preoperative clinic, with score change as the primary outcome.

For secondary objectives, participants completed a post-module satisfaction survey using 5-point Likert scales to rate clarity, usefulness, likelihood of applying tips, contribution to brain health understanding, module length, and overall satisfaction. Open-ended questions captured takeaways and feedback. Demographic information, including age, sex, ethnicity, surgical procedure, and level of education, was also collected.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

A sample size calculation based on a paired t-test was computed for the primary objective. Assuming an effect size of 0.3 (mean pre-post difference of 1.2 points and a pooled standard deviation of 4), a total of 119 participants would provide 90% power to detect this difference at a two-sided α of 0.05. The assumed mean difference of 1.2 points was selected to reflect the minimal clinically important difference (MCID), representing an average improvement of at least one correct response (approximately 5–6%) on the 20-item questionnaire, which was considered a conservative and clinically meaningful change in this context. No pilot data was available for our scale and the test means and standard deviations used in the calculation were hypothesized based on our previous studies, where our patient population tended to be highly educated.35, 36 We applied a 15% correction for non-parametric tests, resulting in a sample size of 136. Additionally, assuming an incompletion rate of 20%, the final sample size was estimated to require a recruitment of 164 patients. This calculation was completed using RStudio (pwr.t.test function).

Data analysis

Missing data were evaluated to assess their extent and potential impact on study findings. We calculated item-level and participant-level missingness for both pre- and post-module questionnaires. To assess whether missing data introduced bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing pre-module baseline scores between participants with complete and incomplete data using a t-test. In the incomplete dataset, scores were computed based on the proportion of correct responses from answered items, scaled to a 20-point scale.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population as mean ± standard deviation, median (interquartile range [IQR]), or count (percentage). For the primary objective, pre- and post-education knowledge scores were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test after non-normality was confirmed by the Shapiro-Wilk test (p = 0.004). Statistical significance was established at α = 0.05, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons across 20 items (adjusted α = 0.0025). To evaluate ceiling effects, a sensitivity analysis excluded participants with near-perfect baseline scores (≥19/20). Linear regression examined associations between baseline scores and demographic variables (age, sex, race, education, dementia family history, surgery type, hospital site). A paired t-test was conducted to evaluate the significance of changes in pre- and post-module scores, and Cohen’s d was used to estimate the magnitude of these changes along with their 95% confidence intervals. To analyze factors influencing score changes, a longitudinal linear mixed-effects model with random intercepts for patients was employed to account for patient-level variability. The model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood with the lme4 package in R, and the significance of fixed effects was assessed using Wald tests. Internal consistency of the 20-item dichotomous knowledge questionnaire was evaluated using the Kuder-Richardson Formula Version 20 (KR-20), with values of approximately 0.70 considered acceptable internal consistency for multidomain knowledge assessments.37

For the secondary objective, satisfaction items (five-point or three-point Likert scales) were summarized as medians (interquartile range) and response proportions. Open-ended feedback was analyzed via rapid content analysis: an initial codebook was inductively generated from the first 10 responses by the first co-author and refined by the second co-author. Both authors then independently coded all responses and discrepancies were resolved by consensus. Final themes were reported as frequencies and percentages. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2).

Results

Of the 251 older adults approached, 87 did not participate due to refusal or failure to meet inclusion criteria (Fig. S1). Among the remaining 164 who completed the module, 23 did not provide complete responses to the questionnaires and were therefore excluded from analysis. The final analytic sample consisted of 141 participants with complete data. Missing data were minimal overall (1.7% of responses). Mean pre-module scores did not differ significantly between complete and incomplete cases (16.3 vs 15.7, p = 0.45), supporting the validity of using complete case analysis. The 20-item knowledge questionnaire demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (KR‐20 = 0.67).

However, many questions had lower correct response rates and indicated specific deficits in knowledge. Only 52.5% correctly recognized that POD is associated with complications such as an increased duration of hospital stay, 58.2% correctly observed that there are practical prevention strategies for POD, and 63.8% correctly answered that changes in energy levels could be a sign of POD.

In the multivariable analysis, participant characteristics including age, sex, race, family history of dementia, surgery type, and hospital site were not significantly associated with baseline knowledge scores (Table S1). Participants with postgraduate education showed a tendency toward higher baseline scores (adjusted β = 1.81, 95% CI: 0.00–3.63) (Fig. S2), though this did not reach statistical significance.

Knowledge score improvement

Median knowledge scores increased from 17 (IQR 15–18) pre-module to 19 (IQR 18–20) post-module (Table 3). Participants showed a significant improvement following education, with median change of 2 points (IQR 0–3, V = 6492, p < 0.001) and mean change of 1.99 points (95% CI: 1.64–2.35, t (140) = 11.04, p < 0.001). The effect size was medium to large (Cohen's d = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.63–0.95). Sensitivity analysis

Excluding of participants with near-perfect baseline knowledge scorers (≥19/20) yielded a subgroup of 113 participants. In this subgroup, knowledge scores were still significantly improved following the module (p < 0.001), indicating the overall outcomes were not driven by high-scoring participants at baseline.

Secondary analysis

Participant feedback was positive across all domains (Fig. S4, Fig. S5, Table S2). Most (90.1%) agreed the information was easy to understand (median 1 [IQR 1–2]), 91.4% found the module helpful (median 2 [1–2]), and 90.7% indicated they would follow the shared practical tips (median 2 [1–2]). Additionally, 87.9% agreed that the module significantly contributed to their understanding of brain health in the surgical context (median: 2 [1–2]). Overall satisfaction was high, with 86.5% either extremely satisfied (27.9%) or satisfied (58.6%) with the module (median: 2 [1–2]). Most (84.4%) felt the program length (∼10–15 min) was “just right,” with 12.1% rating it “too long” and 3.5% “too short” (median 2 [2–2]).

Participants provided optional qualitative feedback by sharing one key lesson learned from the module. From 79 responses, 82 distinct ideas were coded. Frequencies and representative quotes are provided in Table S3. The most common theme was Lifestyle Factors for Brain Health (26.8%), which focused on modifiable lifestyle factors for brain health like exercise, diet, hydration, and sleep. Feedback on Format/Material (23.2%) captured comments on the educational content's clarity, usefulness, and presentation. General Brain Health Knowledge (20.7%) reflected a broader awareness of maintaining cognitive health with age and around surgery. Delirium Awareness, Recognition and Prevention (17.1%) included takeaways about identifying signs of POD and reducing its risk. Self-Awareness and Monitoring (9.8%) reflected increased motivation to monitor personal health. Psychological and Emotional Aspects (2.4%) included reflections on emotional preparedness and patience with recovery. A word cloud summarizing commonly reported ideas is presented in Fig. S6.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that a brief educational module can significantly improve knowledge on brain health, postoperative delirium, and cognitive impairment in older surgical patients. Regression analyses confirmed that the observed knowledge gains were solely attributable to the educational module, with no demographic factors significantly influencing the degree of improvement. This suggests that the impact of the module may be transferable to a broad range of patient subgroups, supporting its general adoption in preoperative settings.

Knowledge gains were highest in the areas related to practical delirium prevention strategies and identification of POD symptoms– areas that also had the lowest baseline scores. Given that delirium is one of the most prevalent postoperative complication among older adults, this knowledge gap is concerning, and may help to explain why older adults have shown reluctance to participate in preoperative cognitive screening in previous research.17 Yet, cognitive screening plays a critical role in identifying patients with pre-existing CI, who are at increased risk for POD and further cognitive decline. Early identification enables clinicians to implement targeted, preventive strategies to reduce delirium risk and support safer perioperative care.38, 39 If patients better understood the impact of brain health on their recovery and long-term independence, they might be more open to cognitive screening. By addressing the knowledge gap, educational interventions may have the potential to improve patient autonomy, support person-centered care, and ultimately contribute to safer surgical journeys for older adults.

There were a handful of items that experienced little to no change statistically, including those regarding lifestyle habits already widely promoted in public health messaging, such as regular exercise and blood pressure and glycemic control. These items had a high level of baseline accuracy, which was likely indicative of a ceiling effect in which the existing knowledge possessed by participants had little opportunity to be further captured as measurable gains. Taken altogether, this pattern suggests that patients may have been more aware of concepts involving physical health rather than cognitive health prior to the study, further establishing the need for explicit education around less understood domains. Items with consistently high baseline performance may be considered for omission or streamlining in future iterations of the educational module.

Some items showed negligible or even negative change, possibly reflecting an issue with ambiguous wording or a difference in interpretation. Additionally, questions involving more complex or abstract cognitive associations, like the functional implications of cognitive impairment, showed limited improvement, possibly indicating the need for more explicit phrasing in future iterations of the module.

Clinical implications

A 10% overall improvement in knowledge, coupled with a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.79), is likely to be meaningful for perioperative practice. Even modest improvements in patient knowledge have been associated with improve adherence to recovery programs,40 reduced anxiety,41 and lower delirium incidence.42 In addition, given the established role of inflammation in POD pathophysiology,43, 44 it is possible that educational interventions may indirectly influence inflammatory pathways through reductions in preoperative anxiety.45, 46, 47 Our findings altogether underscore the clinical utility of patient education as a non-invasive and scalable strategy for enhancing surgical readiness and outcomes. Future research is expected to determine whether improved knowledge from the module translates into behavioral changes, adherence to risk-reduction strategies, or reductions in POD and other adverse perioperative outcomes.

Overall, trends in our study show that the educational module was most effective in improving knowledge on delirium recognition, complications, and prevention. Since POD is an underrecognized condition associated with many adverse outcomes,48 enhancing patient awareness through education may help prevent POD and promote more informed patient engagement in perioperative care. The module’s brief duration (10–15 min) supports seamless integration into pre-admission workflows, while high satisfaction supports its acceptability. Delivering the module remotely prior to the pre-admission visit may reduce disruption to perioperative workflows while ensuring that patients are prepared in advance. Further research should also examine whether the timing of module delivery before surgery influences knowledge retention, satisfaction, or perioperative anxiety, particularly given that patients in this study completed the module at varying preoperative time points.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths include recruitment from two tertiary hospitals, a high questionnaire response rate (86%), and sensitivity analyses demonstrating result robustness. However, the lack of a control group limits causal inference, and knowledge gains may partially reflect test–retest effects. A ceiling effect is possible, as 24% of participants scored ≥ 19/20 at baseline, although sensitivity analysis mitigated this concern. This study evaluated knowledge gained immediately post-module; consequently, long-term knowledge retention and behaviour uptake were not assessed. Lastly, participants were predominantly white and highly educated, which may limit generalizability to populations with greater racial, cultural, and educational diversity. The relatively high baseline knowledge observed among participants may reflect the educational composition of the sample. Randomised controlled trials should investigate whether incorporating this module into preoperative evaluation influences behavior and reduces postoperative delirium.

Conclusions

A brief educational module produced significant improvement in knowledge of brain health and delirium in older patients undergoing surgery, especially in priority areas of baseline deficit. High satisfaction supports its potential role as part of routine perioperative care. Further research is needed to investigate downstream clinical benefits.
