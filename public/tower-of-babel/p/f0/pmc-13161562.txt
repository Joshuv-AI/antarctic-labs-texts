Life-course socioeconomic trajectories and cognitive aging in Chinese middle-aged and older adults
Authors: Mingyue Gao, Wenxin Cai, Yixuan Ma, Min Yang, Lingxiao He, Leah Li, Ya Fang, Duy Nguyen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesSocioeconomic positions (SEPs), operationalized by education, occupation, and income, are widely recognized determinants of cognitive function (CF). However, the effect of life-course SEP on cognitive aging remains inconclusive. This study investigated the impact of life-course SEP trajectories on later-life cognitive aging across SEP dimensions among Chinese adults born in the 1940s-70s who experienced distinct social mobility in the rapid social transformation.MethodsWe used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (2011-2020, 20 055 participants aged ≥45 years). Life-course SEP included early-life and adult education, occupation, and income. CF was measured as a composite cognitive score. Latent class analysis was conducted to identify heterogeneous SEP trajectory groups, and mixed-effects models were applied to estimate the impact of SEP trajectory on cognitive aging.ResultsAll life-course SEP indicators were positively associated with late-life CF, with adult SEP showing stronger impacts than childhood SEP. Upward mobility was notable in education (55.5%) and income (65.6%), whereas occupational transitions were relatively stable (68.0-71.3%). Participants with high early-life SEP and upward mobility had higher CF and slower decline than disadvantaged SEP trajectory groups, especially in women. Education and occupation trajectories had stronger impacts on cognitive aging than income trajectories.Discussion and ImplicationsLife-course SEP exerts protective effects on cognitive aging, with education and occupation showing stronger influences than income. Chinese women could gain greater cognitive benefits from SEP improvements than men. Optimizing lifelong SEP trajectories should be targeted as priorities to mitigate cognitive aging in developing countries.Keywords: Life-course approach, Socioeconomic position, Social mobility, Cognitive decline, Longitudinal trajectories

The role of life-course socioeconomic position (SEP) in cognitive aging among Chinese adults remains unclear. This study adopted a life-course perspective and simultaneously examined childhood and adult SEP across multiple domains. The study shows adults born in 1940-70 experienced distinct educational, occupational, and income trajectories. Advantaged SEP trajectories, marked by high early-life SEP and upward mobility in adulthood, were associated with better cognitive performance and slower cognitive decline. Promoting educational and occupational opportunities across the life course can provide cognitive benefits and reduce disparities, especially for women. Policies aiming at lifelong socioeconomic enhancement may mitigate cognitive aging in developing countries.

Introduction

With the accelerating global aging process, premature cognitive decline has become a pressing public health challenge.1 Identifying modifiable factors associated with late-life cognitive function (CF) is essential for developing interventions aimed at slowing cognitive aging and preventing impairment.2 Socioeconomic position (SEP), commonly measured via education, occupation, and income, has been consistently associated with late-life CF.3

Life-course research shows that SEP in both childhood and adulthood exerts protective effects on later-life CF,4 aligning with sensitive period, pathway, and accumulation models.4 Notably, these mechanisms often overlap. Childhood SEP exerted a long-arm influence on later-life CF, representing a sensitive period.2,4 The pathway model posits that the effects of early-life SEP are mediated by adult SEP.5,6 For instance, childhood socioeconomic advantages may facilitate educational attainment and occupational success, which in turn protect against cognitive decline in later life. Finally, the accumulation model emphasizes the additive effect of SEP across life stages, with disadvantages compounding risk for cognitive decline.7

Despite this evidence, the relative contributions of childhood versus adult SEP remain debated.6,8–10 Some studies suggest childhood SEP effects are mediated by education,11–14 while others indicate adult SEP-CF associations are attenuated when accounting for childhood SEP.15–17 These inconsistencies often vary across education, occupation, and income, each exerting unique effects on cognitive aging at specific life stages. Education builds cognitive reserve, exerting a long-lasting protective impact on later-life CF and potentially buffering against cognitive decline.18 Occupation is another protective factor for CF, through further developing cognitive reserve against aging. With individual education achieving its highest level in young adulthood, mentally demanding occupations provide continuous cognitive stimulation throughout one’s lifespan.19 Income and wealth reflect access to healthier lifestyles, better healthcare, and mentally stimulating environments.20 Evidence shows later-life financial stress can even offset cognitive resilience gained through education and occupation.21,22 However, the distinct roles of childhood and adult SEP across these domains on late-life CF remain underexplored, especially in a rapidly aging population like China.

Dynamic SEP mobility further shapes cognitive outcomes.23,24 For Chinese adults born between the 1940s and 1970s, their life-course SEP mobilities may vary across SEP dimensions4,6,25 and were shaped by historical context: widespread educational reforms have facilitated upward educational mobility,26 while the reform and opening-up have brought about income mobility. However, slower industrial upgrading has constrained occupational mobility to some extent.27 Prior studies have primarily focused on education, with limited exploration of occupational and income trajectories.25,28,29 Therefore, understanding life-course SEP trajectories is necessary to describe SEP mobility patterns among Chinese people systematically.

It is expected that divergent SEP mobility patterns may shape cognitive aging.30,31 Evidence linking life-course SEP to cognitive decline is inconsistent.4 Earlier studies suggested that higher educational attainment might be associated with slower cognitive decline,32 whereas others reported a negligible association.18,33,34 Similar inconsistencies exist for childhood SEP, while evidence for the adult occupation is sparse, especially in developing countries.4,35 Thus, examining life-course SEP trajectories across education, occupation, and income is critical for understanding cognitive aging in China.

To address the theoretical and empirical gaps outlined above, using longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we aimed to: (1) investigate the relative contributions of childhood and adult SEP across dimensions (education, occupation, income) to later-life CF; (2) characterize life-course SEP mobility patterns and identify heterogeneous SEP trajectory groups; (3) examine how SEP trajectories influence cognitive aging, indicated as baseline CF level and decline rate.

Methods

Study population

The CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort of adults aged 45 years and older. The survey started in 2011, with 17 708 participants from 150 counties/districts across 28 provinces. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020 (5 waves total). To maintain the representativeness of the sample, adults from selected households who were 40-44 years at the baseline survey and turned 45 in later waves were added as refreshment samples. A wide range of information was collected, including socioeconomic, health, employment, and retirement data.

In 2014, participants from waves 1-2 were invited to complete a Life History Survey on childhood experiences and important events before age 17. The CHARLS was approved by the biomedical ethics committee of Peking University. All participants provided informed consent.

This study included 20 055 participants, with valid SEP measurements and longitudinal measures of CF from 2011 to 2020. The selection procedure of the study sample is provided in Supplementary Figure 1.

Measures

Childhood SEP measures

According to previous studies,2,4 childhood SEP was usually proxied by parental education (maternal, paternal, or both), parental occupation (maternal, paternal, or both), and household financial situation during childhood. Recent reviews summarized that maternal education had a stronger impact than paternal education on both global CF and CF subdomains.2,8 Possible explanations were that, as the principal caregivers, highly educated mothers devoted more to their children’s cognitively stimulating environments and socializing their children toward the value and enjoyment of learning. Additionally, paternal occupation represents the primary source of income for the family, which corresponds to the highest social class within the family and characterizes childhood SEP.36,37 Therefore, we used maternal education and paternal occupation as proxies for early-life educational and occupational environments, respectively, and supplemented our analysis with parental education and parental occupation.

In CHARLS, childhood SEP information was collected from the life history survey in 2014. Maternal education was defined as “the highest education level of mother completed” and was proxied using paternal education if maternal information was missing. Maternal education was classified into illiteracy, primary or middle school, high school, and college or above. Paternal occupation was collected through “father’s principal occupation before age 17 years” and was dichotomized into agricultural or non-agricultural. Childhood financial situation was derived using “how was the family’s financial situation before age 17 years compared to the average of the community.” It was graded into low (responses: “somewhat worse off,” “a lot worse off”), middle (“same”), and high (“somewhat better off,” “a lot better off”).

Adult SEP measures

Adult SEP included educational attainment, principal occupation, and household income during adulthood.4 Educational attainment was obtained as “the highest education level attained” in the CHARLS 2011 survey and was classified into illiteracy, primary or middle school, high school, and college or higher. Adult occupation was collected through the “history of job that lasted for 6 months or more” question in the 2014 Life History survey. According to the harmonization of work history from ages 15 to 80 years, we derived occupations for 3 life stages: early adulthood (19-24 years), mid-adulthood (25-44 years), and late adulthood (45-60 years). Adult occupations were classified into agricultural jobs, non-working, government jobs, and other jobs. Late-adulthood income was derived as “the sum of all income at the household level” divided by “the total number of people living in the household” from the 2011 survey. It was categorized into low, middle, and high tertiles. The definitions and classifications of life-course SEP are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Cognitive function

Cognitive function in CHARLS was measured longitudinally from wave 2011 to 2020, including 2 domains: episodic memory and executive function. Episodic memory was assessed using both immediate recall (range: 0-10 points) and delayed recall (range: 0-10 points). Executive function comprised orientation (current year, month, date, day of the week, and season; 0-5 points), attention (serial seven subtraction from 100, up to 5 times; 0-5 points), and visuospatial ability (redraw 2 overlapping pentagons; 0-1 point).

A global cognitive score (CF) was computed as the sum of all test scores and ranges from 0 to 31 points. A global cognitive composite z-score (z-CF) was derived as the average of z-scores for each cognitive domain, standardized against baseline means and standard deviations in wave 2011. Higher CF and z-CF scores indicated better cognitive performance.

Covariates

The covariates included variables known to be associated with cognition at older ages, including age (years), gender, marital status (0 = married/cohabitating; 1 = not married nor cohabitating), residence (urban/rural), self-rated health before age 15 (good/poor), activities of daily livings (ADLs, 0 = unlimited, defined as ADL score = 0; 1 = limited, ADL score > 0), instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs, 0 = unlimited, defined as IADL score = 0; 1 = limited, IADL score > 0), chronic disease status (yes/no), history of falls during the past 2 years (yes/no), current smoking (yes/no), alcohol consumption during the past 12 months (yes/no), social engagement (yes/no), and whether has depressive symptoms (0 = no, CES-D score ≤12; 1 = yes, CES-D score >12).

Statistical analysis

First, linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) were used to account for the within-subject correlation of repeated measures and describe the longitudinal cognitive aging process. LMMs, including gender, linear age, quadratic age, and the interaction of gender and age terms, were applied to capture the non-linear cognitive decline for separate genders. Consistent with previous findings,38–40 we observed gender differences in the CF baseline level and decline rate (female vs. male: intercept, −0.14, linear age, −0.005, p < .05). In addition, previous studies showed that gender disparities in SEP-CF association varied across CF domains and social contexts.4,8,38,41 Therefore, sex-stratified LMMs were applied in the subsequent analyses.

To assess associations between life-course SEP and standardized global cognitive function (z-CF), sex-stratified LMMs were fitted in steps. First, the baseline model (Model 1) investigated the impact of each single SEP indicator, after adjusting for age, age squared, and adult covariates. We then added other SEP indicators from the same life stage (childhood or adulthood) (Model 2). Finally, we added all the life-course SEP indicators (Model 3).

To identify heterogeneous patterns of life-course socioeconomic trajectories, latent class analysis (LCA)42 was conducted using childhood and adult SEP measures described previously (Supplementary Table 1). LCA first incorporated childhood and adult SEP measures for specific domains (education, occupation, and income) to identify domain-specific classes and then included all SEP measures together to identify life-course socioeconomic trajectory classes. LCA were fitted with an increasing number of classes sequentially, from 1 to 5, until the model failed to converge. The optimal number of classes was determined by model fit criteria, ie, low Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and adjusted BIC, together with the interpretability of the identified classes.43 Participants were assigned to the most likely class based on the maximum posterior probability, and class membership was used as a categorical exposure in subsequent analyses.

We then estimated cognitive aging trajectories by SEP classes (with adjustment for covariates) using LMMs with quadratic age terms to capture non-linear decline. Interactions between SEP class membership and age terms were tested to examine whether the rate of decline differed across SEP classes. For each SEP, estimated mean trajectories and age-specific differences in z-CF between classes were plotted to visualize the impact of life-course SEP on cognitive aging. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2.

Since LMMs can accommodate unbalanced follow-up data under the missing at random (MAR) assumptions, the study sample included participants with 2-5 CF assessments (average: 3.6 times). The distributions of the covariates were generally comparable across the cross-sectional and longitudinal samples (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Missing data in SEP indicators and covariates (up to 13.5%, Supplementary Table 2) were addressed using Multiple Imputation by Chained Equation (MICE). Twenty-five imputed datasets were generated, and pooled estimates were obtained following Rubin’s rules.44 The distributions of SEP indicators and covariates remained consistent before and after imputation (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Sensitivity analyses were repeated with parental education and parental occupation as early-life educational and occupational environments. The findings shown from Supplementary Table 9 to Supplementary Figure 6 were consistent with those using maternal education and paternal occupation as early-life SEP indicators.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study sample

A total of 20 055 participants (9750 men, 10 305 women) were included in the analysis. The mean global cognitive score was 15.40 ± 5.66, and the mean z-score was 0.10 ± 0.68. Men scored higher than women, both in raw score (16.07 ± 5.12 vs. 14.75 ± 6.07; p < .05) and z-score (0.22 ± 0.60 vs. −0.01 ± 0.72; p < .05).

All life-course SEP indicators showed a positive and linear association with z-CF (p < .05 for linear trend). Education showed a dose-response relationship, with mean z-CF ranging from 0.07 ± 0.61 (illiterate) to 0.28 ± 0.65 (college or above). Occupation and income also had similar protective gradients. Gender differences were observed, with women having greater cognitive benefits from higher education, professional occupations, and higher income compared to men (Table 1).

The impact of childhood and adult SEP on later-life CF

All life-course SEP indicators were positively associated with late-life CF (p < .05, Table 2). Among these, adulthood education showed stronger effects (β = 0.46-0.69, p < .05) than maternal education (β = 0.15-0.33, p < .05). Occupational status across the life course demonstrated similar associations with late-life cognition (male: β = 0.20-0.22; female: β = 0.37-0.40, p < .05). Adulthood income (β = 0.18-0.23, p < .05) had consistently stronger effects than childhood financial status (β = 0.07-0.13, p < .05) (Table 2, Model 1).

Among childhood SEP indicators, paternal occupation and maternal education had the strongest associations with late-life CF, while adult education was more strongly associated with late-life CF (β = 0.42-0.64, p < .05) than adult occupation and household income (Table 2, Model 2). The protective effects of life-course SEP indicators on later-life CF were more pronounced in female than male participants (p < .05), except for childhood financial status and adult household income (Supplementary Table 5).

When all life-course SEP indicators were considered simultaneously (Table 2, Model 3), adulthood education showed the strongest association with late-life CF (male: β = 0.41; female: β = 0.41; p < .05). Smaller yet significant associations were observed for adult occupation (male: β = 0.11; female: β = 0.16; p < .05) and household income (β = 0.11 for both genders; p < .05). Although largely attenuated after adjusting for adult SEP, childhood SEP effects remained significant, with paternal occupation showing the strongest impact (p < .05).

Life-course SEP mobility patterns and latent class of SEP trajectories

Each SEP indicator exhibited distinct life-course mobility patterns, with three-class models demonstrating optimal fitness for capturing the heterogeneous SEP trajectory groups of each SEP indicator (Supplementary Table 6).

Educational mobility and trajectories

Most participants experienced upward educational mobility (55.5%) or remained educational immobile (42.6%), whereas downward mobility was rare (2.0%) (Figure 1A, Supplementary Figure 2a). Three educational trajectory classes were identified: Class 1 (79.8%) represented a pattern of persistently low education (low maternal and modest improvement in adulthood), Class 2 (17.6%) indicated moderate upward mobility (middle maternal education and moderate advancement in adulthood), Class 3 (2.6%) exhibited substantial upward mobility (high maternal education and marked enhancement in adulthood) (Figure 2A).

Occupational mobility and trajectories

Occupation demonstrated the highest degree of stability, with 68.0%-71.3% of respondents maintaining immobility across life stages. Upward mobility (17.4%-24.8%) was relatively more prevalent than downward mobility (7.2%-13.1%) (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure 2b). The identified occupational trajectory classes were as follows: Class 1 (57.0%) representing stable low occupational status, Class 2 (32.8%) exhibiting middle paternal occupation with improvement in adulthood, and Class 3 (10.2%) denoting high paternal occupation with slight enhancement in adulthood (Figure 2B).

Income mobility and trajectories

Income showed the highest overall mobility (65.6% in total), with 49.3% respondents moving upward, 16.3% moving downward, and 34.4% remaining immobile (Figure 1C, Supplementary Figure 2c). Three income trajectory classes were distinguished: Class 1 (24.6%) featured moderate childhood income with downward trends in adulthood, Class 2 (32.0%) represented moderate childhood income with upward trends in adulthood, and Class 3 (43.4%) included those with upper-middle childhood income with upward trends in adulthood (Figure 2C).

Latent class of SEP trajectories

Spearman correlation results supported these mobility trends. Occupational stability exhibited the strongest life-course correlations (r = 0.25-0.66, p < .05), followed by education (r = 0.21, p < .05) and income (r = 0.11; p < .05). For integrated life-course SEP trajectories, a four-class LCA model provided the best fit (Supplementary Table 6) Class 1 (55.0%) represented low childhood SEP with a mild upward trajectory, Class 2 (9.6%) indicated lower-middle childhood SEP with a moderate upward trajectory, Class 3 (22.7%) shows upper-middle childhood SEP with a high upward trajectory, and Class 4 (11.5%) demonstrated high childhood SEP with a prominent upward trajectory (Figure 2D).

Life-course SEP trajectories on cognitive aging

Educational trajectories on cognitive aging

Cognitive aging varied across educational trajectory classes (Figure 3A). At age 45, Classes 2 and 3 had higher baseline z-CF than Class 1 (difference in z-CF: 0.16 and 0.33 for men, 0.36 and 0.59 for women, all p < .05). Furthermore, cognitive decline was slower in Classes 2 and 3 (difference in rate: 0.007 and 0.008 for men, 0.009 and 0.010 for women, all p < .05; Supplementary Table 7). The association between higher educational attainment and later-life CF was magnified with age and was stronger in women than in men. For example, at age 60, the z-CF difference between Class 3 and Class 1 was 0.79 (95% CI: 0.71-0.88) in women versus 0.46 (0.41-0.50) in men; by age 80 the difference increased to 1.06 (0.87-1.24) and 0.62 (0.53-0.72), respectively (Figure 3A; Supplementary Table 8).

CF trajectories and z-CF differences by life-course SEP classes for (A) education, (B) occupation, (C) income, and (D) all SEP indicators by gender. CF = cognitive function; z-CF = composite z-score of the global cognitive function; SEP = socio-economic position.

Occupational trajectories on cognitive aging

More optimal occupational trajectories had a protective impact on later-life CF, the effect amplified with advancing age and was stronger in women than men (p < .05, Figure 3B, Supplementary Table 8). At age 45, the CF difference between Class 3 and Class 1 was 0.31 (0.26-0.37) in men and 0.57 (0.51-0.64) in women. By age 80, this disparity widened to 0.46 (0.41-0.51) in men and 0.81 (0.73-0.89) in women. In general, occupational trajectories exerted weaker effects on cognitive decline than educational trajectories. Compared to Occupational Class 1, Class 2 showed a rate difference of 0.001/year (p > 0.05) in men and 0.002/year (p < .05) in women. Class 3 demonstrated differences of 0.004/year (p < .05) in men and 0.007/year (p < .05) in women (Supplementary Table 7).

Income trajectories on cognitive aging

Cognitive aging primarily diverged between income trajectory class 3 and the other classes. Compared to Class 1, Class 2 showed slightly higher CF levels (difference in CF: 0.03-0.11 in men, 0.06-0.15 in women, p < .05), but a non-significant difference in decline rates (p > 0.05, Figure 3C). Class 3 exhibited stronger protective effects on both CF levels and decline rates, particularly in women. However, CF benefits for income Class 3 (male: 0.26; female: 0.34 for at age 60, p < .05) were smaller than those associated with education (male: 0.46; female: 0.79) and occupation (male: 0.38; female: 0.68, p < .05) at the same age (Supplementary Table 8).

Life-course SEP trajectories on cognitive aging

The protective impact of SEP on cognitive aging was more pronounced when comparing the integrated life-course SEP trajectories than the single SEP trajectories. CF levels were highest in SEP Class 4, followed by Class 3, Class 2, and Class 1 across ages 45-90 years, with a stronger extent in women than in men (Figure 3D). Despite the fact that the cognitive decline rate between Class 1 and 2 was non-significant (p > 0.05), women in Class 3 and both genders in Class 4 exhibited slower decline than Class 1 (p < .05, Supplementary Table 7).

Discussion

Based on a population-based sample of Chinese middle-aged and older adults, our study reported the following findings. First, life-course SEP indicators showed significantly protective impacts on later-life CF, with adult SEPs exerting stronger influences than childhood SEPs. Second, for Chinese adults born 1940-1970, they experienced upward educational mobility (55.5%) and income mobility (65.6%), while maintaining a relatively stable occupational pattern (68.0%-71.3%) from early to later life. Third, SEP trajectories characterized by high early-life SEP with adult upward mobility were associated with higher CF levels and slower decline, indicating a protective role in cognitive aging. Fourth, the impact of life-course SEP on cognitive aging was more pronounced in women than in men, particularly for education and occupation trajectories, which had stronger impacts on cognitive aging than income trajectories.

Childhood and adult SEP impacts on later-life CF

Our findings supported the life-course sensitive period, pathway, and accumulation models. Both childhood and adult SEPs exhibited a significant impact on CF, with a stronger impact of adult SEP, which supported the sensitive period. Childhood SEP effects were largely attenuated when adjusting for adult SEP, reflecting the pathway model. Life-course SEP indicators remained significant when considering all the other SEPs, supporting the additive effect across life stages.

In our study, SEP (education and income) in adulthood showed stronger effects than those in childhood. Consistent with previous findings,45,46 enhancement in education and income during adulthood may offset early disadvantage by improving access to healthcare, nutrition, and opportunities for building cognitive reserve.47 By contrast, both paternal and mid-adulthood occupations had stronger influences on later-life CF than other life stages, different from Western evidence where adulthood occupation predominates the effect across the lifespan.45,48,49 The divergent findings may be explained by the relatively stable occupational changes among Chinese people born between 1940 and 1970 (as described in occupational mobilities), which amplified the prolonged impact of early-life occupation.26

Furthermore, our results showed that education had the greatest impact on later-life CF, followed by occupation and income. The findings were consistent with the lifelong influence of education on cognitive reserve, through improving cognitive performance and protecting against potential negative aging changes.18 Finally, we found that the life-course education and occupation effects on CF were more pronounced among women than men. The gender disparities in SEP-CF association could be explained by the gender difference in cognitive aging and China’s social context. Previous studies reported that women had faster cognitive decline and an excessive risk of cognitive impairment than men.50 In China, women had much fewer opportunities in educational access and labor market participation than men born during the 1940s-70s.51 Therefore, as these generations experienced social development across their life course, women would gain more substantial cognitive benefits as they received educational and occupational opportunities.

SEP mobilities and trajectories for Chinese born 1940-1970

Consistent with prior studies,26,31 substantial upward educational mobility was observed among Chinese adults born 1940-1970, who entered adulthood from the 1960s to 1990s. This trend reflects institutional reforms from 1978 to 1990, which were preceded and accelerated by nationwide literacy campaigns (1950-60) and the resumption of the college entrance examination in 1977.51 Identified education trajectory classes also reflected diverse upward trends across generations. Occupational trajectories had limited mobility (∼30%) compared with other SEP indicators. This pattern was mainly due to the “Employment Inheritance System” in China up to the 1990s26 and was further supported by the stable latent occupational trajectory classes identified. Income showed the highest mobility among the 3 SEP indicators, with latent life-course income classes displaying diverse mobility patterns. This high mobility largely reflected the rapid economic development following China’s reform and opening up in 1978.52

Life-course SEP trajectories on cognitive aging

SEP trajectories characterized by better early-life SEP and upward mobilities were associated with higher CF levels and slower decline rates, indicating a protective role of life-long SEP trajectories on cognitive aging.9,45,46 Consistent with findings of individual life-course SEP indicators, educational and occupational trajectories exerted stronger protective effects on cognitive aging than income trajectories. This suggests that the effect of income on cognitive aging is partially mediated by education and occupation, which are more directly related to cognitive reserve.47,53 Importantly, the integrated life-course SEP trajectories contributed modest marginal additive effects to cognitive aging beyond education and occupation, which also reflects the largely shared underlying pathways and smaller independent accumulation among intercorrelated SEP indicators. Considering the critical role of life-course education and occupation in mitigating cognitive aging, we emphasize the importance of lifelong education and occupation enhancement in reducing cognitive disparities and promoting late-life brain health among disadvantaged groups, particularly women from developing countries.

This study has several strengths. First, CHARLS is a nationally representative cohort of Chinese middle to older age adults with high-quality longitudinal cognitive data (follow-up >80%) and retrospective life-course SEP assessments. Second, we systematically examined the effects of education, occupation, and income across life stages to identify key domain-specific SEP indicators for cognition. Third, we applied LCA as a useful approach to identify heterogeneous multidimensional SEP trajectories42 for Chinese adults born between the 1940s and 1970s. Fourth, mixed-effects models were used to estimate cognitive aging trajectories, accounting for within-individual clustering and unbalanced follow-ups.

However, limitations exist in our study. First, although income varies throughout the life course, objective early-life income was not collected in the life-history survey and was proxied as subjective self-rated status. Second, although we observed robust associations between SEP trajectories and cognitive aging, causal relationships cannot be established due to the observational design. Third, our analysis focused on the social mobility of individuals born between 1940 and 1970 during China’s planned economy and reform eras. Given the rapid social development and demographic changes in China, life-course SEP trajectories and their impact on cognitive aging may differ in younger cohorts, warranting further investigation.

Conclusion

Chinese adults born between 1940 and 1970 experienced upward educational mobility, relatively stable occupational change, and noticeable income mobility. Advantaged life-course SEP trajectories, featured by high early-life SEP and subsequent upward mobility, have a protective effect on higher CF and a slower decline rate in later life. Our findings highlight that policies aimed at enhancing lifelong SEP, particularly for education and occupation, should be prioritized as a key strategy to promote long-term cognitive health, especially for women in developing countries.
