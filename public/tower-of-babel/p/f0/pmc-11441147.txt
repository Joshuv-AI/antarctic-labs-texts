Methods used for caries detection and diagnosis in Ontario dental practices: a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Daniel Han, Akshay Gupta, Abiola Adeniyi, Grace De Souza, Laura E Tam, Svetlana Tikhonova, Jacinta Santos, Abbas Jessani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly detection of caries is essential for applying non-surgical treatment procedures and preventing the formation of cavitated lesions leading to unnecessary removal of tooth structure. Understanding dentists’ preferences for caries detection tools can inform stakeholders about their strategies and knowledge of contemporary, evidence-based caries management approaches. However, there is a lack of research exploring the detection methods of caries commonly used by dentists in Ontario, Canada. The objective of this study was to investigate the methods of caries detection and diagnosis preferred by dentists in Ontario.MethodsA 21-item self-reported survey was mailed to one thousand Ontario dental practices in the Winter of 2022. Descriptive and bivariate data analysis were performed to determine the associations between: demographics and professional practice characteristics (explanatory variables), and methods for detecting and diagnosing dental caries (outcome variables) using SPSS Statistics 29.0.ResultsA total of 325 dentists (33%) responded to the survey, with 274 answering all of the questions completely. The highest proportion of respondents were 35–44 years of age (32.8%) and male (53.4%). More than half of the respondents reported using a dental explorer to assess primary occlusal caries (57.6%), secondary caries (57.1%), and cervical caries (57.5%). Likewise, 57.9% of the participants reported using dental radiographs to diagnose proximal caries. Among additional caries detection tools, digital radiography (89.8%) and traditional radiography (84.7%) were the most used methods/modalities, while cone beam computed tomography was the least (12.8%). Most study participants did not use any caries classification system (77.7%) or caries risk assessment tool (85.3%).ConclusionsParticipants preferred conventional methods for caries detection, instead of contemporary visual-tactile caries lesions classification and/or caries risk assessment systems. These findings indicate a need for continuing dental education programs tailored to evidence-based caries management approaches.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04934-y.

Introduction

The primary objective in managing dental caries is to establish a personalized, sustainable, long-term plan that addresses a tailored treatment and prevents future tooth decay [1]. Early and timely caries detection and risk assessment is essential for effective treatment as it allows for the application of non-surgical caries treatment procedures to control caries process, the preservation of tooth structure, and the prevention of costly restorative treatment [2, 3]. The initial assessment for determining dental caries risk involves considering caries risk factors on a patient level (socio-economic status, medical and dental history, oral health-related behaviours, etc.) combined with the clinical findings (caries experience, dental plaque, hyposalivation, etc.) [1]. Various caries risk assessment approaches are available such as the International Caries Classification and Management System (ICCMS) approach, Cariogram, American Dental Association (ADA) form, Caries Management by Risk Assessment (CAMBRA), etc [4].

Application of caries risk assessment tools offers numerous advantages in the prevention and management of dental caries. By evaluating various risk factors such as diet, oral hygiene practices, fluoride exposure, and past caries experience, dentists can stratify patients into different risk categories, ranging from low- to high-risk [5, 6]. This assessment informs caries classification by identifying patients who may require more frequent monitoring and/or intensive intervention. For example, individuals deemed as high-risk may have lesions classified as more severe or extensive, warranting prompt treatment to prevent further progression [5, 6]. Henceforth, integrating caries risk assessment into clinical practice enhances caries classification accuracy and facilitates personalized management strategies, promoting effective prevention and treatment of dental caries [5, 6].

Among caries lesions diagnosis approaches, the visual-tactile examination method, which assess severity and activity of the lesions, is recognized as a main diagnostic method. Other methods such as radiography, fiber-optic transillumination, laser fluorescence serve as adjunct tools for caries lesions detection [1].

The traditional dental explorer, commonly used as the primary clinical tool for detecting caries lesions, has low specificity (around 40%) and can result in overdiagnosis due to its tendency to stick in fissures, which may not necessarily indicate presence of caries lesion [7–9]. Additionally, the practice of applying pressure with an explorer has been questioned because of reported damage to demineralized surface integrity, which may accelerate lesion progression [10, 11]. The most recent guidelines by ICCMS recommend using a ball end probe with a gentle pressure to make the initial detection of dental caries [12].

Bitewing radiographs are widely used for detecting proximal caries but are less effective for occlusal caries until they have progressed significantly [1, 11, 13]. Radiographic caries detection can result in false positive diagnosis and have specificities ranging from 66 to 98%; however, they are reliable for monitoring caries lesions progression over time [14]. Advanced technologies like fiber-optic transillumination (FOTI) and DIAGNOdent utilize light-based methods for caries detection—FOTI relies on light scattering, while DIAGNOdent measures laser fluorescence within the tooth structure [15, 16]. These methods have varying levels of sensitivity and specificity, but the strength of the evidence is low as the available literature is insufficient to support generalized estimates on the sensitivity and specificity of these diagnostic methods [1]. The use of these diagnostic tools is dependent on the location of the dental lesion and the dentists’ preference [1].

All of these described diagnostic tools have advantages and disadvantages. Some are limited to certain surfaces or are capable of detecting only certain dentinal or enamel lesions, while others are sensitive to confounding factors that may influence their accuracy and lead to a false positive or false negative result [17]. Since a meticulous visual examination method can provide caries lesion’s severity and activity information at one point in time, it is considered the method of choice for routine clinical examinations [18]. All other diagnostic methods should be used as adjunct supportive tools. However, the key question of whether they improve patients ‟dental health outcomes has yet to be answered” [17]. Recent studies suggest that when meticulous visual-tactile caries diagnostic criteria are used to make treatment decisions, the availability of additional diagnostic information, particularly in low caries risk populations, may lead to a considerable number of false positive diagnoses and, consequently, increase the risk of overtreatment [17].

Both general and specialized dentists’ preferences for caries risk assessment and detection tools can be influenced by a variety of factors, including preferential methodology, knowledge and training of dentist, effectiveness of the diagnostic tool in detecting caries lesions, its practicability, and ease of use, with the goal of providing individualized preventative or minimally invasive treatment [19].

To the researcher’s knowledge, no previous studies have investigated the dental caries risk assessment and caries diagnostic methods used by dentists in Ontario or in Canada. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the methods of caries assessment, detection, and diagnosis preferred by dental practices in Ontario. Our specific research objectives were to investigate the use of: (1) the caries risk assessment (CRA) tools (e.g. ADA, ICCMS system, etc.) and (2) the main (visual/visual-tactile) and the additional caries diagnostic methods (e.g., radiography, light-based methods) in dental practices in Ontario.

Methods

The Western University Health Research Ethics Board granted ethical approval for this study (IRB approval number 118766). A STROBE checklist for cross-sectional studies was utilized to guide our study design, including the development of the introduction, methods, results, discussion, and conclusions (Appendix 3, Supplementary Material). A convenience sampling method was employed by administering self-reported paper survey to approximately 1000 dental offices in Ontario. These dental offices were randomly selected from the online repository of dental offices from the Royal College of Dental Surgeons of Ontario, 2022. The inclusion criteria required participants to be: a practicing dentist in Ontario, involved in treating dental caries, and provide a signed informed consent in English (Appendix 1, Supplementary Material). Any respondents who did not meet any one or more of the inclsion criteria were excluded, and consequently their surveys were elimainated from the analysis. To encourage participation, pre-paid return envelopes were included in the survey envelope. The data collection lasted for approximately three months from October to December 2022.

Survey tool

A 21-item paper survey was developed (Appendix 2, Supplementary Material). The questionnaire was adapted from a version of the “Assessment of Caries Diagnosis and Caries Treatment” questionnaire developed in 2006 by the Dental Practice-Based Research Network (DPBRN) and used in countries such as the United States, Turkey, and Brazil [19–21]. The questionnaire comprised of two parts: Part 1 (Q1-10) included questions about the dentists demographics, practice setting, and location of practice, while Part 2 (Q11-21) contained questions pertaining to caries detection and diagnosis, as well as the use of caries risk assessment. To ensure the validity and reliability of the instrument, the following measures were implemented: (1) We adapted questions from the DPBRN, previously used in several research projects. Using a well-tested questionnaire allowed for comparisons with prior studies. These questions were modified (demographics, etc.) to reflect the specific context of Ontario, (2) the questionnaire was reviewed by specialists in operative dentistry and content experts in cariology to ensure its relevance and accuracy and, (3) the final version of the survey was piloted with 10 respondents to assess clarity, comprehensiveness, and syntax.

Outcome variables

Caries detection and diagnosis

Participants indicated the caries detection and diagnosis methods they used in their clinical practice, specifying the approximate percentage of patients for whom each caries detection tool was used. The methods investigated included radiographs, dental explorer (for the detection of primary occlusal caries and secondary caries at the margins of existing restorations), air-drying (for a suspected carious lesion), laser fluorescence (for primary caries on occlusal surfaces), and fiber optic transillumination (for proximal lesions in anterior teeth). Participants were asked to estimate the proportion of patients for whom they used each of the investigated methods. The response options were: 0%, 1–24%, 25–49%, 50–74%, 75–99%, or 100%. Participants were asked if they used any caries lesions classification and caries risk assessment systems. Those who reported using a caries classification and/or risk assessment system were asked to specify the method they use. The caries classification/risk assessment systems that were evaluated included the ICDAS, ICCMS, and ADA systems, with the option for participants to specify any other methods they may have used.

Independent variables

Socio-demographic and professional characteristics

The explanatory variables for this study were the participants’ socio-demographic characteristics (age category and gender), professional characteristics (years of experience, location of undergraduate training, type of dental professional), and practice characteristics (location of practice, type of practice). The study respondents selected their age category as 20–34 years, 35–44 years, 45–54 years, 55–64 years, or 65 years and above. Gender was recorded as male, female, or other, while years of experience was recorded as 10 years or less, 11–20 years, 21–30 years, or more than 30 years. In terms of the type of dental professional, participants indicated whether they had completed specialty training, which was categorized as either a general dentist or specialist. The participants specified the country they received their undergraduate dental degree as either Canada/United States or another country. For dental clinical practice, respondents specified their professional practice type as solo private practice, group private practice, or other. The participants provided information about the location of their clinical practice using the clinic’s postal code and city. This was then recoded as one of the following categories: Central region, Eastern region, Greater Toronto Area, Northern region, Southwestern region, or no postal code/city provided. The participants also provided information on their clinical practice features, including types of additional caries diagnostic equipment, percentage of primary care patients, percentage of patients seeking care, percentage of patients seeking occasional care, percentage of patients seeking symptomatic care, and percentage of patients seeking emergency care.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS® for Mac version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). Descriptive statistics of all variables were computed. Missing data were left as is, and incomplete cases were excluded from the analyses. Bivariate analysis was performed using chi-squared tests to determine the association between the use of any caries classification and risk assessment method and the explanatory variables of the participants (age, gender, years of experience) and professional characteristics (undergraduate training, location of practice, practitioner type, and practice type). Only cases with complete data for the variables of interest were included in these tests. Statistical significance was inferred at p < 0.05.

Results

Out of 1,000 dental offices contacted, 325 (33%) replied to the survey and 274 met the study inclusion criteria. A total of 51 surverys were excluded from the study due to incomplete information (n = 40) or for not meeting one or more inclusion criteria (n = 11).

Socio-demographic, professional, and practice related-characteristics of the participants

A summary of the participants’ socio-demographic, professional, and practice-related characteristics are presented in Table 1. The age categories of 35–44 years and 45–54 years had the highest proportion of respondents, at 32.8% and 25.5% respectively. More than half of the respondents were male (53.4%), and 76.6% of the respondents had more than 10 years of clinical experience. Dentists from the Northern and Central regions had the highest proportion of respondents with 29.9% and 26.6% respectively. Over 80% of the respondents received undergraduate dental training in Canada/United States. The majority were general dentists (92.0%) and worked in group private practices (67.5%). Most participants (76.6%) also reported serving as primary care providers to more than 75% of their patients. About 80% of the dentists surveyed stated that majority of their patients are visiting for regular check-up appointments.

Caries detection and diagnostic visual tactile methods used

Table 2 shows the participants’ use of various caries detection methods. Many survey respondents (85.3%) reported air drying tooth surfaces over 51% of the time when assessing primary caries lesions. Of those who air dry tooth surfaces, 39% reported spending one to two seconds, 34% reported spending three to four seconds, and 24.9% reported spending five seconds or longer. Majority of the participants indicated that they used a dental explorer over 50% of the time when assessing primary occlusal caries (87.1%), recurrent or secondary caries (93.8%), and cervical caries (92%). Most of the participants (78.4%) indicated that they utilize dental radiographs to diagnose primary occlusal or proximal caries in 50% or more of cases. Regarding the use of fiber-optic transillumination to diagnose proximal caries on anterior teeth, 92.6% of the participants reported using it less than 50% of the time.

The use of additional caries diagnostic methods by the participants in the clinical environments where they are employed in is depicted in Fig. 1. In their clinical practices, digital radiography (89.8%) and panoramic radiography (85%) emerged as the most commonly utilized diagnostic tools, whereas cone beam computed tomography was the least frequently used method (13%). Use of Caries lesions classification and/or risk assessment systems

The proportion of participants using a caries lesions classification system is shown in Table 3. Only 23.1% of the participants reported using any caries lesions classification system. Specifically, 8.7% of the entire study population used the ICDAS or ICCMS system, 10% used the ADA system, and 4.4% used other systems. Similarly, only 15.2% reported using any caries risk assessment tools. Of these, 7.5% of the participants used the ICDAS or ICCMS system, 4.4% the ADA system, while 3.3% used other systems.

Discussion

This is the first study of its kind to investigate the preferred methods for caries detection and diagnosis of Ontario dentists and provide valuable insights into the methods and practices employed for the detection and diagnosis of dental caries. Our study highlighted the low utilization of caries lesions classification systems and caries risk assessment tools by Ontario dentists. This observation aligns with the broader trend of the utilization of CRA identified in the literature. The use of CRA is important as it integrates evidence-based approach while taking into consideration various medical, environmental, and social factors for caries diagnosis and management [22]. Cagetti et al. [22] went on to further emphasize that such integration is essential to providing individualized risk-based care for the management of dental caries. The lack of CRA utilization can also be interpreted as deficiency in employing a systematic and up-to-date approach to detecting and diagnosing caries lesions (such relying solely on conventional tools, including dental explorer for detecting dental caries) as highlighted in the results of the present study. This gap between evidence and practice highlights the importance of addressing barriers such as time constraints, lack of continuing education courses related to caries diagnosis, lack of obligatory attendance of these courses, and perceived complexity to facilitate the uptake of modern caries diagnostic approaches among dentists in Ontario and beyond. Dentists in group private practice were more likely to utilize the caries risk assessment systems. We hypothesize that this may be attributed to the comprehensive care provided by dentists in these settings. Given the opportunity to share knowledge and experience, along with effective communication among dentists in group setting, they are better equipped to deliver high-quality patient care. Utilizing the caries risk assessment system can allow dentists to accurately evaluate the prognosis and outcome of specific treatment plans [22].

Regarding the use of visual-tactile methods, many of the participants reported using air-drying to detect dental caries. Similar results were reported by Goel et al. [23], Gupta et al. [24], and Tagliaferro et al. [14], where they examined the effectiveness of air-drying and magnification methods to detect initial caries on occlusal surfaces. Visual-tactile caries lesions diagnosis requires clean teeth, good lighting, and air-drying the tooth surface. In addition to the removal of plaque, air-drying tooth surface is an important step as it allows for clearer visualization of any incipient lesions. Moisture on the tooth surface can obscure early signs of dental caries, making thorough drying imperative for accurate diagnosis.

For tactile detection, this study identified the prevalent use of dental explorer by participants for occlusal, interproximal, and cervical caries. Evidence suggests that the vigorous use of dental explorers can be harmful for the process of remineralization of early caries lesions [19]. Several studies have concluded the ‘disruption’ in the process of remineralization caused by the harmful effect of explorer as compared to other detection tools such a Marquis Probe and Ball-Ended Probe [1]. Furthermore, due to the low specificity of the explorer to detect caries lesions, the likelihood to over-diagnosis increases—as it can ‘stick’ in a deep pit and fissure without the true evidence of caries—is raised [20]. Studies have confirmed that a ‘sticking’ explorer is not necessarily indicative of decay and may be entirely due to local anatomic features [20]. This practice of applying pressure with an explorer has been called into question because of reported damage to surface integrity which may increase lesion susceptibility [10, 11].

The recent clinical guidelines by caries diagnosis and management systems such as ICCMS (2014), recommend using Ball Ended or Marquise Probe to detect carious lesions as an additional tool to visual assessment [14]. Overall, the contemporary caries diagnostic systems (e.g. ICDAS) are based mostly on visual assessment of the tooth surface and ball-ended probe can be utilized to remove tooth biofilm, if needed, or to confirm the presence of cavitation (micro-cavitation or frank cavitation) on the stage of lesion severity assessment. However, there was no explicit question in our survey regarding the use of a ball-ended probe to detect dental caries which is a limitation of this study.

Majority of the study participants reported the used of radiographs for the diagnosis of dental caries, as reflected in studies by Huysmans et al. [25] and Diniz et al. [26]. Results from a study conducted in the United States noted that radiographs are consistently and widely used for the diagnosis of proximal dental caries and less often for the diagnosis of occlusal dental caries [20]. This may be because the radiographic method has been reported as inadequate for the detection of occlusal surface caries until the lesion has advanced through the enamel and into the dentin [20]. False positives can also occur with radiographic diagnosis, and specificities of 66–98% have been recorded in an in vitro study [25]. However, studies have also reported high sensitivity values for radiographs when diagnosing proximal dental caries [7, 8]. Radiographs can be reliable for monitoring the progression of proximal dental caries lesions if a non-surgical treatment, such as fluoride therapy, is being used [20]. Nevertheless, radiographs in conjunction with the other risk assessment and diagnostic tools can be beneficial for comprehensive assessment of caries disease.

Only a small percentage of our respondents reported the use of additional diagnostic tools, including transillumnation and computer-aided design manufacturing, which is consistent with trends reported by Marwah and Gopakumar [27]. While there are documented limitations associated with the utilization of these tools, advantages exist when they are used comprehensively alongside subjective patient data (i.e., history). It was suggested that when meticulous visual-tactile caries diagnostic criteria are used to make treatment decisions, the availability of additional diagnostic information, particularly in low caries risk populations, may lead to a considerable number of false positive diagnoses and, consequently, increase overtreatment [28, 29]. The lack of adaptation to recent technological tools has also been reported by Cheuk et al. [30].

The results of this study emphasize the need for the thoughtful integration of evidence-based caries management approach in undergraduate and continuous dental education in Canada. Despite the presence of the national Core Cariology Curriculum in Canada, there is no evidence of its implementation and evaluation [31]. The results further highlighted the needs of developing up-to-date and robust competencies regarding the detection and diagnosis of dental caries. In Canadian dental education, essential competencies related to contemporary caries management approach such as visual-tactile caries lesions diagnosis, caries risk assessment, risk-based non-surgical and surgical treatments, and follow-ups planning needs to be well integrated in pre-clinical and clinical Cariology curriculums. It is also essential that the corresponding clinical credits for the students related to the aforementioned evidence-based caries management components are assigned. Additionally, there is a need for the better understanding of the barriers and facilitators of the integration of evidence-based caries management approach into dental practice. Lastly, this study highlighted the need for collaboration between the dental schools, regulatory and accreditation bodies, and insurance companies to promote and integrate CRA in dental education and private dental practices. Finally, continuing dental education programs should be made available in the realm of evidence-informed and risk-based caries diagnosis in Ontario and beyond.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. Firstly, the reliance on self-reported survey data introduces the potential for response bias and inaccuracies in reporting. Additionally, our low sample size, limits the generalizability of our findings to other parts of Canada and potentially overlook nuances that a multivariate analysis or imputation approach might have captured. Given the exploratory nature of this preliminary study, traditional reliability measures such as test-retest reliability, inter-rater reliability, and Cronbach’s alpha were not conducted. Instead, we focused on ensuring reliability through standardized data collection procedures (self-reported surveys). The insights gained from this study will guide the design of future research with more extensive reliability testing. Despite these limitations, the study represents an important first step towards understanding the current landscape of caries detection and risk assessment practices among dentists in Ontario and provides a foundation for future research and the development of continuing education initiatives in this geographical area.

Conclusion

This study showed minimal use of caries risk assessment tools by private dentists in Ontario.

This could be due to a lack of training pertinent to CRA in dental education, time constraints, or a lack of resources, including up-to-date technology. Visual-tactile caries detection using dental explorer was the preferred method, while majority of the participants relied on dental radiographs to confirm their diagnosis. Those results underscore the need for implementation of contemporary caries diagnosis and risk assessment approaches in private practices in Ontario and this requires collaboration with dental schools and local community practices. Further research is also warranted to explore the application of knowledge acquired by dental students in North American dental schools to dental practices.
