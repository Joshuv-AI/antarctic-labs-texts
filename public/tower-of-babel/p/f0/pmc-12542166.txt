Effects of mannitol on cardiac function and postoperative arrhythmias after coronary artery bypass grafting: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Masumeh Hemmati Maslakpak, Sohrab negargar, Ali Farbod, Solmaz Fakhari, Ahmadali Khalili, Eisa Bilejani, Vahid Alinejad, Amir Faravan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePostoperative cardiac dysfunction and arrhythmias are significant complications following cardiac surgery, frequently resulting in increased mortality, longer hospital stays, higher healthcare costs, and diminished patient quality of life. This study investigates the effects of mannitol on cardiac function and the incidence of postoperative arrhythmias after coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG).MethodsIn a single-center, double-blind, randomized controlled trial, ninety patients undergoing elective on-pump CABG were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either 200 mL of mannitol 20% (n = 45) or 200 mL of lactated Ringer’s solution (n = 45) during cardiopulmonary bypass priming. Postoperative assessments included cardiac enzyme levels (creatine phosphokinase, creatine kinase-MB isoenzyme, and cardiac troponin I), incidence of arrhythmias (atrial fibrillation, ventricular fibrillation, ventricular tachycardia), and left ventricular ejection fraction. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05.ResultsNo significant differences were observed between the mannitol and control groups in baseline demographics or clinical risk factors (p > 0.05). Left ventricular ejection fraction was similar between the groups (45.22 ± 9.82 in the mannitol group vs. 42.66 ± 7.94 in the control group; p = 0.178). Atrial fibrillation occurred in 9 of 45 patients (20%) in the mannitol group and 12 of 45 (26.7%) in the control group (p = 0.309). Ventricular tachycardia was observed in 2 of 45 (4.4%) vs. 4 of 45 (8.9%) patients, respectively (p = 0.338). Thirty-day mortality was 2 of 45 (4.4%) in the mannitol group and 1 of 45 (2.2%) in the control group (p = 0.50). Cardiac enzyme levels (CPK, CK-MB, and cTnI) showed no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). However, ventricular fibrillation occurred in 2 of 45 patients (4.4%) in the mannitol group versus 8 of 45 (17.8%) in the control group, a statistically significant difference (p = 0.045).ConclusionsIn conclusion, while mannitol was associated with a lower incidence of ventricular fibrillation, this is a single-center study with limited power to detect differences in other outcomes. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings.

Introduction

Coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) is the most common heart surgery performed worldwide [1], with over 370,000 CABG procedures conducted annually in the United States alone [2]. This surgical procedure improves the symptoms and prognosis of patients with multivessel disease and left main coronary artery involvement [1]. Thanks to advancements in surgical techniques, strategies, and equipment, the rate of complications associated with CABG is steadily decreasing [3]. Despite significant progress in cardiac surgery, the inherent risks of such procedures, particularly myocardial injury and postoperative complications, remain a concern. These complications can have serious implications for patient health and necessitate careful management and preventive strategies [4]. Among the strategies employed to reduce complications from cardiac surgery is the use of antioxidant medications before and during the procedure [5–7]. Mannitol, one such medication, is utilized for its antioxidant properties to mitigate oxidative stress induced by cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) [8]. Mannitol provides myocardial protection through multiple mechanisms, including modulation of mitochondrial K_ATP channels and its osmotic diuretic effect, which can reduce cardiac preload and afterload [9, 10]. Furthermore, a study by Shim et al. highlighted the beneficial effects of Mannitol in reducing cardiac enzyme levels [11].

However, some previous studies, mainly in neurosurgical settings, have reported complications such as arrhythmias following mannitol administration [12, 13]. Despite the routine use of mannitol in on-pump CABG for its potential cardioprotective and osmotic diuretic effects, there is a notable lack of high-quality evidence evaluating its specific impact on cardiac rhythm and myocardial injury.

Mannitol may also affect electrolyte balance, including hyperkalemia and hyponatremia, which can impact cardiac and cellular function; these electrolyte disturbances may lead to serious rhythm abnormalities and impair normal cardiac performance [14–18]. Nevertheless, it should be noted that findings from non-cardiac contexts, such as neurosurgery, may not be directly applicable to CPB procedures, as the physiological conditions and mechanisms of arrhythmogenesis differ substantially. Considering mannitol’s effects on fluid-electrolyte balance and its antioxidant properties, its intraoperative use may influence cardiac function and the incidence of postoperative arrhythmias in patients undergoing cardiac surgery. Based on these mechanisms, we hypothesized that priming the CPB circuit with mannitol would reduce postoperative arrhythmias and attenuate cardiac enzyme release compared to standard saline priming. Therefore, this randomized controlled trial was conducted to evaluate the effects of mannitol on cardiac function and postoperative arrhythmias after CABG.

Methods

This study was conducted as a randomized controlled clinical trial from December 2022 to May 2023 at the Shahid Madani Heart and Vascular Center in Tabriz. The trial was a double-blind, parallel design with an intention-to-treat approach, receiving ethical approval from the National Ethics Committee, and the study protocol was registered with the Iranian Clinical Trials Registry (IRCT20221129056660N1).

Patients aged 18 to 70 years who were candidates for elective on-pump CABG were screened for study eligibility. Inclusion criteria included normal kidney function, defined as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) greater than 60 ml/min, and a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of over 30%, as determined by preoperative transthoracic echocardiography. Exclusion criteria included any acute surgical procedures performed within 24 h prior to surgery, previous cardiac surgeries, a body mass index (BMI) greater than 40 kg/m², the need for deep hypothermia, documented allergic reactions, visual or auditory impairments, and any documented psychiatric disorders or dementia. After providing oral explanations of the study’s procedures, potential complications, benefits, and objectives, written informed consent was obtained from participants willing to take part in the study. The sample size estimation was based on the study by Ljunggren et al. [16], considering a power of 80% and a maximum type I error rate of 0.05, with an anticipated dropout rate of 25%. This study is a secondary analysis of a previously reported randomized controlled trial whose primary objective was to evaluate postoperative renal outcomes. The current analysis focuses on secondary cardiac outcomes, including arrhythmias and enzyme levels, which were prospectively recorded.

A post hoc power analysis for the secondary outcome of ventricular fibrillation (VF) was conducted, revealing a power of 55% (Cohen’s h = 0.43, n = 45 per group, α = 0.05), indicating the study was underpowered for VF. Approximately 85–100 patients per group would be needed for 80% power. This limitation is acknowledged, as the sample size was calculated for the primary renal endpoint. Consequently, 45 participants were allocated to each group (totaling 90 participants). We acknowledge this as a limitation, since postoperative arrhythmias were analyzed as secondary outcomes in the present study. Random allocation sequence was generated using random number tables. Ninety patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio using sequentially numbered sealed opaque envelopes to either the intervention or control group, resulting in 45 patients in each group. Prior to patient admission to the operating room, envelopes were opened by an individual solely responsible for preparing the priming solution and randomizing participants. Before surgery, 200 ml of a Mannitol 20% solution were prepared for the Mannitol group, while 200 ml of lactated Ringer’s solution were prepared for the control group; the bottles were covered with opaque tape to maintain blinding.

The pump prime for the Mannitol group comprised 200 ml of a Mannitol 20% solution, 900 ml of Ringer’s lactate solution, 50 ml of sodium bicarbonate, and 5000 units of sodium heparin. In contrast, the pump prime for the control group consisted of 1100 ml of Ringer’s lactate solution, supplemented with 50 ml of sodium bicarbonate and 5000 units of sodium heparin. Cardiopulmonary bypass was performed using the INSPIRE® membrane oxygenator in conjunction with a roller pump heart-lung machine (Stöckert S5®).

The study utilized non-pulsatile flow at a rate of 2.4 mL.m² and moderate hypothermia, maintaining a temperature range of 28–32 °C. The maximum allowable volume of fluid for compensating fluid deficit during cardiopulmonary bypass was set at 1000 ml of Ringer’s lactate solution. To ensure cardiac protection, tepid St. Thomas cardioplegia solution was administered. Throughout the procedure, the patient’s blood pressure was maintained between 50 and 80 mmHg, while the hematocrit level was kept around 24%.

Information regarding coronary artery disease severity (Left Main Coronary Artery involvement, 3-vessel disease, Syntax score) and preoperative cardiovascular medications (including beta-blockers, amiodarone, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and calcium channel blockers) was retrieved from patients’ medical records. Demographic information of patients, LVEF prior to surgery, and baseline cardiac enzyme levels including CPK, CK-MB, and CTNI were recorded before the operation. Surgical methods, anesthesia, perfusion, and postoperative management were routinely implemented for the patients. Throughout the study, the surgical, perfusion, and nursing teams remained blinded to the group assignments of the patients. After completing the surgery and achieving stable conditions, patients were transferred to the intensive care unit (ICU) and were extubated once they met appropriate criteria. Cardiac enzyme levels were measured at 30 min, 24 h, and 48 h post-operation using venous blood samples. Laboratory personnel responsible for measuring serum cardiac biomarkers (CPK, CK-MB, and cTnI) were blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Arrhythmias including atrial fibrillation (AF), ventricular fibrillation(VF), and ventricular tachycardia(VT) were monitored up to three days post-surgery. During ICU stay (24–48 h), patients underwent continuous bedside monitoring, and any suspected arrhythmias were confirmed by 12-lead ECG. After transfer to the general ward, daily 12-lead ECGs were performed, with additional ECGs obtained as clinically indicated. AF was defined as sustained irregular atrial rhythm lasting > 30 s. VT was defined as ≥ 3 consecutive wide QRS complexes > 100 bpm. VF was defined as a chaotic ventricular rhythm with loss of cardiac output requiring defibrillation. Only postoperative ventricular arrhythmias occurring after ICU admission were recorded. Intraoperative events, including transient VF following aortic unclamping, were not included in the analysis.

LVEF was assessed on postoperative day 5 via transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) by the same experienced cardiologist who performed the preoperative echocardiograms. High-quality images, enabling accurate LVEF assessment, were obtained in all patients, with measures taken to ensure patient comfort during the procedure. The variables reported in this study are secondary outcomes of a larger investigation, the primary variable of which has been published in two earlier articles [19, 20]. The hypothesis of this study was evaluated by assessing differences in postoperative cardiac enzyme levels, LVEF, and the occurrence of postoperative arrhythmias between the control group and the group that received Mannitol. The aim was to determine if Mannitol administration had any effect on these parameters, potentially leading to a more favorable postoperative course.

The researchers used the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test to assess whether the data distribution was normal or not. Depending on the outcome, independent t-tests, Chi-square tests, and Mann-Whitney U tests were applied to detect differences and compare the variables under investigation. Effect sizes were calculated using eta-squared (η²) with 95% confidence intervals. The threshold for statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05. No missing data were observed for cardiac enzyme measurements (CPK, CK-MB, and cTnI) at 30 min, 24 h, or 48 h post-operation, as all 90 patients completed the scheduled assessments. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test confirmed that cardiac enzyme data were normally distributed (p > 0.05), allowing the use of parametric tests such as repeated-measures ANCOVA without transformations. For repeated-measures ANCOVA, Mauchly’s test of sphericity was performed, confirming that the sphericity assumption was met for all biomarkers (p > 0.05); thus, no Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied. SPSS version 22 was used to analyze the data.

Figure 1 depicts the patient selection process. Recruitment, conducted from December 2022 to May 2023, continued until the target sample size was reached. Of the 107 initially eligible patients, three were excluded due to documented allergies, two due to a BMI exceeding 40 kg/m², eight were initially scheduled for elective surgery but underwent urgent CABG within 24 h due to hemodynamic deterioration or recurrent ischemic symptoms, and four due to confirmed psychiatric or dementia-related conditions. This careful methodology resulted in a strong sample size of 90 patients, evenly divided into 45 participants in the control group and 45 in the mannitol group, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Notably, no participants were excluded from the study after the intervention commenced, which further bolstered the reliability of the results. As shown in Table 3, the results of the chi-square test indicated no statistically significant difference in LVEF between the two groups (p = 0.178). Additionally, there was no significant difference in the occurrence of arrhythmias, including atrial fibrillation (9 of 45 [20%] in the mannitol group vs. 12 of 45 [26.7%] in the control group; p = 0.309) and ventricular tachycardia (2 of 45 [4.4%] vs. 4 of 45 [8.9%]; p = 0.338). In contrast, a statistically significant difference was observed in the incidence of ventricular fibrillation, which occurred in 2 of 45 patients (4.4%) in the mannitol group compared to 8 of 45 (17.8%) in the control group (p = 0.045). Furthermore, the chi-square test revealed no statistically significant difference in 30-day mortality between the groups (2 of 45 [4.4%] vs. 1 of 45 [2.2%]; p = 0.50). Although 30-day mortality appeared numerically lower in the mannitol group, this difference was not statistically significant, and the study was underpowered to detect differences in mortality.

As shown in Table 4, the repeated measures analysis demonstrated a significant effect of time on CPK levels (p < 0.001), indicating meaningful postoperative changes across time points. A significant group effect was also observed (p = 0.027), suggesting differences between the mannitol and control groups. However, the interaction between time and group was not significant (p = 0.124), indicating that the trend of CPK changes over time was similar in both groups. For CK-MB, there was also a significant effect of time (p < 0.001), reflecting changes across postoperative measurements. However, no significant group effect was detected (p = 0.972), and the time × group interaction was also non-significant (p = 0.602), suggesting similar patterns in both groups. Regarding cTnI, both the time effect (p = 0.026) and the group effect (p = 0.025) were statistically significant, indicating overall changes over time and differences between groups. Similar to the other markers, the interaction between time and group was not significant (p = 0.322), suggesting a parallel trend in both group. Based on partial η² values for the interaction term (Group × Time), the effect size was small for CPK (η² = 0.024) and cTnI (η² = 0.013), and negligible for CK-MB (η² = 0.005), according to Cohen’s criteria.

Table 5 presents the comparison of peak cardiac enzyme levels between the intervention (mannitol) and control groups, as well as their correlation with postoperative LVEF. The mean peak troponin level was higher in the intervention group (1.40 ± 2.08 ng/mL) compared to the control group (0.74 ± 0.77 ng/mL), with similar trends observed for CK-MB (53.95 ± 32.90 vs. 39.56 ± 28.22 ng/mL) and CPK (926.84 ± 1227.09 vs. 626.44 ± 505.14 U/L). However, no significant correlation was found between peak enzyme levels and postoperative LVEF. Pearson correlation coefficients were r = 0.042 (p = 0.697) for troponin, r = 0.140 (p = 0.189) for CPK, and r = 0.002 (p = 0.985) for CK-MB. These findings suggest that while enzyme elevations were numerically higher in the mannitol group, they did not predict short-term ventricular function after surgery.

Discussion

This study, a controlled randomized clinical trial, investigated the effect of mannitol on cardiac function and biomarkers following cardiac surgery. Outcome measurements focusing on cardiac enzyme levels and biomarkers, including arrhythmia, LVEF, mortality, CPK, CPK-MB, and cTnI, displayed varied results after surgery. While no significant differences were found between the groups for most arrhythmias, LVEF, and mortality, the incidence of ventricular fibrillation was higher in the control group compared to the mannitol group. This difference may be related to the effect of mannitol on electrolyte balance and the reduction of oxidative stress, which in turn helps improve sodium channel function and prevents cardiac conduction disturbances [21]. Although the overall incidence of VF (11.1%) was higher than expected, this likely reflects the use of continuous ICU monitoring and rigorous ECG documentation, allowing early detection of brief or self-limiting episodes. No cases of graft occlusion or mechanical complications were identified in these patients. While enzyme levels were elevated in some patients with postoperative VF, a direct correlation with myocardial ischemia due to graft or native vessel pathology could not be established, as no confirmatory imaging or angiography was routinely performed. The absolute risk reduction for ventricular fibrillation was 13.4%, corresponding to a number needed to treat (NNT) of approximately 7. This suggests that for every seven patients treated with mannitol during CPB priming, one case of postoperative ventricular fibrillation might be prevented. Although encouraging, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously given the limited sample size and secondary nature of the outcome.

However, previous studies contradict the findings of the present study, discussing the inductive effect of mannitol on the occurrence of fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia following its infusion [12, 13]. One of the proposed causes for these arrhythmias is hyperkalemia following mannitol administration. Mannitol infusion can induce hyperkalemia in patients, with men or patients with uncontrolled diabetes potentially being at higher risk for developing this phenomenon. High dose and rapid administration of mannitol may contribute to mannitol-induced hyperkalemia [13]. The causes of hyperkalemia following mannitol infusion are not completely clear, and multiple reasons have been suggested. One of the proposed causes is hemolysis of red blood cells [12]. Other proposed causes include bicarbonate dilution-induced acidosis and physiological responses to increased H + concentration [22]. Consequently, healthcare providers should vigilantly monitor for mannitol-induced hyperkalemia. When administering this medication, it is crucial to implement necessary precautions, including continuous ECG monitoring and regular serum electrolyte level measurements, particularly in patients with uncontrolled diabetes. The current study found no significant difference in the incidence of atrial fibrillation between the two groups after mannitol infusion. This result is consistent with previous research. Similarly, Binder et al. concluded that using mannitol as a priming solution does not lead to a statistically significant difference in the rate of postoperative atrial fibrillation when compared to albumin [23]. In contrast to the findings of this study, Dabrowski et al. noted that mannitol administration raises the risk of atrial fibrillation because it increases osmolarity and QTc interval [24]. The variations in study results regarding the effects of mannitol on cardiac arrhythmias following heart surgery can be attributed to several factors, including study design, sample size, type of surgery, patients’ clinical conditions, age, underlying diseases, and medications. Future studies should involve more detailed designs and longer follow-up periods to gain a clearer understanding of mannitol’s impact on cardiac function. Given the small number of events and the fact that the study was not powered to assess mortality, these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in larger studies.

The findings of the current study regarding cardiac biomarkers reveal their changes after surgery. Notably, the interaction between time and group was not statistically significant for any of these biomarkers, indicating that despite post-operative biomarker changes, the pattern of these changes was similar between the intervention and control groups. In contrast to the findings of the current study, Shim et al. reported in their research that using mannitol during off-pump CABG could help reduce significant increases in creatine kinase MB levels in patients. They suggested that the use of mannitol offers beneficial cardiac protective effects, which include maintaining oxygenation, facilitating earlier extubation, and shortening the length of stay in the intensive care unit [11]. Overall, there is limited evidence demonstrating a significant impact of mannitol on cardiac enzymes. The effects observed are likely secondary to its influence on factors such as blood osmolarity, hydration, and electrolyte balance.

Although reductions in cardiac enzyme levels may imply less myocardial injury, our analysis did not demonstrate a significant correlation between peak enzyme levels and postoperative LVEF. This suggests that, enzyme release may not directly reflect short-term ventricular function, and further studies are warranted to clarify this relationship. Although postoperative potassium levels were available from routine clinical care, they were not prospectively collected or measured at standardized intervals across all patients. As a result, these values were excluded from the statistical analysis. This limits our ability to directly evaluate electrolyte-mediated mechanisms, particularly hyperkalemia-related arrhythmias. Moreover, because arrhythmias were secondary outcomes in this study, the possibility of a Type II error cannot be excluded. Another important limitation is that the sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of a broader research project, not specifically powered for arrhythmia incidence.

In addition, the single-center nature of the study may introduce selection bias, and the relatively homogeneous patient population may limit the generalizability of the findings. Future studies should be conducted in multicenter settings, include more diverse patient populations, incorporate standardized postoperative electrolyte monitoring, and extend follow-up periods to capture long-term arrhythmia burden and comprehensively evaluate the impact of intraoperative mannitol administration.

Conclusion

This randomized controlled trial found that mannitol use during CPB was associated with a lower incidence of postoperative ventricular fibrillation, with no significant differences in other arrhythmias, enzyme levels, or mortality. Due to the study’s single-center design and limited sample size, these findings require cautious interpretation. Confirmation in larger, multicenter trials is needed, and mannitol may be considered in select patients pending further evidence.
