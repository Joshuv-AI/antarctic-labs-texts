Clinicopathological significance of Gli1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma: a meta-analysis
Authors: Sisi Li, Mengfan Chen, Shanshan Zhang, Zhong Xu, Hongling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis meta-analysis assessed the clinicopathological significance of GLI1 in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We systematically searched PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, Wan Fang, and CNKI for studies through October 2025. Studies assessing GLI1 by immunohistochemistry in HCC tissue and reporting its clinicopathological correlations were included. Study quality was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Publication bias and sensitivity analyses were performed, and subgroup analyses were conducted when > 4 studies were available. Analysis of ten studies (n = 974) showed significant GLI1 upregulation in HCC versus non-tumorous tissues (OR = 7.06, 95% CI 3.21–15.54, P < 0.0001). Elevated GLI1 was associated with intrahepatic metastasis (OR = 2.51, 95% CI 1.42–4.43, P = 0.002), vascular invasion (OR = 2.98, 95% CI 1.64–5.42, P < 0.001), hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection (OR = 2.32, 95% CI 1.28–4.20, P = 0.006). The significance of advanced pTNM stage (OR = 3.03, 95% CI 1.29–7.10, P = 0.011) disappeared after adjusting for publication bias (adjusted OR = 1.94, 95% CI 0.85–4.40). No significant associations were found with patient age, liver cirrhosis, serum AFP level, and tumor size. Findings are based on observational studies with heterogeneity in GLI1 assessment; evidence certainty for several outcomes is low or very low. These findings support the hypothesis that GLI1 might be involved in aggressive HCC phenotypes and warrant its investigation in future, standardized prospective studies to assess its potential clinical utility.Trial registration: The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD42024500731).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45769-7.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Gli1, Meta-analysis, Clinicopathological featuresSubject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Gastroenterology, Oncology

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks as the sixth most prevalent cancer globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality1. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) accounts for approximately 80% of all primary liver cancer cases2,3. Despite recent declines in HCC incidence in certain regions and advancements in diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, the overall survival (OS) rate of HCC patients remains unsatisfactory4–8. Emerging epidemiological data revealed shifting patterns in major HCC risk factors, underscoring the growing challenges in primary and secondary prevention, early detection, and clinical management of HCC3–5.Consequently, the identification of novel biomarkers has significant potential for improving HCC diagnosis, treatment strategies, and prognostic assessment.

The Hedgehog (Hh) signaling pathway was first identified by Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus in their seminal 1980 Drosophila melanogaster studies. This pathway features three downstream Gli transcription factors, with Gli1 serving as the most critical effector. Beyond its established roles in stem/progenitor cell maintenance, the Hh pathway plays fundamental roles in embryonic development and tissue regeneration9–11.

The canonical Hh signaling pathway is activated when the Hh ligand binds to its receptor Patched (Ptch), relieving the inhibitory effect of Ptch on Smoothened (Smo); subsequent Smo-mediated downstream signaling promotes the nuclear translocation of Gli1, which regulates the transcription of target genes (e.g., MMP9, VEGF, Snail) involved in cell proliferation, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)—key processes driving HCC progression12,13. Accumulating evidence has linked Aberrant activation of the Hh pathway and Gli1 overexpression to the pathogenesis of HCC. For instance, Gli1 can be transcriptionally activated by HBV X protein (HBx) in HBV-related HCC, enhancing the binding of HBx to the Gli1 promoter and promoting HCC cell migration14. These findings highlight Gli1’s potential as a critical mediator in HCC pathogenesis. Gli1 overexpression, in turn, promotes tumor cell proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, drives epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), enhances vascular invasion, and maintains cancer stem cell populations, thereby fueling hepatocarcinogenesis, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance15–18.

Notably, existing meta-analyses by Wang et al.19 and Cheng et al.20 primarily focused on the prognostic value of Hh pathway components or Gli1, without providing a comprehensive assessment of its associations with key clinicopathological characteristics (CPCs) in HCC. Moreover, the meta-analysis by Cheng et al., which evaluated Gli1 in solid malignancies, included only five HCC studies, all of which are encompassed in our current analysis. Existing studies on Gli1 in HCC patients have significant methodological limitations. For instance, some scholars failed to distinguish between hepatocellular carcinoma, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, and hepatoblastoma; misused relative risk (RR) as a statistical reporting metric; and conflated detection methods like immunohistochemistry (IHC) with reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)18. Therefore, a dedicated and methodologically rigorous synthesis focusing specifically on HCC is warranted. To address this gap, we conducted the first meta-analysis exclusively focusing on the correlation between GLI1 protein expression—detected by immunohistochemistry (IHC)—and CPCs in HCC, such as hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection status and vascular invasion. This approach fills the research gap regarding Gli1’s specific role in HCC.

The decision to exclude studies based on mRNA detection (e.g., RT-PCR) and exclusively select IHC-based studies was made for three key reasons: first, IHC is the most widely used method in clinical pathological practice, directly detecting Gli1 protein expression in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues—consistent with the clinical application-oriented goal of identifying actionable biomarkers; second, mRNA expression does not always correlate with protein expression due to post-transcriptional regulation (e.g., translation inhibition, protein degradation), and focusing on protein levels avoids the misleading conclusions caused by this discrepancy21. Therefore, our IHC-only approach provides a more clinically relevant and reliable assessment of Gli1’s role in HCC.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

Our systematic search identified 1667 potentially relevant articles from the databases. Included studies comprised peer-reviewed articles and academic theses. Theses were considered only if they provided unique data not available in published articles and met quality criteria24,25. After removing duplicates and screening titles, abstracts, and full texts against our predetermined criteria, 10 studies comprising 692 unique hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) specimens with immunohistochemically detected Gli1 expression were included in the final quantitative synthesis. The study selection process is summarized in the flow diagram in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement (Fig. 1). The included studies, published between 2009 and 2019, were predominantly conducted in China (n = 8), with a single study from North Korea and Japan respectively. Sample sizes ranged from 32 to 200. The baseline characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. The methodological quality of the included studies, assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), is summarized in Table 1. The overall quality was moderate to high, with the NOS scores ranging from 6 to 9. Eight studies were rated as high quality (NOS score ≥ 7). Gli1 protein is significantly overexpressed in HCC tissues

A meta-analysis of seven studies, encompassing 974 samples (comprising paired HCC and adjacent non-tumorous tissues), was performed to compare Gli1 protein levels between HCC and adjacent non-tumorous liver tissues. The pooled analysis suggested a substantial increase in Gli1 expression in HCC tissues, with a random-effects odds ratio (OR) of 7.06 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 3.21–15.54; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2a). However, this finding was characterized by substantial heterogeneity (I² = 82.5%), and the certainty of evidence was rated as very low according to the GRADE framework, primarily due to this inconsistency. Sensitivity analysis revealed that the significant association was robust to the exclusion of any single study. Notably, the substantial heterogeneity was largely driven by the study by Chen et al. (2010), as its exclusion reduced the I² statistic from 85.3% to 30.6%, although the point estimate remained significant and elevated (OR = 4.85, 95% CI: 3.04–7.73). No significant publication bias was detected by Egger’s test (p = 0.9838).

Association between Gli1 expression and aggressive clinicopathological features

Gli1 overexpression showed statistical associations with several features suggestive of HCC aggressiveness and metastatic potential (Table 2). Specifically, significant associations were observed with intrahepatic metastasis (3 studies; fixed-effect OR = 2.51, 95% CI: 1.42–4.43; p = 0.002), vascular invasion (3 studies; fixed-effect OR = 2.98, 95% CI: 1.64–5.42; p = 0.0003) (Fig. 2b,c) and high tumor grade(3 studies; fixed-effect OR = 2.00, 95% CI: 1.08–3.70; p = 0.028).Notably, very large effect sizes were observed for distant metastasis (3 studies; random-effects OR = 10.12, 95% CI: 3.12–32.86; p = 0.0001), and lymph node metastasis (2 studies; fixed-effect OR = 9.06, 95% CI: 2.54–32.35; p = 0.0007) (Fig. S1a–c).

Regarding tumor stage, Gli1 expression was statistically higher in patients with advanced pTNM stage (III/IV) than in those with early-stage (I/II) disease (5 studies; random-effects OR = 3.03, 95% CI: 1.29–7.10; p = 0.011) (Fig. 2d). However, the robustness of this finding is seriously challenged by substantial heterogeneity among the included studies (I² = 53.5%). After adjustment using the Trim and Fill method, which imputed 2 theoretical studies, the association was no longer statistically significant (adjusted OR = 1.939, 95% CI: 0.854–4.402), indicating that the initial significant result is not reliable.

Association with etiological and baseline patient characteristics

The association between Gli1 expression and hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection was examined in 6 studies. The fixed-effect meta-analysis revealed a positive association (OR = 2.32, 95% CI: 1.28–4.20; p = 0.0057) (Fig. 2e). Egger’s test indicated no publication bias (P = 0.1976) (Fig. S2a). Similarly, an association was observed between Gli1 expression and male gender (7 studies; fixed-effect OR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.27–3.92; p = 0.005) (Fig. S2d). This analysis showed no heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%) or publication bias (Egger’s p = 0.19) (Fig. S2e).

Features not associated with Gli1 expression

In contrast to its association with disease progression, Gli1 expression was not significantly correlated with several other clinicopathological parameters. Analyses yielding non-significant results with low heterogeneity included patient age (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.47–1.10), serum AFP levels (OR = 1.25, 95% CI: 0.74–2.10), and liver cirrhosis (OR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.37–1.07), as these analyses exhibited negligible heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%) and robust sensitivity analyses (Fig. S3a–c).

Conversely, other non-significant associations were less stable, characterized by substantial heterogeneity and high sensitivity in analyses. These included tumor size (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.40–2.44) and differentiation (OR = 3.54, 95% CI: 0.82–15.14) which were unstable, being characterized by substantial heterogeneity and high sensitivity to the removal of individual studies. Similarly, exploratory analyses of capsular and portal vein invasion showed non-significant results with very wide confidence intervals (Fig. S3d–g). The certainty of evidence for these outcomes was graded as very low (Supplementary Table 1).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

To systematically evaluate the robustness of our findings, we conducted comprehensive sensitivity analyses for all outcomes (Supplementary Table 2). Key findings demonstrated that the associations of HBV infection, intrahepatic metastasis, vascular invasion (Fig. S4a–f), distant metastasis, and male gender were highly stable, with consistent effect estimates and statistical significance across all analytical approaches. Conversely, several outcomes demonstrated notable sensitivity in our analyses. The strong association observed for ‘HCC vs Normal Tissue’ (Fig. S4g,h) was accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (I² = 85.3%). This heterogeneity was primarily driven by the study by Chen J 2010, as its exclusion reduced the I² to 30.6%; however, the effect estimate remained statistically significant and elevated. Furthermore, the overall null finding for ‘Tumor Differentiation’ was unstable, as the application of a fixed-effect model or the exclusion of Liu J 2009 each rendered the association statistically significant while simultaneously resolving the heterogeneity. In contrast, the null associations for patient age, serum AFP level, and cirrhosis proved highly stable across all sensitivity analyses, which strengthens the evidence for a genuine lack of association. Although the null finding for tumor size was also consistent in direction, it was derived from analyses with high heterogeneity and therefore warrants cautious interpretation.

Subgroup analyses revealed that GLI1 positivity criteria significantly influenced the association with advanced pTNM stage (p for subgroup difference = 0.0068). Studies using standard criteria showed modest association (OR = 1.84, 95% CI: 0.91–3.72), while those with unclear criteria demonstrated substantially stronger effects (OR = 7.41, 95% CI: 2.97–18.51). In contrast, GLI1 overexpression in tumor tissue remained consistent across all positivity criteria subgroups (p = 0.3696).Sample size stratification showed no significant subgroup differences for either tumor tissue comparison (p = 0.9396) or pTNM staging (p = 0.6024), with consistent effect directions across subgroups. Geographic region analysis similarly revealed no significant differences, suggesting that the findings may be generalizable to populations beyond the Chinese population that constituted most studies. (Fig. S5a–e) These analyses inform the potential utility of GLI1 as an HCC biomarker of GLI1 as an HCC biomarker while underscoring the importance of standardized positivity criteria, particularly for staging-related assessments. Detailed subgroup analysis results are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Publication bias (Supplementary Table 4), as assessed by Egger’s test, was not evident for most key comparisons, including HCC vs. normal tissue, HBV Infection, gender, age and cirrhosis. However, significant publication bias was detected for pTNM stage (p = 0.018). After Trim and Fill adjustment, the association for pTNM stage was no longer statistically significant (OR = 1.94, 95% CI: 0.85–4.40), indicating that the original findings for this outcome may have been influenced by publication bias. Funnel plots and Egger’s test results for all other outcomes are provided in the Supplementary Materials (Fig. S6a–g).

Quality of the evidence (GRADE)

We evaluated the certainty of evidence for all outcomes using the GRADE approach (Supplementary Table 1). All observational studies started as low certainty evidence, with further downgrading based on risk of bias, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias. The evidence was graded as low certainty for vascular invasion, intrahepatic metastasis, tumor grade, HBV infection, gender, and age, as these outcomes maintained the initial low certainty rating without additional serious limitations. Several outcomes were downgraded to very low certainty due to multiple limitations. HCC versus normal tissue and distant metastasis was downgraded one level for very serious inconsistency (I² = 82.5%) and (I² = 52.0%). Lymph node metastasis, pTNM stage, serum AFP level, and cirrhosis were downgraded for serious imprecision, with confidence intervals including the null effect. Critically, this ‘very low certainty’ rating applies to several outcomes with notably large point estimates in our meta-analysis (e.g., distant metastasis [OR = 10.12], lymph node metastasis [OR = 9.06], and HCC versus normal tissue [OR = 7.06]). This underscores that while these large magnitude associations are statistically significant, their true effect is uncertain and may differ substantially from our estimates.

Most notably, tumor size, differentiation, capsular invasion, and portal vein invasion were downgraded two levels for very serious imprecision and inconsistency, reflecting extremely wide confidence intervals and substantial heterogeneity (I² > 60%), also resulting in very low certainty. These ratings indicate that while associations between GLI1 expression and several clinically relevant outcomes are suggested, the true effects may differ substantially from these estimates, particularly for outcomes graded as very low certainty. Further high-quality studies are needed to strengthen the evidence base, especially for outcomes affected by imprecision and heterogeneity.

Discussion

This meta-analysis provides a focused assessment of the relationship between GLI1 protein expression and clinicopathological features in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), employing a methodologically rigorous, IHC-based approach. Our findings elucidate both the significant associations and the specific limitations of GLI1 as a biomarker, offering a clear direction for future research.

Core findings and biological implications

The present analysis suggests that GLI1 protein is significantly overexpressed in HCC tissues compared to adjacent non-tumorous tissues, underscoring the possible fundamental role of the Hh/GLI1 signaling axis in hepatocarcinogenesis24, although with very low certainty of evidence (OR = 7.06). However, it is important to note that the certainty of this evidence is very low due to substantial heterogeneity (I² = 85.3%), and sensitivity analysis revealed that the exclusion of a single study by Chen et al. (2010) reduced heterogeneity to 30.6% while the association remained statistically significant (OR = 4.85, 95% CI: 3.04–7.73). Thus, while consistent across studies, this finding should be considered preliminary and warrants validation in standardized cohorts.

GLI1 expression was closely linked to aggressive tumor phenotypes, but the strength of evidence varies. The results showed statistical associations with intrahepatic metastasis (OR = 2.51, P = 0.002), vascular invasion (OR = 2.98, P < 0.001), while the evidence was low-certainty. Notably, the very large effect sizes for distant metastasis (OR = 10.12) and lymph node metastasis (OR = 9.06) are based on very low-certainty evidence, making these estimates highly uncertain. These findings are biologically plausible, as GLI1 regulates downstream targets such as MMP9, VEGF, and Snail, which are implicated in invasion, angiogenesis, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)—key processes driving HCC progression.

The association with advanced pTNM stage is based on very low-certainty evidence and requires utmost caution. Although the initial meta-analysis yielded a statistically significant odds ratio (OR = 3.03), this finding proved to be methodologically fragile. The detection of significant publication bias, and the subsequent loss of statistical significance after adjustment, suggest that the observed effect size may have been inflated by the selective publication of positive studies. Consequently, the available evidence is insufficient to support a reliable association between Gli1 and advanced pTNM stage. This outcome underscores the critical influence of publication bias on meta-analytic conclusions, especially for outcomes with a limited number of included studies.

Separately, GLI1 expression showed no significant associations with patient age (low-certainty evidence; OR = 0.72, P = 0.127), serum AFP level (very low-certainty evidence; OR = 1.25, P = 0.495), or liver cirrhosis (very low-certainty evidence; OR = 0.63, P = 0.085). These null findings were characterized by low heterogeneity and robustness in sensitivity analyses, suggesting that GLI1 reflects tumor aggressiveness rather than general liver dysfunction or baseline patient characteristics. However, the very low certainty for these outcomes limits the strength of this conclusion. In contrast, associations with tumor size (OR = 0.99, P = 0.980) and differentiation (OR = 3.54, P = 0.090), both based on very low-certainty evidence, were unstable due to high heterogeneity, precluding any definitive interpretation and necessitating further investigation.

We also observed important etiological and demographic correlations with low certainty of evidence. The observed association between GLI1 expression and HBV infection (OR = 2.32) is consistent with and lends observational support to established mechanistic links, such as the transcriptional activation of GLI1 by the HBx protein14. Furthermore, male patients showed higher GLI1 expression (OR = 2.23), aligning with well-documented gender disparities in HCC incidence. While speculative and requiring mechanistic validation, this may be partly explained by the opposing effects of sex hormone pathways—where estrogen signaling is often protective and androgen signaling may promote tumorigenesis25,26. Our results suggest that differential activation of the Hh/GLI1 axis could be one of multiple factors contributing to this disparity, warranting further mechanistic exploration.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It is the first meta-analysis to focus exclusively on IHC-detected GLI1 protein expression in HCC, enhancing clinical relevance and avoiding potential discrepancies between mRNA and protein levels. Adherence to PRISMA guidelines, comprehensive sensitivity analyses, and pre-specified subgroup investigations further strengthen the validity of our findings. Moreover, geographical subgroup analysis showed consistent effects across populations, supporting the generalizability of the results.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. The most fundamental limitation stems from the study design itself: all included studies were observational. Consequently, the initial certainty of evidence for all outcomes was rated as low according to the GRADE framework, and was further downgraded to very low for several key outcomes due to inconsistency (high heterogeneity) and imprecision (wide confidence intervals). This is critically important when interpreting outcomes with large magnitude effect estimates, such as distant metastasis (OR = 10.12), where the very low certainty indicates the true effect may differ substantially. Thus, any clinical implications remain speculative and require prospective validation.

Second, methodological heterogeneity, particularly the lack of a standardized definition for Gli1 positivity, limits the certainty and clinical translatability of our pooled estimates. Our subgroup analysis revealed that the impact of this heterogeneity had differential impacts: while it did not significantly alter the conclusion of Gli1 overexpression in HCC (p for subgroup difference = 0.3696), it critically influenced the association with advanced pTNM stage (p = 0.0068). The high observed heterogeneity for the comparison of Gli1 expression between HCC and adjacent tissues (I² = 85.3%) is a direct reflection of this methodological diversity, fundamentally challenging the derivation of a single, clinically translatable effect estimate.

Third, the precision and robustness of our estimates are constrained by the limited available literature. Several clinically salient associations—most notably distant metastasis (OR = 10.12) and lymph node metastasis (OR = 9.06)—were based on only two or three studies, resulting in very low-certainty evidence. The instability of the intrahepatic metastasis association upon sensitivity analysis is a case in point. Similarly, the small number of studies limits the power of our publication bias assessments, as noted in the Methods.

Fourth, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the geographic concentration of the included evidence, which predominantly originated from China (8 of 10 studies). Although our subgroup analysis did not detect statistically significant regional differences, this imbalance in data sources remains a key limitation, as the etiological profile (e.g., predominant role of HBV) and clinical management of HCC in China may not fully represent the diversity seen in other global populations.

Fifth, the assessment of publication bias has inherent limitations. For outcomes with few studies, Egger‘s test has low statistical power. Therefore, undetected publication bias is possible and may have led to overestimated effect sizes. This concern is illustrated by the pTNM stage outcome, where the significant association disappeared after bias adjustment, serving as a cautionary example for other findings based on few studies.

Finally, our analysis is limited by its scope: it addresses clinicopathological correlations but does not assess the prognostic value of Gli1 expression (e.g., for survival). Since prognostic utility is paramount for clinical translation, this represents a crucial gap for future research.

In conclusion, within the constraints of the current low-certainty evidence, our analysis synthesizes observations that are consistent with the hypothesis of Gli1 involvement in aggressive HCC. Translating this hypothesis into a validated biomarker is entirely contingent upon addressing the limitations outlined above through standardized, large-scale, prospective studies that include prognostic evaluation.

Future research directions

Based on the limitations and uncertainties identified in this synthesis, future research should prioritize several interconnected avenues to clarify the role of GLI1 in HCC. First, prospective, multi-center studies using standardized and validated IHC protocols are needed to confirm the clinicopathological utility of GLI1. Second, the mechanistic interaction between GLI1 and HBx in HBV-related hepatocarcinogenesis warrants deeper investigation. Third, future studies should evaluate the potential additive or synergistic value of combining GLI1 with established markers (e.g., AFP, PIVKA-II) for diagnostic or prognostic stratification.Fourth, given the preliminary but notable link between GLI1 and metastasis, evaluating the therapeutic hypothesis of targeting the Hh pathway in advanced HCC, contingent upon prior validation of GLI1’s role. Finally, and critically, this analysis highlights a fundamental gap: the prognostic value of GLI1 remains unassessed. Therefore, large-scale studies should assess the prognostic value of GLI1 and its potential role in guiding personalized treatment strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, this meta-analysis indicates that GLI1 protein is overexpressed in HCC tissues. It further synthesizes observational evidence suggesting this elevated expression is associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, while concurrently documenting the very low certainty of this evidence base. The findings lend support to the prevailing hypothesis that GLI1 contributes to metastatic spread and vascular invasion in HCC, and reveal an observed correlation with HBV infection. Critically, the initially observed association with advanced pTNM stage failed to withstand statistical adjustment for publication bias, underscoring the fragility of this particular finding. In contrast, no reliable association was found between GLI1 expression and several baseline characteristics, such as patient age or liver cirrhosis status. It is crucial to emphasize that the most striking associations—particularly those with large magnitude effect sizes—are derived from evidence graded as of low or very low certainty and must therefore be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating. Consequently, the translation of GLI1 into a clinically useful biomarker or therapeutic target is not supported by the existing literature but is entirely contingent upon addressing the key limitations identified herein. Future efforts must prioritize standardized analytical validation, prospective study designs, and explicit evaluation of prognostic utility to rigorously define any potential clinical role for GLI1 in HCC management.

Materials and methods

Selection criteria

The inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis were as follows: (1) studies involving patients with a clinicopathological diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), regardless of race, nationality, or age; (2) studies that assessed GLI1 protein expression specifically in HCC tissue using immunohistochemistry (IHC); (3) studies that provided sufficient data on clinicopathological features related to GLI1 expression, allowing for the calculation of odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs); and (4) case-control or cohort studies. The exclusion criteria were: (1) duplicate publications or multiple reports based on the same dataset, in which case the most comprehensive version was retained; (2) studies with an imprecise research design or a sample size of fewer than 20 patients; (3) studies that failed to report critical outcome measures or from which essential data could not be extracted or calculated; and (4) non-clinical study types, including in vitro experiments, bioinformatics analyses, case reports, conference abstracts, and systematic reviews. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the study selection process, two investigators independently reviewed and screened all identified records against these criteria.

Literature search strategy

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA guidelines27. We conducted a systematic search of the PubMed, Web of Science databases, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases, Wan Fang, CNKI from their inception to 2025-10-5,using combinations of keywords: (“Gli-1[Title/Abstract]” OR “Gli-1 protein, human[Title/Abstract]” OR “Glioma-associated oncogene-1[Title/Abstract]” OR “Zinc Finger Protein GLI1[Mesh]”) AND (“Liver Cell[Title/Abstract]” OR “hepatic[Title/Abstract]” OR “hepatocellular[Title/Abstract]”) AND (“Tumors[Title/Abstract]” OR “Neoplasia[Title/Abstract]” OR “Cancer[Title/Abstract]” OR “Carcinoma[Title/Abstract]”).

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two investigators (S.L. and M.C.) independently screened the studies. Any discrepancies were resolved through a consensus discussion. When a consensus could not be reached, a third senior investigator (Z.X.) arbitrated to make the final decision. The same two investigators independently extracted data using a standardized, pilot-tested form. For studies with incomplete data, we contacted authors to obtain full datasets. If multiple publications existed for the same cohort, we selected the version with the most complete data. The extracted data included: first author’s name, publication year, country of origin, sample size, Gli1 detection methodology, Gli1 positivity criteria, and relevant clinicopathological parameters. The methodological quality of each included study was assessed independently by two reviewers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-randomized studies. Two reviewers (S.L. and M.C.) performed the assessments independently. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consultation with a third senior reviewer (Z.X.). Studies with a score of ≥ 7 were considered high quality. The standardized data extraction form used in this study is available in Supplemental Digital Content 7.xlsx.

Statistical analysis

The meta-analysis was performed using Rstudio (version 4.2; URL: https://posit.co/products/open-source/rstudio/) software. We calculated pooled odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all clinicopathological outcomes. Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, with values < 50% indicating low heterogeneity. A fixed-effects model was employed in the absence of significant heterogeneity; otherwise, a random-effects model was utilized. To assess the robustness of the findings, we conducted sensitivity analyses(n > 2), primarily through leave-one-out analysis and model sensitivity analysis (by comparing the results derived from fixed-effect and random-effects models). The robustness of the findings for all outcomes was systematically evaluated and is summarized in Supplementary Table S4. Potential publication bias was assessed(n > 4) through funnel plots and formal statistical testing using Egger’s test. We also planned to use the trim-and-fill method to adjust for potential publication bias if indicated. Substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%) prompted the use of subgroup analyses to investigate its underlying causes. Subgroup analyses were performed(n > 4) based on Gli1 Positivity Criterion, study sample size(stratified by the median for each outcome) and geographic region to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. The quality of evidence for each outcome was evaluated using the GRADE tool28. Consistent with the GRADE system, all included studies were observational, so an initial certainty rating of ‘low’ was assigned to each outcome (e.g., Vascular Invasion, Intrahepatic Metastasis) before evaluating downgrading factors. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value < 0.05.
