Unraveling the Mechanism of Perillyl Alcohol and Its Derivatives as Potent Antifungal Agents against Gray Mold
Authors: Shengnan Shen, Rongyu Zhang, Bo Fang, Jintao Jiang, Keyu Liu, Guanbo Wang, Bing Gong, Wenwen Liu, Yan Luo, Jiangping Meng, Delong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungicides are often limited by poor efficacy and high toxicity. Our previous research identified perillyl alcohol (POH) as a promising plant-derived fungicide. Here, POH exhibits antifungal activity by inducing metacaspase-independent apoptosis in Botrytis cinerea. Furthermore, POH triggers both nonselective and selective autophagy, particularly endoplasmic reticulum autophagy (ER-phagy) and mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy). Ubiquitylation analysis revealed that POH promotes protein ubiquitination. Additionally, transcriptome analysis revealed the suppression of Na/K-ATPase-related gene expression and activity. Importantly, we identified BcBet4 as a direct molecular target of POH, with binding interactions contributing to its mode of action. Through systematic chemical modifications, we developed POH-10, a derivative exhibiting enhanced antifungal efficacy and a stronger binding affinity to BcBet4. These findings provide valuable insights for the development of novel antifungal agents. As a multitarget fungicide, POH holds significant potential for economically and ecologically sustainable crop disease management.

Introduction

Botrytis cinerea, a highly destructive phytopathogen, poses significant threats to global agriculture by infecting over 240 plant species and causing substantial economic losses through both pre- and postharvest infections. Current gray mold control relies heavily on synthetic fungicides, with large quantities being applied to prevent infection and reduce postharvest damage. However, fungicide overuse has resulted in the emergence of resistant fungal strains, compounding disease management challenges.

To address the above issues, natural products demonstrating antimicrobial properties have gained increasing scientific attention as potential alternatives. − Among these products are essential oils (EOs), volatile aromatic liquids of plant origin that serve as bioactive constituents of phytotherapy. As secondary metabolites, EOs have a natural role in plant defense against microorganisms. They and their constituents have exhibited diverse mechanisms of action in combating the pathogens. For instance, thyme EO exerts fungicidal effects through membrane disruption, specifically by altering lipid bilayer fluidity and permeability, ultimately changing fungal morphology. , Similarly, tea tree EO exhibits antimicrobial activity by disrupting bacterial biofilm, damaging the plasma membrane and mitochondria, and limiting the production of volatile sulfur compounds. , Notably, linalool has been shown to impair cell membrane function by reducing membrane potential, destroy cell wall integrity by decreasing the cellular activity of alkaline phosphatase, disrupt cellular energy synthesis by reducing key enzymatic activity in the TAC cycle, and affect plasma membrane function by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase expression. , The application of plant EO is an effective approach to avoiding the development of fungal resistance, compared to conventional synthetic fungicides.

Perillyl alcohol (POH), a natural monoterpene alcohol isolated from plant EOs, has emerged as a promising therapeutic candidate owing to its demonstrated anticancer properties. Indeed, it has been postulated that the anticancer activity of POH is correlated with reduced isoprenylation of Ras and Ras-related proteins, leading to restricted cell proliferation. Notably, statins or POH inhibit respiratory syncytial virus infection through interference with Rho GTPase prenylation and activity. Garcia reported that POH specifically inhibits the α1 subunit of Na/K-ATPase in glioblastoma cells, thereby regulating cell proliferation and/or cell death. Ansari discovered that POH, as a potent glyoxylate cycle inhibitor, binds to the active sites of isocitrate lyase (ICL1) and malate synthase (MLS1), resulting in inhibition of ICL1 and MLS1 mRNA expressions. While these mechanisms have been well characterized in animal systems, their implications in plant–pathogen interactions remain unexplored.

POH is effective against periodontal pathogens such as Fusobacterium nucleatum and Porphyromonas gingivalis while maintaining noncytotoxic properties in murine macrophages. It disrupts calcineurin-mediated signaling, compromises cell wall integrity, and suppresses virulence factors including morphogenetic transitions and biofilm formation in Candida species. POH has also been reported to have antifungal activity on important agricultural pathogens. Previously, we found that POH confers resistance to B. cinerea in plants by upregulating both jasmonic acid levels and kaempferol biosynthesis and has broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties, inhibiting fungal growth by disrupting cell membranes. Although the precise antifungal mechanism of POH remains unclear, it is hypothesized to involve cell membrane disruption and the reduction of isoprenylated Ras and Ras-related proteins. The aim of this work was to verify these hypotheses and investigate the antifungal mechanism of POH against plant pathogens. Here, we demonstrate that POH triggers metacaspase-independent apoptosis, enhances the autophagy and protein degradation, and reduces both the activity and expression level of ATPase. Furthermore, POH directly binds to geranylgeranyl transferase BcBet4. These multitarget effects collectively contribute to its antifungal activity, suggesting considerable potential for broader application.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Fungal Strains

Perillyl alcohol (CAS Registry No. 18457-55-1) and BioTracker 640 Red C2 (FM4-64) synaptic dye were acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MI, U.S.A.). 2′, 7′-Dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), 4′, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), Annexin V-FITC kit, and Fluo-3/AM were acquired from Beyotime Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

The B. cinerea strain B05.10, fluorescently labeled strains (BcRtn1-GFP, BcIlv2-GFP, BcGFP-SKL, BcTaf14-GFP, and GFP-BcAtg8), and mutants (ΔBcMac1, ΔBcMac1, and ΔBcMac1Mac2) were grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA) at 25 °C as previously described.

Fluorescence Microscopy

After being cultured for 7 days on PDA, the conidia were washed down, collected, and diluted to 4.6 × 104 conidia/mL. Conidia were cultivated in a liquid YEPD medium at 25 °C in a shaker at 100 rpm. After 24 h of cultivation, the suspension was divided into two groups: one treated with H2O and the other with POH (200 μg/mL). After 4 more hours of incubation under the same conditions, the suspension was examined under a fluorescence microscope. Intracellular ROS accumulation was assessed by staining with 10 μM 2, 7-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCHF-DA). Phosphatidylserine (PS) exposure was monitored via Fourier infrared transform chromatography (FITC)-coupled Annexin V staining according to the kit protocol. The intracellular Ca2+ levels were observed using the nonfluorescent dye Fluo-3/AM. All probes and kits were used in accordance with the manufacturers’ instructions.

To examine the effect of POH on autophagy, selected GFP-tagged strains (BcRtn1-GFP, BcIlv2-GFP, BcGFP-SKL, BcTaf14-GFP, and GFP-BcAtg8) were grown in a liquid YEPD medium for 24 h and then transferred to another YEPD medium with POH (200 μg/mL) for further 4 h. The mycelia were stained with FM 4–64 and were examined by a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Transmission Electron Microscopy

To study autophagy and plasma membrane morphology, conidia were collected and cultured in a liquid YEPD medium for 24 h. POH (200 μg/mL) was then added to the conidial suspension for an additional 4 h. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using the method described by Wang. Following initial fixation in 2.5% (v/v) glutaraldehyde and 3% (v/v) paraformaldehyde, mycelia were postfixed in cacodylate buffer. After dehydration by ethanol series, samples were embedded in resin, sectioned, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The ultrastructure was finally examined and imaged using a JEM-1230 transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan).

Transcriptome Analysis

Following 24 h of growth in a YEPD medium, the conidial suspension was aliquoted and treated for 4 h with either POH (200 μg/mL) or an equal volume of H2O (control). Total RNA was obtained with the TRIzol reagent (Magen, Guangzhou, China). RNA quality and concentration were assessed on a NanoDrop ND-2000 system (Thermo Scientific, U.S.A). RNA sequencing was performed with 200-bp paired-end reads on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between POH-treated and H2O-treated B05.10 were determined using DESeq2. Genes with |log2 fold change| > 2 and a P-value <0.05 were considered to be differentially expressed. The functional profile of these DEGs was interrogated through GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using clusterProfiler R. The transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis were carried out by APTBIO Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Ubiquitinome Analysis

Mycelia were collected and treated with or without POH, as detailed in the ‘Fluorescence microscopy’ section. After lysing the mycelial samples in SDT buffer, the total protein was extracted from the lysate. Protein quantification was carried out using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, U.S.A). Following separation and trypsin digestion, lysine-ubiquitinated peptides were enriched using a PTMScan Ubiquitin Remnant Motif (K-ε-GG) Kit. The enriched peptides were then subjected to LC–MS/MS analysis over a 120 min gradient on a Q Exactive mass spectrometer coupled to an Easy nLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Sequences of the differentially expressed proteins were searched locally using the NCBI BLAST + client software (ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/executables/blast+/LATES), followed by gene ontology (GO) terms and sequence annotation through the online program Blast2GO (https://www.blast2go.com). Subsequently, the gene and pathway annotations were further classified using the KEGG (http://geneontology.org/).

Obtaining Knockout Mutants

To construct the gene knockout vector pBcBet4, a fusion PCR method was employed. Briefly, three DNA fragments were amplified and fused into a single fragment, including a 1 kb fragment upstream homology arm, the HPH gene coding sequence, and a 1 kb fragment downstream homology arm. This fragment was then introduced into B. cinerea protoplasts via PEG-mediated transformation, as previously described. The transformants were selected on PDA containing 100 μg/mL hygromycin. Then, the mutants were confirmed by PCR to determine HPH and the target gene BcBet4. Finally, single conidial isolates were obtained by plating conidial suspensions on the selective media and transferring single colonies to new plates.

Protein Extraction and Western Blotting

The strains overexpressing specific genes (BcGFP-SKL, GFP-BcAtg8, BcRtn1-GFP, and BcIlv2-GFP) were cultured in a liquid YEPD medium for 24 h at 120 rpm in a shaker, followed by treatment with POH for an additional 4 h as previously described. The mycelia were harvested and suspended in a protein extraction buffer. Proteins from each strain were resolved by SDS-PAGE, transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, and subjected to immunoblot analysis. Membranes were probed with an anti-GFP antibody (dilution 1:5000; cat. no. 32146) and an anti-actin antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, U.S.A) as a loading control. The level of autophagy was determined by calculating the ratio of free GFP to the total (intact BcRtn1-GFP/BcIlv2-GFP/GFP-BcAtg8 and free GFP).

BcBet4 Protein Purification in Escherichia coli

The gene encoding BcBet4 was cloned into the pET28a (+) expression vector, creating an in-frame fusion with a C-terminal 6× His tag. Protein overexpression was performed overnight at 17 °C in E. coli BL21. Following purification with Ni Sepharose 6 Fast Flow (GE Healthcare), the protein was subjected to buffer exchange into storage buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, NaCl 300 mM, glycerol 10% (w/v)) using a PD-10 desalting column and stored at −80 °C until further use.

Drug Affinity-Responsive Target Stability and Cellular Thermal Shift Assay

To assess binding of BcBet4 to POH, we applied the Drug Affinity-Responsive Target Stability (DARTS) strategy based on a previously reported method. Briefly, we constructed a prokaryotic expression vector for BcBet4, which expressed and purified its protein. Protein concentration was quantified using the BCA assay. Subsequently, proteins (5 mg/mL) were incubated with POH at a final concentration of 200 μg/mL at 4 °C for 4 h. The samples were incubated with 2 mg/mL Pronase (catalog no. 16404921; Roche, USA) for 30 min at 25 °C, respectively. After termination with SDS-PAGE buffer and a 10 min boil, samples were resolved by 15% Tris-bis gel electrophoresis and stained with Coomassie blue for visualization. Western blot (antibodies against His) was also used to analyze protein content, and then the protein bands were detected and visualized using ImageJ.

CETSA was conducted as previously described. The proteins were equally divided into two parts for parallel treatment with either POH or DMSO at 25 °C for 30 min. Each group was then split into 5 equal parts and subjected to heating at a temperature range (40–70 °C) for 3 min each. Following centrifugation at 12,000g for 15 min at 4 °C, the supernatants were collected, boiled with loading buffer, and then subjected to Western blot analysis to determine target protein levels.

Synthesis of Perillyl Alcohol Derivatives

All commercial reagents were used as received without further purification. Reactions were stirred magnetically and monitored by thin-layer chromatography (TLC) on Silica Gel 60 F254 plates. Visualization was achieved under UV light (254 nm) or by staining with potassium permanganate (KMnO4) or phosphomolybdic acid. NMR spectra (1H, 400 MHz; 13C, 101 MHz) were acquired on a Bruker Avance III spectrometer. High-resolution mass spectra were recorded on a Thermo Q-Exactive mass spectrometer with ESI. 1H NMR chemical shifts are reported in units of parts per million (ppm) relative to tetramethylsilane (TMS, δ = 0 ppm). 13C NMR shifts are expressed in ppm using the residual solvent signal (CHCl3 δ = 77.0 ppm) as an internal reference. Coupling constants (J) are given in Hertz (Hz). Multiplicities are abbreviated as s (singlet), d (doublet), t (triplet), m (multiplet), and br (broad). Additional experimental details are described in the Supporting Information.

Statistical Analysis

Experimental results are presented as means ± SD. Statistical analyses were processed using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Duncan’s multiple-range tests. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Each experiment was performed with at least three biological repetitions.

Results

POH Induces Metacaspase-Independent Apoptosis in B. cinerea

While previous studies have found that POH disrupts cell membranes against B. cinerea, its precise antifungal mechanism requires further elucidation. We analyzed the effect of POH on the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), which is an irreversible factor in the apoptosis pathway and a key indicator of cellular viability. Using the fluorescent dye JC-1, we investigated mitochondrial dysfunction following POH treatment. The result showed that POH treatment increased the green fluorescence intensity, reflecting MMP depolarization, while H2O-treated mycelia showed no green fluorescence (Figure A). Given that POH induces apoptosis via ROS accumulation in leukemia cells, we hypothesized that a similar mechanism might operate in B. cinerea. To test this, we measured ROS levels in the B05.10 strain following POH treatment using the fluorescent probe DCHF-DA. As shown in Figure B, the higher fluorescence intensity of the POH-treated mycelia was measured in comparison to that of the H2O-treated mycelia. Since disruption of calcium homeostasis represents a hallmark of apoptosis, we compared intracellular calcium distribution between different treatment groups. In the POH-treated mycelia, fluorescence mainly concentrated on/in organelles; however, the fluorescence of H2O-treated mycelia was evenly distributed throughout the cytoplasm (Figure C). Annexin V, an indicator of apoptosis, can bind with high affinity to phosphatidylserine upon its externalization to the outer plasma membrane leaflet. Red fluorescence was found in the POH-treated mycelia (Figure D), but this phenomenon was not detected in the H2O-treated mycelia. Collectively, these results demonstrate that POH is a potent inducer of apoptosis in B. cinerea.

The metacaspase played a central role in the progression of yeast apoptosis. To further explore the apoptosis pathway induced by POH in B. cinerea, we conducted a sensitivity analysis of three metacaspase mutants (ΔBcMca1, ΔBcMca2, and ΔBcMca1Mca2) to POH. Three mutants revealed no significant differences in POH sensitivity compared to wild-type B05.10 after 4 days of exposure (Figure E and F). This result suggested that POH induced a metacaspase-independent apoptotic pathway in B. cinerea.

Ubiquitylation Analysis of B05.10 Treated with POH

Previous studies have demonstrated that ubiquitin can stimulate apoptosis. To explore whether POH enhances ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation in B. cinerea, we quantified ubiquitin levels in mycelia with or without POH treatment. Notably, POH-treated mycelia exhibited significantly elevated ubiquitin concentrations compared to the H2O-treated control (Figure A). To comprehensively profile the ubiquitinated proteome of B. cinerea in response to POH, we performed ubiquitinomics to compare the profiles between POH-treated and H2O-treated mycelia. Ubiquitinomics analysis subsequently revealed 2163 lysine ubiquitinated peptides derived from 1660 unique proteins in POH-treated versus control samples. Cellular localization analysis indicated that these ubiquitinated proteins mainly localized in the membrane, cytoplasm, and endoplasmic reticulum (Figure B). Their functions were also annotated by GO enrichment analysis. Interestingly, ubiquitinated proteins were significantly associated with membrane and ATPase activity (Figure C). KEGG enrichment analyses further revealed that pathways including N-glycan biosynthesis, cellular senescence, calcium signaling pathway, mRNA surveillance pathway, autophagy, and ubiquitin-mediated proteolysis were upregulated under POH exposure (Figure D).

(C) GO enrichment analysis of identified ubiquitinated proteins. (D) KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the identified ubiquitinated proteins. (E) Ubiquitinated proteins in mitochondria. (F) ER-localized biogenesis and forward transport of ubiquitinated proteins.

Interestingly, five mitochondrial membrane proteins, including four outer membrane proteins, BcTom70, BcMop1, BcMim1, and BcFis1, and one inner membrane protein, BcPhb1, showed ubiquitination patterns potentially affecting mitochondrial integrity and function. , Five enzymes located in the mitochondria were also modified by ubiquitination (Figure E). Additionally, 19 ER-associated proteins, such as BcSrpα, BcSurF4, BcSec24, BcSec63, BcSar1, and so on, were found to be involved in protein synthesis and transport and exhibited ubiquitination modifications (Figure F). These modifications may regulate Pom33 nuclear envelope distribution through ubiquitination and facilitate Erv46 degradation via E3 ligase activity, thereby influencing α1-antitrypsin secretion. −

Eukaryotic cells possess two primary protein degradation systems: the ubiquitin proteasome and the autophagy lysosome pathway. , Given that previous studies have identified ubiquitinated proteins in mitochondria and ER as key regulators of autophagy, we investigated whether POH could induce autophagy. To investigate the effect of POH on selective autophagy in B. cinerea, we utilized strains such as BcGFP-SKL (a peroxisome marker), BcRtn1-GFP (an ER marker), BcIlv2-GFP (a mitochondria marker), and BcTaf14-GFP (a nucleus marker) to monitor selective autophagy. Under POH treatment, free GFP fluorescence was observed throughout the cytoplasm of these strains, whereas in untreated strains, GFP fluorescence was restricted to the ER, nucleus, mitochondria, and peroxisome (Figure A–D). To further assess selective autophagy changes, the autophagic flux was monitored via immunoblot. The results demonstrated that the level of free GFP was significantly higher in POH-treated mycelia than that in H2O-treated mycelia (Figure E–H). Additionally, using the GFP-BcAtg8 strain to detect the nonselective autophagy, we observed GFP fluorescence in both vacuoles and cytoplasm when the strain was treated with POH. In contrast, without POH treatment, GFP was detected only in the cytoplasm but not in the vacuole (Figure I). Western blot analysis also demonstrated that POH promoted the degradation of GFP-BcAtg8 (Figure J). Furthermore, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided further confirmation of these findings, showing ER-whorl, mitochondria, and autophagosomes in the vacuole after POH treatment (Figure K). Collectively, these results demonstrated that POH could stimulate both ubiquitination-dependent proteolysis and autophagic protein degradation pathways.

Transcriptome Analysis of B05.10 Treated with POH

To further investigate the antifungal mechanism of POH at the molecular level, transcriptome analysis was performed on the B05.10 strain with or without POH treatment. A total of 3915 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (log2 (fold change) > 2 and adjusted p-value <0.05), including 1742 upregulated and 2173 downregulated transcripts in POH-treated samples (Figure A). KEGG enrichment analysis of these DEGs was carried out, and the most significantly enriched terms are presented in Figure B. These DEGs revealed significant associations with metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, cell cycle, and ribosome biogenesis. Notably, POH treatment induced systemic downregulation of the BcBot1–BcBot7 clustera critical genetic locus encoding enzymes for botrydial biosynthesis (a sesquiterpene cyclase, three P450 monooxygenases, an acetyltransferase, a transcription factor, and a putative dehydrogenase) (Figure C). In contrast, most botcinic acid biosynthesis genes (BcBoa1-–BcBoa4 and BcBoa10–BcBoa13), which mediate detoxification, were up-regulated in response to POH treatment, suggesting a compensatory mechanism against xenobiotic stress. Furthermore, POH treatment differentially regulated the major facilitator superfamily (MFS) multidrug transporter genes, down-regulating 13 and up-regulating 10, indicating its potential to attenuate fungal multidrug resistance (Figure D).

Considering that POH can inhibit Na+/K+-ATPase activity for brain tumor treatment, we analyzed the transcription level of ATPase-related genes in transcriptome data. Among 36 ATPase-related genes, 3 exhibited significant downregulation, while 33 showed no difference in expression compared to the control. We further determined the expression of three ATPase-related genes using RT-PCR. The result showed that 3 ATPase-related genes were significantly downregulated in mycelia treated with POH (Figure E). Additionally, the effect of POH on Na+/K+-ATPase activity at different time points was evaluated. As shown in Figure F, Na+/K+-ATPase activity was significantly inhibited by POH at different time points.

Binding of POH to BcBet4

POH has been identified as a geranylgeranyl transferase inhibitor that suppresses protein prenylation. In B. cinerea, we identified BcBet4, a homologue of geranylgeranyl transferase, as a putative molecular target of POH. To elucidate the POH-BcBet4 interaction mechanism, molecular docking studies were conducted to identify key amino acid residues involved in their binding. AlphaFold2 was utilized to predict the 3D structures of BcBet4, revealing that A621 would play a key role in facilitating the binding of POH to BcBet4. The result indicated that POH forms a single hydrogen bond with A621, yielding a calculated binding affinity score of −5.32 kcal/mol (Figure A).

To further examine the direct interaction between POH and BcBet4, we employed the DARTS assay and CETSA. As shown in Figure B,C, POH reduced the proteolysis of the BcBet4 protein treated with proteases, and the protein treated with proteases exhibited sufficient digestion. Conversely, there was no significant change in the untreated BcBet4 protein without Pronase under the same conditions. In addition, CETSA assays showed that POH enhanced the thermal stability of BcBet4 compared to those treated with DMSO (Figure D,E). To further investigate whether the antifungal activity of POH was mediated by BcBet4, we generated targeted gene deletion mutants of BcBet4 via a homologous recombination strategy. POH treatment did not suppress the growth of the ΔBcBet4 mutants compared with that of B05.10 (Figure F,G). Collectively, the results demonstrated that BcBet4 is a direct target of POH that mediates its antifungal activity.

Improvement of the Inhibitory Capacity of POH through Molecular Design and Systematic Chemical Modifications

Despite the broad-spectrum antifungal potential of POH, its biological activity limits its commercial applicability. To improve its efficacy, we designed a series of POH derivatives based on chemical modification approaches. A total of 16 novel POH derivatives with diverse substituents were systematically synthesized (Figure A). The antifungal activity of these compounds was evaluated based on the conidial germination rate of B. cinerea. Our results revealed that compound POH-10 (Triazole, Figures S1–S3) exhibited the superior antifungal activity, completely inhibiting conidial germination of B. cinerea at 100 μg/mL, whereas POH achieved only 75.3% inhibition at the same concentration (Figure B). Furthermore, POH-10 exhibited broad-spectrum activity, suppressing conidial germination by over 90% in multiple pathogenic fungi, including Colletotrichum camelliae, Fusarium graminearum, Phytophthora nicotianae, Fusarium oxysporum, Valsa mali, and Botryosphaeria dothidea (Figure C).

(C) Inhibitory activity of POH-10 against conidial germination in multiple plant pathogenic fungi. Conidia of different species were exposed to POH-10 for 8 h before germination assessment. Each value represents the mean of triplicate measurements, while the vertical bar indicates the standard error. Data marked with asterisks are significantly different from the control as assessed by the two-tailed Student’s t-test: **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05.

Given its superior performance, we further investigated POH-10 in plant infection assays. At 25 μg/mL, POH-10 significantly reduced disease lesions on tomato leaves compared with POH, and at 100 μg/mL, it completely prevented infection (Figure A,B). To investigate the mechanism of action of POH-10 against B. cinerea, we assessed its effects on cell membrane integrity, reactive oxygen species within the mycelium, and intracellular calcium ion concentration in the cytoplasm. POH-10-treated mycelia exhibited strong PI fluorescence, while ddH2O-treated mycelia showed weak red fluorescence, as their intact membranes prevented the dye from entering most cells (Figure C). Similar results were observed in conidia, with over 90% of the POH-h-treated conidia exhibiting PI uptake compared to only 6% in ddH2O-treated conidia (Figure D,E). These results indicate that POH-10 affected plasma membrane permeability. Additionally, POH-10-treated mycelia displayed strong GFP fluorescence that suggested that compound POH-10 induced ROS accumulation (Figure F). We also observed that compound POH-10 disrupted intracellular calcium ion homeostasis (Figure G). Control mycelia exhibited normal internal morphology, featuring well-distributed organelles within an ample cytoplasm, intact subcellular structures, and smooth, rounded cell walls and membranes. Upon treatment with 50 μg/mL POH-10, significant changes in the ultrastructure of the mycelium were observed. The number of cellular organelles decreased, and their structure was damaged, resulting in an increase in intracellular vacuoles and the convergence of numerous vacuoles (Figure H).

To further investigate the interaction between POH-10 and BcBet4, we performed molecular docking studies to identify the key amino acid residues involved in their binding. Using AlphaFold2, we predicted the 3D structures of BcBet4, which revealed that E355, Y356, Q301, F302, F298, F246, Y299, and W249 would play a key role in facilitating POH binding, yielding a calculated docking score of −8.10 kcal/mol (Figure A).

An enlarged view highlights key hydrogen-bond interactions. (B) DARTS assay confirming the direct binding of POH-10 to BcBet4. (C) CETSA demonstrating thermal stabilization of BcBet4 in the presence of POH-10.

To further validate the direct binding between POH-10 and BcBet4, we conducted a DARTS assay and CETSA. As shown in Figure B, POH-10 protected BcBet4 from proteolysis when treated with proteases, whereas the protein was sufficiently digested in the absence of POH-10 at 1 mg/mL. In contrast, untreated BcBet4 (without Pronase and POH-10) remained stable under the same conditions. Additionally, the CETSA results showed that BcBet4 exhibited increased tolerance to higher temperatures (at 70 °C) in the presence of POH-10, manifesting as enhanced stability with increasing temperature. Meanwhile, the quantities of BcBet4 decreased in the DMSO-treated control (Figure C). Our findings supported the hypothesis that POH-10 can directly bind to BcBet4.

Discussion

The application of POH to postharvest tomatoes has been considered a suitable alternative treatment to replace synthetic fungicides. While previous studies have reported its efficacy against plant pathogens, the molecular mechanism of antifungal activity is not yet clear. This study elucidates the multifaceted mode of action of POH against B. cinerea, revealing that its antifungal effects are mediated through metacaspase-independent apoptosis driven by ROS accumulation, autophagy induction, proteasomal degradation, and plasma membrane destabilization.

Eugenol targets oleate delta-12 desaturase (FAD2) and disrupts cell membrane integrity, thereby inhibiting the proliferation of B. cinerea. Furthermore, eugenol has been shown to induce autophagy in MDA-MB-231 and SK-BR-3 cells by upregulating LC3 expression, as well as to promote apoptosis through modulation of caspase-3 and caspase-9. Although metacaspase-dependent apoptosis has been reported in response to perillaldehyde via mitochondrial cytochrome c release, fungal apoptosis can proceed through both metacaspase-dependent and -independent routes. , Our genetic analysis demonstrated that knockout of the two metacaspase genes in B. cinerea failed to decrease the fungal sensitivity to POH. Therefore, we propose that POH-induced apoptosis is independent of metacaspases in B. cinerea. This is consistent with observations in Aspergillus nidulans, where dihydrosphingosine and phytosphingosine-induced apoptosis occurs independently of metacaspases yet requires mitochondrial involvement. POH treatment induces ER-phagy and mitophagy, whereas this sustained external stress, coupled with excessive selective autophagy, ultimately leads to apoptosis.

POH induced the excessive production of ROS, which contributes to oxidative stress and also impairs protein folding capacity, ultimately inducing ER stress and apoptosis. Mitophagy serves as a protective mechanism against oxidative stress by selectively removing fissioned, dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby preventing the accumulation of oxidative damage. We further identified that POH promotes the ubiquitylation of mitochondrial import receptor subunits BcTom70 and mitochondrial fission 1 protein BcFis1. Previously, it was shown that Parkin ubiquitylates several mitochondrial proteins, including the mitochondrial import receptor subunits TOM20, TOM40, and TOM70, mitochondrial Rho GTPases, and mitochondrial fission 1 protein, ultimately leading to autophagy to eliminate damaged mitochondria. Thus, the ubiquitination of mitochondrial outer membrane proteins facilitates mitochondrial autophagy to eliminate dysfunctional mitochondria. Therefore, several factors caused by POH led to an increase in mitophagy.

Mitochondria–endoplasmic reticulum communication is known to mediate oxidative stress. Our data reveal POH-induced ubiquitination of ER-associated proteins, including BcErd2, BcSec63, BcErv41, BcErv46, and BcSar1. For instance, the induction of SAR1a/b ubiquitination by abiraterone disrupts ER homeostasis, elevates ROS levels, and ultimately leads to ER stress and apoptosis. The E3 ubiquitin ligase membrane-associated ring-CH–type finger 2 (MARCH2) promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of Evr46, thereby reducingα1-antitrypsin and haptoglobin secretion. Ubiquitin-dependent protein degradation is executed through two major pathways: the ubiquitin-proteasome system and selective autophagy. In this work, autophagy was determined by using fluorescent protein-labeled strains after POH treatment. We first demonstrated that POH enhances selective autophagy (ER-phagy and mitophagy) and nonselective autophagy. In gastric cancer cells, perillaldehyde activates the phosphorylation and activity of AMP-activated protein kinase to induce autophagy and inhibit the growth of gastric cancer. Similarly, in B. cinerea, perillaldehyde has been shown to induce both selective and nonselective autophagy, thereby affecting fungal growth and pathogenicity.

Our results revealed that POH and its derivative POH-10 significantly modulate the protease resistance and thermal stability of BcBet4. These findings were further validated through molecular docking studies and Western blot analysis, which confirmed BcBet4 as a direct target of POH and POH-10. As a geranylgeranyl transferase inhibitor, POH suppresses RSV A2 infection of HEp-2 cells in a dose-dependent manner and promotes apoptosis in immortalized human vascular smooth muscle cells in vitro. GGTI-298 (geranylgeranyl transferase I inhibitor) also induced apoptosis in smooth muscle cells. These interactions occur in the form of noncovalent binding, and this explains why POH induces apoptosis of B. cinerea. POH, a BcBet4 inhibitor, blocks farnesyl and geranylgeranyl transfer, disrupts GTPase prenylation, and increases apoptosis. Our ubiquitinome data showed that POH treatment significantly increased Rho-GTPase ubiquitination. We therefore hypothesized that POH might inhibit BcBet4 activity, which in turn would block Rho-GTPase activity. However, how BcBet4 regulates apoptosis will be the key focus of our future work.

In this study, we successfully developed more effective antifungal agents through a structure-based rational design. The POH-derived POH-10 exhibited significantly enhanced antifungal activity against B. cinerea in both in vitro and in vivo assays. Furthermore, we demonstrated that POH-10 could effectively increase binding to BcBet4, implying that POH-10 can indeed exert antifungal effects, and the antifungal mechanism of POH-10 requires further experimental validation.

In conclusion, we propose a mechanistic model for POH’s action on B. cinerea. It induces mitochondrial-derived ROS overproduction, leading to oxidative stress, leading to oxidative stress. Simultaneously, ROS damages the Ca2+ channels in the endoplasmic reticulum, leading to the release of Ca2+. It also disrupts mitochondria, triggering a pronounced loss of the MtΔψ. When oxidative stress is not eliminated by nonselective autophagy and selective autophagy, it activates the apoptosis cascade. Additionally, POH facilitates partial protein ubiquitination of the ER and mitochondria, thereby inducing selective autophagy for the clearance of damaged organelles.
