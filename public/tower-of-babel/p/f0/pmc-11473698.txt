Hepatocyte-specific GDF15 overexpression improves high-fat diet-induced obesity and hepatic steatosis in mice via hepatic FGF21 induction
Authors: Kento Takeuchi, Kanji Yamaguchi, Yusuke Takahashi, Kota Yano, Shinya Okishio, Hiroshi Ishiba, Nozomi Tochiki, Seita Kataoka, Hideki Fujii, Naoto Iwai, Yuya Seko, Atsushi Umemura, Michihisa Moriguchi, Takeshi Okanoue, Yoshito Itoh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
GDF15 and FGF21, stress-responsive cytokines primarily secreted from the liver, are promising therapeutic targets for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). However, the interaction between GDF15 and FGF21 remains unclear. We examined the effects of hepatocyte-specific GDF15 or FGF21 overexpression in high-fat diet (HFD)-fed mice for 8 weeks. Hydrodynamic injection of GDF15 or FGF21 sustained high circulating levels of GDF15 or FGF21, respectively, resulting in marked reductions in body weight, epididymal fat mass, insulin resistance, and hepatic steatosis. In addition, GDF15 treatment led to early reduction in body weight despite no change in food intake, indicating the role of GDF15 other than appetite loss. GDF15 treatment increased liver-derived serum FGF21 levels, whereas FGF21 treatment did not affect GDF15 expression. GDF15 promoted eIF2α phosphorylation and XBP1 splicing, leading to FGF21 induction. In murine AML12 hepatocytes treated with free fatty acids (FFAs), GDF15 overexpression upregulated Fgf21 mRNA levels and promoted eIF2α phosphorylation and XBP1 splicing. Overall, continuous exposure to excess FFAs resulted in a gradual increase of β-oxidation-derived reactive oxygen species and endoplasmic reticulum stress, suggesting that GDF15 enhanced this pathway and induced FGF21 expression. GDF15- and FGF21-related crosstalk is an important pathway for the treatment of MASLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75107-8.

Introduction

Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), now termed metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), is the most common liver disease among patients with obesity and insulin resistance1–3. Hepatic steatosis, the predominant form of NAFLD, begins with increased intrahepatic lipid accumulation, leading to disruption of the hepatic disposal of excess free fatty acids (FFAs)4,5. FFAs are lipotoxic factors that trigger the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and mitochondrial damage, resulting in progression from simple steatosis to more aggressive stages of NAFLD3,6. Although numerous studies have identified multiple therapeutic targets for NAFLD, Rezdiffra (resmetirom) is currently the only approved drug for treating nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH)7. In patients with NAFLD, the background metabolic status of the liver differs according to disease stage, thereby hindering the classification of the underlying pathophysiology. Therefore, steatosis inhibition via early intervention that regulates lipid homeostasis and reduces ROS production is crucial for the prevention of NASH and more advanced liver diseases. We previously reported the protective role of liver-derived FGF21 in a mouse model of diet-induced obesity and NAFLD8. FGF21 is a pleotropic hepatokine that regulates lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, and energy homeostasis, which are closely implicated in the development of NASH9. Ongoing phase III trials are evaluating the clinical efficacy and safety of FGF21 analogs in patients with NASH10,11.

Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), also known as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug-activated gene-1 and macrophage inhibitory cytokine-1, is a stress response-associated cytokine predominantly expressed in the liver and shares some downstream signaling pathways with FGF2112,13. As a member of the transforming growth factor β superfamily and the glial cell-derived neurotropic factor family, GDF15 binds to glial cell-derived neurotropic factor family receptor α-like (GFRAL) and activates its coreceptor RET in the brain14. GDF15 is synthesized as a pro-GDF15 dimer in the cytoplasm and subsequently cleaved by the proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin family of proteases, followed by its transport to the Golgi apparatus for maturation and secretion as mature 25-kD GDF15 into the bloodstream15. In humans, the circulating levels of mature GDF15 are extremely low under normal physiological conditions16. Conversely, elevated plasma GDF15 levels have been reported in many diseases, including obesity, cardiovascular disease, renal failure, diabetes mellitus, and cancer, indicating that GDF15 might serve as a diagnostic biomarker for these disorders17.

GDF15 and FGF21 are key endocrine regulators of systemic energy metabolism and glucose homeostasis; thus, they have robust potential for the treatment of obesity and associated metabolic diseases. The increased expression levels of GDF15 and FGF21 in response to mitochondrial stress further characterizes them as mitokines18. Mitochondrial dysfunction due to various etiologies can lead to the increased phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α) and increased levels of activation transcription factor 419. Downstream of eIF2α phosphorylation directly induces the transcription of Fgf21 and indirectly induces Gdf15 expression via the induction of C/EBP homologous protein16. Moreover, Gdf15 and Fgf21 mRNAs are degraded following the upregulation of CCR4-NOT Transcription Complex Subunit 6-like (CNOT6L) deadenylase in the liver of a diet-induced obesity mouse model, suggesting CNOT6L inhibitors as a potential pharmacologic option for the treatment of NASH (20).

FGF21 binds to FGF receptor 1/Klotho β. Although FGF receptor 1 is ubiquitously expressed in various organs and Klotho β is predominantly expressed in the adipose tissue, brain, liver, and pancreas20,21, the only known receptor for GDF15 is the GFRAL/RET receptor complex expressed in hindbrain neurons. Previous studies have demonstrated that GDF15 predominantly acts to reduce energy intake and that FGF21 might be crucial in increasing energy expenditure22. However, a recent study reported that GDF15 promoted weight loss by enhancing energy expenditure in muscles23. These results suggesting that GDF15 uses other signaling pathways raise the possibility of additional therapeutic targets and highlight the need for further investigation to identify the primary targets and fundamental mechanisms of action involving GDF15 and FGF21 in humans.

In the present study, we aimed to examine the synergistic interaction between GDF15 and FGF21 in a mouse model of high-fat diet (HFD)-induced NAFLD. We found that hepatocyte-specific GDF15 or FGF21 treatment improved obesity and hepatic steatosis, as expected. Furthermore, body weight (BW) loss was observed immediately although GDF15 treatment led to a significant reduction in total food intake in the late, and not early, period. These findings indicated that the antiobesity effect of GDF15 treatment might be mediated via other signaling pathways. Further analysis revealed that GDF15 treatment induced FGF21 expression in liver, whereas FGF21 treatment did not induce GDF15 expression. Continuous exposure of excess FFAs in the liver increased β-oxidation-derived ROS and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress in hepatocytes, suggesting that GDF15 enhanced this pathway and induced FGF21 expression. These results suggest that liver-derived GDF15 might improve obesity and hepatic steatosis by decreasing appetite and increasing FGF21.

Results

Delivery of GDF15 and FGF21 via a single hydrodynamic injection resulted in persistently high circulating levels of GDF15 and FGF21

First, we examined GDF15 and FGF21 expression levels in mice fed with normal chow (n = 17) or HFD (n = 17) for 8 weeks after a single hydrodynamic injection of pLIVE-control (n = 5), pLIVE-GDF15 (n = 6), or pLIVE-FGF21 (n = 6) (Fig. 1A). Hepatic Gdf15 and Fgf21 mRNA expression levels were significantly higher in mice transfected with pLIVE-GDF15 and pLIVE-FGF21 than in those transfected with pLIVE-control at the end of the treatment (Fig. 1B, Supplemental Fig. S1A). We confirmed that serum FGF21 levels were sustained using this gene delivery method8 and found that serum GDF15 and FGF21 levels were increased in HFD-fed mice and were much higher in those transfected with pLIVE-GDF15 and pLIVE-FGF21, respectively (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Treatment with GDF15 and FGF21 improves obesity, dyslipidemia, and insulin resistance

We assessed BW, liver weight/BW ratio, and epididymal fat weight/BW ratio at the end of the 8-week treatment period. Although HFD-fed mice gained BW and became obese, GDF15 and FGF21 treatment reduced the increase in BW even in the chow-fed mice (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1C). The liver/BW ratio was decreased in FGF21-treated chow-fed mice, and the HFD-induced elevation in the epididymal fat/BW ratio was considerably suppressed by GDF15 and FGF21 treatment (Fig. 2B, Supplemental Fig. S1D). The average food intake per mouse, which was similar in HFD-fed mice treated with GDF15 during the first 6 weeks, decreased in the last 2 weeks of the treatment. The average food intake/BW increased significantly in GDF15-treated mice (Fig. 2C). Conversely, FGF21 treatment increased the average food intake/mouse and the average food intake/BW throughout the treatment period (Supplemental Fig. S1E). Furthermore, feeding mice with HFD for 8 weeks increased serum total cholesterol levels but decreased triglyceride levels, with no change in FFA levels (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Fig. S1F). GDF15 and FGF21 treatment decreased total serum cholesterol and triglyceride levels in chow- and HFD-fed mice (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Fig. S1F) but reduced serum FFA levels in chow-fed mice (Fig. 2D, Supplemental Fig. S1F). HFD-fed mice exhibited high blood glucose, insulin, and homeostasis model assessment-insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) levels, all of which were reduced by GDF15 and FGF21 treatment (Fig. 2E, Supplemental Fig. S1G). Altogether, these results demonstrated that GDF15 and FGF21 improved HFD-induced obesity, epididymal fat hypertrophy, hyperlipidemia, and systemic insulin resistance but had no effect on serum FFA levels.

GDF15 and FGF21 treatment improves hepatic steatosis

The serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, which were elevated in GDF15-treated chow-fed mice, were decreased by approximately 50% in HFD-fed mice hemodynamically injected with both GDF15 and FGF21 (Fig. 3A, Supplemental Fig. S1H). The amelioration of hepatic steatosis was confirmed by hematoxylin/eosin and oil red O staining (Fig. 3B, Supplemental Fig. S1I). These results indicated that treatment with GDF15 and FGF21 considerably reduced hepatic steatosis. However, GDF15 treatment had no effect on the mRNA levels of Srebp1c, Fas, Acox1, and Cpt1 (Fig. 3C).

Liver and serum FGF21 expression is increased in mice treated with GDF15 for 8 weeks

Because GDF15-induced BW loss was observed much earlier than its appetite-lowering effect, we explored alternative mechanisms of BW loss and found that hepatic Fgf21 mRNA levels were significantly higher in chow- and HFD-fed mice transfected with pLIVE-GDF15 than in control mice (Fig. 4A). Serum FGF21 levels after GDF15 treatment were also increased in chow-fed mice and remained similar to controls in HFD-fed mice (Fig. 4B). In contrast, FGF21 treatment had no effect on hepatic Gdf15 mRNA levels but decreased elevated serum GDF15 levels in HFD-fed mice (Supplemental Fig. S1J,K). Generally, circulating levels of GDF15 and FGF21 are elevated during obesity and related metabolic complications13. However, GDF15 treatment did not downregulate FGF21 expression despite the antiobesity and antihepatic steatosis effects of GDF15, which were also observed with FGF21 treatment.

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying the effect of GDF15 on the upregulation of FGF21 expression, we assessed the molecules upstream of Fgf21 transcription. The hepatic mRNA levels of Cnot6l deadenylase, which degrades Gdf15 and Fgf21 (20), were increased by HFD and decreased by GDF15 treatment (Fig. 4C). GDF15 promoted the splicing efficiency of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1) in the livers of both chow- and HFD-fed mice (Fig. 4D). Although GDF15 treatment had no effect on peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) α protein levels, it unexpectedly increased the protein levels of phosphorylated eIF2α (p-eIF2α) and spliced XBP1 (XBP1s) in both chow- and HFD-fed mice (Fig. 4E,F). Overall, these results indicate that GDF15 treatment enhanced FGF21 expression by decreasing CNOT6L and upregulating the ER stress-related pathway.

Liver and serum FGF21 expression is increased in mice treated with GDF15 for 2 weeks

Next, we examined the early effect of GDF15 on FGF21 expression after 2 weeks of treatment (Fig. 5A). Hepatic fgf21 mRNA expression and serum FGF21 levels were significantly increased by GDF15 treatment in normal chow-fed mice, comparable to that observed after 8 weeks of GDF15 treatment (Fig. 4A,B, Fig. 5B). Furthermore, hepatic fgf21 mRNA expression levels were slightly increased by GDF15 treatment in HFD-fed mice whereas serum FGF21 levels were similar between GDF15-treated and untreated HFD-fed mice (Fig. 5B). Although GDF15 treatment had no effect on PPARα expression (Fig. 5C), GDF15 promoted eIF2α phosphorylation but not HFD-induced XBP1 splicing (Fig. 5D). Finally, GDF15 upregulated Erk1/2 phosphorylation, a downstream pathway of GFRAL/RET (Fig. 5E). These findings indicate that GDF15 induced FGF21 expression via activation of Erk1/2 signaling in the early stage of GDF15 treatment.

GDF15 induces FGF21 expression in AML12 cells

Finally, we used an in vitro model of FFA supplementation to determine the direct effect of GDF15 on hepatic FGF21 expression. In this paradigm, treatment of AML12 cells with palmitic acid and oleic acid leads to the accumulation of lipid droplets and the induction of ER stress24. As expected, the incubation of cultures with 200-µM palmitic acid and 100-µM oleic acid for 24 h induced lipid accumulation and ROS production; however, GDF15 treatment had no overt effect (Fig. 6A). FFAs increased the mRNA levels of Gdf15 and Cnot6l (Fig. 6B), whereas GDF15 overexpression with pLIVE-GDF15 increased the expression of FGF21 and CNOT6L (Fig. 6C). However, silencing Cnot6l transcription using short interfering RNA (siRNA) did not affect GDF15 expression and decreased FGF21 expression (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, the addition of 10 ng/mL of recombinant GDF15 protein increased FGF21 and CNOT6L mRNA levels. These findings suggested that GDF15 treatment increased FGF21 expression in the absence of a decrease in CNOT6L and that FFA-induced CNOT6L had no effect on GDF15 and FGF21 expression. To investigate ER stress-related pathways, we assessed the splicing efficiency and the protein levels of XBP1s and p-eIF2α. As shown in Fig. 6F,G, GDF15 treatment increased XBP1s and p-eIF2 levels, with no effect on PPARα expression. Overall, these findings suggested that GDF15 augmented the ER stress-related induction of FGF21 in vitro.

In mice, GFRAL is widely distributed in the brain and peripheral tissues, including the liver25. Using quantitative PCR, one study reported that liver Gfral mRNA expression was 70% lower than that of the brainstem in mice26. However, AML12 cells expressed only approximately 3% of Gfral/Ret mRNA (Supplementary Fig. S2A). Silencing Gfral transcription using siRNA reduced the phosphorylation of eIF2α (Supplemental Fig. S2B) and Erk1/2 (Supplemental Fig. S2C) in FFA-treated AML12 cells overexpressing GDF15. These findings suggested that GDF15 bound to GFRAL to promote downstream signaling, including Erk1/2 phosphorylation, in AML12 cells, albeit with weaker activity than observed in liver cells. GDF15 can bind to TGFβR1 and TGFβR12 in several organs, including the liver27, indicating that GDF15 can promote Erk1/2 signaling and the activity of several kinases including those that phosphorylate eIF2α via GFRAL/RET and other receptors.

Discussion

GDF15 and FGF21 have similar effects on obesity- and metabolic syndrome-associated complications, including steatotic liver disease13,28. We previously reported that hepatocyte-derived FGF21 induced lipolysis in adipose tissue and improved obesity-related insulin resistance via direct action in the liver, revealing FGF21 as a promising therapeutic strategy against MASLD8. Although GDF15 from the liver is also a promising approach12,29–31, clinical trials have not progressed, except for those evaluating the inhibition of GDF15 signaling for the treatment of cachexia, with many uncertainties regarding GDF15’s action in the extrabrain32,33. GDF15 exerts pleiotropic effects in different cells and pathologic conditions. Studies have reported contradictory effects of GDF15 on cancer cell survival and apoptosis34–36. GDF15 promotes late-stage hepatocarcinogenesis; thus, caution is warranted regarding its therapeutic application in the context of inflammation-induced carcinogenesis37.

In the present study, as expected, GDF15 treatment decreased food intake/mouse (Fig. 2C). However, although weight loss was observed from the beginning of the treatment period, appetite loss was observed later in the treatment period, and the food intake/BW was increased throughout the treatment (Fig. 2A,C), which is consistent with a previous study demonstrating that GDF15 overexpression improved hepatic steatosis through PPARα induction despite an increase in food intake/BW (30). For further analysis, we aimed to determine the cause underlying the lack of a reduction in food intake for several weeks after GDF15 administration. In particular, in the first 4 weeks of the 8-week treatment period, we observed a marked BW loss despite no change in food intake (Fig. 2A,C). The GFRAL-RET coreceptor complex facilitates GDF15 activity in the brain, whereas specific receptors for GDF15 in the liver have not been identified; these findings indicate another mechanism of GDF15 except for appetite loss. Recently, GDF15 has been reported to promote weight loss by enhancing energy expenditure in muscles23. In the liver, beta-arrestin 1 is important for the transportation and maturation of GDF15 protein, and mature GDF15 deficiency directly enhances hepatic lipogenesis38. A recent study reported that CNOT6L regulated the mRNA levels of Gdf15 and Fgf21 via its deadenylase activity in hepatocytes39. These reports provide evidence of the direct action of GDF15 in various organs and indicate that GDF15 expression is linked to FGF21 expression. Although FGF21 expression should be downregulated with improvements in obesity and fatty liver40, GDF15 treatment induced hepatic FGF21 expression and sustained circulating FGF21 levels (Fig. 4A,B), which, in part, could contribute to the reduction of BW and hepatic steatosis in the early stage of treatment. FGF21 is primarily expressed in several metabolically active tissues, such as the liver, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and pancreas41. Given that obesity and epididymal fat were reduced in GDF15-overexpressing HFD-fed mice, HFD-induced elevation of circulating FGF21 levels originating from the liver, adipose tissue, and other tissues must be suppressed, except for liver-derived GDF15. Therefore, despite increased FGF21 expression in the liver, serum FGF21 levels did not differ between untreated and GDF15-treated HFD-fed mice.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which GDF15 upregulates FGF21 expression, we examined previously reported upstream pathways of FGF21 transcription. First, we noted that HFD-induced Cnot6l expression was markedly reduced by GDF15 treatment. However, CNOT6L is elevated in murine models of NAFL and in patients with NAFLD and decreases in parallel with clinical improvement. These findings may reflect improvements in NAFLD. We also showed that the expression levels of both Fgf21 and Cnot6l mRNAs were increased in GDF15-overexpressing AML12 cells treated with FFAs in vitro (Fig. 5C). Although the induction of FGF21 by GDF15 treatment observed in vitro is consistent with our in vivo findings, increased Cnot6l expression differed, probably because there was no decrease in lipid accumulation following GDF15 treatment in AML12 cells. Our further analyses revealed that silencing Cnot6l did not increase the expression levels of GDF15 or FGF21 (Fig. 5D). These findings suggest that CNOT6L is induced by HFDs and FFAs, as previously reported39, but is not likely to be regulated by the exogenous expression of GDF15 or FGF21. Overall, these results indicate that the upregulation of FGF21 expression via GDF15 does not involve CNOT6L and that CNOT6L expression may be downregulated with improved liver fat deposition. In addition, the observed increase in FGF21 and CNOT6L in cultures supplemented with 10 ng/mL of recombinant GDF15 protein for 12 h (Fig. 5E) suggests the autocrine effect of liver-derived GDF15 and the involvement of signaling pathways downstream of GDF15, such as p38 and mitogen-activated protein kinase, as previously reported42,43.

We also evaluated the role of PPARα in FGF21 induction44 and found that GDF15 treatment in HFD-fed mice had no effect on PPARα protein levels (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, we failed to observe the induction of PPARα in GDF15-overexpressing AML12 cells (Fig. 5G). These findings are inconsistent with previous studies showing that GDF15 overexpression promotes PPARα activation, suppresses lipogenesis, reduces ROS production, and inhibits the activation of the AIM2 inflammasome29.

Finally, we examined ER stress signaling that could induce FGF2145,46. Surprisingly, we found elevated p-eIF2a levels and increased XBP1 splicing in the livers of GDF15-treated mice (Fig. 4F) and in FFA-treated AML12 cells overexpressing GDF15 (Fig. 6F,G). Furthermore, 2 weeks of GDF15 treatment promoted eIF2a phosphorylation via the activation of Erk1/2 (Fig. 5D, E), suggesting that GDF15 induces FGF21 in the livers of HFD-fed mice and FFA-supplemented hepatocytes via the augmentation of ER stress, a finding that is consistent with a previous report47 but contradicts several recent studies on GDF1512,16,17. However, GDF15 has various cell- and environment-specific effects47,48. For example, GDF15 translocates into mitochondria and induces ROS production and cell death in hepatocellular carcinoma cells34. A recent study reported that treatment with exogenous GDF15 reduced cytokine-induced beta cell death and that the downregulation of intracellular GDF15 was beneficial for beta cell survival and delayed the development of diabetes35. These findings contrast the proapoptotic effects of GDF15 observed in cancer49 and elevated circulating GDF15 levels observed in patients with diabetes13. Therefore, the biologic activity of GDF15 is complicated and requires careful evaluation in the context of specific stimuli50.

We noted that serum FFA levels were not improved in HFD-fed mice even after GDF15 treatment (Fig. 2D). Because hepatic processes are continuously induced by HFD- and fat-derived FFAs, hepatic fatty acid oxidation may serve as the main hepatic FFA disposal system and improve hepatic steatosis. Although GDF15 induced appetite loss at the end of the experimental period, the food intake/BW ratio was higher in GDF15-treated mice than in control mice. Although hepatic lipid disposal via mitochondrial beta oxidation is relatively intact5, the liver is continuously exposed to FFAs and excessive β-oxidation might produce more ROS, leading to ER stress and FGF21 expression. Such changes were not observed following FGF21 administration, indicating that GDF15 might cause greater liver stress than FGF21. The levels of hepatic Gdf15 mRNA and serum GDF15 were sustained over a long period of > 8 weeks, with no observable harm in HFD-fed mice, as evidenced by the lack of deterioration in the serum levels of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and ALT (Fig. 3A). However, GDF15 treatment in normal chow-fed mice led to elevated serum ALT levels, albeit without fatty liver (Fig. 3A). As shown in Fig. 4F, eIF2α phosphorylation was induced after GDF15 treatment in normal chow-fed mice despite the lack of a change in hepatic steatosis. Furthermore, in normal chow-fed mice, only 2 weeks of GDF15 administration tended to induce eIF2α phosphorylation and led to significant induction of Erk1/2 phosphorylation, which is downstream of GFRAL/RET (Fig. 5D,E). Overall, these findings indicate that high circulating GDF15 levels increase the risk of cellular stress under normal conditions.

Lipolysis is tightly regulated under normal conditions. Conversely, in the context of obesity and insulin resistance, excessive lipolysis causes hepatic steatosis because FFAs released from peripheral adipose depots constitute a major source of triglycerides in the liver of patients with NAFLD. Mitochondrial β-oxidation is the dominant oxidative pathway for the disposal of FFAs in the liver51. Fatty acid β-oxidation breaks down long-chain acyl-CoA into acetyl-CoA, which is involved in numerous biochemical pathways. For example, it can be used as a starting material for the biosynthesis of lipids such as triglycerides, phospholipids, and cholesterol. Moreover, for energy generation, acetyl-CoA may enter the citric acid cycle and become oxidized to produce energy if oxygen is available.

The results of our study and previous reports revealed that GDF15 treatment can prevent the progression of obesity-related NAFLD via the direct effect of GDF15 and the GDF15-mediated regulation of FGF21. Although GDF15 has been reported to inhibit lung and colon cancers50, it also induces cell proliferation and might promote hepatocellular carcinoma and liver fibrosis37. Therefore, GDF15 and FGF21 may be useful therapeutic approaches, particularly for early-stage NAFLD with preserved mitochondrial function and low risk of hepatocarcinogenesis. Future studies should elucidate the therapeutic potential of GDF15 and its associated factors in NASH with obesity-related complications such as sarcopenia.

Methods

Animals and treatment

In the present study, a mouse model of diet-induced obesity and NAFLD was used. Briefly, 8-week-old male C57/BL6J mice were purchased from Charles River (Japan). All animals were housed under conventional conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and a 12-h/12-h light–dark cycle, provided with food and water ad libitum, and housed in transparent polymer X cages (catalog no. CL-0104-2; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan), with a maximum of eight mice housed per cage. The control group (n = 17) were fed a normal chow diet, and the HFD group (n = 17) were fed an HFD (catalog no. D12492; Research Diet, Tokyo, Japan) for 8 weeks. In both groups, 12 mice were hydrodynamically injected with a pLIVE vector expressing mouse Gdf15 (n = 6) or Fgf21 (n = 6) once a week before the introduction of the specific diet, whereas the remaining 5 mice were hydrodynamically injected with the control pLIVE vector. To model the early phase of HFD feeding, the control group (n = 8) were fed a normal chow diet and the HFD group (n = 8) were fed an HFD for 2 weeks. In both groups, five mice were hydrodynamically injected with pLIVE vectors expressing mouse Gdf15, and the remaining three mice were injected with the control pLIVE vector. At the end of treatment, liver, epididymal fat, and blood samples were extracted from all animals for downstream analyses.

The animal study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals established by the Kyoto Prefectural University of Medicine (Kyoto, Japan) and approved by the Institutional Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments (permission no.: M2023-145). All procedures were approved and supervised, and the study was reported in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines.

Hydrodynamic gene transfer via tail vein injection

Wild-type mouse Gdf15 and Fgf21 cDNA constructs obtained by PCR amplification were subcloned into the pLIVE vector (Mirus Bio, Madison, WI, USA), and the pLIVE vectors expressing mouse Gdf15 or Fgf21 were delivered via hydrodynamic tail vein injection7. Tail vein injections were performed using 40 µg of the indicated plasmid in TransIT-EE® solution according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Mirus Bio).

Analysis of liver architecture

As previously described, liver sections were stained with hematoxylin/eosin or oil red O using standard techniques8.

Cell culture and transfection

AML12 cells, a mouse hepatocyte line obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA), were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 in medium including a 1:1 mixture of high-glucose DMEM/Ham’s F-12 supplemented with L-glutamine, 1.0-mM sodium pyruvate, 15-mM HEPES, 10% fetal bovine serum, 1× insulin-transferrin-selenium solution (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), and 40 ng/mL dexamethasone (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). The cultures were transfected with pLIVE-control or pLIVE-GDF15 for 48 h using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 42 h of transfection, the cultures were treated with 200-µM palmitic acid and 100-µM oleic acid (Sigma Aldrich) for 6 h. Silencer® Select siRNA targeting Cnot6l (catalog no. s106848; Invitrogen) or Gfral (catalog no. s118777; Invitrogen) was transfected at a final concentration of 25 nM using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX transfection agent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. After transfection, the cultures were incubated for another 48 h before downstream analyses. All transfection assays were repeated at least three times. Intracellular ROS production was measured using a highly sensitive DCFH-DA ROS assay kit (Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan).

Two-step real-time PCR

PCR was performed as previously described4,8. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from whole-liver tissue using an RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA) and reverse-transcribed using random primers and Superscript RNase H− reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen). The PCR products were sequenced to confirm the specificity of the primer pairs (Supplemental Table 1). Target gene levels were presented as the ratio of gene expression in the treated group to the gene expression in the control group. Fold changes were determined using point and interval estimates.

Immunoblotting

Whole-liver protein lysates were separated using SDS-PAGE and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, which were probed with primary antibodies to eIF2α, p-eIF2α, XBP1s, Erk1/2, or phosphorylated Erk1/2 (p-Erk1/2) from Cell Signaling Technology (Beverly, MA), PPARα (GTX101098) from GeneTex (Irvine, CA), unspliced XBP1 (clone F-4; sc-8015 F-4) from Santa Cruz (Dallas, TX, USA), GDF15 (bs-3818R) from Bioss (Boston, MA), and actin from Sigma Aldrich. Next, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse or anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (Invitrogen). The proteins were visualized using electrochemiluminescence (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL). Immunoblots were scanned, and quantitative densitometric analysis of the bands was conducted using ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD).

Tissue and plasma biochemical analyses

Serum levels of AST, ALT, total cholesterol, triglycerides, FFAs, and glucose were measured at SRL (Tokyo, Japan). Serum levels of GDF15, FGF21, and insulin were measured using ELISA kits for mouse GDF15 (ab216947), mouse FGF21 (ab212160), and mouse/rat insulin (Morinaga, Yokohama, Japan). HOMA-IR was calculated using the following equation: HOMA-IR = fasting glucose (mM) × fasting insulin (microunits/mL)/22.5.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± standard error of the mean. Significant differences were determined using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (Fig. 2A,C; Supplemental Fig. S1C, 1E) or Student’s t-test and ANOVA, when appropriate. Differences were considered significant at a p level of < 0.05.
