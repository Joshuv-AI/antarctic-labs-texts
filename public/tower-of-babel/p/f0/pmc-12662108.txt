Network analysis of antiseizure medication use, efficacy, and safety in epilepsy: A retrospective cohort study in a tertiary care center
Authors: Miguel Angel Morales-Morales, Emilio García-Gómez, Daniel San-Juan, Carlos Trenado, Daniela Carolina Pimentel-Saona, Yazmin Palomares-Salazar, Manuel Alejandro Del Río-Quiñones, Mónica Alejandra Montalvo-Hérnandez, Christopher De Jesús-Fernández, Jesús Iván Ruiz-Gutierrez, Clio Rubinos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Network analysis revealed key ASM prescription clusters.•Valproate remains dominant despite safety concerns.•Levetiracetam use is increasing in real-world settings.•Accessibility heavily influenced prescription decisions in LMICs.•Seizure freedom improved over 10 years, showing meaningful clinical progress.Keywords: Epilepsy, Antiseizure medications, Prescription patterns, Network analysis, Drug safety

Introduction

Anti-seizure medications (ASM) are the primary treatment for epilepsy, aiming to prevent seizures while minimizing adverse effects [1]. About 75 % of the global burden of neurological disease is found in low and middle-income countries (LMICs), the same countries that have the smallest neurological workforce and limited access to ASM [2]. A systematic review reported a high prevalence of epilepsy in Mexico, of 3.9 to 41 per 1,000 inhabitants, which has not changed in five decades, being a Latin-American developing country with one of the highest prevalences of epilepsy in the region [1,3].

The availability of over 24 ASM on the market underscores the complexity of selecting the most appropriate treatment, particularly considering the constant evolution of first-line treatment guidelines in high-income countries (HICs) [4,5]. Between the late 20th century and 2008, an important shift occurred in the use of first-generation ASM such as carbamazepine, phenytoin and valproate. Their use has progressively declined with the introduction of second and third-generation ASM, which offer improved safety profiles regarding concerns about teratogenicity and better tolerability [6].

Effective seizure control in epilepsy depends on selecting an appropriate ASM that aligns with the patient’s needs, ensuring they understand the administration schedule and maintain compliance. The selection of ASM is influenced by individual circumstances: age, sex, child-bearing potential, comorbidities, tolerability, seizure type, and epilepsy syndrome [7]. Adverse effects significantly impact therapeutic success, not only by leading to potential treatment discontinuation but also by impairing adherence, ultimately hindering the attainment of desired therapeutic outcomes at optimal dosage levels [2]. Globally, ASM prescription patterns differ due to several factors, such as the teratogenic risks and neurodevelopmental impairment associated with first-generation ASM, adherence challenges, adverse effects, limited access to newer ASM, and financial constraints. These factors collectively drive changes in prescription patterns as newer ASM are introduced, particularly in high-income countries [8]. However, there is a lack of evidence of similar changes in prescription patterns in developing countries. Meanwhile, available information about these types of studies comes from HICs [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. Despite growing use of network analysis in pharmacoepidemiology, its application in modeling complex treatment sequences and polytherapy patterns for ASM remains limited. Traditional descriptive statistics and pairwise models summarize frequencies but overlook the higher-order structure of real-world polytherapy and sequencing. Network analysis, by modeling the sequential flow of drugs, can pinpoint influential agents and outcome-linked communities that remain unseen with conventional methods. Applying this lens to routine care in a resource-constrained tertiary center clarifies how combinations and ordering relate to effectiveness and tolerability.

Based on the global shift toward newer ASM and the constraints of a public health system in an LMIC, we formulated the following testable hypotheses:

•H1:Despite the increasing availability of newer ASM, first-generation drugs, particularly valproate, would maintain or increase their influence (as measured by PageRank centrality) in P2 (2021–2023) compared to P1 (2011–2013), driven by broad-spectrum efficacy and low cost.

H1:Despite the increasing availability of newer ASM, first-generation drugs, particularly valproate, would maintain or increase their influence (as measured by PageRank centrality) in P2 (2021–2023) compared to P1 (2011–2013), driven by broad-spectrum efficacy and low cost.

•H2: The modular patterns of polytherapy, reflecting a greater adoption of second- and third-generation ASM, would be associated with a significantly higher rate of seizure freedom and a lower occurrence of adverse effects compared to P1.

H2: The modular patterns of polytherapy, reflecting a greater adoption of second- and third-generation ASM, would be associated with a significantly higher rate of seizure freedom and a lower occurrence of adverse effects compared to P1.

This study aimed to evaluate ASM prescription patterns, indications, adverse reactions, and therapeutic effectiveness in a single neurological center in a developing country using a network mathematical analysis.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study by collecting paper and digital medical records of patients diagnosed with epilepsy from the National Institute of Neurology and Neurosurgery of Mexico City, Mexico. A total of 228 patients were enrolled in two follow-up phases, the first phase (P1) from January 2011 to December 2013 with a total of 100 patients and the second phase (P2) from January 2021 to December 2023 with a total of 128 patients. Our study included patients who met the following inclusion criteria: [I] confirmed diagnosis of epilepsy according to the ILAE criteria [13], [II] follow-up for more than 2 years in the outpatient epilepsy clinic (this criterion was operationalized by requiring documented, continuous clinical care in our institutional epilepsy clinic covering at least 24 months) [III] aged > 15 years old, and [V] taking any ASM available for epilepsy in Mexico. This study followed the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by our institution's Clinical Research Ethics Committee.

The variables analyzed included age, sex, epilepsy type according to the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) classification [13] and the type of ASM prescribed. Additionally, pharmacoresistance in epilepsy was assessed, as defined by the ILAE as the persistence of seizures despite adequate trials of at least two appropriately selected and dosed ASM [14]. Seizure-free, defined as at least 1 year of seizure freedom or a period that is three times the longest time between seizures they experienced in the previous year [15], and the presence and number of adverse effects (any effect reported such as rash, tremor, drowsiness, among others) were chosen to assess the differences in efficacy and safety of the ASM between the two phases. Additionally, the tolerability of ASM was defined as the patient’s ability to endure treatment-associated adverse effects without requiring discontinuation, balancing efficacy with the preservation of quality of life and minimizing the risk of medication-related morbidity.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS program (IBM, version 25.0).

We used descriptive statistics, such as frequencies and percentages. To detect statistical differences between the two phases, we used the chi-squared test for categorical variables with more than two levels. In addition, we used the independent T-test to evaluate continuous variables such as age, number of drugs, and number of adverse effects. A p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered significant.

Sample size calculation

We calculated the sample size for a one-tailed test comparing two independent proportions (P1 = 50 % and P2 = 70 %) based on the prevalence of drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE) in our outpatient clinics (≈56 %) [16], with a 95 % confidence level and 80 % power. The initial estimate was 71 participants per group, adjusted to 84 per group to account for a 15 % loss to follow-up. Thus, a total of 168 participants will ensure sufficient power to detect a significant difference.

Network analysis, centrality measures and modularity analysis

A network analysis was performed by using MATLAB Version 9.12.0 (R2022a), Natick, Massachusetts: MathWorks Inc., 2022, to evaluate the influence of different ASM and their interaction. In defining ASM networks, we set up each node as a specific type of ASM. The network is a directed graph where its edges denote directed connections to other ASM that were subsequently administered to the patient (Fig. 1). An edge (connection) exists from drug A to drug B as long as drug B was added to the regimen of a patient already taking A (politherapy addition) or if drug B replaced drug A (sequential monotherapy). The direction of the edge thus represents the chronological sequence of prescription (Fig. 1). The direction of the edge thus represents the chronological sequence of prescription within the patient's follow-up. This approach explicitly captures the temporal relationship of order of administration, which is the critical dimension for evaluating evolving prescribing patterns and therapeutic sequencing.

To quantify the relevance of a particular ASM within its network, we utilized parameters such as centrality measures (page rank, degree, betweenness, and closeness), which are also useful for comparing the overall trend of ASM usage between the considered phases (P1 and P2). Moreover, we considered ASM networks corresponding to the subgroups of seizure-free patients as well as patients with reported adverse effects (Fig. 1). It is important to note that medication dosages were not included as a variable in the network construction or subsequent centrality and modularity analyses; the focus remained on the sequential pattern of prescription choice.

Additionally, we obtained network parameters that assessed the strength of the division of a network into modules, that is the so-called modularity. Note that for our results, we focused on Page rank, which highlights the relevance of a specific ASM in relation to other ASM within the network by providing a Page rank value between 0 and 1 (0 denoting low importance, while 1 denotes high importance), as well as modularity. For the analysis of modularity, we made use of the BiMAT package [17], specifically designed to address the structure and dynamics of bipartite networks. The bipartite graphs generated by BiMAT, visualized in Fig. 4, represent the detected modular structure (the clusters of ASM). These graphs link the ASM nodes with the identified modules or connectivity characteristics, illustrating the strong within-module associations. From these relationships, modularity measures were calculated (Qb), which evaluate the quality or strength of node partitioning into modules or clusters within the network.

In our context, the identified modules represent clusters of ASM that share common characteristics related to the therapeutic mechanism needed for the type of epilepsy the patient has, suggesting interaction patterns relevant to their functional or therapeutic classification. This approach allowed us to identify underlying structures in the data, facilitating the interpretation of the relationships between the ASM studied.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of 228 patients with epilepsy (PWE) were included in the analysis, divided into two time periods. In P1, there were 100 patients, 56 % of whom were women, with a mean age of 34 years. The most common epilepsy type was focal epilepsy, at 69 %. In this period, only 9 % (p = 0.001) of patients were seizure-free for one year, most were on polytherapy, and 10 % reported adverse effects. In P2, 66 % of patients were women, with a mean age of 33 years. Focal epilepsy remained the most common epilepsy type (70.3 %). The percentage of patients who were seizure-free for one year increased to 25.8 % (p = 0.001). Polytherapy remained the primary treatment approach, and adverse effects were reported in 17 % of patients. A detailed comparison of clinical characteristics is presented in Table 1.

Patients with seizure freedom

The page rank analysis revealed that among the subgroup of seizure-free patients, lamotrigine and valproate were the most influential ASM, both with a rank value of 0.13 in P1. In P2, valproate had the highest rank value at 0.20, further supporting its significant impact on treatment efficacy.

Patients with adverse effects

Among the subgroup of patients who reported any type of adverse effect, the drugs with the greatest influence in the P1 was valproate, with a rank value of 0.14, and lamotrigine, for the P2, with a value of 0.27. This shows an association between the use of these drugs in polytherapy and the greater occurrence of adverse effects.

Discussion

The network analysis used in the present study revealed the trends in the prescriptions of ASM in a developing country such as Mexico, using centrality and modularity measurements. Our results indicate that valproate is the drug with the greatest influence in the networks of both phases (P1 and P2). Importantly, we observe that such influence consistently remains to the present years as covered by P2. Clinically, the PageRank value (0.15 in P1 vs. 0.19 in P2) signifies that valproate is the most central agent in the prescription flow; an increasing PageRank indicates a deepening reliance on valproate as a consistent initiating agent or a frequent cornerstone drug in subsequent polytherapy regimens.

These findings differ from a study conducted in England that evaluated three phases of ASM use between 2003 and 2016. In that study, valproate usage remained stable during the first 2 phases (2003–2007 and 2007–2011) but declined in the final phase. Additionally, there was a noticeable decrease in the use of carbamazepine and phenytoin, with a growing preference for levetiracetam in more recent years [9]. The latter was also observed in our study, since the network analysis revealed levetiracetam as a drug with great influence at present, although in previous years, it did not have great relevance. Additionally, in the UK, electronic primary care records from 2000 and 2010 for patients with a new diagnosis of epilepsy show a shift toward increased prescriptions of lamotrigine and levetiracetam and fewer prescriptions of phenytoin and carbamazepine as first-line treatments [10]. This trend suggests a preference for levetiracetam and lamotrigine due to their effectiveness and lower risk of drug interactions [11]. Other HICs, such as Canada or the United States of America, describe an increasing trend in the use of new-generation ASM, gradually replacing older medications. However, challenges remain in fully adopting these practices due to health plan resource limitations. Importantly, while the newer ASM may offer better tolerability, they do not necessarily guarantee better efficacy [18,19].

Consistent with our observations, focal epilepsy emerged as the most frequent type of epilepsy [20]. In a Mexican epidemiological study that analyzed 6,653 patients from the Multicenter Epilepsy Registry (2021–2022) conducted in 89 hospitals across 31 Mexican states, showed that the majority of focal epilepsy types were of structural etiology (46.1 %), followed by unknown etiologies, (40.9 %), genetic (12.9 %), infectious (2.9 %), metabolic (1.4 %) and immune-mediated (0.9 %). Moreover, within the structural etiologies, cortical developmental malformations and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy were the most common causes [20].

In a Japanese study that included 24,691 patients diagnosed with epilepsy from 2014 to 2019. Patients were further categorized as having either focal or generalized epilepsy. The study investigated trends in ASM prescriptions, revealing a growing preference for levetiracetam, which had become the most prescribed newer drug. In contrast, valproate remained the most prescribed traditional ASM [11]. It is plausible that the Japanese population was prescribed these ASM following their designation as first-line therapies following national clinical guidelines [21]. These findings align with our results, which demonstrate higher PageRank scores for levetiracetam in recent years (2021–2023) and consistent usage patterns for valproate across both earlier and current years, which is information that a developing country has not provided.

A study in Germany that employed surveys from cohort studies conducted in 2008, 2013, 2016 and 2020 examined trends in ASM prescription patterns among all PWE with special consideration for women of childbearing potential and older adult (≥65 years old) patients and found a significant decrease in the prescription of valproate and carbamazepine, whereas prescriptions of levetiracetam increased significantly even in monotherapy. Also, lamotrigine remains without prescription changes. In general, they found a significant increase in the prescription frequency of third-generation ASM such as lacosamide and eslicarbazepine, from 59.3 % to 84.2 %, and was accompanied by a decrease in the frequency of first- and second-generation ASM (5.4 % to 2.1 % and 34.9 % to 12.6 %, respectively) [22].

A nationwide population-based study in Italy (2000–2005) examined the prevalence and prescribing patterns of ASM in general practice. The study reported a widespread increase in the use of newer ASM primarily due to their application in mood disorders and pain management, which contributed to higher prescription rates. However, older ASM continue to remain the first-line treatment for epilepsy, with carbamazepine, phenobarbital (PB) and valproate being the most commonly prescribed. Newer ASM were mostly used in patients aged 65 years and older [23]. In comparison, our study identified a notable increase in valproate prescription alongside carbamazepine, both exhibiting similar prescription rates according to the page rake values during the initial phase. This pattern aligns with the current clinical guidelines in Mexico [24]. Furthermore, the preference for valproate may be attributed to its broad-spectrum therapeutic efficacy [25].

This trend in the use of ASM in our study could be explained by the availability of certain ASM in Mexico and the easy pathway to access and low cost [26]. However, even among patients prescribed ASM in LMICs, challenges such as difficulties in obtaining the medications or treatment discontinuation due to socioeconomic and cultural factors contribute to the secondary treatment gap. This term highlights the phenomenon where patients are classified as untreated in epidemiological surveys, despite having initiated therapy, underscoring the need for targeted strategies to address these barriers [27,28]. The 2013 PAHO report highlighted that four first-generation ASM— phenobarbital, phenytoin, carbamazepine and valproate —are included in the list of essential medicines and should be accessible at the primary healthcare level in most Latin American and Caribbean (LAC) countries. According to the report, 92 % of countries reported the availability of phenobarbital (excluding Guatemala and Colombia), 92 % reported the availability of phenytoin (except Guatemala and Haiti), 80 % reported the availability of carbamazepine (excluding El Salvador, Guatemala, Haiti, Venezuela, and Antigua and Barbuda), and 72 % reported the availability of valproate (excluding Costa Rica, El Salvador, Guatemala, Haiti, Honduras, Venezuela, and Antigua and Barbuda). However, these figures do not account for distribution disparities across different regions within countries. Notably, only 62 % of countries reported consistent year-round availability of these ASM at the primary healthcare level [29]. Probably, due to income disparities and significant differences between HICs and LMICs ASM prices are affecting affordability [30,31].

According to prescription trends in LMICs, phenytoin was the most prescribed ASM, while levetiracetam and vigabatrin were the least prescribed as monotherapies. Meanwhile, the combination of phenytoin and phenobarbital was the most common [32]. Another study, which included several LMICs such as Vietnam, India, the Philippines, Malaysia, Indonesia, and others, found that levetiracetam, topiramate, and lamotrigine were the most accessible ASM, while lacosamide was the least accessible [33]. A study conducted in Bangladesh (1998––2013) evaluated the trend of ASM use, identifying carbamazepine as the most commonly prescribed, followed by valproate, phenobarbital and phenytoin. The study also assessed treatment effectiveness by measuring the decrease in seizure frequency. The best results were obtained with phenytoin and phenobarbital, followed by carbamazepine and valproate and adverse drug reactions were observed mainly in phenytoin and carbamazepine. These results align with our P1 (2011–2013) results, where valproate and carbamazepine had the highest page rank value. In contrast, for seizure-free patients, the ASM with the highest page rank value were valproate and lamotrigine, followed by carbamazepine and benzodiazepine, particularly in those experiencing adverse effects [34]. One of the reasons that could explain the difference in the leading ASM in these regions is the more accessible cost, such is the case in Bangladesh, where phenytoin and phenobarbital are the most affordable options, whereas valproate and carbamazepine can be twice as expensive, and lamotrigine can cost up to six times more [33]. This economic factor may significantly influence prescribing patterns [35].

Regarding modular associations in PWE achieving seizure-free, P2 results identified a strong combination involving oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, topiramate, levetiracetam, valproate, lacosamide, and brivaracetam. Conversely, in patients experiencing adverse effects, P2 modular analysis highlighted a strong association with the combination of valproate, brivaracetam, topiramate, oxcarbazepine, lamotrigine, and phenytoin. Notably, additional clusters were identified in each group due to the use of centrality measures provided by the software employed. Qb in seizure-free patients was higher compared to those in patients with adverse effects. This disparity may be attributed to PWE adherence to treatment, driven by their satisfaction with its impact on their well-being.

A longitudinal observational study conducted in China (2019 – 2022) assessed 323 patients with DRE identified focal epilepsy as the most prevalent subtype. The study findings revealed that the combination of valproate/lamotrigine/topiramate demonstrated the highest efficacy in reducing seizure frequency with 17.9 % of the patients becoming seizure-free, followed by lamotrigine/valproate/levetiracetam at 12.8 % seizure-free. The lamotrigine/valproate/phenobarbital combination was the most associated with the highest incidence of adverse effects [12]. These results may align with the modularity patterns observed in our current treatment strategies in P2.

Network analysis is an established tool in the broader field of pharmacology and drug interaction studies, its specific application to evaluate sequential prescribing patterns and therapeutic trajectories of ASM in resource-constrained settings represents a valuable extension of its utility [[36], [37], [38]]. Nevertheless, our study presents several limitations inherent to its real-world, retrospective design. Firstly, the analysis focused on ASM combinations and sequences currently used, prioritizing a real-world evaluation over a standardized randomized clinical trial design. This population-level network analysis did not incorporate critical variables such as drug dosage or the precise time elapsed between prescription changes, focusing solely on the chronological order of administration. Furthermore, the frequency of adverse effects was based solely on patient self-reports or physician assessments during clinic consultations, potentially underreporting their true incidence. The retrospective cohort design introduces a significant risk of selection bias, especially due to the 10-year interval separating P1 and P2. Over this period, substantial changes occurred in clinical guidelines, ASM availability, and institutional referral patterns, which may have led to differences in the characteristics of the patient populations enrolled in each phase. Additionally, aggregating the data into two distinct 3-year phases treats each period as temporally static, potentially masking year-by-year evolutionary trends in prescribing patterns.

A key limitation is the limited generalizability of the findings. Our results are derived from a single public third-level neurological institute, the national referral center for complex cases. While this provides unique insight into specialized care under public, resource-constrained conditions, it may not represent prescribing patterns in general practice or community settings. Finally, although we discuss the critical influence of economics on ASM use in LMICs, the analysis lacks integration of specific economic data (e.g., cost per defined daily dose or regional availability statistics) that could empirically explain the persistent reliance on older medications like valproate.

We recommend future research to build upon our findings, utilizing the specific cluster information evident in the bipartite graphs (Fig. 4). These graphs, generated with the biMAT package, could help identify novel dual-drug therapy regimens, potentially guiding the development of new clinical trials to optimize epilepsy management.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the enduring influence and efficacy of valproate as a central component of epilepsy management in a developing country. Despite the growing adoption of newer ASMs, like levetiracetam, valproate continues to play a crucial role due to its broad-spectrum efficacy, cost-effectiveness, and availability. The observed improvements in seizure freedom rates underscore the advancements in personalized therapeutic approaches. However, the persistence of adverse effects emphasizes the need for ongoing evaluation of ASM combinations to enhance both safety and efficacy.
