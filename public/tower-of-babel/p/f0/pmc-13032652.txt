Spatial multiomics dissects the SARS-CoV-2-induced disruption of cell adhesion and immune dynamics in the human hippocampus
Authors: Hui Wei, Zhongman Jin, Caiyun Zhu, Pengsheng You, Jiaping Sui, Lian Huang, Nian Liu, Wanwan Zhu, Qi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection is associated with neurological complications, yet the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. Here, we used an integrated spatial multiomics approach to investigate the effects of SARS-CoV-2 on human hippocampus–entorhinal cortex (HP–EC) tissues from autopsies of individuals with severe COVID-19. Our analysis revealed a cascade of pathological events initiated by viral infection. First, we found that SARS-CoV-2 preferentially infects neurons, with the highest viral loads in the dentate gyrus. Second, this infection led to significant reductions in key cell–cell adhesion proteins, including contactin-associated protein (CASPR), myelin basic protein (MBP), and zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), which collectively indicate compromised axoglial junctions and blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity. Third, we identified a paradoxical immune landscape characterized by impaired immune surveillance, which involved reduced T cells and homeostatic microglia, coupled with focal reactive microgliosis. Fourth, spatial transcriptomics revealed upregulation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/Wnt stress-response pathways and downregulation of homeostatic functions in infected cells. Finally, we observed increased somatic proximity between neurons and vascular cells, indicating altered neurovascular architecture. Collectively, these findings provide a comprehensive spatial characterization of the pathological landscape in the human brain following SARS-CoV-2 infection. This work offers novel insights into COVID-19 neuropathology and highlights key molecular processes, such as the disruption of junctional proteins and immune surveillance pathways, that warrant further investigation as potential avenues for future therapeutic strategies to mitigate the long-term neurological consequences of the disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03751-0.

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) has caused more than 770 million confirmed cases of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and more than 7 million deaths globally as of September 2025 [1]. While primarily a respiratory pathogen, SARS-CoV-2 is increasingly recognized for its detrimental effects on the central nervous system (CNS) [2–4]. A significant proportion of patients experience a wide spectrum of neurological and psychiatric symptoms, collectively termed neuroCOVID-19, which includes cognitive deficits, dementia, epilepsy, and psychotic disorders [4–7]. Large-scale cohort studies have confirmed a higher incidence of these diagnoses following SARS-CoV-2 infection compared with matched cohorts recoverying from influenza or other respiratory tract infection [4].

The mechanisms underlying COVID-19-associated neurological damage are multifaceted and remain incompletely elucidated. The proposed pathways include systemic inflammation [8–10], autoimmunity [9], microvascular complications [11, 12], and hypoxia [13]. However, a central question persists: does SARS-CoV-2 directly invade the CNS to cause pathology, or are the observed effects primarily indirect? Evidence from animal models has confirmed that SARS-CoV-2 can indeed undergo neuroinvasion, likely via the olfactory nerve, and reach susceptible regions such as the hippocampus, leading to microglial activation, synaptic loss, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) dysfunction [14–17]. Furthermore, viral RNA and proteins have been detected in the brains of patients months after the initial infection, suggesting that the CNS may serve as a viral reservoir [3]. Beyond active replication, the viral spike (S) protein alone can induce syncytium formation and apoptosis, potentially initiating neurodegenerative processes [18–20].

However, a gap remains in the precise spatial and cellular characterization of SARS-CoV-2 infection in human brain regions vital for cognition and emotion, particularly those within the olfactory projection pathway, such as the limbic system. Bulk transcriptomic and proteomic studies of postmortem brain tissues have revealed widespread dysregulation of immune and metabolic pathways in patients with COVID-19 [21–23]. However, these high-throughput approaches, while providing valuable insights, inherently lack spatial resolution, obscuring the critical interactions between infected and uninfected cells within the tissue architecture. This gap underscores the urgent need for in situ investigations that can precisely map the virus and simultaneously determine the ensuing host responses at the cellular level in human brain samples.

We addressed these gaps employed an integrated spatial multiomics approach to analyse autopsy-derived hippocampus‒entorhinal cortex (HP‒EC) tissues from patients with severe COVID-19 and uninfected controls. Our study was designed to address three key questions. First, what is the precise spatial distribution and cellular tropism of SARS-CoV-2 in the human HP‒EC? Second, how does the infection disrupt critical cellular structures, particularly those mediating cell‒cell adhesion and BBB integrity? Third, what alterations in the local immune microenvironment and transcriptomic landscape contribute to neuropathology? By combining multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC), RNA in situ hybridization, and in situ spatial transcriptomics, we provide a high-resolution map of SARS-CoV-2-induced neuropathology and novel mechanistic insights into COVID-19-associated CNS involvement.

Our findings indicate that SARS-CoV-2 infection preferentially targets neuronal populations in the hippocampus, disrupts key adhesion molecules, including contactin-associated protein (CASPR) and zona occludens-1 (ZO-1), and fosters​ an altered immune microenvironment characterized by both impaired immune surveillance and compensatory microglial activation. These results reveal a complex interplay between direct viral effects, structural impairment, and immune dysregulation, providing novel insights into the mechanisms by which SARS-CoV-2 affects the human brain and potentially contributes to long-term neurological sequelae.

Methods

Specimen collection

HP–EC tissues were obtained from the National Human Brain Bank for Developmental and Functional Research (NHBBDF). The cohort consisted of 20 samples collected predominantly in late 2022, when the Omicron variant was the dominant SARS-CoV-2 strain in China.

Following NHBBDF protocols [24], an experienced pathologist collected each sample during autopsy within 9 h of death. To ensure biosafety, tissues were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Servicebio, China, Cat#: G1101), dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene (Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., China, Cat#: 10023418), and embedded in paraffin (Solarbio, China, Cat#: YA0012). Subsequently, 5 μm-thick sections were prepared using a microtome (Leica, Germany, Cat#: RM2235).

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences & Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (Approval No. 2022125). All procedures adhered to the NHBBDF guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki [25], with informed consent obtained from the donors. RNAscope in situ hybridization

SARS-CoV-2 RNA was detected in the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using the RNAscope™ Multiplex Fluorescent Detection Reagents v2 (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, ACD, USA, Cat#: 323110) with the RNAscope™ Probe targeting the viral spike gene (V-nCoV2019-S, ACD, USA, Cat#: 848561). Briefly, sections were dewaxed, treated with hydrogen peroxide to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval. After protease digestion using the PretreatPro reagent to enhance probe accessibility, the sections were hybridized with the target probe at 40 °C for 2 h in an ACD HybE™ II Hybridization System. Following hybridization, signal amplification was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The viral RNA signals were subsequently visualized by incubation with Opal 570 fluorophore (Asbio, China, Cat#: ASOP570) at 40 °C for 20 min.

For co-localization studies, a simultaneous immunofluorescence assay was conducted. After the RNAscope procedure, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mixture of primary antibodies: mouse anti-NeuN (1:500, Abcam, UK, Cat#: ab104224) to label neurons, and rabbit anti-SARS-CoV-2 S protein (1:50, Abcam, UK, Cat#: ab272504). The following day, sections were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C with corresponding secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor® 488 (1:500, Abcam, UK, Cat#: ab150113) and donkey anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor® 594 (1:500, Abcam, UK, Cat#: ab150064). Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI). Finally, the entire tissue section on each slide was imaged using an automated slide scanning system (Axioscan7, ZEISS, Germany) with a 20× objective lens to acquire 14-bit images.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry

mIHC staining was performed using a tyramide signal amplification (TSA) method with a commercial kit (AlphaTSA® Multiplex IHC Kit; Alpha X Biotechnology, China, Cat#: AXT37100031). 6 distinct mIHC panels were designed, with each panel targeting the SARS-CoV-2 S protein, various cell markers, and extracellular proteins, as detailed in Supplementary Table 1.

FFPE tissue sections were initially processed by dewaxing and rehydration. Antigen retrieval was then carried out using a sodium citrate buffer at pH 6.0, heated to 95 °C for 10 min. To minimize nonspecific binding, the sections were treated with hydrogen peroxide and a commercial blocking solution (Beyotime, China, Cat#: P0103). The sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed a 10-minute incubation at 37 °C with a poly-horseradish peroxidase (poly-HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody. Subsequently, a tyramide working solution was applied to the sections and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to enable signal amplification. For the sequential detection of multiple targets across 6 rounds, a critical stripping step was implemented between each staining round. Antibody complexes from the previous round were removed using an antigen retrieval buffer. After all target proteins were sequentially labeled, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Finally, the entire tissue section on each slide was imaged using an automated slide scanning system (Axioscan7, ZEISS, Germany) with 20× and 40× objective lenses to acquire 14-bit images.

To ensure staining specificity, rigorous controls were included. Full-channel isotype controls were performed by applying relevant immunoglobulin isotypes at a concentration equivalent to the highest primary antibody concentration used in the study. For the SARS-CoV-2 S antibody specifically, preabsorption controls were conducted by pre-incubating the primary antibody with a specific human SARS-CoV-2 S peptide prior to its application on the tissue sections.

TUNEL staining combined with neuronal immunofluorescence staining

To specifically identify apoptotic neurons, double-labeling was performed using Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay in combination with immunofluorescence for the neuronal marker NeuN. The TUNEL staining was carried out using a commercial kit (Beyotime, China, Cat#: C1090) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with modifications for co-staining. For neuronal labeling, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a mouse monoclonal anti-NeuN primary antibody (1:500, Abcam, UK, Cat#: ab104224). This was followed by a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C with an Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody (1:500, Abcam, UK, Cat#: ab150113). Subsequently, sections were equilibrated with the recommended buffer, followed by incubation with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 15 min. After thorough washing, cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. The entire tissue area on each slide was imaged using an Axioscan7 automated slide scanning system (ZEISS, Germany) with a 20× objective lens. Finally, 14-bit images were acquired.

Xenium in situ spatial transcriptomics

To precisely map the spatial transcriptomic landscape within the human HP–EC following SARS-CoV-2 infection, we performed Xenium in situ spatial transcriptomic analysis (10x Genomics, USA) on 2 human HP–EC samples that exhibited strong SARS-CoV-2 S protein signals in prior mIHC staining.

A custom targeted detection panel of 316 genes was employed. This panel integrated 266 genes from the commercially available Xenium Human Brain Gene Expression Panel, which is optimized for characterizing major cell types in neural tissues (e.g., neurons, astrocytes, microglia, oligodendrocytes, vascular cells, and immune cells), with 50 custom-selected genes. The custom genes were chosen to specifically interrogate pathways relevant to our study, including SARS-CoV-2 viral entry factors, glial and vascular cell state markers, oxidative stress response, apoptosis, mitochondrial metabolism, and cell adhesion molecules (see Supplementary Table 2 for the complete gene list).

For the 316 target genes, a total of 319 probes were designed. Separate probes targeted the S, N, E, and ORF1ab regions of the SARS-CoV-2 genome, which were collectively analyzed under the “SARS-CoV-2” gene identifier. Two negative control probes were included to assess non-specific binding and confirm RNA-specificity of the signal. The workflow involved probe hybridization to target RNA, ligation, and rolling circle amplification to generate multiple copies of gene-specific barcodes for each RNA molecule. After amplification, background fluorescence was quenched. Slides were then loaded into the Xenium analyzer for automated cyclic fluorescence imaging and data acquisition. Following Xenium imaging, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the same sections for histological reference.

Xenium data processing

The raw data generated by the Xenium platform were directly imported and converted into Seurat R package (v.5.0.1) objects. Data normalization was performed using the integrated SCTransform function in Seurat.

To annotate cell types within each DAPI-defined segment on the Xenium slides, we employed the robust cell type decomposition (RCTD) method implemented in the spacexr R package (v.2.2.1). A publicly available single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq) dataset of the human HP–EC, comprising 219,058 cells and published by Daniel Franjic et al. [26], served as the reference for deconvolution. The two Seurat objects were subsequently batch corrected using the Harmony R package (v.1.2.0) to mitigate non-biological technical variations. Initial deconvolution using the public reference dataset revealed a degree of cell state mixing among microglia, astrocytes, and oligodendrocyte populations. To enhance resolution, subclasses of these glial cells were integrated separately after batch correction, prior to natural dimensionality reduction.

Definition of SARS-CoV-2-positive cells

SARS-CoV-2-positive cells were defined as those with expression values greater than 0 for the combined SARS-CoV-2 gene in the scale data. This threshold, which corresponds to expression above the dataset mean, was chosen to minimize false-positive assignments.

Differentially expressed gene analysis

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis was conducted using the FindMarkers function in the Seurat. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for significant DEGs was performed using the enrichGO function in the clusterProfiler R package (v.4.10.0). To ensure the biological relevance of the enrichment results, genes exhibiting expression patterns inconsistent with their annotated cell-type identity (e.g., lineage-restricted markers such as TRAC or CD163 expressed in neurons, or neuronal markers such as SLC17A6 expressed in glia, suggestive of mis-segmentation or ambient RNA) were manually curated and excluded from the GO analysis. These filtered genes are annotated in grey in Supplementary Table 4.

To control the false discovery rate (FDR) in multiple testing, the P values from the DEG analysis were adjusted using the Benjamini‒Hochberg (BH) procedure via the fdrtool R package (v.1.2.18). P values for the GO enrichment analysis results were adjusted directly within the enrichGO function using the Storey‒Tibshirani method.

Spatial neighborhood analysis

A custom Python script was developed to analyse cellular neighbourhood relationships. The core algorithm involved comparing the observed number of nearest-neighbour cell pairs within a pre-defined distance threshold between two cell groups against a null distribution generated by random spatial perturbation. The statistical significance of the observed spatial associations was assessed by comparing the observed count to this null model [27]. The resulting P values from these multiple comparisons were subjected to FDR control using the BH procedure.

Tissue segmentation and quantitative morphometry

To enable regional comparison of pathological changes within the HP–EC complex, we segmented the tissue into seven anatomically defined subregions, including the entorhinal cortex (EC), subiculum (SUB), hippocampus (CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4), and dentate gyrus (DG). Quantification was performed across all available fields of view (FOVs) from each tissue section.

Prior to automated analysis, vascular lumens and other non-tissue void areas within each FOV were manually excluded to prevent potential overestimation of pathological signals. Fluorescence intensity thresholds for each channel were calibrated individually for each section. Cellular features, including counts of specific cell types, were quantified using the HighPlex FL module in HALO® software (v.3.1.3; Indica Labs). For non-cellular structural assessments, area-based measurements were performed using the HALO® Object Colocalization module.

Statistical Analysis

Data points identified as statistical outliers by exceeding the population mean by more than twice the standard deviation were excluded from subsequent analyses. Group comparisons were performed using two tailed unpaired t tests for continuous variables. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All the statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (v.8.0.2).

Results

Study overview

To systematically investigate the impact of SARS-CoV-2 on the human brain, we established a comprehensive analytical pipeline integrating multimodal in situ approaches. We examined a well-characterized cohort of 20 autopsy-derived HP‒EC samples, comprising 9 individuals with severe SARS-CoV-2 infection (COVID-19 group), all of whom were unvaccinated and ultimately succumbed to respiratory failure, and 11 uninfected controls (CTL group) (Table 1). Our integrated methodology included (1) mIHC​ with 6 antibody panels to detect the SARS-CoV-2 S protein, identify brain cell subtypes, and characterize immune cell populations (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1); (2) ​​RNAscope in situ hybridization​​ for the precise spatial localization of the SARS-CoV-2 RNA (Fig. 1A); and (3) ​​Xenium in situ spatial transcriptomic analysis of​​ selected high-viral-load samples for high-resolution, genome-wide transcriptional profiling within the intact tissue architecture (Fig. 1B). The entire HP‒EC region was systematically subdivided into 7 anatomical subregions, including EC, SUB, hippocampus (CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4), and DG, based on the Allen Human Brain Anatomy Atlas [28] for a regional comparative analysis.

SARS-CoV-2 exhibits preferential neuronal tropism in the hippocampal formation

Both mIHC and RNAscope assays confirmed significantly higher loads of SARS-CoV-2 viral RNA and protein in HP‒EC samples from the COVID-19 group than in those from the control group (P < 0.01, Fig. 2A–E, Supplementary Fig. 1‒3). Spatial analysis within the examined HP–EC region revealed that viral proteins were more abundant in hippocampal subfields than in EC (Fig. 2F).

Cell type-specific analysis revealed that NeuN+ neurons were the most frequently infected cell population, with a notable predilection for the dentate gyrus (Fig. 2G‒L, Supplementary Fig. 4A‒D). A comparatively lower, yet detectable, viral signal was observed in CD31+ vascular endothelial cells within the HP–EC​ (Fig. 2M and N, Supplementary Fig. 4E). This pattern of viral distribution​ suggests that within the hippocampus, viral spread is more consistent with local parenchymal dissemination via neuronal and glial networks​ than with extensive haematogenous routes. Furthermore, TUNEL staining combined with mIHC staining revealed a significant increase in neuronal apoptosis in the COVID-19 group compared with controls, indicating an association between SARS-CoV-2 infection and enhanced neuronal cell death ​(Supplementary Fig. 5).

SARS-CoV-2 infection disrupts neuroglial junctional complexes and compromises structural integrity

To evaluate the impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection on essential brain adhesion complexes, we analyzed key neurostructural proteins and identified extensive alterations across the HP–EC region. The area of the signal for CASPR, which is essential for maintaining axoglial junctions and myelin integrity, showed a significant reduction in all examined brain regions in the COVID-19 group compared with controls (Fig. 3A–C, Supplementary Fig. 6A and B). This reduction indicates impaired axoglial integrity and was further supported by a decreased signal area for myelin basic protein (MBP), a key myelin sheath component, across all HP–EC subregions. (Fig. 3D–F, Supplementary Fig. 6C and D). Similarly, the expression of ZO-1, a pivotal tight junction protein essential for BBB integrity, showed a decreasing trend throughout the HP–EC in infected individuals (Fig. 3G–I, Supplementary Fig. 6E and F). These changes suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection impairs myelin sheath connectivity and tight junction integrity in the brain.

SARS-CoV-2 infection results in an altered immune microenvironment with impaired surveillance​ function

Given the observed structural disruptions and systemic immunosuppression, we profiled the local immune landscape using a comprehensive panel of cellular markers (Fig. 4A–E). Our analysis revealed a regionally heterogeneous immune landscape. Compared with those of the control group, the density of M1 macrophages (CD86+ & MHCII+) showed a region-dependent alterations, with a significant decrease specifically in CA3 (Fig. 4F and G, Supplementary Fig. 7A). By contrast, M2 macrophages density (CD163+ & CD206+) was reduced across multiple HP–EC subregions in COVID-19 tissues (Fig. 4H and I, Supplementary Fig. 7B). This reduction in macrophage abundance, particularly within the M2 subset, coincided with decreased densities of CD4+ T cells (CD3+ & CD4+) and CD8+ T cells (CD3+ & CD8+) in most subregions (Fig. 4J–M, Supplementary Fig. 7C and D). Furthermore, we detected a reduction in homeostatic, surveillant microglia (P2RY12+ & TMEM119+) across most HP–EC subregions (Fig. 4N and O, Supplementary Fig. 7E). Given that P2RY12 loss is an established marker of microglial activation, this shift indicates a transition away from a surveillance state. Collectively, the pervasive depletion of surveillant immune populations, such as​ T cells and homeostatic microglia, suggests a substantial impairment of immune surveillance within the infected CNS microenvironment.

Conversely, we observed a concomitant compensatory response characterized by an increased density of reactive microglia (Iba1+ & NOX2+) in specific regions, notably the subiculum, in COVID-19 tissues (Fig. 4P and Q, Supplementary Fig. 7F). Thus, the overall microglial response is characterized by a loss of homeostatic microglia alongside a focal increase in reactive populations. In situ spatial transcriptomics reveals cell type-specific responses to SARS-CoV-2 infection in the human brain

To gain deeper mechanistic insights into the cellular responses to infection at molecular resolution, we performed high-resolution Xenium in situ spatial transcriptomic analysis on two HP–EC sections with a confirmed high viral load (Fig. 5A and B). Following quality control, 316,546 single cells into 8 distinct cell types (Fig. 5C). We detected a mean of 214 transcripts per cell, corresponding to an average of 59 encoding genes, including four SARS-CoV-2-encoded genes (S, N, E, and ORF1ab, collectively referred to as “SARS-CoV-2”; see the Methods) (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Subclustering analysis revealed 36 transcriptionally distinct subpopulations within these major cell types. Neuronal subclusters included layer-specific excitatory neurons (ExCs) in the EC, region-specific ExCs in the HP and SUB (as defined by the Allen Institute), and inhibitory neuron (InN) subclusters characterized by canonical inhibitory markers (Fig. 5C1–C3 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Microglia and astrocyte subclusters were defined using functional gene signatures associated with homeostasis, phagocytosis, and reactivity (Fig. 5C4–C5 and Supplementary Fig. 8). The oligodendrocyte subclusters included oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), committed oligodendrocyte precursors (COPs), and mature oligodendrocytes (OLGs), which were identified by maturation markers (Fig. 5C6 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Immune cell subclusters, including T cells, myeloid cells, and macrophages, were identified using canonical markers (Fig. 5C7 and Supplementary Fig. 8). The vascular cell subclusters included endothelial cells (Endos), pericytes (PCs), smooth muscle cells (SMCs), and vascular leptomeningeal cells (VLMCs), based on known vascular markers (Fig. 5C8 and Supplementary Fig. 8). Across the major cell types, the average number of detected transcripts per cell was comparable: neurons (222 ± 13, mean ± SD), astrocytes (216 ± 12), microglia (215 ± 14), vascular cells (213 ± 14), oligodendrocytes (210 ± 9), and immune cells (210 ± 12) (Supplementary Table 3)​.

Spatial mapping demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 RNA was enriched in hippocampal subregions, particularly in the HP and SUB, consistent with our protein-level mIHC findings (Fig. 5D and E). Quantification revealed the highest degree of viral RNA colocalization with microglia (3.2% of total microglia) and the lowest with peripheral-derived immune cells (1.5% of total immune cells) (Fig. 5F). This transcript-level cellular tropism suggests that resident glial cells, especially microglia, may serve as important reservoirs for viral persistence in the CNS.

We next performed a DEG analysis between SARS-CoV-2-positive and SARS-CoV-2-negative cells within each cell type (q value < 0.05, log2fold change (log2FC) > 0.1) (Supplementary Table 4). This analysis identified 24–137 DEGs per major cell type, with astrocytes showing the most pronounced transcriptomic changes (137 DEGs) and immune cells exhibiting the fewest alterations (24 DEGs) (Fig. 6A). Upregulated genes were consistently enriched for pathways involved in viral entry (e.g., ACE2, GNLY, HS3ST4) and apoptosis​ (e.g., CASP8) across multiple cell types (Fig. 6B). Conversely, downregulated genes were predominantly associated with immune cell activation (e.g., CD163 and TRAC), immune regulation (e.g., ANXA1 and MS4A6A), and vascular integrity (e.g., MYH11, ACTA2, and THBS1) (Fig. 6B). These findings suggest that SARS-CoV-2 infection induces a coordinated downregulation of immune surveillance and vascular homeostasis, alongside an upregulation of viral entry and apoptotic pathways.

SARS-CoV-2 infection disrupts kinase signalling and cell‒cell interactions in the human brain

The GO enrichment analysis of the DEGs revealed distinct biological processes affected by SARS-CoV-2 infection. Upregulated DEGs were predominantly enriched in the mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade and Wnt signalling pathway (Fig. 6C), both of which are known to be involved in inflammatory responses and cellular stress pathways [29, 30]. Analysis of the downregulated DEGs revealed their functional impacts were highly specific to each cell type (Fig. 6D). In neurons, significant downregulation was observed for genes involved in phospholipase activity regulation and morphogenesis. Meanwhile, astrocytes and oligodendrocytes exhibited downregulation across a spectrum of processes, including gliogenesis, myelination, cell–cell junction assembly, oxidative stress response, and chemotaxis.​ For vascular cells, the impairments centered on smooth muscle contraction, cellular responses to transforming growth factor beta, and extracellular matrix organization. As for microglia and immune cells, the most significantly downregulated terms were linked to immune cell activation, response to lipoprotein particles, and phagocytosis.

SARS-CoV-2 infection has also been reported to promote cell‒cell fusion via protein-mediated interactions with ACE2 and host proteases, leading to syncytium formation that may exacerbate disease severity [19]. To explore potential infection-induced alterations in cellular interactions, we developed a custom Python script to analyze spatial neighbourhood relationships (see the Methods). This analysis compared the number of observed nearest-neighbour cell pairs within a 20 μm distance threshold between infected and noninfected cell populations relative to random spatial distributions [27]. In noninfected cells, somatic contact or proximity was observed at baseline levels, as expected for a normal brain tissue architecture (Fig. 6E). However, SARS-CoV-2-infected cells exhibited a significant increase in somatic proximity (q value < 0.05), with particularly enhanced interactions between neuronal and vascular cells, as well as between the neurons themselves (Fig. 6E and F). This remodelling of cellular spatial relationships suggests that SARS-CoV-2 infection drives the reorganization of the brain microenvironment, potentially facilitating viral spread or altering neural circuit function through mechanisms beyond its direct cytopathic effects. The increased neuronal–vascular proximity may reflect either compensatory mechanisms to maintain metabolic support or pathological changes contributing to the BBB dysfunction identified in our immunohistochemical analyses.

Discussion

This study provides a high-resolution, multiomics characterization of SARS-CoV-2 neuropathology in the human HP‒EC, revealing an integrated framework linking viral neurotropism, structural disruption, and immune dysregulation (Fig. 6G). Our integrated analysis demonstrates that SARS-CoV-2, once present in the CNS, preferentially infects hippocampal neurons, disrupts critical cell‒cell adhesion complexes, and triggers a paradoxical immune landscape characterized by surveillance failure coupled with focal inflammation (Fig. 6G).

Using complementary spatial approaches (mIHC, RNAscope and Xenium spatial transcriptomics), we observed that SARS-CoV-2 exhibits preferential neuronal tropism within the hippocampal formation. Our different detection platforms revealed assay-dependent cellular distributions. For instance, mIHC and RNAscope detected viral protein and RNA primarily in neurons, whereas Xenium transcriptomics showed an enrichment in microglia. We interpret these patterns as being consistent with initial neuronal infection, followed by microglial uptake of viral material during clearance attempts [31, 32]. Importantly, the pattern of viral distribution within the HP‒EC, characterized by abundant neuronal and glial infection but comparatively lower vascular signal, suggests that intra-CNS viral spread occurs primarily through local parenchymal dissemination via neuronal and neuroglial networks rather than extensive haematogenous routes [2, 16, 33]. This inference concerns within-region distribution and does not preclude prominent neurovascular unit pathology, as evidenced by the disruption of axoglial and tight-junction–associated proteins described below. Our findings extend previous reports of viral RNA and protein persistence in the CNS [3, 34] by precisely mapping their cellular and spatial distributions and directly linking them to structural and functional perturbations.

A central finding of our work is the significant disruption of key neuroglial junctional proteins (CASPR, MBP, and ZO-1), indicating compromised axoglial integrity, myelination, and BBB function. The reductions in CASPR and MBP expression suggest direct attack on the paranodal junctions and myelin sheaths, which are critical for saltatory conduction and neuronal communication [35–37]. This result could provide a mechanistic explanation for the synaptic loss and signal transduction deficits observed in brain organoids [38] and may contribute to the cognitive decline and processing deficits reported in patients with long COVID-19 [7, 39–41]. Concurrently, the downregulation of ZO-1 expression suggests a leaky BBB, a phenomenon reported in individuals with neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases, which could permit the influx of neurotoxic serum components and peripheral immune cells, exacerbating neural damage [42–44].

Another intriguing finding is the paradoxical immune landscape within the CNS. We observed a pervasive reduction in the number of immune surveillance cells, including T cells and homeostatic microglia (P2RY12+ & TMEM119+), coupled with focal increases in the number of reactive microglia (Iba1+ & NOX2+) in specific regions such as the subiculum. This dual state of ​​impaired immune surveillance​​ and ​​compensatory local inflammation is consistent with the systemic immunosuppressive state reported in severe COVID-19 patients [45, 46], characterized by monocyte expansion and reduced T cell numbers [47]. The convergence of peripheral and CNS immune dysfunction suggests that SARS-CoV-2 may induce a coordinated, multi-compartment immune deficit that extends beyond the periphery to affect CNS immune surveillance, potentially creating a permissive environment for viral persistence. The subsequent focal microglial activation likely represents a localized, albeit insufficient, attempt to contain the infection and clear cellular debris, ultimately contributing to a chronic neuroinflammatory state [21, 48, 49].

Spatial transcriptomic analysis provided molecular insights into these phenotypic observations. In SARS-CoV-2-infected cells, we observed upregulation of MAPK and Wnt signalling pathways, indicating activation of core inflammatory and stress-response programs [50–52], alongside broad downregulation of genes involved in immune function, myelination, and vascular integrity. This transcriptional signature reveals a coordinated suppression of homeostatic cellular functions concurrent with stress pathway activation, providing a molecular foundation for the structural and immune alterations described above. The convergence of pathway-level changes across multiple cell types suggests that SARS-CoV-2 infection triggers a systemic disruption of CNS homeostasis that extends beyond directly infected cells.

A particularly novel finding is the increased somatic proximity between infected cells, especially neurons and vascular elements. This spatial remodelling may result from virus-induced cytopathic effects, such as spike protein-mediated syncytium formation [18–20, 53], or represent a dysregulated attempt at neurovascular coupling to meet the metabolic demands. This altered cellular architecture may facilitate viral spread and disrupt neural circuit function, representing a new mechanism for neurological sequelae beyond direct neuronal death.

In the current postpandemic era, global attention to COVID-19 is waning, yet the burden of long COVID-19 persists. The pandemic is expected to exhibit recurrent waves of varying intensity in the foreseeable future. Our findings have important implications for clinical management. The observed disruptions in cell–cell adhesion and altered immune dynamics highlight the need for vigilant monitoring of neurological symptoms in COVID-19 patients, especially those with severe infections. Early detection of cognitive changes and memory deficits may allow the timely administration of interventions that can reduce long-term neurodegenerative risks. Therapeutic strategies focused on stabilizing junctional complexes (e.g., ZO-1 and CASPR) could help preserve BBB integrity and axonal function. Furthermore, immunomodulatory treatments should be carefully designed to enhance CNS immune surveillance without aggravating neuroinflammation.

We acknowledge several limitations of our study. First, the moderate sample size and regional focus restricted to the HP–EC limit the generalizability of our findings to other brain regions. Second, the presence of false-negative cells for “SARS-CoV-2” transcripts may underestimate the true extent of infection. Third, we did not examine peripheral nervous system tissues (e.g., olfactory or vagal neurons) and therefore cannot determine the initial route by which SARS-CoV-2 enters the brain. Fourth, due to the nature of autopsy studies, we cannot completely rule out the influence of pre-existing comorbidities on the observed neuropathology, although we have made efforts to control for these factors by using a well-matched control group and by demonstrating a direct spatial correlation between viral load and tissue damage. Finally, a methodological consideration for our exploratory differential expression analysis is the use of library size–based normalization (SCTransform). In settings with high viral transcript counts, this can lead to an apparent reduction in host genes after normalization, a known technical artifact that may influence effect size estimates [54]. Future studies with larger cohorts and expanded sampling across multiple brain regions will be essential to validate and generalize our findings. The integration of advanced imaging techniques and single-cell multiomics approaches may further elucidate the spatiotemporal dynamics and molecular mechanisms underlying SARS-CoV-2-induced neuropathology.

In conclusion, this study provides a detailed characterization of the effects of SARS-CoV-2 on the human brain, showing that the virus not only disrupts cell‒cell adhesion and BBB integrity but also alters the immune cell density and transcriptional profiles. These findings improve our understanding of the complex interplay between viral infection, structural changes, and immune responses within the CNS. By providing insights into the underlying mechanisms of SARS-CoV-2-induced brain damage, this work lays the foundation for developing targeted interventions that may reduce the long-term neurological consequences of COVID-19. As we continue to elucidate the intricate relationship between SARS-CoV-2 and the brain, our findings highlight the importance of sustained research efforts to mitigate the neurological burden of this disease.
