Inhibiting HSP27 activates the XBP1s/CerS1 interplay, which triggers DRP1-driven mitophagy, thereby protecting against cell death and promoting the KSHV lytic cycle in primary effusion lymphoma cells
Authors: Roberta Gonnella, Vincenzo Corrado, Giulio Francesco Scaffidi, Rossella Benedetti, Michele Di Crosta, Roberta Zarrella, Maria Saveria Gilardini Montani, Roberta Santarelli, Mara Cirone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPEL is an aggressive B-cell lymphoma that in the majority of cells harbors latent KSHV, although appropriate stimuli can induce viral replication. These include HDAC inhibitors such as butyrate, activation of endoplasmic reticulum (ER)/UPR stress, and exogenous administration of ceramide 18. These treatments reduce cell survival, but also activate adaptive branches of the UPR such as the Ire1α-XBP1s axis and/or trigger macroautophagy to counteract cell death, processes whose output may be manipulated by KSHV. HSPs are also upregulated by several cytotoxic treatments and support both cell survival and KSHV replication, suggesting a complex relationship between cell and viral fate. In this study, we demonstrate that HSP27 inhibition reduces PEL cell survival, activates ER stress including XBP1s, and upregulates CerS1, the enzyme that synthesizes ceramide 18. We further discovered a crosstalk between XBP1s and CerS1 that enhances protection against ER stress during HSP27 inhibition also promoting DRP1-dependent pro-survival mitophagy and triggers KSHV reactivation from latency. In conclusion this study suggests that HSP27 plays a previously unrecognized central role in controlling the UPR, CerS1 and mitochondrial autophagy, influencing both cell survival and KSHV lytic cycle in PEL cells.Subject terms: B-cell lymphoma, Biochemistry

Introduction

Heat shock proteins (HSPs) and the unfolded protein response (UPR) play a crucial role in enabling tumor cells to cope with intrinsic and extrinsic stress. HSPs, a family of chaperone proteins classified by their molecular weight, are commonly overexpressed in tumor cells [1]. Specific HSPs, such as BiP and some small HSPs, localize to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), where they aid in protein folding and prevent their accumulation in the ER, thereby regulating the activation of the UPR sensors Ire1α, PERK, and ATF6 [1, 2]. On the other hand, ER stress, through the activation of Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) may regulate the expression of HSPs such as HSP90 [3] or through the activation of p38 Mitogen Activated Protein Kinase (MAPK) may activate HSP27 [4] whose inhibition in turn triggers ER stress [5]. UPR activation can also be triggered by several other stimuli including changes in Ca2+ levels, oxidative stress, and the reduced or too high availability of nutrients such as lipids. Interestingly, the ER regulates the synthesis of sterols and phospholipids, as well as that of several enzymes involved in lipid metabolism [6] such as, for example, ceramide synthases (CERSs) enzymes. Interestingly, ER stress can be transmitted from the ER to mitochondria and vice-versa via the mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum (MAM), a platform for ER-mitochondria communication which is involved in the regulation of mitochondrial autophagy (mitophagy) [7]. Some HSPs belonging to the HSP70 family, e.g. mortalin, mainly reside in mitochondria, where they act as chaperones and stress survival factors [8]. More recently, HSP27 has been reported to play a role in the protection of mitochondrial function, by interacting with CerS1 and inhibiting the synthesis of C18-ceramide, whose accumulation causes mitochondrial dysfunction and activates mitophagy [9]. HSPs, including HSP27, play an important role also in the nucleus, where may sustain the expression of molecules involved in DNA repair mechanisms through homologous recombination (HR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), as we previously observed in primary effusion lymphoma (PEL) cells [10]. PEL is an aggressive type of lymphoma whose pathogenesis is closely associated with the gammaherpesvirus known as Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV). This oncovirus remains latent in most PEL cells, but can be reactivated by certain stimuli. Both UPR activation and HSPs influence the survival of PEL cells [11] and control KSHV lytic cycle activation both in vitro [12] and in vivo [13]. Regarding UPR, it has been reported that the promoter of the viral replication and transcription activator (RTA), a protein playing a key role in the switch from latency to lytic replication, contains X-box binding protein 1 spliced (XBP1s)-responsive elements [14]. Therefore, XBP1s, originating from the endoribonuclease activity of Ire1α, in addition to numerous other processes, can directly induce KSHV lytic reactivation [14–16]. However, the other two branches of the UPR, PERK and ATF6, play a role in viral replication, although KSHV can actively modulate the overall output of the UPR to its own advantage [15]. HSP70 and HSP90 are also essential for the KSHV lytic cycle, the former by contributing to the formation of replication and transcription compartments (RTCs), required for viral genome amplification [17], and the latter by sustaining several transcription factors known to be HSP90 client proteins [18]. Accordingly, we have previously shown that HSP70 and HSP90 support KSHV lytic reactivation by stabilizing transcription factors critical for lytic viral gene expression [19]. However, the impact of HSP27 on the regulation of KSHV life cycle has not been investigated so far. As reported above, HSP27 plays a role in preventing ER stress and protecting from mitochondrial function. Considering that ER stress [14–16] and mitophagy may be activated by KSHV viral interferon regulatory factor 1 (vIRF-1) to sustain viral replication [20], we explored the possibility that HSP27 inhibition could alter cell death/survival balance in PEL cells and result in the activation of KSHV lytic cycle. This hypothesis is also supported by a recent study showing that KSHV-mediated activation of mitochondrial fission, which precedes mitophagy and is regulated by the dynamin-related protein (DRP1), promotes viral replication [21]. Furthermore, inhibition of HSP27 upregulates CerS1, which synthesizes ceramide C18, whose exogenous administration can also trigger KSHV replication [22].

Results

Targeting HSP27 triggers ER stress, activates UPR and reduces cell survival in PEL cells

HSP27 has been reported to confer protection against ER stress in prostate cancer cells, whereas its inhibition has been shown to induce ER stress in these cells [5]. In this study, we found that HSP27 inhibition by J2 led to an impairment of survival in PEL cells (Fig. 1A) and asked if it could correlate with the induction of ER stress and UPR activation. To address this question, BC3 and BCBL-1 lymphoma cells were treated by J2, at 10 and 20 µM doses, and the activation of UPR was investigated. We observed the upregulation of the pro-survival UPR marker Binding Immunoglobulin Protein (BiP) and the pro-apoptotic molecule C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) (Fig. 1B), along with the appearance of the spliced and activated form of X-box binding protein 1 (XBP1s) (Fig. 1C), generated by the endoribonuclease activity of Inositol Requiring Enzyme 1 (IRE1) α. These findings confirm that cell death was associated with ER stress induction. Treatment with J2 also increased PARP1 cleavage in PEL cells (Fig. 1D), suggesting that reduction of cell survival was linked to apoptosis induction. Similar to J2 treatment, activation of the UPR (Fig. 1E) and decreased cell viability (Fig. 1F) were observed following HSP27 silencing with specific siRNA both in BC3 and BCBL1 cells, further supporting the protective role of this HSP in PEL cells.

HSP27 inhibition activates a positive feed-back loop between XBP1s and CerS1

ER stress may alter lipid metabolism [23] and on the other hand, dysregulating the lipid production, e.g. increasing the amount of ceramides, may result in ER stress [24]. Here, we asked if ER stress induced by HSP27 inhibition could influence the expression of CerS1, involved in ceramide 18 synthesis, and found that this enzyme was upregulated in J2-treated cells (Fig. 2A) as well as following HSP27 silencing (Fig. 2B). Silencing of another HSP, namely HSP110, slightly affected the expression level of CerS1 (Supplementary Fig. S1), suggesting a more specific involvement of HSP27 in this effect. Given that the expression and/or function of enzymes regulating lipid synthesis and modification may be affected by ER stress [6], and considering that XBP1s plays a key role in the translation of de novo lipogenesis genes [25], we explored whether XBP1s activation by HSP27 inhibition could influence CerS1 expression level in PEL cells. As shown in Fig. 2C, inhibition of XBP1s by 4µ8C counteracted the J2-induced increase in CerS1, suggesting that XBP1s was involved in this effect. We then found that inhibition of CerS1 by P053, in turn, reduced XBP1s expression (Fig. 2D), highlighting a previously unrecognized positive feedback mechanism between these two molecules, triggered by HSP27 inhibition. This feedback mechanism occurred at the transcriptional level, as the mRNA expression level mirrored the results obtained at the protein level when we used 4u8c or P053 in J2-treated cells (Fig. 2E). However, we also found that the interaction between Cers1 and XBP1s slightly increased following J2 treatment (Supplementary Fig. S2). The finding that HSP27 can influence CerS1 expression through XBP1 suggests an additional level of CerS1 regulation by this HSP. This may occur alongside the previously reported negative control of CerS1 activity through its direct interaction with HSP27 [9]. However, a slight involvement of ATF6 and PERK in the regulation of Cers1 was also observed in J2-treated PEL cells (Supplementary Fig. S3), likely correlating with the cross-talk between the UPR sensors.

XBP1-CerS1 interplay activates DRP1-dependent mitophagy

HSP27 has been reported to protect mitochondrial function by interacting with CerS1 and reducing ceramide 18 production [9]. Moreover, as said above, ER stress may affect mitochondria, as it can be transmitted to these organelles via MAM [7]. Therefore, here we asked whether HSP27 inhibition could affect mitochondria and alter mitochondrial dynamic in PEL cells. We observed that ROS, primarily produced by damaged mitochondria, increased in J2-treated cells (Fig. 3A), suggesting that this drug was inducing mitochondrial dysfunction. Moreover, DRP1, a protein involved in mitochondrial fission [26], which is a process activated following mitochondrial damage, was upregulated by J2, both in BC3 and BCBL 1 cells (Fig. 3B). We then asked whether the activation of XBP1s and the upregulation of CerS1 could contribute to increase the expression level of DRP1 and found that the inhibition of XBP1 by 4µ8C (Fig. 3C) or that of CerS1 by P053 (Fig. 3D) counteracted the upregulation of DRP1 induced by J2. This suggests that XBP1s, Cesr1 and their interplay played a key role in upregulating DRP1 in J2-treated PEL cells.

Besides mitochondrial fission, DRP1 may promote mitophagy, also independently of Parkin, a process that strongly influences cell survival [27, 28]. Therefore, we evaluated mitophagy in PEL cells treated by J2 and found that the number of yellow puncta, revealing the co-localization of MitoTracker (a mitochondrial marker green) and LysoTracker (a lysosomal marker red), typically induced by mitophagy activation, increased in J2-treated cells. They also increased in PEL cells exposed to carbonyl cyanide 3-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) [29] (Fig. 4A) a drug causing dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) [30], which was used as positive control for mitophagy activation. These results suggest that HSP27 inhibition was activating mitochondrial autophagy. Interestingly, mitophagy was counteracted by DRP1 inhibition by Mdivi-1 [31] (Fig. 4A), confirming the pivotal role of this protein in driving mitochondrial autophagy in J2-treated PEL cells.

To further evaluate autophagy activation by J2 through DRP1, we monitored the autophagic flux and found that the increase of LC3II and the reduction of p62/SQSTM1 were induced by J2 and counteracted by Mdivi-1 (Fig. 4B). We then evaluated the expression level of Hydroxyacyl-CoA Dehydrogenase Trifunctional Multienzyme Complex Subunit Alpha (HADHA), a protein mainly degraded through mitophagy [32] and found that its expression level was reduced by J2-treatment. HDHA reduction was counteracted by pretreatment with Mdivi-1 (Fig. 4C) as well as by DRP1 silencing in J2-treated BC3 and BCBL1 cells (Fig. 4D), further confirming the pivotal role of DRP1 in mitophagy induction. Given that the interplay between XBP1s and Cers1 was leading to the activation of DRP1, also their inhibition prevented HADHA reduction in J2-treated cells (Fig. 4E, F).

Mitophagy prevents cell death and contributes to KSHV lytic cycle activation in J2-treated cells

We then evaluated if mitophagy could influence PEL cell death/survival and found that Mdivi-1 as well as DRP1 silencing further reduced cell survival compared to J2 treatment (Fig. 5A, B), indicating that mitophagy was playing a protective role during HSP27 inhibition. XBP1s activation [14, 33] and the administration of exogenous ceramide 18, lipid primarily synthetized by CerS1, has been reported to trigger KSHV replication [22]. As mitochondrial fission and mitophagy can also sustain KSHV replication [21, 34], here we asked whether DRP1-driven mitophagy, activated by XBP1-CerS1 interplay, could activate KSHV lytic cycle in cells undergoing HSP27 inhibition. To this end, PEL cells, pretreated or nor with the DRP1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 and exposed to J2 were assessed by IFA for expression of the early and late lytic antigens Kaposi’s Sarcoma-associated Herpesvirus bZIP protein (KbZIP) and gp64, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5C, the number of cells expressing these lytic proteins increased following J2 treatment and was reduced by its combination with Mdivi-1, suggesting that mitophagy induced by DRP1 contributed to lytic cycle activation in cells in which HSP27 was inhibited. This effect may correlate with the reduction of cell death which allows the virus to replicate before the host cell will die.

Discussion

This study shows that inhibition of HSP27 impairs PEL cell survival and activates a previously unrecognized positive feed-back loop between XBP1s and CerS1 which, besides counteracting cell death, promotes KSHV lytic cycle. The novel regulatory axis between XBP1s and CerS1, discovered in this study, implies that CerS1, by sustaining XBP1s, potentially amplifies the activity of this adaptive branch of the UPR. On the other hand, XBP1s, by upregulating CerS1, in addition to other previously discovered enzymes involved in lipid synthesis [35], can strengthen endoplasmic reticulum (ER) homeostasis altered by chaperone inhibition, e.g. by amplifying the amount of ER membranes. This interaction adds a new layer to the complex integration between lipid metabolism, regulated by CerS1 [34] and proteostasis, mainly regulated by XBP1s [36], under stress conditions caused by HSP27 inhibition. Thus, HSP27, an HSP for long time considered a passive gatekeeper, emerges from current and recent studies as an important stress protective factor [5]. Indeed, its inhibition perturbs proteostasis by activating XBP1s and shapes the lipid-mediated signaling landscape, by upregulating CerS1. Furthermore, XBP1s/CerS1-interplay by mediating DRP1 upregulation triggered mitophagy, as revealed by lysotracker and mitotracker co-staining and HADHA, LC3I/II and p62 expression level. Mitophagy played a key role in sustaining cell survival in PEL cells subjected to HSP27 inhibition and promoted the activation of KSHV lytic cycle. Accordingly, mitophagy, as well as mitochondrial fission, process regulated by DRP1 and preceding mitophagy, have been reported to be activated by KSHV and facilitate its replication [21, 37].

The balance between cell death and survival, sensed by KSHV, regulates the latent-to-lytic switch, which in turn influences the survival of virus-infected tumor cells. Previous studies have indeed reported that KSHV is able to activate emergency replication in cells in which apoptosis cannot be avoided [38] and that it may also hijack processes activated by tumor cells to resist cell death to sustain its replication. These include the activation of macroautophagy as well as that of the adaptive branches of UPR, processes that are manipulated by viruses to their own advantage [14–16, 39, 40]. We and other authors have also shown that HSPs, including HSP70 and HSP90, which mainly protect cells from death, can simultaneously enable KSHV replication in PEL cells [17, 19, 41, 42]. These findings suggest that adaptive cellular stress responses that extend cell lifespan may give the virus time to escape from the dying cell, avoiding death with it and ensuring its survival in a new host cell. The present study also highlights how UPR, ceramides, and mitophagy, three cellular signals often considered in isolation for their effect on cell death and KSHV lytic cycle activation, may converge and influence both processes in cells where HSP27 is inhibited. This is important as, a massive activation of lytic replication induces cell lysis in most KSHV-bearing tumor cells, while a moderate viral production, by a limited number of cells, may contribute to viral carcinogenesis [43]. Therefore, from an anticancer therapeutic perspective, the results of this study, although they would benefit from further validation in primary PEL cells or in vivo studies, suggest that inhibition of DRP1-driven mitophagy combined with J2 treatment could anticipate lymphoma cell death induced by this drug and simultaneously prevent KSHV from having time to escape and spread the infection. Indeed, the expression of late lytic viral antigens and the release of virus from a small percentage of PEL cells, although lytic for them, may promote carcinogenesis [43].

Materials and methods

Cell cultures and treatments

BC3 and BCBL1 KSHV-positive PEL cell lines were used in this study. BC3 cell line was purchased by ATCC (CRL-2277) and BCBL1 was kindly supplied by prof. P. Monini (Istituto Superiore di Sanità, Rome, Italy). They were grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine (FBS) (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA), streptomycin/penicillin (100 mg/mL) (Aurogene, Rome, Italy) and L-glutamine (2 mM) (Aurogene, Rome, Italy), at 37 °C in an incubator supplemented with 5% CO2 in humified setting.

Cells were seeded in 6 wells plates at concentration of 4 × 105/ml and were treated with the following chemicals: J2 (HSP27 inhibitor, Selleckem, Houston, TX, USA; cat. n. S3404), 4µ8C (IRE1 RNAse inhibitor, Selleckem, Houston, TX, USA cat. n. S7272), P053 (CerS1 inhibitor, Cayman Chemicals, Ann Arbor, Michigan, USA; cat n. 26113), Mdivi-1 (Drp1 inhibitor, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; cat. n HY-15886), CCCP (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; cat. N. HY-100941), Ceapin A7 (ATF6 signaling inhibitor, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; cat. n. HY-108434), GSK2656157 (PERK inhibitor, MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA; cat. n. HY-13820). Drugs were added to cell cultures at the final concentration of 20 µM (J2), 20 µM (4µ8C), 100 nM (P053), 50 µM (Mdivi-1), 20 µM (CCCP), Ceapin A7 (6 µM), GSK2656157 (20 µM).

Cells were pre-treated with 4µ8C (20 µM), P053 (100 nM), Mdivi-1(50 µM), Ceapin A7 (6 µM), and GSK2656157 (20 µM) for 1 h before adding J2 (20 µM) and were collected 24 h later or at the time point indicated in each figure. Untreated cells were used as a control group (CT).

Trypan blue exclusion assay

Cell viability was assessed by Trypan blue exclusion assay, 24 h after treatments. Cell suspension was mixed with trypan blue (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA; cat. n. T8154), a dye that can stain dead cells exclusively, therefore live cells were recognized and counted by light microscopy using a Neubauer hemocytometer. The experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Immunofluorescence

Cells were harvested 24 h after treatments then centrifuged at 1200 g, washed 1x with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA cat n. 18912–014), seeded onto multispot microscope slides (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; cat. n. 2320) and left to air dry. Cells were fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, PA, USA; cat. n. 157-8) for 30 min, washed 3 times in PBS, permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA; cat. n. T-8787)–PBS for 5 min at RT, then incubated with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA)–1% glycine–PBS for 30 min at RT. Samples were then incubated with the primary antibody diluted appropriately (mouse monoclonal anti KBZIP dil. 1: 300, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA, cat n. sc-69797) and mouse monoclonal anti gp64 dil 1: 50, (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA, cat n. sc-65444) for 1 h at RT. After three washes with PBS, sample were incubated with secondary antibody (Cy3-conjugated sheep anti-mouse IgG, dil. 1:1000, Jackson Immuno Research. cat n. 515-165-062) in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. After three washes with PBS, samples were stained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA, USA; cat n. D9564) and microscope slides were mounted using PBS-glycerol 1:1. Slides were visualized by a Apotome axio observer Z1 inverted microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany), equipped with an Axiocam MRM Rev.3 camera at 10 × magnification.

MitoTrackerTM Green-FM staining and Lysotracker TM Red-FM Staining

BC3 cells were seeded on 6 wells plates and then treated with CCCP (20 µM) or J2 (20 µM). Cells that underwent double treatment with J2 and P053 were first incubated with P053 (100 nM) for 1 h then they were supplemented with J2 (20 µM), cells without any treatment represented negative control (CT). The day after mitochondrial labelling was performed by adding MitoTrackerTM Green-FM (Invitrogen, MA, USA; cat n. M7514) fluorescent dye (50 nM) to cell culture medium for 30 min at 37 °C. Cells were then washed with PBS, re-seeded in 6-wells plates in RPMI supplemented with Lysotracker dye (50 nM) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After one washing with PBS cells were spotted on microscope slides and finally analyzed with Apotome Axio Observer Z1 inverted microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with an AxioCam MRM Rev.3 (Germany) at 10× magnification.

Measurement of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production

Cells were incubated with 10 µM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA; Molecular Probes, CA, USA) for 20 min at 37 °C. DCF-DA is a fluorogenic dye that, after diffusion into the cell, is oxidized by H2O2 into 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein (DCF), a highly fluorescent compound that can be detected by fluorescence spectroscopy. After incubation with DCF-DA, cells were washed with 1x PBS, and then analyzed in FL-1 by a FACS-Calibur flow cytometer.

Western blot analysis

Cells were collected after treatments, washed with PBS, centrifuged at 1500 RPM and lysed in RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 8), 0.5% deoxycholic acid, 0.1% SDS, protease and phosphatase inhibitors). After detecting protein concentration using Quick Start Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA; cat n. 5000206), equal amounts of proteins (10 µg) from each sample were loaded onto 4–12% NuPage Bis-Tris gels (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA; cat n. NP0323BOX), subjected to electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham, Buckinghamshire, UK, cat n. 10600002). After transfer, membranes were cut into slices corresponding to a suitable molecular weight range and, after 30 min incubation in a blocking solution (PBS, Tween 20 0.1%, BSA 2%), each of them was incubated with appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. RNA interference

Silencing of HSP27, HSP110 and DRP1 was achieved by using specific small interfering RNAs (siRNA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Heidelberg, Germany, sc-29350, sc-35597, and sc-43732 respectively). PEL cells were seeded in six-wells culture plates at a concentration of 4 × 105/ml in RPMI medium without serum and antibiotics. For transfection 10 µl of INTERFERin reagent (Polyplus, Illkirch, France; cat. n. 101000016) in combination with 50 pmol of specific small interfering RNA (siRNA duplex) or with non-targeting (scramble) siRNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Heidelberg, Germany cat. n. sc-29350) were diluted in 200 µl RPMI without serum and antibiotics, incubated for 10 minutes at RT then added to each well. For HSP27 silencing in BCBL1 cells, siRNA from Dharmacon, Lafayette, CO, USA (cat. n. L-005269-00) was used at the same concentration as for Santa Cruz Biotechnology’s siRNA. In all experiments, the final siRNA concentration was 25 nM/well.

After 24 h, cell viability was assessed by trypan blue exclusion assay, and cells were harvested for downstream analyses. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription and real time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

BC3 and BCBL1 cell lines were seeded in 6 wells plates at concentration of 4 × 105/ml and pretreated with 4μ8 C (20 μM) or P053 (100 nM) for 1 h before adding J2 (20 μM), untreated cells were used as negative control (CT). Four hours later total RNA was extracted by using TRIzolTM Reagent (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA, cat. n.15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed on total RNA and subsequently quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR) was carried out by using SensiFast SYBR No-ROX kit (Bioline, TN, USA; cat. n. BIO-94005). For measurement of spliced XBP1 (XBP1s) we used a Forward primer designed to spam the 26 base pair intron and thus to anneal only the spliced XBP1 mRNA, as previously described [44].

Immunoprecipitation

PEL cells (1 × 10⁷ per sample) were treated with J2 (20 μM) for 4 h, harvested, and lysed in RIPA buffer. Protein concentrations were determined, and 500 μg of extracted proteins from each sample were subjected to a pre-clearing step by adding 20 μl of Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA, sc-2003) for 1 h at 4 °C on a rocker platform in a final volume of 500 μl. Following centrifugation, supernatants were transferred to fresh tubes and incubated overnight at 4 °C with 4 μl of primary antibody (mouse monoclonal anti-CerS1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., Dallas, TX, USA, cat. no. 293497) on a rocker platform. Subsequently, 20 μl of resuspended Protein A/G PLUS-Agarose were added, and samples were incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with rocking. Beads were pelleted by centrifugation, washed three times with lysis buffer, and immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted with gel loading buffer at 85 °C for 10 min, followed by western blot analysis.

Densitometric analysis

Bands derived by western blots were quantified by densitometric analysis through ImageJ software, from the NIH website (http://imagej.nih.gov).

Statistical analysis

Results are shown as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed with Graphpad Prism® software (Graphpad software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Students' test or a nonparametric one-way ANOVA test were used to measure statistical significance. Difference was considered statistically significant when p-value was: * < 0.05.
