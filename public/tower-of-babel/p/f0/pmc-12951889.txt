Rapid assessment of phytoplankton assemblages using Next Generation Sequencing and Barcode of Life Data System: a widely applicable HAB-ID toolkit for detecting and monitoring biodiversity loss and harmful algal blooms
Authors: Natalia V. Ivanova, L. Cynthia Watson, Jérôme Comte, Kyrylo Bessonov, Arusyak Abrahamyan, Sophie Crevecoeur, Susan B. Watson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Harmful algal blooms have important implications for the health, functioning, and services of aquatic ecosystems. Our ability to detect and monitor these events is often challenged by the lack of rapid and cost-effective methods to identify bloom-forming organisms and their potential for toxin production. Here, we developed and applied a combination of DNA barcoding and Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) for the rapid assessment of phytoplankton community composition with a focus on two important indicators of ecosystem health: toxigenic bloom-forming cyanobacteria and impaired planktonic biodiversity. To develop this molecular toolset for identification of cyanobacterial and algal species present in HABs (harmful algal blooms), hereafter called HAB-ID, we achieved three goals: creating a validated reference database, optimizing molecular protocols, and developing original bioinformatics pipeline tailored to uncertainty of algal taxonomy. The BOLD (Barcode of Life Data System) 16S reference database from cultures of 211 cyanobacterial and algal strains representing 102 species with particular focus on bloom and toxin producing taxa was constructed with Sanger sequencing and further refined using Single Molecule Real Time Sequencing (SMRT-sequencing). Using the new reference database of 16S rDNA sequences and constructed mock communities of mixed strains for protocol validation, we developed new NGS primer sets which can recover 16S from both cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algal chloroplasts. We also developed DNA extraction protocols for cultured algal strains and environmental samples, which match commercial kit performance and offer a cost-efficient solution for large scale ecological assessments of harmful blooms while giving benefits of reproducibility and increased accessibility. Our innovative bioinformatics pipeline was designed to handle low taxonomic resolution for problematic genera of cyanobacteria such as the Anabaena-Aphanizomenon-Dolichospermum complex, two clusters of Anabaena (I and II), Planktothrix and Microcystis. This newly developed HAB-ID toolset was further validated by applying it to assess cyanobacterial and algal composition in field samples from waterbodies with recurrent HABs events.

Introduction

Outbreaks of harmful algal blooms (HABs) dominated by toxigenic and nuisance cyanobacteria are increasingly reported at the global scale (Winter et al., 2011; Stumpf et al., 2012; Bullerjahn & Post, 2014; Steffen et al., 2014; Pick, 2016) with adverse effects on the health, resilience of aquatic food-webs and many negative socioeconomic impacts, such as decreased water quality, recreation, businesses and property values (Michalak et al., 2013; Roegner et al., 2014; Smith et al., 2019). Under high nutrient concentration, dominance of bloom-forming cyanobacteria is associated with reduction of overall phytoplankton diversity and biomass, resulting in lower zooplankton community diversity affecting aquatic food-webs (Watson, McCauley & Downing, 1997; Bockwoldt et al., 2017; Glibert, 2017). HABs have garnered significant national and international attention, yet their management remains a major problem, as these events and their associated risks are difficult to identify and predict in a timely fashion. Expedient detection and accurate identification of toxigenic and bloom-forming species are essential to assess the potential risks associated with a bloom development, and to evaluate the main factors that drive their spatial and temporal dynamics. This information is fundamental to any effective management plan developed to predict, manage, and reduce HAB frequency, severity, and toxicity.

Traditionally, cyanobacteria and eukaryotic microalgae have been classified and identified by microscopic analysis of key morphological/cellular characteristics such as pigmentation, cell arrangement and size (unicell/filament/trichome/colony), specialised cells (heterocytes/akinetes/zygospores), gas vacuoles and sheath, cell wall, flagella, plastid number and arrangement, division planes, etc. (Komárek & Anagnostidis, 1986; Komárek & Anagnostidis, 1989; Baker, 1991; Baker, 1992). However, many cyanobacteria are difficult to identify due to cryptic species and these diagnostic characters (e.g., size, colonial configuration, gas vacuoles, and specialised cells) vary under different environmental conditions and can be lost during cultivation (Rudi et al., 1997; Lyra et al., 2001). Komárek & Anagnostidis (1989) noted that up to 50% of strains in culture collections did not correspond to diagnostic characters of the taxa to which they were initially assigned. Because of these issues with traditional identification methods, cyanobacterial systematics have been undergoing widespread revision using a polyphasic approach, combining molecular analysis of 16S rRNA gene and other markers (Dvořák et al., 2015) with biochemical and other traits (Komárek, 2016). 16S rDNA is commonly used to identify algae and cyanobacteria and has been applied in DNA barcoding of harmful cyanobacteria (Kurobe et al., 2013), phylogenetic evaluation of heterocytous cyanobacteria (Rajaniemi et al., 2005), and accessing symbiotic cyanobacteria community in ascidians (López-Legentil et al., 2011). DNA barcoding utilizes sequence diversity in short standardized gene regions for species identification and discovery (Hebert et al., 2003) and although the use of DNA barcodes for species identification has been increasing, there are still no comprehensive reference libraries for freshwater phytoplankton, especially for toxin-producing species.

As a fundamental part of this project outcome, we developed a curated reference database with focus on bloom-and toxin-producers, generated for cyanobacterial 16S and algal 16S chloroplast rDNA. This database was derived from culture collections and is hosted in Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD) (Ratnasingham & Hebert, 2007), an analytical workbench and repository for DNA barcodes linking voucher specimen information (collection data and digital images) with sequence data, including laboratory audit trail and sequence trace files.

Given the socioeconomic importance of HABs, a rapid method for community-wide phytoplankton assessment offers an important tool to detect and monitor for bloom-forming and toxigenic taxa and serve as an effective early warning system for the development of potentially harmful blooms. Molecular techniques such as quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) have been used for the rapid detection of toxigenic and bloom-forming cyanobacterial and algal species (Vaitomaa et al., 2003; Hotto, Satchwell & Boyer, 2007; Rinta-Kanto et al., 2009; Scherer et al., 2017), but this technique is limited to a few species at a time. Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) offers an alternative and potentially more powerful metagenomic approach to rapidly and accurately identify multiple species from a mixed sample. This approach has been used successfully in previous studies to assess environmental samples for bacterial, cyanobacterial and phytoplankton composition (Eiler et al., 2013; Scherer et al., 2017), diatom species assemblages (Zimmermann et al., 2015), and for evaluating methodological biases in mock communities (Kermarrec et al., 2013; Singer et al., 2016b; Singer et al., 2016a). Yet, all studies to date have been conducted alone with their own sets of primers and experimental conditions, moreover, there is still no standardized and comprehensive database of cyanobacterial and phytoplankton sequences which is urgently needed.

Here we report the results of a multi-year study designed to develop a HAB-ID toolset for rapid assessment of algal and cyanobacterial diversity with focus on two important indicators of ecosystem health: toxigenic bloom-forming cyanobacteria and impaired planktonic biodiversity using a combination of DNA barcoding and NGS. This work systematically addressed three main objectives (Fig. 1): (1) construction of a reference BOLD database using combination of Sanger and the Single Molecule, Real-Time (SMRT) sequencing on PacBio Sequel I instrument for algal and cyanobacterial 16S ribosomal RNA, using isolates from the Laurentian Great Lakes and other regions; (2) optimization of Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) primers and DNA extraction protocols using mock communities generated from known cyanobacterial and algal cultures; and (3) application of the HAB-ID tool to environmental samples and comparison of its performance with microscopy.

Materials & Methods

Portions of this text were previously published as part of a preprint: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2019.12.11.873034v1.full.pdf (Ivanova et al., 2019).

Strains and culture conditions

The 211 of 263 analyzed cyanobacteria and algal strains used in this study are listed in public BOLD dataset: DS-ECCSTAGE: https://doi.org/10.5883/DS-ECCSTAGE along with collection information and microscopic images. Strains which failed to sequence or did not pass validation were moved into private problematic samples project ECPS.

Of these strains, 91 were collected from the Laurentian Great Lakes and other waterbodies throughout Canada and first isolated into filtered lake water using sterile micropipetting and repeated washings. Once established they were transferred to growth media and housed at the Canadian Centre for Inland Waters in Burlington, Ontario (CCIW). Cyanobacteria stock cultures were maintained as batch cultures in 55 ml tubes at 18 °C in Z8 media (Kotai, 1972) at a 16:8 h light:dark light regime of 50 µmol photons m−2 s−1. Eukaryotic strains were maintained at 14 ± 1 °C in Chu10 (Chu, 1942), WC (Guillard & Lorenzen, 1972) or BBM (Bold, 1949) at a 16:8 h light:dark light regime of 110 µmol photons m−2 s−1. All strains were maintained as monoalgal (non-axenic) batch cultures and transferred bimonthly or monthly using sterile techniques. To augment the database, additional strains were obtained from the following culture collections: the Canadian Phycological Culture Collection (CPCC; Waterloo, Canada), the Metropolitan Water District of Southern California (G. Izaguirre), the University of Zurich (F. Juttner), the Norwegian Institute for Water Research (NIVA; Oslo, Norway), the Pasteur Culture Collection (PCC; Paris, France), the Polar Cyanobacteria Culture Collection (PCCC; Quebec, Canada), the Culture Collection of Algae at Göttingen University (SAG; Göttingen, Germany), and the University of Texas Culture Collection (UTEX; Texas, USA).

All strains for DNA barcoding were pelleted and stored in Lysing Matrix A tubes containing one ml of ddH2O at −80 °C until extraction.

Preparation of mock communities

Mixes of 4–16 strains of cyanobacteria and algae were prepared in a single flask to produce mock communities using equal volumes from each culture regardless of density. A total of nine mock communities were created in 2016, which were split between two experiments: Mock1 and Mock2 (Table S1). Subsamples of 20–40 ml from each culture mix were filtered onto 0.22 µm polycarbonate or polyethersulfone membranes in replicates, initially frozen at −80 °C, transported on dry ice and kept at −20 °C prior to extraction. For some mock communities one subsample was preserved in Lugols’s solution to measure the relative proportion (by cell number) of each species using microscopy (Utermöhl method). To evaluate possible bias from DNA extraction and PCR amplification, mock communities for Mock community 1 experiment were extracted and amplified separately, then pooled for the first NGS run while for the second run DNA was pooled prior to PCR amplification. For the second experiment (Mock community 2) each filter was split in half and extracted using two DNA extraction methods.

Environmental samples—collection sites

Environmental samples were collected from permanent Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) survey stations in Lakes Erie, St. Clair and Winnipeg. No specific permissions were required for the sampling stations/activities because the sampling stations were not privately owned or protected. This study did not involve endangered or protected species.

Environmental samples were collected from three waterbodies which exhibit annual HABs due to excessive nutrient loading: Lake Erie, Lake St. Clair, and Lake Winnipeg. Lake Erie, the smallest of the Laurentian Great Lakes, is of enormous economic value (Smith et al., 2019), providing drinking water for an estimated 11.5 million North American residents (International Joint Commission, 2014) and generating more than seven billion dollars in revenue each year from fishing and tourism (Smith et al., 2019). In the last couple of decades, Lake Erie has been experiencing annual blooms of toxic cyanobacteria with increasing toxicity and duration (Stumpf et al., 2012; Ho & Michalak, 2015; Watson et al., 2015). Other water bodies connected to Lake Erie (e.g., Lake St. Clair) are also exhibiting frequent blooms, while the past decade or so has seen an alarming rise in HABs in Lake Winnipeg, dubbed the ‘sixth Great Lake’.

Samples were collected from four permanent Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC) survey stations in Lake Erie (LE879, LE880, LE885, and LE970) in August 2015 and May 2016, five stations in Lake St. Clair (LSC008, LSC134, LSC136, LSC139, and LSC142) in September 2016, and four stations in Lake Winnipeg (LWW1, LWW2, LWW5, and LWW8) in September 2017 (Table S2). Water samples were collected from a depth of 1 m in Lakes Erie and Winnipeg and 0.5 m in Lake St. Clair using Niskin or van Dorn bottles. Water samples were filtered through Millipore 0.22 µm Sterivex filters (800–1,000 ml) using a peristaltic pump, 0.22 µm polycarbonate (250 ml), or polyethersulfone (300 ml) membrane filters, and immediately stored at −80 °C for DNA extraction as outlined below. Subsamples from the five Lake Erie stations collected in August 2015 were also processed for phytoplankton community composition and biomass (1% v/v final conc. Lugol’s iodine solution) for comparison purposes between NGS and microscopic identification.

Microscopic analysis

Morphological identification and enumeration of phytoplankton in environmental samples were analyzed at the Algal Ecology & Taxonomy Inc. (ATEI; Winnipeg, Manitoba) with an exception for samples from station LE881 and LE885 collected on August 18, 2015, which were analyzed at Université du Québec à Montréal (UQAM) (Quebec). Cells were enumerated on a Leitz Diavert inverted microscope at 125-625X magnification (ATEI; Utermöhl technique) following Findlay & Kling (1998) and an Olympus IMT-2 inverted microscope at 100–400X magnification (UQAM; Utermöhl technique) following Hudon, Paquet & Jarry (1996), after sedimentation of a known volume of sample in a counting chamber. Taxa were identified to the lowest taxonomic level possible (usually species). For comparison with NGS, all identifications were used at the genus level, considering species complexes and taxa with low resolution.

DNA extraction

Throughout the duration of the project three commercial kits: PowerWater (MO-BIO), Sterivex PowerWater (MO-BIO) and DNeasy PowerSoil (QIAGEN) were used for DNA extraction from mock communities and environmental samples. For mock communities and samples from Lake Erie filters were placed into Lysing Matrix A bead beating tubes (MP Biomedicals); 50% bleach, and 70% ethanol or ELIMINase (Decon Labs Inc.) and 70% ethanol followed by flame sterilization were used to decontaminate instruments between samples. DNA extraction procedure was carried out in UV sterilized laminar hood according to manufacturers instructions using alternate lysis protocol with incubation at 65 °C for 15 min. DNA was eluted in 100 µl of elution buffer.

Samples from Lakes Winnipeg and St. Clair were extracted with DNeasy PowerSoil kits (QIAGEN) at ECCC following manufacturer’s protocol. This kit features standard two ml beat beating tubes with grinding media, which fit into mini-centrifuge, and efficient removal of inhibitors, enabling DNA extraction from water and sediment samples. Therefore, we chose this kit for processing of field samples at ECCC.

In parallel with commercial kits, we tested in-house DNA extraction based on previously published DNA extraction protocol for plants (Ivanova, Fazekas & Hebert, 2008; Ivanova, Kuzmina & Fazekas, 2011) with minor adjustments mostly to the lysis stage to accommodate for different sample types (Table 2). Econospin column (Epoch Life Science) were used in all protocols. The buffer formulations were as follows: 5M GuSCN Buffer: 5 M GuSCN, 16.7 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 8.3 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.4, 3.3% Triton X-100; PWB: 3 M GuSCN, 10 mM EDTA pH 8.0, 5 mM Tris–HCl pH 6.4, 2% Triton X-100, 50% ethanol; WB: 60% ethanol, 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl pH 7.4, 0.5 mM EDTA pH 8.0.

PCR recipe for all workflows

Each PCR reaction contained a total volume of 12.5 µL consisting of 5% trehalose [D-(+)-Trehalose dehydrate] (Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich), 1.25 µL of 10x reaction buffer (Life Technologies, Invitrogen), 2.5 mM of MgCl2, 1.25 pmol each of forward and reverse primer (Integrated DNA Technologies), 50 µM of dNTP (KAPA Biosystems), 0.3 U of Platinum Taq DNA polymerase (Life Technologies, Invitrogen) and two µL of DNA template. For reverse primer cocktails (e.g., CYA781R-a and CYA781R-b, CYA781R-a_t1 and CYA781R-b_t1, CYA781Rd_t1 and CYA781R-euk_t1) primers were diluted to 10 µM, mixed by volume in equal ratios and same volume was used as for 10 µM (0.125 µL per reaction). All primers utilized in this study are listed in Table 3.

Sanger sequencing workflow for reference library generation

The target genetic marker (16S) was amplified using PCR primers PLG1-1 and PLG2-1 (Urbach, Robertson & Chisholm, 1992) to amplify ∼1,100–1,200 bp of 16S and CYA106F-GC and CYA781R(ab) (Nubel, Garcia-Pichel & Muyzer, 1997) (Table 3) to amplify ∼800 bp of 16S; followed by cycle sequencing with a standardized commercially available BigDye Terminator v3.1 kit using corresponding forward and reverse primers to produce bidirectional sequences with partial sequence overlap. Sequencing reactions were analyzed by high-voltage capillary electrophoresis on an automated ABI 3730xL DNA Analyzer.

SMRT-sequencing workflow for reference library generation

Previously extracted DNA was consolidated from 96-well plates into a single 384-well plate using Biomek NXP, data tracking was facilitated by LIMS. The target genetic marker 16S cyanobacterial rDNA or 16S chloroplast for eukaryotic strains, was amplified using the Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) for 20 cycles with fusion primers containing gene-specific sequence and PacBio tail as an adapter): PLG1-1-PB1/PLG2-1-PB1 (Urbach, Robertson & Chisholm, 1992), PB36-PB1/ PB38-PB1 (Webb & Maas, 2002) and 1664R-PB1 (Lee et al., 2017) targeting long fragments of 16S rDNA for the reference library (Table 3), followed by 20 cycles of PCR amplification with PacBio fusion primers labelled with V2 UMI-tags using Platinum Taq as described in Hebert et al. (2018) to produce barcoded amplicon libraries. After PCR2 three 384-well plates were pooled into a single tube for reference library generation.

The thermocycling profiles for Platinum Taq PCR1 were as follows: PLG1-1 and PLG2-1 primers: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 20 cycles of: denaturation at 94 °C—1 min; annealing at 66 ° C—1 min, extension at 72 °C for 2 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

PB36-PB38 and PB36-1664R: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 20 cycles of: denaturation at 94 °C—1 min; annealing at 55 °C—1 min, extension at 72 °C for 2 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

PCR2 with PacBio primers with UMI-tags consisted of 20 cycles of initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 20 cycles of: denaturation at 94 °C—1 min; annealing at 64 °C—1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. DNA template and labeled primer transfers were done on Biomek FXP robot.

SMRTbell® Template Prep Kit 1.0, Sequel Binding Kit 3.0 and Sequel Sequencing Plate 3.0, 45 pM loading concentration, 20 h movie time and SMRT Cell 1M v3 LR were used for sequencing of reference strains on a single SMRT-cell on PacBio Sequel I instrument according to manufacturer’s instruction.

Bioinformatics—SMRT-sequencing

Reads from reference culture strains were assembled into CCS (circular consensus sequences) with accuracy 99.9%, min CCS read length 1000, max CCS read 2500, min number of passes 3, and demultiplexed using python scripts: https://gitlab.com/biodiversity/PacBio_FASTQ_to_ID_to_Barcode (commit 10474672, CCS2ReadsDemultiplexer.py (DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17850625) was modified to allow processing read up to 3,000 bp; 0 tolerance to mismatches was used to assign reads to MID-tags. Resulting sequences were clustered into OTUs with 99% accuracy and keeping singletons: Cutadapt (v1.8.1) was used to trim primer sequences; Sickle (v1.33) was used for filtering (less than 200 bp were discarded), and Uclust (v1.2.22q) was used to cluster OTUs with 99% identity and minimum read depth of 1. Clustered sequences (FASTA files) were renamed to append process ID to OTU names using awk bash command on Linux server and log file of session for each primer set was saved in PuTTY: for f in *.fasta;do awk ’/>/{sub(“>”,“&“FILENAME”_”);sub(/\.fasta/,x)}1′*.fasta;done. Note: This command runs indefinitely, therefore, the PutTTY terminal was closed after few minutes and the output was edited in Notepad++ to remove duplicates by searching for the first repeated sequence name and deleting the rest of the output. The resulting 16S sequences from each corresponding output per primer pair were identified to Class using 16S diversity tool in Geneious software and filtered using Excel, using tsv outputs from 16S diversity tool. The remaining cyanobacterial and chloroplast sequences were assembled in Codoncode along with existing sequences generated using Sanger sequencing and exported as FASTA files. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.450 in Geneious. Geneious tree builder with default parameters was used to generate Tamura-Nei neighbour joining trees for reference library validation.

After submission of eukaryotic strains with high sequence divergence BOLD systems algorithms were not able to handle data alignments and taxon ID tree generation, therefore data was processed in Geneious software v 11.0.4. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v 7.308 (Katoh et al., 2002; Katoh & Standley, 2013), with the following parameters: Algorithm: Auto; Scoring matrix: 200 PAM/2; Offset value: 0.123. The neighbor-joining tree was generated using Tamura-Nei distance model, exported as newick format and visualized in iTOL (Letunic & Bork, 2016). Refined DNA sequences recovered from the algal strains were deposited in project STAGE and corresponding public dataset DS-ECCSTAGE in the Barcode of Life Data System (BOLD) accessible at http://www.boldsystems.org/ (raw SMRT data are available as a project attachment file “SMRT-DATA.zip” under publicly available STAGE project in BOLD).

The Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) workflow

The target genetic marker 16S cyanobacterial rDNA or 16S chloroplast for eukaryotic strains was amplified using PCR for 30 cycles with fusion primers targeting shorter internal fragment of ∼400 bp (Table 2) labelled with IonExpress UMI (Unique Molecular Identifier) tags using Platinum Taq as described in Hebert et al. (2013).

For the 24-replicate setup with annealing temperature gradient two µl of DNA from each sample was added to each 12 wells of the row into 96-well plates containing PCR mix with fusion primers with IonXpress UMI tags (Plate 1—CYA359F-CYA781R, Plate 2—CYA359Fd-CYA781Rd-euk), resulting in 24 PCR replicates per sample with each combination of primer pair and sample labelled with different UMI tag; after pooling, each sample was represented by two UMI-tags. For the three-replicate setup without annealing temperature gradient, each DNA sample was added in three replicates to a well with PCR master mix containing all primers (CYA359F-781R, CYA359Fd-CYA781Rd-euk); after pooling, each sample was represented by two UMI-tags.

Single round PCR with fusion primers thermocycling consisted of: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 30 cycles (35 cycles for environmental samples) of denaturation at 94 °C—1 min; annealing gradient (56−61 °C) or annealing at 58 °C for simplified PCR setup—1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

In the later high-throughput phase of the project, utilizing already optimized primer cocktails, samples from Lakes St. Clair and Winnipeg were extracted in three replicates using DNeasy PowerSoil kits and amplified with M13-tailed complete primer cocktail using two rounds of PCR. PCR1 products were diluted 2x and an aliquot of two µl was transferred to each well of PCR2 plate containing unique IonXpress UMI-tag to allow comparison of sample replicates.

PCR1 with M13-tailed primers thermocycling consisted of: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 20 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C—1 min; annealing at 58 °C—1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

PCR2 with M13 IonXpress1-96 primers thermocycling consisted of: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 2 min followed by 20 cycles of: denaturation at 94 °C—1 min; annealing at 51 °C—1 min, extension at 72 °C for 1 min; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min.

Pooled amplicon libraries were purified using paramagnetic beads prepared as described in Rohland & Reich (2012) using bead to product ratio 0.8:1. Each library was normalized to 13 pM for templating reaction. Ion PGM Template OT2 Hi-Q View kit, 316 or 318 chips and Ion PGM Sequencing Hi-Q View Kit for Ion Torrent PGM were used for sequencing, according to manufacturer’s instructions.

Only high-quality reads (QV > 20 and longer than 200 bp) assigned to correct Ion Express MID tags were used in NGS data analysis. The following bioinformatics workflow was used to process NGS data: Cutadapt (v1.8.1) was used to trim primer sequences; Sickle (v1.33) was used for filtering (less than 200 bp were discarded) and Uclust (v1.2.22q) was used to cluster OTUs with 99% identity and minimum read depth of 10. Local Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) 2.2.29+ algorithm was utilized to match resulting OTUs to custom reference library database for 16S generated from available BOLD records DS-ECCSTAGE dataset in BOLD; BLAST output results were parsed using custom built python script (Valdez-Moreno et al., 2019) OTUBLASTParser.py (commit 4b00fcda) with the following 16S Identity Filter (Species 99%, Genus 97%, Family 95%, Order 90%); matches were concatenated using custom script ConcatenatorResults.py (commit 4b00fcda), exported to Excel, and further processed in Tableau software 10.2 (min coverage 10). Both scripts are available at https://gitlab.com/biodiversity/OTUExtractorFromBLASToutput (DOI: https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.17850628).

NGS data for mock communities and environmental samples were submitted to ENA archive https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ena/browser/view/PRJEB27854.

16S data parsing to improve BLAST algorithm performance

Correct taxonomic assignments remain problematic for some cyanobacteria, especially for Anabaena, Aphanizomenon, Dolichospermum complex (Wacklin, Hoffmann & Komarek, 2009). Moreover, Microcystis and Planktothrix have low interspecific divergence with 16S. Therefore, to ensure accurate identification with less false-positive species-level top hits using BLAST algorithm, we assigned sequences belonging to these groups to superficial taxonomic groups, based on Taxon ID tree. Results

Reference library

All Sanger sequences for 16S rDNA were uploaded to BOLD, followed by validation of data using BOLD Taxon ID trees or neighbour-joining trees generated in Geneious software. Strains with questionable placement were sent to experts for re-identification, re-ordered from culture collections, or removed from the dataset. The resulting BOLD reference database consisted of cyanobacteria and algae isolated from the Great Lakes and other areas. Following the first round of sequencing using Sanger technology, DS-ECCSTAGE dataset contained a total of 203 strains with 16S sequence data, including nuisance and toxic genera (e.g., Microcystis, Planktothrix, Aphanizomenon, Dolichospermum, Phormidium).

Second round of reference library verification involved SMRT-sequencing on PacBio Sequel I instrument. PacBio SMRT sequencing generates highly accurate HiFi reads (https://www.pacb.com/blog/steps-of-smrt-sequencing/). Library preparation steps polish fragment ends and convert DNA fragments into circular SMRTbell templates through ligation of hairpin adapters containing priming sites for sequencing primers. Each molecule, bound to a DNA polymerase at the priming site, is loaded into a nanoscale well called zero-mode waveguide (ZMW), where nucleotide incorporation events produce detectable light signals recorded in real time. Because the template is circular, the polymerase can make multiple passes over the insert, and these passes are combined to create a high-accuracy circular consensus sequence (CCS). Therefore, this technology is perfect for generating accurate long reads from the mixed cultures.

Resulting SMRT sequences contained both cyanobacterial, chloroplast and bacterial reads with PLG1-1 and PLG 2-1 primer being the most specific (Fig. 2). Bacterial reads were filtered and not used for further assembly, while cyanobacterial and chloroplast reads were used to verify and improve Sanger sequencing results (Table S5). Average Sanger sequencing length was 1,038 ± 174 bp (RSD = 17), while SMRT-refined sequencing length consisted of 1,410 ± 141 bp (RSD = 10). The chloroplast 16S rRNA sequences of Pediastrum, Gonium, Chlamydomonas, Closterium, Coccomyxa, Staurastrum and Tetraspora contained long insertions which were removed from the alignment for the taxon ID tree generation but were retained in the final dataset to enable further studies of group I introns (Table S5, highlighted in yellow). Three of these insertions detected in STAGE200-18 (Closterium closteroides _EC-CPCC288, STAGE196-18 Chlamydomonas acidophila EC-CPCC121) and STAGE202-18 (Coccomyxa simplex _EC-CPCC175) were identified by RNAweasel tool (https://megasun.bch.umontreal.ca/apps/rnaweasel/ as group I introns (derived, B1 and IA3 subgroups) respectively. Group I introns were previously reported for 16S rRNA of eukaryotic algae (Durocher et al., 1989; Turmel et al., 1995; Ustinova, Krienitz & Huss, 2001).

Class level taxonomy of clustered CCS reads (percent of total for each primer pair) inferred from 16S diversity tool in Geneious software.

Following reassembly of Sanger sequencing backbone sequences with SMRT-data generated from multiple primer sets (Table 3) and verification, problematic strains were transferred to private project on BOLD containing problematic samples (ECPS), while remaining 211 16S sequences were left in the validated DS-ECCSTAGE reference database.

As previously reported by Rajaniemi et al. (2005), heterocystous cyanobacteria formed a monophyletic group according to 16S rDNA sequence data. Within this group, Anabaena and Aphanizomenon were intermixed. All planktonic Anabaena with gas vesicles were recently transferred into the genus Dolichospermum (Wacklin, Hoffmann & Komarek, 2009). Such mixed groups represented a real challenge for accurate taxonomic assignments using BLAST-search algorithm. Therefore, we created groups, corresponding to closely related taxa (some with identical or nearly identical sequences), based on 16S data. In our study 24 strains of one Anabaena, five Aphanizomenon and fifteen Dolichospermum isolates formed an intermixed cluster which we named Anabaena-Aphanizomenon-Dolichospermum complex, hereafter called AAD Complex (Fig. 3). From the Anabaena strains which did not cluster with the above-mentioned species complex, two (A. doliolum and A. vigiueri) were named Anabaena I and remaining strains, including A. circinalis, A. variabilis and A. flos-aquae, were named Anabaena II. Validation of the sequence data involved checking placement on each strain on the taxon ID tree (Fig. 3), as well as NCBI blast searches for questionable sequences and verification of taxonomic information based on morphology, which resulted in taxonomy updates for some strains (e.g., STAGE142-17—Hyella fontana—EC-CalAq994 was reidentified as Odorella benthonica (Table S5).

Neighbor-joining tree visualized in iTOL, colored ranges correspond to taxa complexes and genera with low resolution used in BLAST search assignments and OTUBLASTparser.py script.

This validated reference database contains 211 strains represented by 11 genera of Bacillariophyta, six Charophyta, 17 Chlorophyta, two Cryptophyta, 25 Cyanobacteriota, two Euglenida, four Ochrophyta and one genus of Rhodophyta. Detailed taxonomic breakdown is available for download from DS-ECSTAGE dataset in BOLD. This database can be used as a local BLAST search database for environmental samples or for identification or verification of strains.

Validation using mock communities

Mock communities assembled from cultures used to generate reference library enabled the validation of extraction protocols and minimization of PCR bias prior to working on field samples.

While working on the mock community dataset and reference library strains it was observed that some of the strains produced heterogeneous sequence data, resulting in background noise or failures in Sanger sequencing, or additional unexpected strains detected in mock community samples (Fig. 4); see Table S1 for detailed breakdown of expected strains and those detected by microscopy and NGS. Microscopy data (Fig. 4) for four filters from mock community confirmed that some of the analyzed strains contained contaminants of other cyanobacteria or algae, easily detected with NGS sequencing due to high sensitivity of the method. SMRT sequencing in the later phase of the project enabled recovery of target sequences for some of these problematic strains (e.g., Euglena gracilis, Asterionella formosa, Tabellaria fenestrata).

Our data demonstrated 99–100% efficient recovery of cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algae submitted for analysis, excluding problematic strains not matching expected taxonomy or failing to produce sequence data in Sanger or SMRT, which were removed from the reference database (Fig. 4). All negative DNA extraction controls did not produce PCR products in any mock community experiments.

Microscopy

Our reference database did not contain taxa from some phyla which were detected by microscopy. Therefore, we included only those phyla present in our database for direct comparison of microscopy with NGS. The total number of genera for the four Lake Erie sites in August 2015 detected with microscopy and NGS are presented in Table 4. Overall, NGS detected significantly more genera than microscopy. A two-tailed t-test was conducted using on-line calculator (https://www.socscistatistics.com/tests/studentttest/) and revealed a significant difference across all samples (t(df) = −6.609, p = 0.0006). The result is significant at p < 0.05; the number of detected genera ranged from 18 to 22 for microscopy and 30 to 35 for NGS.

Cyanobacteria and Bacillariophyta genera were dominant in most samples followed by representatives of Chlorophyta (Fig. 8). For all stations, NGS detected more genera. According to both microscopy and NGS results, Cyanobacteria were dominant in all Lake Erie samples collected in August 2015 during the bloom period.

Discussion

In this work, we developed a HAB-ID toolset to detect toxigenic bloom-forming cyanobacteria and assess community composition and diversity using a combination of DNA barcoding and NGS. Built around a diverse reference BOLD database dataset DS-ECCSTAGE for algal chloroplast and cyanobacterial 16S ribosomal RNA, the HAB-ID toolset successfully recovered distinct species in mock communities and provided accurate and more detailed taxonomic composition of natural phytoplankton in comparison to microscopic enumeration.

Isolating and maintaining monospecific strains of cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algae is not a trivial task, particularly with large culture collections. Strains can be mislabeled, lose morphological or biochemical traits or harbor contaminants present at isolation (e.g. for cyanobacteria with significant sheaths), with implications for experimental work—as a result, axenic strains are very difficult to obtain (Rippka et al., 1979). According to the study of Cornet et al. (2018) on contamination level of publicly accessible cyanobacterial genomes, 21 out of 440 surveyed genomes were highly contaminated (mostly with Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes). In our study we used primers preferentially amplifying cyanobacterial 16S (Urbach, Robertson & Chisholm, 1992; Nubel, Garcia-Pichel & Muyzer, 1997), however, some strains failed to sequence due to overlapping heterogeneous signal in Sanger sequencing, which can be indicative of contamination. Moreover, both microscopy and NGS detected algal/cyanobacterial contaminants in some of the cultures used to create reference library (Fig. 4), and placement in the NJ tree also revealed that some of the strains were incorrectly identified by the culture collection. All such questionable strains and those which failed to produce sequencing data were removed from our validated reference dataset and placed into unpublished private BOLD project ECPS. The DNA from some of these strains was successfully assessed later using new technological advances such as SMRT (Single-Molecule, Real-Time) Sequencing using PacBio Sequel instrument, which allow accurate long reads via CCS (Circular Consensus Sequences) and, unlike Sanger sequencing enable separation of mixed signal in contaminated strains. These technological advances enabled us to failure-track our DNA collection and recover more 16S rDNA sequences for BOLD reference database.

Some traditional markers such as ntcA and rbcL (Lindell & Post, 2001; Paul, Alfreider & Wawrik, 2000), generally used for working with phytoplankton, were less scalable for high-throughput processing due to lower amplification/sequencing success. In the first project phase, sequencing success for 16S, rbcL, and ntcA across the 53 strains was 96%, 64%, and 19%, respectively, supporting the selection of 16S as the preferred marker. The 16S rDNA is commonly used for cyanobacteria (Garcia-Pichel, López-Cortés & Nübel, 2001; Rajaniemi et al., 2005; Kurobe et al., 2013; Kermarrec et al., 2013), and therefore, was selected as a primary marker due to its scalability for high-throughput screening. We chose internal 16S V3-V4 region for NGS analysis using existing primers for 16S of cyanobacteria (Nubel, Garcia-Pichel & Muyzer, 1997) because this region contains significant information for phylogenetic assignments (Yu & Morrison, 2004). Furthermore, the length of the region is approximately 400 bp, which makes it compatible with Ion Torrent NGS sequencing platforms. Many of the previous studies on eukaryotic algae have utilized 18S rDNA instead of 16S (Nakada, Misawa & Nozaki, 2008; Hamsher et al., 2011; Haddad et al., 2014; Ren et al., 2015), however, we chose 16S chloroplast rDNA as a single marker to enable simultaneous detection of cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algae for assay simplicity. While these primers are widely used in cyanobacterial studies (Casamayor et al., 2000; Garcia-Pichel, López-Cortés & Nübel, 2001; Geißet al., 2004), Zwart et al. (2005) made modifications to these primers to increase their specificity. In our case, the availability of longer sequences in the reference database enabled us to verify primer binding sites, focusing on strains underrepresented in NGS runs, and improve their recovery by introducing degeneracies in existing CYA359F–CYA781R primers or slightly shifting CYA781R primer position to enable detection of 16S chloroplast of eukaryotic strains. The most noticeable improvement of read coverage for eukaryotic strains in mock communities and in environmental samples (Fig. 5) using new primer cocktail CYA359Fd-781Rd-euk was for the following eukaryotic orders: Chlamydomonadales (75.82%), Volvocales (79.26%), Chromulinales (90.76%), Synurales (92.03%) and Euglenales (100%).

While commercially available kits allow easy standardization between different labs, those protocols are time consuming. If processed in the centrifuge, MO-BIO PowerWater columns were difficult to transfer into clean tubes due to splashing of buffers and required frequent change of gloves. Because volumes listed in the protocol are close to the lid and can cause potential tube leaks and cross-contamination, we reduced the transfer volume to 625 µl. In this study, we detected contamination in only one negative extraction control extracted with PW kit. All contaminants detected in negative controls were filtered from the results for environmental samples. The CCDB GF extraction protocols utilize spin columns for binding high-quality DNA (Epoch Life Science) and custom buffers which allow efficient lysis of cyanobacteria and algae, and easy removal of inhibitors with reduced number of handling steps (i.e., the inhibitor removal step is omitted and only single load of sample is required for DNA binding). Contrary to MO-BIO PowerWater kit, these columns are easy to transfer into clean tubes and do not require frequent change of gloves. The protocol is scalable and available in 96-well format.

Overall CCDB extraction protocols (CCDB-GF-algal and CCDB-GF-algal-Sterivex) resulted in similar taxonomic recovery from mock community and environmental samples in comparison with MO-BIO PowerWater kits (e.g., total number of taxa detected using CCDB-GF extraction protocols and 24 PCR replicates for polycarbonate filters from 4 stations was 48 taxa versus 43 taxa for polycarbonate filters, and 37 vs. 40 with MO-BIO PowerWater kit for Sterivex filters (Fig. 6). The CCDB extraction protocols are therefore a good alternative to commercial kits that may be subject to manufacturing changes. In January 2016 MO-BIO was merged with Qiagen and we noticed better quality binding columns in DNeasy PowerWater and DNeasy PowerSoil kits. However, DNeasy PowerWater kit was reported to be incompatible with filters preserved in ethanol (Hinlo et al., 2017), which can be a potential issue for remote sampling without immediate access to cold storage.

After evaluating primer specificity on mock communities and environmental samples, we eliminated the temperature gradient for annealing stage and reduced the number of PCR replicates from 24 to 3, without noticeable impact on the number of genera recovered and read coverage (Figs. 1 and 6). The next optimization stage included incorporation of M13-tailed primers for high-throughput workflow, compatible with robotic plate-based PCR setup for incorporation of IonXpress UMI-tags. However, while processing samples from Lakes St. Clair and Winnipeg, we noticed increased formation of primer dimers generated mostly by original primer pair CYA359F-CYA781R. Based on our rigorous primer evaluation, this primer pair does not contribute much to taxonomic recovery from environmental samples. Therefore, this primer pair can be excluded and replaced with modified primer cocktail CYA359Fd-CY781Rd-CYA781-euk. Also, pooled libraries can be purified on E-gel SizeSelect II 2% agarose gels prior to sequencing to ensure complete primer dimer removal. Because PCR products amplified with M13-tailed primers are approaching the read length limit of Ion Torrent PGM with Hi-Q View chemistry, the fully automated workflow of Ion Torrent Chef/S5 instruments with Ext sequencing chemistry is a better fit for high-throughput analysis of environmental samples, as confirmed by our preliminary data.

We compared two approaches for identifying and quantifying phytoplankton assemblages. Clearly, both have limitations and biases; however, we argue that our NGS protocol offers a potentially new and powerful tool that can be better standardized than traditional microscopical analyses. We observed that microscopy results correlated with NGS data for Lake Erie samples collected in August 2015. However, taxonomic expertise is increasingly rare and costly, and as noted above, relies largely on morphological traits, many of which vary with environmental conditions and state of sample preservation (Evans, Foulds & Carr, 1976; Morales & Trainor, 1997). Furthermore, few analysts remain current with evolving systematics and nomenclature, resulting in differences in classification and nomenclature. Not surprisingly, different labs vary in taxonomic expertise and the classification and counting methods used, and the few inter-lab comparisons published show serious discrepancies amongst analysts; for example, there is a strong relationship between the number of microscope fields examined and the reported species diversity in a sample (Britton & Greeson, 1987; Davis et al., 2002; Rott, Salmaso & Hoehn, 2007; Haiyu, Lijuan & Boping, 2016). Unlike microscopy, NGS protocols and bioinformatics offer methods which can be easily standardized across different facilities, and identification results will rely on reference database, such as validated BOLD dataset DS-ECCSTAGE, containing sequences for most important bloom-forming cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algae found in Canadian waterbodies. While this method is therefore limited by the size of the reference database, this can be increased over time as new species are isolated and expanded to include other microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, and microzooplankton. Above all, the selection of the method(s) used clearly depends on the nature of the questions being addressed, however we believe that the best approach would ideally combine both NGS and microscopy, since the latter provides additional insight into field samples—inorganic and organic detritus, dead and dividing cells, colony configuration, cell size, presence of specialized cells etc.—not revealed by molecular analyses.

Conclusions

Overall, this study resulted in the development of a method for the rapid assessment of environmental samples for major algal/cyanobacterial taxa and potentially harmful/invasive species. Curated BOLD reference dataset DS-ECCSTAGE containing 211 sequences representing 102 species can be used for identification of culture collection strains using Sanger or SMRT sequencing or for NGS assessment of algal blooms or for routine monitoring of phytoplankton community diversity. We utilized this database along with mock community experiments to validate existing primer cocktails and develop new NGS primer cocktail targeting 16S chloroplast rDNA in eukaryotic strains. Evaluation of commercial and in-house DNA extraction protocols indicated their general suitability for application to environmental samples. Simplified PCR setup produced comparable sequence data on environmental samples, while reducing input of DNA, which can be stored for additional analyses; and Ion Torrent S5 and M13-tailed primer cocktails enabled high-throughput processing of environmental samples. Developed methodology relies on publicly accessible BOLD 16S reference dataset DS-ECCSTAGE along with open source bioinformatic tools tailored to deal with taxa with low resolution. The HAB-ID toolset allows the reproducible detection of cyanobacteria and eukaryotic algae in environmental samples using high throughput and cost-effective NGS; moreover, its taxonomic scope can be further expanded with addition of more strains to the BOLD database, enabling its application for real-time monitoring of HABs temporal dynamics and species diversity issues in a wide range of waterbodies.
