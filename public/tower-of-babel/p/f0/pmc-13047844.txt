Querciphoma Phyllanthi-Emblicae, a Novel Leaf Endophyte in Amla (Phyllanthus Emblica L.) from Yunnan, China
Authors: Hongbo Jiang, Chengjie Li, Sheng Xu, Jayarama Darbhe Bhat, Turki M Dawoud, Laith Khalil Tawfeeq Al-Ani, Jianchu Xu, Rungtiwa Phookamsak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring our ongoing study of fungal endophytes associated with economic plants in Yunnan, China, Querciphoma phyllanthi-emblicae sp. nov. is introduced and illustrated. The new species was isolated from healthy leaves of Phyllanthus emblica (amla or Indian gooseberry) in Honghe County. Morphologically, the new species is typical of Querciphoma in having eustromatic, uni- to multi-loculate, setose conidiomata and ellipsoidal, hyaline, aseptate conidia. However, the new taxon can be distinguished from other Querciphoma species by producing long-necked pycnidia that are smaller than other Querciphoma species. Multigene phylogenetic analyses of a concatenated ITS, LSU, SSU, and tef1-α sequence matrix, based on maximum-likelihood and Bayesian inference criteria demonstrated that Q. phyllanthi-emblicae is a sister species to Q. foliicola in Leptosphaeriaceae. This is the first report of a fungal endophyte in Querciphoma occurring on Phyllanthus emblica. In addition, a synopsis of morphological features and geographic distributions of all Querciphoma species is provided in this article.Keywords: Leaf-inhabiting fungal endophyte, new species, phoma-like, Pleosporales, taxonomy

Introduction

Querciphoma was initially introduced as a monotypic genus by Crous and Groenewald [1], to accommodate a phoma-like fungus and is typified by Querciphoma carteri (Gruyter & Boerema) Crous. Crous and Groenewald [1] established the genus to accommodate Coniothyrium carteri (Gruyter & Boerema) Verkley & Gruyter which was originally described as Pyrenochaeta minuta J.C. Carter in 1941 and reclassified as Phoma carteri Gruyter & Boerema in 2002 [1]. Therefore, based on chapter VII (rejection of names) of the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants in both Shenzhen Code of 2018 [2] and Madrid Code of 2025 [3], Querciphoma carteri was recognized as a synonym of Querciphoma minuta (J.C. Carter) Crous & P.M. Kirk [4]. Hence, Q. minuta is currently treated as the type species of Querciphoma [4,5]. Currently, three species are accepted in the genus viz. Querciphoma foliicola Crous, Q. minuta (type species), and Q. styphnolobii H.X. Gao & Z.X. Zhu [6].

Revealing the sexual and asexual morph connection of the genus Querciphoma, Q. styphnolobii has since been linked to a sexual morph [5]. Except for Q. styphnolobii, other species of the genus are currently only reported by an asexual stage [1,7]. The coelomycetous asexual morph is characterized by pycnidial, globose, eustromatic, uni- to multi-loculate, setose conidiomata, with papillate ostioles, phialidic, hyaline, smooth, doliiform conidiogenous cells with periclinal thickening at the apex and hyaline broadly ellipsoidal, aseptate, smooth-walled conidia, becoming brown and verruculose with age [1]. The sexual morph was described based on Q. styphnolobii and is characterized by black, semi-immersed to superficial, solitary, globose, rough or hairy, setose ascomata, with short papilla, thin-walled peridium, 8-spored, bitunicate, fissitunicate, cylindric-clavate asci, and muriform, mostly ellipsoidal, light brown ascospores, surrounded by a mucilaginous sheath [5].

Species of Querciphoma were mostly reported as saprobes [1,5,7–10]. Querciphoma foliicola was reported on a leaf litter in Mpumalanga, South Africa [7], and Q. styphnolobii was reported as a saprobe on the bark of Styphnolobium japonicum in Henan, China [5]. Only Q. minuta was reported as a pathogen causing dieback of oaks [11], branch and stem canker on various hosts such as Acer platanoides, Aesculus hippocastanum, Carpinus betulus, Fagus sylvatica, Platanus hispanica, and Quercus robur [10,12], or associated with veteranization wounds in living oak trunks [13] and Coraebus florentinus in declining oak forests in European countries [14].

This study aims to introduce a novel species of Querciphoma viz.nd Q. phyllanthi-emblicae through morphological comparison and multigene phylogeny of a combined ITS, LSU, SSU, and tef1-α sequence dataset. The new species was isolated from a fresh healthy leaf of amla (Phyllanthus emblica L.), which is the first report for the genus to occur as a fungal endophyte on this host in the world.

Materials and methods

Collection, fungal isolation, morphological observation, and conservation

The fungal samples were isolated from fresh healthy leaves of Phyllanthus emblica (amla or Indian gooseberry), which were collected from Honghe County, Yunnan Province, China, in September 2021. The fungal isolation and morphological induction of an endophyte followed the method as described by Suwannarach et al. [15] and Luo et al. [16], but were adjusted as followed: the leaf samples were initially cleaned with running tap water, and each leaf was cut into several circular small pieces (Radius of 5 mm). The leaf fragments were surface sterilized using 75% ethanol for 30 s, 5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) for 3 min, and 95% ethanol for 30 s, followed by three rinses with sterilized water and dried with sterile paper tissues. Leaf fragments were then placed onto the sterile Petri dish containing a mixture of potato dextrose agar (PDA) and 0.03% rose Bengal (for inhibiting bacteria and restriction of the excessive and rapid growth and spread of fungal mycelia) and incubated at 25–30 °C in the dark for 2–5 days. As individual hyphal tips grew out from the edges of leaf-bits, the mycelia were picked using sterilized needles, placed onto new PDA plates and incubated at 25–30 °C for purification. The pure culture was subcultured to new PDA plates and incubated for 1–8 weeks. Culture characteristics on PDA medium were observed, the sporulation was examined and described after 4 weeks of incubation. To ensure the correctness of the fungus, the duplicated strain was re-isolated from the sporulation in vitro using single-spore isolation [17].

Micromorphological features, viz. conidiomata, conidiogenous cells, and conidia, were examined and photo-captured by a Nikon compound microscope (model ECLIPSE Ni-U, Japan) connected with a Nikon DS-Ri2 camera (Tokyo, Japan) using DIC microscopy. Measurements of morphological features were made in Tarosoft® Image Frame Work software version 0.9.7(Taro Software Inc., Herndon, Verginia, USA). The photo-plate was edited and combined in Adobe Photoshop CS6 software (Adobe Systems Inc., USA).

Dried culture fungarium was preserved and deposited in the herbarium of the Cryptogams Kunming Institute of Botany Academia Sinica (KUN-HKAS). The axenic living cultures were preserved at the Kunming Institute of Botany Culture Collection (KUNCC). Index Fungorum number is provided for the newly described taxon following the guideline of Index Fungorum [6].

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

The genomic DNA was extracted from fresh mycelia grown on PDA medium for 4 weeks using the Biospin Fungus Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (BioFlux®, Hangzhou, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Partial DNA sequences of the internal transcribed spacers (ITS: ITS1-5.8S-ITS2), the 28S large subunit rDNA (LSU), the 18S small subunit rDNA (SSU), and the patrial protein coding gene of the translation elongation factor 1-alpha (tef1-α) were amplified by primer pairs ITS5/ITS4 [18], LR0R/LR5 [19], NS1/NS4 [18], and EF1-983F/EF1-2218R [20], respectively. PCR was performed in a 25 μL total volume containing 2 μL of DNA template, 1 μL of each forward and reverse primers (10 μM), 12.5 µl of 2 × Power Taq PCR Master Mix (mixture of EasyTaqTM DNA Polymerase, dNTPs, and optimized buffer, Beijing Bio Teke Corporation (Bio Teke), China), and 8.5 µl of sterilized double-distilled water (ddH2O). The PCR thermal cycle profiles for ITS, LSU, SSU, and tef1-α gene were processed under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 50 s, elongation at 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min, and finally kept at 4 °C. PCR products were sent to TsingKe Biological Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd, China, for purification and sequencing. Newly generated consensus sequences were deposited in GenBank database and accession numbers are provided in Table 1.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic analyses

The newly generated sequences were subjected to the nucleotide BLAST search (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi, accessed on October 10, 2025) for discovering closely related taxa of the new isolates (KUNCC 25–20527 A and KUNCC 25–20527B). Similarity indices from both ITS and LSU Blastn search indicated that our new isolates (KUNCC 25–20527 A and KUNCC 25–20527B) belong to Leptosphaeriaceae. Therefore, to reveal the accurate phylogenetic placement of the new isolates, multigene phylogeny of representative genera in Leptosphaeriaceae were analyzed based on maximum-likelihood [21] and Bayesian inference criteria [22]. Sequence datasets of representative taxa in Leptosphaeriaceae (Table 1) were retrieved from recent publications [5,7], downloaded from GenBank (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/nucleotide; accessed on October 30, 2025). Preliminarily individual DNA sequence matrixes were aligned via the online platform, MAFFT v. 7.511 [23]. The ambiguous sites of the aligned sequence datasets, such as the complementing of the missing bases at the start and end of the consensus sequence, were trimmed and improved where necessary using BioEdit v. 6.0.7 [24]. Individual gene datasets were prior analyzed by maximum-likelihood (ML) criterion to compare the congruence of tree topologies and further a concatenated ITS, LSU, SSU, and tef1-α sequence matrix was analyzed.

ML analysis was performed via the online portal CIPRES Science Gateway v. 3.3 [25], with RAxML-HPC v.8 on XSEDE (8.2.12) tool, using default settings but following the adjustments: the GAMMA nucleotide substitution model and 1000 rapid bootstrap replicates. The evolutionary model of nucleotide substitution for Bayesian inference (BI) analysis was selected independently for each locus using MrModeltest 2.3 [26]. GTR+I + G was the best-fit for ITS, LSU, SSU, and tef1-α loci under the Akaike information criterion (AIC). BI analysis was performed via MrBayes v. 3.2.6 [27]. Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling (MCMC) was used to determine the posterior probabilities (PP) [28,29]. Two parallel runs of six simultaneous Markov chains were run for 1,000,000 generations and trees were sampled every 100th generation. The 0.15 “temperature” value was set in MCMC heated chain. All sampled topologies beneath the asymptote (20%) were discarded as part of a burn-in procedure and the remaining 8,000 trees were used for calculating posterior probabilities (PP) in the 50% majority rule consensus tree (when split frequency lower than 0.01).

The tree topologies generated in this study were visualized on FigTree v. 1.4.0 (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/, accessed on November 1, 2025). The phylogram was edited and redrawn by using Microsoft Office PowerPoint 2016 (Microsoft Inc., Redmond, WA, USA) and converted to tiff file on Adobe Photoshop CS6 software (Adobe Systems Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The final alignment and phylogram were submitted to TreeBASE (submission ID: 32421, https://www.treebase.org/, accessed on February 4, 2026).

Results

Phylogeny

The concatenated ITS-SSU-LSU-tef1-α sequence matrix consists of 39 strains of representative species within Leptosphaeriaceae, with three species of Phaeosphaeriaceae: Paraphoma radicina (CBS 111.79), Phaeosphaeria ampeli (MFLUCC 18–1641), and Phaeosphaeriopsis yuccae (MFLUCC 16–0558) as the outgroup taxa. The dataset contains a total of 3,735 characters, including gaps (ITS: 1–670 bp, LSU: 671–1,478 bp, SSU: 1,479–2,842 bp, tef1-α: 2,843–3,735 bp). The best scoring ML tree was selected to represent the phylogenetic relationship of the novel taxon with other species accepted in Querciphoma, Leptosphaeriaceae (Figure 1), with the final ML optimization likelihood value of −14579.166752 (ln). All free model parameters were estimated by GTRGAMMA model, with 737 distinct alignment patterns and 40.40% undetermined characters or gaps. Estimated base frequencies were in the following: A = 0.242454, C = 0.230832, G = 0.269724, T = 0.256990, with substitution rates AC = 1.627885, AG = 3.182051, AT = 2.340808, CG = 0.677212, CT = 6.780979, GT = 1.000000. The gamma distribution shape parameter alpha is 0.116179 and the tree-length is 1.767837. The final average standard deviation of split frequencies at the end of total MCMC generations was calculated as 0.005240 in Bayesian inference analysis.

The tree is rooted to Paraphoma radicina (CBS 111.79), Phaeosphaeria ampeli (MFLUCC 18–1641), and Phaeosphaeriopsis yuccae (MFLUCC 16–0558). Phylograms generated from ML and Bayesian inference were similar topologies and the ML phylogenetic tree is shown in Figure 1. Multigene phylogeny demonstrated that most representative genera formed well-resolved clades (up to 70% MLBS and 0.95 BYPP; Figure 1) within Leptosphaeriaceae, excepted for Ochraceocephala, Plenodomus, Pseudoleptosphaeria, and Sphaerellopsis, concurring with the previous studies [5,30–32]. Ochraceocephala and Pseudoleptosphaeria are represented by a single species; however, these two species always formed stable lineages within Leptosphaeriaceae [5,30–32]. Whereas Plenodomus and Sphaerellopsis have shown a better phylogenetic resolution when more taxa were included. In this study, the new species, Querciphoma phyllanthi-emblicae (KUNCC 25-20527A and KUNCC 25-20527B) formed a well-resolved subclade with other species of Querciphoma in Leptosphaeriaceae. Querciphoma phyllanthi-emblicae has a close relationship with Q. foliicola (CPC 45029) with significant support values (79% MLBS, 0.90 BYPP; Figure 1).

Culture characteristics

Colonies were grown on PDA, reaching 50 mm after 4 weeks at room temperature (25–30 °C), under normal light conditions, floccose, cottony, circular, raised, dense, pale grey at the margin, pale brown to dark brown at the middle. Mycelium superficial to immersed in media, with branched, septate, dark brown, smooth hyphae.

Notes

Multigene phylogeny demonstrated that Querciphoma phyllanthi-emblicae (KUNCC 25–20527 A and KUNCC 25–20527B) is sister to Q. foliicola and clustered with Q. minuta (CBS 105.91 and CBS 101633) and Q. styphnolobii (HMJAU 34703) (Figure 1), within Querciphoma in this study. Through nucleotide comparisons, Q. phyllanthi-emblicae differs from Q. foliicola in 40/569 bp of ITS (7.03%, including 14 gaps), and 13/755 bp of LSU (1.72%, without gap), respectively. Morphologically, Q. phyllanthi-emblicae is similar to Q. foliicola and Q. minuta in having doliiform, phialidic conidiogenous cells, and small, aseptate, ellipsoidal, smooth-walled conidia. The new species can be distinguished from Q. foliicola in having smaller (70–160 × 95–187 µm vs. 150–250 µm diam.), solitary, long-necked, setose pycnidia, and slightly larger (4–6 × 2–3 µm vs. 3–5 × 2 µm), oblong to ellipsoidal, hyaline to pale brown conidia, whereas Q. foliicola has glabrous (without setae) pycnidia, with 1–2 papillate necks, and ellipsoid to subcylindrical, hyaline conidia (Table 2), while Q. minuta has larger (150–250 µm diam.) pycnidia, with 1–3 papillate ostioles, covered by verruculose, septate setae, and hyaline, smooth-walled conidia, becoming brown, verruculose with age (Table 2).

Discussion

To date, there are four species accommodated in Querciphoma, including the novel species introduced herein (Table 2) and all of these species are well-studied and clarified with phylogenetic evidence couple and morphological characteristics. Species of Querciphoma are distributed in China, Germany, the Netherlands, South Africa, and USA (Table 2), mainly as saprobes and pathogens [1,5,7–10,12]. Querciphoma phyllanthi-emblicae is introduced in the present study as a leaf endophyte in Phyllanthus emblica (amla or Indian gooseberry), suggesting that the genus Querciphoma is not limited to saprobic and pathogenic lifestyles, but could also be associated with plants as an endophyte. This study is reported of Querciphoma as an endophyte occurring in Phyllanthus emblica in Yunnan, China, for the first time.

The ecological roles of fungal endophytes, pathogens, and saprobes are not always clearly delineated. Certain species, termed “opportunistic pathogens” or “latent pathogens,” exist as endophytes within healthy hosts and may transition to a pathogenic state when the host encounters the environmental stressors (such as drought or disease), injury, or compromised immunity [33–36]. Even though the new species, Q. phyllanthi-emblicae, is reported as an endophyte in this study, the species may exhibit its pathogenicity under above conditions. Additionally, the present phylogeny showed that Querciphoma phyllanthi-emblicae clustered Querciphoma foliicola, which was basal to Querciphoma minuta, while Q. minuta was well known as a pathogen [10–14]. This may provide indirect, albeit cautionary, indications of its potential pathogenicity. In order to explore the pathogenicity of Q. phyllanthi-emblicae, in accordance with Koch’s postulates, standardized inoculation experiments should be conducted on the original host and its closely related economic crops in the future. Meanwhile, future researches should be focused on investigating whether host–fungal interactions undergo alterations under the conditions of water, nutrient, and other stressors.

Based on morphological characteristics (Table 2), the new species is typical of Querciphoma in having setose, stromatic pycnidial conidiomata, with short to long ostiolar neck, phialidic, doliiform to ampulliform conidiogenous cells and hyaline to pale brown, ellipsoidal to oblong, or subcylindrical, aseptate conidia. Even though, it is difficult to distinguish species of Querciphoma only based on morphological features, the interspecific status of these species could be well resolved based on multigene phylogenetic evidence. Notably, the morphology of Q. styphnolobii differs significantly from that of the other three species of Querciphoma, especially in sporulation, size of conidia, and the number of conidial septation, suggesting that the morphological characteristics of species in Querciphoma could be expanded. However, Q. styphnolobii formed a well-resolved subclade with other Querciphoma species and is basal to Q. foliicola, Q. minuta and Q. phyllanthi-emblicae (Figure 1).

Phyllanthus emblica is native to Asian regions viz. Bangladesh, Borneo, Cambodia, China, East Himalaya, India, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Vietnam, and West Himalaya and has been introduced into Cuba, Mauritius, Trinidad and Tobago, USA, and Windward Is [37]. As a medicinal and economic plant, the fruit of Phyllanthus emblica includes high levels of ascorbic acid (vitamin C) and bioactive compounds such as gallic acid and flavonoids, contributing to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor effects [38,39]. It is utilized in traditional Ayurvedic medicine for various health conditions, including gastrointestinal issues and cancer support [40,41]. However, researches on fungi associated with Phyllanthus emblica were limited. To date, there are only 24 fungal species reported to have occurred on Phyllanthus emblica in the current global scale, which are distributed from China, India, Indonesia, Myanmar, Nepal, and Thailand [42]. Although the species number of fungi associated with Phyllanthus emblica is placed in a low level, continued sampling from varied habitats and different countries will be crucial to expand the full knowledge of fungal species diversity and their ecological significance on Phyllanthus emblica.
