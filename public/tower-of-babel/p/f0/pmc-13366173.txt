Residual metal single-atom catalysis: enhanced photocatalytic hydrogen production via ligand-modulated d-orbital splitting
Authors: Kun Tang, Qi Zhao, Jin-Yue Guo, Bing-Bao Mei, Chang He, Jiang-Yang Shao, Zailai Xie, Jiannian Yao, Yu-Wu Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study demonstrates that residual palladium (Pd) species, often considered unintended impurities in organic photocatalysts, can function as highly efficient catalytic sites when atomically dispersed and strategically coordinated within molecular frameworks. A series of pyridine-terminated organic small molecules are synthesized to anchor Pd single atoms via monodentate coordination, which significantly modulates the electronic structure and catalytic behavior. Through a combination of spectroscopic, electrochemical, and computational methods, we reveal that the dz2 orbital of Pd plays a pivotal role in facilitating hydrogen adsorption by optimizing orbital overlap with hydrogen. Furthermore, a low-energy pathway mediated by pyridyl-hydrogen radicals drastically reduces the energy barriers for hydrogen transfer steps. The pyrene core serves as a photochemical charge reservoir, while terminal pyridine groups act as active sites, collectively enhancing exciton dissociation and charge separation. This work provides new insights into the elusive role of residual metals in photocatalysis and introduces the concept of residual metal single-atom catalysis, a strategy for designing efficient metal–organic hybrid systems with high atomic utilization and well-defined mechanisms.

Introduction

Photocatalytic hydrogen production stands as a pivotal research avenue for tackling global energy and environmental challenges.1–4 Among diverse catalytic materials, organic catalysts have garnered substantial attention owing to their distinct advantages, including tunable structures, broad accessibility, and environmental benignity.5–14 In the synthesis of these organic catalysts, metal catalysts such as Pd, Pt, Ru, and Ir are commonly employed, inevitably introducing metal residues.15 These residual metal species—particularly when present in nano- or atomic-scale forms embedded in the organic matrix—can act as unintended catalytically active sites and exert a profound impact on photocatalytic hydrogen evolution behavior.16–21 For example, studies have demonstrated that residual Pd species in polymer-based photocatalysts can exhibit intrinsic hydrogen evolution activity as co-catalysts; this disrupts the accurate assessment of the organic catalyst's inherent catalytic performance and may even lead to misinterpretation of structure–activity relationships.22,23 In recent years, this critical issue has attracted growing attention within the research community.

In metal-catalyzed reactions, the dispersion state and coordination environment of metal elements exert a decisive influence on their catalytic performance.26–29 Notably, metal centers dispersed as single atoms typically exhibit exceptional catalytic activity and selectivity, owing to their high atomic utilization efficiency and unique electronic structures.29–34 Furthermore, the d-orbital structure of metal catalytic centers plays a crucial role in catalytic reactions. The occupied states and energy levels of these d-orbitals can be precisely tuned by tailoring the coordination environment, e.g., ligand type, coordination number, and local symmetry.35–38 This, in turn, influences the adsorption strength of reaction intermediates and consequently dictates the reaction pathway.39,40 Thus, understanding and modulating the local coordination structure of metal centers is crucial for the rational design of high-efficiency catalysts.

Against this backdrop, this study presents the concept of residual metal single-atom catalysis, in which the residual metals present in organic photocatalysts mainly act as single-atom catalysts (SACs) for the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the catalytic activity can be effectively enhanced by the organic ligand-modulated d-orbital splitting. In this context, a series of organic small-molecule photocatalysts with terminal pyridyl groups have been synthesized via Pd-catalyzed coupling reactions (Fig. 1a). By engineering the electronic properties and coordination capacities of the terminal pyridyl functional groups, we systematically regulate the coordination interactions between the organic molecules and residual Pd species. Intriguingly, the residual Pd is found to be dispersed predominantly in a single-atom form on the substrate surface. This is in contrast with the previously reported studies based on polymer systems, in which the residual Pd is present as nanoparticles or metal clusters.22,23 Benefiting from the excellent uniform dispersibility of small molecules in organic phases, we employ transient absorption spectroscopy (TAS) to accurately assign the photophysical processes of these small molecules and elucidate the underlying electron transfer process. Notably, the Pd residue is clearly shown to enhance the photocatalytic hydrogen production, and the functional pyridyl groups prove to be indispensable to this end. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that single-atom Pd may interact with pyridyl groups via a mono-coordination mode, where its dz2 orbital plays a pivotal role in significantly lowering the energy barrier of the hydrogen atom adsorption and thus enhancing the HER activity (Fig. 1b). This finding offers a new strategy for the design of photocatalytic systems featuring low residual metal levels and high atomic efficiency.

Results and discussion

We synthesized four tetra-substituted organic small molecules, m-TAPyr, o-TAPyr, TAPyr, and PhPyr, via Pd(0)-catalyzed C–N coupling reactions to introduce amine substituents with distinct terminal groups onto a pyrene core (Fig. 1a). Among these, m-TAPyr, o-TAPyr, and TAPyr bear pyridine-based terminals, and m-TAPyr and o-TAPyr are further functionalized with a methoxy group on the pyridine ring to modulate its electronic properties and steric configuration. In contrast, PhPyr incorporates a phenyl terminal group in place of pyridine. To obtain samples with varying residual Pd contents, we employed a controlled synthesis using different Pd catalyst loadings, followed by purification through multiple rounds of column chromatography, with or without the use of a polythiol-functionalized adsorption material. For instance, m-TAPyr samples with Pd contents ranging from 8604 ppm to below the detection limit (<1 ppm) have been obtained, as determined by the inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis. In addition, Pd-free TAPyr samples have been previously demonstrated to show considerable HER activity via a pyridine–hydrogen radical [Py–H]˙ intermediate.41

The above four compounds exhibit closely resembling absorption and emission profiles (Fig. S1). The absorption band observed around 490 nm in the visible region is assigned to a charge transfer (CT) transition from a hybrid n/π orbital (delocalized over the triarylamine and pyrene units) to the π* orbital of the pyrene core.40 The UV absorption near 350 nm is attributed to a CT transition from the triarylamine–pyrene donor segment to the terminal pyridine or phenyl acceptor moiety. Owing to the stronger electron-withdrawing character of pyridine relative to the phenyl group, the absorptions of pyridine-functionalized compounds are slightly red-shifted with respect to that of PhPyr. In addition, the emission spectrum of PhPyr shows a minor red shift compared to those of the pyridine-containing analogues.

These small molecules exhibit good solubility in polar organic solvents, allowing us to readily tune the size and morphology of their assemblies in mixed solvents. Taking m-TAPyr as an example, the addition of a large amount of pure water into its solution in DMF prompted the molecules to assemble into uniform spherical nanoparticles with a diameter of approximately 10 nm, as confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis (Fig. 2a). Under the same conditions, o-TAPyr and TAPyr exhibited similar assembly behavior, while PhPyr with reduced solubility was found to primarily yield spherical nanoparticles with a diameter of about 100 nm (Fig. S2). These nanospheres are present as a stable colloidal dispersion with high optical transparency, thereby facilitating subsequent spectroscopic characterization (Fig. S3).

To investigate the distribution of residual Pd on the organic matrix, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) observations. As shown in Fig. 2b and S4, no metal aggregates were observed in the TEM and bright- and dark-field scanning TEM (STEM) images of the m-TAPyr sample with a Pd content of 8604 ppm. This suggests that the palladium residue may exist in the form of small clusters or single atoms. This speculation was further confirmed by aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field SEM (AC-HAADF-STEM) imaging, which clearly revealed numerous single Pd atoms along with a small number of metal clusters distributed on the substrate surface (Fig. 2c and d). This high dispersion of Pd is likely attributed to the sustained solubility of the small molecules during synthesis, which inhibited metal aggregation, and the subsequent effective purification in removing any potential metal particles. A single-atom distribution of Pd is also observed on the surfaces of the assemblies of the other three compounds (Fig. S5). In comparison, when the same amount of Pd catalyst is used in the synthesis, the residual Pd contents in o-TAPyr and PhPyr are much lower than those in m-TAPyr and TAPyr. In addition, almost no distinct single atoms were detected on the m-TAPyr sample with a Pd content of less than 1 ppm. The number of metal atoms observed in TEM images shows a good correlation with the metal residual content measured by ICP-MS, which suggests that the observed Pd single atoms are not due to other impurities.17

A thin film of TAPyr with Pd2+ coordinated to its terminal pyridine groups was then prepared by a stepwise assembly method for use as a reference in valence state analysis.42 In the corresponding X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis, characteristic peaks assigned to Pd2+ 3d5/2 and 3d3/2 were observed at binding energies of 338.04 and 343.34 eV, respectively (Fig. 2e). In contrast, the signals of residual Pd in the TAPyr assembly appeared at 337.36 and 342.50 eV, which were shifted by approximately 0.8 eV toward lower binding energy compared to those of Pd2+. This suggests that the residual Pd is in a nearly zero-valent state, which is consistent with the catalytic behavior of Pd0 catalysts under anhydrous and oxygen-free conditions and also supported by the reported studies on Pd2(dba)3 catalytic systems.22–25

To further characterize the valence state and local structure of the residual metal, Pd K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) analyses were performed. As shown in Fig. 2f, XANES analysis shows that the Pd K-edge white-line intensities of m-TAPyr and PhPyr (with Pd contents of 380 and 610 ppm, respectively) are very close to that of Pd foil, further supporting that the residual Pd in these materials is close to the zero-valence state. In the R-space spectra derived from the Fourier transformation of the Pd K-edge EXAFS χ(k) data, m-TAPyr exhibits a peak at approximately 1.30 Å, and a peak at around 1.55 Å is observed for PhPyr (Fig. 2g). These peaks are tentatively assigned to Pd–N and Pd–C scattering paths, respectively. The shorter Pd–N distance compared to Pd–C may be related to the coordination interaction between Pd and pyridine nitrogen atoms, as supported by DFT calculations (discussed below). Moreover, the characteristic Pd–Pd bonding signal of Pd foil near 2.56 Å is almost absent in both m-TAPyr and PhPyr, indicative of the single-atom dispersion of residual Pd in these systems. The wavelet transform analysis of the χ(k) spectra also supports the aforementioned conclusions (Fig. S6).

We subsequently investigated the photophysical and photochemical properties of the m-TAPyr solution and its colloid with a residual Pd content of <1 ppm. As shown in Fig. 3a, the nanosecond TAS of a deaerated solution of m-TAPyr in DMF exhibits a transient absorption signal in the range of 500–750 nm, which demonstrates an exceptionally long lifetime reaching the millisecond scale. A signal with the same shape was observed in the non-deaerated solution; however, its lifetime decreased significantly to a few nanoseconds (Fig. S7a). These observations indicate that this detected absorption signal can be assigned to the triplet excited state of m-TAPyr. The negative band in the 450–500 nm region, which closely resembles the steady-state absorption band of m-TAPyr, can be attributed to ground-state bleaching. Upon adding triethanolamine (TEOA) at a volume ratio of 4% to the DMF solution of m-TAPyr, a new and distinct absorption signal emerged around 500 nm (Fig. 3b). The lifetime of the signal at 500 nm was also notably prolonged compared to that before the addition of TEOA (Fig. 3c). We assign this signal to the one-electron reduced product of m-TAPyr, [m-TAPyr]˙−, which is generated after the triplet excited state of m-TAPyr receives an electron from TEOA. As shown by the decay curve at 625 nm, the kinetic decay process of the composite signal—formed by the superposition of the triplet-state signal and the generated radical signal after adding TEOA—closely matches that of the single triplet-state signal before the addition of TEOA. This indicates that the triplet-state signal undergoes faster decay after TEOA is introduced, supporting the previous conclusion as well (Fig. S7b).

Ultrafast spectroscopy was employed to probe the events preceding the formation of the triplet signal. As presented in Fig. 3d, upon excitation with a 490 nm ultrafast laser pulse, positive signals emerged at around 375 nm and beyond 600 nm on the femtosecond to picosecond timescale, which could be assigned to the singlet excited state of m-TAPyr. Ground-state bleaching was observed in the 450–500 nm region. Due to spectral overlap with the singlet excited-state absorption, particularly at shorter wavelengths, the absorption signal at 375 nm exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease within the same temporal window. The lifetime of the singlet excited state was determined to be approximately 4.4 ns by fitting the decay kinetics at 610 nm (Fig. 3i). In aqueous systems, where the small molecules underwent aggregation, the singlet excited state of m-TAPyr was significantly quenched, as reflected by a pronounced reduction in the lifetime of the transient absorption signals (0.48 ns). As shown in Fig. 3e and i, the signal intensity decayed to a low level within a few picoseconds. In addition to being quenched by water, part of the singlet excited state underwent intersystem crossing to the long-lived triplet state. The decay kinetics recorded at 626 nm and 671 nm displays asynchronous behavior (Fig. 3g). The longer-wavelength signal shows extended decay dynamics, indicating a greater contribution from the triplet excited state at this wavelength. Upon the addition of TEOA to this system, the lifetime of the triplet signal further decreased (Fig. 3h and i), while a new positive signal appeared at around 550 nm (Fig. 3f and j), consistent with the formation of [m-TAPyr]˙− as observed in pure DMF solution. These observations support the earlier conclusion that electron transfer occurs from TEOA to the triplet excited state of m-TAPyr.

Subsequently, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the intermediates generated upon photoexcitation (Fig. 4a). After irradiating the m-TAPyr/DMF system in the presence of the radical trapping agent 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) with visible light for 3 min, a distinct [DMPO-O2]˙− adduct signal was observed. This is indicative of the electron transfer from the excited state of m-TAPyr to molecular oxygen, thereby generating superoxide radicals. This process was suppressed in the m-TAPyr/DMF/H2O system, likely due to the significant quenching of the excited state by water molecules. Upon further addition of TEOA, a new six-line signal was observed. The hyperfine coupling constants indicate that this signal corresponds to a sterically hindered carbon-centered radical trapped by DMPO. Notably, the intensity of this radical signal remained independent of the Pd content in the system (Fig. S8), suggesting that it was generated prior to any interaction with Pd species. It is thus inferred that the radical species likely originates from either [m-TAPyr]˙− or a pyridine hydrogen radical (Py–H˙) formed via its subsequent reaction with water.36

To further investigate the electron transfer process between the potential reactive intermediates of m-TAPyr and TEOA and confirm the signals of these intermediates, photoelectrochemical systems were constructed and examined. The pyridine group, owing to the lone pair electrons on its nitrogen atom, can coordinate with Lewis acid sites on metal or metal oxide surfaces and exhibit stability in aqueous environments (Fig. 4b). It has thus been employed as an anchoring group to immobilize functional molecules on electrode surfaces, an area that has attracted increasing research interest in recent years.43 In this study, we used FTO/TiO2 and FTO/NiO as substrates for the photoanode and photocathode, respectively. By simply immersing the substrates in a solution of m-TAPyr for 20 min, we prepared TiO2/m-TAPyr photoanodes and NiO/m-TAPyr photocathodes. Successful adsorption of m-TAPyr was confirmed by absorption spectroscopy (Fig. 4c).

A two-compartment electrochemical cell was assembled using the prepared electrodes as working electrodes, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode. As shown in Fig. 4d, upon visible light irradiation, the TiO2/m-TAPyr photoanode exhibited a prompt photocurrent with a transient density exceeding 300 µA cm−2, which rapidly decayed to below 50 µA cm−2. This observation indicates rapid electron injection from the photoexcited m-TAPyr into the conduction band of TiO2, generating the one-electron oxidized species [m-TAPyr]˙+. The subsequent decay likely reflects increased charge recombination between injected electrons and holes. When TEOA was introduced into the anodic electrolyte, the rapid current decay was suppressed, and a stable photocurrent density above 200 µA cm−2 was maintained. This suggests that TEOA efficiently reduces [m-TAPyr]˙+ back to m-TAPyr, thereby completing the cycle of electron transfer. Ultrafast TAS of the electrode provided additional evidence for electron injection. The singlet excited-state absorption at around 600 nm decayed within picoseconds, accompanied by the rise of a spectral feature attributed to [m-TAPyr]˙+ (Fig. 4e and S9a).

For the NiO/m-TAPyr photocathode, illumination induced rapid hole injection into NiO, generating a negative photocurrent of approximately 60 µA cm−2 (Fig. 4d). In this process, m-TAPyr was reduced to [m-TAPyr]˙−, which subsequently transferred an electron to dissolved oxygen, leading to the formation of reactive oxygen species. Ultrafast spectroscopic studies of the photocathode revealed rapid decay of the singlet excited-state absorption within picoseconds, followed by the emergence of a new signal (Fig. 4f). Kinetic traces at 550 and 590 nm showed a rise and subsequent fall in signal intensity between 10 and 100 ps after pump excitation (Fig. S9b), consistent with the formation and evolution of [m-TAPyr]˙−.

Based on this experimental evidence, we have demonstrated that the triplet excited state of m-TAPyr can be reductively quenched by the sacrificial donor TEOA. Moreover, the oxidized species [m-TAPyr]˙+, generated through oxidative quenching of its excited state, whether from the triplet or singlet manifold, is capable of oxidizing TEOA to regenerate m-TAPyr. These results highlight the potential of m-TAPyr to serve as an efficient organic photosensitizer in photocatalytic applications.

Subsequently, we investigated the interaction between Pd as an electron acceptor and the organic small molecule m-TAPyr. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the experimental results, a series of samples with gradually varying Pd content was employed as the subject of study. Each sample solution was calibrated using absorption spectroscopy to guarantee that the concentration differences among the tested samples were within 5% (Fig. S10). As expected, both the fluorescence intensity and fluorescence lifetime of these samples at equal concentrations decreased gradually with increasing Pd content, indicating that the singlet excited state of m-TAPyr is oxidatively quenched by Pd atoms via an electron transfer process (Fig. 5a, b and Table S1). However, after the addition of TEOA to the m-TAPyr/DMF solution, the fluorescence lifetime increased rather than decreased (Fig. S11 and Table S1), ruling out the possibility of reductive quenching of the singlet excited state by TEOA. The prolonged fluorescence lifetime may be attributed to the increased polarity and alkalinity of the solvent induced by TEOA, thereby enhancing the stability of the excited state. Consistent with prior observations, the fluorescence lifetime still decreased with increasing Pd content even in the presence of TEOA (Fig. S11). The lifetime of the singlet excited state signal in TAS exhibited a more significant decreasing trend (Fig. 5c), supporting the above assertions. However, in the aggregated state of m-TAPyr in water, no clear correlation was observed between the singlet excited-state lifetime and Pd content (Fig. 5d). This is likely due to the fact that the majority of the singlet-state quenching is dominated by water molecules and aggregation-induced effects, obscuring the influence of trace amounts of residual Pd. Additionally, the aggregation largely suppresses photoinduced charge transfer and reduces transfer efficiency. Apart from the intrinsic quenching effect of water toward excited states, water-driven aggregation breaks the homogeneous dispersion observed in neat solvent and alters the interaction mode between organic molecules and Pd. Such aggregation restricts random intermolecular collisions, and only Pd atoms anchored at favorable sites on aggregated substrates can efficiently accept electrons. A similar submergence was also noted for other intermediate signals discussed earlier (Fig. S12).

Considering that the electron transfer to residual Pd potentially facilitates the reduction reactions on the Pd site, the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution activity of nanospheres prepared using m-TAPyr with different Pd loadings was evaluated (Fig. 6a). When the Pd content was below 1 ppm, m-TAPyr produced only about 0.4 µmol of hydrogen over 4 hours, indicative of relatively low activity (0.35 mmol g−1 h−1). As the Pd content increased, the hydrogen evolution activity showed approximately linear growth and reached 5.3 mmol g−1 h−1, suggesting that Pd acted as an electron acceptor during catalysis and participated in the formation of hydrogen molecules. Based on the observed trend, the activity had not yet reached saturation even at a Pd content of 8604 ppm. This aligned with observations from AC-HADDF-STEM analysis, showing that a significant portion of Pd remained in the form of single atoms under these conditions. Thus, further increasing the Pd content would still probably increase the number of active sites for catalytic hydrogen evolution. This suggests that in such small molecule systems, residual metal species tend to remain highly dispersed, leading to a higher saturation threshold for Pd in terms of hydrogen evolution activity—a distinct difference from polymeric systems. In polymeric systems, the decreased solubility of the organic material after polymerization often promotes the aggregation of residual metals through surface deposition of metal residues. For example, the previously reported polymer F8BT showed a Pd saturation limit of only 200 ppm, with Pd existing mainly in the form of clusters or particles.16

We further evaluated the HER activities of three additional samples with different Pd contents (Fig. 6a). The results show that TAPyr, which lacks methoxy substitution on the pyridine ring, exhibits similar catalytic behavior to that of m-TAPyr, with activity increasing significantly as the Pd content rises. In contrast, o-TAPyr, which bears a methoxy group ortho to the pyridine nitrogen, displays markedly different performance. Although its hydrogen evolution activity is still dependent on the Pd content, the overall activity remains very low, and increasing the Pd content has only a minimal enhancing effect. In addition, the pyridine-free PhPyr molecule exhibits no detectable activity across all tested residual Pd concentrations. To better visualize the above difference, we calculated the average H2 evolution activity per Pd atom using the data collected at the maximum Pd loading for all four catalysts (Fig. 6b). m-TAPyr and TAPyr exhibit comparable activities, which are far superior to those of o-TAPyr and PhPyr. These findings indicate that the terminal pyridine groups in these small molecules are essential for hydrogen generation, and the improvement in HER activity strongly depends on the interaction between pyridine and Pd. Compared to m-TAPyr, the weak response of o-TAPyr to Pd can be attributed to the ortho-positioned methoxy group. This configuration reduces the nucleophilicity of the pyridinic nitrogen and creates considerable steric hindrance around it, weakening the interaction between pyridine and Pd. This interpretation is also supported by the fact that, under identical synthetic conditions using the same loading of Pd catalyst, the residual Pd content in o-TAPyr is much lower than that in m-TAPyr and TAPyr (Table S2).

We treated m-TAPyr (Pd, 8604 ppm) with excessive 1-octadecanethiol to poison the residual Pd species. After poisoning, the catalytic activity decreased to 40.7% of the initial level (Fig. 6c). This result suggests that the metal species on the organic substrate do not exist exclusively as single atoms. Most Pd single atoms are readily deactivated upon poisoning, while a small fraction of metal clusters or ligand-protected metal species still retain catalytic activity. This finding is consistent with the TEM observations. In addition, we conducted hydrogen evolution experiments in heavy water (D2O). The signal of deuterium gas (D2) was detected by GC-MS, confirming that the protons of the evolved hydrogen originate from water molecules (Fig. S13). The generation rate of D2 is slightly lower than that of H2 under the same conditions, affording a kH/kD value of 1.3 with no pronounced kinetic isotope effect (KIE) observed. This finding rules out the H–O bond cleavage or Pd–H formation as the rate-determining step. However, due to the extremely low Pd loading in our catalyst system and the transient nature of the possible active intermediates, no resolvable characteristic signals were experimentally observed.

To further explore the photocatalytic HER mechanism, we conducted a theoretical investigation. First, we performed structural optimization and TD-DFT calculations for four organic small molecules.44–46 The energy level alignments and electron excitation analysis of the four molecules are shown in Fig. S14. These compounds show very similar computed energy gaps from S0 to S1, consistent with their comparable absorption spectra over the visible region.

Electrostatic potential (ESP) calculations suggest that the introduction of pyridine groups results in more electron-rich regions (Fig. 7a), which is favorable for metal adsorption. Among the four structures, the meta-methoxy-substituted pyridine derivative (m-TAPyr) exhibits the most negative ESP value of −42.3 kcal mol−1, followed by TAPyr with an ESP value of −40.8 kcal mol−1. In contrast, the ortho-methoxy group in o-TAPyr likely delocalizes the electron density around the pyridinic nitrogen, leading to a relatively less negative ESP of −30.8 kcal mol−1. Meanwhile, the phenyl-terminated analog PhPyr shows a more uniform ESP distribution, which is less conducive to directed metal deposition. Atomic population analysis is performed to determine the Hirshfeld charges on the coordinating nitrogen atoms (or carbon atom) for the four molecules to quantitatively evaluate their ligand donor strengths (Fig. 7a). The nearly identical Hirshfeld charges (−0.17) on the pyridine N atoms across all three compounds indicates their similar intrinsic electron-donating ability of the ligands. However, in terms of ESP, the unsubstituted and meta-methoxypyridine analogs show comparable ESP minima, both of which are more negative than that of the ortho-methoxypyridine compound. This difference arises from the positional effect of the substituent. The ortho-methoxy group resides adjacent to the coordination site, inducing substantial steric repulsion and twisting of the pyridine ring plane. Meanwhile, its electron cloud creates a shielding effect around the nitrogen atom on the molecular surface. Such spatial perturbations markedly diminish the negative ESP magnitude. For PhPyr, the Hirshfeld charge and ESP minimum of the corresponding carbon atom are considerably lower than the values for pyridine nitrogens. These results indicate that the molecules with pyridine groups more readily coordinate to the metal atom, especially for m-TAPyr and TAPyr.

We further calculated the adsorption energies of a Pd single atom at various sites on the phenylpyridine unit of m-TAPyr. The results indicate that axial coordination of Pd to the pyridinic nitrogen is the most thermodynamically favorable mode, with the most negative adsorption energy of −0.6 eV (Fig. S15). Therefore, we optimized the structures of Pd coordinated to the three aminophenylpyridine units (m-Py–Pd, o-Py–Pd, and Py–Pd) of m-TAPyr, o-TAPyr, and TAPyr and Pd adsorbed on the biphenyl unit (Ph–Pd) of PhPyr and analyzed their HOMO electron density distributions and the projected density of states (PDOS) of the five degenerate d-orbitals of Pd (Fig. 7b). In m-Py–Pd and Py–Pd, the Pd atom is axially coordinated to the pyridinic nitrogen with a N–Pd bond length of 2.04 Å. In o-Py–Pd, however, steric hindrance from the ortho-methoxy group results in a large angle (92.3°) between the Pd–N bond and the pyridine plane. Notably, the HOMO electron density in all three pyridine-coordinated structures is predominantly localized on the Pd dz2 orbital. In contrast, the Pd atom in Ph–Pd sits above the benzene ring, interacting mainly through π-d orbital overlap. The bond length of C–Pd (2.13 Å) is longer than that of N–Pd, which is consistent with the EXAFS results. PDOS analysis revealed significant splitting of the Pd d-orbitals in m-Py–Pd and Py–Pd due to axial coordination, with the HOMO primarily contributed by the dz2 orbital. Molecular orbital (MO) composition analysis of the four structures determined that the exact percentage of Pd dz2 character in the HOMO for m-Py–Pd, Py–Pd, o-Py–Pd, and Ph–Pd is 68.4%, 31.9%, 68.6% and 25.8%, respectively. These results quantitatively confirmed the larger contribution of the Pd dz2 orbital in the HOMO for m-Py–Pd and Py–Pd than that for o-Py–Pd and Ph–Pd. By comparing the full m-TAPyr–Pd system against the previously truncated model, we confirm that key core electronic and geometric properties are qualitatively consistent: the Pd–N bond distance (2.07 Å), HOMO spatial distribution (mainly on the Pd atom and contributed by the dz2 orbital), and the dz2 orbital character (68.4%) around the Pd center are well retained in the truncated system (Fig. S16). These results validate the rationality of the truncated simplification strategy.

The orbital symmetry for the dz2 orbital favors the overlap with the s orbital of hydrogen, thereby facilitating the hydrogen adsorption process and reducing the energy barrier. In contrast, o-Py–Pd showed smaller d-orbital splitting and reduced dz2 character, correlating well with the lower hydrogen evolution activity of o-TAPyr compared to m-TAPyr and TAPyr. For Ph–Pd, the minimal d-orbital splitting and absence of dominant dz2 character in the HOMO were observed, consistent with its lack of hydrogen evolution activity.

Pd natural atomic orbitals (NAOs) analysis showed that the energy alignment of dz2 for the four structures is m-Py–Pd = Py–Pd > o-Py–Pd > Ph–Pd, which is the same as the order of the percentage of Pd dz2 character in the HOMO acquired from MO composition analysis (Fig. 7d). The dz2 orbital of m-Py–Pd and Py–Pd occupies the highest energy level among the Pd d-NAOs of each structure, indicating a specific energy evolution trend of this orbital under varied coordination environments. Similar correlation is observed between the dz2 orbital energies of the four molecules and their HER activity: the higher d-orbital energy corresponds to the higher HER activity. These results highlight the crucial role of the axial Pd–pyridine coordination in enhancing the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution activity.

As mentioned previously, [m-TAPyr]˙− serves as a key intermediate in the catalytic cycle. Fig. 7c displays its optimized structure and spin density distribution, indicating that the unpaired electron is predominantly localized on the central pyrene core. This observation is consistent with the fact that both photogenerated electrons and holes in m-TAPyr are primarily distributed on the pyrene moiety. Acting as an electron donor, [m-TAPyr]˙− facilitates proton extraction from a water molecule via the terminal pyridine group, which in turn accepts the unpaired electron from the pyrene unit. This step proceeds through a proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) process, yielding a pyridine–hydrogen radical species, [m-TAPyr–H]˙, in which the unpaired electron is largely delocalized over the phenyl pyridine subunit (Fig. 7c). This process occurs readily, overcoming a low energy barrier of approximately 9.4 kcal mol−1 (Fig. S17). These results highlight the role of the pyrene core as the primary photo-responsive center, which accepts and stores electrons derived from the sacrificial agent. Subsequent dark reduction processes occur mainly on the phenylpyridine unit (m-Py), where further catalytic reductions take place.

Based on these results, we further computed the free energy and electronic energy profiles for the dark reaction steps of hydrogen evolution involving the m-Py structure, both with and without the participation of Pd atoms, and located the relevant transition states. As shown in Fig. 7d, after accepting one electron, the Pd atom in m-Py–Pd interacts with a hydrogen atom from a water molecule located on the opposite side of the pyridine ring. The system then overcomes an energy barrier of 7.9 kcal mol−1via a transition state (TS1) to form a linearly coordinated Pd–H hydride species. Upon a second photoinduced reduction, this intermediate accepts an additional electron. A second H2O molecule then coordinates to Pd from the equatorial position, and the Pd–H species undergoes protonation via a transition state (TS2) with an energy barrier of 21.6 kcal mol−1, leading to the formation of a dihydride intermediate (H–Pd–H). The two hydrides in this intermediate are bound to Pd in a nearly perpendicular arrangement. Subsequently, the two hydrogen atoms approach each other on the opposite side of the pyridine ring, forming an H–H bond. The energy of the system decreases further, and molecular hydrogen is finally released by overcoming an adsorption free energy of 20.0 kcal mol−1, thereby completing the catalytic cycle.

Additionally, the reaction pathway involving [Py–H]˙ was investigated. Similar to the results obtained with the complete m-TAPyr structure, calculations for the truncated fragment (m-Py) indicate that it interacts with a hydrogen atom of a water molecule via the pyridinic nitrogen. In the presence of an ambient electron source, this process proceeds through a transition state with a free energy barrier less than 0 (−0.6 kcal mol−1), leading to the formation of [Py–H]˙ (Fig. S18). This negative free energy barrier suggests that the reaction is highly thermodynamically favorable and may not involve a well-defined transition state. The identified structure merely corresponds to a saddle point (with a small barrier of 0.9 kcal mol−1) on the electronic potential energy surface within the DFT framework. Two such [Py–H]˙ radicals can approach each other and form a hydrogen molecule via another transition state (Fig. S18). However, this step is associated with a relatively high energy barrier of 28.6 kcal mol−1, which may rationalize the strong dependence of the overall catalytic activity on the presence of an external cocatalyst.

Interestingly, when a hydrogen atom is transferred from [Py–H]˙ to Py–Pd, rather than being derived directly from water and photoinduced electrons, the process proceeds through an essentially barrierless path with a free energy barrier of −8.8 kcal mol−1via TS3, leading to the formation of the Py–Pd–H species. The subsequent second hydrogen atom acquisition step also occurs with minimal obstruction, surmounting a barrier of only 2.1 kcal mol−1 through TS4. Both barriers are significantly lower than those associated with the two-step hydrogen adsorption process without the involvement of [Py–H]˙ (7.9 and 21.6 kcal mol−1, respectively). Notably, the hydrogen transfer events occur within the equatorial plane of the Pd center. The remarkably low energy barriers can be attributed to the efficient overlap between the s orbital of hydrogen and the dz2 orbital of Pd. This overlap is facilitated by the monodentate coordination of pyridine to Pd, which affords considerable spatial flexibility for the hydrogen transfer process. All the aforementioned transition state structures have undergone vibrational analysis, confirming that the vibrational mode of their single imaginary frequency corresponds to the structural changes in the target reaction, and the intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) scans have successfully connected the expected reactant and product structures (Fig. S19).

On the other hand, analysis of the adsorption energies of H atoms at different environmental sites supports the same conclusion. As illustrated in Fig. S20, the adsorption energies (ΔGH*) of H atoms on the pyridinic N atoms of m-Py and Py are relatively close, being 0.87 and 0.79 eV, respectively—far lower than those of o-Py–H* (1.15 eV) and Ph–H* (1.27 eV). The absence of pyridinic N atoms in Ph renders the direct proton reduction on its surface extremely challenging. In the presence of Pd, the absolute values of the H atom adsorption energies on the Pd surface are significantly reduced compared to those without Pd. The adsorption energies of the first H atom are −0.11 eV (o-Py–Pd), −0.17 eV (both m-Py–Pd and Py–Pd), and 0.17 eV (Ph–Pd), respectively. The adsorption energies for the second H atom on these sites are even lower, consistent with the observed enhancement of catalytic activity with the introduction of metallic Pd. Among these, the adsorption energies of m-Py–Pd and Py–Pd are as low as 0.004 and 0.006 eV, respectively, indicating that these sites can be regarded as ideal catalytic sites for proton reduction.

These factors collectively contribute to a marked enhancement in the catalytic activity by ligand-supported Pd SACs for hydrogen evolution. It should be emphasized that the theoretical calculations described above are based on the simplified model of a single metal atom interacting with an isolated molecular fragment, aimed at elucidating the underlying mechanisms under idealized conditions. In real systems, the situation is likely more complex; for example, Pd atoms may coordinate with pyridine and interact with the surrounding π–electron system or coordinate with multiple pyridine groups simultaneously. Additionally, the hydrogen evolution process may also involve metal cluster pathways.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that residual palladium (Pd) species, often regarded as unintended impurities in organic photocatalysts, can efficiently facilitate charge separation and serve as highly efficient catalytic sites when atomically dispersed and strategically coordinated within molecular frameworks. In this work, using organic small molecules as supports, pyridine groups are introduced as functional ligands to anchor Pd single atoms. It is found that the monodentate coordination of pyridine to Pd induces significant splitting of the Pd d-orbitals, with the dz2 orbital substantially contributing to the HOMO of the system. This results in enhanced overlap with the s orbital of hydrogen, thereby significantly promoting hydrogen atom adsorption on the metal center. Moreover, the pyridine moiety can directly extract a proton from a H2O molecule and, in the presence of an electron donor, form a pyridyl-hydrogen radical with a low energy barrier. The subsequent two-step hydrogen atom transfer from the pyridyl-hydrogen radical to Pd also occurs with minimal energy barriers, representing a key low-energy pathway that greatly enhances the overall hydrogen evolution activity.

Additionally, the small molecule support offers advantages such as ease of synthesis, high solubility, and tunable electronic properties compared to polymeric and inorganic materials,47–52 which facilitate structural design and mechanistic studies. In this work, the pyrene core acts as a photochemical charge reservoir, where the singlet excited state is oxidatively quenched by Pd, and the triplet excited state is reductively quenched by the sacrificial agent. Both the oxidized and reduced species further participate in catalytic cycles. The active sites are located on the terminal pyridine groups, spatially separated from the light-absorbing center. All of these features promote exciton utilization and enhance charge separation efficiency. This work offers new insights into the elusive role of residual metals in influencing the activity of organic photocatalysts and provides a new strategy for the rational design of high-performance single-atom catalysts.
