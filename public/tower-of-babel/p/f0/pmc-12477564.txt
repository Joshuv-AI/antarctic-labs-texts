Primary Pulmonary Epithelial–Myoepithelial Carcinoma With Prominent Reactive Pneumocytes: Clinicopathological Insights Into a Rare Case and Literature Review
Authors: Asuka Okada, Sae Hato, Motohiro Nishimura, Maiko Takeda, Tomomi Fujii, Chiho Ohbayashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPrimary salivary gland‐type tumors may rarely be accompanied by hyperplastic alveolar cells, a pattern referred to as pneumocytic adenomyoepithelioma (PAM). Most previously reported cases have relied solely on immunohistochemical findings. In this report, we present a case involving three distinct types of epithelial cell components, identified through dual immunohistochemical staining for p40/TTF‐1, in addition to the detection of HRAS mutation using next‐generation sequencing. The female patient, in her 70s, underwent left lower lobectomy after a chest CT scan revealed a 20 mm solid mass in the left lower lobe. The final histopathological diagnosis was a primary pulmonary epithelial‐myoepithelial carcinoma (EMEC). This paper presents a case of a primary pulmonary EMEC exhibiting so‐called PAM morphology and includes a brief review of the literature.Keywords: epithelial‐myoepithelial carcinoma, HRAS, myoepithelial carcinoma, pneumocytic adenomyoepithelioma, pulmonary salivary gland‐type tumors

Introduction

Pulmonary salivary gland‐type tumors are rare, and unlike their counterparts in the salivary glands, the majority are malignant, accounting for approximately 0.2% of all primary lung cancers [1]. Among these, mucoepidermoid and adenoid cystic carcinomas comprise 90% of cases [2]. Other histological types, including epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma (EMEC) and myoepithelial carcinoma (MEC), are extremely rare [3].

Pneumocytic adenomyoepithelioma (PAM) was first described by Chang et al. in 2007 as a rare variant of EMEC [4]. Similar to EMEC, PAM exhibits a biphasic epithelial composition of ductal cells (DCs) and myoepithelial cells (MCs). However, PAM is distinguished by thyroid transcription factor‐1 (TTF‐1) expression in DCs, supporting the diagnosis of EMEC with alveolar cell differentiation. Conversely, Yuan et al. have proposed that the TTF‐1 positive cells represent entrapped pneumocytes (PCs) rather than neoplastic components [5].

The fourth edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of lung tumors [6] lists PAM as a rare variant of EMEC, a pulmonary salivary gland‐type tumor. However, in the fifth edition, PAM is not recommended histological term and instead associates it with MEC [7].

Clinical Summary

The female patient was in her 70s, with a 15 pack‐year smoking history, and no prior history of tumor resection of the head and neck region or breast tumors. An abnormal shadow was incidentally noted on a chest radiograph obtained during observation for an unrelated condition. Physical examinations, blood tests, and tumor marker evaluations revealed no abnormalities.

Chest CT revealed a 20 mm mass with a notched contour in the left lower lobe. FDG‐PET/CT demonstrated a maximum standardized uptake value of 5.7 within the mass, with no abnormal FDG uptake observed elsewhere. Bronchoscopy was performed; however, a definitive diagnosis could not be established, and a left lower lobectomy was subsequently performed.

Pathological Findings

Macroscopically, a well‐defined, 2 × 2 cm mass with central necrosis was identified. The tumor was partially separated by interlobular septa; however, no fibrous capsule was present. While in contact with the bronchiole, no continuity with the bronchus was demonstrated (Figure 1a,b).

Microscopically, the tumor exhibited branching tubular and small acinar structures with indistinct lumens embedded in a fibrohyalinous stroma, Regular branching suggested a pattern consistent with branching from the terminal airways to the alveolar ducts (Figure 2a,b). Elastica van Gieson (EVG) staining demonstrated fragmented elastic fibers within the tumor, consistent with alveoli entrapment. Moderate invasion of the pulmonary vein was observed (Figure 2c).

The epithelial component exhibited a biphasic structure, with two types of cells forming the inner layer on closer observation. One type consisted of columnar cells with small oval nuclei and decapitation secretion on the luminal side, resembling club cells (also known as Clara cells), and immunohistochemically expressed the PC phenotype, that is, CAM5.2, AE1/AE3, CK7, TTF‐1 (8G7G3/1), Napsin A, and EMA. The other inner layer cells were cuboidal cells with larger, pale nuclei and nucleoli, accompanied by a moderate amount of cytoplasm. They were negative for TTF‐1 and p40, positive for keratins, and negative to weakly positive for EMA (Figure 2f), and were identified as DCs. The outer layer consisted of polygonal cells with round nuclei and abundant pale eosinophilic or clear cytoplasm (Figure 2b,d), exhibiting the MC phenotype, that is, positive for p40, p63, S‐100, and SOX10. Ki‐67 labelling index of each cell were MC, 30.1%; DC, 20.7%; PC, 5.4%. To clearly identify these three cell types, dual immunostaining with P40 (brown DAB [3,3′‐diaminobenzidine]) and TTF‐1 (red AEC [3‐amino‐9‐ethylcarbazole]) were performed (Figure 2e). A biphasic pattern of PC/MC was predominant (Figure 3a), but a DC/MC pattern was also observed in some areas (Figure 3b). MCs and a small number of DCs were identified at the site of vascular invasion, with no PCs observed (Figure 3c,d).

Genomic Findings

DNA was extracted from FFPE samples using the QIAamp DNA FFPE Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed on a 4200 TapeStation using the Genomic DNA ScreenTape Assay (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Regarding the next‐generation sequencing (NGS) analysis, the AmpliSeq for Illumina Cancer Hotspot Panel v2 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) was employed, as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Detection and analysis were performed using the Miniseq and VariantStudio Software (Illumina). Single‐gene mutations were detected in two genes, both of which resulted in amino acid changes: HRAS p.G13R (native variant frequency: 11.1%) and PIK3CA p.H1047R (alternative variant frequency: 13.8%).

Discussion

Herein, we reported a case of primary pulmonary EMEC with PAM morphology. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case in English literature in which a mutation in the HRAS gene has been identified. Similar to salivary glands, pulmonary EMEC presents with a biphasic tubular structure comprising DCs and MCs. In PAM, DCs are characterized by TTF‐1 expression. Chang et al. initially described five PAM tumors, all in female patients in their 50s to 60s, with mass sizes ranging from 0.8 to 2.6 cm. Since all patients underwent wedge resection, the tumors are presumed to be intraparenchymal tumors. They interpret TTF‐1‐positive cells as differentiated alveolar epithelial cells [4], but it is difficult to completely rule out the possibility that these cells are entrapped PC. Yuan et al. cytogenetically demonstrated that the TTF‐1‐positive epithelial cells actively proliferating in pulmonary metastatic lesions of primary pleomorphic adenoma of the salivary gland are not tumor cells [5]. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) revealed PLAG‐1 gene rearrangement in MCs of both the salivary gland tumor resected 35 years prior and the pulmonary lesion, indicating late recurrence, however, not in the TTF‐1‐positive PCs of the lung tumor. Using this as an index case, they retrospectively examined seven cases with PAM morphology, including five cases of primary salivary gland‐type tumors of the lung. They concluded that PAM represents a variant of MEC with reactive hyperplasia of the PCs.

Accurate assessment of pulmonary tumor origin requires evaluating alveolar elastic fibers in the surrounding parenchyma. Special histochemical stains, including EVG, should be used, rather than relying solely on imaging or gross pathological examination. In the present case, fragmented alveolar elastic fibers were identified within the tumor. Moreover, the indistinct lobular architecture and regular branching support the hypothesis that the PCs originated from entrapped alveolar epithelium. The results of dual immunohistochemical staining demonstrated the presence of MCs and a small number of DCs at the site of vascular invasion, yet no PCs. These findings provide compelling evidence that PCs are non‐neoplastic.

Most pulmonary salivary gland‐type tumors are centrally located thought to originate from the bronchial submucosal glands. In rare cases, including the present case, these tumors arise in peripheral regions of the lung that lack a typical distribution of bronchial glands. However, the mechanisms underlying salivary gland epithelial differentiation in the terminal bronchioloalveolar region remain poorly understood. Similarly, the cause of hyperplasia in PCs entrapped within salivary gland‐type tumors remains unknown. This rare phenomenon, typically observed in slowly progressive tumors, has been found in solitary fibrous tumors exhibiting an adenofibromatous pattern [11].

Histological type‐specific genetic abnormalities have been identified in salivary gland tumors. Recently, such molecular findings have become increasingly important for diagnosis. In salivary gland myoepithelial tumors, fusion of PLAG1 has been detected in over 50% of cases, including cases transformed from pleomorphic adenoma (PA). Additionally, fusion of HMGA2 and EWSR1 has been observed in a smaller subset of cases [12].

Leduc et al. have analyzed eight cases of thoracic myoepithelial tumors using FISH analysis and identified gene rearrangements of EWSR1 in three cases and FUS in one case. Three tumors were found to be intraparenchymal; however, they lack morphological features consistent with those of PAM [13]. Yuan et al. have conducted FISH analysis on two pulmonary tumors; nonetheless, their findings are not consistent with MEC. Yanagawa et al. have examined pulmonary EMEC using both Sanger sequencing and pyrosequencing, yet have not detected any pathogenic mutations [14]. Comprehensive genomic profiling is necessary to identify the full spectrum of genetic alterations associated with these tumors.

In this case, gene analysis using NGS detected mutations in HRAS and PIK3CA. While HRAS mutations have been frequently reported in salivary EMEC, they are not typically associated with MEC. In a study by Urano et al. involving 87 cases of EMEC across various anatomical sites, HRAS mutations have been identified in 55% and 6% of cases at exon 3 (p.Q61R) and exon 2 (p.G13R), respectively, while PIK3CA mutations have been detected in 20.7% of cases [15]. Therefore, based on genetic testing, these findings suggest a strong molecular association with EMEC, supporting the diagnosis in the present case.

Mutation‐specific antibodies targeting HRAS p.Q61R have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity for detecting mutant HRAS and KRAS proteins at this codon position [16, 17], making them valuable tools in the differential diagnosis of melanocytic tumor, thyroid tumors and salivary tumors including EMEC. However, while MC expression correlates strongly with underlying genetic alterations, DCs typically do not express these mutations immunohistochemically. Furthermore, as observed in the present case, HRAS mutations at positions other than p. Q61R do not elicit reactivity with these antibodies.

This study had a few limitations. First was the inability to confirm the absence of HRAS mutations in PCs. Future studies utilizing single‐cell ribonucleic acid sequencing or other advanced methods may clarify the nature of PCs through cell‐type‐specific genetic profiling. Additionally, while the prognosis of pulmonary salivary gland‐type tumors with PAM features is generally favorable, pulmonary EMEC remains a potentially malignant neoplasm, with reported instances of recurrence and metastasis [18]. A fatal case has been documented [19]; nonetheless, the tumor in this instance was not genetically confirmed. Surgical resection remains the standard treatment [20]. In the present case, complete resection was achieved, with no recurrence observed after 54 months of follow‐up. However, due to the known potential for late recurrence, long‐term clinical monitoring is recommended. Moreover, evaluations should involve the exclusion of pulmonary metastases originating from primary salivary gland tumors.

In conclusion, we reported a rare case of a pulmonary salivary gland‐type tumor with features of PAM, ultimately diagnosed as EMEC based on the identification of three distinct cell types via dual immunohistochemical staining and confirmation through NGS. Diagnosing salivary gland‐type tumors from small biopsy specimens remains challenging. Additionally, the expression patterns of p40 and TTF‐1 can be misleading, particularly in high‐grade adenosquamous carcinomas. Integrating molecular data with clinical and imaging findings is recommended to improve diagnostic accuracy and guide appropriate management.
